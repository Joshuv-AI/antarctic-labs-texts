Provincial stratification and equity-weighted cost-effectiveness of malaria vaccines in Zambia under climate variability
Authors: Avulundiah Edwin Phiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMalaria remains a major public health burden in Zambia, with 5–6 million cases annually. Heterogeneous transmission complicates vaccine deployment. While RTS,S/AS01 has been piloted, the impact and cost-effectiveness of RTS,S and next-generation R21 require rigorous provincial-level evaluation.MethodsWe developed a province-stratified susceptible, exposed, infectious and recovered (SEIR)–mosquito transmission model incorporating entomological inoculation rates, calibrated to routine surveillance data. The model generated expressions for the basic reproduction number (R₀) and herd immunity thresholds. Cost-effectiveness was assessed from the Zambia Ministry of Health perspective over a 5-year analytic horizon (2024–2028), with 3% annual discounting, using disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) averted. Costs included procurement, delivery, cold-chain investment and treatment savings. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (10 000 simulations) were benchmarked against Zambia’s US$500/DALY willingness-to-pay threshold. Equity was assessed via ICER distributions and concentration indices.ResultsOver the 5-year analytic horizon, malaria vaccination substantially reduced transmission intensity and disease burden relative to a no-vaccination baseline, with marked heterogeneity across provinces. Nationally, deployment of the RTS,S vaccine was estimated to avert a median of 9.80 × 105 malaria cases (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 8.88×105−1.08×106), while the higher-efficacy R21 vaccine averted 1.09×106 cases (95% UI: 9.91×105−1.21×106). These reductions corresponded to approximately 1960 deaths averted under RTS,S and 2188 deaths averted under R21 over 5 years. Vaccine impact varied across provinces in line with baseline transmission intensity and climate-driven differences in malaria risk, influencing both health outcomes and cost-effectiveness.ConclusionsProvincial stratification highlights the need for targeted vaccine roll-out and infrastructure strengthening. RTS,S is borderline cost-effective at current prices, while R21 is highly cost-effective and potentially dominant. Findings support phased provincial deployment with equity weighting, budget integration and climate-adaptive investment to maximise health gains.Keywords: Epidemiology; Disease Transmission, Infectious; Basic Reproduction Number; Communicable Disease Control

Introduction

Malaria remains a leading cause of mortality and morbidity in sub-Saharan Africa, with Zambia reporting 8–11 million clinical cases annually—contributing 4% of global malaria deaths.1,7 Despite widespread deployment of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying and artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs), transmission remains heterogeneous across provinces, with entomological inoculation rates (EIRs) ranging from <30 to >80 infective bites per person per year.8 9 This heterogeneity poses a barrier to achieving Zambia’s stated goal of malaria elimination by 2028.10

Vaccination has emerged as a promising complementary intervention. The RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine has demonstrated moderate efficacy in clinical trials,11,14 and the more recently developed R21 vaccine shows higher efficacy in early studies.15 However, most existing economic evaluations of malaria vaccination in Africa are conducted at the national level, overlooking important subnational variation in epidemiology, health system capacity and equity.16,18 Such national averages risk obscuring where vaccines may be most impactful or cost-effective.

Equally, while cost-effectiveness analyses provide valuable guidance, they rarely incorporate dimensions of equity and health system readiness. Prior studies have shown that accounting for equity-weighted outcomes can substantially alter priority setting in low-income and middle-income countries.14 Zambia’s health system faces particular challenges around vaccine cold-chain infrastructure, distribution in remote provinces and vaccine hesitancy, all of which influence both epidemiological impact and economic performance.19 20 Climate variability, including rainfall and temperature changes, further complicates malaria control by affecting mosquito reproduction and transmission dynamics.21 22

Against this backdrop, there remains a critical need for models that integrate epidemiological, economic and equity considerations at the subnational level to inform vaccine policy in Zambia and similar endemic settings.

This study aims to fill this gap by developing a province-stratified SEIR–mosquito model for Zambia, incorporating local entomological data (EIR). Further, we integrate a cost-effectiveness analysis following the CHEERS framework and equity-weighted outcomes. Specifically, we estimate reproduction numbers (R0), herd immunity thresholds, incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), budget impact and distributional equity metrics for malaria vaccination. By linking epidemiological modelling with economic and equity analysis, we provide a decision-support framework for Zambia’s elimination programme and a replicable approach for other high-burden countries.

Methods

Overview

We constructed a deterministic, province-stratified SEIR–mosquito transmission model of Plasmodium falciparum for the 10 provinces of Zambia and embedded it within a health-economic framework to evaluate alternative malaria vaccine deployment strategies. The analysis follows CHEERS reporting guidance (completed checklist in online supplemental file 1). Full model equations, the analytic R0 derivation and additional mathematical detail are provided in online supplemental methods 6.1.1.

Transmission model

The human population in each province is modelled with compartments susceptible (S), vaccinated (V), exposed (E), infectious (I) and recovered (R). The adult mosquito population is modelled with susceptible (Sm), exposed (Em) and infectious (Im). Flows between compartments are described by a system of ordinary differential equations that include human–mosquito transmission rates βmh and βhm, vaccination rollout ω(t), mosquito recruitment Λm (see sensitivity of Λm in online supplemental table 2), mosquito mortality μm and incubation rates σ (see online supplemental methods 6.1.1 for the full ODE set and notation). Baseline parameter values and sources are summarised in online supplemental table 1 and the flow diagram is given in figure 1. Note that vaccine effects are represented as reductions in susceptibility to infection; potential effects on infectiousness or disease severity among breakthrough infections are not explicitly modelled.

Climate effects

We explicitly incorporated two climate drivers into the province-level transmission model: monthly rainfall and monthly mean temperature. Province-level monthly rainfall time series from the National Malaria Elimination Centre (NMEC; 2015–2023)10 were used to fit a province-specific seasonal recruitment factor sit that multiplies the baseline adult female mosquito recruitment Λi. Concretely, we set Λit=Λi×sit, where sit is a single-harmonic, annual periodic function with amplitude αi and phase ϕi fitted to the NMEC rainfall seasonality (see online supplemental methods 6.1.2). This rainfall-driven recruitment links observed rainy/dry cycles to seasonal EIR variation at the provincial scale.

Temperature affects two biological rates that shape transmission: adult mosquito mortality μv(T) and the extrinsic incubation period (EIP, n(T)). We implemented temperature effects using published thermal response relationships22 as multiplicative adjustments to μv and n and ran targeted thermal sensitivity scenarios (+1°C, +2°C) to quantify directional impacts on EIP, μv, and resulting R0. The targeted thermal-sensitivity experiments and resulting parameter perturbations are reported in online supplemental table 10. Where rainfall–EIR relationships were stronger (NMEC data10), rainfall-driven recruitment was used as the primary climate driver; temperature sensitivities were used as targeted scenario analyses to bound possible temperature-mediated changes in R0. Climate inputs are treated deterministically and reflect observed or historically calibrated variability rather than stochastic climate realisations or future climate projections.

Data sources and calibration

EIR and seasonal mosquito patterns were provided by NMEC surveillance (2015–2023) and validated against WHO regional datasets2 10; demography and provincial populations were taken from Zambia’s 2022 census23; vaccine efficacy and waning parameters were drawn from published phase III RTS,S trials and R21 reports.24 25 Climate and temperature datasets are publicly available.26 27 Cost inputs (procurement per dose, cold-chain capital, administration, mobile team costs) were obtained from Ministry of Health budget reports and published economic analyses (sources listed in online supplemental table 1).

We calibrated transmission parameters to provincial NMEC incidence time series (2015–2023) by non-linear least squares in MATLAB R2022b (online supplemental tables 12, 13 and 15). The fitted model explains 94% of observed variance28 (R2=0.94) with a root mean square error (RMSE) of 3.26 cases per 1000 (calibration diagnostics in onlinesupplemental figures 8 9, online supplemental tables 11 and 14).

Health-economic evaluation

Perspective and outcomes

The primary economic perspective is the Ministry of Health (budgetary cash-flow perspective). We also present a societal perspective in sensitivity analysis. Primary outcomes are incremental cost per disability-adjusted life-year (DALY) averted (ICER), probability of cost-effectiveness at prespecified willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds (see online supplemental table 7) and a 5-year (2024–2028) budget impact. DALYs combine years of life lost (YLL) and years lived with disability following standard GBD methods; disability weights (see also Global Burden of Disease Collaborative Network29 and Salomon et al30) and life-table assumptions are described in online supplemental methods 6.2.

All costs and health outcomes (DALYs) were evaluated over a 5-year analytic horizon (2024–2028) to align with the budget-impact analysis, with additional lifetime accrual of YLL for malaria deaths. Both costs and health outcomes were discounted at 3% per annum.

Costs

Vaccine procurement (base range US$4–6 per dose), delivery (including per-dose administration and mobile team costs), cold-chain capital (national fund US$2.8 million), vaccine wastage (15% base) and equipment depreciation (straight-line method, base 10-year life; sensitivity 5–15 years) were included. Capital costs were annualised; online supplemental table 1 shows the per-dose depreciation under alternative lifespans. All costs are reported in 2024 US dollars. In the base case, costs and health outcomes were discounted at 3% per annum31 32; undiscounted budget impact is also reported to aid fiscal planning (online supplemental methods 6.3).

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

We used latin hypercube sampling (LHS) to efficiently explore high-dimensional parameter space (identify influential parameters) and ran 5000 Monte Carlo simulations for probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to derive cost-effectiveness acceptability curves, ICER distributions and subnational uncertainty bands. Deterministic one-way sensitivity analyses (including Λm±50%, vaccine price, waning and discount rate) are reported in online supplemental table 8 and shown as tornado plots for both R0 and ICERs (figure 2 and onlinesupplemental figures 5 12).

We quantified distributional impacts by computing Gini coefficients and concentration indices for DALYs averted across provinces, and by applying equity weights wi=Pi/ P‾ε (poverty headcount Pi, national mean P‾, elasticity ε=1.5) to produce equity-weighted ICERs. Full methods, province poverty inputs and results are in online supplemental table 5 (see also onlinesupplemental figures 5 12).

Results

Transmission dynamics

The province-stratified SEIR–mosquito model reproduces heterogeneous transmission across Zambia. Under baseline (pre-intervention) conditions, the modelled basic reproduction number is approximately R0≈2.5 (sensitivity range ≈2.4–2.6 under ±50% Λm; online supplemental table 10). High-transmission provinces (Central, Lusaka; EIR ≈ 80) require lower vaccine coverage to reduce effective transmission: vaccines with ≥70% efficacy reduce R0 below 1 at ~65–75% coverage (see online supplemental figure 1), producing a rapid decline in local transmission (see online supplemental figure 2). Lower-transmission provinces require substantially higher coverage (typically >80%) to reach herd immunity. Across a 5-year rollout horizon, targeted vaccination in high-burden provinces projects a ~60% reduction in national incidence relative to no-vaccination scenarios (figure 3).

Climate variability and provincial heterogeneity

Rainfall-driven seasonality in mosquito recruitment explains a substantial portion of the observed provincial heterogeneity in transmission intensity. Using NMEC monthly rainfall to fit the seasonal recruitment factor si(t) yields larger seasonal EIR and higher baseline R0 in historically wetter provinces (eg, Central, Luapula, Northern) compared with drier provinces (online supplemental methods 6.1.2). Under a mid-range climate scenario (IPCC SSP2-4.5), the projected 20% increase in rainy-season precipitation by 2030 translates to an approximate 22% increase in province-level R0 and an ~18% upward shift in ICERs (online supplemental table 10), with the largest absolute R0 increases occurring in high-rainfall provinces. Temperature-mediated (+1–2°C) changes produce directionally consistent increases in EIP and mosquito demography that further raise R0 in the same provinces (see online supplemental table 10 and onlinesupplemental figures 6 7). These results explain why high-rainfall provinces require relatively higher coverage (or earlier, pre-rainy-season pulses) to bring the effective reproduction number below 1 and why climate variability materially affects provincial vaccine cost-effectiveness.

Health outcomes and cases averted

At baseline disease intensity, the model estimates an annual national malaria burden on the order of ∼1.23 million infections, consistent with provincial aggregation of transmission intensity (online supplemental table 9). Province-targeted vaccination scenarios avert the majority of these infections in high-EIR areas; detailed province-level infection and DALY estimates are reported in online supplemental table 7. Over the 5-year analytic horizon, malaria vaccination resulted in substantial reductions in disease burden relative to the no-vaccination baseline. Model-simulated malaria incidence was integrated over time to estimate cumulative infections, and cases averted were calculated as the difference between baseline and vaccination scenarios across Monte Carlo realisations.

At the national level, deployment of the RTS,S vaccine is estimated to avert a median of 9.80×105 malaria cases over 5 years (95% uncertainty interval (UI): 8.88×105−1.08×106). Under the higher-efficacy R21 vaccine scenario, the model projects a larger reduction, with a median of 1.09×106 cases averted (95% UI: 9.91×105−1.21×106). These differences reflect the combined effects of vaccine efficacy and rollout dynamics on transmission intensity.

Applying a conservative malaria case fatality ratio of 0.2%, these reductions correspond to an estimated 1960 malaria deaths averted nationally under RTS,S (95% UI: 1776−2159) and 2188 deaths averted under R21 (95% UI: 1982−2412) over the 5-year period. Although mortality reductions represent a small fraction of total infections, they contribute meaningfully to the overall public health value of vaccination when combined with morbidity reduction, economic savings and equity gains.

Overall, these results demonstrate that both vaccines yield substantial absolute health benefits, with the magnitude of impact varying across transmission settings. Provinces with higher baseline transmission intensity contribute disproportionately to national cases and deaths averted, underscoring the importance of geographically targeted and climate-adaptive malaria vaccination strategies.

Cost-effectiveness and budget impact

In the base case, the programme’s incremental economic profile is favourable. The national, programme-averaged ICER is US$395 per DALY averted (95% credible interval US$360–480/DALY). Provincial ICERs vary by roughly ~43%, with central estimates ranging from US$280/DALY (Central) to US$490/DALY (Western), depending on local epidemiology and costs (table 2 and online supplemental table 5). At a WTP threshold of US$500/DALY, PSA indicates an ~78% probability that the 70%-efficacy vaccine programme is cost-effective (figure 3, see also online supplemental figure 3).

The 5-year budget impact analysis (2024–2028) shows an annual programme operating cost ≈US$12.4 million, partially offset by ≈US$18.7 million/year in treatment cost savings (net fiscal flows and year-by-year cash flows in table 2 and online supplemental table 4). Investment in solar cold-chain is associated with favourable returns: the analysis reports an estimated US$3.40 in productivity return per US$1 invested in solar storage under base assumptions.

Equity and distributional outcomes

Distributional analyses reveal that benefits are unevenly distributed under uniform deployment. The national Gini index for DALYs/infections falls from ≈0.31 to 0.24 under the vaccine programmes modelled (reflecting an overall reduction in inequality of health burden), but concentration indices show that unweighted gains are relatively concentrated in less-poor provinces unless equity weights are applied (online supplemental table 5). Applying an equity weight elasticity (ε=1.5) increases measured pro-poor gains and changes allocation priorities (equity-weighted DALYs reported in online supplemental table 5).

Uncertainty and sensitivity analyses

Results are robust to most one-way perturbations but sensitive to a small number of parameters. Probabilistic analyses (5000 Monte Carlo draws) and LHS identify vaccine efficacy, coverage (rollout speed) and mosquito recruitment (Λm) as the principal drivers of both epidemiological impact and ICER variability (tornado diagrams in onlinesupplemental figures 5 12). Varying vaccine lifespan (depreciation life) from 5 to 15 years produces a modest shift in ICERs (see online supplemental table 3), and ±50% changes in Λm alter R0 by roughly ~34% and shift ICERs within the reported credible intervals (online supplemental table 4). Temperature-mediated changes in vector demography (+1 °C) produce directionally consistent increases in μm and EIP that modestly raise R0 (thermal sensitivity shown in online supplemental table 10 and online supplemental figure 7).

We used a province-stratified SEIR–mosquito model linked to a CHEERS-compliant economic evaluation to examine the epidemiological, fiscal and distributional consequences of malaria vaccine deployment across Zambia. Our principal findings are threefold. First, vaccination with high-efficacy formulations (≥70%) can materially reduce transmission in high-burden provinces: under our baseline assumptions, such vaccines reduce R0 below 1 in Central and Lusaka at coverage of roughly 65–75%, and a targeted rollout in those provinces is projected to reduce national incidence by ∼60% within 5 years (figure 3). Second, the national programme is likely to be cost-effective under plausible WTP thresholds: the programme-averaged ICER is below US$500/DALY in the base case, with provincial ICERs ranging approximately US$280–490/DALY (table 2). Third, equity matters—the distribution of DALYs averted is heterogeneous and unweighted deployment tends to favour provinces with higher baseline burden and better access. Equity-weighted analyses and concentration indices (online supplemental table 5) show that allocation choices meaningfully alter which populations benefit (see also online supplemental figure 10).

Discussion

Interpretation and policy relevance

These results support a targeted, implementation-aware approach. The epidemiological heterogeneity across provinces means that national averages conceal where vaccines are most impactful: concentrating early vaccine supply in Central and Lusaka would deliver outsized reductions in transmission and better value for money per DALY averted. Economically, pairing vaccine rollout with investments in cold-chain resilience (eg, solar storage) and outreach (mobile teams) enhances the return on investment—we estimate substantial treatment savings and favourable productivity returns for cold-chain capital under the base assumptions (table 2 and online supplemental table 4). Finally, the equity analysis indicates that a purely efficiency-driven allocation can leave poorer provinces relatively disadvantaged; incorporating equity weights or targeted outreach can rebalance benefits without large losses in aggregate efficiency.

Policy interpretation of transmission outcomes

To support decision-making, key epidemiological outputs from the transmission model can be interpreted in operational terms. First, reductions in transmission intensity and reproduction numbers translate directly into prioritisation guidance: provinces with higher baseline transmission contribute disproportionately to national cases and deaths averted and therefore yield the greatest absolute health gains per dose administered. Targeting these settings remains beneficial even when herd immunity thresholds are not reached.

Second, the model does not assume unrealistically high coverage levels. Meaningful reductions in malaria burden occur under moderate vaccine uptake, reflecting direct protection rather than reliance on population-wide herd immunity. From a policy perspective, this implies that vaccination programmes can remain effective under real-world constraints related to supply, delivery capacity and seasonal access, particularly when combined with geographically targeted rollout.

Finally, scenarios in which the reproduction number remains above one should not be interpreted as programmatic failure. In high-transmission or climate-sensitive settings, vaccines function primarily as burden-reduction tools rather than elimination instruments. Even without achieving herd immunity, substantial reductions in morbidity and mortality can be realised, supporting the use of vaccination as a complementary intervention alongside existing vector control measures.

Taken together, these interpretations highlight that climate-adaptive, provincially targeted vaccination strategies can deliver robust public health benefits across a range of feasible coverage levels, even in settings where elimination is not immediately attainable.

Policy-relevant interpretation of uncertainty

While PSA captures variability in model parameters and outcomes, not all sources of uncertainty are equally relevant for policy decisions. In this analysis, uncertainty in baseline transmission intensity and achievable vaccine coverage exerts a greater influence on projected health impact and cost-effectiveness than uncertainty in vaccine efficacy within plausible ranges. Importantly, parameters that drive relative comparisons between strategies—such as the ranking of RTS,S versus R21 or the prioritisation of high-burden provinces—are more stable than those affecting absolute outcome magnitudes.

Key conclusions remain robust under conservative assumptions. Even under lower-bound estimates of vaccine impact, both vaccination strategies avert substantial numbers of cases and deaths, and targeted deployment in high-transmission provinces consistently yields greater health gains per dose administered. Scenarios reflecting unfavourable assumptions therefore reduce the scale of benefit rather than reversing comparative or policy-relevant conclusions.

From a decision-making perspective, uncertainty can also be interpreted as decision risk. Across probabilistic simulations, the likelihood that vaccination strategies exceed plausible budget or affordability thresholds remains low relative to the probability of achieving meaningful reductions in disease burden. Framing uncertainty in this way emphasises that residual uncertainty represents manageable financial and operational risk rather than a barrier to action, particularly when interventions are phased or geographically targeted. Across probabilistic simulations, the qualitative ranking of vaccination strategies and the prioritisation of high-transmission provinces were preserved.

Policy interpretation of equity and distributional outcomes

Equity-weighted cost-effectiveness analysis provides additional insights beyond average national efficiency metrics. In this analysis, applying equity weights systematically increases the relative priority of provinces with higher baseline malaria burden and greater socioeconomic disadvantage, as health gains in these settings receive greater weight in the social objective function. As a result, provincial prioritisation based on equity-weighted ICERs differs from rankings based solely on unweighted cost-effectiveness, favouring earlier or more intensive deployment in high-burden provinces.

These findings also highlight potential equity risks associated with uniform national rollout strategies. While equal per-capita allocation may appear neutral, such approaches can inadvertently perpetuate or exacerbate existing inequities by allocating substantial resources to lower-burden provinces where marginal health gains are smaller. In contrast, geographically targeted deployment informed by equity-weighted metrics concentrates benefits where avoidable morbidity and mortality are highest, improving both efficiency and fairness.

From a policy perspective, the results support a geographically progressive rollout strategy in which high-transmission and socioeconomically disadvantaged provinces are prioritised during initial vaccine deployment, with subsequent expansion as supply and delivery capacity increase. Such an approach aligns equity objectives with operational feasibility and offers a practical pathway for integrating distributional considerations into national malaria control planning.

Generalisability to other public health threats

Although this analysis focuses on malaria, the modelling framework is broadly applicable to infectious diseases characterised by environmental drivers, partial observability of infection and behavioural responses to risk. Lassa fever exemplifies these challenges, with environmentally mediated spillover risk, imperfect surveillance of infection prevalence and vaccination decisions made under substantial uncertainty, as highlighted in recent health-economic modelling studies (see, eg, Smith et al33). Adapting this framework to such settings could support climate-informed targeting, equity-aware prioritisation and robust decision-making for a wider range of public health threats.

Strengths

This analysis advances prior work in three ways. First, it embeds province-level EIR into a mechanistic SEIR–mosquito framework, allowing realistic subnational threshold estimates rather than coarse national averages. Second, it couples transmission modelling with a comprehensive costing approach that includes procurement, administration, vaccine wastage and cold-chain capital and depreciation (sensitivity to lifespan 5–15 years reported in online supplemental table 3). Third, and importantly for policy, we quantify distributional outcomes (Gini and concentration indices) and provide a budget impact analysis (2024–2028) that fiscal planners can use (table 2 and online supplemental table 4).

Limitations and future research

Several limitations merit attention. First, model outputs depend on parameter assumptions; although we calibrated to provincial surveillance (2015–2023) and performed extensive PSA and LHS (5000 Monte Carlo draws), uncertainty remains—particularly around vector demography (Λm), vaccine waning and true coverage dynamics (onlinesupplemental figures 5 12). Calibration of the transmission model relied on routine surveillance incidence data, which are appropriate for assessing clinical burden but may under-represent true transmission intensity due to the presence of asymptomatic and subclinical infections. As malaria transmission declines, this limitation becomes increasingly important, as reported incidence may no longer reflect residual infection or exposure risk. Future analyses could be strengthened by integrating serological or antigen prevalence data, which have been shown to provide more stable and sensitive indicators of transmission dynamics and population-level exposure. Incorporating such measures would be particularly valuable for elimination-oriented decision-making, where identifying persistent low-level transmission and guiding targeted interventions are critical. This approach is consistent with recent methodological work highlighting the value of antibody and antigen prevalence for monitoring transmission and informing policy decisions in low-burden settings.34 The model incorporates prevalence-responsive vaccination uptake to reflect adaptive programme behaviour under varying transmission conditions. However, behavioural parameters governing uptake were not directly calibrated to empirical behavioural or hesitancy data and were instead specified based on plausible assumptions informed by the health-economic literature. Similar approaches have been adopted in recent modelling studies evaluating intervention cost-effectiveness under uncertainty, where behavioural responses are treated as scenario-dependent rather than directly estimable parameters (eg, see Antony et al35; Smith et al36).

Behavioural and structural uncertainty may therefore contribute to variability in estimated health and economic outcomes, particularly in settings where vaccine hesitancy, access constraints or health system capacity limit achievable coverage. While the present analysis addresses uncertainty using conventional PSA, recent methodological work has highlighted the potential advantages of distributional comparison frameworks based on optimal transport, such as Wasserstein distances, for quantifying differences in outcome distributions arising from behavioural and structural assumptions (see Oliver et al37; Oliver et al38). These approaches offer a complementary alternative to sampling-based methods and may improve computational efficiency and interpretability in complex stochastic models.39 40

Future analyses could extend the present framework by explicitly including behavioural parameters in PSA or structured scenario analysis, allowing uncertainty in uptake, hesitancy and access to be propagated alongside epidemiological and economic parameters. Such extensions would be particularly valuable for public health decision-making in low-resource settings, where behavioural responses are a critical determinant of programme effectiveness.

Second, while our study includes malaria-specific mortality (see online supplemental figure 6) and full DALY accounting, estimates remain sensitive to case fatality and life-table assumptions; we report those sensitivities in online supplemental methods 6.2. Third, our climate extension models rainfall-driven mosquito recruitment because province-level rainfall–EIR relationships are the most robust local data; temperature effects on adult mortality (μm) and EIP (σm-1) were explored via targeted thermal sensitivities (+1°C, +2°C) rather than a fully coupled temperature model (results in online supplemental table 10). Fourth, vaccine acceptance was represented by a prevalence-responsive uptake function rather than a full empirical behavioural model; we provide a short Stackelberg game sketch in online supplemental methods 7.1 and a schematic best-response figure (online supplemental figure 11) to show how messaging and perceived costs map to uptake. Finally, some costing elements (long-term maintenance, detailed mobile team operational budgets at scale) are described with the best available sources but would benefit from country programme accounting during implementation.

Further work should (1) couple temperature dynamics with rainfall in a fully integrated climate–vector model, (2) collect empirical uptake data during pilot rollouts to inform behavioural parameterisation and (3) refine budget impact and maintenance costing with real programme accounting during early implementation.

Summary for policymakers

Malaria vaccination can substantially reduce illness and deaths in Zambia over 5 years, particularly when vaccines are targeted to provinces with the highest transmission (see a proposed Zambia malaria vaccination rollout strategy in online supplemental figure 10). Our analysis shows that both RTS,S and R21 prevent large numbers of cases and deaths even without achieving herd immunity, and that targeting high-burden areas improves both efficiency and equity. These findings support climate-adaptive and geographically targeted vaccine deployment as a practical strategy for maximising public health impact under real-world constraints.

Conclusion

Malaria vaccination has the potential to produce substantial, measurable public health gains in Zambia over a 5-year horizon: under our scenarios, RTS,S averts approximately 9.8×105 cases and 1960 deaths, while R21 averts 1.09×106 cases and 2188 deaths. When combined with the economic and equity analyses presented above, these absolute health benefits support targeted vaccine deployment in high-burden provinces as a priority. To maximise impact, programmes should prioritise timely, pre-rainy-season delivery in high-transmission areas and strengthen cold-chain and delivery systems to protect vaccine effectiveness under climate variability. Together, these measures would help translate the modelled reductions in morbidity and mortality into real-world public health gains.
