Integrated proteomic and transcriptomic analyses reveal that the Rj4-mediated immunity network restricts soybean-rhizobia symbiosis
Authors: Jia-he Gao, Fang Tang, Ya-wen Wang, Qiu-yuan Liu, Feng-yan Yi, Zhi-qiang Zhang, Cui-ping Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLegumes-rhizobia symbiosis has high specificity regulated by a specific class of genes, such as Rj4. Rj4 encodes a thaumatin-like protein belonging to the PR-5 family that restricts soybean from nodulation with many strains of Bradyrhizobium elkanii. How Rj4, a member of broad-spectrum resistance family, specifically regulates nodulation remains unclear. To uncover the molecular mechanism of Rj4, current study integrated transcriptome and proteome to analyze the downstream regulatory pathways and key genes mediated by Rj4, through investigating the gene expression and protein abundance in the roots of soybean BARC2 (Rj4/Rj4) after 0, 6 and 24 h post inoculation (hpi) with B. elkanii USDA61.ResultsThe results showed that a total of 1660 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and 2633 differentially abundance proteins (DAPs) were identified, in which resistance-related genes and symbiosis-related genes showed opposite expression trend. The key symbiosis-related genes, such as ENOD55, PUB1, and NFP, were up-regulated at 6 hpi but down-regulated at 24 hpi compared with control group. Conversely, the expression of most plant immunity related genes exhibited an initial decrease at 6 hpi followed by an increase at 24 hpi, suggested Rj4 restricted host plant from nodulation in early process through induction of plant immunity related genes and pathways. The key genes involved in plant immunity included pattern-triggered immunity (PTI)-related genes, such as pattern recognition receptors FLS2 and EFR, mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) signaling pathway MPKs, calcium ion related genes CNGCs, CaMCML, and ROS related genes RBOH, as well as effector-triggered immunity (ETI) related resistance (R) genes RPM1 and Ptis, plant hormone signal transduction related genes JAZ and TGA, and flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthesis gene Cyps. We validated 8 DEGs via qRT-PCR, showing consistent trends with RNA-Seq (Spearman r > 0.9).ConclusionsThese findings provide new insights into how plant immunity inhibits legume-rhizobial symbiosis in the Rj4-mediated regulatory network, and provide key candidate genes to study legume-rhizobial nodulation specificity for future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12047-1.

Introduction

Legumes account for approximately 27% of global crop production [1], largely due to their unique ability to establish symbiotic relationships with rhizobia, enabling biological nitrogen fixation, although other factors such as their nutritional value and environmental adaptability also contribute to their significant role in global crop production. This process contributes an estimated 55–60 million tons of fixed nitrogen annually [2], comparable to nearly half of the world’s synthetic nitrogen fertilizer production worldwide, thus playing a pivotal role in sustainable agriculture [3]. One of the most remarkable characteristics of this symbiosis is its high degree of specificity, regulated by a series of finely tuned molecular interactions between host plants and rhizobial partners.

Host specificity in legume-rhizobia symbiosis manifests both inter- and intra-specifically, influenced by multiple molecular checkpoints. Initially, flavonoids secreted by the host roots act as chemical signals that activate rhizobial synthesis of lipo-chitooligosaccharide signals, known as Nod factors [4]. These Nod factors are recognized by plant transmembrane Lysin motif (LysM) -domain receptor-like kinases in a strain- and ecotype-specific manner, adding another layer to the determination of host range [5]. Recent studies indicate that distinct regions within the LysM1 domain contribute to symbiosis specificity across and within legume species [6]. Similar to the Nod factors-LysM receptor interaction, surface polysaccharides or secreted proteins by rhizobia are structurally recognized by host cell surface-localized receptors, serving as a third layer to modulate host range [7–9]. In addition, effector proteins secreted by the type III secretion system (T3SS) interact with plant intracellular immune receptors, serving as a solid layer to determine nodulation specificity [10].

In soybean, several host genes including Rj2, Rfg1, and Rj4 have been identified to restrict nodulation with specific rhizobial strains through genotype-dependent recognition, often requiring functional T3SS [11]– [12]. Among the host genes identified in soybean, Rj4 gene encodes a thaumatin-like protein (TLP), a member of the pathogenesis-related protein 5 (PR-5) family [15]. While TLPs are broadly involved in plant immunity, Rj4 confers a highly specific nodulation restriction phenotype, particularly against B. elkanii strains such as USDA61 [14]. Many strains of B. elkanii can form symbiotic relationships with soybean, exhibiting low nitrogen-fixation efficiency but displaying high competitiveness against other rhizobia with superior nitrogen-fixation capacity [13]. Rj4 expression is induced upon rhizobial B. elkanii USDA61 infection [16], and the Rj4 allele is prevalent in wild soybean (Glycine soja) and Southeast Asian cultivars, but is rare in North American varieties [17]. However, the downstream regulatory mechanisms by which Rj4 exerts its dual role in defense and symbiosis restriction remain poorly understood.

Although previous studies have focused on the identification and localization of the Rj4 gene, few have explored the molecular networks and dynamic responses involved in Rj4-mediated symbiosis. There is a pressing need to investigate how Rj4 influences downstream gene expression and protein abundance during the early stages of rhizobial infection.

In this study, we conducted an integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis of soybean roots from the Rj4-genotype cultivar BARC2 following inoculation with B. elkanii USDA61 at different time points. By capturing the temporal dynamics of gene and protein expression, this work aims to identify key regulators and pathways modulated by Rj4, explore potential crosstalk between immunity and nodulation, and provide novel molecular insights into the mechanism of host restriction. This study not only fills an important knowledge gap in Rj4-mediated symbiotic regulation but also lays a foundation for future breeding strategies aimed at optimizing soybean-rhizobia compatibility.

Results

Transcriptomic and proteomic profiling reveals time-dependent molecular alterations after infection

A total of 31.6 gigabases (Gb) clean data were obtained from transcriptome sequencing of 9 samples (roots from 3 different sampling time, with 3 replicates per sampling time), after removing low quality and short reads. The percentage of Q30 bases was >93%. Clean reads were compared with the reference genome for sequence alignment, and the comparison ratio >93%. A total of 47,749 unigenes were detected, including 47,228 annotated genes and 521 novel genes. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to assess temporal changes at transcriptomic and proteomic levels (Fig.1A, C). In both datasets, the first two principal components explained the majority of variance (transcriptome: PC1 54.3%, PC2 22.3%; proteome: PC1 41.2%, PC2 33.6%), clearly separating mi (mock inoculation), 6 hpi, and 24 hpi samples with minimal overlap between 95% confidence ellipses. These results indicate progressive, time-dependent molecular alterations following infection. Violin plots further confirmed comparable global expression levels across groups (Fig.1B, D), with increased variability at 6 hpi and 24 hpi, suggesting elevated heterogeneity during the infection process.

Transcriptome analysis was performed on comparison groups of mi vs. 6 hpi, mi vs. 24 hpi and 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, in order to analyze which genes and pathways were involved in Rj4-mediated restriction of nodulation during early B. elkanii USDA61 inoculation stages. A total of 1660 DEGs were identified, and the number of DEGs varied between groups. There were lowest number of DEGs in mi vs. 6 hpi, with 109 up-regulated and 79 down-regulated DEGs, and highest number of DEGs in 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, in which 927 up-regulated and 108 down-regulated genes were identified, suggesting that most of the DEGs were induced to increase the expression by B. elkanii USDA61 after 6 h of inoculation. In addition, 54 and 475 unique DEGs were identified in mi vs. 6 hpi and mi vs. 24 hpi (Fig. 2A), respectively.

To compare the protein abundance changes of soybean BARC2 after B. elkanii USDA61 inoculation in different time points, proteome analysis was carried out. DIA (Data-independent Acquisition) analysis was performed on 3 comparison groups to quantify and identify the DAPs. A total of 23,739 proteins were identified. Among these proteins, 1290, 1603, and 522 DAPs were found in mi vs. 6 hpi, mi vs. 24 hpi and 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, respectively (Fig. 2B). There were 919 up-regulated DAPs and 371 down-regulated DAPs in mi vs. 6 hpi, and 893 up-regulated DAPs and 710 down-regulated DAPs in mi vs. 24 hpi. In addition, there were 522 DAPs in 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, including 187 up-regulated and 335 down-regulated DAPs (Fig. 2C). In contrast to the trends exhibited on the transcriptome result, most of the DAPs were present in mi vs. 6 hpi and mi vs. 24 hpi groups, whereas less DAPs were identified in 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, indicating more genes were subjected to post-transcriptional regulation upon B. elkanii USDA61 inoculation.

To further explore the functional enrichment of DEGs and DAPs in soybean response to B. elkanii USDA61 before and after inoculation, all 23,739 proteins were aligned to the GO (Gene Ontology), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), and KOG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups) database. As shown in Fig. 3A, a total of 21,246 proteins were found in the 3 databases. Of these, 20,941, 7,567 and 7,580 proteins were annotated in GO, KEGG, and KOG databases, respectively.

KOG analysis is a database of direct homologous classification of proteins. Each KOG term’s proteins are assumed to descend from one ancestral protein. The DAPs aligned to KOG database were classified into 25 groups based on their functions, which include involvement in RNA processing and modification [A], defense mechanism [V], and cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis [M] (Fig. 3B). The group labeled [R] “general function prediction only” had the most genes, followed by signal transduction mechanisms [T] and post translational modification, protein turnover, chaperones [O]. Figure 2B also showed other groups as below. Their functions are interconnected and collectively contribute to the complex biological processes occurring in the post-inoculation soybean root. The findings suggest a multifaceted response involving various cellular and metabolic pathways, highlighting the intricate regulatory mechanisms at play.

Function annotations for DAPs and DEGs

GO enrichment analysis was performed on all DAPs and DEGs of 3 comparison groups, which were classified into 3 GO categories: ‘biological process’, ‘cellular component’, and ‘molecular function’. The most enriched category for both DEGs and DAPs was ‘biological process’, where the main functions were defined as ‘metabolic process’ and ‘cellular process’. In all comparison groups, ‘binding’ and ‘catalytic activity’ were the most enriched GO terms annotated under ‘molecular function’, while ‘cell’ and ‘membrane’ were top 2 enriched GO terms under ‘cellular component’ (Table 1). In the mi vs. 6 hpi comparison, 158 DEGs (97 upregulated DEGs) were classified into 39 categories while 1140 DAPs (799 upregulated DAPs) were categorized into 41 categories. For mi vs 24 hpi, 533 DEGs (307 upregulated DEGs) were classified into 41 categories and 1454 DAPs (799 upregulated DAPs) were grouped into 43 categories. In 6 hpi vs 24 hpi, 770 DEGs (683 upregulated DEGs) were classified into 43 categories and 470 DAPs (161 upregulated DAPs) were classified into 42 categories (Supplemental Fig. 1). In both, the mi vs 6 hpi and mi vs 24 hpi comparisons, fewer DEGs were enriched in GO terms than DAPs. However, in the 6 hpi vs 24 hpi comparison, more upregulated DEGs were enriched in GO terms than upregulated DAPs.

GO enrichment analysis revealed distinct, time-dependent biological processes at both transcriptomic and proteomic levels in response to B. elkanii USDA61 inoculation (Fig. 4). At the transcriptome level, early infection (mi vs. 6 hpi) was significantly enriched in terms such as “extracellular region”, “response to nitric oxide”, “seed germination”, and “terpenoid biosynthetic process”, indicating that early responses involved extracellular signaling, immune perception, and initial defense activation. Moreover, enrichment in"oxidation reduction process” and “oxidoreductase activity” suggest active redox regulation and ROS signaling during the initial immune response. By 24 hpi (mi vs. 24 hpi), transcriptomic enrichment shifted toward “heme binding”, “tetrapyrrole binding”, and “oxidoreductase activity with incorporation or reduction of molecular oxygen”, reflecting a transition to stress homeostasis and metabolic reorganization. These changes imply a suppression of early immune signaling and a redirection toward cellular maintenance and oxidative balance. Proteomic data further supported these temporal dynamics. At 6 hpi, differentially abundant proteins (DAPs) were highly enriched in “ribosome biogenesis”, “ribonucleoprotein complex assembly”, “rRNA processing”, and “chromatin assembly”, highlighting rapid activation of the translational machinery and nuclear processes needed for early defense protein synthesis. At 24 hpi, enriched proteomic GO terms included “protein complex assembly”, “mitochondrial functions”, “catalytic activity”, and “oxidoreductase activity”, suggesting that sustained infection leads to a shift toward protein turnover, energy regulation, and metabolic remodeling. Together, these findings illustrate a coordinated temporal response in soybean BARC2 roots: from early immune activation and transcriptional machinery mobilization to later structural reorganization and metabolic adaptation. This dynamic regulation provides key insights into the molecular mechanism of Rj4-mediated inhibition of rhizobial symbiosis.

KEGG pathway analysis was performed on all DEGs and DAPs (Fig. 5). A total of 320 DEGs and 953 DAPs were annotated by KEGG analysis. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed time-specific molecular responses to infection at both transcriptomic and proteomic levels. Transcriptomic profiling at 6 hpi revealed enrichment in metabolic pathways and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, along with valine, leucine, and isoleucine degradation, and isoflavonoid biosynthesis. These pathways indicate that the early response involves metabolic reprogramming and activation of flavonoid-related signaling. At 24 hpi, transcriptomic enrichment expanded to include phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, circadian rhythm regulation, and stilbenoid biosynthesis, reflecting a transition from metabolic adaptation to broader physiological modulation under stress. Proteomic analysis at 6 hpi highlighted early activation of translation-related pathways, including ribosome assembly, ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, and spliceosome function, as well as starch and sucrose metabolism, indicating high biosynthetic demand. By 24 hpi, DAPs were significantly enriched in fatty acid degradation, plant-pathogen interaction, and proteasome-related pathways, suggesting a shift toward defense responses and protein turnover. The consistent enrichment of phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and secondary metabolite pathways at both time points emphasizes their central role in defense and symbiosis regulation. Collectively, the KEGG results demonstrate a dynamic transition from metabolic and biosynthetic activity at 6 hpi to immune-related and catabolic processes at 24 hpi, reflecting time-resolved regulation of symbiosis suppression mediated by the Rj4 genotype.

Protein-protein interaction networks analysis of defense related pathways

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using DEPs and the STRING database (Fig. 6). A total of 57 protein nodes and 192 high-confidence interaction edges (combined score > 0.4) were identified. Node size represents connectivity, node color indicates protein abundance (pink for high, mint green for low), and edge color/thickness corresponds to interaction strength. Heat shock proteins (HSPs), including HSP90-5, HSP90-2, HSP90-6, and HSP80A, exhibited the highest degrees of interaction. HSP80A showed the highest protein abundance. These HSPs interacted with disease resistance-related proteins such as Suppressor of G2 allele of skp1 (SGT1) and Resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. maculicola 1 (RPM1) to form the HSP90-SGT1-RPM1 module, which also showed links to metabolic proteins including Chalcone Synthase (CHS) and Chalcone Isomerase (CHI). A basic Leucine Zipper (bZIP) transcription factor, Tetraspanin Galectin 1 (TGAL1), was identified interacting with Cochlin (COCH). Other transcription factors, such as WRKY and basic Helix-Loop-Helix (bHLH) family members, were involved in inter-module connections, contributing to the complexity of the network.

Integrated analysis of transcriptome and proteome

To assess the correlation between transcriptomic and proteomic responses following inoculation with rhizobia B. elkanii USDA61, a nine-quadrant analysis was performed to compare expression ratios of mRNAs and proteins. In the mi vs. 6 hpi comparison (Fig. 7A), 1,290 DAPs and 267 DEGs were identified, but only a limited number of matched pairs were detected in quadrants 3 (mRNA↑/protein↑, 8 pairs), 1 (mRNA↓/protein↑, 1 pair), 7 (mRNA↑/protein↓, 4 pairs), and 9 (mRNA↓/protein↓, 3 pairs), indicating low concordance between transcript and protein levels and suggesting a predominant role of post-transcriptional regulation at early stages. In contrast, the mi vs. 24 hpi comparison (Fig. 7B) identified 1,083 DAPs and 421 DEGs, with increased numbers of co-regulated pairs in quadrants 3 [8], 1 [21], 7 [7], and 9 [22], suggesting improved transcript-protein concordance and indicating 24 h as a key stage for coordinated transcriptional and translational regulation.

Representative functional genes in each quadrant were further analyzed for the mi vs. 6 hpi and mi vs. 24 hpi comparisons. In mi vs. 6 hpi (Table S1) quadrant 3 (mRNA↑/protein↑), key immune-related genes were significantly upregulated, including SBT1.2 (a subtilisin-like protease involved in cell wall remodeling), PPO (polyphenol oxidase), and PR10 (a pathogenesis-related protein). SBT1.2 showed consistent upregulation at both transcript and protein levels and interacted with MPK3, CDC48, SCPL24, and spast (Fig. 7C). In the mi vs. 24 hpi group (Table S2), IOS1 (an LRR receptor-like kinase) and ACO1 (an ethylene biosynthesis enzyme) were also upregulated, suggesting roles in symbiotic signaling and hormone regulation. Quadrant 7 (mRNA↑/protein↓) included several bHLH transcription factors and CRK2, indicating possible translational repression or post-transcriptional control. In quadrant 1 (mRNA↓/protein↑), genes such as DAD2 and PER45 showed protein accumulation despite reduced mRNA levels, suggesting involvement of protein stability or negative feedback mechanisms. Genes like D7OMT, HSP83A, and CYP81E7 were transcriptionally upregulated but exhibited low protein abundance (Fig. 6B-D). Quadrant 9 (mRNA↓/protein↓) included PER12, OMT3, WAXY, and DMR6, reflecting transcriptional and translational suppression under incompatible symbiosis. Overall, Pearson correlation analysis revealed poor concordance between transcript and protein expression patterns, likely due to the combined effects of translational regulation, protein localization, modification, and degradation.

Defense related regulate network

The process of rhizobia infection in soybean was regulated by plant signaling pathways, which subsequently triggered biological processes related to symbiosis and resistance. In this study, soybean BARC2 did not form symbiotic nodules with B. elkanii USDA61, we hypothesized that this phenomenon associated with the widespread activation of resistance genes and the suppression of genes related to symbiosis. Therefore, the current study primarily focused on plant signal transduction, resistance pathways, and the metabolic pathways of secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids and isoflavonoids, which regulated the symbiosis between rhizobia and soybean. Due to the significant inconsistencies between transcript expression and protein abundance, we integrated the DEGs and DAPs into a regulatory network from the transcriptome and proteome data. This network comprehensively described the regulatory relationships and expression levels of candidate DAPs and DEGs involved in the suppression of symbiosis-related resistance and signaling pathways between soybean BARC2 and B. elkanii USDA61. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of plant-pathogen interaction and plant hormone signaling pathway indicated that there were 7 DEGs in mi vs. 6 hpi, 8 DEGs in mi vs. 24 hpi, while there were 59 DEGs in 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi. However, there were 25 DAPs in mi vs. 6 hpi, 6 DAPs in 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, and 44 DAPs in mi vs. 24 hpi. The plant innate immune system consisted of PTI and ETI, each of which had multiple key genes in response to symbiosis regulation (Fig. 8). The DEGs and their encoding proteins identified in the plant-pathogen interaction include the PRRs gene FLS2, PAMPs gene elf18, MAPK signaling pathway MPKs, WRKY transcription factor family WRKY23, calcium ion related genes CNGCs, CaMCML and RBOH and ROS related genes RBOH, which were involved in pattern-triggered immunity (PTI). Effector-triggered immunity (ETI) related resistance (R) genes RPM1 and Rdl9 and R genes regulatory factors EDS1, HSP90, SGT1 and Pti1. These genes were regulated by hormones such as salicylic acid, auxin, cytokinin, brassinosteroids, and jasmonic acid. Jasmonic acid regulated COI1, JAZ, and MYC2, while salicylic acid affected the expression of NPR1 and TGA. Auxin regulated the differential expression of AUX, GH3, and SAUR. Cytokinin-related gene A-ARR and brassinosteroid-related genes BKI1 and TCH4 also showed differential regulation after infection. In addition, 33 genes and their corresponding proteins were involved in flavonoid and isoflavonoid pathways, including Cyps, D7OMT7, CCOAOMAT2, CHS and so on. (Fig. 9)

(Fig. 10). qRT-PCR results showed that the expression trends of most genes were consistent with RNA-seq data, and the corresponding Spearman correlations between qRT-PCR and RNA-seq were above 0.9, which indicated that the transcriptome data was accurate and reliable.

The expression trends between qRT-PCR and RNA-Seq were largely consistent, and Spearman correlation coefficients exceeded 0.9, supporting the accuracy and reliability of the transcriptomic data

Discussion

The symbiosis between soybean and rhizobia is highly specific, and elucidating its underlying mechanisms is essential for improving nitrogen use efficiency and crop yields. This study integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which the Rj4 genotype of soybean inhibits symbiosis with rhizobia B. elkanii USDA61. A dynamic expression pattern was observed across 3 time points, with the highest number of DEGs appearing in 6 hpi vs. 24 hpi, while most DAPs occurred in mi vs. 6 hpi. The relatively low Pearson correlation between mRNA and protein levels suggested substantial post-transcriptional regulation, emphasizing the complementary nature of transcriptomic and proteomic data in dissecting symbiotic inhibition.

Immune responses in symbiotic inhibition

In soybean BARC2, nodulation appears to be suppressed through activation of both PAMP-triggered immunity (PTI) and effector-triggered immunity (ETI). PTI is initiated by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as FLS2, which senses PAMPs like elf18, activating calcium channels (CNGCs) and leading to intracellular Ca²⁺ influx. This signal is transmitted via calcium-binding proteins (CaM, CDPK) to activate RBOHBs (NADPH oxidases) and the MAPK pathway, which ultimately induces WRKY transcription factors and expression of defense genes such as PR1, initiating hypersensitive response (HR) [18]– [19]. Consistent with this pathway, our data showed upregulation of CNGC13, CPK11, RBOHB, and CML48 at the transcript level post 24 hpi, while their corresponding proteins remained suppressed, indicating post-transcriptional regulation that may fine-tune immune responses.

In ETI, resistance proteins such as RPM1 trigger HR upon effector recognition [20], aided by cofactors like SGT1, HSP90, and RAR1 to stabilize R protein conformation [21]. However, in our study, RPM1 protein was downregulated, potentially reducing ETI effectiveness. Additionally, Pti1, which interacts with Pto to enhance HR [22], was also transcriptionally induced but translationally suppressed. This pattern was observed across several PTI/ETI components, suggesting coordinated post-transcriptional suppression following rhizobial infection. Our PPI network analysis identified HSP83 and HSP90 as immune hubs with extensive interactions. Although nine HSP-related DEGs were upregulated, their protein expression was consistently reduced at 6 hpi, indicating possible mRNA degradation or delayed translation [23]. These results underscore the role of heat shock proteins in immune regulation during early symbiotic stages. Genes KCS1 and KCS10, involved in long-chain fatty acid biosynthesis and cuticular wax formation [24], were transcriptionally activated but showed no detectable protein, suggesting roles in HR-associated lipid remodeling may also be regulated post-transcriptionally. Post-transcriptional regulation, including mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and protein turnover, may play a critical role in modulating the temporal and spatial dynamics of symbiosis-related proteins, allowing plants to precisely coordinate immune suppression and nodule development, warranting further study of RNA-binding proteins, microRNAs, and protein degradation pathways. Importantly, EDS1, a key regulator of TIR-NB-LRR-mediated defense, was upregulated at both transcript and protein levels. EDS1 links recognition of bacterial effectors (e.g., AvrRps4) to downstream signaling through interaction with RPS4, acting both as a transducer and effector target [25], indicating its critical role in rhizobia-triggered immune activation.

Additionally, SBT1.2, a subtilisin-like protease known to be involved in broad-spectrum pathogen defense [26], showed significant induction at both transcript and protein levels at 6 hpi in our data, suggesting its active involvement in early defense responses. Antioxidant and redox-related pathways were also implicated. LHY, encoding a MYB114 transcription factor, may suppress anthocyanin biosynthesis, which contributes to pathogen resistance via antioxidant capacity [27]. GGT3 (glutathione hydrolase 3) was downregulated post 6 hpi, possibly reflecting glutathione accumulation as a stress response [28]. In contrast, GSTU18, encoding glutathione S-transferase U18-like, was upregulated in our transcriptome. GSTs are known for their roles in detoxification and resistance to biotic stress [29], as well as maintaining ROS homeostasis [30].

Taken together, these findings suggest that soybean BARC2 initiates robust PTI and ETI responses following B. elkanii USDA61 inoculation, but employs post-transcriptional regulation to prevent excessive immune activation. This immune activation, combined with redox balance and stress responses, contributes to early suppression of symbiotic establishment.

Hormonal regulation of symbiosis

Plant hormones play multifaceted roles in growth, immunity, and nodulation, with hormonal signaling acting as a key modulator of symbiotic interactions [31]. Cytokinins and auxins promote nodule formation, while jasmonic acid (JA) generally inhibits it, and salicylic acid (SA) and flavonoids exert diverse regulatory effects [32]. Rhizobia-derived cytokinins induce nodule primordium formation via genes such as NSP2 and LHK1, regulated by MtRR1 and MtRR9, which trigger cortical cell proliferation [33]– [34]. In our study, A-type ARR DEGs showed an initial decrease followed by an increase, suggesting stage-specific activation of cytokinin signaling. Similarly, auxin pathway genes including SAUR1, GH3.1, and IAA9 were upregulated post-inoculation, consistent with their role in early nodulation; GH3.1’s induction parallels its pathogen-responsive behavior in rice [35]. JA signaling showed concentration-dependent effects—suppressing nodulation at high levels and promoting it at low levels [36]– [37]—and our data revealed transcriptomic upregulation of COI1, JAZ, and MYC2, implicating JA in early nodulation regulation. For SA signaling, while SA generally enhances pathogen resistance [38], Nod factors (NFs) reduce SA accumulation, modulating defense [39]. In our proteome data, NPR1 levels increased, TGA decreased, and TGAL1 showed strong interaction with COCH (a regulator of NPR5), aligning with known SA pathway dynamics involving TGA2/3 and PR1 expression [40]. Brassinosteroids (BRs), known to negatively regulate nodulation [41], were also implicated; BKI1 transcripts initially rose then declined [42], and multiple TCH4 genes—associated with cell wall remodeling and responsive to both auxin and BR—were upregulated [43]. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that hormonal signaling—including cytokinin, auxin, JA, SA, BR, and flavonoids—forms an integrated system that governs nodulation outcomes and immune modulation in soybean.

Flavonoid and isoflavonoid pathways in symbiosis inhibition

Flavonoids, derived from the phenylpropanoid pathway, and isoflavonoids, a subclass of flavonoids specialized in symbiotic interactions and pathogen defense, are essential secondary metabolites involved in both symbiosis and pathogen defense in legumes like soybean [44]– [45]. Under nitrogen deficiency, soybean roots secrete flavonoids to attract rhizobia and induce nod gene expression, promoting nitrogen-fixing nodule formation [46]. In this study, multiple genes and proteins related to flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthesis were differentially regulated following rhizobial inoculation. Several Cytochrome P450 (CYP) genesCYP71, CYP73, CYP75, CYP81, CYP93, and CYP98—were identified as DEGs, encoding hydroxylases that modify isoflavonoids and regulate synthesis of flavonoids, isoflavonoids, and lignin [47]– [48]. Notably, CYP81E1/E7, encoding isoflavone 2’-hydroxylase-like enzymes, exhibited opposing transcript and protein trends, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation. Chalcone synthase (CHS), the key enzyme catalyzing the first committed step in flavonoid biosynthesis [49], was initially downregulated at the transcript level but later upregulated, with six CHS genes (e.g., CHS1, CHS3, CHS5) showing this pattern. However, four corresponding CHS proteins exhibited contrasting trends, further supporting the presence of translational regulation. In contrast, D7OMT, encoding a methyltransferase that catalyzes antimicrobial and antioxidant isoformononetin [50]– [51], showed consistent upregulation at both mRNA and protein levels. Another enzyme, CCOAOMT2, important in lignin biosynthesis and pathogen resistance, was transcriptionally upregulated throughout, though its protein levels peaked early and later declined—echoing reports that CCOAOMT6 gene knockout enhances lignin degradation and disease susceptibility [52]. Together, these data suggest that rhizobial infection induces a coordinated but complex regulation of flavonoid/isoflavonoid biosynthetic genes in soybean BARC2. The early activation of key enzyme genes and their translation into proteins indicates a rapid metabolic response. It is further hypothesized that some of these regulatory genes may contribute to symbiosis inhibition through negative feedback loops that restrict nodule development, serving as a molecular switch balancing defense and symbiosis.

Symbiosis-related genes in Rj4-mediated inhibition

Among the key symbiosis-related genes, NFP, PUB1, and ENOD55 showed dynamic transcriptional changes following inoculation with B. elkanii USDA61, indicating their possible roles in the Rj4-mediated symbiotic inhibition. NFP (Nod factor perception) encodes a LysM-type receptor-like kinase responsible for recognizing Nod factors produced by rhizobia [53]. In this study, NFP was significantly upregulated at 6 hpi, which is consistent with its early role in initiating symbiotic signaling. However, its expression sharply declined at 24 hpi, suggesting that the downstream nodulation pathway may be actively suppressed after initial Nod factor recognition. This transient induction followed by repression implies that NFP may be post-transcriptionally regulated or counteracted by immune signaling, particularly under the influence of Rj4. PUB1 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that has been identified as a negative regulator of nodulation, particularly by promoting degradation of Nod factor signaling components [54]. Our transcriptome data showed that PUB1 was also upregulated at 6 hpi, possibly as a feedback mechanism to attenuate Nod signaling or in response to pathogen-like signals. Its expression also decreased by 24 hpi, further supporting the idea that PUB1 may serve as a molecular switch coordinating symbiosis suppression via ubiquitination of symbiosis-associated proteins. ENOD55, an early nodulin gene associated with infection thread formation and nodule primordium development [55], exhibited a similar expression trend: upregulation at 6 hpi followed by suppression at 24 hpi. This expression pattern suggests that the symbiotic program was initially activated but later repressed. As ENOD55 is a known marker of successful rhizobial colonization, its temporal expression change indicates that the Rj4-mediated response might allow initial bacterial recognition but rapidly triggers downstream immunity to inhibit further nodule development. Together, these results indicate that NFP, PUB1, and ENOD55 are transiently activated in the early symbiotic window (6 hpi), but their suppression at 24 hpi coincides with the activation of plant immune pathways. This supports the hypothesis that Rj4 may function as a regulatory hub controlling the balance between early symbiosis signaling and immune suppression, ultimately inhibiting nodule formation.

In summary, this integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analysis has elucidated key molecular mechanisms by which the Rj4 genotype of soybean inhibits early-stage symbiosis with B. elkanii USDA61. Our findings demonstrate that both immune responses—particularly PTI and ETI—and hormone signaling pathways are significantly involved in this inhibition process. The activation of defense-related genes, especially those encoding calcium channels, WRKY transcription factors, heat shock proteins, and EDS1, highlights a robust immune response modulated through post-transcriptional mechanisms. Hormonal pathways involving cytokinin, auxin, JA, SA, and BR showed stage-specific regulation, suggesting complex hormonal crosstalk that balances defense and nodulation. Moreover, the differential regulation of flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthetic genes, including CYPs, CHS, D7OMT, and CCOAOMT2, points to their role not only in signaling but also in the metabolic fine-tuning of symbiosis. The expression divergence observed between transcripts and proteins underscores the importance of post-transcriptional regulation in coordinating these responses. Additionally, dual-function genes such as NFP and PUB1 further suggest that Rj4 may act as a central node mediating trade-offs between immunity and symbiotic compatibility. Collectively, this study offers novel insights into the early molecular events that lead to nodulation inhibition in non-compatible interactions and lays a foundation for future genetic or molecular strategies to improve rhizobial symbiosis and nitrogen fixation in soybean.

This study reveals the dynamic regulation of transcription factors during the early stages of soybean–rhizobium symbiosis, particularly highlighting the dual roles of key genes involved in immunity and flavonoid biosynthesis pathways. These findings underscore the complexity of plant responses to microbial signals and open new avenues for future research. Potential directions include functional validation of key transcription factors and their interacting proteins through gene editing, construction of regulatory networks to elucidate downstream mechanisms, and further exploration of the crosstalk between immune and symbiotic signaling. Moreover, integrating multi-omics approaches with natural germplasm variation analysis may accelerate the breeding of soybean cultivars with enhanced nitrogen fixation efficiency, contributing to the advancement of sustainable agriculture.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, bacterial culture, and inoculation

Bradyrhizobium elkanii USDA61 was cultured on yeast extract mannitol (YEM) agar plates (5 g/L mannitol, 5 g/L sodium gluconate, 0.5 g/L yeast extract, 0.2 g/L MgSO₄·7 H₂O, 0.1 g/L NaCl, 0.5 g/L K₂HPO₄, 15 g/L agar) at 28 °C for 5–7 days. Bacterial cells were collected from plates and diluted with sterile distilled water to an optical density (OD₆₀₀) of approximately 0.1 for inoculation.

The soybean cultivar BARC2 (PI547895) [56] used in this study was obtained from the USDA GRIN-Global, National Plant Germplasm System (NPGS). Seeds were surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 1 min, thoroughly rinsed five times with sterile distilled water, and planted in sterilized pots containing vermiculite and perlite (1:2, v/v). Plants were grown in a growth chamber set to a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 26 °C.

Five-day-old soybean seedlings were inoculated by applying 10 mL of B. elkanii USDA61 bacterial suspension or sterile distilled water (mock inoculation, mi) to each plant’s roots. Root tissues were harvested at 6 h and 24 h post-inoculation (hpi), immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80 °C until use. Three independent biological replicates per treatment, each comprising at least 3 seedlings, were collected for subsequent analyses.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and RNA-seq analysis

A total of 9 independent RNA-Seq libraries, corresponding to the 3 sample sets (mi, 6 hpi, and 24 hpi), were constructed and sequenced. Total RNA of root samples was extracted using Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, USA). RNA integrity was confirmed by an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and checked by RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Then, mRNA was enriched by Oligo (dT) beads, break into short fragments, and reverse transcribed into cDNA by using NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina. After the double-stranded cDNA fragments were purified, end-repaired, addition of one base “A” at 3’-end, and adapters ligation, the reaction product was purified with magnetic beads, taken to size selection by agarose gel electrophoresis and PCR amplification. The resulting cDNA library was sequenced using Illumina Novaseq 6000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Raw sequencing reads were filtered by fastp 0.18.0 [57] to get high-quality clean reads. Then rRNA reads were removed by mapping short reads to ribosome RNA database using Short reads alignment tool Bowtie2 2.2.8 [58]. For reference-guided assembly, the clean reads were mapped to the reference genome (https://ftp.ensemblgenomes.ebi.ac.uk/pub/plants/release-47/gtf/glycine_max/) using HISAT 2. 2.4 [59].

Gene expression was quantified by RSEM [60] as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 (version 1.26.0) [61], defined by a threshold of |log₂ fold change| ≥ 1 and adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses were performed using the clusterProfiler package (v3.14.3) in R [62, 63]. GO terms and KEGG pathways with adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

The RNA-seq raw data generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under the BioProject accession number [PRJNA1273609].

Protein extraction, digestion, Nano-HPLC-MS/MS, and data processing

Protein extraction, digestion, Nano-HPLC-MS/MS, and data processing

The root samples (mi, 6 hpi and 24 hpi) were used for protein extraction. Total proteins were extracted using the cold acetone method [64]. Root samples were ground to power in liquid nitrogen, dissolved in 2 mL lysis buffer containing 8 M urea, 2% SDS and 1x Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche Ltd. Basel, Switzerland), then sonicated 30 min on ice, centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 30 min at 4℃ and transferred the supernatant to a new tube. The total proteins were precipitated with ice-cold acetone at −20℃ overnight, followed by washing the precipitates 3 times with acetone and sonication on ice to re-dissolve the proteins in 8 M Urea. Protein concentration and quality was determined by BCA Protein Assay Kit and SDS-PAGE, respectively. The proteins were digested into peptides with sequence-grade modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI). After digestion, the peptide mixture was dissolved in the Buffer A (20 mM ammonium formate in water, pH10.0, adjusted with ammonium hydroxide), and then fractionated by high pH separation with Ultimate 3000 system (Thermo Fisher scientific, MA, USA) and a reverse phase X Bridge C18 column (4.6 mm x 250 mm, 5 μm, Waters Corporation, MA, USA). A linear gradient was applied to the reverse phase separation, starting from 5 to 45% Buffer B (20 mM ammonium formate in 80% ACN, pH 10.0, adjusted with ammonium hydroxide) in 40 min. The column was re-equilibrated at the initial condition for 15 min and maintained at 1mL/min at 30℃. Then fractions were collected, and each fraction was dried with a vacuum concentrator.

The peptides were re-dissolved and analyzed by on-line nanospray LC-MS/MS using an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer coupled to an EASY-nLC™ 1200 System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Then 3 µL peptide sample was loaded onto the analytical column (Acclaim PepMap C18, 75 μm x 25 cm) with a 120-min gradient, from 5 to 35% Buffer B (B:0.1% formic acid in ACN), maintaining at 200 µl/min flow rate with the column temperature of 40 °C. The mass spectrometer was run under data independent and dependent acquisition (DDA and DIA) modes separately, and automatically switched between MS and MS/MS mode.

Proteomics data processing

Spectronaut™ Pulsar X (Biognosys AG, Switzerland) was used to process and analyze the raw data of DIA with default parameters. Retention time prediction type was set to dynamic iRT (internal Retention Time). The ideal extraction window was dynamically determined by Spectronaut Pulsar depending on iRT calibration and gradient stability [65]. FDR was set to 1% as the cut-off on precursor and protein level and decoy generation was set to mutated, which was like scrambled but only applied a random number of AA position swamps (min = 2, max = length/2). All selected precursors passing the filters were used for quantification and only the average top 3 filtered peptides which passed the 1% FDR cut off were used to calculate the major group quantities. Student’s t-test was used to screen the differentially abundance proteins (DAPs) with the criteria of fold change > 1.5 or < 0.67 and P < 0.05. The functions and classification of annotated proteins were analyzed based on blast against the GO, EuKaryotic Orthologous Groups (KOG), and the KEGG database [66]. The Venn diagram showed the distribution of genes and database matching results. The StringDB protein interaction database (http://string-db.org/) was used to identify protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks. The PPI pairs were extracted with a combined score of > 0.400.

Transcriptomic and proteomic joint analysis

For the joint omics analysis, the DEGs and DAPs detected in the transcriptome and proteome were counted separately, then the correlation analysis was performed by R (version 3.5.1, https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/R-3.5.1-win.exe). A nine-quadrant map was drawn based on changes in the expression of the gene in the transcriptome and proteome, followed by performing the quantitative analysis and enrichment analysis of the genes in each region of the nine-quadrant map.

Verification of transcriptome data by quantitative real-time PCR

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to validate the transcriptomic data by determining the expression levels of 8 genes with each 3 replicates. These 8 genes were selected from the DEGs of soybean BARC2 treated by rhizobia. β-actin gene ACTB was chosen as reference gene. A 15 µL of each sample was used in the reaction, including 1 µL cDNA, 7.5 µL 2×SG Green qPCR Mix, 0.25 µL of each 10 μm primer, and 6 µL ddH2O. The reactions were was run on a StepOnePLUS instrument from Applied Biosystems, USA. The cycling program was: 40 cycles of 95 °C for 20 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Gene-specific primers (Table 2) were designed using Primer3Plus (https://dev.primer3plus.com). The relative gene expression was quantified with the 2 –∆∆Ct method.
