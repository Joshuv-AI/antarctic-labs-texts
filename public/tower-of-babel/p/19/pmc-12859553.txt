A pathogen peptidoglycan scaffold coated with artificial biomembrane promotes broad resistance to bacterial infections by dynamically reprogramming macrophage metabolism
Authors: Junjie Guo, Shuo Jia, Zibo Mai, Chaonan Wang, Zheng Jia, Jiaqing Wang, Xinran Yao, Jiaqi Liu, Fang Wang, Junwei Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The increasing severity of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria and the shortage of effective treatment strategies urgently require the development of new immunotherapies to combat superbug infections. Trained immunity may offer a novel and effective mechanism to combat resistant superbugs. However, there are currently few materials capable of effectively activating trained immunity, highlighting the need for new agents that provide more durable protection. In this study, we developed a bacterium-like particle (BLP) based on protein-free artificial biomembrane coating immune activator, named LM@pBLP, which features a simple and rapid preparation process, excellent biocompatibility, long-term stability, and a cost-effective advantage. LM@pBLP trains the immune system to target a broad range of pathogens, offering rapid, broad-spectrum, and long-lasting protection against MDR infections. After stimulation with LM@pBLP, it activates glutathione metabolism and amino acid metabolism, induces macrophage metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming changes, and regulates phagocytosis and inflammatory responses to infection. Additionally, LM@pBLP regulates reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby maintaining oxidative stress homeostasis. Our study demonstrates that LM@pBLP primarily provides rapid, broad-spectrum, and long-lasting protection for experimental animals by activating trained immunity, which opens a new avenue for addressing MDR infections.

Introduction

Globally, the misuse of antimicrobial drugs has led to an increasing resistance of microorganisms to antibiotics, especially to multiple antibiotics, a phenomenon known as multidrug resistance (MDR) [1]. The spread of multidrug-resistant bacteria in society has led to increases in morbidity, mortality, healthcare spending, and antibiotic use [2]. The World Health Organization (WHO) reports that “superbugs” - microorganisms resistant to a variety of antibiotics - have emerged as one of the foremost global health threats, contributing to the deaths of millions worldwide [3]. If the current trend of inappropriate and excessive antibiotic use persists, antibiotic resistance is projected to cause 10 million deaths globally by 2050 [4]. Traditionally, approaches to address antibiotic resistance include optimizing antibiotic use, promoting research and development, improving healthcare facilities to implement preventive and control strategies, and limiting the use of antibiotics in agriculture and livestock [5]. However, simply reinforcing the current strategies is unlikely to adequately tackle the growing and increasingly severe issue of antibiotic resistance. Given the pressing challenges of antibiotic resistance and the current scarcity of effective antimicrobial agents, it is essential to explore alternative, non-antibiotic approaches to address resistant infections [6]. Through immunoprophylaxis, vaccines can reduce the occurrence of infections and lower dependence on antibiotic treatments, thereby alleviating the issue of antibiotic resistance [7]. This has led to growing attention on vaccine strategies in combating super-resistant bacteria.

In order to combat antibiotic resistance, vaccine development has mainly targeted pathogens that are susceptible to resistance, frequently cause infections, necessitate antibiotic treatment, or result in complications [8]. While antimicrobial resistance was once seldom seen in Bordetella pertussis, it now seems to be on the rise among individuals who lack full protection or are only partially protected, possibly due to the incomplete effectiveness of the vaccine [9]. Additionally, the effectiveness of vaccines is constrained by several factors, especially the limitations of serotype coverage. Serotype replacement often leads to the dominant presence of serotypes not covered by the vaccine in the population [8]. For example, after the introduction of the heptavalent PCV (PCV7), serotype 19A (which is not included in this vaccine) significantly increased in patients [10,11]. This indicates that when a vaccine only covers a subset of pathogenic serotypes, antibiotic pressure can still drive the development of resistance [11]. Overall, these traditional vaccines typically only prevent the serotypes included in the vaccine, resulting in limited coverage. However, recent research has revealed that cells of the innate immune system can undergo long-term adaptation, enhancing their response capabilities - a phenomenon known as trained immunity [12]. This innate immune response does not depend on specific antigens. By stimulating the innate immune system, it triggers a nonspecific immune response, providing broad-spectrum defense when facing different pathogens [13]. Thus, this idea implies that immunological tactics that trigger memory-like innate immune responses could offer a fresh and practical way to fight drug-resistant superbugs.

Recent study by Yan et al. [14] shows that a protein-free trivalent vaccine can extend the survival time of mice infected with multiple pathogens by harnessing trained immunity mechanisms. However, despite using a 10-fold dose to enhance the immune response, the vaccine's long-term protective effect against MRSA has not significantly improved. β-glucan, well-known inducers of trained immunity, nevertheless exhibit limitations when administered alone in vivo, such as a short half-life and multiple adverse effects that hinder the induction of durable immune responses [15]. This is due to the fact that one significant difference between trained immunity and classical immune memory is the length of the effect: trained immunity's memory lasts less time than classical adaptive immune memory [16]. Recent studies have found that after initial microbial exposure, the stable memory alveolar M-cell phenotype can persist for at least four months [17], suggesting that trained immunity can be maintained for a relatively long period under certain conditions. Furthermore, β-glucan can cause hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) to preferentially develop toward bone marrow, producing trained monocytes that offer some protection against tuberculosis, according to recent mice research [18]. These findings suggest that optimizing novel strategies may effectively extend the duration of immune memory and enhance long-term immune protection.

Bacterium-like particles (BLPs) are hollow particles composed of peptidoglycan (PGN) from Gram-positive bacteria. Initially derived from food-grade Lactococcus lactis, BLPs have been widely used in vaccine delivery and immunoenhancement studies due to their safety and immunostimulatory properties [19]. In our previous work, we demonstrated that the pathogenic bacterium Listeria monocytogenes can also be used to produce BLPs (pBLPs). The unique PGN structure of L. monocytogenes more effectively activates pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), significantly upregulates the expression of TLR1 and TLR2 in macrophages, activates the NF-κB signaling pathway, and induces the production of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines. Moreover, pBLPs can trigger trained immunity through the mTOR/HIF-1α pathway, showing superior immunostimulatory potential compared to probiotic-derived BLPs [[20], [21], [22]]. In recent years, lipid metabolism has emerged as a key regulatory factor in the induction and maintenance of trained immunity. For example, cholesterol biosynthesis has been shown to enhance innate immune memory responses [23,24]. Based on this, we designed a novel immune activator, LM@pBLP, by self-assembling an artificial lipid membrane composed of the anionic phospholipid DOPA and cholesterol on the surface of pBLPs. Previous studies have shown that cholesterol-rich liposomes can induce stronger complement activation [25], while negatively charged liposomes are readily engulfed by peritoneal macrophages [26]. This system not only retains the strong immunogenicity of pathogen-derived BLPs but also seeks to further enhance trained immunity through lipid component modulation, providing a promising strategy for the development of innate immune enhancers.

The primary objective of this study is to develop LM@pBLP, an artificial biofilm-coated pathogen-derived peptidoglycan scaffold, and to investigate how trained immunity mechanisms can rapidly and effectively defend against a range of bacterial infections, while also addressing the issue of long-term immune protection efficacy. First, we employed an efficient and rapid encapsulation method to successfully develop LM@pBLP. Using fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, flow cytometry, and transmission electron microscopy, we confirmed the encapsulation's effectiveness and success. Next, we demonstrated that LM@pBLP provides rapid, broad-spectrum, and long-lasting protection. Within two days, LM@pBLP provided complete protection against S. aureus infection, demonstrating broad defense against various pathogens, with protection lasting for 21 days. We found that LM@pBLP offers long-lasting immune protection, further confirming its prolonged efficacy. To prove LM@pBLP can train immunity, we used in vitro, in vivo, and ex vivo models. Our findings indicate that LM@pBLP has the capability to alter macrophage metabolism and activity, providing heterologous protection against S. aureus infection. Through transcriptome analysis, we further explored the mechanisms of LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity, providing molecular-level evidence for its long-term protective effect. Finally, we evaluated its clinical translational potential and compared it with vancomycin, demonstrating superior efficacy against MRSA infection. In conclusion, this study demonstrates that LM@pBLP, through trained immunity mechanisms, provides rapid and effective protection against various bacterial infections, while addressing the issue of long-term efficacy. This finding provides new insights and opportunities for future vaccine design, offering a new approach to tackling MDR infections.

Materials and methods

Materials

The Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences (Chinese Academy of Sciences, China) provided the Raw264.7 cells, which were then grown in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) with 10 % FBS (CLARK Bioscience, USA) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Biotopped, Beijing, China) added. RAW264.7 cells were cultured in an incubator maintained at 37 °C with 5 % CO2.

We acquired female Kunming mice of 4–6 weeks (18–22 g) from Changsheng Biotechnology Company (Liaoning, China). All animals were free to drink and eat on a 12-h light-dark cycle.

S. aureus CMCC26003 was acquired from the China Medical Culture Collection Center, whereas E. coli CVCC230 and Clostridium perfringens type A (C57-8) were procured from the China Veterinary Drug Administration. L. monocytogenes is stored in our laboratory. E. coli pET28a-GFP/Rosetta [27], which that fluoresces green is stored in our laboratory. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus strain (MRSA) USA300 (ATCC BAA-1717) was generously supplied by Dr. Yanhua Li from Northeast Agricultural University.

Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) supplied the β-glucan (CAS: 9041-22-9) and Aladdin (Shanghai, China) provided the DOPA [1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphate (sodium salt)], cholesterol, and chloroform. 5′-Deoxy-5'-(methylthio)adenosine (MTA, S880063), 2-Deoxy-D-Glucose (2-DG, D807272), and Rapamycin (RAPA, S115842) were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China), while Rapamycin (RAPA, S115842) was also obtained from Aladdin (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of LM@pBLP

As previously mentioned [21], pBLPs were prepared through hot acid pretreatment. In brief, bacteria from fresh overnight cultures were harvested and rinsed once with sterile distilled water. L. monocytogenes were then suspended in 10 % trichloroacetic acid, boiled for 45 min, washed three times, and stored at −80 °C. Lipid membrane encapsulation was conducted following the previously outlined procedure [28]. After combining DOPA and cholesterol at a 4:1 M ratio and dissolving them in 8 mL of chloroform, a lipid film was produced using a rotary evaporator (INDUCTION MOTOR, China). The pBLP was reconstituted in 1 mL of a phosphate-buffered calcium solution containing 12.5 mM CaCl2. Thereafter, the lipid film was rehydrated in 1 mL of the pBLP solution and vortexed for 15 min (Qilin Medical Instrument Factory, China). The lipid membrane-coated pBLP, named LM@pBLP, was stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Characterization of LM@pBLP

The morphology of LM@pBLP was analyzed in comparison to unencapsulated pBLP using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Hitachi, Japan). Furthermore, dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Malvern Panalytical, UK) was employed to ascertain the average particle size and Zeta potential of LM@pBLP subsequent to lipid membrane encapsulation. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to confirm the encapsulation of pBLP with lipid membrane. The encapsulation of pBLP by the lipid membrane was verified and the encapsulation efficiency was assessed through flow cytometry (Becton, Dickinson and Company, USA).

Stability studies

Following the protocol described in Ref. [28], LM@pBLP was stored separately at 4 ± 1 °C and 27 ± 2 °C. Particle size and zeta potential alterations were assessed via dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Malvern Panaco Instruments, United Kingdom) following 30, 60, and 90 days of storage.

In vivo toxicity assessment

Based on the protocol outlined in Ref. [28], mice were randomly assigned into three groups (n = 5). The control group was administered PBS via intraperitoneal injection on days 0, whereas the LM@pBLP group received LM@pBLP (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL) through the same route and schedule. Body weight was recorded daily throughout the experiment. On days 10 and 21, all animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and major organs were harvested for histological examination using H&E staining.

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China) [28]. The in vitro cytotoxicity of LM@pBLP was tested in RAW264.7 cells. Briefly, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in DMEM medium in flat-bottom 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) at a density of 5000 cells per well. Then, the cultures were incubated with 100 μL of LM@pBLP solutions (1 × 109, 2 × 109, 3 × 109, and CFU/mL) for 24 h. Cells treated with DMEM without LM@pBLP served as control wells. Wells containing DMEM and LM@pBLP but no cells were used as blank wells. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added, and the incubation was continued at 37 °C for another 30 min. The OD450 values of the cultures were recorded. The cell viability (%) was calculated using the formula: Cell viability (%) = (sample − blank)/(control − blank) × 100 %.

Animal experimental models

For rapid protection, forty mice were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 10). The control group received PBS via intraperitoneal injections on days 0 and 2; the infection group received intraperitoneal injection of PBS on day 0 and S. aureus (1 × 109 CFU/200 μL) on day 2; the LM@pBLP group received intraperitoneal injection of LM@pBLP (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL) on day 0 and S. aureus (1 × 109 CFU/200 μL) on day 3; the LM@pBLP + β group received intraperitoneal injection of LM@pBLP + β (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL includes 100 μg of β-glucan) on day 0 and S. aureus (1 × 109 CFU/200 μL) on day 2. Mouse body weight and survival were tracked up to day 5. Mouse activity was assessed for damage using a modified version of the referenced methodology [29]. After the challenge, all mice were euthanized through cervical dislocation on day 5. Samples of serum, lung, liver, and spleen tissues were collected for subsequent analysis.

For broad-spectrum protection, mice were randomly assigned into twelve groups (n = 10). The control groups received PBS via intraperitoneal injections (i.p.) on days 0 and 7. The infection groups received intraperitoneal injection of PBS on day 0, followed by an intraperitoneal injection of S. aureus (5 × 108 CFU/200 μL), E. coli (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL), or C. perfringens (2 × 108 CFU/200 μL) on day 7, respectively. The LM@pBLP(1 × 108 CFU/200 μL) groups or LM@pBLP + β (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL includes 100 μg of β-glucan) groups received intraperitoneal injection of LM@pBLP or LM@pBLP + β on day 0, followed by an intraperitoneal injection of S. aureus (5 × 108 CFU/200 μL), E. coli (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL), or C. perfringens (2 × 108 CFU/200 μL) on day 7, respectively. Mouse body weight and survival rate of mice were assessed for a duration of 3 or 7 days post-infection, and the mice were evaluated based on established criteria [29]. Ultimately, all mice were slain through cervical dislocation, and spleen and liver tissues were collected for subsequent examination.

For long-term protection, mice were randomly assigned into twelve groups(n = 10). The control groups only received PBS. The infection groups received PBS (i.p.), and then intravenously injected with MRSA (5 × 108 CFU/100 μL) on days 7, 14, or 21, respectively. The LM@pBLP groups or LM@pBLP+β groups received intraperitoneal injection of LM@pBLP (2 × 108 CFU/200 μL) or LM@pBLP+β (2 × 108 CFU/200 μL includes 200 μg of β-glucan), and then intravenously injected with MRSA (5 × 108 CFU/200 μL) on days 7, 14, or 21, respectively. Mouse body weight and survival rate were observed for 15 days post-infection. Mouse activity was assessed for damage using a modified version of the referenced methodology [29]. Ultimately, all mice were slain through cervical dislocation, and kidney tissues were collected for subsequent examination.

Treatment of systemic MRSA infection, mice were randomly assigned into three groups(n = 10). Mice were injected intravenously with MRSA (5 × 108 CFU/100 μL). One hour after infection, the mice were injected intraperitoneally with PBS, vancomycin (25 mg/kg) or LM@pBLP (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL), respectively. Mouse body weight and survival rate were observed for 14 days post-infection. Mouse activity was assessed for damage using a modified version of the referenced methodology [29]. Ultimately, all mice were slain through cervical dislocation, and kidney tissues were collected for subsequent examination.

Models of trained immunity

For the in vitro trained immunity model, according to the method described in Refs. [27,30], Raw264.7 cells were stimulated with PBS, β-glucan (30 μg/mL) [31], or LM@pBLP (0.01 U/well) for 24 h. The cells were then washed three times with PBS and cultured in fresh medium for 6 days. Afterward, the cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 24 h. Cells were collected in Trizol, and culture supernatants were harvested for subsequent experiments.

Inhibition of the inducing steps of trained immunity was performed according to the method outlined in Ref. [30]. First, Raw264.7 cells were treated for 1 h with 3 mM of MTA or 15 mM of 2-DG to validate the role of the mTOR pathway in LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity. To investigate the role of epigenetics in LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity, Raw264.7 cells were initially treated for 1 h with 10 μmol/L of RAPA. Subsequently, the stimuli were directly added to initiate primary activation without prior washing. After the initial stimulation, the subsequent procedures were performed according to the established training protocol.

In the trained immunity ex vivo model, thirty mice were randomly allocated into three groups (n = 10), following the procedure outlined in Refs. [27,31]. On days 0 and 3, the control group received PBS via intraperitoneal injections (i.p.); β-glucan group received 500 μg β-glucan (i.p.); LM@pBLP group received LM@pBLP (1 × 108 CFU/200 μL) (i.p.). Peritoneal macrophages were collected from all groups on day 6 and incubated for 2 h. Then, cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and collected at 1, 6, 12, and 24 h post-stimulation for subsequent analysis.

In the in vivo model of trained immunity, fifteen mice were randomly allocated to one of three groups (n = 5), as detailed in references [29,32]. The control and infection groups received PBS via intraperitoneal injection (i.p.) on days 0 and 3, while the LM@pBLP group received LM@pBLP (i.p.). On day 7, all groups, except the control group, were intraperitoneally administered 1 × 109 CFU/200 μL of S. aureus. Mouse body weight and survival rate were assessed for three days following infection, with scoring conducted according to the method outlined in reference [28]. At the conclusion of the experiment, all mice were euthanized through cervical dislocation on day 10. Samples of liver, spleen, kidney tissues, and serum were collected for subsequent analysis.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

The RNA-seq analysis was carried out by NovelBio Bio Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China, after 6 h of LPS stimulation of mouse peritoneal macrophages. Gene Ontology (GO) annotations were retrieved from the official website (http://www.geneontology.org/). Pathway analysis was conducted using the KEGG database. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was carried out with GSEA software v4.1.0 (Broad Institute), using the 50 Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB as input. Fisher's exact test was employed to identify significantly enriched GO categories and pathways, with a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted following the protocol described by Shi et al. [32] using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri). cDNA synthesis was performed using ReverTra Ace® (TRT-101, TOYOBO, Shanghai, China). Real-time PCR was subsequently performed with SYBR Green dye (EnzyArtisan, Shanghai, China) on an Applied Biosystems® 7500 Real-Time PCR System. β-actin functioned as the housekeeping control, and relative mRNA expression levels were determined using the 2−ΔΔCT method. A compilation of primers utilized in this investigation is presented in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

According to the ex vivo model, peritoneal macrophages were collected and incubated for 2 h on the 6th day. Then, cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) and collected for subsequent analysis 6 h after stimulation. Briefly, cells were harvested by low-speed centrifugation and washed with PBS. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer, and protein concentrations were determined via the BCA assay. Twenty micrograms of each protein sample were separated by 12 % SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with 1 × Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) containing 5 % (w/v) non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following this, the blots were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system. The intensity of bands was quantified and normalized using ImageJ software.

Measurement of bacterial load

According to the method of reference [34], spleens, livers and kidneys were collected under aseptic conditions, weighed, and then homogenized in sterile PBS. The homogenates from each organ were serially diluted to 10−5 of the original concentration, inoculated onto TSA agar plates, and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C prior to colony enumeration.

Histopathological detection

According to the method of reference [33], the mouse organs were collected under aseptic conditions, and fixed in 10 % paraformaldehyde. The preparation of pathological sections was delegated to Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, Hubei, China).

Phagocytic activity of RAW 264.7 cells

Following the method of reference [25], the impact of LM@pBLP on the phagocytic activity of RAW264.7 cells was assessed. The trained macrophages were treated overnight, followed by co-culture with GFP-labeled E. coli (green fluorescent strains) for 1 h. Adding a mixture of double antibiotic of gentamicin and ampicillin to eliminated un-phagocytosed bacteria. Subsequently, the nuclei of the cells were stained with DAPI (resulting in blue fluorescence), and phagocytosis in RAW264.7 cells was observed under fluorescence microscopy. Captured images are analyzed using the image-processing software ImageJ.

The phagocytic activity of macrophages is quantified using two parameters: the uptake ratio and the phagocytic index [33,34]. The phagocytic index was calculated according to the following formula: the uptake ratio = (the number of phagocytosing macrophages/total number of counted macrophages) × 100 %. The phagocytic index reflects both the extent and efficiency of phagocytosis, taking into account the number of bacteria engulfed per macrophage and the proportion of macrophages involved. It is calculated using the following formula: phagocytic index = (the number of phagocytosed bacteria/total number of counted macrophages) × (the number of phagocytosing macrophages/total number of counted macrophages.) × 100 %.

Glucose and lactate determination

Lactate and glucose levels in cell supernatants and serum were measured using lactate and glucose test kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) according to Ref. [27].

ROS and NO determination

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using a commercial assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). RAW264.7 cells and positive control samples were incubated with the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) for 20 min. After incubation, the cells were washed, resuspended, and the fluorescence intensity was measured at 525 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer. The total NO concentration in the cell supernatant were determined using a nitrate reductase assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), as previously described by Jia et al. [22]. Absorbance was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader.

Antioxidant enzyme assay

Superoxide dismutase (SOD), malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) were measured using the Antioxidant Assay Kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China), as outlined in Ref. [35].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Group comparisons were conducted using either one-way ANOVA (for single-factor analysis) or two-way ANOVA (to assess the interaction between two independent variables), depending on the experimental design. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was defined as a P-value of less than 0.05 (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001).

Results

Preparation and characterization of LM@pBLP

Characterization was conducted to confirm the successful encapsulation of the lipid membrane. The successful encapsulation of the lipid membrane was first confirmed by TEM images, which showed that the LM@pBLP surface had an extra shell in contrast to the edge of the uncoated pBLP (Fig. 1A). Second, DLS measurements revealed that following lipid membrane encapsulation, pBLP's size grew from 1039 nm to 1500 nm and its zeta potential increased from −10.96 mV to −21.44 mV (Fig. 1B and C). Subsequently, flow cytometry analysis indicated that the fluorescence intensity of FITC-labeled lipid membrane-coated pBLP was significantly higher than that of uncoated pBLP, confirming the presence of the coating on the pBLP surface. Moreover, the encapsulation efficiency reached 99 % (Fig. 1D). Rapid protection against S. aureus by a single intraperitoneal injection of LM@pBLP

Based on previous research on β-glucan-induced trained immunity, we aimed to enhance the efficacy of LM@pBLP by incorporating β-glucan, named LM@pBLP+β. To evaluate the rapid protective ability of LM@pBLP, we intraperitoneally immunized mice with PBS, LM@pBLP, or LM@pBLP+β and subsequently infected them with a lethal dose of S. aureus on day 3 (Fig. 3A). The results indicated that LM@pBLP treatment resulted in a total survival rate of 100 %, in contrast to the infection group or the LM@pBLP+β group (Fig. 3B). Then, we found that mice in the LM@pBLP group experienced slight weight loss, while notable weight gain was observed on day 1 after the challenge (Fig. 3C). LM@pBLP treated mice also exhibited significantly lower severity scores (Fig. 3D). These findings indicate that LM@pBLP elicits a faster and more effective response to S. aureus infection. To assess the potential effects of β-glucan itself, either in terms of toxicity or immune modulation, we compared the effects of β-glucan alone (100 μg) (Fig. S1A). Unlike LM@pBLP and LM@pBLP+β, β-glucan did not provide protection against S. aureus infection (Figs. S1B, C, D). Finally, mice immunized with β-glucan showed bacterial loads similar to those of the infected group after infection. (Fig. S1E and F).

Subsequently, we assessed cytokine-associated gene expression in the spleens of both treated and control mice. These findings indicated that, in the LM@pBLP group, pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) demonstrated a more rapid increase and attained elevated levels (Fig. 3F), signifying a quantifiable, swift, and strong immune response in our model. Additionally, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was also elevated within one day post-infection in both the LM@pBLP and LM@pBLP+β groups of mice, suggesting superior inflammatory balance, which may be the reason for the rapid protection observed (Fig. 3F). The expression of chemokines CCL2 and CCL7 in the spleens of treated mice was increased (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, in the LM@pBLP group, the gene expression encoding IFN-β and TGF-β was higher at 3 days post-immunization. The TLR2 expression levels in the LM@pBLP+β and LM@pBLP groups were similar to the control group (Fig. 3F). The rapid response led us to hypothesize that LM@pBLP may affect the innate immune response through a mechanism known as trained immunity, thereby improving survival.

Trained immunity is associated with epigenetic remodeling and metabolic reprogramming [36]. Following that, we examined the spleens of mice three days after they had been infected with S. aureus for the presence of trained immunity markers. The analysis of metabolic and epigenetic factors related to trained immunity demonstrated elevated transcription levels of HDAC7 and mTORC2 in both groups (P < 0.001). However, the levels of HIF-1α did not show significant changes in the LM@pBLP+β group, remaining close to the control levels (Fig. 3G). Next, we examined whether the increased expression levels of epigenetic molecules three days after LM@pBLP or LM@pBLP+β were accompanied by an increase in glycolysis. In the LM@pBLP group, glucose consumption (P > 0.05) and lactate release (P < 0.001) both increased, indicating the induction of glycolysis (Fig. 3H).

We immunized mice with PBS, LM@pBLP+β, or LM@pBLP and then infected them intraperitoneally with E. coli, C. perfringens, and S. aureus, 7 days post-immunization (Fig. 4A). No mortality was observed in cases of E. coli and S. aureus infections. Compared to the other groups, LM@pBLP provided the most significant improvement in survival time for mice infected with C. perfringens, while all other infected mice succumbed within 4 days (Fig. 4B). In conclusion, LM@pBLP increases inflammatory responses and antibacterial activity by inducing trained immunity in macrophages via glycolysis and epigenetic reprogramming.

The decomposition of glutamine is critical for the induction of trained immunity, which occurs through its conversion to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG) [37]. α-KG plays a key role in multiple metabolic and cellular pathways. Furthermore, α-KG is a cofactor necessary for the hydroxylation required for HIF-1α degradation, facilitating its degradation, which may explain the downregulation of HIF-1α. Additionally, glutamate can produce glutathione, which helps control the redox balance. Glutathione(GSH), an important antioxidant, is synthesized from glutamate (Glu), cysteine (Cys), and glycine (Gly). Through intermediates of the glycolytic pathway (such as 3-PG), GAPDH directly affects the synthesis of serine, thus playing a dual role in cellular metabolism by linking glycolysis to amino acid metabolism. Serine is converted into glycine, which is then involved in the synthesis of GSH (Fig. 9F). KEGG analysis revealed that a large number of genes are enriched in amino acid metabolism pathways, including glycine, serine, and cysteine metabolism (Fig. 9F). Subsequently, we observed that LM@pBLP training markedly upregulated the expression of several key enzymes involved in the GSH synthesis pathway (Fig. 9F). The synthesis of GSH mediated by Gclc is essential for the immune response in trained immunity [38]. By controlling ROS buffering through GSH synthesis, mTOR activity is regulated, which in turn induces immune metabolic changes.

Research indicates that the mechanism of GSH synthesis necessary for gene expression in β-glucan-trained BMDMs entails the recruitment of transcriptional regulators in a manner dependent on H3K27 demethylation [38]. We analyzed the expression of KDM6, a member of the KDM family of histone demethylases that target H3K27me3, and found that KDM6 expression was upregulated (Fig. 9B). This suggests that gene expression after LM@pBLP training involves H3K27 demethylation by transcriptional regulators. To validate the role of epigenetics in LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity, we utilized the histone methylation inhibitor MTA (Fig. 10A). As expected, the expression levels of IL-6, IL-1β and TNF-α in macrophages from the LM@pBLP group were significantly lower upon secondary stimulation when treated with the inhibitor, compared to untreated cells (Fig. 10B).

Our findings suggest that LM@pBLP stimulation activates glutathione and amino acid metabolis (particularly glutamine metabolism) creating a favorable environment for metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming through the mTOR/HIF1-α signaling pathway, thereby promoting the induction of trained immunity.

LM@pBLP demonstrates robust efficacy in a systemic MRSA infection model

Specifically, we evaluated the immunomodulatory and anti-infective efficacy of LM@pBLP in a murine model of MRSA infection, using vancomycin as the standard-of-care comparator. To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of LM@pBLP, mice were intravenously injected with MRSA, followed by administration of PBS, vancomycin, or LM@pBLP 1 h post-infection (Fig. 11A). Discussion

One of the biggest problems facing modern healthcare is the quick spread of multidrug-resistant (MDR) microorganisms. There are few effective preventive or treatment options available for many of these infections due to their widespread medication resistance, underscoring the urgent need for the creation of novel defense mechanisms. The primary advantage of trained immunity lies in its nonspecific protective effects. Therefore, vaccine strategies based on trained immunity, due to their broad-spectrum protection potential, are emerging as a promising approach for developing effective vaccines against bacterial pathogens. In this study, we designed a pathogen peptidoglycan skeleton coated with an artificial biomembrane, LM@pBLP, which has a simple, reliable, environmentally friendly, and fast production process, as well as good biocompatibility, high encapsulation efficiency, and stability. Even in the absence of specific protein antigens, LM@pBLP can still induce rapid, broad-spectrum, and long-lasting protection. Our study showed that LM@pBLP provides enhanced protection in MRSA-infected mice, offering effective early defense, accelerating late-stage recovery, and maintaining its protective effects for up to three weeks. To explore the immune mechanisms of LM@pBLP, we analyzed the function of trained immunity in LM@pBLP -mediated fast protection, demonstrating that LM@pBLP stimulates the trained immunity response via metabolic reprogramming triggered by GSH/ROS, among other elements. This finding highlights the significant potential of trained immunity in preventing co-infections and combating antibiotic resistance associated with multidrug-resistant bacteria.

In this study, we developed a bacterium-like particles (BLPs) based on protein-free artificial biomembrane coating immune activator, named LM@pBLP. By using chemical modification or physical encapsulation techniques, bacterial nanoparticles can be individually encapsulated to enhance their bioavailability and reduce side effects, thereby improving the effectiveness of microbial therapy [39]. Bacterium-like particles (BLPs), derived from lactic acid bacteria, are non-living peptidoglycan (PGN) shells that lack proteins and nucleic acids. These particles retain the native bacterial morphology while eliminating biosafety concerns [19]. Recognized by Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), BLPs have been shown to act as natural immune enhancers by promoting dendritic cell maturation and enhancing immune responses [40]. In our previous studies, we also demonstrated that BLPs can induce trained immunity by activating the TLR2-mediated mTOR/HIF-1α pathway, thereby modulating cytokine release and immune responses. However, BLPs prepared from probiotic strains often induce relatively weak immune activation. This is likely due to their high biocompatibility, which limits effective engagement of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and results in insufficient innate immune stimulation [21]. Pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) are conserved microbial components found in infectious pathogens that activate PRRs on innate immune cells, thereby initiating and amplifying immune responses [41,42]. Notably, pathogenic bacteria such as Listeria monocytogenes [43], Staphylococcus aureus [44], and Streptococcus suis [45] possess PGN structures that differ significantly from those of probiotic strains, resulting in distinct immunostimulatory characteristics. Among these pathogens, Listeria monocytogenes shows particularly strong potential as a source of BLPs. In our previous work, we demonstrated that pBLPs derived from L. monocytogenes markedly increased the expression of TLR1 and TLR2 in macrophages, activated downstream NF-κB signaling, and triggered robust cytokine production [21]. Additionally, pBLPs can induce trained immunity via the mTOR/HIF-1α pathway [22]. Given its structural and functional advantages, pBLPs may more closely mimic pathogen-associated immune activation, offering a promising approach for enhancing the efficacy of immune modulation strategies.

Liposomes, as an important immunomodulatory carrier, were first introduced by Bangham et al., in 1965 [46]. Owing to its myriad advantages, including superior biocompatibility, various loading modalities, substantial loading capacity, simplicity of preparation, and the possibility to attain desired functionalities via surface modification [47,48], it is widely applied. Liposomes are spherical vesicles with a soft lipid bilayer structure, made up of phospholipids and cholesterol. These vesicles have both hydrophilic and hydrophobic regions, which are stabilized by hydrophobic interactions and intermolecular forces. This study utilized anionic lipid DOPA and cholesterol to self-assemble into a lipid membrane on the surface. Research indicates that charged and larger liposomes exhibit enhanced absorption by phagocytic cells, whereas negatively charged liposomes are preferentially internalized by peritoneal macrophages [26]. Liposomes can closely interact with the immune system, triggering the required immune response to achieve specific and efficient immune effects. These characteristics position LM@pBLP as a promising immune activator, opening new avenues for research in immune therapy and infection prevention.

The production process of LM@pBLP is simple, reliable, environmentally friendly, and rapid. The procedure employs interfacial self-assembly technology to create a comprehensive nanocoating on the surfaces of individual bacterial cells. Calcium ions facilitate the rapid formation of a stable surface lipid membrane from a mixture of lipids, calcium ions, and target bacteria within minutes, achieved through simple vortexing in a biologically relevant medium. The hydrophobic layer of this lipid membrane effectively prevents the diffusion of hydrophilic substrates, thereby enhancing the membrane's resistance to environmental damage. This study involved dissolving DOPA and cholesterol in chloroform at a molar ratio of 4:1. The solvent was subsequently eliminated via rotary evaporation, leading to the formation of the lipid membrane. The lipid membranes were subsequently combined with the pBLP solution and underwent self-assembly through vortexing. This method enables the efficient self-assembly of the lipid membrane and pBLP in less than 15 min, employing a rapid and straightforward procedure that can be conducted under mild conditions.

LM@pBLP exhibits good biocompatibility. Lipid membranes were first introduced by Bangham et al., in 1965 [46] and subsequently recognized as carriers with notable biocompatibility and biodegradability [49]. Chowdhuri et al. [50] encapsulated E. coli in liposomes to examine the effects of liposomes on bacterial vitality and survival, further supporting the use of lipid carriers in biological applications. Moreover, pBLP lacks intracellular bacterial components such as proteins, nucleic acids, or phospholipids. During its preparation, all intracellular components are removed, leaving behind only the peptidoglycan (PGN) shell structure of the cell wall, which retains its original shape and size post-processing. This feature further enhances its safety. Previous studies have demonstrated that BLP derived from Lactococcus lactis exhibits excellent biocompatibility [51]. The previous studies conducted by us also demonstrated that pBLP has good biocompatibility in mice [21]. This study further validated the in vitro and in vivo safety of LM@pBLP. The findings indicate that LM@pBLP exhibits adequate biosafety.

LM@pBLP exhibits high encapsulation efficiency and stability. The stability and encapsulation efficiency of the lipid membrane directly affect its performance. Cholesterol increases the stability of the lipid bilayer by intercalating between lipid chains, which inhibits interactions among the lipid chains [52]. Using DLS technology, we measured the size and zeta potential of LM@pBLP. The absolute value change of zeta potential is closely related to the stability of liposomes. The high potential value (absolute value) is the reason for the dynamic stability of these systems [53]. After packaging, the absolute value of the zete potential increases. Experimental results showed that the zeta potential increased after encapsulation, indicating an improvement in stability. Therefore, the stability of the LM@pBLP remained largely unaffected after being stored for three months at either 4 °C or room temperature. Cholesterol plays a dual role by not only stabilizing the lipid membrane but also increasing its ability to encapsulate various compounds [54]. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the pBLP encapsulation rate in the lipid membrane was as high as 99 %, suggesting that this formulation exhibits excellent encapsulation efficiency and may enhance immune responses.

Our results show that LM@pBLP provides faster and more complete protection compared to LM@pBLP+β. In the mouse infection model, we observed that LM@pBLP significantly extended protection against multiple pathogens, with the protective effect lasting up to three weeks. Mice treated with LM@pBLP exhibited complete protection within two days after S. aureus infection, accompanied by enhanced bacterial clearance in the lungs and spleen. The immune response noted two days later may result from the activation of innate immunity, indicated by a marked increase in IL-6 and IL-1β levels. The immunological responses indicate that the host is successfully managing S. aureus infection, with IL-6 and IL-1β serving pivotal functions in this mechanism. Its levels rapidly increase on day 3 post-infection, further confirming the immune system's rapid response to S. aureus. Traditionally, adaptive immune responses involving T and B lymphocytes were thought to be the only source of immunological memory, taking days or weeks to work. In contrast, the innate immune response responds to infections within hours to days without immunological memory in a general manner. However, recent research on trained immunity reveals that epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming can help innate immune cells form long-term immunological memory, which leads to a stronger immune response upon re-stimulation. Given the quick reaction of the innate immune system, we propose that trained immunity may be the mechanism behind the quick protection conferred by LM@pBLP.

A key attribute of trained immunity is its non-specificity, enabling it to confer extensive protection against several infections. The study demonstrates that bacterial infections were effectively controlled in all experimental models, as evidenced by prolonged survival times or significant reductions in bacterial load. BCG-induced trained immunity has demonstrated the ability to reprogram macrophages and monocytes, offering enduring protection against subsequent infections. Furthermore, the study found that after 21 days of initial training, LM@pBLP still provided significant protection, successfully preventing systemic MRSA infection. This result further confirms the broad-spectrum protective ability and long-term effects of LM@pBLP. In summary, LM@pBLP demonstrates strong broad-spectrum protective effects, capable of inducing a rapid protective response against MDR bacteria and providing long-lasting protection.

In our study, we found that LM@pBLP-β induced a weaker trained immunity response compared to LM@pBLP alone. Trained immunity is a form of innate immune memory in which an initial stimulus causes functional changes in immune cells. Although activation returns to baseline after the stimulus is removed, epigenetic reprogramming persists. This enables a heightened transcriptional and functional response upon subsequent exposure to the same or different stimuli. In contrast, innate immune tolerance refers to a state where immune cells fail to activate gene transcription and do not respond functionally to restimulation [55]. These two states represent opposing functional adaptations of innate immunity. However, such tolerance is inconsistent with the significantly improved survival and reduced bacterial burden observed in mice treated with LM@pBLP+β following infection. Although β-glucan is widely recognized as a potent inducer of trained immunity, the LM@pBLP-β formulation in this study did not exhibit enhanced effects; rather, it showed diminished training efficacy compared to LM@pBLP alone. This may be because the selected dose is not the optimal one for producing synergistic effects, which could have led to the reduced efficacy observed. Additionally, the encapsulated form of the compound may have affected its bioavailability and immune recognition, contributing to the diminished response. On one hand, complexation may alter its receptor-binding dynamics; for example, since Dectin-1 and TLR2 recognize distinct components of fungal cell walls [56], Inoue et al. [57] demonstrated that the structural and spatial presentation of microbial ligands can directly influence innate immune responses. On the other hand, immune agonists targeting different pathways do not always yield synergistic responses; rather, they may compete for intracellular signaling machinery or activate opposing regulatory circuits. For example, simultaneous stimulation of Dectin-1 and TLR4 has been shown to suppress inflammatory cytokine production compared to individual stimuli [58]. Similarly, the combination of the nanoemulsion adjuvant Adjuplex with TLR agonists (CpG or GLA) unexpectedly diminished CD8+ T-cell memory formation and impaired protection against Listeria monocytogenes, particularly in the liver [59]. These findings underscore the importance of carefully evaluating potential crosstalk and antagonism when designing combination immunotherapies. While targeting multiple innate pathways holds promise, the complexity of immune signaling networks necessitates thorough investigation into the compatibility and mechanistic interactions of combined components.

In mechanistic studies, we emphasized the key role of trained immunity in the rapid protection induced by LM@pBLP treatment through in vitro, in vivo, and ex vivo experiments. Recently, trained immunity has evolved to elucidate how innate immune cells demonstrate an augmented response to secondary stimuli, a phenomenon facilitated by epigenetic, metabolic, and functional reprogramming instigated by the initial exposure [60]. Prior studies have shown that β-glucan-induced training immunity augments macrophage production of inflammatory cytokines upon subsequent activation [61]. IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α are commonly recognized as key functional cytokine markers of trained immunity. In vitro model, the results showed that after LM@pBLP stimulation, levels of cytokine markers such as IL-6 increased in RAW264.7 cells, before returning to baseline levels. Upon LPS re-stimulation, macrophages markedly elevated the secretion of critical cytokines including IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, IL-12, and TGF-β, indicating an enhanced immune response. These features indicate the presence of immune memory, reflecting the characteristic phenotype of trained immunity. In the ex vivo model, we also found that the LM@pBLP -induced trained immune response promoted the elevation of TNF-α and IL-1β. Cheng et al. reported that human monocytes experience a metabolic transition from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis when acquiring trained immunity [12]. We observed an increase in lactate and a decrease in glucose, although the difference was not statistically significant. In vivo studies further showed that LM@pBLP pretreatment enhanced the mice's defense against S. aureus. LM@pBLP can train the innate immune response, thereby mediating rapid protection. In conclusion, these evidences support the effectiveness of LM@pBLP in inducing trained immunity in mice, promoting bacterial clearance, reducing organ damage, and improving overall survival, highlighting its role in trained immunity induction.

Our data show that LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity improves macrophage phagocytic capability, bacterial clearance in vivo, and survival outcomes. Defense against reinfection relies on macrophages and pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are essential elements of the innate immune system [62]. Cellular training mediated by β-glucan causes myeloid cells to undergo epigenetic reprogramming, which boosts their antibacterial and inflammatory responses. According to previous studies, β-glucan improves the phagocytic activity and cytokine production of mice peritoneal macrophages as well as the innate immune cells' reactivity to infections. Our research shows that LM@pBLP-induced trained immune responses significantly increase the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in macrophages. The increase in these pro-inflammatory factors plays a crucial role in enhancing immune responses and improving bacterial clearance. In addition to pro-inflammatory cytokines, macrophage phagocytosis is a critical defense mechanism against bacterial infections [31]. The activation of macrophages notably enhances bacterial clearance and increases survival rates in infected mice. Pan et al. demonstrated that trained immunity induced by BSNP enhances the ability of macrophages to phagocytize bacteria [31]. Our study also found that macrophages trained with LM@pBLP exhibit higher phagocytic capacity. The enhancement of phagocytosis may be related to the action of lipid membranes. Negatively charged liposomes have been shown to effectively activate the human complement system. Complement receptor 3 (CR3), a key receptor on macrophages, plays a critical role in pathogen uptake, serving as one of the most prominent phagocytic receptors [63]. The data align with the trained immunity phenotype, indicating an enhancement in the antibacterial activity of innate immune cells.

Moreover, LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity is closely associated with reduced ROS levels and increased NO production. After the second stimulation, we observed a reduction in ROS production, which was consistent with the phenotypic changes observed in β-glucan-trained monocytes and Lactobacillus plantarum-induced cells [12,64]. β-glucan induces a pro-inflammatory phenotype in macrophages through an mTOR-dependent mechanism, but does not trigger ROS production [65]. This result aligns with earlier studies indicating that β-glucan possesses antioxidant activity [66,67]. This aligns with our transcriptomic analysis, where we found an upregulation of glutathione metabolism. As an antioxidant, glutathione (GSH) regulates ROS concentrations, protecting macrophages from oxidative stress damage and helping them maintain high phagocytic activity. Additionally, KEGG analysis found that the upregulation of glutamine metabolism promotes the production of α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). α-KG, as a positive regulator of the histone demethylase (KDM) family members KDM6A and KDM6B, plays a key role in cellular pathways. KDM6A and KDM6B regulate inflammation by removing repressive methyl marks from histones and modulating the accessibility of cytokine promoters. GO analysis revealed that LM@pBLP significantly upregulates the positive regulation of IFN-γ production and activates NK cells. IFN-γ and TNF-α play important roles in classical macrophage activation and promoting phagocytosis. Recent study has shown that NK cells exhibit characteristics of adaptive immunity [68]. NK cells, which are essential parts of the innate immune system, are activated during infection and have “memory” characteristics in subsequent infections. These “memory” NK cells undergo secondary amplification, leading to rapid degranulation and cytokine release, which in turn triggers a protective immune response [69]. Recent studies further suggest that NK cells have a self-renewal abilities and can maintain the survival of secondary memory NK cells months after a recall response [70]. These findings emphasize the importance of NK cell memory in immunological responses. The activation of NK cells may be the reason why LM@pBLP is able to prolong the duration of immune memory. In summary, our study presents compelling evidence that trained immunity induced by LM@pBLP boosts the phagocytic capacity of macrophages. The intricate role of macrophages in anti-infective immunity warrants further investigation.

Metabolic reprogramming is a dynamic process that facilitates the reprogramming of immune cell functions, enabling them to respond to pathogen detection and form innate immune memory [71]. Metabolic pathway changes support cell biosynthesis and energy needs and influence immune cell destiny and function, including bone marrow cells [72]. β-glucan and BCG training enhance the shift from metabolism to glycolysis via mTOR stimulation of the HIF-1α pathway [12,73]. In this study, we validated the role of the mTOR pathway and glycolysis. Trained Raw 264.7 cells and murine peritoneal macrophages exhibited increased glucose consumption and elevated lactate production, which was consistent with previous findings where β-glucan stimulation enhanced glycolysis and lactate levels in monocytes [12]. Transcriptomic analysis revealed the upregulation of genes related to glycolysis, and the mTOR pathway was also activated. To further confirm the role of these mechanisms, we used inhibitors of the mTOR pathway and glycolysis. The results showed that inhibition of these signaling pathways in vitro reduced the enhanced responsiveness of trained cells. This further confirmed the close relationship between mTOR and HIF-1α regulation and the enhanced glycolysis induced by LM@pBLP.

In the KEGG analysis, we found that several metabolic pathways were enriched, including those affecting glutamine, glutathione, and amino acid metabolism. Glutamine catabolism and glutathione metabolism are vital for β-glucan-induced immunity. One of the major metabolic alterations seen in macrophages trained with BCG and β-glucan has been confirmed to be an increase in glutamine metabolism. α-KG plays a key role in multiple metabolic pathways: as an intermediate in energy metabolism in the TCA cycle, as a precursor in glutamine formation during amino acid synthesis, and as a cofactor for α-KG-dependent dioxygenases [74]. This change also promotes the generation of more citrate, which is then involved in acetylation reactions. It is believed that ROS generated during metabolism are crucial for trained immunity. Research indicates that metabolic reprogramming during immune responses results in elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [75]. The activation of mTOR is involved in inducing and/or maintaining trained immunity [12], but high concentrations of ROS inhibit mTOR activation [38]. However, studies have shown that after ROS production in β-glucan-trained monocytes and macrophages, the antioxidant glutathione (GSH) is triggered to buffer the rising ROS levels, thereby enhancing mTOR signaling in trained monocytes [38]. By targeting Gclc in monocytes, the antioxidant effect of GSH plays a crucial regulatory role in metabolic and epigenetic changes [38]. Therefore, our study suggests that GSH serves as a major metabolic regulator in LM@pBLP-trained immunity by controlling ROS levels. Other studies have shown that oxidized low-density lipoprotein (OXLDL) can stimulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) production via the mTOR pathway and is significant in enhancing trained immunity [67]. Further investigations are needed to investigate the detailed processes of ROS generation in trained immunity.

In conclusion, our results show that multiple key metabolic pathways are upregulated, which correlates with enhanced trained immunity. The metabolic pathway changes we observed involve not only glycolysis, activation of the mTOR pathway, and glutamine metabolism, but also glutathione metabolism and ROS regulation. These mechanisms collectively promote the enhancement of immune cell functions and the formation of immune memory. Although these results highlight the essential function of metabolic control in in vitro trained immunity, our comprehension of tissue-specific metabolic reprogramming in innate immune cells is still constrained.

Epigenetic reprogramming is the foundation of trained immunity. Cellular metabolism is directly related to epigenetic reprogramming and plays an important regulatory function in the process. Metabolic pathways and signaling molecules are closely linked to the epigenetic modifications that transpire during trained immunity [76]. The KEGG analysis revealed enrichment in multiple amino acid metabolism pathways including glutathione metabolism. The increase in amino acid metabolism, especially the enhancement of glutamine metabolism, helps generate more citrate, which is then used for acetylation reactions. This process is essential for the functional regulation of cells. Glutamine, the most common amino acid in the body, is turned into glutamate and then further processed into α-KG, resulting in an increase in α-KG levels [77]. α-KG is an important metabolic product that can directly regulate the activity of epigenetic enzymes, particularly histone and DNA modification enzymes [78]. Specifically, α-KG influences gene expression regulation by modulating histone demethylases (KDMs), such as KDM6A and KDM6B. These enzymes remove the H3K27me3 repressive mark, thereby promoting gene transcriptional activation [79]. The availability of α-KG affects chromatin remodeling, subsequently influencing cellular fate and differentiation processes [74]. In cells trained with LM@pBLP, we observed upregulation of KDM6, indicating a reduction in H3K27me3 marks. This further supports the role of KDM6 in the epigenetic changes induced by LM@pBLP. Furthermore, the GSH pathway is the most significant non-enzymatic antioxidant mechanism in mammals. GSH itself is considered a critical component in controlling cell destiny [80]. Beyond its role in regulating gene expression via GSH-modulated transcription factors, there is growing evidence indicating that GSH significantly contributes to epigenetic regulation [81]. HAibo Su et al. [38] demonstrated that H3K27me3 demethylation is a new histone modification mark, and in β-glucan-trained BMDMs, GSH deficiency leads to damage. In specific chromatin regions, GSH promotes the removal of the H3K27me3 repressive mark through redox signaling, a process considered one of the key mechanisms in trained immunity [38]. To further confirm the role of histone methylation processes in trained immunity, we used MTA inhibitors. The results indicated that inhibiting histone methylation in vitro reduced the enhanced responsiveness of trained cells. This result further confirms that LM@pBLP-induced trained immunity is closely associated with histone methylation. Despite significant findings, further research into other potential epigenetic changes is still necessary, and their specific roles and mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

This study is limited by the inability to evaluate the rapid protective effect of LM@pBLP in various multidrug-resistant bacterial infection models, owing to the absence of additional MDR bacterial strains. Furthermore, the efficacy of LM@pBLP should be tested in various bacterial infection models. In future experiments, our goal is to determine whether there exists an optimal dose required to achieve synergistic immune modulation. Future studies should delve deeper into the mechanisms of trained immunity, including dynamic monitoring of the temporal expression patterns of cytokines,a systematic analysis of the innate immune cell populations involved, the specific ligand–receptor interactions and molecular pathways through which LM@pBLP induces trained immunity, as well as the generation and long-term maintenance of immune memory. Furthermore, the role of lipid metabolism in trained immunity warrants additional exploration, particularly whether the addition of exogenous cholesterol changes cholesterol metabolism and causes its downregulation. Future studies should concentrate on the mechanisms of trained immunity, as this focus will yield significant insights for enhancing vaccine design. Moreover, its potential synergistic effects when combined with other immunotherapeutic strategies warrant further study.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we describe a novel immune activator with the potential to reduce the use of antimicrobial agents and demonstrate promise in preventing MDR pathogen infections. LM@pBLP targets multiple pathogens through trained immunity, inducing rapid, robust, and broad protection against MDR infections. Transcriptomic analysis indicated that LM@pBLP stimulation enhanced glutathione and amino acid metabolism, with a specific focus on glutamine metabolism. This stimulation fostered an environment favorable for metabolic and epigenetic reprogramming through the mTOR signaling pathway, thereby facilitating the induction of trained immunity. Moreover, LM@pBLP exerts antioxidant effects by activating glutathione metabolism and regulating ROS levels. Owing to its remarkable capacity to activate trained immunity and enhance host defense mechanisms, LM@pBLP demonstrates strong potential for clinical translation and may serve as a novel vaccine candidate or an effective immunostimulatory adjuvant. Our study highlights the importance and potential of designing vaccines targeting MDR infections through trained immunity.
