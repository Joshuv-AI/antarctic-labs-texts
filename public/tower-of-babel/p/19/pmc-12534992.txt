Plasma and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers in aged dogs with cognitive decline
Authors: Abdullatif Alsulami, Sean W Boland, Stephanie McGrath, Evan L MacLean, Caitlin S Latimer, Martin Darvas, Julie A Moreno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCanine cognitive dysfunction syndrome (CCD) is a naturally occurring progressive neurodegenerative disease that commonly affects geriatric dogs, with age being the primary risk factor. CCD presents a valuable model for studying aging and neurodegeneration due to natural development of the disease and similarities to Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In this study, we evaluated biomarkers that are relevant for human neurodegeneration, including neurofilament light chain (NfL), glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), amyloid-beta and the amyloid-beta (Aβ1−42/1−40) ratio, to explore the differences between healthy aging and CCD.ResultsOur results demonstrate significant associations between age and dementia biomarkers, with reduced Aβ1−42/1−40 ratios in plasma, and elevated NfL levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) at older ages. These biomarkers were also associated cognitive impairment, as assessed by owner-directed CCD surveys. Notably, NfL levels in plasma showed a strong positive correlation with both age and cognitive decline, suggesting its potential utility as a non-invasive diagnostic tool for CCD. While plasma NfL is promising, it is non-specific and can also be elevated due to other neurological conditions. Therefore, combining NfL with other biomarkers, such as GFAP and Aβ, alongside clinical assessments, may enable a more accurate diagnosis of CCD.ConclusionOur findings further support the use of dogs with CCD as a model for studying AD biomarkers, with implications for the development of therapeutic interventions in both dogs and humans.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-025-05027-w.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, Canine cognitive dysfunction, Neurofilament light chain, Glial fibrillary acidic protein, Beta-amyloid, Plasma, Cerebrospinal fluid

Background

Aged dogs are known to demonstrate clinical signs of cognitive decline as part of a syndrome called canine cognitive dysfunction (CCD), which shares remarkable similarities with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [1, 2]. The number of companion dogs over the age of 10 years has grown by 15% in the past 20 years, according to the American Veterinary Medical Foundation. With the steadily increasing canine population, continued research into cognitive decline in aging dogs is critical, particularly given the implications and translational value for human aging and neurodegeneration. The prevalence of CCD is estimated to range from 14 to 35% of the aged canine population, with the incidence increasing exponentially with age [3–5]. In a study of 180 geriatric dogs (aged 11–16 years), 28% of dogs 11–12 years old and 68% of dogs 15–16 years old were found to exhibit signs of cognitive impairment [6]. In a two-year longitudinal study of dogs over the age of 8 years, 33% of dogs with normal cognitive status progressed to mild cognitive impairment, and 22% of dogs with mild impairment progressed to CCD [5].

Pathologically, CCD is characterized by early inflammatory glial phenotypes, loss of synaptic proteins, and the accumulation of misfolded proteins, specifically amyloid-beta (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau (P-tau), in the brains of affected dogs [3, 5, 7–9]. Aβ deposits have been observed in aging dog brains, with the amyloid load often correlating with CCD signs [10, 11]. P-tau protein has also been detected in the aging dog brain, though with less frequency and density than in human AD brains [3, 12]. Given the shared pathological characteristics, CCD has emerged as a naturally occurring surrogate for the development of diagnostic tools and therapeutic interventions for human AD [13], offering a valuable model due to its analogous histopathological, behavioral, and cognitive alterations. In human Alzheimer’s disease and related tauopathies, phosphorylation at specific residues on tau is an early and consistent feature. Antibodies that recognize phosphoserine 396 (pS396) preferentially detect hyperphosphorylated tau aggregates from AD brains. Tau phosphorylated at threonine 217 and 231 (pT217 and p-T231) are early accumulating tau species in AD, accurately predicting amyloid pathology and increasing with disease progression. These phosphorylationspecific antibodies (pS396, pT217 and pT231) therefore serve as common biomarkers for AD and related diseases and are being explored for diagnosis and therapy.

In humans, biomarkers for the diagnosis of AD have been extensively studied [14–16]. These include Aβ, P-tau, neurofilament light chain (NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), which are measurable in both blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Aβ reflects amyloid plaque pathology, P-tau indicates tau tangle formation, NfL marks axonal degeneration, and GFAP represents astrocyte activation and neuroinflammation. Our data show that we can successfully detect Aβ, GFAP, and NfL in plasma and CSF derived from young and aged dogs and that these biomarkers correlate with neuropathology, suggesting their utility in diagnosing CCD, similar to AD. Studies in humans have demonstrated the feasibility of detecting P-tau and Aβ in blood [17–19] and CSF [20–22]; for the diagnosis of AD, though these approaches have not yet reached routine clinical use. Here, we examined Aβ, NfL, and GFAP in plasma and CSF samples from dogs with and without symptoms of cognitive impairment, to assess the utility of these biomarkers in the context of CCD. Identifying known AD biomarkers in dogs and correlating these measures with cognitive impairment and pathology has the potential to further validate the dog as a model for AD research, advancing preclinical diagnostic and therapeutic trials using a large animal model with naturally occurring dementia.

CADES and CCDR owner surveys

The primary cognitive functional outcomes are based on two owner-directed surveys, the Canine Dementia Scale (CADES) and the Canine Cognitive Dysfunction Rating scale (CCDR) [1, 2]. CADES is unique from CCDR in its ability to differentiate clinical stages of dementia (mild, moderate, or severe cognitive impairment).

Collection of CSF and plasma

Once enrolled the dogs underwent anesthesia for the collection of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Anesthesia was approved and performed by the CSU veterinary teaching hospital anesthesiology team. Anesthesia was determined by the anesthesiologist for each individual patient but commonly used delivery of methadone (0.1-0.5 mg/kg) or butorphanol (0.1-0.5 mg/kg) SQ/IM (for healthy energetic animals) the addition of acepromazine 0.01–0.02 mg/kg SQ/IM or dexmedetomidine 3–5 mcg/kg IM may have been utilized. Whole blood was collected, placed into EDTA blood collection tubes and centrifuged to separate plasma. Samples were stored at −80 C until used for experimentation.

Tissue collection and immunohistochemistry

Participating dogs with owner consent of necropsy undergo extraction of the brain which are collected and cut in half sagittally, and half was placed immediately into 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) 72–96 h prior to trimming, processing and embedding. The other half of the brains were cut in half and snap frozen for further protein analysis. Tissues were processed using a Leica TP1020 Automatic Benchtop Tissue Processor and embedded in paraffin wax (Cancer Diagnostics, Cat #: EEPAR56). Tissues were sectioned on a Thermo Scientific HM 325-2 Manual Microtome at 5 μm thickness and mounted on positively charged glass slides (Superfrost Plus, Cancer 232 Diagnostics, Cat #: 4951) for staining and analysis. To determine histological changes immunohistochemical stains were performed on whole brain sagittal sections. To deparaffinize the tissue sections, slides were heated for 20 min at 60 °C followed by a series of incubations in xylene and graded ethanol (xylene, 1 part xylene to 1 part 100% EtOH, 100% EtOH, 95% EtOH, 70% EtOH, 1.0 M TBS) for 5 min each. Heat- and chemical-induced antigen retrieval performed on the tissue by incubating in 0.01 M sodium citrate (pH = 6.0) for 20 min at 95 °C. Performed removal of endoperoxides through incubation in 0.3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min at room temperature. Wash steps and tissue permeabilization were performed using 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 2% Triton-X in 1.0 M TBS. Tissues were blocked in 10% goat or donkey serum diluted in 1.0 M TBS for 1 h at room temperature. After being diluted to their optimized concentrations, primary antibodies were incubated on the tissue at 4 °C overnight. Astrocytes were identified using a rabbit anti-S100 calcium-binding protein β (S100β) antibody at a 1:750 concentration (Abcam, Cat #: ab41548). Microglia were identified using a goat anti-ionized calcium binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1) antibody at a 1:400 concentration (Abcam, Cat #: ab5076). Detected phosphorylation of tau using a rabbit anti-phospho-Tau (T217) (1:100; Abclonal, Cat #: AP1233) and rabbit anti-phospho-Tau (T231) (1:800; Abclonal, Cat #: AP0053) antibody. Slides were then washed using 2% BSA in 1.0 M TBS before incubation with the secondary antibody at a 1:250 concentration for 1 h at room temperature. The following secondary antibodies were used for the astrocyte and microglial stains, respectively: goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Vector Labs, Cat #: BA-1000) and donkey anti-goat secondary antibody (Jackson ImmunoResearch, Cat #: 705-065-147). Following secondary incubation, tissues were incubated an additional hour in ABC mix from an ABC HRP peroxidase detection kit (Vector Laboratories, Cat #: PK-4000). An ImmPACT DAB Substrate, Peroxidase (HRP) Kit (Vector Laboratories, Cat #: sk-4105) was used as chromogen, with a fixed chemical reaction period for each antigen of interest. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat #: 7231) and bluing reagent (Cancer Diagnostics, Cat #: FX2107). Stained tissue was preserved under a glass coverslip with mounting medium (Richard-Allen Scientific, Cat #: 4112) and stored at room temperature. Whole tissue images were taken for analysis using an Olympus BX53 microscope with an Olympus DP70 camera using an Olympus UPlanSApo 20x objective (N.A. = 0.75). Representative images were taken using an Olympus BX53 microscope with an Olympus DP70 camera using an Olympus UPlanSApo 20x objective (N.A. = 0.75) and Olympus UPlaNFL N 40x objective (N.A. = 0.75).

Meso Scale Discovery (MSD) method

Frozen dog cortex samples were homogenized in chilled RIPA buffer (1% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 150mM sodium chloride, 50mM Tris hydrochloride, 0.5mM magnesium sulfate, Complete Mini protease inhibitor and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail- all from Sigma-Aldrich; St. Louis, MO) by sonication on ice (3 × 10 pulses – separated by 5 min). Homogenates were centrifuged for 45 min at 21,000 × g and 4 °C. Supernatants containing RIPA-soluble proteins were aliquoted and transferred into a new storage tube. The remaining pellet was washed with RIPA buffer and centrifuged a second time 45 min at 21,000 × g and 4 °C. Supernatants from the RIPA wash were combined with the first RIPA fraction, aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. The remaining pellet was resuspended in in chilled 5 M guanidine hydrochloride (Gu-HCl) with Complete Mini protease inhibitor, sonicated on ice (1 × 10 pulses), and centrifuged for 30 min at 21,000 × g and 4 °C. The supernatant (Gu-HCl faction) was aliquoted, transferred into a new storage tube and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. Total protein content for RIPA and Gu-HCl fractions was determined using BCA assay kits (Pierce; Rockford, IL). For quantification of beta-amyloid, we used the MSD Aβ Peptide Panel kit (V-PLEX K15200G, Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD), which is a multiplex kit for quantification of Aβ40 and Aβ42. We used 200 ng protein from the Gu-HCl fraction in duplicate for each sample and followed the manufacturer’s instructions for performing the assays. Absolute concentrations for Aβ40 and Aβ42 were calculated using standards from the kit. For quantification of P-tau, we coated MSD bare plates (MSD #L15XA-3 Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD) with 40 ng per well of a monoclonal antibody for pSer396-tau (Clone PHF-13, BioLegend #829003, San Diego, CA) over night at 4 °C, blocked unspecific binding sites with 5% bovine-serum albumin for 1 h, incubated 2,000 ng protein from the RIPA fraction in duplicate for each sample for 1 h, and detected pSer396-bound P-tau antigens with 50 ng per well of biotinylated total-tau antibodies (Clone TAU-12, BioLegend #806506, San Diego, CA) for 1 h, and incubated antibody-antigen sandwiches with sulfo-tag streptavidin compound (Meso Scale Diagnostics, Rockville, MD) at a 1:1 molar ratio relative to biotinylated antibodies. Concentrations of P-tau were recorded as arbitrary units. All data are presented as mean +/- SEM.

Neurology 4-Plex E Assay (N4PE)

250 µl of plasma and 50 µl of CSF were aliquoted into conical tubes and packaged for shipping to Quanterix Corporation in Billerica, MA 01821. Sequence comparisons show that canine neurofilament light (NfL) protein is 98.35% identical to the human NfL sequence, and canine GFAP isoforms exhibit high homology with their human counterparts ranging from 92 to 94%. supporting the use of these human assays in dogs. The amyloidβ precursor protein (APP) is also highly conserved; the longest canine isoform (APP-770) shares 969% identity with human APP and the Aβ peptide sequence itself is identical in dogs and humans. Data is presented as mean +/- SEM. Differences between CCD negative and positive groups were analyzed by a t-test. Statistical analysis was completed using Graphpad Prism. Significance is denoted throughout the manuscript as * = p ≤ 0.05, ** = p ≤ 0.01, and *** = p ≤ 0.001.

Statistical analysis

This study analyzed baseline associations between dog age, symptoms of dementia (CADES, CCDR) and concentrations of NfL, GFAP, and Ab (in CSF and plasma. Dogs were categorized based on their CADES scores and age: young (< 8 years old), cognitively impaired (CCD+; CADES score > 8) and CCD- (CADES score < 8; >8 years old). Prior to conducting group comparisons, normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and Levene’s test was performed to check for homogeneity of variance. For group comparisons, two-sample t-tests were used where assumptions of normality and homogeneity were met. One-way ANOVA was used to compare more than two groups.

Pearson’s correlation was computed to assess the association between CADES and CCDR scores, and dog age and biomarker concentrations (NfL, GFAP, and Ab). In addition, multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the independent effect of independent variables (age and NfL levels in the plasma) on NfL levels in the CSF. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2), with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Results

To investigate and confirm that the pathological features correspond to cognitive impairment in CCD+ (positive) and CCD- (negative) dogs, canine brain samples were collected at end of life, fixed paraffin embedded and analyzed. Immunohistochemical analysis of neurodegeneration hallmarks in the hippocampus and frontal cortex is shown in Fig. 1. Even though there was no statistically significant difference between Aged non-demented (CCD-) and aged demented (CCD+) dogs (Supplementary Fig. 1), we did see a higher amyloid-beta burden in CCD + dogs. Furthermore, tau phosphorylation, detected by T217 and T231 antibodies, showed increased phosphorylation in the cells of the hippocampus associated with CCD + dogs, pointing to heightened tau pathology in these regions. These findings demonstrate the utility of owner-report measures, such as CADES, for identifying dogs with the aggregation of toxic proteins in the brain. We next measured Aβ1−42 protein levels in frontal cortex of brain homogenates using MSD assay (Fig. 2A). We found increased Aβ1−42 levels in the cortex of aged dogs. The observed elevation of Aβ1−42 aligns with the known age-related beta-amyloid elevation in CCD [23], and AD. In contrast, phosphorylation of tau at serine 396 (S396) site did not differently significantly between younger and aged dogs (Fig. 2B). In our cohorts of healthy young, aged and aged dogs with cognitive dysfunction, we measured concentrations of NfL, GFAP, Aβ1−40, and Aβ1−42 in both the CSF and plasma (Figs. 3 and 4) to investigate potential differences between dogs with and without cognitive impairment. NfL concentrations were elevated in CSF and plasma of aged and aged demnted dogs compared to the younger cohort. While the difference in CSF NfL was not statistically significant between aged and CCD + dogs, plasma concentrations were significantly higher in CCD + dogs (Fig. 3), suggesting that plasma NfL may serve as a marker of cognitive impairment. Similarly, GFAP concentrations, a known biomarker for neurodegeneration, were tending to increase in both CSF and plasma of CCD + dogs, though statistical significance was not reached.

Aβ concentrations in CSF and plasma have been proposed as important biomarkers for neurodegenerative diseases, and specifically AD [24]. However, the ability to establish differences between cognitively impaired dogs’ protein ratios remains complex [25, 26]. We measured concentrations of both isoforms of Aβ, Aβ1−40 and Aβ1−42, in CSF and plasma samples. Aβ1−40 and Aβ1−42 are two isoforms of amyloid-beta peptides, and their concentrations can provide critical insights into amyloid pathology. Aβ1−42 is more prone to aggregation and is a key component of amyloid plaques, while Aβ1−40 is more abundant, it is less likely to aggregate [27]. Even though Aβ1−40 and Aβ1−42 biomarkers did not show significant differences between CCD + and CCD- dogs individually, the ratios are shown to be a more sensitive marker of amyloid burden. The ratio of Aβ1−42 to Aβ1−40 is used to assess amyloid burden, with a lower ratio indicating increased plaque formation, as seen in AD and other cognitive diseases, like CCD. The ratio of Aβ1−42/1−40 was significantly lower in CCD + dogs in the CSF, indicative of a more severe condition (Fig. 4). This finding is consistent with the accumulation of amyloid plaques, indicating a more advanced pathological state in these animals. In plasma, however, the ratio was not significantly different, highlighting the challenges of using peripheral biomarkers for diagnosing cognitive dysfunction.

To assess the potential of NfL, GFAP, and Aβ isoforms as biomarkers for CCD, we conducted a series of correlation analyses. Biomarker concentrations were measured in both plasma and CSF, and their relationships with several critical clinical parameters were evaluated, including the age of the dogs, their CADES and CCDR scores. By examining these correlations, we aimed to determine how well each biomarker reflects the progression of neurodegenerative changes, cognitive decline, and the overall severity of CCD.

GFAP levels in the CSF showed a significant positive correlation with age (p = 0.006, r = 0.55, Fig. 5), reflecting age-related neuroinflammation. However, neither CADES nor CCDR scores were significantly correlated with GFAP levels in the CSF, suggesting that GFAP may not directly capture cognitive impairment in CCD based on our sample size. Similarly, plasma GFAP levels, while elevated in CCD + dogs compared to aged and young dogs, did not show a significant correlation with age or dementia scores. This suggests that plasma GFAP may be less sensitive than CSF GFAP for detecting neuroinflammation. However, further studies with larger sample sizes could help clarify its potential as a non-invasive biomarker.

Next, we examined the Aβ1−42/Aβ1−40 ratio. In both the CSF and plasma. The plasma ratio was significantly negatively correlated with age (p = 0.025, r = −0.47, Fig. 6). This finding reflects a worsening Aβ ratio with aging, consistent with increasing amyloid pathology. The CSF Aβ1−42/Aβ1−40 ratio was also significantly negatively correlated with CADES scores (p = 0.011, r = −0.52), indicating that lower ratios in the CSF are associated with more severe cognitive impairment. Interestingly, this correlation was not observed using plasma measurements, suggesting that while plasma amyloid measurements reflect systemic amyloid dynamics, the CSF ratio may serve as a more sensitive marker for cognitive decline in CCD. NfL concentrations in CSF and plasma were significantly positively correlated with age (p = < 0.001, < 0.001; r = 0.71, 0.73, respectively), reinforcing NfL’s potential as a biomarker for aging and cognitive decline in dogs. Notably, CSF and plasma NfL concentrations showed a strong positive correlation with CADES scores (p = < 0.001, < 0.001; r = 0.48, 0.76, respectively, Fig. 7), suggesting that higher peripheral NfL concentrations are associated with greater cognitive impairment in both the CSF and plasma. Importantly, we observed a significant positive correlation between NfL concentrations in plasma and CSF (p = 0.016, r = 0.47, Fig. 7), suggesting that plasma NfL levels can guide central nervous system pathology. Expanding on this finding, we ran a multiple regression analysis to determine the role of age in influencing the association between NfL values in the CSF and the plasma. After including age in the model, the association between plasma and CSF NfL was positive but did not reach statistical significance (Supplement Table 3 and Table 3).

Discussion

Aging is the most significant risk factor for neurodegenerative diseases in humans, such as AD [28, 29]. While, traditional laboratory modelsoften fail to capture the complexity of natural aging, dogsprovide an excellent model for studying cognitive decline and neurodegeneration which allows for efficient study of disease progression and the effects of aging on the brain [30]. CCD typically manifests in dogs over the age of 10 and shares many pathological features with AD, including amyloid angiopathy and increased amyloid burden [3, 4]. The severity of CCD increases with age, much like AD in humans [6]. The median lifespan of beagles, a commonly studied breed, is approximately 13.9 years [31], with age ranges in dogs corresponding to human age groups: < 5 years in dogs correlates to < 40 years in humans, 5–9 years to 40–60 years, and > 9 years in dogs to over 60 years in humans [32, 33]. The condensed lifespan in dogs makes it feasible to study brain aging.

The current diagnosis of CCD is primarily based on owner-directed surveys and the exclusion of other possible neurological or systemic conditions. Developing a diagnostic test that can be performed through a simple blood draw during routine veterinary visits would be highly beneficial for both owners and dogs. Based on previous research in both dogs and humans, we hypothesized that biomarkers such as NfL, GFAP, Aβ isoforms and P-tau could be correlated with behavioral and pathological markers of cognitive decline in aging dogs.

Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that CCD + dogs had a higher amyloid burden in the frontal cortex compared to non-dented dogs. Biochemical assays further demonstrated age related increase of Aβ1−42 in the frontal cortex in aged dogs (Fig. 2). These results align with previous studies on dogs with CCD [11, 23], which also observed increased amyloid pathology using similar biochemical techniques. Notably, no significant changes were detected in tau phosphorylation at the S396 site, which could be attributed to the sensitivity of the assay used in dogs or the specific brain region analyzed.

Among the biomarkers measured, plasma NfL emerged as a promising candidate. It was significantly correlated with age, cognitive impairment (as measured by CADES and CCDR scores) and CSF NfL levels.In addition, the positive association between plasma and CSF NfL levels suggests that peripheral measurements may reflect central pathology. However, when age was included in a multiple regression model, the relationship between plasma and CSF NfL lost statistical significance, indicating that age may confound this association. These findings underscore the importance of considering age as a covariate in biomarker analyses. These findings align with established data from human studies of AD and other dementias, where biomarkers like NfL have been shown to be an early indicator of cognitive decline, mild cognitive impairment [34], AD [35], and frontotemporal dementia [36]. Aging is a strong prognostic factor for cognitive impairment in both humans [37] and dogs [38]. In this study, we showed that age was significantly associated with several dementia biomarkers, NfL, GFAP and Aβ1−42/1−40 ratio.

Although NfL is a promising biomarker for cognitive decline and neurodegeneration in humans, it is a non-specific neuronal protein that can be elevated due to various brain injuries or diseases. Increased plasma NfL levels have been documented in canine meningoencephalitis [39], epilepsy [40], hypoxic injury [41], and even normal aging in dogs [42]. Therefore, it is essential to pair NfL changes with other biomarkers and clinical assessments for accurate CCD diagnosis. In our study, we also assessed the Aβ1−42/Aβ1−40 ratio and GFAP levels. Aβ1−42/Aβ1−40, known to decrease in human patients with AD and other dementias, decreased in both plasma and CSF of aged dogs with CCD. However, it was not statistically significant in plasma (Fig. 4). In addition, GFAP, a protein abundant in astrocytes, has been identified in plasma from human patients with early and late-stage AD [43, 44]. In our study, we observed a slight but not significant increase in GFAP levels in CSF and plasma samples from CCD + dogs (Fig. 3), further highlighting the role of glial activation in CCD pathology [45, 46].

It is important to note that our study is based on association analyses rather than mechanistic investigations. The findings indicate that a panel of biomarkers, rather than a single marker, are necessary to accurately reflect the complex neurodegenerative processes underlying CCD pathology. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these biomarkers and to further elucidate the biological changes in CCD.

Overall, these results prove the translational value of the canine model for investigating neurodegeneration. Through establishing associations between peripheral and central biomarkers and cognitive decline, our study lays the groundwork for the development of minimally invasive diagnostic tools that may eventually inform both veterinary practice and human AD research.

Conclusion

Diagnosing CCD is crucial for several reasons, including effective management for dog owners and clinicians. Importantly, the proper diagnosis of CCD will allow researchers to understand disease progression related to cognitive decline and behavioral changes and develop therapeutics to intervene earlier in disease, increasing the healthspan of these animals. Also, due to dogs aging naturally and spontaneously acquiring neurodegenerative diseases similar to AD and AD related dementias, a set of diagnostic tools, including NfL, GFAP, Ab1−42/1−40 biomarkers paired with clinical, behavioral, and pathological changes will allow researchers to better understand the progression of disease in a model with a shortened lifespan, compared with humans.

Our findings demonstrate that biomarkers such as NfL, GFAP, and Aβ1−42/Aβ1−40 ratio correlate with pathological changes and cognitive impairment in aging dogs, aligning with similar observations in human neurodegenerative diseases like AD. While NfL shows promise as a diagnostic tool, its non-specificity underscores the importance of combining it with additional biomarkers and clinical assessment tools for accurate CCD diagnosis.

These results provide critical insights into the pathophysiology of CCD, and offering a framework for developing early diagnostic tools and therapeutic interventions. Leveraging biomarkers in naturally aging canine models not only benefits canine health but also enhances translational research, bridging the gap between preclinical studies and human clinical applications. By refining diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, we can improve the health of aging dogs and gain valuable insights applicable to human aging and neurodegenerative diseases.
