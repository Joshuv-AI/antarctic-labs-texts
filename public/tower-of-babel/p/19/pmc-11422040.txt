Influential reinforcement parameters, elemental mapping, topological analysis and mechanical performance of lignocellulosic date palm/epoxy composites for improved sustainable materials
Authors: Faris M. AL-Oqla, Mohammed T. Hayajneh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The value of biomaterials for green products has begun to develop more ecofriendly and renewable sustainable materials for a better circular economy and to reduce carbon footprints. This work presents integrated investigations of the lignocellulosic date palm/epoxy composites at various reinforcement condition parameters for sustainable structural materials where elemental mapping, topological analysis, and mechanical performance have been performed. Mapping energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy was utilized to assess the composite composition properly. Elemental mapping and a scanning electron microscope were employed to evaluate the chemical composition of the composites. The mechanical performance of the produced composites was also explored in terms of ultimate tensile strength, tensile modulus, elongation at break, and impact energy properties. The effects of fiber loading, fiber length, and fiber width (as long fiber, short fiber, and long-thin fiber) were investigated for the date palm fiber/epoxy composites. Results have revealed that the composite behavior was affected by several influential reinforcement parameters. The energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy maps by C–K, O–K, Si–K, K-K, and Ca-K demonstrated that the composites contain mainly carbon, silicon, and oxygen. It was evident that the modulus of elasticity property of short fiber composites exhibits an increasing trend with higher fiber content, even at 35 wt%. Moreover, the enhancement of tensile strength for the short fiber size composites reached 72.5 %. However, such tensile strength of thin fiber size/epoxy composites achieved 135.7 % at 25 wt% indicating superior development of this mechanical property. The long date palm fiber composites demonstrated the best value of modulus of elasticity and the maximum impact energy of 15.3 kJ/m2 attained at 25 wt%, which is about 112.5 % enhancement. Scan electron microscope was capable of confirming that broken fibers were not separated from the matrix indicating the good adhesion between the fiber and the matrix that supports their good mechanical performance.

Introduction

Lignocellulosic composite materials are considered one of the sustainable and eco-friendly material solutions that have gained significant attention in various industries due to their unique combination of properties and sustainability advantages [1,2]. Such materials utilize available resources and wastes to produce materials with desired mechanical properties for various industries, including aerospace, household, automotive, power generation, pharmaceutical, and electronic applications [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. These composites are made from natural fibers such as plant-based materials embedded in a matrix, providing a lightweight, renewable, and environmentally friendly alternative to traditional synthetic composites [[7], [8], [9]]. Natural fiber composites are materials made by combining natural fibers such as plant, animal, or even mineral fibers with a matrix material such as resin or plastic. This results in a strong, environmentally friendly alternative to traditional composite materials, which often use synthetic fibers. One of the key benefits of natural fiber composites is their eco-friendly nature. These composites are biodegradable, meaning they can break down naturally over time, reducing environmental impact. In addition, they are recyclable, promoting a circular economy and minimizing waste [[10], [11], [12]].

In the construction world, natural fiber composites are building a solid foundation. From decking and cladding to roofing materials, these composites provide durability and design flexibility while being environmentally friendly [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Considering the environmental impact, natural fiber composites have a lower carbon footprint compared to traditional composites made from non-renewable resources [[17], [18], [19], [20]]. By using natural fibers sourced from plants like sisal, palm, hemp, jute, or flax, these composites help reduce greenhouse gas emissions and promote sustainability [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. Reinforcing natural fibers with polymer-based matrices can improve subsequent mechanical performance as fibers can help withstand higher stress in the matrix. The final performance of the natural fiber-reinforced polymer composites can be affected by several factors, including fiber type, fiber content and orientation, fiber size, and compatibility with the matrix [[25], [26], [27], [28]]. Proper adhesion of fibers with polymers can enhance stress transfer efficiency inside the composite resulting in the desired performance for structural applications. However, natural fibers absorb moisture and swell when exposed to high humidity or water. This can affect the durability and dimensional stability of the composite, requiring protective coatings or treatments to overcome [[29], [30], [31], [32]]. Additionally, achieving a strong bond between the natural fibers and the matrix can be challenging, negatively impacting the overall performance and longevity of the composite. Fiber length and size can dramatically affect this bond between the composite components. However, selecting appropriate natural fibers for composite samples is a crucial step in preparing for tensile testing. When selecting natural fibers, factors such as tensile strength, elastic modulus, and elongation at break must be taken into account to ensure that the composite material meets the desired mechanical properties [26,[33], [34], [35], [36]].

Date palms, on the other hand, are among the oldest cultivated plants and are available worldwide [37]. These trees are found in many countries, especially in the Arabian Peninsula, and have been an integral part of people's lives for centuries. Throughout history, date palm trees have not only provided food in the form of delicious dates, but also materials for shelter, tools, and even medicine [37,41,42]. The availability of these trees has made them a symbol of resilience and sustainability in arid regions and makes plenty of waste that can be utilized in natural fiber composites due to the desired properties of their fibers.

Therefore, this work aims to investigate the most influential structural integrity parameters that affect the mechanical performance of date palm fiber/epoxy resin composites. The study considered different reinforcement conditions with different arrangements of fiber content, fiber size, and geometry to promote the development of better bio-based materials for sustainable eco-friendly products. Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS/EDX) and scanning electron microscope (SEM) were used to evaluate the chemical composition of the composites through elemental mapping analysis. Various mechanical performances and fracture behaviors were studied to demonstrate the effect of reinforcement parameters on the overall properties of the composite, including tensile strength and modulus, impact energy, and elongation at break properties. Furthermore, a microscopic analysis was performed to reveal the fiber topology and interfacial bonding of the composite to study the effects of reinforcing conditions and justify the overall mechanical performance that would improve the reliability of such composites for sustainable design opportunities.

Materials and methods

Materials

In this study, epoxy was used as the matrix of the composite material and date palm fibers (DPF) as reinforcement to create composites with different reinforcement conditions. Epoxy was purchased from Turkey. The density of the resin was 1.22 g/ml. The viscosity of the resin was 800 mPa s at 20 Co, whereas the hardener density and viscosity at room temperature were 0.96 g/ml and 400 mPa s. Curing time at room temperature is about 24 h. The epoxy resin and hardener were mixed in a 1:1 resin-to-hardener ratio. The date palm leaf fibers shown in Fig. 1 were obtained from date trees in Jordan. They have been washed with distilled water and then air-dried to remove unwanted dust and moisture. Date palm fibers were then cut and chopped into three categories of fiber sizes; Long fibers with a length of 60.0 ± 1.3 mm and 12.0 ± 0.6 mm width, short fibers with a length of 20.0 ± 1.8 mm and 12.0 ± 0.6 mm width, and finally long with very thin fibers 60.0 ± 1.8 mm long and 3.0 ± 3.7 mm width. The fiber density of date palm fiber has been measured to be 0.9 g/cm3. Hand layup and mixing procedures were utilized to produce the different date palm-epoxy composites. Fibers were mixed with the epoxy at different reinforcement conditions of fiber loading (15 wt%, 25 wt%, and 35 wt%), fiber size, and geometry in a silicon mold to produce specimens for mechanical testing.Fig. 1Long, thin, and short fibers used in the preparation of composites, the samples of composites, and specimens used in the work.Fig. 1

Mechanical tests

Tensile test

The mechanical performance of the produced composites was investigated by conducting tensile tests following the ASTM D3039-3039M standard to assess how the weight percentage and fiber size of date palm fibers impact the epoxy matrix. The speed of the crosshead in the tensile test was 2 mm min−1. In this test, all tensile strength, tensile modulus, and elongation at break properties were determined. Each test was carried out three times, and the average values were taken into account.

Impact test

Additionally, impact testing can provide a comprehensive understanding of the behavior of composites related to impact resistance. The energy absorbed by the composite through rapid loading conditions can determine the behavior of the composite under impact conditions. Therefore, a Charpy-type instrument was used to determine the effect of fiber content and fiber size on the impact strength of the composite according to the ASTM D6110 standard. This may lead to a better understanding of the role of fiber size and geometry in developing fiber-resin interfacial strength.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy-EDS/EDX

One of the most useful analytical instruments for general materials analysis is EDS. Electron microscopy and elemental spectroscopy work hand in hand to give a better understanding of materials. This is usually performed via the EDS technique as an advanced micro-analytical process. When a high-intensity electron beam affects a material's inherent electron structure, EDS detects and interprets the distinctive x-rays that are produced from that substance. The discrete energy of the X-rays arises from the electrons in the sample being excited and relaxed. The orbital structure of each element in the sample determines these energies. This makes it possible to analyze each substance precisely to determine its elementary constituents in a non-destructive manner. Elementary mapping is used to present complex data in an understandable, visually appealing format that helps researchers communicate complex information effectively. Elemental mapping leverages the compositional precision inherent in methods such as EDS microanalysis and combines it with appropriate imaging. The process of elemental mapping involves collecting incredibly detailed elemental composition information about the surface of a sample using EDS analysis. This method allows the study of complex systems made of composite materials, precipitates, or minerals while using the chemical properties of the materials to gain new insights.

Scanning electron microscope Characterization

The morphology of the composites was investigated with a scanning electron microscope (SEM). An electron microscope (Quanta™ 450 FEG SEM) was used to examine fiber dispersion in epoxy, demonstrating pull-out and bonding at the matrix-fiber interface after tensile tests. Furthermore, the surface morphology and cross-section of date palm fibers were examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM).

Mechanical performance of the DPF/epoxy composites

The mechanical performance of the produced composites was investigated in terms of ultimate tensile strength, tensile modulus, and elongation at break properties as quasi-static behavior, as well as the composites' dynamic response when sudden stress was applied to the DPF/epoxy composites. The effects of fiber loading, fiber length, and fiber width (represented by long fiber (L.F.), short fiber (S.F.), and long-thin fiber (T.F.)) on the mechanical characteristics and fracture reaction of the composites were examined. Fig. 3 shows the ultimate tensile strength of the L.F. date palm/epoxy composites with fiber loading as compared to the epoxy matrix. It can be demonstrated that adding long date palm fiber to the epoxy increased the composite's tensile strength at all fiber content. However, the improvement varied depending on the fiber composition of the composite. The 25 wt% case demonstrated the greatest improvement in this mechanical property, reaching more than 127 % of the original epoxy matrix. This implies good interfacial bonding between the fiber and the matrix, which improves stress transfer inside the composite.Fig. 3Tensile strength of the composites with fiber content for the L.F. case.Fig. 3

Tensile strength of the composites with fiber content for the L.F. case.

Fig. 4 shows Young's modulus of composites in the case of L.F. date palm and epoxy. The modulus of elasticity of this type of composite was increased by L.F. date palm. This feature was likewise improved at all fiber content compared to the epoxy matrix, with the highest results at 25 wt% fiber content, but somewhat lower at 35 wt%. The modulus of elasticity of the composite was enhanced due to the proper adhesion between the fiber and the matrix indicating the added value of the long date palm fiber in the composite. However, this property was slightly reduced at 35 wt% case due to fiber agglomeration inside the composites and the insufficient wetting matrix for this large content of the fibers.Fig. 4Modulus of elasticity of the composites with fiber content for L.F. case.Fig. 4

Modulus of elasticity of the composites with fiber content for L.F. case.

Fig. 5 shows the elongation at break property of long date palm fiber/epoxy composites as a function of fiber loading. It can be noticed that the composite strain property was reduced as fiber content increased indicating the proper adhesion between the fiber and the matrix that restricted the free elongation of the epoxy and changed the behavior of the composite to be more brittle. It can be noticed that the strain of the original epoxy matrix was 0.31 % and reduced to 0.27 %, 0.25 % and only 0.21 % in the case of 15 wt%, 25 wt%, and 35 wt% respectively. The relatively significant reduction in strain characteristic with fiber content (about 32 % reduction at 35 wt% fiber content) illustrates the good bonding between long date palm fibers and epoxy. This is because the elongation at the break property of the date palm fiber is lower than that of the matrix, resulting in reducing the ductility of the composite while increasing the fiber content.Fig. 5Elongation at break property of the composites with fiber content for L.F. case.Fig. 5

Elongation at break property of the composites with fiber content for L.F. case.

On the other hand, the comparison of the ultimate tensile strength property of the date palm fiber with epoxy at different fiber sizes and fiber content compared to the L.F/epoxy case is demonstrated in Fig. 6. It can be shown that the tensile strength of the composites of T.F. type was the best at both 15 wt% and 25 wt% fiber content followed by L.F. type. However, the L.F. type was the best at 35 wt% fiber content followed by the T.F. type. In contrast, the S.F. type-based composite was the worst compared to other long fibers. That is; the 15 wt% fiber content was capable of enhancing the tensile strength of the composite comparable to the epoxy matrix, but this fiber content can be enhanced to develop better mechanical strength properties at 25 wt%. Conclusions

Biocomposite materials' structural integrity is of paramount importance for the overall composite performance. As several structural parameters are involved in the manufacturing of natural fiber-reinforced composite materials, the interaction between such parameters has to be investigated. It was shown that adding long date palm fiber to the epoxy has increased the tensile strength property of the composite at all fiber contents. However, the improvement was varied with fiber content in the composite. The 25 wt% case was found to have the highest improvement in this mechanical property to more than 127 % of the original epoxy matrix. This indicates the good interfacial bonding between the fiber and the matrix, which leads to better stress transfer efficiency within the composite. The C–K map of EDS indicated that carbon (C) was the main content of the composite due to being used as an anti-charge coating as well as being a component of cellulose. The modulus of elasticity of the composite was enhanced due to the proper adhesion between the fiber and the matrix indicating the added value of the long date palm fiber in the composite. Furthermore, the tensile strength of T.F. type composites was the best at both 15 wt% and 25 wt% compared to other fiber sizes. However, the S.F. type composite based performed the worst. Date palm type L.F. showed the best value of elastic modulus at 25 wt%, outperforming both type T.F. and type S.F. composites with an improvement of more than 30.4 % compared to the epoxy matrix. The improvement in tensile strength for the S.F. size was about 72.5 %. The strain property, on the other hand, decreased with all fiber content, indicating the brittle behavior of the composite relative to the original matrix. In addition, superior enhancement of the impact energy property was achieved in all fiber sizes. It was enhanced to more than 112 % in the case of L.F.-based composites due to the good interfacial bonding between the fiber and the matrix where the composite could store more energy during plastic deformation. Furthermore, the absence of pullout fibers inside the composites as demonstrated in the microscopy study is evidence of the good adhesion between the fiber and the matrix that resulted in proper stress transfer efficiency. The brittle nature of fiber breakage also indicates the proper compatibility between porous fiber and the matrix, which justifies the low strain behavior of the composites.
