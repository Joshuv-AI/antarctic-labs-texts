Protein-losing enteropathy in a paediatric patient with Kawasaki disease: a rare clinical manifestation
Authors: Rattakarn Yuwattana, Kwannapas Saengsin, Rekwan Sittiwangkul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryKawasaki disease (KD) is an acute vasculitis of childhood, which can lead to complications affecting multiple organ systems. Protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) is an extremely rare complication of KD, characterised by excessive protein loss through the gastrointestinal tract, leading to hypoalbuminaemia, oedema and immune dysfunction. We report a case of an early childhood boy with intravenous immune globulin (IVIG)-resistant incomplete KD who developed PLE. The patient presented with prolonged fever, conjunctivitis, mucosal changes and cervical lymphadenopathy. He subsequently developed generalised oedema, hypoalbuminaemia and pleural effusion. Diagnosis of PLE was confirmed by elevated stool alpha-1 antitrypsin levels. The patient improved with a second dose of IVIG and supportive therapy. This case highlights the importance of recognising PLE as a potential complication of KD and the importance of early diagnosis and proper treatment to reduce the risk of adverse sequelae.Keywords: Gastroenterology, Cardiovascular medicine, Pediatrics

Background

Kawasaki disease (KD) was previously known as acute febrile mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome.1 2 The exact aetiology of the disease is unknown.3 This condition can cause severe inflammation and necrosis of all blood vessels, especially the medium-sized arteries. Coronary arteries, in particular, are specifically impacted.1 4 Increased microvascular permeability is essential in KD vasculitis, causing hypoalbuminaemia and non-cardiogenic oedema.4,6 Protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) is a clinical entity characterised by abnormal protein loss from the digestive tract, leading to decreased serum proteins, lymphocytopenia and hypogammaglobulinaemia.7 PLE can be presented with oedema, ascites, pleural effusion and pericardial effusions.7 We describe the rare clinical condition of a case of intravenous immune globulin (IVIG) resistance KD with PLE in a paediatric patient.

Case presentation

A previously healthy boy in early childhood presented with a 7 day history of high-grade fever, upper respiratory symptoms and diarrhoea. Three days before admission, he developed bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis, dry cracked lips, watery diarrhoea and reduced oral intake. He had no significant past medical history or known allergies. On examination, the patient was febrile (38°C), tachycardic (142 bpm), tachypnoeic (40 breaths/min), with blood pressure of 90/45 mmHg and oxygen saturation of 96%. He exhibited injected bulbar conjunctiva, cracked lips, strawberry tongue and a left cervical lymph node enlargement of 1 cm. No rash or extremity changes were noted.

Investigations

Inflammatory markers: Elevated C-reactive protein (CRP) 148 mg/L (normal <5.0) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) 94 mm/hour (normal 0–10).

Complete blood count: Haemoglobin 124 g/L, white blood cell count 17×109 /L (93% neutrophils), platelet count 342×109 /L.

Electrolytes: Hyponatraemia (Na 131 mmol/L, normal 135–145).

Liver function tests: Alanine aminotransferase(ALT) 138 U/L, Aspartate aminotransferase(AST) 65 U/L (normal ALT 9–25 U/L) (normal AST 21–44 U/L), hypoalbuminaemia (2.9 g/dL, normal 3.5–5.2 g/dL).

Urinalysis: WBC 5–10 cell/High-power field with no organisms present in his urine

Chest X-ray (figure 1): Presence of right pleural effusion, no cardiomegaly.

Imaging of chest X-ray posteroanterior upright. Right pleural effusion was present.

Echocardiography: No coronary artery abnormalities or dilatation.

Differential diagnosis

The patient met criteria for incomplete KD based on the 2017 American Heart Association guidelines. Other differentials considered included bacterial sepsis, viral exanthems, systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis and toxic shock syndrome. The persistence of fever, mucocutaneous findings, elevated inflammatory markers and sterile pyuria strongly favoured KD.

Treatment

The patient was diagnosed with incomplete KD and was treated with high-dose IVIG (2 g/kg) in a single infusion and aspirin 50 mg/kg/day. The day after IVIG administration, his diarrhoea symptom was improved, but he developed severe hyponatraemia with a serum sodium level of 110 mmol/L. A 3% NaCl solution was used to correct the sodium imbalance, and his serum sodium levels returned to normal. However, 36 hours after receiving the first IVIG infusion, he developed a persistent fever, leading to a diagnosis of IVIG-resistant KD. A second dose of IVIG (2 g/kg) was administered along with continued aspirin therapy. Supportive care, including diuretics for fluid overload and nutritional optimisation, was provided.

Outcome and follow-up

Following IVIG resistance, the patient developed generalised oedema, decreased breath sounds in his right lung and increased diarrhoea. A chest X-ray revealed right pleural effusion. Laboratory investigations showed low serum albumin (2.3 g/dL), and stool alpha-1 antitrypsin levels were elevated at 6.94 mg/dL, confirming the diagnosis of protein-losing enteropathy. Repeat echocardiography revealed right pleural effusion, small pericardial effusion, but no coronary dilatation. After receiving the second dose of IVIG, his clinical condition improved significantly. His fever subsided, generalised oedema resolved, and his serum albumin levels increased from 2.3 to 2.6 g/dL. Six weeks after discharge, a follow-up echocardiogram showed no coronary abnormalities, and the patient remained asymptomatic with continued clinical improvement.

Discussion

KD is a childhood vasculitis that presents as an acute febrile illness with an unknown aetiology.8 In 20%–25% of untreated KD cases, coronary artery complications can develop.8 9 This patient was diagnosed with incomplete KD based on the 2017 American Heart Association guidelines.9 He exhibited a persistent high-grade fever for 7 days and met 2 out of 5 clinical criteria, including bilateral non-purulent conjunctivitis and cracked lips or strawberry tongue. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers (ESR and CRP) along with five out of six supplemental laboratory findings, including increased platelet count, low serum albumin, elevated ALT, high WBC and sterile pyuria. Echocardiography did not show any coronary artery dilatation. He was treated with a high dose of IVIG (2 g/kg) and aspirin (50 mg/kg/day) to reduce the risk of coronary artery complications.10 Despite treatment, he remained febrile with a temperature exceeding 38.0°C for over 36 hours after the completion of IVIG infusion, leading to a diagnosis of IVIG-resistant KD.2 8 The Kobayashi Score (KS), a widely used tool for predicting IVIG resistance, was calculated at 4, which included hyponatraemia, transaminitis, elevated CRP and a high percentage of neutrophils. A KS greater than four is associated with a higher likelihood of IVIG resistance and increased risk of coronary artery involvement due to persistent inflammation. Prior studies have also identified additional risk factors for IVIG resistance, including male sex, cervical lymphadenopathy, thrombocytopenia, palm and sole desquamation, increased total bilirubin, elevated alkaline phosphatase, increased lactate dehydrogenase and persistently elevated CRP.3 11

Protein-losing enteropathy is a rare but significant complication characterised by excessive protein loss through the gastrointestinal tract, leading to hypoalbuminaemia, lymphocytopenia and hypogammaglobulinaemia.7 Common causes of PLE include intestinal inflammation, lymphatic obstruction and infectious processes. The associated clinical manifestations range from oedema, ascites, pleural effusion and pericardial effusion. Kim and Noh (2008) reported a case of a 5-year-old girl diagnosed with KD who developed IVIG-resistant disease complicated by PLE, presenting with marked hypoalbuminaemia, gastrointestinal symptoms and peripheral oedema.8 Diagnosis was confirmed through elevated faecal alpha-1-antitrypsin and imaging revealing intestinal oedema. Initial treatment with IVIG alone was inadequate, necessitating additional anti-inflammatory treatment with a second dose of IVIG combined with corticosteroids, alongside supportive albumin supplementation. Clinical outcomes improved following this treatment approach.12 Similar to our reported patient in this case, PLE was suspected due to generalised oedema and diarrhoea, and the diagnosis was confirmed by an elevated stool alpha-1 antitrypsin level (6.94 mg/dL). Stool alpha-1 antitrypsin measurement is a valuable non-invasive diagnostic tool for detecting PLE. Elevated stool alpha-1 antitrypsin levels reflect abnormal leakage of plasma proteins into the gut lumen. Reported sensitivity for detecting PLE using this marker is 93.3% with specificity of 90%.13 Interpretation of elevated stool alpha-1 antitrypsin levels should always be correlated with clinical findings and laboratory parameters, including serum albumin levels, to establish a diagnosis of PLE accurately.

Several studies have examined the role of vascular leakage in KD, highlighting its inflammatory basis. The primary pathological mechanism of KD vasculitis is increased microvascular permeability, which contributes to systemic complications.5 6 One potential factor involved in vascular permeability is vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which has been implicated in vascular leakage in KD.7 14 VEGF promotes nitric oxide (NO) production in endothelial cells by stimulating nitric oxide synthase activity, leading to increased vascular permeability.15 A recent study further suggests that VEGF-induced vascular permeability is mediated by NO, which is activated through VEGF receptor interactions.16 The systemic inflammation in KD increases microvascular permeability, contributing to coronary artery vasculitis and involvement of other organs.17 Patients with IVIG-resistant KD are recognised to have a more severe and prolonged systemic inflammatory response compared with IVIG-responsive patients. Several studies have demonstrated that IVIG-resistant KD patients exhibit higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and VEGF, which are key mediators promoting vascular endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular permeability.18 19 This heightened inflammatory burden can further compromise endothelial integrity, exacerbating vascular leakage in many organ systems such as the gastrointestinal tract. One retrospective study reported that IVIG-resistant KD patients had a higher incidence of hypoalbuminaemia, peripheral oedema and gastrointestinal symptoms compared with those who responded to initial IVIG therapy, supporting the notion that IVIG resistance may predispose to complications such as protein-losing enteropathy.20 As seen in this case, hypoproteinaemia due to PLE may present with oedema, diarrhoea and malabsorption.

Patient’s perspective

Seeing our child endure a prolonged fever and worsening swelling was extremely stressful. The uncertainty and fear of complications were overwhelming. Once a clear diagnosis was made and treatment started, his condition gradually improved. Watching him recover and regain his energy was a huge relief. We appreciate the medical team’s care and clear communication throughout this challenging time.
