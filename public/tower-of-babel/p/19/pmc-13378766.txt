The microbiological profile of diabetic foot infections: historical trends and relationship with amputation
Authors: Diego Agustín Abelleyra Lastoria, Filzah Khan, Robert Leatherby, Amber Arnold, Peter Edward Holt, Paul Moxey, Anna Louise Pouncey, Iain Nicholas Roy, James John Rowland Budge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the microbial composition of diabetic foot infections (DFIs) is required to deliver appropriate antimicrobial therapy and to establish prognosis.ObjectivesWe aimed to evaluate historical trends in the microbiological profile of DFIs and to explore the relationship between antimicrobial resistance and the risk of amputation.Patients and methodsThis was a retrospective study of patients with DFI treated in a tertiary vascular surgery centre between 2014 and 2024. The prevalence of different DFI organisms and the percentage of patients with organisms exhibiting resistance to DFI first-line antibiotics were calculated. The relationship between resistance to first-line options and amputation was established.ResultsThere were 1164 samples collected from 274 patients (215 males, 78.5%). Prevalence of Enterococcus faecalis (R2 = 0.49, P = 0.017) and Proteus mirabilis (R2 = 0.52, P = 0.029) increased during the study period. Developing resistance to a first-line penicillin-allergic (PA) option was associated with a lower amputation-free probability [hazard ratio (HR) = 1.56, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–2.43, P = 0.046] compared to patients who remained susceptible. This association was not statistically significant for those developing resistance to first-line non-penicillin-allergic options (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 0.96–2.37, P = 0.078).ConclusionsPresence of organisms resistant to PA antibiotics is associated with a higher risk of amputation in a South-West London DFI population. This study highlights a rise in resistance to first-line antibiotics used in PA patients, underscoring the need for continuous surveillance of DFI microbiology resistance patterns to guide empirical therapy.

Introduction

Diabetic foot infections (DFI) are one of the most severe and costly complications of diabetes mellitus. Approximately 50% of diabetic foot ulcers become infected and may lead to deterioration in physical function and quality of life.1 The lifetime incidence of DFI is 15–20% among diabetic patients.2 The estimated annual NHS cost of diabetes and foot complications is between £837–£962 million, approximately 1% of the NHS budget.3

What begins as minimal trauma in a neuropathic foot may quickly progress to a non-healing ulcer colonized by bacteria. The combination of neuropathy, hyperglycaemia and diabetic changes to the innate immune system leads to progression from colonization to infection in 50% of cases.1

The most commonly isolated bacteria in DFIs include aerobic organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus and anaerobes like Bacteroides and Streptococcus species.4 Gram-negative bacteria such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Escherichia coli are frequently implicated in chronic infections and are found as colonizers of wet wounds.4,5

Traditional approaches to DFI management have focused predominantly on glycaemic control, wound debridement and broad-spectrum antibiotics. Yet these strategies fail to account for the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of DFI microbiology. The identification of the microbiological composition of DFIs is crucial for effective management, as empirical treatment with broad-spectrum antibiotics is not always effective in targeting the causative pathogens. The importance of targeted therapy is underscored by the rise of antibiotic resistance in many of the bacteria involved in DFIs.6 This results in prolonged infections and an increased risk of complications including amputation.

Though multiple studies have provided descriptions of the most common organisms involved in DFIs,6–8 there is a paucity of literature evaluating historical trends in the microbiological composition of this condition. Such analysis would allow for the evaluation of the adequacy of current guidelines and aiding in the refinement of empirical treatment strategies. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate historical trends in the microbiological profile of DFIs and to explore the relationship between antimicrobial resistance and the risk of amputation.

Patients and methods

This was a retrospective study of all patients with DFI treated at our tertiary vascular surgery centre in South-West London from January 2014 to December 2024. Both inpatients and outpatients were included. The study was prospectively registered (AUDI004368), and the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist was followed9 (Table S1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).

Data collection

The definitions of variables used are presented in Table S2. Analysis of clinical coding data from hospital records (using ICD-10 codes) and a dedicated diabetic foot database was performed to identify the results of microbiological samples of all patients with diabetes mellitus treated at our centre across 10 years. A retrospective review of the case notes was carried out to identify patients with DFI. In addition, patient age and sex were identified, together with clinically relevant comorbidities (smoking status, chronic kidney disease, peripheral vascular disease and osteomyelitis) and HbA1c. Latest HbA1c within three months prior to study entry was handled as a categorical variable depending on whether this was above or below 48 mmol/mol. Amputation outcomes (including both minor or major amputations) were identified through the amalgamation of a locally held research database, searching local records for the relevant procedure codes and the national vascular registry data for our unit. Those patients undergoing amputation for malignancy, trauma or congenital deformity were excluded as well as those under the age of 18. Data were extracted from local electronic patient records and a linked database with amputation outcomes from our primary rehabilitation centre. All data for the project were securely stored on a password-protected, General Data Protection Regulation compliant, secure cloud server owned by City St George’s, University of London.

Microbiological data

Bone, tissue and wound culture results for patients with DFI were analysed. Data were manually checked to ensure that these were taken from diabetic foot ulcers. The results of these included the date of collection, all micro-organisms grown and the susceptibility to over 200 antibiotics tested in our centre.

Trust microbiological guidelines were evaluated. First-line options for non-penicillin allergic patients with DFI included amoxicillin/clavulanate, amoxicillin and doxycycline. Cefuroxime, ciprofloxacin, co-trimoxazole, doxycycline or metronidazole were indicated in DFI patients allergic to penicillin. These were pooled into PA and NPA options, respectively. Patients were categorized as resistant to PA or NPA first-line options if any of the microorganisms grown in the culture was resistant to one of the aforementioned antibiotics.

Non-pathogenic commensal organisms, such as Staphylococcus epidermis and Staphylococcus hominis, were excluded from analyses if they grew in a tissue or wound swab, since these specimens are often prone to contamination. We included commensal organisms in our analysis only if these grew in a bone sample, and no other pathogenic organisms which may be driving the infection were identified in the culture. We have adopted this approach to mirror local microbiological practice. Commensal organisms were identified through discussions with a consultant microbiologist specialized in DFI, and are listed in Table S3. In addition, organisms were pooled according to gram status and morphology. Due to the reporting system in our trust, non-descriptive culture results like ‘gram positive cocci’ were included. However, these were not included in the subsequently described analyses of resistance nor individual organisms’ historical trends due to their composition not being specified, and they were only included in the analysis of historical trends in gram status.

Data analysis

The prevalence of different microorganisms among all culture results (organism-level) and the percentage of patients that grew each organism in question (patient-level) were calculated for every year in the study period. In addition, the percentage of patients exhibiting culture results which had a microorganism resistant to any first-line options was calculated. Historical trends were evaluated with simple linear regression (due to the normal distribution of yearly prevalence values), with P-values, the coefficient of determination (R2) and the slope of the regression trend-line with 95% CIs presented. P-values associated with the F-statistic of the linear regression model were calculated by testing the null hypothesis that the coefficient of the slope of the yearly prevalence trend-line was zero. Trends were calculated by taking the entire cohort into consideration and stratified according to whether samples originated from bone versus tissue/wound cultures.

A Cox Proportional Hazards regression model adjusted for patient age, duration of disease, co-morbidities and number of cultures (standardized using z-score scaling prior to regression analysis) was performed at the patient level to explore the amputation-free survival probability after having developed resistance to first-line antibiotics (any culture resistant to one or more drugs). A sub-group analysis of multidrug resistance was conducted (any culture resistant to multiple drugs). Resistance was modelled as a static baseline covariate. We opted for this approach because the purpose of this study was to evaluate resistance as a prognostic marker at the time of clinical evaluation, and because a time-dependent model would be highly sensitive to the frequency and timing of repeat cultures. Comorbidities adjusted for were smoking status (former, current or never-smoker), presence of chronic kidney disease and peripheral vascular disease and HbA1c at the time of entry into the study (defined as the date of first culture). We also adjusted for diagnosis of osteomyelitis (confirmed either clinically or radiologically) at any point during the study period. Follow-up was calculated until the date of amputation (event) or the date of the last known clinical contact (censoring). To account for repeated measures, resistance status was determined at the patient level by determining the first date in which a patient exhibited a resistant culture. In polymicrobial infections, patients were deemed ‘resistant’ if a single organism of those grown were resistant to first-line therapy.

The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Differences in survival between groups were visualized using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, with time-to-event measured in days from the first culture. Data were presented as HR and 95% CI. The critical level of statistical significance was set to P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A full list of R packages used can be found in Table S4.

Results

Study population

There were 1164 samples (679 tissue, 191 wound and 294 bone samples) collected from 274 patients (215 males, 78.5%), yielding a total of 2785 isolates. Mean patient age was 62.8 ± 13.3 years (range 23–95 years). All patients had amputation data available. A total of 211 patients (77.0%) had more than one culture result.

Historical trends in microbiological profile

The most common individual organisms were Staphylococcus aureus (13.1%) and Enterococcus faecalis (9.6%) (Figure 1, Table S5). Of the most common individual organisms, only Enterococcus faecalis and Proteus mirabilis became more prevalent at the patient level over time (P = 0.043 and P = 0.029, respectively). Additionally, both became more prevalent among all isolated organisms over time (P = 0.017 and P = 0.010, respectively). Significant temporal trends were not identified for any other organism (Figure 2). On analysis of pooled data according to gram status, there were no significant temporal trends observed (Figure S1).

Historical trends in antibiotic resistance

The percentage of patients with an organism resistant to PA or NPA first-line antibiotic options across the study period are presented in Table S6. There was a significant increase in the percentage of patients with an organism exhibiting resistance to PA first-line antibiotic options (R2 = 0.68, P = 0.002). This was not the case for NPA first-line antibiotic options (R2 = 0, P = 0.890) (Figure 3). High resistance rates among first-line NPA options were largely driven by organism resistance to doxycycline and ciprofloxacin, whereas co-trimoxazole, metronidazole and cefuroxime exhibited lower organism resistance rates (Figure S2). Resistance rates to amoxicillin and amoxicillin/clavulanate were higher than those observed in doxycycline (Figure S3). Beta haemolytic streptococcus group B was the only individual organism among the most common isolates that exhibited increasing resistance rates over time, whereas Pseudomonas aeruginosa became less common over time (Figure S4).

A comparison of temporal trends in resistance to penicillin allergic and non-penicillin allergic first-line antibiotic options.

Relationship between antibiotic resistance and amputation

A total of 104 patients underwent an amputation (38.0%). Of 131 patients who were susceptible to first-line NPA options, 38 (29.0%) underwent an amputation, compared to 66 (46.2%) of 143 patients who developed resistance. There were 181 patients who remained susceptible to first-line PA options, of which 53 (29.3%) underwent an amputation. This was compared to 51 (54.8%) of 93 patients resistant to first-line PA options undergoing an amputation. Baseline characteristics were similar between groups (Table S7).

All models satisfied the proportional hazards assumption (Global Schoenfeld test P > 0.05). Developing resistance to a first-line PA option was associated with a lower amputation-free probability (HR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.01–2.43, P = 0.046) compared to patients who remained susceptible (Figure 4; Table S8). This association was not statistically significant for those developing resistance to first-line NPA options (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 0.96–2.37, P = 0.078; Figure 5). There were 36 and seven patients who developed multi-drug resistance to first-line NPA and PA options, respectively. No significant association between resistance and amputation was observed for these groups (Table S8).

Kaplan–Meier curve comparing amputation-free survival probability according to resistance status to first-line non-penicillin allergic options.

Stratification of samples according to origin

Sub-group analyses of temporal trends stratifying samples into bone or tissue/wound cultures were conducted (Figures S1–S7). No significant deviations from the primary analyses were observed, with the exception of loss of a significant temporal trend in Enterococcus faecalis (Figure S5), and in resistance to first-line PA options among bone samples (Figure S7). In addition, gram-positive cocci were more commonly isolated over time in bone samples, with gram-negative rods becoming less common (Figure S1).

Discussion

We conducted a historical analysis of the microbiological composition of DFI. We found that the presence of a resistant organism to first-line PA therapy was associated with undergoing an amputation in our South-West London population. This was not the case for NPA options, though it approached significance. This is of concern considering that microbial cultures were increasingly resistant to PA first-line antibiotic options. A concerning trend was the increasing resistance rates over time of Beta-haemolytic streptococcus group B. This is a significant organism with considerable virulent potential.6 Improvements in antimicrobial stewardship are required to enhance limb salvage rates.

The overall microbiological profile of our study population is consistent with the polymicrobial nature of DFIs described in other work,6 with Staphylococcus aureus being the most common isolate.8,10,11 However, the microbiological landscape of DFIs varies globally, with regional differences in bacterial prevalence and resistance patterns.6,12 Our findings are therefore likely to be applicable to the DFI population in England only, specifically to the large population base in South-West London. The evolving microbiological landscape observed in our centre underscores the need for other centres to employ similar methods to determine the adequacy of current antimicrobial guidelines.

The chronic nature of DFIs combined with frequent antibiotic exposure and impaired host immunity in diabetic patients increases the likelihood of developing resistant pathogens.13 We found that the presence of a resistant organism to first-line PA options was associated with a lower amputation-free survival. This suggests that resistance to first-line PA antibiotics serves as a prognostic marker with regards to amputation risk, which may inform discussions with patients. The association between antimicrobial resistance and limb loss is multifactorial in aetiology. Resistant organisms often possess enhanced virulence factors that enable them to form dense biofilms or evade host immune responses, leading to more extensive and rapidly destructive local disease.14 In addition, failed antibiotic courses expose the patient to multiple agents, which can lead to even broader spectra of resistance.8

The observed association between antibiotic resistance and amputation is of concern considering that patients are becoming increasingly resistant to PA first-line antibiotic options. This is likely driven by commonly used agents such as doxycycline and ciprofloxacin, with the latter being the only oral switch available for pseudomonal infections.15 This restricts the ability to safely discharge patients on definitive suppressive therapy. Conversely, the lower resistance rates observed for co-trimoxazole and metronidazole suggest these may be effective alternatives when clinically indicated. Though we did not observe a significant association between multidrug resistance to first-line PA and NPA options and amputation, there were only seven and 36 patients in these groups, respectively. It is possible that these analyses were therefore underpowered.

There has been a recent emphasis in current microbiological practice to accurately determine whether patients are penicillin allergic. This is the most commonly noted drug allergy in the UK, reported by about 10% of the population.16 However, less than 10% of reported penicillin allergy is confirmed by formal testing.17 Our findings suggest that DFI patients labelled as penicillin allergic may receive inadequate antimicrobial therapy owing to increasing resistance rates over time. Given the association between antibiotic resistance and amputation, efforts should be made to implement hospital-wide penicillin allergy de-labelling programmes to ensure patients receive appropriate therapy. This may be achieved through widespread use of tools such as PEN-FAST, which aims to identify low-risk penicillin allergies that do not require formal skin testing, and thereby reduce unnecessary penicillin avoidance.18

Sub-group analyses of temporal trends stratifying samples into bone or tissue/wound cultures were conducted. These did not significantly affect the results, with the exception of trends according to morphology and in resistance to first-line PA options among bone samples. Though this could be attributed to differing microbiological yields in bone samples, these represented only 25% of the total number of cultures. Further work with a larger number of bone specimens is required to further scrutinize the trends observed.

The limitations of this work must be taken into account when interpreting its results. First, our patient population was limited to a tertiary centre in South-West London. This may introduce biases related to local prescribing practices and limit generalizability. As aforementioned, there are significant regional differences in bacterial prevalence and resistance patterns.6,12 Further work in other centres is required to establish whether our described patterns of resistance and their association with amputation hold in other patient populations. Second, like the majority of studies pertaining to DFIs, our study is limited by current culture methods’ inability to accurately characterize the microbiological composition of DFIs.19 Third, though we have adjusted for a large amount of important clinical confounders, we were unable to obtain data pertaining to ulcer severity classification, number of previous amputations and duration of antibiotic exposure due to these not being consistently recorded during our study period. It is therefore unclear whether their inclusions would have affected our results, and it is possible that residual confounding may have contributed to the observed association between resistance and amputation. Fourth, our trust’s microbiological database presents organisms as either resistant or susceptible to antibiotics. Distinctions with regards to partial susceptibility could therefore not be factored into our analyses. Fifth, given the exploratory nature of this study, we performed multiple temporal trend analyses across various organisms and resistance profiles. Corrections for multiple temporal comparisons were not performed due to these being hypothesis-generating. Therefore, P-values should be interpreted as measures of evidence of strength rather than as confirmatory tests. Finally, because this was a retrospective observational study, our findings demonstrate association between resistance and amputation rather than causation. Prospectively collected data may elucidate a causal relationship between these two variables.

The presence of organisms resistant to PA antibiotics is associated with a higher risk of amputation in a South-West London population. This study observed a concerning rise in antibiotic resistance to first-line antibiotics used in penicillin-allergic patients. These findings underscore the need for continuous surveillance of DFI microbiology and resistance patterns to guide empirical therapy and improve patient outcomes. Improvements in antimicrobial stewardship are required to enhance limb salvage rates.
