Critical care resource disparities in China: a nationwide survey and policy recommendations for health equity

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritical care medicine plays a pivotal role in addressing life-threatening conditions; however, its development in China is hindered by resource scarcity and inequitable distribution. Existing studies have mostly focused on ICU bed numbers or selected regions, and have rarely provided a comprehensive, nationwide assessment of ICU beds, staffing and equipment using standardized methods, nor have they systematically quantified population-based equity of ICU resources. To address this evidence gap and describe the current landscape of critical care capacity in China, we conducted a nationwide survey of critical care resources. The survey aimed to describe the current status of resources, quantify population-based equity, and explore regional and hierarchical disparities that may affect access to critical care.MethodsThis cross-sectional survey was conducted from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023, encompassing public comprehensive hospitals, private hospitals, and specialty hospitals across 31 provinces (including municipalities) in China. A total of 4,744 hospitals with a comprehensive ICU provided valid responses and were included in the analysis; the overall response rate among eligible hospitals invited through provincial health authorities was approximately 89.80%. The study focused on institutions with comprehensive intensive care units (ICUs). Hospitals were stratified by level, including tertiary A and B, secondary A and B, and county-level hospitals. Data were collected using the “Critical Care Medicine Resource Survey Form” via Wenjuanxing, an online survey platform. The survey evaluated spatial distribution (bed count and density), equipment availability (ventilators, ECMO, and CRRT), and human resources (staffing and qualification disparities). Responses were analyzed to identify regional and hierarchical variations in resource allocation and utilization.FindingsAmong the 4,744 hospitals, there were 83,632 operational ICU beds, corresponding to a national average of 5.95 ICU beds per 100,000 population. The total ICU workforce comprised 46,137 physicians (a physician-to-bed ratio of 0.55:1) and 134,056 nurses (a nurse-to-bed ratio of 1.60:1). Key equipment included 74,842 invasive ventilators, 13,389 non-invasive ventilators, 2,328 ECMO devices and 12,346 CRRT machines. ICU bed density varied from 2.29 to 10.53 beds per 100,000 population across provinces. The Gini coefficients for ICU beds, physicians and nurses allocated by population were all < 0.2, and there were no statistically significant differences in ICU beds or physician numbers per 100,000 population across provincial GDP or GDP per capita tiers. From 2015 to 2023, ICU beds increased by 80.0%, physicians by 73.4%, nurses by 61.5%, and ECMO devices by 512.8%. Although population-based distribution appeared relatively equitable, regions differed in absolute bed density, staffing ratios and geographic accessibility, and advanced technologies such as ECMO were available in 29.2% of hospitals.InterpretationDespite remarkable progress and optimization in ICU resource expansion, substantial gaps remain in China compared to developed countries, particularly regarding ICU bed densities and staffing ratios. Our analysis indicates relatively equitable distribution of ICU beds, physicians and nurses across provinces when assessed on a per population basis, as reflected by low Gini coefficients, but this population-based equity does not account for differences in disease burden, age structure, urban-rural composition or geographic accessibility. Accordingly, population-based equity of resources does not necessarily imply equity of access or adequacy of care. Moreover, this survey focused on resource quantity and did not assess perceived adequacy, organizational models or clinical outcomes, so no direct conclusions can be drawn about quality of care.These findings suggest that efforts to strengthen critical care in China should focus on improving resource density and staffing in underserved regions, enhancing accessibility in geographically vast areas, and promoting efficient use and regional sharing of advanced technologies such as ECMO through organized referral networks.Keywords: Critical Care Resource, Nationwide Survey, China

Introduction

Critical care medicine is fundamental to reducing mortality in life-threatening conditions, serving as a core safeguard for healthcare systems responding to acute emergencies and severe illnesses [1]. In recent years, frequent global public health emergencies, such as the influenza pandemic, the Ebola epidemic, and the widespread COVID-19 pandemic, have clearly exposed the critical importance and scarcity of ICU resources [2]. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the sudden surge of critically ill patients placed unprecedented pressure on ICU resources worldwide. Many countries and regions faced severe challenges, including an insufficient supply of ICU beds, shortages of essential medical equipment, and overworked medical staff [3]. These events have profoundly highlighted significant deficiencies in the quantity, regional distribution, emergency allocation, and capacity of ICU resources to respond to large-scale public health crises, prompting governments, health management authorities, and society at large to undertake deep reflection and re-examination of the current state of critical care infrastructure.

Internationally, ICU capacity varies widely across income settings. High income countries such as the United States and Canada have reported ICU bed densities ranging roughly from 12 to 30 beds per 100,000 population, whereas many middle and low income countries have substantially fewer ICU beds but sometimes comparable or better outcomes for specific conditions, depending on organization, staffing and case mix [4]. For example, some countries with relatively low ICU bed density have achieved favorable mortality through optimized care pathways and regionalization of services [5]. These observations underscore that ICU bed numbers alone cannot fully explain quality of care, and that contextual factors such as case burden, health system organization and workforce competence are critical.

In China, persistent imbalances in the allocation of critical care medical resources remain a pressing concern [6].Despite rapid infrastructure expansion, China’s ICU resources remain critically insufficient. By 2021, ICU bed density was only 4.75 per 100,000 population—a fraction of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) average of 25.84 per 100,000 and below the national guideline target of 2–8% of total hospital beds [7]. Concurrently, human resource gaps are acute: physician-to-bed ratios (0.55:1) and nurse-to-bed ratios (1.60:1) fall far below international standards, impeding service quality and patient outcomes [8, 9]. These deficiencies reflect broader systemic challenges, including fragmented resource allocation, inadequate training, and inefficient utilization, particularly in under-resourced regions.

Previous reports from china have mainly described regional ICU bed numbers or trends over time, but there is limited nationwide information that simultaneously covers ICU beds, staffing, equipment and information systems, using a standardized survey across all provinces. Moreover, few studies have quantified how equitably ICU resources are distributed by population, or explored how this relates to provincial economic development. Addressing these gaps is essential to understand the current status of critical care in China in an international context and to guide further system strengthening. Our objectives are threefold:

Methods

Study design and period

A nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted in China from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2023, in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. This design facilitated systematic assessment of critical care resource distribution, capacity, and equity across the country.

Study setting

China is administratively divided into 34 provinces, autonomous regions and municipalities directly under the central government. The health system is organized into a tiered hospital structure, including tertiary hospitals (Grade A and B), secondary hospitals (Grade A and B) and county level hospitals, which differ in service scope, bed numbers and technical capacity [10]. This stratification ensured representation across diverse healthcare capacities and geographic regions, with higher tiers indicating greater resource availability and quality (e.g., Grade A surpassing Grade B within the same tier). Comprehensive ICUs are typically located in secondary and tertiary hospitals, with critical care for rural populations often concentrated in county level and prefecture level centers. This survey was conducted within this existing administrative and hospital tier structure, enabling comparison of ICU resources across diverse regions and levels of care.

Survey scope and hospital selection

The survey covered all 31 provinces, autonomous regions, and municipalities directly under the central government in Chinese mainland, excluding China’s Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan. With the assistance of the Critical Care Medicine Branch of the Chinese Medical Association, the target hospitals were determined. Each provincial critical care medicine branch provided a list of hospitals within their jurisdiction that have comprehensive intensive care units (ICUs). Within each province, we aimed to include all tertiary hospitals and a broad sample of secondary and county-level hospitals, covering both urban and rural areas.

Data collection methods

Data were collected via an online survey platform, Wenjuanxing (analogous to SurveyMonkey), through a structured instrument titled the “Critical Care Medicine Resources Survey Form.” The questionnaire was designed by a multidisciplinary team of experts and digitized by two postgraduate students specializing in statistics, who generated survey links and QR codes for dissemination. Responses were submitted by hospital administrators or ICU directors responsible for resource management and clinical operations at participating institutions.

The survey addressed key domains of critical care resources, including:

Spatial distribution: Total number of ICU beds and ICU bed density (beds per 100,000 population).

Data collection and quality control

Target hospitals were selected with assistance from provincial health administrative departments to ensure geographic and institutional representativeness. The survey questionnaire underwent pilot testing to confirm reliability and validity before nationwide distribution. Data accuracy was ensured through a double-entry verification process by independent staff, with outliers or inconsistent responses identified and excluded following manual review by the research team.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0. Descriptive statistics summarized the distribution of ICU resources, equipment availability, and human resources. Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as means ± standard deviations; non-normally distributed variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and proportions. Comparative analyses explored differences across regions (eastern vs. western China) and hospital levels. For normally distributed continuous variables, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed; non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H test, with post-hoc Dunn’s test or Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for pairwise comparisons as appropriate. Categorical data were assessed using chi-square tests. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with significance level set at p < 0.05. Given the large sample size, we also report effect sizes (e.g., differences in means or medians and their 95% confidence intervals) where appropriate to complement p values.

To evaluate the equity of health resource allocation, Gini coefficients and Lorenz curves were utilized. The Gini Coefficient (GI), derived from the Lorenz curve, quantifies distribution equity and is widely applied internationally to assess resource fairness [11].

It is calculated as the ratio of the area between by the Lorenz curve and the line of absolute equality (Area A) to the total area of the right triangle formed below and to the right of the line of absolute equality (Area A + Area B), expressed as: Gini = A/(A + B). The formula is:

In the formula above, k represents the category (e.g., province), X denotes the cumulative percentage of the population, and Y represents the cumulative percentage of resources. The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 0, indicating greater equity and values closer to 1 signifying greater inequity. Established thresholds interpret equity as follows: 0.2–0.3 (relatively equitable), 0.3–0.4 (moderately equitable), > 0.4 (inequitable), and 0.5–0.7 (highly inequitable).

Historical comparison (2015 vs. 2023)

For descriptive comparison of ICU resource growth, we used national ICU resource data from 2015 obtained from and internal reports coordinated by the same national critical care network. These 2015 data were collected using the identical online survey form as in 2021–2023, and may differ in completeness and variable definitions for some indicators. Therefore, comparisons between 2015 and 2023 should be interpreted as approximate trends rather than precise longitudinal estimates. All statistical tests comparing 2015 and 2023 are exploratory and do not imply causal relationships.

Results

National overview of ICU medical resources

ICU beds

Among the 4,744 hospitals nationwide, the total number of operational ICU beds is 83,632, averaging 17.63 ± 35.39 beds per ICU. Regional disparities are evident: Qinghai and Tianjin average fewer than 13 ICU beds per ICU—significantly below the national mean—while Ningxia and Shanxi exceed 23 beds per ICU. The national average bed density stands at 5.95 beds per 100,000 population, with substantial variation ranging from 2.29 in Jilin Province to 10.53 in Ningxia. ICU bed density therefore ranged from 2.29 to 10.53 per 100,000 population across provinces (see Table 2).

Overall situation of the number of ICU Beds

Medical and nursing staff

The total number of ICU medical physicians (including residents, attending and senior intensivists and other physicians working full time in ICUs) is 46,137, with an average of 9.72 ± 8.93 per ICU and a mean medical personnel-to-bed ratio of 0.55:1. Regions average ranges from 7.59 to 12.3 per ICU, with most areas maintaining 9–10 personnel per ICU, indicating relatively consistent distribution. However, the personnel-to-bed ratio varies widely from 0.39:1 to 0.75:1, highlighting significant regional disparities.

The total number of nursing staff is 134,056, averaging 28.26 ± 32.41 per ICU, with a mean nurse-to-bed ratio of 1.60:1. Notably, Qinghai averages 20.41 ± 13.59 nurses per ICU, significantly below the national mean, while Jiangxi and Ningxia exceed 35 nurses per ICU. The nurse-to-bed ratio ranges from 1.04:1 to 1.91:1, further illustrating considerable regional variation (see Table 3).

Medical equipment

Nationwide, there are 74,842 invasive ventilators (average: 15.78 ± 16.94 per ICU), 13,389 non-invasive ventilators (averaging: 2.82 ± 4.51 per ICU), 2,328 ECMO devices (average: 0.49 ± 1.18 per ICU), and 12,346 blood purification devices (average: 2.6 ± 3.66 per ICU). Among hospitals with at least one of the listed devices, 99.62% possess invasive ventilators, 76.08% have non-invasive ventilators, 29.19% with ECMO, and 86.15% are equipped with CRRT devices. Discussion

This nationwide cross-sectional survey—encompassing 4,744 hospitals across all 31 provinces and municipalities of mainland China from 2021 to 2023—provides the most current and comprehensive evidence regarding the distribution of intensive care unit (ICU) resources and the equity of critical care infrastructure in China. By evaluating spatial, human, and technological resource allocation, the study offers important insights into recent achievements and ongoing challenges in Chinese critical care services.

Expansion of ICU resources and national achievements

Over the decade from 2015 to 2023, China has experienced substantial expansion in ICU resources. The survey documents an 80% increase in ICU beds, 73% and 61% growth in ICU medical and nursing personnel respectively, and pronounced increases in essential equipment—including a 138% rise in invasive ventilators and a five fold increase in ECMO devices. These gains reflect intensified national attention to critical care preparedness, most notably catalyzed by the COVID-19 pandemic but also facilitated by long-term policy priorities [12]. Despite these advances, national ICU bed density in 2023 was 5.95 beds per 100,000 population, which, despite marked progress, remains below international benchmarks reported in developed countries [13, 14].

Patterns of disparities and regional variation

Despite overall improvement, notable disparities persist within China [15]. Provincial bed density varies considerably, ranging from 2.29 per 100,000 in Jilin to 10.53 in Ningxia. Staffing ratios also demonstrate substantial regional fluctuation, with medical personnel-to-bed ratios spanning 0.39:1 to 0.75:1 and nurse-to-bed ratios between 1.04:1 and 1.91:1. Western provinces tend to have higher per capita resource allocation, whereas the northeast lags behind national norms. Certain provinces, such as Qinghai and Tianjin, consistently report below-average resources, while others—including Ningxia, Shanxi, and Chongqing—frequently exceed national means. These data underscore that, while overall access has increased, regional imbalances in both resource density and staffing persist.

In contrast, resource distribution of key equipment—such as ventilators, ECMO, and CRRT devices—demonstrates relatively greater national uniformity. Statistical analyses (p > 0.05) reveal no significant differences in equipment availability across major geographic or economic regions, suggesting the impact of centralized procurement mechanisms and national-level deployment strategies [16].

Equity in resource allocation and policy influence

Population-based Lorenz curve analyses yield Gini coefficients below 0.2 for ICU beds, medical personnel, and nursing staff, suggesting a relatively even population-based distribution when assessed at the provincial level. However, this approach does not adjust for differences in disease burden, age structure, urban-rural composition or travel time to ICU facilities, and therefore cannot capture “needs-based” equity or equity of access. Regions with similar bed numbers per 100,000 population may nonetheless have different adequacy of ICU capacity, depending on case mix and geography. Previous studies have shown that in European countries, the low accessibility of ICUs is associated with a higher proportion of COVID-19 deaths [17]. Moreover, we did not collect data on perceived adequacy of ICU capacity, satisfaction among clinicians or patients, or patient outcomes. As such, our results cannot determine whether ICU resources are sufficient or insufficient in any specific region, and we avoid making prescriptive recommendations for increasing beds in particular areas based solely on these data.

Furthermore, correlation analyses indicate no statistically significant association between resource allocation and regional GDP or per capita GDP, diverging from international patterns where economic strength is often a primary determinant of health infrastructure [18]. These findings signal the strong influence of national policy frameworks—such as the “Outline of the National Medical and Health Service System Plan (2015–2020) [19]”—which emphasize equity and planned development, especially in traditionally underserved western provinces.

The nearly universal availability of essential equipment (e.g., 99.62% of hospitals equipped with invasive ventilators) contrasts with continuing gaps in advanced technologies, such as ECMO (present in only 29.19% of hospitals). This suggests that, while baseline emergency response capability is robust, there is an ongoing need for strategic investment in high-end interventions. Because ECMO is a complex, high risk therapy that requires specialized teams and sufficient case volume, it is neither expected nor desirable that all hospitals provide ECMO. Rather than interpreting the current distribution as a simple “coverage gap”, our findings highlight the need to develop regional ECMO networks, with designated high volume centers, standardized referral pathways and coordinated retrieval systems, to ensure that eligible patients can access ECMO when indicated.

Practical implications of the findings

The achievement of equitable resource distribution by population is a commendable milestone, offering a strong foundation for future advancement in China’s critical care system. Nonetheless, absolute ICU resource densities and staffing ratios remain below international benchmarks, and regional imbalances continue to affect service delivery. To address these ongoing challenges, several practical directions should be considered:

Enhancement of resource density and staffing: Targeted strategies must focus on increasing the number of ICU beds and healthcare personnel in less-resourced regions, particularly provinces in the Northeast and specific areas in the West. Routine assessment and direct investment will help bridge persistent gaps and improve nationwide preparedness.

Strengths and limitations

This survey represents the largest multicenter assessment of ICU bed, staff, and equipment distribution in China, featuring robust sample size, stratification by hospital hierarchy and region, and integrated analysis by economic tier. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged: the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference; data collection during the COVID-19 pandemic may over-represent short-term resource mobilization; self-reported data carry the risk of reporting bias; core qualitative indicators (e.g., staff expertise, patient outcomes) were not assessed; and the exclusion of Hong Kong, Macao, and Taiwan limits estimates of Eastern region resources. Data on PDMS and AI utilization were not systematically collected and that their inclusion would be valuable in future surveys. Fine-grained rural-urban differentials within provinces were not explored, potentially masking localized inequities.

Conclusion

In summary, China’s ICU resources have undergone rapid expansion and substantial progress toward equitable distribution, largely attributable to centralized planning and policy commitment. Remaining challenges—in particular, sub-national disparities in absolute resource density, staffing ratios, and advanced equipment access—will require ongoing, targeted intervention. Sustained government commitment to differentiated policies, strategic investment, and optimized deployment models is essential to ensure that critical care capacity matches evolving public health needs across diverse geographic and economic contexts. Future research incorporating longitudinal designs, qualitative quality indicators, and granular spatial analysis is warranted to further elucidate and address remaining gaps in ICU resource equity and effectiveness.
