Adequacy of antenatal care and maternal smoking: impacts on gestational age and birth weight among preterm births in a high-risk referral cohort in Western Iran
Authors: Sajad Nourolahi, Mahnaz Shafieian, Ashraf Direkvand Moghadam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreterm birth and low birth weight (LBW) are leading causes of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Adequate antenatal care (ANC) and reducing modifiable maternal risk factors such as smoking are critical, yet evidence from high-risk Middle Eastern populations remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of 451 preterm neonates delivered at a tertiary referral hospital in Ilam, western Iran. ANC adequacy was assessed primarily using the Kessner index and tested against alternative indices. Maternal exposures included smoking, age, hypertension, and diabetes. Logistic regression models assessed associations, with robustness tested through dose–response splines, propensity score weighting, and E-values.ResultsAdequate ANC was protective, reducing odds of earlier gestational age (aOR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.36–0.76), extremely low birth weight (RRR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.10–0.76), and LBW overall (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.39–0.87). Maternal smoking increased risks, more than tripling ELBW (RRR = 3.75, 95% CI: 1.65–8.54) and doubling LBW (aOR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.20–3.94). Dose–response analysis showed steadily declining LBW risk with increasing ANC utilization, plateauing beyond WHO recommendations. Findings were robust across alternative definitions and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionsAmong preterm births in this high-risk Iranian cohort, adequate ANC was associated with more favorable gestational-age and birth-weight profiles, while maternal smoking increased the severity of prematurity and fetal growth restriction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-08859-y.

Introduction

Preterm birth and low birth weight remain major global public-health challenges. In 2020 an estimated 13–14 million infants were born preterm (< 37 completed weeks of gestation), and preterm complications remain the leading cause of death among children under five years of age worldwide [1]. Rates vary geographically, with the highest burden concentrated in low- and middle-income countries. Reducing the incidence and consequences of preterm birth and fetal growth restriction is therefore a priority for neonatal morbidity and mortality reduction [2].

A national meta-analysis of 19 studies covering over 41,000 births in Iran estimated the pooled prevalence of preterm delivery at approximately 10% (95% CI: 9–12%), with notable regional variation ranging from 5% to nearly 20%. The findings indicate that preterm birth remains a persistent and substantial obstetric challenge across the country, highlighting the need for enhanced antenatal care, maternal education, and risk management strategies to mitigate its adverse outcomes [3].

A broad body of epidemiologic evidence implicates multiple, potentially modifiable maternal and health-service factors in the causal pathway to preterm delivery and low birth weight. Among these, adequacy of prenatal (antenatal) care and maternal tobacco exposure are consistently identified as important determinants [4, 5]. Observational studies and systematic reviews indicate that timely and adequate antenatal care characterized by early initiation, an appropriate number of visits, and quality interventions—are associated with lower risks of both preterm birth and low birth weight, although heterogeneity exists by setting and by how adequacy is measured [6].

Maternal cigarette smoking during pregnancy has one of the clearest and most robust associations with fetal growth restriction and preterm delivery [7]. Meta-analyses and recent reviews demonstrate a dose response relationship: active smoking increases the odds of low birth weight and preterm delivery, and exposure to environmental tobacco smoke also confers measurable risk [4, 8]. Biologic mechanisms are plausible and well described, including nicotine-induced vasoconstriction of utero placental circulation, carbon monoxide-mediated impairment of oxygen delivery, oxidative stress, and direct toxic effects on placental structure and function mechanisms that lead to placental insufficiency and fetal growth restriction [9].

Despite these known associations, important gaps remain. First, many studies use differing metrics for prenatal care adequacy (number of visits, timing of first visit, APNCU/Kessner indices), complicating comparability [10]. Second, the relative contributions of service access, behavioral risk factors such as smoking, and maternal comorbidity (hypertension, diabetes) across gestational-age strata are not always quantitated in the same study [11]. Finally, while interventions such as group prenatal care have shown promise in some contexts, evidence for scalable antenatal service improvements that reduce prematurity and very low birth weight at population level remains an active area of research [12].

Maternal medical conditions such as chronic hypertension and diabetes are well established risk factors for preterm birth and fetal growth restriction. Both conditions can impair placental perfusion and nutrient delivery, contributing to shortened gestation and reduced birth weight. Therefore, these comorbidities were considered key covariates in our analytic models to account for underlying maternal health influences on neonatal outcomes. In this study, antenatal care adequacy was evaluated primarily using the modified Kessner index, which accounts for both the timing of the first visit and the number of visits relative to gestational age. This index is widely applied in similar hospital-based studies and is particularly appropriate for Iranian maternal health data, where visit schedules are standardized across gestational periods.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This cross-sectional study was based on data from the Premature Infant Registry Project at Ilam University of Medical Sciences, covering all singleton preterm deliveries between January 2022 and December 2024. This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary referral hospital in Western Iran, which provides specialized maternal and neonatal services. Data were obtained from medical records of mothers who delivered preterm infants at this center.

Study population

This study included only preterm births (< 37 completed weeks) with complete maternal and neonatal records. Analyses evaluated gestational age categories within this preterm cohort. Exclusion criteria were multiple pregnancies, major congenital anomalies, and missing data on key exposures or outcomes.

Ordinal logistic regression models were subsequently fitted to estimate associations between ANC adequacy and gestational age category, accounting for the derived weights.

Sensitivity Analyses included repeating all models under alternative ANC definitions, applying propensity score weighting to reduce confounding by indication, and calculating E-values to quantify the robustness of findings to potential unmeasured confounding.

Statistical Analysis

Brant Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression (teffects ipw command in Stata) to model the probability of receiving adequate antenatal care as a function of maternal age, parity, BMI, education, socioeconomic status, smoking, and other baseline covariates. Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was then applied to generate a weighted pseudo-population for outcome analysis. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with SMD < 0.1 considered acceptable. Effective sample size (ESS) after weighting was also calculated to evaluate weight stability.

Model assumptions were examined prior to interpretation. The proportional-odds assumption for ordinal logistic regression was tested using the Brant test (χ² = 7.82, df = 8, p = 0.45); no violations were observed. To assess robustness, a multinomial logistic regression model was also fitted, which produced consistent results. Covariates were selected a priori based on previous literature and a directed acyclic graph (DAG) depicting plausible causal relationships among antenatal care, maternal characteristics, and neonatal outcomes. The final adjustment set included maternal age, parity, BMI, education, socioeconomic status, smoking status, hypertension, diabetes, nutrition, and psychosocial stress.

Effect estimates were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) or relative risk ratios (RRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Post-hoc power analysis indicated that with N = 451, the study had approximately 80% power (α = 0.05) to detect odds ratios ≥ 1.8 for inadequate ANC and earlier gestational-age category, assuming a 30% prevalence of inadequate care. Power for low birth weight analyses was approximately 75% for detecting odds ratios ≥ 1.6. The smallest subgroup, ELBW (n = 28), provided only 40% power for similar effect sizes.

All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Restricted cubic spline models were fitted using the mkspline command in Stata, and E-values were calculated using the ‘EValue’ R package (version 4.1.3). Figures were generated using the ‘ggplot2’ package in R.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ilam University of Medical Sciences Institutional Ethics Committee (IR.MEDILAM.REC.1399.069), with a waiver of informed consent due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized records.

Results

Study population

Of 512 preterm deliveries identified, 24 multiple gestations, 13 with congenital anomalies, and 24 with incomplete data were excluded, yielding a final analytic sample of 451 singleton preterm births. The mean maternal age was 29.8 ± 6.2 years, with 17.1% (n = 77) aged < 18 years, 68.7% (n = 310) aged 18–35 years, and 14.2% (n = 64) aged > 35 years”). Approximately 35% of women had inadequate antenatal care, and 5.8% reported smoking during pregnancy. The average gestational age was 33.43 ± 3.71 weeks, and the mean birth weight was 2250.61 ± 758 g. Maternal characteristics, socioeconomic indicators, and obstetric history are summarized in Table 1. The majority of participants were multiparous and had completed at least secondary education. Maternal sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by outcome group are reported in Table 1.

Propensity score–weighted models produced results nearly identical to conventional multivariable regression, suggesting limited residual confounding by measured factors.

Robustness checks indicated stable findings. Propensity score–weighted models closely matched conventional regression, and E-values suggested that unmeasured confounders would need risk ratios ≥ 2.8 (ANC) and ≥ 3.8 (smoking) to fully explain away observed associations. The near-identical results indicate that residual confounding by measured covariates is unlikely to explain the observed associations. An unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both ANC adequacy and LBW by a risk ratio of at least 2.8 (above and beyond measured covariates) to fully explain away the protective effect observed (Fig. 3; Tables 5 and 6).

When the observed-to-expected ANC utilization ratio was modeled as a continuous predictor using restricted cubic splines, a graded protective effect on LBW emerged. The predicted probability of LBW declined steadily as ANC utilization increased. Mothers who attended fewer than half of the expected number of visits (obs/exp < 0.5) had an estimated LBW probability of approximately 35–40%, whereas those who achieved or exceeded the expected schedule (obs/exp ≥ 1.0) had substantially lower risks, approaching 15–20%. The confidence bands around the spline estimates did not include the null for most of the observed range, reinforcing the robustness of the association.

Figure 3 illustrates the strength of unmeasured confounding that would be required to fully account for the observed associations between antenatal care (ANC) adequacy and maternal smoking with adverse birth outcomes. The E-values indicate the minimum association (on the risk ratio scale) that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome, beyond measured covariates, to explain away the observed effect. For ANC adequacy, the E-values were 2.8 for low birth weight (LBW) and 3.2 for extremely low birth weight (ELBW), suggesting that only a confounder with moderately strong associations could nullify the protective effect. For maternal smoking, the E-values were 3.8 for LBW and 4.0 for ELBW, indicating particularly robust associations that are unlikely to be explained by residual confounding. The dashed line at E = 1 represents the null threshold, where no unmeasured confounding would be needed.

Discussion

In this study of 451 preterm births in a referral hospital in Western Iran. Consistent with regional data, the majority of participants had secondary education or less and belonged to low or middle SES categories. We found that ANC, as defined by the Kessner index, was strongly associated with improved neonatal outcomes. Adequate ANC was associated with lower odds of earlier gestational age among preterm births, indicating a protective effect even within the preterm spectrum. Maternal smoking, by contrast, was associated with markedly increased risks of LBW and ELBW. These associations were robust across multiple sensitivity analyses, including alternative ANC adequacy indices, dose–response modeling, and robustness checks using propensity score weighting and E-values.

From a policy perspective, our results underscore the importance of ensuring timely initiation and completion of recommended antenatal visits. Strengthening ANC outreach particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged women and integrating smoking-cessation counseling into routine care could substantially reduce preventable preterm and low-birth-weight deliveries. These findings align with national priorities under Sustainable Development Goal 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and may guide regional health authorities in designing targeted ANC quality-improvement initiatives in high-risk referral settings [13].

Our findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating the protective effects of timely and sufficient ANC on adverse perinatal outcomes [6, 14]. Studies from both high-income and low- and middle-income countries have reported reduced risks of preterm birth and LBW with adequate ANC utilization [2, 6, 15]. Similar to reports from other Middle Eastern settings, a population-based cohort in southern Iran found that water-pipe smoking among pregnant women (~ 8.2%) was associated with increased risk of LBW [16]. In Saudi Arabia, exposure to secondhand smoke (about 31% of cohort) was associated with a small but significant reduction in mean birth weight, reinforcing that even passive or light exposure has measurable effects [17]. Useful for showing similar regional / Middle Eastern patterns. The magnitudes of association we observed are broadly comparable to those reported in population-based studies, despite our focus on a high-risk referral population.

From a policy perspective, our results underscore the importance of ensuring timely initiation and completion of recommended antenatal visits. Strengthening ANC outreach particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged women and integrating smoking-cessation counseling into routine care could substantially reduce preventable preterm and low-birth-weight deliveries. The dose–response analysis provided further support for a graded protective effect of ANC adequacy. These observed gradients in gestational age and birth weight therefore reflect differences within preterm births, rather than comparisons between preterm and term deliveries. The predicted probability of LBW declined steadily as the observed-to-expected ANC visit ratio increased, with the steepest benefits observed as women approached or achieved the recommended schedule. Several studies have demonstrated a graded, dose-response relationship between ANC utilization and improved birth outcomes. For example, in Bangladesh, mean birth weight rose steadily as the number of ANC visits increased, with most gains observed up to about six visits, after which marginal improvements diminished [19]. In Lao PDR, risk of low birth weight decreased in a linear fashion with increasing visit frequency, especially when the first visit occurred in the first trimester [20]. Similarly, pooled DHS data from sub-Saharan Africa found that mothers with ≥ 8 ANC visits—but fewer than that—showed increasing protection against LBW [21]. In Rwanda, improvements in both the quality and consistency of ANC were associated with declines in LBW prevalence over time [22].These findings provide strong external support for our observation that the key benefit of ANC is achieved once women meet the recommended contact schedule, with diminishing returns beyond that threshold.”

ANC appears to influence fetal growth and gestational duration through distinct yet complementary mechanisms. Regular ANC visits enable nutritional counseling, iron and folate supplementation, and early detection of intrauterine growth restriction, all of which support optimal fetal growth and reduce the risk of low birth weight [23]. Conversely, the protective effect of ANC on preterm birth likely operates through the early identification and management of pregnancy complications such as hypertensive disorders, urinary and genital tract infections, and premature rupture of membranes. By preventing or mitigating these conditions, ANC contributes to prolonging gestation and improving neonatal survival [24]. These findings underscore the multidimensional role of ANC in improving both the quality and duration of pregnancy outcomes.

Evidence from high-income countries similarly supports the protective role of adequate antenatal care. Studies from the United States, Canada, and Western Europe demonstrate that even in health systems with universal ANC coverage, delayed initiation or insufficient visits are associated with elevated risks of preterm birth and low birth weight, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged women [25, 26]. These findings suggest that the benefits of ANC extend beyond access alone and depend on timely, high-quality, and comprehensive care. The consistency of this association across diverse settings reinforces our conclusion that adequate ANC exerts a multidimensional protective effect on both fetal growth and gestational duration. The findings have practical implications for maternal and neonatal health policy in Iran and comparable settings. Strengthening early initiation and completion of recommended ANC visits could substantially reduce preterm birth severity and low birth weight in high-risk populations. Integrating targeted ANC outreach programs particularly for socioeconomically disadvantaged women and reinforcing smoking-cessation counseling during pregnancy may enhance the effectiveness of existing services. In referral hospitals, systematic screening for tobacco use and individualized ANC scheduling could provide feasible, evidence-based approaches to improving perinatal outcomes.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. It is among the first from Iran to rigorously evaluate ANC adequacy using multiple definitions, while also incorporating advanced methods such as restricted cubic splines, propensity score weighting, and E-values. The use of a high-risk referral population also ensured sufficient numbers of adverse outcomes to detect meaningful associations.

Limitations

Although advanced analytical methods such as propensity score weighting and E-values were applied to strengthen causal inference, the retrospective observational design precludes definitive causal conclusions. Self-reported smoking may have been underreported, leading to conservative effect estimates. Missing data were minimal and did not materially affect results, but some degree of residual confounding from unmeasured or imperfectly measured variables cannot be excluded. Both ANC adequacy and smoking exposure were based on clinical records and, in the case of smoking, partially on maternal self-report. Consequently, some degree of misclassification is possible. Underreporting of smoking, in particular, would likely bias associations toward the null, leading to conservative effect estimates. Similarly, incomplete or inconsistently recorded ANC visit data may have resulted in non-differential misclassification, which would attenuate rather than exaggerate the observed associations. Finally, the single-center design may limit generalizability to other regions with different maternal risk profiles.

Conclusions

Among preterm births in this high-risk referral population, adequate antenatal care was associated with improved gestational-age and birth-weight outcomes, whereas maternal smoking was linked to greater severity of prematurity and growth restriction.

Recommendations for future research

Collect more detailed data on smoking intensity, timing, and cessation during pregnancy.

Assess ANC content and quality beyond visit frequency and timing.

Conduct community-based studies to improve generalizability beyond high-risk referral populations.

Explore intervention trials combining enhanced ANC packages with structured smoking cessation support.
