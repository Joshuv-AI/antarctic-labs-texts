Liver-directed base editing of ABCC6 prevents ectopic calcification in a variant-humanized mouse model of pseudoxanthoma elasticum
Authors: Lauren C. Testa, Dora Obiri-Yeboah, Hooda Said, Ping Qu, Michael A. Levine, Mohamad-Gabriel Alameh, Kiran Musunuru, Qiaoli Li, Xiao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE) is an autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder characterized by ectopic calcification of elastic fibers throughout the skin, retina, and arteries. It is caused by pathogenic variants in ABCC6, which encodes a transmembrane transporter that primarily localizes to hepatocytes. Loss of ABCC6 function in hepatocytes leads to systemic deficiency of inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi), a potent inhibitor of calcification; such depletion of PPi from the circulation is responsible for multisystemic ectopic calcification seen in PXE. Therefore, liver-targeted variant correction by genome editing and subsequent restoration of systemic PPi may offer a one-and-done therapeutic approach for PXE. The ABCC6 c.3490C>T (p.R1164X) variant is one of the most common variants found in PXE patients. Here, we show that liver-directed correction of the R1164X variant by adenine base editing restores plasma PPi and prevents ectopic skin calcification in mice fed a standard diet or an “acceleration diet” that exacerbates ectopic calcification. These results provide fundamental insight into the molecular etiology of PXE and provide a proof-of-principle that genetic correction of ABCC6 defects through adenine base editing may represent a novel, permanent therapy for the treatment of PXE.

Introduction

Pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE [MIM: 264800]) is an autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder caused by pathogenic variants in ATP-binding cassette subfamily C, member 6 (ABCC6),1,2,3 characterized by ectopic calcification of elastic fibers throughout the skin, retina, and medium-sized arteries. There are no available treatment options for the underlying cause of PXE. Durable and, ideally, curative therapies are needed to address the significant unmet medical needs of PXE patients.

ABCC6 encodes ABCC6, an efflux transporter localized to the plasma membrane mainly in hepatocytes in the liver and, to a much lesser extent, in the kidneys.4 Two hypotheses have attempted to explain the pathogenesis of PXE. The “cell hypothesis” suggests that local absence of ABCC6 activity within connective tissue, even if normally expressed only at low levels, is responsible for calcification of these tissues.5,6,7 The alternative, though not mutually exclusive, “metabolic hypothesis” suggests that loss of ABCC6 function in hepatocytes results in the deficiency of systemic factors that normally prevent ectopic calcification.8,9,10,11 Externalization of ATP across the ABCC6 transporter results in the conversion of extracellular ATP to AMP and inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) in the plasma by the enzyme ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase (ENPP1).12,13 PPi is a potent inhibitor of calcification and systemic PPi depletion underlies the pathogenesis of PXE.12,13,14,15

As clinical manifestations of PXE are due, at least in part, to a metabolic defect, we hypothesized that liver-directed genetic therapies may be sufficient to prevent or halt the ectopic calcification of PXE. In vivo gene editing is an emerging therapeutic approach for direct correction of pathogenic variants within the body. Gene-editing tools include nucleases, cytosine base editors, adenine base editors, and prime editors. Base editors are attractive because they can efficiently introduce precise single-nucleotide alterations without generating double-strand breaks.16,17 Our group has demonstrated the ability of adenine base editors to make specific DNA edits with high efficiency and specificity in the liver in disease mouse models and non-human primates, and has developed a bespoke liver-directed adenine base editing therapy for an infant with a severe urea cycle disorder.18,19,20 Out of the pathogenic ABCC6 variants described in a large cohort of PXE patients, the ABCC6 c.3490C>T (p.R1164X) variant is the second-most-common pathogenic single nucleotide variant,21 and the most common disease allele that is well-positioned for ABE (due to its location relative to a canonical NGG protospacer-adjacent motif [PAM] and nearby bystander adenines), by A-to-G conversion of the variant on the opposite strand.

We and others have previously shown that including DNA nucleotides within the protospacer of the guide RNA (gRNA), a strategy termed hybrid gRNAs, improves on-target editing and reduces bystander editing (unwanted A-to-G conversion nearby the pathogenic variant) and off-target editing (unwanted A-to-G conversion at other genomic sites).22,23 We identified a candidate DNA/RNA hybrid gRNA, hyb18, that corrected the R1164X variant in homozygous cells and in the livers of variant-humanized mice with higher on-target editing and lower off-target and bystander editing compared to treatment with the standard gRNA. Treatment with both the standard gRNA and the hybrid gRNA restored plasma levels of PPi in a short-term study.22 Here, we sought to assess the effects of liver-specific correction of the R1164X variant on elastic fiber calcification in extrahepatic connective tissue. Treatment of homozygous R1164X mice with lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) containing mRNA encoding the adenine base editor ABE8.8 and hyb18 resulted in efficient variant correction in the liver and restoration of membrane-targeted ABCC6 expression in hepatocytes and restored plasma levels of PPi. Ectopic calcification of vibrissae in muzzle tissue was fully prevented in LNP-treated mice fed either a normal diet or an experimental diet that exacerbates muzzle calcification.24,25 Muzzle tissue of LNP-treated mice showed very low corrective editing, providing evidence that variant correction in the liver alone was sufficient to prevent muzzle skin calcification.

Results

The earliest site of calcification in the Abcc6−/− mouse, a well-characterized animal model of PXE, is the connective tissue dermal sheath of vibrissae in muzzle skin.24,26 Muzzle calcification is slowly progressive and begins at 5–6 weeks postnatally, but can be accelerated by an experimental diet that is high in phosphorus and low in magnesium.25 To establish the progression of muzzle calcification in our novel R1164X mouse model, we harvested muzzle tissue from homozygous R1164X mice at 14 and 18 weeks of age, along with 18-week-old heterozygous mice, and assessed calcification by micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning. Calcification in homozygous mice was progressive between 14 and 18 weeks of age. Similar to heterozygous Abcc6−/− mice and individuals that are heterozygous for ABCC6 variants, the 18-week-old heterozygous mice showed no ectopic calcification (Figure 1D). Based on these findings, we selected the 18-week-old time point as our endpoint for subsequent muzzle calcification studies.

Long-term effects of treatment with LNPs containing ABE8.8 mRNA and hyb18 gRNA on ectopic muzzle calcification

In previous work, we identified a gRNA, termed PXE1, that is positioned directly upstream of an NGG PAM, the canonical PAM that is recognized by S. pyogenes Cas9 (Figure 2A).22 PXE1 appeared favorable for adenine base editing correction of the R1164X variant because it positions the pathogenic adenine at position 4 of the protospacer, within the editing window of the ABE8.8 deaminase domain, which typically falls between positions 3 and 8 of the protospacer (Figure 2A). We previously showed that treatment of homozygous R1164X mice with LNPs containing ABE8.8 mRNA and the hybrid gRNA hyb18 (with DNA nucleotide substitutions at positions 5, 6, and 7 of the spacer) improves corrective editing and reduces bystander and off-target editing relative to treatment with the standard PXE1 gRNA (Figure 2A).22 To assess the effects of liver-directed adenine base editing of the R1164X variant on muzzle calcification, we treated five-week-old R1164X/R1164X mice with 2.5 mg/kg LNPs containing ABE8.8 mRNA and hyb18 gRNA, the same LNPs as those used in our previous study, by retroorbital injection. Wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous littermates treated with PBS were used as controls. Mice were sacrificed at 18 weeks old. Whole-liver tissue was collected for next-generation sequencing to assess corrective editing of the R1164X variant and nearby bystander editing. LNP-treated mice showed a mean 35% (SD = 5.1%) whole-liver corrective editing, with an additional 3% (SD = 0.66%) bystander editing of nearby adenines at positions 9, 10, and 11 of the hyb18 protospacer (Figure 2B). Muzzle calcification was measured by micro-CT scanning and by measuring the calcium content within each muzzle. Additional muzzle tissue was also collected from each treated mouse for genomic DNA extraction and next-generation sequencing to verify that treatment-induced changes in muzzle calcification were not due to variant correction within the muzzle tissue itself. Quantification of calcification mass by micro-CT showed that wild-type and heterozygous 18-week-old mice had no muzzle calcification (Figure 2C). PBS-treated homozygous mice displayed notable muzzle calcification at 18 weeks of age. Muzzle calcification was almost entirely prevented in all LNP-treated mice (Figure 2C). Untreated homozygous mice had elevated calcium content within their muzzles, which was normalized in LNP-treated homozygous mice (Figure 2D). There was no evidence of corrective editing within the muzzle tissue of any of the LNP-treated mice (Figure 2B), suggesting that corrective editing within the liver alone was sufficient to prevent ectopic mineralization of muzzle connective tissue.

Prevention of multisystemic ectopic calcification in mice exposed to acceleration diet

Our group has previously shown that an “acceleration diet” that is high in phosphorus and low in magnesium can exacerbate ectopic calcification of the Abcc6−/− mouse model when mice are fed the diet between 5 and 15 weeks of age.24,25 We now aimed to assess whether liver-directed correction of the R1164X variant is able to restore plasma levels of PPi and prevent diet-induced muzzle calcification. We treated five-week-old homozygous R1164X mice with 2.5 mg/kg LNPs containing hyb18 gRNA and ABE8.8 mRNA, the same LNPs as the previous experiment, and initiated the acceleration diet at the time of treatment. Mice were maintained on the acceleration diet for 13 weeks and were sacrificed at 18 weeks of age. Five-week-old wild-type, heterozygous, and homozygous littermates were treated with PBS and maintained on the acceleration diet for 13 weeks as controls. To determine the effect of variant correction on ABCC6 protein expression and localization in the liver, we conducted immunofluorescence of the ABCC6 protein in liver sections. Wild-type mice had clear protein localization to the plasma membrane of hepatocytes. Untreated homozygous mice showed only background antibody signal. Protein expression and localization were restored in treated mice that were fed the acceleration diet (Figure 3B).

As plasma PPi is depleted in both the R1164X and Abcc6−/− mouse models, we sought to assess whether PPi could be restored in LNP-treated mice that were fed the acceleration diet. Mice were bled at five weeks old, immediately prior to initiation of the acceleration diet and treatment with either PBS or 2.5 mg/kg LNPs containing ABE8.8 mRNA and hyb18. Mice were bled again at 18 weeks old, immediately prior to sacrifice. Wild-type mice had the highest plasma PPi levels both before and 13 weeks post-PBS treatment, heterozygous mice showed intermediate PPi levels, and PBS-treated homozygous mice showed decreased PPi at baseline and 13-weeks post-PBS treatment (Figure 3C). LNP-treated homozygous mice showed depleted PPi pre-treatment, but PPi levels were restored to at least those of heterozygous mice by 18 weeks of age (Figure 3C). As heterozygous R1164X mice and human patients that are heterozygous for ABCC6 variants do not show clinical evidence of disease, we predict that PPi levels of heterozygous mice should be sufficient to prevent clinical manifestations of disease.

We then assessed muzzle calcification by micro-CT scanning and quantification of calcium content of muzzle tissue. Micro-CT scanning revealed no calcification in wild-type or heterozygous mice. PBS-treated homozygous mice showed severe calcification up to 4-fold greater than the calcification seen in PBS-treated homozygous mice fed a standard diet, suggesting that the acceleration diet was able to successfully exacerbate muzzle calcification in these mice (Figure 3D). Strikingly, muzzle calcification was fully prevented in all nine LNP-treated homozygous R1164X mice (Figure 3D). PBS-treated homozygous R1164X mice had elevated calcium content within the muzzle tissue, which was restored back to wild-type levels in LNP-treated R1164X mice (Figure 3E). Together, these findings suggest that liver-directed adenine base editing to correct the human R1164X variant is sufficient to restore plasma pyrophosphate and prevent ectopic muzzle calcification, even in mice with severe calcification exacerbated by the acceleration diet.

Discussion

We have previously shown that liver-directed adenoviral gene therapy of the human ABCC6 gene can restore plasma PPi and prevent ectopic muzzle calcification in Abcc6−/− mice.10 Here, we show that liver-directed correction of the ABCC6 R1164X variant restored plasma PPi and prevented muzzle calcification. This suggests that restoration of ABCC6 function in the liver, and subsequent restoration of PPi in the circulation, may be sufficient to prevent ectopic calcification of distal cell types (e.g., skin). Next-generation sequencing of muzzle tissue from treated mice showed only trace levels of variant correction within the muzzle, further validating that correction of the R1164X variant in the liver is responsible for prevention of muzzle calcification. These findings support the metabolic hypothesis of PXE, which proposes that absence of ABCC6 function in the liver results in systemic metabolic changes that drive calcification of distal tissues.11 More studies are needed to fully understand if restoration of ABCC6 expression in the liver is sufficient to prevent all disease manifestations across the lifespan. Our findings described here have clear potential therapeutic implications. While the prevention studies conducted here may not accurately reflect a “real-life” scenario in which patients are treated after symptom onset, there is theoretically a long therapeutic window between the appearance of relatively benign skin findings in childhood and adolescence and more clinically-significant ocular and vascular findings that present in adulthood.27 Plasma PPi could be measured prior to LNP treatment, and since LNPs have been safely re-dosed in humans,19 the drug product could potentially be re-dosed until PPi levels are sufficiently restored, which may halt the ocular or vascular calcification that reduces quality of life in PXE patients.

More work is needed to determine the corrective editing threshold that is sufficient to prevent ectopic calcification, and to determine whether ectopic calcification can be reversed by liver-directed variant correction. We observed that even the mouse with only 15.8% whole-liver corrective editing (Figure 3A) exhibited full restoration of plasma PPi and complete prevention of muzzle calcification, suggesting that the editing threshold may be as low as 16%.

The work in this study could open the door to a new therapeutic modality for many PXE patients—not just for patients with the R1164X variant, but those with other editable pathogenic variants in the ABCC6 gene. Identical pathogenic variants in ABCC6 can also cause type 2 generalized arterial calcification of infancy (GACI, [MIM: 614473]), an ectopic mineralization disorder characterized by arterial calcification which often results in death within the first six months of life.28 Therefore, liver-directed, corrective adenine base editing of GACI-causing ABCC6 variants may be suitable as a treatment for this severe condition.

RNA production

100-mer gRNAs were chemically synthesized under solid phase synthesis conditions by a commercial supplier (Agilent or Integrated DNA Technologies) with end-modifications as well as heavy 2′-O-methylribosugar modification as previously described20: for cellular studies, 5′-mX∗mX∗mX∗XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXGUUUUAGAGCUAGAAAUAGCAAGUUAAAAUAAGGCUAGUCCGUUAUCAACUUGAAAAAGUGGCACCGAGUCGGUGCU∗U∗U∗U-3’; for mouse studies, 5′-mX∗mX∗mX∗XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXGUUUUAGAmGmCmUmAmGmAmAmAmUmAmGmCAAGUUAAAAUAAGGCUAGUCCGUUAUCAmAmCmUmUmGmAmAmAmAmAmGmUmGmGmCmAmCmCmGmAmGmUmCmGmGmUmGmCmU∗mU∗mU∗mU-3’; where the Xs indicate the spacer sequence positions (specified in Figure 2A), and “m” and ∗ respectively indicate 2′-O-methylation and phosphorothioate linkage. ABE8.8 mRNA was produced via in vitro transcription (IVT) and purification as previously described.19,20 In brief, a plasmid DNA template containing a codon-optimized ABE8.8 coding sequence and a 3′ polyadenylate sequence was linearized. An IVT reaction containing linearized DNA template, T7 RNA polymerase, NTPs, and cap analog was performed to produce mRNA containing N1-methylpseudouridine. After digestion of the DNA template with DNase I, the mRNA product underwent purification and buffer exchange, and the purity of the final mRNA product was assessed with spectrophotometry and capillary gel electrophoresis. Elimination of double-stranded RNA contaminants was assessed using dot blots and transfection into human dendritic cells. Endotoxin content was measured using a chromogenic Limulus amebocyte lysate (LAL) assay; all assays were negative.

LNP formulation

LNPs were formulated as previously described,20 with the lipid components (SM-102, 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, cholesterol, and PEG-2000 at molar ratios of 50:10:38.5:1.5) being rapidly mixed with an aqueous buffer solution containing ABE8.8 mRNA and a gRNA in a 1:1 ratio by weight in 25 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.0), with an N:P ratio of 5.6. The resulting LNP formulations were subsequently dialyzed against sucrose-containing buffer, concentrated using Amicon Ultra-15 mL Centrifugal Filter Units (Millipore Sigma), sterile-filtered using 0.2-μm filters, and frozen until use. The LNPs underwent quality control for particle size (Z-Ave, hydrodynamic diameter), polydispersity index as determined by dynamic light scattering (Malvern NanoZS Zetasizer) and total RNA encapsulation as measured by the Quant-iT Ribogreen Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Next-generation sequencing

NGS was performed as previously described.20 For targeted amplicon sequencing of the R1164X variant, PCR reactions were performed using NEBNext Polymerase (NEB) using the primer sets listed below and designed with Primer3 v4.1.0 (https://primer3.ut.ee/). The following program was used for all genomic DNA PCRs: 98°C for 20 s, 35× (98°C for 20 s, 57°C for 30 s, 72°C for 10 s), 72°C for 2 min. PCR products were visualized via capillary electrophoresis (QIAxcel, QIAGEN) and then purified and normalized via an NGS Normalization 96-Well Kit (Norgen Biotek Corporation). A secondary barcoding PCR was conducted to add Illumina barcodes (Nextera XT Index Kit V2 Set A and/or Nextera XT Index Kit V2 Set D) using ≈15 ng of first-round PCR product as template, followed by purification and normalization. Final pooled libraries were quantified using a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then after denaturation, dilution to 10 pM, and upon supplementation with 15% PhiX, underwent single-end or paired-end sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq System. The amplicon sequencing data were analyzed with CRISPResso2 v229 and scripts to quantify editing. For on-target editing, A-to-G editing was quantified at the site of the target adenine and at the site(s) of each potential bystander adenine in the editing window.

Mouse studies

Humanized PXE mice homozygous for the ABCC6 R1164X allele on the C57BL/6J background were generated as littermates/colonymates with heterozygous and wild-type C57BL/6J mice as previously described.22 Mice were maintained on a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, with a temperature range of 65°F–75°F and a humidity range of 40%–60%, Mice were fed ad libitum with a standard chow diet (LabDiet, Laboratory Autoclavable Rodent Diet 5010), or with an experimental diet with altered mineral composition compared to the standard chow diet (Inotiv, rodent diet TD.00442, Madison, WI, USA). Genotyping was performed using PCR amplification from genomic DNA samples (prepared from clipped tails) followed by Sanger sequencing or NGS. Roughly equal numbers of female and male littermates/colonymates were used for experiments at ages ranging from five to 18 weeks, with random assignment of animals to various experimental groups when applicable, and with collection and analysis of data performed in a blinded fashion when possible. LNPs were administered to mice at a dose of 2.5 mg/kg via retro-orbital injection under anesthesia with 1%–2% inhaled isoflurane. Blood samples were collected via the tail tip prior to treatment at five weeks of age, or at time of sacrifice, at 18 weeks of age. Mice were euthanized 13 weeks after treatment (at 18 weeks of age), and eight liver samples (two from each lobe) and one muzzle sample were obtained from each LNP-treated mouse on necropsy and processed with the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (QIAGEN) as per the manufacturer’s instructions to isolate genomic DNA. Additional sections of liver tissue were frozen in OCT blocks for immunofluorescence. Muzzle samples were obtained on necropsy and either fixed in 10% formalin for micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) scanning or flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen to measure calcium content. Euthanasia in all instances was achieved via terminal inhalation of carbon dioxide followed by secondary euthanasia through cervical dislocation or decapitation, consistent with the 2020 American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines on Euthanasia. NGS results from the liver samples were averaged to provide quantification of whole-liver editing.

Measurement of plasma PPi

Plasma samples collected pre-treatment and 13 weeks post-treatment were diluted 1:1 in Tris-acetate pH 8.0 and centrifuged with a 30K filter (OD030C34, Cytiva) at 14,000 × g at 4°C for 20 min. The PPi concentrations were measured in platelet-free plasma using ATP sulfurylase to convert pyrophosphate into ATP in the presence of excess adenosine 5′ phosphosulfate followed by a luminescent assay of ATP, as described previously.25

Immunofluorescence of ABCC6 protein

One liver sample from each mouse was frozen in OCT and cut into 5-micron sections. Slides were blocked with blocking buffer (2% BSA in PBST 0.05% Tween) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody (rabbit polyclonal ABCC6, Aviva Systems Biology, San Diego, CA #OACD00728, 1:25 dilution in blocking buffer) was added to the slides at 4°C overnight, and one section of each slide was incubated in blocking buffer without primary antibody as a no-antibody control. Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA #A-21206, 1:400 dilution) was added to the sections for 1.5 h at room temperature in the dark. DAPI-Fluoromount G (Southern Biotech #0100-20) was added to sections and mounted on coverslips. 40X images with 2.0X digital zoom were acquired with a Keyence microscope (model number BZ-X810) as Z-stacks (pitch of z stack = 0.4 μm, range = 4.5 μm, 13 single-plane images per z stack). For each plane of the z stack, a single-channel image in the GFP channel (corresponding to ABCC6 staining), a single-channel image in the blue channel (corresponding to DAPI), and an overlay image of the two channels was taken. Images within each channel of each experiment were taken at the same exposure and gain. Z-stacks were merged by Full Focus within Keyence software. The mean intensity value of each Full Focus z stack was measured in FiJi by batch processing.30

Micro-computed tomography scanning of muzzle biopsies

Micro-CT scanning was done as previously described.25 Left-side muzzle skin biopsies were fixed in 10% formalin followed by micro-CT using a SkyScan 1275 scanner (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA). Scans were acquired at source voltage of 55 kV, source current of 181 μA, and image pixel size of 15 μm. The calcified tissue mass for each sample was calculated using CTAn’s three-dimensional (3D) analysis function. Voxel intensities between grayscale values of 45–255 were classified as mineralized, which distinguishes minerals from soft tissue.

Calcium content assay

Quantification of calcium content within muzzle tissue was done as previously described.25 Right-side muzzle skin biopsies were decalcified with 1.0 M HCl. The content of solubilized calcium was measured by a colorimetric assay (Stanbio Laboratory, Boerne, TX, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical tests were done in GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
