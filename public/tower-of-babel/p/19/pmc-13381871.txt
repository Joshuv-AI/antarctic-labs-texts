Bridging quantum noise and classical electrodynamics with stochastic methods
Authors: Felix Hitzelhammer, Johannes Stowasser, Lukas Hanschke, Katarina Boos, Tobias C. Sutter, Michael Haider, Christian Jirauschek, Kai Müller, Gabriela Slavcheva, Ulrich Hohenester
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of emerging technologies in quantum optics demands accurate models that faithfully capture genuine quantum effects. Mature semiclassical approaches reach their limits when confronted with quantized electromagnetic fields, while full Hilbert space treatments are often computationally prohibitive. To address these challenges, we develop a framework based on coupled stochastic processes with a common cross-covariance structure that can be easily coupled to various types of Maxwell solvers. Our approach accounts for the non-commutativity in the quantum-to-classical transition in a natural way, and has the ability to capture quantum optical signatures while retaining compatibility with classical electromagnetics. For benchmarking, we compare our simulation results with experimental emission spectra of a strongly driven InGaAs quantum dot, finding excellent agreement. Our results highlight the potential of tailored stochastic processes for simulating non-classical light in complex photonic environments.

Introduction

Modeling light–matter interactions on the nanoscale across different architectures in quantum photonics is essential for advancing the design and fabrication of the key components of many emerging quantum technologies1–5. Prominent examples include quantum communications, photonic quantum computing, and high-precision quantum sensing6–9. Classical electromagnetic solvers based on Maxwell’s equations provide a powerful framework for simulating complex nanostructures10–12, but they are inherently restricted to classical light propagation and cannot capture non-classical features such as photon correlations or entanglement. These phenomena require quantum simulation approaches, typically formulated through (quantum) master equations or related quantum-optical models implemented on classical computers. However, these models often describe idealized systems and cannot account for propagation and quantum interference effects in realistic extended macroscopic devices and circuits with different materials, geometries, and boundary conditions. The latter is of particular importance for the design and optimization of the key components of quantum-photonic technologies, e.g., quantum emitters, quantum memories, and repeaters, etc. There is currently a lack of design, modeling, and simulation tools for quantum photonics that would allow predicting and optimizing the quantum performance of such realistic devices. Achieving peak quantum performance of the individual quantum-photonic components is paramount for the realization of the quantum photonic integrated circuits on a chip. Integrated architectures and mass production of consistently highly-performant components, by common consent, represent the way forward to achieving scalability. A unified approach that bridges quantum and classical descriptions is therefore highly desirable not solely from a long-standing foundational point of view13–15 but also for the practical realization of quantum technologies.

Quantum descriptions such as the Gorini-Kossakowski-Sudarshan-Lindblad formalism16,17 or quantum stochastic differential equations18 are elegant and fundamental. However, they do not provide access to the spatial and temporal properties of the electromagnetic field, nor do they offer a natural route for coupling to classical Maxwell solvers. One could instead rely on quantum electrodynamics methods, which quantize the electromagnetic field, but these approaches can quickly become computationally prohibitive for large or complex systems due to the exponential growth of the Hilbert space19,20. Furthermore, semiclassical approximations that allow coupling to electromagnetic solvers, e.g., based on the Maxwell-Bloch equations21,22 or more advanced Maxwell-Bloch Langevin approaches23–26, must be used with caution, as they cannot accurately capture quantum features such as non-classical photon statistics and correlations. This motivates the use of stochastic differential equations (SDEs), which are intrinsically driven by noise, to capture correlations, fluctuations, and noise spectra beyond average dynamics.

The derivation of suitable SDEs from the underlying quantum dynamics employs phase-space representations27–29, quantum trajectory methods30–32, or quantum Langevin equations33,34. Phase-space methods, such as the Wigner or positive P representations, map the density matrix onto a quasi-probability distribution, which is governed by a Fokker–Planck equation (FPE). By extracting drift and diffusion from this FPE, one obtains the corresponding SDE35–38. Quantum trajectory methods, also known as Monte Carlo wave-function approaches, describe the system in terms of individual stochastic realizations of its wavefunction. They naturally incorporate the randomness associated with quantum jumps, continuous monitoring, and measurement back-action30–32. Quantum Langevin equations offer an operator-level description of system-bath interactions. Under suitable approximations, such as linearization or semiclassical limits, they can be recast as SDEs for classical variables (c-numbers)14,33,34,39. Here, we introduce an SDE approach, without relying on wave-function unraveling, phase-space representations, or operator-valued noise.

As a first validation of our approach, we consider the Mollow spectrum, which serves as a compelling example of the successful interplay between theory and experiment40,41. It arises from the resonance fluorescence of a strongly driven two-level system (TLS)42, exhibiting a characteristic triplet structure that reflects coherent light–matter interactions. Excitonic transitions in solid-state systems, such as semiconductor quantum dots (QDs), can be modeled as effective TLSs, and the associated Mollow spectrum has been experimentally observed43,44. The Mollow spectrum not only benchmarks theoretical models but also illustrates the importance of capturing fluctuations and correlations in open quantum systems45. Beyond providing a benchmark for theoretical models, the sidebands in resonance fluorescence can exhibit non-classical photon correlations, highlighting the relevance of the Mollow triplet for quantum-optical control and photon-correlation experiments46,47. The Mollow triplet emission provides a promising approach for single-photon and sequential photon generation48, as well as for light–matter entangled-state generation44,49.

Motivated by the incompatibility between quantum simulations and Maxwell solvers, we introduce a stochastic Bloch-vector approach underpinned by SDEs, and validate it by comparison to dedicated experiments. This method links the system’s quantum dynamics to electromagnetic fields, providing a bridge between quantum emitter dynamics and spatio-temporally resolved classical fields. We show that SDEs can properly account for the dynamics of open quantum systems and offer the potential for integration with Maxwell solvers23,50,51. Finally, we argue that deriving a stochastic Bloch vector from a non-symmetric diffusion matrix addresses a critical gap in the stochastic modeling of systems subject to non-trivial noise processes.

Results

SDEs for symmetric vs. non-symmetric diffusion matrices

In the following, we introduce the concept of SDEs for non-symmetric diffusion matrices (see the central Eqs. (7) and (10)). To better motivate this innovation, we start by briefly summarizing the standard path from a (quantum) master equation to an SDE relying on an ordinary symmetric diffusion matrix (see Eqs. (1) and (2)). After fixing the operator ordering, the master equation can be mapped to a phase-space quasi-probability distribution by replacing quantum operators with corresponding differential operators33,52. From there, one derives an FPE, whose main building blocks are the drift matrix A, responsible for the deterministic evolution, and the diffusion matrix D, describing the stochastic fluctuations53. The system’s dynamics can be recast into two coupled linear SDEs, each driven by additive noise with a shared cross-covariance structure. We start by defining a non-symmetric diffusion matrix D as a linear combination of the second-order cumulant matrix M and the drift matrix A, 7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-{{\bf{D}}}={{\bf{A}}}{{\bf{M}}}+{{\bf{M}}}{{{\bf{A}}}}^{{{\rm{T}}}}\,,$$\end{document}−D=AM+MAT,which is reminiscent of an algebraic Lyapunov equation53. Emission spectrum of an InGaAs quantum dot

For model validation, the simulation results are compared to experimental measurements on an InGaAs QD, where the negatively charged exciton serves as a TLS. For the measurement, the sample is cooled to 4.2 K and integrated into a resonance fluorescence setup to separate the laser signal from the QD emission by cross-polarized filtering. The emission is recorded with a high-resolution scanning Fabry–Perot interferometer. Using our stochastic approach, we successfully reproduce the power-dependent emission spectrum of a monochromatic resonantly driven QD for moderate Rabi energies. The model also captures the behavior of the QD emission under varying laser detuning.

We first investigate how the emission spectrum evolves with increasing laser power. A continuous wave laser tuned in resonance with the QD transition at the energy E = 1362.0315 meV is used, and the excitation power is increased from 1 nW to 2.21 μW in quadratically increasing steps. Discussion

We demonstrate that allowing a non-symmetric diffusion matrix in the derivation of SDEs fills a critical gap in the modeling of non-trivial noise processes. As a quantum optical example showcasing the benefits of our approach, we accurately reproduce the power- and detuning-dependent emission spectrum of an InGaAs QD. Here, the resulting smooth transition from quantum to classical regimes guarantees compatibility with classical electromagnetic solvers.

The core of our formulation is a factorization of the non-symmetric diffusion matrix, which yields a doubled system of SDEs. In this work, we have employed SVDs for the factorization, but one could also use other schemes, such as matrix square roots, which might be beneficial for large matrices in order to speed up the simulations. The controlled distribution of noise contributions across these SDEs facilitates the encoding of correlated fluctuations that symmetric-diffusion models cannot represent. Conceptually, the non-symmetric diffusion matrix captures the consequences of operator non-commutativity in quantum mechanics. For the case of a TLS, which in our work is realized by an InGaAs QD, the system of SDEs may be viewed as a stochastic Bloch-vector description whose solutions can be injected into electromagnetic solvers via a source-current coupling. In doing so, it reproduces experimentally relevant spectral features, while retaining a possible path to device-scale modeling through the EM-coupling.

This is of particular interest because many Maxwell-Bloch solvers either neglect or approximate vacuum-induced fluctuations and therefore struggle to reproduce the incoherent part of the spectrum of a quantum emitter21–26. We are now able to naturally supply FDTD solvers with the missing quantum noise, which enables quantitative predictions of emission spectra in linear photonic environments without back action. While we here focus on the steady-state dynamics, the stochastic framework itself is not limited to steady-state conditions. Although our interface with FDTD does not yield a fully self-consistent simulation that includes the back-action on the quantum emitter, it can nevertheless model linear photonic environments that act as passive filters, such as optical cavities that transmit certain wavelengths and waveguides designed to pass specific modes.

Compared to prior stochastic approaches in quantum optics, most of which target ensembles of TLSs39,69, our approach addresses a single TLS without any approximations. Moreover, we anticipate no major obstacles standing in the way of an extension to multi-level systems using generalized Bloch vectors22,70,71.

Our derivation is currently established for two-time correlations. Many key observables in quantum optics fall into this category, and even second-order coherence can be handled when the required averages are recast in terms of two-time correlations42. Still, the extension to multi-time correlations and non-Gaussian noise processes is an attractive goal, since it enables the modeling of single photon emission72,73. In this work we have determined the noise matrices in Eq. (10) such that they lead to the proper equation of motion for the covariance matrix, see Eq. (46). We can speculate that along the same lines one could introduce higher-order noise contributions74 that have to be chosen such that they lead to the corresponding equations of motion for the higher-order correlation matrices, although further work is needed to work out the details.

In our current approach, we have introduced the relaxation and dephasing rates as parameters that have to be obtained from experiment or independent theoretical approaches. A particularly convenient one is the framework of macroscopic quantum electrodynamics75, which avoids the explicit quantization of electromagnetic fields by introducing material noise terms, and allows to derive optical decay rates in a first-principles manner. Quite generally, in the weak coupling regime, such rates could be easily obtained from supplementary FDTD simulations and introduced into our approach. In the strong-coupling regime, the mutual light–matter interaction has to be described explicitly76. Early work on Random Electrodynamics77,78 has shown that quantum effects in light–matter interactions can be mimicked through fluctuating classical fields, which has been used as the basis for the modern theory of the Casimir effect78. Although beyond the scope of this paper, we envision that within a related approach, the radiative decay of quantum emitters could be directly accounted for in FDTD through the consideration of the back-action of the emitted random fields on the quantum emitters. Future work should also address possible non-Markovian contributions in the dynamics of the quantum emitters, see ref. 20 for a detailed discussion.

From a computational perspective, our approach requires simulations for a sufficiently large number of stochastic noise realizations, which leads in general to significantly longer runtimes in comparison to more direct approaches. However, the major achievement of this work is the demonstration that genuine quantum effects can be modeled with classical FDTD solvers, despite the disbelief and reservations of the community. For example, a possible caveat is that the quantum-classical correspondence is believed to fail for certain limiting cases, such as the single two-level atom14. Considering that the approach holds potential for further improvements, such as the self-consistent treatment of light–matter interactions discussed above, and the fact that classical FDTD solvers can be employed without any major modification, we think that the overhead in computer time is acceptable, in particular in view of the fast performance of FDTD simulations and the possibility of parallelization. However, one can also envision scenarios where our approach in its present form can already outperform more traditional approaches. A new generation of spectroscopy and microscopy techniques relies on photon correlation measurements for multiple emitters, harnessing all possible kinds of spatial, temporal, and spectral correlations79. In a traditional approach, one first has to characterize the field propagators for all possible source and field positions as well as delay times, before convoluting them with the sources. In contrast, in our approach, the quantum properties of the sources are directly imprinted on the fields, and photon correlations can be computed from the simulated fields at different positions and times in a massively parallel fashion without explicitly establishing a link between sources and fields. In such setups, our approach appears to be superior to the traditional approaches, both regarding conceptual simplicity and computational speed.

Finally, despite the fact that our approach removes operator ordering on the quantum side, it relocates the choice to the classical post-processing stage. One must adopt a consistent ordering when mapping stochastic variables to observables. Notably, recent research has focused on filtering out the coherent and incoherent components of emission spectra, a task that our framework could support80–82.

In conclusion, our approach offers a practical route to quantitatively link quantum noise and classical fields, while also providing a general methodology for tailoring correlated noise in stochastic models beyond quantum optics.

Stochastic approach

We follow the standard theory of linear SDEs, matrix analysis, and Gaussian processes53,87–89. The main modification is a non-symmetric diffusion matrix. The second-order cumulants do not depend on any particular operator ordering. The non-commutativity of the operators involved is responsible for the non-symmetric matrix D. The equation of motion for the covariance matrix C is given by1446\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\partial }_{t}{{\bf{C}}}={{\bf{A}}}{{\bf{C}}}+{{\bf{C}}}{{{\bf{A}}}}^{{{\rm{T}}}}+{{\bf{D}}}\,,$$\end{document}∂tC=AC+CAT+D,where A and D are the drift and diffusion matrices, respectively. Experimental methods

Resonance fluorescence measurements were performed under resonant excitation of a single QD. The setup was designed to separate the reflected laser from the emission signal using cross-polarized filtering. The sample was cooled to 4.2 K in a liquid helium bath cryostat.

The sample was mounted on a piezoelectric positioning system, allowing 3D movement with 50 nm precision, and contacted via high-frequency connectors for voltage control of the Schottky diode. Excitation and collection were performed through a high-numerical-aperture (NA = 0.77) objective.

The optical head consisted of three stages: imaging, excitation, and detection. Excitation light, delivered via an optical fiber, was linearly polarized and reflected by a polarizing beamsplitter to illuminate the sample. Emitted resonance fluorescence photons were collected through the same objective, passed through the polarizing beamsplitter and additional polarizers, and coupled into a single-mode fiber for detection by a Fabry–Perot cavity. More precisely, the QD emission was detected with a scanning Fabry–Perot interferometer and a single-photon avalanche diode. A quarter-wave plate above the cryostat window corrected for polarization distortions, and the fiber coupling acted as a spatial filter to further suppress residual laser light.
