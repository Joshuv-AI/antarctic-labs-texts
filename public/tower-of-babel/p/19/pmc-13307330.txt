Recognition and Lysis of Capsulolabral Adhesions Following Hip Arthroscopy and Their Role in Labral Pathology
Authors: Nicholas A. Felan, Neil Blanchard, Tyler J. Uppstrom, Joseph Featherall, Keisuke Nakayama, Marc J. Philippon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCapsulolabral adhesions are a common cause of revision hip arthroscopy. Recent evidence suggests a rehabilitation protocol emphasizing early range of motion and circumduction supplemented by oral losartan may be beneficial to prevent adhesion formation. Despite best efforts, adhesions may still form and are often associated with labral pathology. The present technique shows the mechanism by which adhesions elicit labral pathology. Additionally, we share the senior author's preferred method for lysis of adhesions.

Capsulolabral adhesions are a common cause of revision hip arthroscopy. In this technique video, we will show the association of labral pathology with the presence of capsulolabral adhesions. Additionally, we will share our technique to adequately resect adhesions and successfully re‐establish the capsulolabral interval, which allows for labral refixation and appropriate excursion of the capsular tissue for repair and/or reconstruction. Notably, the presenting patient was an elite athlete who previously underwent hip arthroscopy for a labral tear and presented with recurrent hip pain. After establishment of anterolateral and midanterior portals, diagnostic arthroscopy of the hip was performed. Here, we identified the presence of an adhesion between the labrum and capsule—a capsulolabral adhesion—at the 9 o'clock position. Using an arthroscopic probe, tension was placed on the capsulolabral adhesion resulting in displacement of the labrum from the acetabulum. Here, we can appreciate the presence of recurrent labral tearing as a result of the capsulolabral adhesion. It's important to note that the patient's prior labral tear was identified and repaired from the 11 to 3 o'clock position, such that this new tear was in a unique position, directly corresponding to the location of the adhesion. Next, a radiofrequency probe was inserted into the hip joint and used to separate the capsule from the labrum. It is important to accurately identify and develop the capsulolabral interval to ensure adequate removal of the adhesion to (1) allow for labral refixation when indicated and (2) allow appropriate capsular excursion for subsequent repair or reconstruction, without damaging the nearby native labral tissue. Dissection of the capsulolabral interval was continued from the 9 to 1 o'clock positions until complete re‐establishment of the interval was achieved. Next, our attention was turned to the 1 o'clock position where a capsulolabral adhesion had formed at the site of the patient's previous labral tear repair. In a similar fashion, a radiofrequency probe was carefully inserted into the hip joint and used to re‐establish the capsulolabral interval indicated by the orange arrows. Dissection of the capsulolabral interval was meticulously continued more medially and laterally until re‐establishment of the interval was achieved. Additionally, frayed labral tissue was debrided. Here, we can appreciate adequate re‐establishment of the capsulolabral interval following complete lysis of adhesions. After re‐establishing the capsulolabral interval, labral refixation can be performed in accordance with the surgeon's preference. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70068.

Hip arthroscopy has seen an increase in utilization in response to an increasing number of indications over the last few decades. As the number of primary hip arthroscopy procedures being performed increases, so do the incidence of complications and patients requiring revision surgery. 1

The most common causes for revision arthroscopic surgery are residual impingement, femoral neck over‐resection, capsule or labral deficiency, and the presence of capsulolabral adhesions. 1 , 2 Capsulolabral adhesions may form secondary to an unregulated healing response and can result in postoperative pain and decreased hip range of motion. 2 Although the exact etiology is unknown, adhesions are often associated with revision surgeries, younger patients, and patients with limited postoperative motion or lack of circumduction. 1 , 3 The aim of the present technical note is to show the mechanism by which adhesions between the capsule and the labrum may lead to a labral injury via a shearing mechanism. Additionally, we will share the senior author's pearls to prevent adhesion formation, as well as the surgical treatment and postoperative management used to treat and prevent this complication (Table 1).

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Indications

Patients indicated for arthroscopic lysis of adhesions include patients who have undergone prior hip arthroscopy with recurrent pain, range of motion restrictions, and/or mechanical symptoms with evidence of intracapsular adhesions on magnetic resonance imaging with or without recurrent labral tearing.

Patient Positioning and Traction

The patient is placed in a supine position on a traction table (Steris/Amsco, Mentor, OH). After induction of general anesthesia, a bilateral lower extremity examination is performed to assess hip range of motion. The patient's feet are then placed into foam boots and an extra‐wide, well‐padded perineal post is placed lateralized toward the ipsilateral side in the perineal space to minimize pressure on the pudendal nerve. Traction is gently applied to the leg with 15° of internal rotation, 10° of lateral tilt, 10° of flexion, and neutral abduction. Appropriate distraction of the hip joint is confirmed via ultrasonography.

Procedure

Standard anterolateral and midanterior portals are created (Figure 1). The patient's prior incisions are used if appropriately positioned. Using a 17‐gauge needle, the hip joint is aspirated, and fluid cultures are sent per the authors’ standard protocol for revision hip arthroscopy. A diagnostic arthroscopy is completed with a 70° arthroscope (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) to evaluate the labrum, capsular integrity, articular cartilage, and location of the capsulolabral adhesions. An interportal capsulotomy measuring approximately 25 mm is performed, with care taken to preserve the iliofemoral ligament medially.

Diagram of left hip and anterolateral and midanterior portal placement. Green dot represents midanterior portal; blue dot represents anterolateral portal.

An arthroscopic probe is used to evaluate the capsulolabral adhesions and the integrity of the labral attachment. As shown in Video 1, the capsulolabral adhesions may result in recurrent labral tearing at the site of prior repair, or in previously intact locations. In the case presented, the patient showed adhesions at the 1 o'clock position, a location where his labrum was previously repaired, and at the 9 o'clock position, a location where his labrum was noted to be intact during his initial arthroscopy (Figure 2). Using the probe, placing tension on the capsulolabral adhesions results in elevation of the labrum from the acetabulum in these locations (Video 1). Next, a narrow radiofrequency probe (EFLEX Ligament Chisel; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is used to elevate the capsule from the labrum via lysis of capsulolabral adhesions. A key technical point is to clearly identify native labral tissue either medially or laterally to the adhesion, as the capsulolabral interval is more readily apparent. Dissection of the capsulolabral adhesions is then carried medially and laterally, to ensure this interval is developed circumferentially around the labrum.

Arthroscopic images of the left hip showing capsulolabral adhesions suspected to have led to the labral tear as viewed from the midanterior portal with the patient in the supine position. (A) Adhesion formation at the site of prior repair at the 1 o'clock position. (B) Adhesion formation at a new labral tear site at the 9 o'clock position.

If there is a recurrent or new tearing of the labrum in the locations of the capsulolabral adhesions, repair of the labrum is performed. Gentle acetabuloplasty is performed in these locations to create a bony bed for anchor placement. A 2.3 mm Osteoraptor (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is placed, and the suture is passed in a pierced fashion through the labrum and arthroscopic knots are tied. After repair of the labrum, additional concomitant procedures are performed as indicated and are beyond the scope of this technical note.

After all intra‐articular procedures are completed, the operative leg is released from traction and restoration of the suction seal is confirmed. 4 , 5 Additional peripheral compartment procedures (including osteoplasty, capsular closure/reconstruction, etc.) are performed as indicated and are beyond the scope of this technical note. Prior to capsulotomy closure, the excursion, quality, and quantity of capsular tissue is evaluated with an arthroscopic grasper. If the capsular tissue is unable to be closed primarily with appropriate tension, capsular reconstruction is performed, as previously described. 6

Postoperative Protocol

Postoperative rehabilitation includes 50% flat foot weight‐bearing for the first 10 to 14 days following surgery (unless a microfracture is performed, in which case patients are 20 lbs flat foot weight‐bearing for 6 weeks following surgery). Continuous passive motion is used for 4 to 6 h per day for 4 weeks postoperatively. Patients are placed in a hinged hip brace for 2 weeks postoperatively to limit extension and external rotation and use an antirotation bolster at night for 2 weeks postoperatively. Physical therapy begins on postoperative day 1 with a focus on passive motion, followed by active motion, and finally strengthening. Hip circumduction exercises are performed several times daily to prevent recurrent adhesions. In addition to the postoperative rehabilitation protocol, patients are prescribed losartan 12.5 mg to be taken twice daily for 12 weeks postoperatively, as prior studies have suggested losartan to be an effective treatment for arthrofibrosis. 3

DISCUSSION

The present article shows a technique for arthroscopically treating dense, painful capsulolabral adhesions. The key technical goals of this procedure are preserving the labral and capsular tissue while appropriately restoring the capsulolabral interval. In addition, postoperative care should focus on the minimization of the risk of fibrosis and adhesion reformation. The images and video presented here provide an example of thick, strong fibrous tissue leading to elevation of the labrum away from the femoral head and subsequent disruption of the suction seal. In this case, the adhesions were so dense that the frequent forceful eversion of the labrum with hip flexion led to recurrent labral tearing. Thus, adhesions may not only lead to a decreased range of motion and pain but also to recurrent labral tearing.

The increasing prevalence of hip arthroscopy surgery has led to an increased need in the treatment of postoperative complications and persistent pain after hip arthroscopy. 7 Causes of pain after hip arthroscopy may include residual cam deformity from under‐resection, instability from cam or pincer over resection, retearing of the labrum, cartilage defects, previously unaddressed instability, rotational abnormalities, and development of intra‐articular adhesions. There is increasing focus on intra‐articular adhesions as a common source of pain after hip arthroscopy; however, diagnostic criteria and data on their frequency and mechanisms of pain generation remain somewhat limited. 8 , 9

Intra‐articular adhesions as a cause of pain after hip surgery were initially described after both open and arthroscopic treatment of femoroacetabular impingement. 10 , 11 In 16 consecutive patients undergoing hip arthroscopy for persistent pain after surgical hip dislocation without obvious osseous or cartilaginous causes of pain, all patients had adhesions between the capsule and either the labrum or the head neck junction. Arthroscopic release of these adhesions showed improvement in these patients; however, diagnosis can be difficult and there is not yet a unified definition for symptomatic adhesions. 10 , 11

A number of strategies have been described to treat capsulolabral adhesions and minimize the risk of their formation. Specific protocols for both early active and passive postoperative range of motion, with a focus on circumduction, have been developed to minimize adhesion formation. 3 The use of antifibrotic agents, such as losartan, in early reports have been shown to decrease revision rates and theoretically decrease the risk of adhesion formation. 12 , 13 Minimal dissection of the capsule labral junction at the time of initial arthroscopy has also been shown to decrease adhesion formation as a cause of revision surgery. 14 We recommend the described technique to all hip preservation surgeons performing lysis of adhesions in the revision setting.
