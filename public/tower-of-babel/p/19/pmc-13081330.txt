Reframing disease burden: validation of DALY-per-case as a per-diagnosis severity metric
Authors: Omar Freihat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPopulation-weighted metrics (incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), mortality to incidence ratio (MIR) can obscure per-case severity for less prevalent but high-impact conditions. This paper introduces DALY per case, total DALYs divided by incident cases, as a standardized estimate of healthy life-years lost per new diagnosis, integrating years of life lost (YLL) and years lived with disability (YLD). Validated using cancers and applied across diverse diseases, the metric enables prevalence-independent severity comparisons.MethodsUsing GBD 2021, we computed DALY per case across diseases (all ages, both sexes), validated on 34 cancers, and tested generalizability in five non-cancer conditions (type 2-diabetes, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, ischemic heart disease, Alzheimer’s). We compared rankings with incidence, mortality, and total DALYs. A 2-Dimensional framework plotted total DALYs (population burden) vs. DALY-per-case (individual severity) with median-based quadrant thresholds. Uncertainty intervals (UIs) were propagated per GBD conventions; stability was assessed via relative UI width, band-crossing, and sensitivity analyses. Construct/convergent validity used correlations with 5-year survival Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Program (SEER) and MIR; full and reduced regressions tested independence.ResultsHigh-severity cancers included malignant bone tumours (27.6 DALYs/case), neuroblastoma (26.3), and brain/CNS (24.9), contrasting with population-dominant burdens such as lung (46.5 million DALYs; 20.4/case) and colorectal (24.4 million; 11.1/case). Relative uncertainty spanned 27% (breast) to 96% (Hodgkin lymphoma); rankings were largely preserved despite wide UIs in select sites. DALY-per-case correlated inversely with 5-year survival (r=-0.72, p < 0.001) and positively with MIR (r = 0.75, p < 0.001). In regression, MIR showed the strongest effect (β = 0.52, p = 0.06); survival lost significance when MIR was included, indicating shared but non-redundant variance.ConclusionsDALY-per-case provides a disease-agnostic toolkit, including a 2Dimensional burden-severity framework and validation against existing indicators, to quantify per-diagnosis severity and inform policy across communicable and non-communicable diseases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12963-026-00469-2.

Introduction

The global burden of disease, spanning communicable and non-communicable conditions, reached 2.8 billion DALYs in 2021, with projections of increase due to demographic shifts [1]. This underscores the need for complementary metrics that better quantify individual-level severity alongside population burden to guide prevention and research. Current indicators such as incidence, mortality, DALYs, and MIR, focus on population trends but often mask per-case severity across diverse diseases [2].

Established metrics like incidence and mortality highlight high-burden conditions [2], while DALYs integrate YLLs and YLDs [1]. MIR, calculated as mortality divided by incidence, proxies survival and healthcare performance but lacks YLD detail [3]. These population-weighted measures can obscure individual severity, necessitating a new approach.

Because these measures are population-weighted and dominated by high-prevalence conditions, per-case severity may be obscured [1]. As an illustration from oncology, rarer cancers can impose substantial individual burden despite modest aggregate DALYs [1, 4, 5]. MIR, while a crude survival proxy, omits disability detail and struggles to reflect prognosis heterogeneity [2, 3]. Clinicians’ observed severity gradients (e.g., rapid vs. indolent courses) underscore the need for a standardized, prevalence-independent severity measure across diseases [6].

Building on prior uses in infectious disease epidemiology [7, 8], this study validates DALY-per-case, defined as total DALYs divided by incidence, estimating per-diagnosis healthy life years lost by integrating mortality and disability. Although YLDs are commonly presented as prevalence-based quantities, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) estimation framework links incidence, duration, remission, and mortality through compartmental disease modelling to ensure internal consistency between incidence and prevalence (9,30,36,31). Within this structure, DALYs are computed as the sum of YLLs and YLDs, representing the total health loss attributable to disease. Under these modelling assumptions, particularly in relatively stable epidemiologic conditions, total DALYs approximate the expected lifetime health loss attributable to incident cohorts. Consequently, dividing total DALYs by incident cases provides an estimate of the average expected healthy life-years lost per new diagnosis [9–11]. Unlike frequency-based incidence and mortality, prevalence-weighted DALYs, or MIR’s survival proxy, DALY-per-case quantifies individual burden, enabling severity comparisons across diseases.

Importantly, DALY-per-case is not introduced as a fundamentally new burden construct, but as a standardized per-incident normalization of established DALY components within the GBD framework. Whereas DALYs per capita reflect societal burden and indicators such as case-fatality risk (CFR) or MIR approximate lethality, DALY-per-case quantifies the average expected healthy life-years lost per new diagnosis at the population level by integrating both fatal (YLL/I) and non-fatal (YLD/I) components. Its contribution lies in formal validation, theoretical positioning within the GBD structure, and development of an interpretive framework that separates intrinsic per-diagnosis severity from overall population burden.

Comparative epidemiology increasingly requires metrics beyond infectious settings [12, 13]. Traditional indicators like case-fatality rate or YLL per case focus on mortality, while DALYs, combining fatal and non-fatal outcomes, can still obscure per-case severity due to population-weighting [14, 15]. This drives the need for individual-level measures, with DALY-per-case integrating YLD and YLL per diagnosis, aligning with GBD calls for unified survival-morbidity metrics across conditions [1, 14, 16].

The significance of DALY-per-case lies in complementing existing indicators, extending burden-of-disease concepts from foodborne and infectious diseases [17, 18]. Epidemiologically, it ranks conditions by severity and impact on life expectancy and quality, highlighting high-severity, lower-prevalence entities that contribute disproportionate individual burden [19, 20]. It can also track therapeutic progress sensitively, revealing per-case burden reductions obscured by aggregate DALYs [21]. Clinically, it aligns epidemiological data with clinicians’ severity observations, aiding research advocacy [22]. For policy, it identifies conditions with high lethality and disability [23].

By filling the gap in per-case severity quantification, DALY-per-case reframes epidemiology from a purely population-driven to a multidimensional perspective, integrating both prevalence and individual severity. This disease-agnostic approach has the potential to sharpen research priorities, align clinical and epidemiological perspectives, and inform more equitable strategies to address the projected rise in global disease burdens.

Methods

Study design

This population-based descriptive study evaluates DALY-per-case as a per-incident severity indicator defined as the average number of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost per new diagnosis. The metric was computed globally across cancer sites, as a validation group, and compared with established measures including incidence, mortality, total DALYs, and the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR). DALY-per-case represents an aggregate, population-based estimate of expected health loss per incident case and does not imply measurement of individual patient-level severity.

Data sources

Both incidence and DALY estimates were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study conducted by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), (14). Incidence represented new cancer cases, and DALYs were the sum of years of life lost (YLLs) and years lived with disability (YLDs). For each cancer type, global totals (all ages, both sexes) for incidence, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) were extracted. Cancer categories followed GBD classifications aligned with ICD-10 definitions.

Total DALYs, YLL, and YLD are expressed in absolute years. Incidence is expressed as number of new cases. Accordingly, DALY-per-case is expressed in units of years lost per incident case. No rate standardization (per 100,000 population) was applied in the derivation of DALY-per-case, as the metric represents an average per diagnosis rather than a population rate.

where “I” represents incident cases. enabling decomposition of per-case burden changes into survival-related versus morbidity-related contributions.

Uncertainty propagation

Uncertainty intervals for DALY-per-case were derived from published 95% uncertainty bounds for DALYs and incidence from GBD 2021. Because posterior draw-level data were not available for recomputation of derived ratios, conservative bound division was applied.

where DALYL and, DALYU denote lower and upper bounds of total DALYs; IL and IU denote lower and upper bounds of incidence.

This method preserves conservative coverage and may yield wider intervals than simulation-based propagation using posterior draws.

Metric definition and scope of application

Using IHME GBD 2021 global totals (all ages, both sexes), DALY-per-case was computed across cancer sites as the average expected healthy life-years lost per incident diagnosis at the population level. The interpretation of DALY-per-case relies on the assumption that, within the GBD modeling framework, total DALYs approximate the expected lifetime health loss attributable to incident cases under relatively stable epidemiologic conditions. This assumption reflects the internal consistency of GBD’s compartmental modeling linking incidence, mortality, duration, and disability weights.

DALY-per-case is intended for comparative population-level epidemiology within the standardized GBD framework. It is appropriate for cross-disease severity ranking, temporal trend analysis, and policy prioritization at national or global scales. It does not represent individual-level clinical severity and should not be interpreted as a prognostic measure for specific patients. Application outside standardized GBD case definitions or in data-sparse settings requires cautious interpretation and, where possible, local validation.

Validation data

To assess construct and convergent validity, we used indicators from the SEER program (National Cancer Institute, USA). Age-adjusted incidence (2018–2022) and mortality (2019–2023) from SEER Explorer informed mortality-to-incidence ratios (MIR = mortality ÷ incidence). Five-year relative survival by cancer site was extracted from SEER [24], and when available, checked against CONCORD-3 [25]. Median age at diagnosis from SEER captured demographic variation across sites. These SEER-based parameters were aligned with global DALY and incidence estimates from IHME GBD 2021 to ensure consistency between burden- and survival-based severity frameworks. Validation used cancer as the primary exemplar due to availability of independent survival benchmarks enabling construct validation. To assess generalizability, five non-cancer diseases were selected to represent distinct epidemiologic and clinical patterns, including communicable and non-communicable conditions, acute and chronic trajectories, and varying mortality–morbidity compositions. This structured selection allowed evaluation of DALY-per-case performance across heterogeneous disease profiles within the GBD framework.

This disease-agnostic formulation extends prior work by introducing (i) a quadrant-based 2D framework with median thresholds for total DALYs and DALY-per-case to visualize/classify burdens, and (ii) regression models testing independence from MIR and survival. Validation used cancer as the exemplar condition given availability of survival benchmarks. To assess generalizability beyond oncology, five non-cancer diseases were selected to represent distinct epidemiologic and clinical profiles: tuberculosis (communicable, potentially curable infection with substantial YLL contribution), HIV/AIDS (chronic infectious disease with prolonged disability and mortality), ischemic heart disease (high-mortality non-communicable condition), type 2 diabetes mellitus (high-prevalence chronic metabolic disease with dominant YLD contribution), and Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias (neurodegenerative condition characterized by prolonged disability and lower acute mortality). This spectrum enabled evaluation of DALY-per-case performance across diseases with differing mortality-morbidity compositions.

Analysis

DALY-per-case was computed for major cancers as mean years lost per case with 95% UIs. Cancers were ranked by DALY-per-case and compared with rankings by incidence, mortality, and total DALYs. To improve visualization of the highly skewed distribution of population burden, total DALYs were log-transformed prior to plotting. Log-transformed total DALYs (population burden; x-axis) were plotted against DALY-per-case (individual severity; y-axis), yielding four quadrants: high-high, high-low, low-high, and low-low. Median-based thresholds (total DALYs: 10 million; DALY-per-case: 18) were derived empirically from the distribution and stress-tested (± 20%) for stability. For uncertainty interpretation and categorical comparison, severity bands were defined on the continuous DALY-per-case scale (years per incident case). Based on distributional clustering of observed values, three interval domains were specified: low severity (< 10 years per case), moderate severity (10–20 years per case), and high severity (> 20 years per case). These thresholds were empirically derived to facilitate interpretability rather than reflect externally imposed clinical cutoffs, consistent with established methodological approaches for categorizing continuous epidemiologic measures when no predefined severity standards exist [26, 27].

Stability classification was determined by the position of the 95% uncertainty interval (UI) relative to these predefined interval domains. A cancer was classified as “stable” within a severity category when the point estimate and most of the UI remained within a single interval domain. Cancers were classified as “variable” when the UI substantially crossed adjacent severity thresholds, indicating instability in categorical placement. Interpretation of uncertainty intervals followed established Global Burden of Disease methodological standards [9, 11]. Relative UI width was calculated as (upper-lower)/mean and expressed as percent variability; across cancers, relative UI widths.

Construct validity was assessed using Pearson’s correlation between DALY-per-case and 5-year relative survival (SEER). Because DALY-per-case values were right-skewed, logarithmic transformation was applied prior to correlation analysis to better approximate normality and satisfy parametric assumptions.

To assess convergent validity and redundancy with mortality-based indices, we fit multiple linear regressions with DALY-per-case as the outcome and MIR, 5-year survival, and median age at diagnosis as predictors. Multicollinearity was evaluated via variance inflation factors (VIFs). We then ran partial correlations to test independence: (a) DALY-per-case with survival controlling for MIR, and (b) DALY-per-case with MIR controlling for survival.

Results

Two-dimensional burden analysis

A scatter plot of DALY-per-case vs. total DALYs classified 34 cancers into four quadrants via median thresholds for population burden (10 million total DALYs; x-axis) and individual severity (18 DALYs/case; y-axis) (Fig. 1). The upper-right quadrant (high burden-high severity) included tracheal, bronchus, and lung cancer (46.5 million; 20.4), stomach (22.8 million; 18.5), liver (12.9 million; 24.4), and pancreatic (11.3 million; 22.3). The lower-right quadrant (high burden-moderate severity, 6–11 DALYs/case) comprised breast (20.6 million; 9.7), colorectal (24.4 million; 11.1), and prostate (8.1 million; 6.1). The upper-left quadrant (low burden < 10 million; high severity) included brain and central nervous system (CNS) cancers (8.9 million; 24.9), leukemia (10.9 million; 23.8), malignant neoplasms of bone and articular cartilage (2.5 million; 27.6), and mesothelioma (0.7 million; 21.6). The lower-left quadrant (low burden; low severity < 6 DALYs/case) included thyroid (1.2 million; 5.0), testicular (0.6 million; 6.1), and non-melanoma skin cancers (1.2 million; 0.2). This quadrant-based 2D framework reveals divergences in disease burdens, illustrated by brain/CNS (high severity, low population impact and extends as a visualization toolkit for comparative epidemiology across conditions, as shown for tuberculosis (5.6 DALYs/case) and diabetes (3.2).

Cancer rankings by burden and severity

Cancers were ranked by total DALYs and by DALY-per-case to clarify population vs. individual burden (Table 1). By total DALYs, tracheal/bronchus/lung ranked 1 st (46.5 M), followed by colorectal (24.4 M), stomach (22.8 M), and breast (20.6 M), reflecting high incidence and mortality. In contrast, DALY-per-case emphasized per-case severity: malignant neoplasms of bone/articular cartilage (27.6; rank 1), neuroblastoma/other peripheral nervous cell tumors (26.3; rank 2), and brain/CNS (24.9; rank 3), despite lower total-DALY positions (23rd, 34th, 10th). Notable divergences included breast (4th by total DALYs; 26th by DALY-per-case) and prostate (11th; 28th), indicating high population burden but lower per-case severity, versus brain/CNS (10th; 3rd), indicating high individual severity. Lung (1st in total DALYs; 10th in DALY-per-case), liver (6th; 4th), pancreatic (7th; 7th), and stomach (3rd; 11th) ranked highly across both dimensions, underscoring dual burden.

Uncertainty analysis

Incorporation of 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) confirmed the stability of DALY-per-case rankings (Table 2). High-severity cancers, including malignant neoplasms of bone and articular cartilage (DALYS-per-case 27.6 years; 95% uncertainty interval (UI): 20.1–38.5), neuroblastoma (26.3; 16.8–41.2), and brain/CNS cancers (24.9; 18.7–33.4), consistently ranked among the top across UI ranges. Low-severity cancers, such as thyroid (DALYS-per-case 5.0 years; uncertainty interval (UI): 4.0–6.2.0.2), prostate (6.1; 5.1–7.2), and breast (9.7; 8.5–11.1), remained stable at the lower end. Mesothelioma (21.6; 18.7–25.0) and pancreatic cancer (22.3; 19.1–26.3) also maintained high-severity positions. Cancers with wider UIs, such as neuroblastoma and Hodgkin lymphoma (18.4; 11.0–28.8.0.8), showed variability but retained high severity. These results demonstrate the robustness of DALY-per-case rankings to statistical uncertainty.

Correlation with 5-year survival

To assess alignment with cancer prognosis, DALY-per-case estimates were compared with 5-year relative survival rates from population-based cancer registries (SEER) across available cancer types, excluding categories lacking survival data (other malignant neoplasms, other neoplasms, other pharynx cancer, neuroblastoma) (Table 3). A strong inverse Pearson correlation was observed between DALY-per-case and 5-year survival (r = − 0.72) (Fig. 2), indicating that cancers associated with lower survival tended to incur higher average healthy life-years lost per diagnosis.

Cancers with poor survival, such as pancreatic cancer (22.3 DALYs per case; 13.5% survival), esophageal cancer (22.5; 23.5%), liver cancer (24.4; 22.8%), and lung cancer (20.4; 32.6%), exhibited high DALY-per-case values. Conversely, cancers with favorable survival, including thyroid cancer (5.0; 98.9%), prostate cancer (6.1; 97.9%), testicular cancer (6.1; 94.9%), and malignant skin melanoma (5.5; 96.1%), showed lower DALY-per-case values.

Multivariate and partial correlation validation

To assess convergent validity and potential redundancy with established mortality-based indicators, DALY-per-case was regressed on 5-year survival, median age at diagnosis, and mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR). The overall model was statistically significant (F(3,24) = 11.98, p < 0.001) and explained 60.0% of the variance (R² = 0.60; adjusted R² = 0.55).

In the fully adjusted model (Table 4), MIR demonstrated the largest standardized coefficient (β = 0.52) and approached statistical significance (p = 0.06), whereas 5-year survival (p = 0.20) and median age (p = 0.23) were not independently significant after adjustment. These findings indicate that DALY-per-case is strongly influenced by mortality burden but is not entirely explained by survival or age structure.

Reduced and partial correlation analyses further showed attenuation of the survival association after controlling for MIR, suggesting shared variance between mortality-based indicators and DALY-per-case. However, the persistence of residual variance in the multivariable model indicates that DALY-per-case captures additional information beyond MIR alone, consistent with its integration of both fatal (YLL) and non-fatal (YLD) components.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that DALY-per-case robustly quantifies per-diagnosis severity, showing a strong inverse correlation with 5-year survival (r = −0.72), meaning that cancers associated with poorer prognosis correspond to substantially higher average healthy life-years lost per new diagnosis. This alignment with survival gradients supports construct validity, indicating that DALY-per-case behaves consistently with established prognostic patterns while additionally incorporating morbidity burden. Multivariable analyses further demonstrated partial independence from the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR), validated across 34 cancer types. Extension to tuberculosis, diabetes, HIV/AIDS, ischemic heart disease, and Alzheimer’s disease within the validated 2D burden-severity framework supports its applicability across diverse diseases within the standardized GBD framework.

It is important to clarify that DALY-per-case is not proposed as a new burden construct, but as a standardized per-incident normalization of established DALY components within the GBD framework. Its contribution lies in formal validation against survival benchmarks, demonstration of partial independence from MIR, and the development of an interpretive framework separating intrinsic per-diagnosis severity from overall population burden. Unlike case-fatality risk (CFR) or MIR, which approximate lethality, and unlike DALYs per capita, which reflect societal burden, DALY-per-case integrates both fatal (YLL/I) and non-fatal (YLD/I) components to quantify expected healthy life-years lost per new diagnosis.

Epidemiologically, DALY-per-case distinguishes high-prevalence conditions with moderate per-case severity from high-severity conditions with disproportionate individual burden, insight not obtainable from incidence or survival alone [21, 28]. Compared with existing indicators, DALY-per-case offers clear advantages, unlike MIR, criticized for weak theoretical grounding and sensitivity to registry completeness [3], it rests on YLL + YLD and is consistently estimable across countries via GBD data [4, 21, 28, 29]. Case-fatality and median survival, though clinically intuitive, are often unavailable outside high-income registries; DALY-per-case enables severity comparisons in data-limited settings, aligning with WHO equity priorities [23]. By incorporating YLD, it captures morbidity among survivors, effects often missed by survival endpoints [19, 30]. Across epidemiology, clinical practice, and policy, DALY-per-case supplies a standardized severity scale for cross-disease and cross-regional comparisons and supports burden decomposition to distinguish incidence-driven changes from per-case severity shifts [4, 21, 28].

In clinical practice, from a methodological perspective, DALY-per-case bridges classical measures of case fatality and disability weighting, providing a per-incident metric consistent with GBD’s standardized structure. It quantifies the combined loss of life quantity and quality per diagnosis, complementing survival endpoints such as overall and progression-free survival in oncology, by flagging conditions where therapeutic advances could yield substantial per-patient gains [4, 19]. It also highlights survivorship and long-term morbidity such as cardiotoxicity, chronic pain, requiring proactive follow-up [19, 30]. In policy, pairing total DALYs (population burden) with DALY-per-case (individual severity) in a 2D framework categorizes conditions into high-burden/high-severity, high-burden/low-severity, low-burden/high-severity, and low-burden/low-severity groups, operationalizing WHO’s call to balance broad-reach interventions with targeted efforts for high-severity conditions [4, 23, 31]. Extension beyond cancer illustrates the metric’s disease-agnostic applicability. HIV/AIDS demonstrated per-case severity within the high-severity range (> 20 DALYs per case), comparable to intrinsically severe cancers such as pancreatic and liver cancer and positioning it within the high-severity quadrant of the 2D framework [10, 32]. In contrast, type 2 diabetes and Alzheimer’s disease exhibited lower per-case severity (< 10 DALYs per case), aligning with lower-severity cancer categories and occupying the low-severity quadrants despite substantial total population burden [32]. Ischemic heart disease occupied an intermediate severity range (10–20 DALYs per case), corresponding to moderate-severity placements within the framework [32]. These patterns reinforce the distinction between population burden and per-diagnosis severity and demonstrate consistent interpretability across communicable and non-communicable diseases.

Interpretation in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) requires caution. Elevated DALY-per-case values may reflect delayed diagnosis, limited treatment access, and systemic healthcare constraints rather than intrinsic biological severity alone [4, 23]. In addition, GBD estimates in data-sparse settings rely more heavily on statistical modelling where registry coverage is incomplete, potentially widening uncertainty intervals. Accordingly, DALY-per-case should be interpreted cautiously for subnational resource allocation and ideally validated using country-specific survival, stage-at-diagnosis, or registry-linked data before informing local policy decisions [4, 23].

Integrating DALY-per-case into comparative risk assessment and burden decomposition clarifies temporal shifts in disease severity [16, 21, 28]. As a per-diagnosis complement to population-weighted measures (total DALYs, MIR) [3],, it helps distinguish incidence-driven changes from true reductions in individual burden, refining trend interpretation across regions and time [4, 31]. The metric is mathematically compatible with GBD procedures for YLL and YLD [10], and with the standardized disability-weight system [14]. Thus, DALY-per-case serves as a unifying per-incident metric for comparative epidemiology, enabling decomposition of mortality and morbidity across diseases and periods [21, 28, 31].

Statistical validation shows DALY-per-case quantifies a distinct severity construct integrating fatal and non-fatal outcomes. Its strong inverse correlation with 5-year survival and positive association with MIR establish convergent validity. Multivariable and partial-correlation analyses indicate it is not a surrogate for MIR or survival: when both entered, only MIR was borderline significant, and the DALY-per-case/MIR association persisted after adjusting for survival, supporting partial independence and discriminant validity. This mirrors comparative epidemiology, where composite burden measures combining mortality and morbidity outperform single-dimension indicators in explaining disease impact [11].

Although DALY-per-case correlates with the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR), the two measures differ conceptually. MIR estimates the probability of death among newly diagnosed cases and has been criticized for sensitivity to registry completeness and limited theoretical grounding as a survival proxy [3, 33]. In contrast, DALY-per-case integrates both fatal (YLL/I) and non-fatal (YLD/I) components within the GBD framework [9, 10, 32, 34], providing a continuous, health-adjusted severity scale rather than a lethality ratio. Its persistence after adjustment for survival indicates that it is not redundant with MIR but extends it by incorporating morbidity among survivors.

From an interpretive standpoint, these results position DALY-per-case between survival and population burden. Its close alignment with survival validates it as a prognostic proxy, while residual independence from MIR shows added information from disability weighting. This dual correspondence supports DALY-per-case as a composite severity index for both high- and low-data settings. Prior work has called for per-incident indicators to disentangle gains in survival from reductions in morbidity [4]. DALY-per-case advances measurement by providing a unified severity scale, harmonized with GBD architecture and empirically calibrated against survival, bridging clinical outcomes and population health metrics.

Policy implications

From a policy perspective, DALY-per-case complements population-weighted measures by distinguishing intrinsic per-diagnosis severity from aggregate burden. While total DALYs prioritize high-prevalence conditions, per-case normalization highlights diseases associated with disproportionate individual health loss [9, 32]. Pairing total DALYs with DALY-per-case aligns with WHO priority-setting principles that balance efficiency with severity considerations [9, 35]. In LMICs, interpretation requires caution because modelled inputs and healthcare access gaps may influence estimates; local validation is recommended before subnational resource allocation.

Limitations

The principal limitation of DALY-per-case is that it is derived from modeled population-level DALY estimates within the GB) framework rather than from individual-level longitudinal clinical data. Accordingly, it reflects average expected health loss per diagnosis at the population level and does not represent patient-specific clinical severity. DALY estimates depend on modeled mortality rates, disability weights, disease duration assumptions, and adjustments for under-ascertainment [9, 36, 37], and DALY-per-case necessarily inherits these assumptions.

Age structure may influence per-case estimates. Conditions affecting younger populations generate larger years of life lost (YLL) contributions due to higher remaining life expectancy, potentially elevating DALY-per-case relative to late-onset diseases. Additionally, disability weights may incompletely capture psychosocial or context-specific burdens, particularly in low-resource settings [38].

Cross-disease comparability is conditional rather than absolute. Although the GBD framework standardizes case definitions, duration modeling, and disability weights across conditions [32], communicable and non-communicable diseases differ in natural history, surveillance completeness, and case ascertainment. In low- and middle-income countries, greater reliance on modeled inputs may widen uncertainty intervals and influence per-case estimates. Therefore, DALY-per-case should be interpreted as a standardized comparative metric within the GBD architecture rather than a universal biological severity constant.

Finally, the ecological design limits patient-level inference. However, the strong correlation with survival supports construct validity at the population level.

Conclusion

DALY-per-case is a standardized per-incident severity indicator that complements traditional population-weighted burden metrics. It aligns with survival gradients, integrates fatal and non-fatal components, and enables structured differentiation between intrinsic per-diagnosis severity and aggregate population burden.

Implications for future work

Future research should validate DALY-per-case using patient-level longitudinal cohorts, explore age-standardized per-case formulations, and assess performance in subnational and registry-linked datasets. Simulation-based uncertainty propagation using GBD posterior draws may further refine interval estimation. Application of DALY-per-case in time-series analyses could also help distinguish therapeutic improvements in survival from reductions in morbidity burden. Integration into national reporting systems may enable real-time monitoring of per-diagnosis severity trends across diseases.
