Analysis of predictive factors for post-orthodontic stability: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaochen Zhang, Axian Wang, Xu Zhang, Haolin Zhang, Zixuan Chen, Xulin Liu, Bin Yan, Xiaodong Zhang, Zexu Gu, Yanning Ma, Zuolin Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background​​ Orthodontic relapse remains a challenge in clinical practice. This study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the independent contributions and time-dependent patterns of multidimensional factors influencing the relapse risk, to provide insights for formulating personalized stability strategies.Methods​​ This multi-center retrospective cohort study included 474 participants with a median follow-up of 6 years. Variables included baseline, anatomical, treatment, and retention factors. Relapse was assessed by comparing intraoral photographs at post-treatment and follow-up (≥ 3 years post-debonding). Multivariate logistic and Cox regression analyses were performed to identify relapse predictors, with stratified analyses by molar relationship.Results​​ Relapse rate was 21.3%. Protective factors included female sex (odds ratio [OR] = 0.475, p = 0.013) and tooth extraction treatment (OR = 0.437, p = 0.006). Convex facial profile (OR = 2.057, p = 0.031), high-angle vertical pattern (OR = 2.086, p = 0.043) and longer follow-up duration (OR = 1.152, p = 0.010) were risk factors. Retainer adherence was strongly protective: full-time wear (OR = 0.070, p < 0.001) and nighttime wear (OR = 0.316, p = 0.004) during the first year, as well as longer total wear duration (1–2 years: OR = 0.408, p = 0.028; >2 years: OR = 0.352, p = 0.006) significantly reduced relapse risk. Cox regression confirmed that full-time wear (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.231, p < 0.001) and wear duration > 2 years (HR = 0.366, p < 0.001) significantly reduced the instantaneous hazard of relapse. Stratified analysis revealed convex profile (OR = 5.706, p = 0.006) and high-angle pattern (OR = 4.234, p = 0.015) specifically increased relapse risk in Class II patients, whereas female sex (OR = 0.101, p = 0.003) was most protective in Class III patients.Conclusions​​ Convex facial profile and high-angle pattern indicated increased relapse risk, particularly in Class II patients. Female sex (especially in Class III patients) and extraction treatment showed protective effects. ​​High retainer adherence (full-time wear and prolonged use) was the most critical stability factor.​​ Clinical practice calls for personalized retention strategies based on individual risk profiles.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08204-x.

Background​​

The success of orthodontic treatment depends not only on achieving optimal occlusion and aesthetics, but, crucially, on the stability of these outcomes​​. However, ​​orthodontic relapse, defined as any unfavorable changes in tooth positions after orthodontic treatment, remains a prevalent clinical challenge [1–3]. Long-term follow-up and short-term observation data have indicated that approximately 70% of treated patients experience some degree of relapse [4–6]. Only 30% to 50% of patients can effectively maintain satisfactory tooth alignment a decade after treatment, and this proportion drops to about 10% after 20 years [7]. Relapse undermines patient satisfaction, wastes clinical resources, and potentially necessitates retreatment [8].

Relapse has a multifactorial etiology, arising from the interaction of biological, treatment-related, patient-related, and environmental factors [9]. It is considered inevitable due to the inherent biological memory of periodontal tissues [10] and underlying growth or aging patterns [11, 12]. Besides, numerous studies have investigated various factors associated with relapse, such as tooth extraction [13, 14], dental crowding [15], retainer types [16], and retention protocols [17], establishing a foundational understanding of relapse mechanisms. However, the more critical need in clinical practice lies in identifying the most pivotal predictors, thereby controlling risk factors while bolstering and reinforcing protective factors. A deeper exploration into the dynamic interplay of these variables is therefore essential to elucidate their interrelationships and achieve more precise risk prediction and assessment [18].

Bellot-Arcís et al. identified predictors of stability 4–10 years post-treatment [19], and other analyses have examined relapse in specific patient groups, such as those with ​​cleft lip and palate [20], or treated with lip bumpers [21] and transpalatal arches [22]. In particular, Class II [23] and ​​Class III [24] malocclusion groups may exhibit unique predictors. Collectively, these studies suggest that relapse may be partially controllable. However, much of the existing literature emphasizes static outcomes rather than the dynamic process of relapse over time, and the impact of various factors on stability remains inadequately explored. Furthermore, methodological challenges in long-term follow-up studies often result in limited sample sizes, constraining the generalizability of the findings.

The present study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the clinical predictors of relapse using both static (multivariable logistic regression) and dynamic (Cox proportional hazards regression) models, with analyses stratified by molar relationship to uncover subgroup-specific predictors. This approach was designed to elucidate not only the cumulative risk factors and time-dependent effects, but also their category-specific heterogeneity, thereby providing novel evidence-based insights and a theoretical foundation for formulating personalized stability strategies.

Methods

Study design and ethical approval

This was a multicenter, retrospective cohort study conducted at three participating institutions: the Department of Orthodontics at the Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University, the Department of Orthodontics at the Stomatological Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, and the Department of Stomatology of General Hospital of Northern Theater Command. All experiments were performed in accordance with Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatological Hospital of the Fourth Military Medical University (IRB-YJ-2022035).

Participants and eligibility criteria

The study cohort included patients who completed comprehensive orthodontic treatment between January 2009 and December 2021. All treatments were performed by certified orthodontic specialists with extensive and comparable clinical experience, and all interventions followed standardized protocols to ensure consistency in treatment approach and quality. All cases included in the study achieved clinically satisfactory dental alignment and occlusal outcomes at the end of treatment. The study initially screened the clinical records of patients at the participating institutions. After an initial exclusion of cases with incomplete medical records, the remaining eligible patients were contacted via telephone or text message to schedule a follow-up appointment. Patients who were successfully recalled underwent clinical examination, photographic records, and documentation of retainer wearing condition during follow-up. The final study cohort was derived from the pool of successfully recalled patients after the strict application of the eligibility criteria.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 9 to 60 years, regardless of gender; (2) completion of comprehensive orthodontic treatment; (3) availability of complete clinical records at pretreatment (T1), immediately post-treatment (T2), and at a long-term follow-up of at least 3 years after debonding (T3). Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of orthognathic surgery, cleft lip and palate, or other craniofacial syndromes; (2) receipt of only phase I functional treatment without subsequent comprehensive therapy.

Retention protocol​

Following appliance removal, maxillary and mandibular vacuum-formed or Hawley retainers were routinely provided. Some patients received a lingual fixed retainer bonded to the mandibular six anterior teeth along with a maxillary removable retainer. The choice between removable and fixed retainers was primarily based on clinical assessment, considering factors such as initial severe lower anterior crowding or tooth rotations, oral hygiene status, and the patient’s preference. Patients were advised to wear their removable retainers full-time during the first post-treatment year.

Predictors and outcomes

Based on literature review and expert consultation, the following variables were collected as potential predictors: (1) baseline characteristics:​​ gender, age at T1, oral bad habits, family history of malocclusion, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) status, and presence of periodontitis; (2) anatomical characteristics: dental crowding, molar and canine relationship, overbite, overjet, Curve of Spee, maxillary incisor inclination, facial convexity, and vertical skeletal pattern; (3) treatment details: tooth extraction, molar distalization, and arch expansion; (4) retention-related factors: retainer type (removable vs. lingual fixed), retainer wear frequency during the first year, retainer wear duration, and follow-up duration. Maxillary incisor inclination [25] and vertical skeletal pattern [26] were assessed from lateral cephalometric radiographs, while facial convexity was evaluated by the profile photographs. The dental models were used to measure degree of dental crowding, overbite, overjet, and Curve of Spee. Fifty randomly selected cases were re-evaluated for validity of the measurements. Information for the other predictors was obtained from the patients’ medical records and follow-up data. The classification criteria for each variable are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

The primary outcome of this study was the presence or absence of relapse at T3. Relapse was evaluated by two independent, experienced orthodontists through comparison of standardized intraoral photographs taken at T2 and T3 (Fig. 1). No adjunctive tools such as dental casts or intraoral scan data were used for this assessment. The medical staff responsible for photography received relevant training and followed standardized photography protocols, including patient positioning, shooting angles, and five standard views (labial, right and left lateral in occlusion, and upper and lower arch views). Relapse was defined by the presence of any detectable deviation from T2, observed at T3 in one or more of the following changes: (1) tooth alignment or arch width (crowding, spacing, tooth rotation, or arch narrowing/widening); (2) anterior overbite (increase or decrease); (3) sagittal occlusal relationship (changes in molar/canine relationship) or anterior overjet (increase or decrease). Disagreements were arbitrated by a third specialist to reach a final consensus on relapse status.

Sample size calculation

As a retrospective study, the final sample size was determined by all eligible patients during the study period, maximizing statistical power and generalizability. A total of 474 participants were ultimately included. To evaluate the statistical power of the current sample size, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 software. Under the conditions of a sample size of 474, a relapse event rate of 21.3%, a type I error (α) set at 0.05, and an effect size set at 0.25 (representing a small-to-moderate effect), the analysis demonstrated that the statistical power of this study to detect significant predictors in the logistic regression model exceeded 99%. This confirms that the obtained sample size is sufficient for the primary analyses conducted in this study.

Statistical analyses

Inter-rater agreement and reliability were assessed using Cohen’s Kappa coefficient for categorical judgments and the Interclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for continuous measurements. Categorical variables were described as frequencies and percentages, with intergroup comparisons and difference of variable distribution using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The normality of continuous variables was examined with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR), and intergroup comparisons using the Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. The primary analysis involved identifying independent predictors of relapse. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the cumulative relapse risk. Variables with a significance level of p < 0.2 in univariate analyses were included in a multivariable logistic regression model using the Enter method, which aimed to reduce the risk of omitting potentially important predictors. Variable selection was also based on their clinical relevance. Results were reported as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). In the Cox regression model, the time-to-event was defined as the follow-up duration (T2 to T3). The same variable selection procedure was applied for inclusion in the multivariable Cox model. Results were reported as hazard ratio (HR) with 95% CI. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). Hosmer-Lemeshow test and a plot of Martingale residuals against follow-up duration was used to evaluate the goodness-of-fit. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residual test. Additionally, stratified multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed by molar relationship subgroups (Class I, II, and III) to identify potential subgroup-specific predictors. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS V.29.0 (IBM). All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant flow

The participant flow is presented in Fig. 2. From an initial screening of 2150 clinical records of patients, 872 cases were excluded due to incomplete records. The remaining 1278 eligible patients were contacted for follow-up, with 778 successfully recalled. After applying the eligibility criteria, 197 were excluded due to history of orthognathic surgery, cleft lip and palate, or for having received only phase I functional treatment. An additional 107 patients were excluded because of the short follow-up duration. Consequently, 474 participants constituted the final study cohort.

Baseline data

Inter-rater reliability for relapse evaluation, as measured by Cohen’s Kappa, was 0.886 (p < 0.001). Reassessment of qualitative judgments and quantitative measurements for the variables also showed excellent reliability, with Kappa / ICC values ranging from 0.962 to 0.990 (p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 2). We first compared the baseline characteristics between the patients included in the study and potentially comparable non-recalled patients, and no systematic differences were found (Supplementary Table 3). Baseline demographic, clinical, and treatment characteristics of the cohort are presented in Table 1. The overall cohort had a median age of 15.0 years (IQR: 12.0–21.0). Females accounted for 73.6% (n = 349) of the participants. The cohort was categorized into a relapse group (n = 101) and a non-relapse group (n = 373), yielding an overall relapse rate of 21.3%. The median follow-up duration for the cohort was 6.0 years (IQR: 4.4–8.3). Median follow-up duration was significantly longer in the relapse group (7.0 years, IQR: 5.0-9.2) than in the non-relapse group (5.9 years, IQR: 4.1-8.0; p = 0.001). Comparative analysis between the groups revealed statistically significant differences in the following baseline and treatment-related variables: gender distribution, vertical skeletal pattern, utilization of arch expansion, and use of lingual fixed retainers. Additionally, significant differences were observed in patient-reported retainer wear frequency and retainer wear duration. All other measured baseline characteristics showed no statistically significant differences between the groups.

Logistic regression analysis​

The VIF values for included variables in the multiple regression were < 5, demonstrating the absence of significant multicollinearity (Supplementary Table 4). Following variable assignment and quantification (Supplementary Table 5), multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed (Table 2). Protective factors included female sex (OR = 0.475, 95% CI: 0.265–0.853, p = 0.013) and treatment involving tooth extraction (OR = 0.437, 95% CI: 0.241–0.792, p = 0.006). Risk factors were convex facial profile (OR = 2.057, 95% CI: 1.067–3.964, p = 0.031) and high-angle pattern (OR = 2.086, 95% CI: 1.025–4.246, p = 0.043). Regarding retainer usage, both nighttime wear (OR = 0.316, 95% CI: 0.144–0.693, p = 0.004) and full-time wear (OR = 0.070, 95% CI: 0.034–0.141, p < 0.001) were associated with significantly reduced risk compared to irregular wear. Longer retainer wear duration demonstrated a protective effect, with wear duration of 1–2 years (OR = 0.408, 95% CI: 0.183–0.907, p = 0.028) and > 2 years (OR = 0.352, 95% CI: 0.168–0.739, p = 0.006) significantly reducing relapse risk relative to < 1 year. Longer follow-up duration was correlated with a cumulative increase in relapse risk (OR = 1.152, 95% CI: 1.034–1.283, p = 0.010). The result of Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed χ² = 7.651 (p = 0.552), suggesting that the model adequately fit the data.

Cox regression analysis

The VIF values for all included variables were < 5 (Supplementary Table 6). Multivariate Cox regression analysis indicated that full-time retainer wearing significantly reduced the instantaneous hazard of relapse (HR = 0.231, 95% CI: 0.143–0.375, p < 0.001) compared to irregular wear. Retainer wear duration > 2 years was also associated with a substantially reduced risk (HR = 0.366, 95% CI: 0.205–0.656, p < 0.001). Female sex (HR = 0.668, 95% CI: 0.429–1.042, p = 0.075), tooth extraction (HR = 0.623, 95% CI: 0.383–1.013, p = 0.056) and retainer wear duration of 1–2 years (HR = 0.568, 95% CI: 0.314–1.027, p = 0.061) were identified as borderline protective factors (Table 3). The Martingale residuals were randomly distributed around zero with no obvious systematic trend, indicating an adequate model fit (Supplementary Fig. 1). Schoenfeld residual test revealed that retainer wear duration of 1–2 years, retainer wear duration > 2 years, and tooth extraction did not satisfy the proportional hazards assumption (Supplementary Table 7).

Multivariable logistic regression analyses stratified by molar relationship were subsequently performed (Table 5). Discussion

Standardized intraoral photographs are widely employed to document occlusal relationships, tooth alignment, and soft-tissue features [27]. In this study, relapse was assessed using these photographs to capture clinically detectable changes. This method closely aligns with the real-world scenario and pragmatic objective of identifying relapse in anterior tooth alignment or occlusion that are significant enough to draw the attention of clinicians or patients and potentially trigger interventions [28, 29], thereby reflecting clinically relevant outcomes. Previous research has supported the use of intraoral photographs for evaluating orthodontic treatment needs [30]. While traditional methods, such as the Peer Assessment Rating (PAR) Index [31] or Little’s Irregularity Index (LII) [32], can quantify occlusal changes, they may be less practical for rapid stability monitoring and decision-making during routine clinical follow-up. We suggest that clinical photographic assessment and metric analyses offer complementary perspectives, each illuminating distinct aspect of the complex phenomenon of relapse. However, the photograph-based approach may lack sensitivity in detecting subtle or early relapse signs, such as minor occlusal or alignment changes [33]. It also depends on the high-quality photographic documentation taken at ideal angles [34]. Nevertheless, the high inter-rater consistency observed in this study (Cohen’s Kappa = 0.886) suggests that the judgments can be applied consistently, supporting the reliability of the identified predictors.

From the patient’s perspective, any deviation from the final outcome is likely to be regarded as relapse, whether it represents true relapse related to the previous treatment or arises independently from normal aging [18, 35]. Therefore, the relapse observed in this study represents a composite outcome: (1) the inherent biological tendency for relapse (e.g., periodontal ligament and gingival fiber tension); (2) changes attributable to natural growth and aging processes; and (3) the influence of other identifiable predictive factors. The clinical challenge lies in the fact that these processes are often highly overlapping and occur simultaneously, which makes it difficult to distinguish their individual contributions or isolate the effect of each factor [18]. Given that the first two categories of factors are often intrinsic and thus make relapse to some extent unavoidable, this study focuses on identifying factors within the third category to help predict and reduce relapse risk.

A notable finding from multivariate logistic regression was that longer follow-up duration was associated with higher cumulative relapse risk. However, time itself does not directly cause relapse. Instead, by extending the observation period, it allows underlying unstable factors, such as residual growth and physiological aging changes, to gradually accumulate, which increases the chance of detecting relapse events [36]. This may also be related to the behavioral factor that the retainer wearing compliance of the patients decreases over time [19]. Therefore, the exposure-time effect of relapse should be understood as a potential time-dependent and progressive pattern. Interestingly, this effect was not observed in Class III malocclusion patients in stratified analysis, implying greater challenges in achieving early stability, possibly due to their higher residual growth potential [37]. Given the observed time-related pattern, we incorporated follow-up duration as the time scale in the Cox analysis as a shift from static risk factor identification toward dynamic and temporal risk management [38]. This model serves as a complement to the cumulative risk analysis to screen for and identify key risk factors, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding of relapse risk.

Specific skeletal and growth pattern may serve as underlying cause of relapse. Convex facial profile was identified as a significant risk factor, specifically in Class II patients. This may explain the reportedly higher relapse tendency in Class II malocclusions compared to Class I cases [39, 40]. Interestingly, while molar relationship itself was not a significant predictor, convex facial profile was predominantly distributed among Class II patients, suggesting that facial convexity may serve as a more direct morphological indicator related to the mechanism of relapse. A similar trend was observed for the high‑angle pattern. As Goldberg et al. reported, high-angle pattern correlates with a greater tendency toward crowding [41], which may be associated with specific growth tendencies [42]. Additionally, in growing high-angle or skeletal Class II mandibular retrognathia patients, the mandibular arch often exhibits a characteristically narrow anterior and wide posterior V-shaped form that may challenge stable finishing [43, 44]. Poor arch form control may increase susceptibility to relapse. However, some studies have demonstrated that high-angle patients experience less relapse in Curve of Spee and overbite [45, 46], indicating that skeletal morphology may exert dimension-specific effects on stability. Since these predictors were not significant in the Cox model, they may not accelerate relapse onset.

Some adolescents in this cohort underwent two-phase treatment, and the stability following functional therapy is highly dependent on growth pattern as well as post-treatment growth direction and amount [47]. Previous studies have reported no significant difference in short-term relapse between two-phase and direct camouflage treatment [48], and the improvements in overjet and ANB angle achieved through early intervention did not demonstrate a significant long-term stability advantage [49]. Therefore, it can be inferred that whether early functional therapy is performed appears to have limited influence on stability after comprehensive orthodontic treatment, which is likely governed more by inherent growth patterns. The potential impact of functional therapy may be modified or neutralized during subsequent comprehensive treatment. The high‑angle and convex profile risk factors identified in this study represent such relapse-prone growth patterns.

Female sex was identified as a significant protective factor in the logistic regression model. This may be partially related to sex differences in compliance with retainer wear [39]. However, the protective effect remained even after adjusting for adherence-related variables, suggesting the existence of additional mechanisms beyond behavioral differences. ​One contributing element may be related to the divergent growth patterns between sexes. According to one study, PAR scores increase markedly between ages 12–22 in males and 19–39 in females [50]. In the present cohort, males were significantly younger than females (13.0 vs. 15.0 years, p = 0.002), potentially placing them in an instability phase during the median 6-year follow-up. Since males exhibit prolonged growth and later maturation [51], their younger age may also reflect​ greater residual growth potential. Therefore, the association observed in this cohort likely results from the convergence of​ multiple factors, such as behavioral and growth patterns, and the specific age distribution of our sample. Interestingly, stratified analysis showed that the protective effect was most pronounced in Class III patients, likely due to the longer duration of mandibular forward growth commonly observed in males [52]. However, not all studies have identified sex as a significant predictor [19, 53]. Little et al. found untreated females with normal occlusion even exhibited a greater tendency toward late physiological mandibular arch contraction and crowding [54]. These findings suggest that additional attention may be warranted for adolescent male patients, especially with Class III malocclusion, whereas standard follow-up monitoring remains essential for other patients.

Direct comparisons between extraction and non-extraction treatments show conflicting stability outcomes [14, 36, 55–58], and both tend to yield more relapse than untreated cases [11, 12]. In this study, tooth extraction emerged as a protective predictor in the multivariate logistic model. However, its effect was not statistically significant within any of the subgroups. The proportional hazards testing also indicate that this protective effect may not be constant within the observation period. This discrepancy raises the possibility that the protective role of extraction may represent a stability benefit achieved through optimized treatment strategies. Theoretically, tooth extraction may facilitate a more stable arch morphology by providing adequate space for alignment without exceeding the original arch boundaries [15]. In contrast, non-extraction treatment often relies on arch expansion and incisor proclination to resolve crowding, which may elevate instability due to subsequent soft tissue pressure and aging processes [22, 59]. Since case characteristics and treatment strategies are likely more homogeneous within each subgroup, the benefit of extraction in avoiding detrimental procedures does not stand out. Arch expansion was associated with an increased relapse risk in the univariate analysis in the present study. Previous evidence suggested that excessive expansion should be avoided [54], and any expansion beyond the original arch form increases relapse risk [18]. Therefore, limiting arch expansion to within the original morphology rather than beyond physiological boundaries may improve stability.

Full-time retainer wear was identified as the most effective strategy (OR = 0.070, HR = 0.231), differing from several studies reporting comparable outcomes with shorter full-time periods [17, 60–62]. A recent review also concluded that evidence remains insufficient to strongly favor either protocol [63]. However, other studies have suggested that wear of < 9 h per day was associated with poorer alignment stability [64], and full-time wear may be particularly critical during the initial retention phase [65]. In this study, full-time wear represents a behavioral pattern of higher adherence, which is critical for maintaining stability [66]. The stratified analysis reinforced the importance of adherence. It is reasonable to speculate that early establishment of strict wear habits may have lasting benefits. The Cox model further indicated that full-time wear continuously minimized the instantaneous relapse risk at any time. In contrast, the value of nighttime retainer wear seems to lie primarily in its compensatory protective mechanism, as it lacks the capacity for immediate and continuous stabilization (HR = 0.805, p = 0.432). Based on these observations, a sequential retention strategy is proposed: during the high-risk period early in retention (first 1–2 years) [9, 66], full-time wear is recommended to minimize early relapse, with a transition to nighttime wear for long-term stability in patients with stable outcomes.​.

Prolonged retainer wear duration, especially beyond two years, emerged as another protective factor. Nevertheless, clinical recommendations regarding retention duration vary considerably, influenced by multiple factors [18, 67]. While some scholars have suggested that permanent retention may be necessary to prevent relapse in high-risk patients [68, 69], long-term retainer use is challenging due to declining patient compliance over time [70]. The proportional hazards testing also indicate that this protective effect may vary over time, which reflects the complexity of clinical decision-making regarding optimal retention duration. However, the significant protective effect observed over the entire follow-up period still to some extend reflects the direction of its association with the relapse outcome. Although fixed retainers are generally considered more reliable than removable retainers for long-term stability [66, 71, 72], the current study did not detect a significant effect of them. This may be due to the relatively small subgroup of patients with fixed retention (n = 41) or the possible influence of other stronger predictors. However, fixed retainers carry risks of debonding and gingival inflammation, necessitating regular clinical supervision [71, 73]. Evidence shows that combining fixed and removable retainers may yield more stable lower incisor alignment than fixed retainers alone [74].

This study also has limitations. Data on retainer wear frequency and duration were obtained from retrospective self-reports, which are susceptible to recall bias and subjective reporting. Potential overestimation of adherence likely results in a conservative estimate of the protective effect. Furthermore, the reduced subgroup sample sizes may constrain the statistical precision, leading to some relatively wide confidence intervals that may limit the generalizability of the specific findings. Moreover, photograph-based assessment may lack sensitivity for detecting subtle occlusal or alignment changes, leading to potential underestimation of relapse risk. Additionally, the binary outcome does not allow for separate analysis of stability in the three-dimensional directions, which limits a more granular understanding of relapse patterns. Future prospective studies should include objective methods to monitor adherence, and more detailed investigations are planned to extend the findings.

Conclusions

Convex facial profile and high-angle vertical pattern were associated with an increased relapse risk, particularly in Class II patients. In contrast, extraction treatment and female sex (especially in Class III patients) showed protective effects. ​​High retainer adherence, characterized by full-time wear and prolonged use, was the most significant protective factor across all risk models. Consequently, personalized retention strategies based on individual risk profiles are recommended. High-risk patients (e.g., those with a convex profile and/or high-angle pattern, particularly accompanied by Class II malocclusion) are advised to strictly wear retainers full-time initially, transitioning to nighttime wear once stability is achieved. Other patients still require standard follow-up monitoring.
