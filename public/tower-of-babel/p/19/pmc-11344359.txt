Evaluation of the management of a first crisis of atrial fibrillation in a group of Cameroon’s urban setting subjects
Authors: Loïc Alban Mbosso Tasong, CN Nganou-Gnindjio, Honoré Kemnang Yemele, Jules Thierry Elong, Laura Murielle Makowa Kuate, Simeon Kuissu, Alain Patrick Menanga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia encountered in clinical practice. When atrial fibrillation is first diagnosed, it tends to be permanent and associated with significant morbidity and mortality. We aimed to study the management of a first episode of atrial fibrillation in a group of patients in Yaounde, Cameroon.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study with data collected from the Cardiology department of Yaounde Central Hospital and the internal medicine department of Yaounde General Hospital over five years (January 2017 to December 2021), for a duration of 4 months, from February 2022 to May 2022. All patients older than 15 years with a first episode of atrial fibrillation were included, and all patients with incomplete medical records were excluded. The association between different variables was assessed using a χ² test and logistic regression method with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.ResultsOf the 141 patients recruited, the mean age was 68.5 ± 10.6 years. The sex ratio (M/F) was 0.7. The main associated factors and co-morbidities were hypertension in 70.2% (99) patients, heart failure in 36.9% (52) patients and a sedentary lifestyle in 33.3% (47) patients. The most common anticoagulant treatment was AntiVitamin K, used in 64.5% (91) of patients. Heart rate control was the most commonly used symptom control strategy in 85.1% (120) patients, mainly with beta-blockers in 52.5% (74). We found 1.4% (2) participants who were not treated with antithrombotics as recommended. Treatment of arrhythmia due to co-morbidities was not always recommended. The complication rate was 94.3% (133) patients. Control of the bleeding risk due to antithrombotic therapy and monitoring of anticoagulant therapy were not optimal. The heart rate control strategy had a higher success rate, and the sinus rhythm maintenance rate at one year was 61.7% (37) participants.ConclusionThe management of a first episode of atrial fibrillation at Yaoundé’s Central and General Hospitals is not always performed according to current recommendations and is far from optimal. However, nearly two out of three patients maintained sinus rhythm for one year.

Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the uncontrolled and rapid electrical activity of the atrial myocardium [1]. Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia encountered in clinical practice [2], and its incidence increases with age. It is a major complication of rheumatic heart disease which is highly prevalent in Africa [3]. There are other causes of atrial fibrillation that are just as frequent in Africa including hypertension, thyroids disease, structural heart disease and other diseases [3]. The current prevalence of atrial fibrillation is approximately 2% in the world population, and it is expected to double in the next 50 years due to the increased life expectancy of the population [4, 5]. In Europe, AF affects approximately 8 million people and is estimated to rise to 18 million by 2060 [6]. According to the Screening for the Atrial Fibrillation in the Elderly (SAFE) study, the prevalence of atrial fibrillation in individuals older than 65 years is 7.2%, while it is 10.3% in individuals older than 80 years [7]. McDonald et al. reported a significant increase in emergency department visits for atrial fibrillation between 1993 and 2004 in the USA [8]. In Africa, a 2021 study by Traore et al. in Mali reported an in-hospital prevalence of 4.8% [9]. In studies conducted in Cameroon, Boombhi J et al. found a prevalence of AF of 8.1% for an average age of 65.9 ± 12 years in 2019 at the Yaoundé’s Central and General Hospitals [2]. The main risk factors for atrial fibrillation in Cameroon are arterial hypertension, heart failure and valvular heart disease [10–12].

According to the European Society of Cardiology recommendations, the management of atrial fibrillation has three components: prevention of thromboembolic events, symptom control through rhythm or heart rate control, and screening for and correction of co-morbidities [1, 10]. Compared with ventricular rate control, rhythm control strategies for atrial fibrillation are less common in sub-Saharan Africa than in the rest of the world [13]. Yameogo et al. reported a proportion of rhythm control strategies of 10.2% for a percentage of heart rate control of 91.5% [14]. In Cameroon, Boombhi J et al. found only a heart rate control strategy and a proportion of 14.2% of patients with a high cardioembolic risk but without anticoagulation in two hospitals in the city of Yaounde [2]. Antivitamin K aims to prevent thromboembolic complications, but patients are exposed to hemorrhagic risk justifying rigorous monitoring, which is not always optimal in sub-Saharan Africa [13, 15]. Unfortunately, patients with atrial fibrillation in Africa are often undertreated with antithrombotics, as cost and access to monitoring are significant barriers [13]. Atrial fibrillation can cause severe hemodynamic and thromboembolic complications and is associated with an increased risk of stroke, heart failure and death [16]. This risk is greater in Africa than in other regions due to limited access to health care and suboptimal treatment [13]. Furthermore, when first diagnosed in Africa, atrial fibrillation tends to be permanent and associated with significant morbidity and mortality [13].

Faced with a socioeconomic and health context that is not conducive to compliance with the recommendations of learned societies, and in which the diagnosis of atrial fibrillation is often made late after the onset of the revealing complications, we aimed to study the management of a first episode of atrial fibrillation in Cameroon’s urban setting. The hope is that an improved understanding of current state of management of atrial fibrillation in a low-resource setting in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

Study Design and setting

We conducted a retrospective study with data collected from the cardiology department of Yaounde Central Hospital (YCH) and the internal medicine department of Yaounde General Hospital (YGH) over five years from January 2017 to December 2021, for a duration of 4 months, from February 2022 to May 2022. These departments have high-quality technical resources for treating cardiovascular diseases in sub-Saharan Africa.

Study population

The source population consisted of patients who had been admitted for atrial fibrillation or who had experienced an episode of atrial fibrillation during their stay at the central and/or general hospital of Yaounde. Inclusion criteria consisted of patients older than 15 years of age with a first episode of atrial fibrillation. Exclusion criteria consisted of patients with insufficient medical records or discharged against medical advice (Fig. 1).

Sample size estimation

The sample size was estimated to be 115 participants using the Lorentz formula.

N = P (1-P) (Z) ²/d² [17]

N = sample size.

P = Expected proportion of subjects with the variable under study (when unknown, p = 0.5 is used).

d = inaccuracy deviation (tolerated margin of error) accepted on each side to give the result. This percentage is usually 5%, which implies a 95% confidence interval.

Z = Confidence level according to the reduced centered normal law (for a confidence level of 95%, Z = 1.96).

This value was obtained using a 5% error and the prevalence of Boombhi J al. (8.1%) [2].

n₀ =0.081(1-0.081) (1.96*1.96)/(0.05*0.05) = 114.39.

Data collection

We retrospectively collected data from the medical records of patients with a primary or associated diagnosis of atrial fibrillation confirmed by ECG. The data were collected using a data extraction sheet. The data were then integrated into a database. For all the participants, we reported age, sex, risk factors and co-morbidities, consultation reason, EHRA score [1], CHA2DS2-VASc score [1], HAS-BLED score [1], electrocardiogram data, cardiac ultrasound data, anticoagulant treatment, symptom control strategy, and evolution under treatment.

Definition of terms

First crisis of atrial fibrillation: first episode of atrial fibrillation diagnosed using a resting electrocardiogram in symptomatic or non-symptomatic patients.

Statistical analysis

The data were collected and analyzed using SPSS 25 software. Quantitative variables were described by central tendency (mean) and dispersion (standard deviation) parameters. Qualitative variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. We performed the χ2 test to determine the factors associated with the return to sinus rhythm. Then, we used a multivariate analysis using the binary logistic regression method to determine the predictor factors with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The Institutional Ethical Review Board of the Université des Montagnes (Cameroon) approved the study, and the ethics clearance number 2022/081/UdM/PR/CEAQ was obtained. Before the survey, we received authorisation from the hospital institutions and free, informed and signed consent from the various participants.

Results

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the sample

Of the 141 participants recruited, the mean age was 68.5 ± 10.6 years, with extreme values of 33 and 95 years. The age group represented the most was between 60 and 70 years, with 39% (55) participants. The predominant sex was female with 58.9% (83) participants, with a sex ratio (M/F) of 0.7:1.0. As shown in Fig. 2, the main associated factors and co-morbidities were hypertension in 70.2% (99) patients, heart failure in 36.9% (52) patients, a sedentary lifestyle in 33.3% (47) patients and heart valve disease in 22.7% (32) patients.

Therapeutic modalities

The most common anticoagulant treatment was K-antivitamins with 64.5% (91) patients. As shown in Fig. 3, 1.42% (2) patients with high thromboembolic risk and no high bleeding risk did not receive anticoagulants. Table 2 shows that the most common initial therapeutic strategy was heart rate control in patients with 85.1% (120) patients, based on beta-blockers in 52.5% (74) patients. Amiodarone was the only drug used for rhythm control regardless of co-morbidity. For heart rate control, the most commonly used molecules were beta-blockers in patients with heart failure more precisely 61.8% (34) patients with a reduced ejection fraction and 72.2% (13) patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction and in patients with ischemic heart disease it was 92.3% (12) of patients, as well as digoxin in 75% (3) patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease or asthma and in 60% (3) patients with cardiothyreosis.

Evolution under treatment

As shown in Table 3, 94.3% (133) of our population had complications; the most common complication was heart failure with 57.9% (77) patients. The predominant mode of progression was prolonged persistent atrial fibrillation with 34.7% (49) cases. Table 4 shows that the target international normalised ratio was achieved in 45.5% (45) of the patients who received anticoagulant treatment with K-antivitamins. The target heart rate was achieved in 76.7% (92) patients. The sinus rhythm was restored in 42.6% (60) patients. The results presented in Table 5 shows that, there is a significant association between the screening and treatment of co-morbidities and return to sinus rhythm, with an odds ratio of 12.179 (2.452–60.490) and a p-value of 0.002. There was also a significant association between ventricular rate below 110 bpm and return to sinus rhythm, with an odds ratio of 2.468 (1.021–5.964) and a p value of 0.045. Participants with obesity were less likely to return in sinus rhythm (OR: 0.242; 95% CI 0.070–0.837; p = 0.025). The rate of maintenance of sinus rhythm at one year in patients whose sinus rhythm could be restored was 61.7%.

Discussion

This work aimed to pilot the improvement of AF management in our context by taking stock of the management and fate of patients with this condition for the first time in our context. In our study, the mean age was 68.5 ± 10.6 years. The predominant sex was female with 58.9% (83) participants, with a sex ratio (M/F) of 0.7:1.0, which is very similar to the results obtained by Boombhi et al. in Cameroon, who reported a mean age of 65.9 ± 12 years [2], thus demonstrating the importance of age in the incidence of this condition. The main associated factors and co-morbidities were hypertension in 70.2% (99) patients, heart failure in 36.9% (52) patients, a sedentary lifestyle in 33.3% (47) patients and heart valve disease in 22.7% (32) patients (Fig. 2). These results are similar to the distributions of risk factors in sub-Saharan Africa described in the literature [13].

The proportion of patients in whom a cardiac ultrasound was performed was 81.6%, similar to that obtained by Ntep-Gweth et al. in Cameroon (82%) [11] but much lower than that obtained by Meiltz et al. (98%) [18], showing that more patients in our setting do not benefit from paraclinical diagnostic evaluation. Cardiovascular ultrasound was not performed for some patients due to poverty and/or refusal. The most common cardiac abnormality was valvular disease (60%), followed by dilated cardiomyopathy (45.2%), and 1.7% of patients had no underlying cardiac disease (Table 1). Coulibaly et al. in ivory coast reported hypertensive heart disease (45.6%) and valvular heart disease (27.6%) as the main cardiopathies [19]. These results show that the majority of episodes of atrial fibrillation occur on an underlying cardiac substrate.

The most common anticoagulant treatment was antivitamin K (64.5%), whereas it was used in 58.7% of the Boombhi et al. studies [2]. The accessibility of these molecules can explain this difference. Indeed, despite the need for frequent blood checks, VKA remains more available and financially more accessible than the new oral anticoagulants in Cameroon [2]. The most common initial therapeutic strategy was heart rate control (85.1%), mostly with beta-blockers (52.5%). Boombhi et al. reported that the only therapeutic strategy used was heart rate control [2]. Higher rates of rhythm control were noted in the European Registry, with 46.8% in the study by Meiltz et al. and 59.8% in the study by Kirchhof et al. [18, 20]. This difference can be explained by the relatively advanced age of our population, which made the heart rate control strategy the most acceptable. The literature also describes a preference for heart rate control in the approach to symptom control among practitioners in sub-Saharan Africa [13].

For the prevention of thromboembolic events, it is recommended that antithrombotic therapy be initiated in patients at high risk of thromboembolism unless they are at increased risk of bleeding and that such treatment be considered for patients at high risk of thromboembolism, taking into account the risk/benefit assessment of the patient [1, 21, 22]. Two patients (1.4%) with increased thromboembolic risk and without high bleeding risk did not receive anticoagulants (Fig. 3). Although lower [2] than those obtained by Boombhi et al. in Cameroon (14.2%), these results show that some of the population is not treated with antithrombotics as recommended. Amiodarone was the only drug used for rhythm control regardless of co-morbidity, and it is not recommended for patients with cardiothyireosis [21, 22]. Additionally, it was the only molecule used for rhythm control in patients without underlying heart disease (Table 2). In contrast, its use in this situation is recommended only as a second-line treatment, with flecainide, propafenone or vernakalant being the first-line treatment [20–22]. This can be explained by the fact that amiodarone is more accessible than other antiarrhythmic drugs in Cameroon. Concerning heart rate control, in patients with heart failure, the main molecules used for long-term control of heart rate were beta-blockers (61.8% for patients with heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction and 72.2% for patients with heart failure with a preserved ejection fraction), with no difference from the recommendations [1]. In severe asthma or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are recommended as first-line therapy [1]. This was not the case in our study, in which digoxin was the most commonly used molecule (75%). Patients with cardiothyireosis benefited essentially from digoxin for heart rate control (60% of patients) compared with 40% for beta-blockers. These results are not inconsistent with the recommendations against using amiodarone for heart rate control in patients with dysthyroidism [21].

In our study, 94.3% of our population had complications (Table 3). The most common complication was heart failure (57.9% of patients), a result similar [23, 24] to that obtained by Kamdem et al. (45%) in Cameroon and Youssouf et al. (53.8%) in Mali. This finding again demonstrated the causal relationship between atrial fibrillation and heart failure. Thromboembolic events accounted for 44.9% of the complications, with ischemic stroke being the most common occurring at 30.1%. Atrial fibrillation is a pathology that causes stroke, multiplying the risk of stroke by 7 [1]. Hemorrhagic events due to anticoagulant treatment accounted for 7.5% of the complications, whereas this figure was only 1.5% in the study by Meiltz et al. [18]. None of the patients with a bleeding event in our study had a high bleeding risk at the time of diagnosis, suggesting that the control of bleeding risk due to antithrombotic therapy and the monitoring of anticoagulant therapy are much less optimal in our setting. We recorded eight deaths (6.1% of complications), 3 (2.3%) of which were in the hospital. Youssouf et al. reported a 5.76% mortality rate in Mali [24], which is higher than that reported by Meiltz et al. (2.6% of deaths were attributable to a cardiovascular cause) [18]. Therefore, sub-Saharan African patients managed for atrial fibrillation would have more fatalities than those managed in European settings. Less than half (45.5%) of our patients on VKA had an INR within the target values (Table 4), which can be explained by compliance concerns. The target heart rate was achieved in 76.7% of patients, similar to the result obtained by Kirchhof et al. (78.6%) [20]. The sinus rhythm was restored in 42.6% of the population (36.7% in the heart rate control group, 70% in the cardioversion group and 81.8% in the cardioversion and heart rate treatment group). Meiltz et al. achieved a 90.4% success rate in the rhythm control strategy [18]. These results show, on the one hand, that rhythm control is much less successful in our context than in Europe, and on the other hand, sinus rhythm can be restored in some patients without using antiarrhythmics. Patients who were screened and managed for co-morbidities were 12 times more likely to return to sinus rhythm (OR 12.179; 95% CI 2.452–60.490; p = 0.002) (Table 5). This reinforces the idea that the management of atrial fibrillation should be holistic, systematically integrating the screening and treatment of various co-morbidities [1]. We found a significant association between ventricular rate below 110 bpm and return to sinus rhythm, with an odds ratio of 2.468 (CI 1.021–5.964) and a p value of 0.045. These results are similar to those of Holmqvist et al. [25] who found that the lower the heart rate, the less likely atrial fibrillation was to evolve into a permanent form (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.79 to 0.89, p < 0.0001). The explanation suggested is that heart rate may be associated with other (unmeasured) factors that influence the likelihood of atrial fibrillation progression, such as autonomic tone [25]. Participants with obesity were less likely to return in sinus rhythm (OR: 0.242; 95% CI 0.070–0.837; p = 0.025). It is possible that these obese patients had associated undiagnosed obstructive sleep apnea syndrome. Obstructive sleep apnea syndrome has been shown to be a factor in the progression of atrial fibrillation to the permanent form [1, 25]. The rate of maintenance of sinus rhythm at one year in patients whose sinus rhythm could be restored was 61.7%. Bouzelmat et al. in Morocco obtained a rate of maintenance of sinus rhythm at six months of 78% [26]. The rate of maintenance of sinus rhythm at 2 years described in the literature is approximately 60% [26].

Limitations

This study was carried out in a single town in Cameroon which may not be representative of other healthcare facilities in the country. In addition, this is a retrospective study, so there is a limit to the reliability of the data collected.

Conclusion

Managing a first episode of atrial fibrillation in a Cameroonian urban setting is not always performed according to recommendations and is far from optimal. There has been improvement in this population, but much remains to be done to reduce mortality from this disease.
