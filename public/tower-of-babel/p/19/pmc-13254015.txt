Evaluation of Guanfacine Prescribing Practices for Attention Deficit Hyperactive Disorder Management: A Retrospective Medical Records Within a Child Development Service
Authors: Oluwatomi Adeola Ibilola, Karen Redhead, Brad Jongeling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimGuanfacine is a second line option for management of Attention Deficit Hyperactive Disorder (ADHD) and can be prescribed adjunctively with stimulants or as monotherapy. This medical record review evaluated prescribing practices, vital sign changes over time, and client outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective medical record review examined 171 records of clients on guanfacine modified release within the Child Development Service (CDS) in Perth, WA, between February 22, 2022, and May 5, 2022. Clients on guanfacine for one week or less were excluded; those who had ceased guanfacine at the time of review were included.ResultsClinicians completed serial vital sign measurements during guanfacine titration for 92 clients (54.12%), partially completed them for 58 clients (34.12%), and did not complete them for 20 clients (11.76%). No significant changes in systolic/diastolic blood pressures or heart rate were observed following guanfacine prescription. Clients on adjunctive therapy reported fewer side effects than those on monotherapy, 26.7% vs. 50.0%, χ2 (1, N = 166) = 9.59, p = 0.02. This relationship was strongest in clients with an Autism diagnosis, 20.0% vs. 51.6%, χ2 (1, N = 61) = 6.61, p = 0.01. A similar but not significant trend was observed in children with ADHD alone, 25.8% vs. 63.6%, χ2 (1, N = 60) = 3.26, p = 0.071.ConclusionsClients prescribed guanfacine adjunctively with stimulants reported fewer side effects compared to monotherapy, supporting early adjunctive use in suitable cases. Blood pressure and heart rate remained stable across the cohort. Serial vital sign measurement was performed in 50% of cases.

Introduction

Guanfacine, a second line treatment for ADHD also has a role in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), anxiety, tics, and sleep difficulties with/without ADHD [1, 2, 3]. In Australia, guanfacine is only available as a modified release tablet and was approved in 2018 as monotherapy for ADHD in children aged 6–17 years when stimulants are contraindicated or not tolerated. It was approved for adjunctive use in 2019 for those with a sub‐optimal response to stimulants [4, 5].

Guanfacine, an alpha‐2A‐adrenergic agonist originally approved for hypertension, acts on pre‐frontal cortex and midbrain receptors of the central nervous system [6]. Common side effects include somnolence, headache, fatigue, bradycardia and syncope [7, 8]. The guanfacine product information (GPI) recommends measuring blood pressure (BP) and heart rate (HR) before starting treatment, after dose increases and intermittently during maintenance therapy [7]. Broken/crushed tablets alter pharmacokinetics, acutely increasing risk of hypotension [7]. Short term paediatric trials of monotherapy and adjunctive therapy demonstrated a dose dependent decrease in mean HR by 6–9 beats/min and BP by 4–5 mmHg systolic and 3 mmHg diastolic [7].

ADHD is prevalent in children with ASD, with 28%–68% of ASD children also diagnosed with ADHD [9]. Guanfacine has been shown to be effective in children with ADHD and is generally well tolerated in children with ASD, presenting fewer side effects compared to other ADHD medications [10, 11, 12].

Despite the established role of guanfacine in ADHD [13, 14, 15], limited research [16] describes real‐world drug utilisation practices in children, particularly those with co‐occurring ASD [10, 17]. This study evaluated the prescribing practices of clinicians, their comfort in prescribing guanfacine and their compliance with vital signs monitoring [4, 5, 18]. Additionally, we assessed changes in vital signs and investigated side effects in clients prescribed guanfacine. By reviewing the prescribing practices, monitoring compliance and client outcomes, we aimed to gather insights to improve patient care and clinician practice.

Methods

Data were extracted from Genie clinical software Version 9.38, an electronic medical practice software. Data extracted included birthdate, age, sex, height, weight, systolic and diastolic BP of all clients prescribed guanfacine. Figure 1 shows the caseload and number of clients prescribed guanfacine and audited.

Flowchart of client records included in audit.

Ethics and study approval were received on 15/11/21 from the Child and Adolescent Health Service (CAHS), Governance Evidence Knowledge Outcomes (GEKO).

A total of 358 clients had guanfacine prescription on 15/11/2021. Of these, 171 clients were selected and reviewed based on the most recent prescription issue date. The sample size was calculated based on a 95% confidence interval with a 70% compliance for monitoring side effects, HR and BP [19]. To assess whether the reviewed sample was representative of the full cohort, demographic and clinical characteristics (age, sex, blood pressure and BMI) were compared between included and excluded clients. Clients prescribed guanfacine 1 week prior to ethics approval were excluded. Selecting the most recent consecutive cases ensured that the audit captured contemporary practice.

The data extracted from the 171 full records included: prescriber status; guanfacine dose and indications; date of first prescription of guanfacine and the dates of the next two subsequent visits; serial measurements of BP, HR, weight, and height at initial prescription and subsequent follow‐ups for a minimum of three visits; all current diagnoses, medications and their indications. The total numbers of patients on the paediatric caseload with singular diagnosis of ADHD or ASD and with dual diagnosis of ADHD and ASD diagnoses were determined. In addition to the retrospective review of guanfacine prescription, clinicians completed a survey (supplementary appendix) which was sent to all 39 paediatricians employed within Child Development Services (CDS) at the time of the study. The survey was completed anonymously and included nine questions on prescription of guanfacine.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using Stata/IC, version 16.0. Continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviation [SD], or as median with interquartile range (IQR) for skewed data. Categorical variables were summarised using percentages. Tests for linearity, normality, multicollinearity and heteroskedasticity were performed to ensure a model fit. Parametric (t‐tests) and nonparametric (chi‐square tests) were used to compare clinical and descriptive features of clients. Linear mixed models' analyses were used to compare systolic and diastolic pressures and HR measurements, accounting for within individual variability. Missing data were documented, and observations with missing values were excluded.

Results

On November 15, 2021, CDS recorded 13 121 active paediatric patients in metropolitan Perth (point‐in‐time data). Of these, 2280 (17.4%) had an ADHD diagnosis, 2147 (16.4%) had an ASD diagnosis, and 485 (3.7%) had both. The 2280 ADHD cases identified do not represent the total number of diagnosed cases, as free‐text diagnoses were not captured. However, this limitation does not significantly impact the study.

Among this caseload, 358 clients were prescribed guanfacine. Records for 171 of these were reviewed in detail, data on BMI and blood pressure were available for 78 clients; all other baseline variables had complete data. Of the 187 excluded clients, BMI data were available for 75 and blood pressure data for 68 clients. There was no difference in sex distribution, mean age, BP, or body mass index (BMI) between included and excluded clients (Table 1).

Vital Signs Measurements

The guidelines for BP and (HR) monitoring during titration of guanfacine according to GPI [7] were followed in 92 (53.80%) clients, partially followed in 59 (34.50%) clients and not followed in 20 (11.70%) clients. A total of 156 clients had BP measurements recorded and 128 clients had HR measurements recorded. On average, children had 3.4 measurements recorded over time with a range of 1 to 17. The fixed effects estimate was 0.002235 CI [−0.000123, 0.00483], 0.000678 CI [−0.0115, 0.00250] and 0.00220 CI [−0.00602,0.00163] for systolic pressure (SBP), diastolic pressure (DBP) and HR respectively. The effects were highly variable between individual clients: SBP (SD 7.14), DBP (SD 4.60) and HR SD (8.019). Overall, there was no significant change in systolic, diastolic pressures and HR after prescription of guanfacine.

Prescribing Practice and Survey

Paediatricians initiated guanfacine in 150 cases (87.7%) while registrars initiated guanfacine in 11 cases (6.4%). Additionally, ten clients (5.8%) were prescribed guanfacine prior to their initial visit to the service.

Twenty paediatricians and one registrar (53.8%) participated in the survey regarding guanfacine prescription. Among respondents, 18 (85.7%) expressed being ‘very comfortable’ with prescribing guanfacine when indicated, comprising 17 paediatricians and one registrar. One paediatrician (4.8%) reported feeling somewhat uncomfortable, while the remaining two (9.5%) expressed some level of comfort with guanfacine prescription. Among the respondents, eight clinicians expressed interest in receiving further information on guanfacine's safety. All participants welcomed recommendations aimed at enhancing guanfacine prescription practices within CDS.

In total, 17 clinicians (81.0%) surveyed were comfortable with General Practitioners (GP) co‐prescribing guanfacine. One clinician expressed dissatisfaction with GP co‐prescription, while the other three were undecided.

Paediatricians' suggestions to improve Guanfacine prescription practice were: ‘cross titration information from clonidine to guanfacine’,‘A printed form for patient to record weekly/second weekly BP when titrating as I rarely get feedback from GP’,‘Universal guanfacine initiation genie template’‘Strongly support GP co‐prescription. Guanfacine as a Mono‐Agent/Adjunctive Agent

Stimulants were initially trialled in 150 (87.7%) clients. In 21 clients (12.3%) guanfacine was the first line medication; this was due to contraindications (CI) to stimulants in five clients and parental/clinician choice in 16 clients. Contraindications included hallucinations, anxiety, a reported family history of sudden cardiac disease, weight concerns, and aggressive behaviour.

On average, clients trialled stimulants for 17.1 ± 11.9 months with a range of (1–60 months) prior to receiving a prescription of guanfacine. The mean guanfacine dosage prescribed was 2.58 mg ± 1.09 with a range of 0.5–6 mg.

Guanfacine was prescribed as a mono‐therapeutic agent in 77 clients (45.0%) and in combination with stimulants in 94 clients (55.0%). Guanfacine was prescribed as monotherapy for intolerable stimulant side effects in 43 clients (55.8%), parental choice in 13 clients (16.9%), unclear reasons in eight clients (10.4%), insufficient symptom control with stimulants in six clients (7.8%), contraindication to stimulants in five clients (6.5%) and behavioural issues in two clients (2.6%).

Guanfacine was prescribed adjunctively with stimulants for insufficient symptom control in 54 clients (57.4%), stimulant‐related side effects in 29 clients (30.9%), unclear reasons in six clients (6.4%), parental choice in two clients (2.1%), and behavioural issues in three clients (3.2%).

Children with ADHD and ASD were no more likely to use guanfacine as monotherapy than those with ADHD alone, 49.2% vs. 48.4%, χ 2 (1, N = 125) = 0.01, p = 0.934.

Side Effects

No information was recorded regarding the presence/absence of guanfacine side effects in four patients. Of the 167 patients, 62 (37.1%) had documented side effects from guanfacine, with descriptions recorded for 45 patients (totalling 70 side effects; see Table 3), while no side effects were documented for the remaining patients. No difference was found in the mean dose of guanfacine prescribed in those with and without side effects, [2.69 mg vs. 2.54 mg, t (150) = 0.79, p = 0.433]. Discussion

Our findings show that the majority of clinicians in our service performed vital signs monitoring, with 54% fully and 35% partially complying. Documentation of BP and HR may have been impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic (2020–2022), during which telehealth consultations were a significant part of service delivery. Our review found no significant differences in BP and HR values before and after guanfacine prescription, aligning with Huss et al., who reported small reductions in BP and HR, with asymptomatic clients during the first 4 weeks of dose optimisation [12]. The most frequently reported side effects, sleepiness and fatigue, were also consistent with findings from Huss et al. [12].

Findings from our study highlight the potential benefit of using guanfacine as adjunctive therapy. Children on combined therapy were less likely to report side effects compared to those on monotherapy (26.7% vs. 50.0%, χ 2(1, N = 166) = 9.59, p = 0.02). This relationship was most pronounced in clients with both ADHD and autism diagnoses, though a similar trend was observed in clients with ADHD without autism.

About 20%–35% of children with ADHD have an inadequate response to initial stimulant treatment; early trials of second‐line agents may improve individual and family functioning and provide future socio‐economic benefits [20]. The variable interval between the commencement of guanfacine after stimulant medication (ranging from one to 60 months) may reflect factors such as the timing of guanfacine's availability in Australia and parental or clinical confidence. At the time of this study, about 13% of patients diagnosed with ADHD within our service were prescribed guanfacine. Other studies have reported the underutilisation of guanfacine extended release for ADHD management [21, 22]. Considering our study findings of fewer side effects when used as an adjunctive treatment, we hope this study increases clinicians' confidence in prescribing guanfacine for clients with suboptimal response to stimulants.

In our study, 12.3% of clients received guanfacine as their first medication, while 2% had guanfacine for a diagnosis other than ADHD. Prescribing second‐line medication options as first line has also been reported in more complex ADHD presentations [23]. In clinical practice, prescribers are expected to follow recommended medication guidelines to support safe, effective, and optimal patient outcomes.

Limitations

The sample included 48% of patients in our service who were prescribed modified‐release guanfacine between 2018 and 2021. Although only a subset of clients was reviewed, comparisons between included and excluded clients showed no differences. As this was a retrospective audit, the results are dependent on the accuracy of medical records. Assessing growth in clients would have been valuable, but this was limited by a lack of detailed data and incomplete records of growth parameters during the guanfacine dose optimisation period. Finally, the audit reflects practice up to early 2022, prior to publication of the national ADHD clinical guidelines (2022) and prescriber manual (2024). Prescribing practices may have changed since this time; however, these findings provide a useful benchmark for future comparison.

Conclusion

This retrospective medical record review of 171 patients on guanfacine demonstrated that prescribers largely adhere to PBS guidelines and national medication standards for monitoring vital signs. Our findings indicate that side effects are reduced when guanfacine is used adjunctively with stimulants. Further research across other child development services, both nationally and internationally, is needed to support our findings and to better inform prescribing practices in clinical settings. To support routine practice, we developed clonidine‐guanfacine titration (available on request) and GP co‐prescription templates and pharmacist‐led educational support sessions based on feedback from the clinicians surveyed in this review. We recommend paediatricians consider early adjunctive guanfacine prescription when indicated and collaborate with GPs to improve support and medication safety during dose titration.
