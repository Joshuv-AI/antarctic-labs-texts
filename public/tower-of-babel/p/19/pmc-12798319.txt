Noise-optimized cone-beam computed tomographic imaging of different post materials with multiple settings: A comparative analysis
Authors: Solaleh Shahmirzadi, Dilek Helvacioglu-Yigit, Umut Seki, Maharaj Singh, Sarang Saadat, Husniye Demirturk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe aim of this study was to evaluate the effect of 4 different post materials on the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) in various tooth regions using multiple cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) imaging settings and metal artifact reduction (MAR) algorithms.Materials and MethodsForty single-rooted teeth with 4 different post materials were mounted in a skull and scanned using the Planmeca ProMax 3D Max under different conditions: 90 and 96 kVp; no, medium, and high MAR settings; and voxel sizes of 100 and 200 μm. Image analysis was performed with ImageJ software to calculate CNR. The dose-area product (DAP) was recorded, and the effective dose was calculated.ResultsWhen all other parameters were held constant, each factor significantly influenced mean CNR (P<0.05). Higher CNR values were observed in the middle third of roots at 96 kVp, with a voxel size of 200 μm, and with medium or high MAR. No significant difference was found between medium and high MAR. Gold posts showed the lowest mean CNR, while no significant differences were observed among the other post materials.ConclusionThe combination of low resolution, medium MAR, and 96 kVp provided the highest image quality and CNR for visualizing teeth with post materials using the Planmeca ProMax, while also producing one of the lowest effective doses.Keywords: Cone-Beam Computed Tomography, Signal-To-Noise Ratio, Dental Materials

Introduction

In recent years, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has emerged as an accurate and readily accessible imaging modality for the comprehensive management of dental disease. It can be used across the continuum of care, from diagnosis to treatment planning and, when needed, follow-up evaluation of healing.1

At present, there is insufficient information regarding the extent to which the parameters available on CBCT machines affect image quality. To provide patients with the highest level of dental care, radiologists must determine which acquisition settings are most appropriate. This decision is influenced by several factors, including the patient’s age, sex, and clinical needs, and it directly affects both the resolution and the field of view of the images.2 An optimal imaging technique must be capable of producing high-quality images with adequate contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) efficiency while maintaining the required radiation dose. The CNR is defined as the ratio of the signal difference (contrast) to the noise level of the image, serving as a quantitative measure of image quality.2

CBCT is commonly used to evaluate and monitor dental posts before and after placement. However, metal-containing materials in dental posts are known to cause significant beam hardening and streaking artifacts. These artifacts degrade contrast and CNR, obscure anatomical structures, and complicate the assessment of regions of interest.3 To accurately visualize root canals adjacent to dental posts-which can directly influence treatment outcomes-it is essential to improve image quality by addressing such artifacts. To date, no data exist on the effect of different CBCT settings on CNR in relation to various post materials. Such information could reduce the likelihood of misdiagnosing procedural errors during or after post placement in root canals.

CNR, as a measure of image quality, can be enhanced by adjusting several factors during the scanning process, including the number of basis images and tube voltage (kVp).4 Increasing kVp improves image homogeneity and uniformity.5 Spatial resolution is also closely linked to voxel size, with resolution and detail improving as voxel size decreases.

Optimizing exposure settings in radiology has always posed challenges. To achieve acceptable image quality at the lowest feasible radiation dose, it is necessary to balance the influence of exposure settings on both image quality and patient dose. Technical image quality analyses, such as CNR studies, provide an objective framework for evaluating this balance.

The Planmeca CBCT system (ProMax® 3D Max; Planmeca, Helsinki, Finland) incorporates a metal artifact reduction (MAR) algorithm that can mitigate artifacts from metallic materials. Additionally, the operator can select from 4 resolution levels offered by the machine to achieve the best possible image quality. Dental posts are available in multiple types and materials, including stainless steel, titanium, carbon, cast, and fiber. Fiber posts have gained popularity because of their elastic properties similar to dentin, their ease of application, and their esthetic benefits. However, some glass fiber, carbon fiber, and quartz fiber posts exhibit radiopacity lower than that of dentin. The radiodensity level varies depending on the type and composition of the post.6 This feature may limit the diagnostic accuracy of CBCT in radiological evaluations.7 Conversely, materials containing alloys with high atomic numbers may generate pronounced artifacts, potentially masking fractures, perforations, or voids in the root canals.

Optimization of CBCT devices can be achieved by adjusting exposure parameters to reduce artifacts and improve other aspects of image quality, while maintaining an appropriate radiation dose. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the CNR produced by 4 different types and compositions of dental posts in various tooth regions under varying exposure parameters, including resolution, kVp, and MAR.

Materials and Methods

The Medical Ethics Committee of Kocaeli University, Kocaeli, Turkey, approved the use of human teeth extracted for this study (Review No. KÜ GOKAEK 2017/165).

Forty single-rooted, non-carious mandibular premolars were collected. Teeth with open apices, fractures, root resorption, anatomical abnormalities, structural defects, or any history of prior root canal treatment were excluded. The crowns were removed using a high-speed handpiece with diamond burs under water cooling. Root canals were prepared with ProTaper Next rotary files up to size X5 (Dentsply/Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland). After canal preparation, obturation was performed with gutta-percha and AH26 silver-free sealer (Dentsply Sirona, Bensheim, Germany) using the lateral compaction technique.

Following obturation, the roots were stored under 100% humidity for 1 week. Based on preliminary CBCT assessments of the largest diameter at the 4-mm apical canal segment, ten groups of 4 teeth were formed. Within each group, the 4 teeth were randomly assigned to 4 experimental groups (n=10 each). Group 1 consisted of 10 titanium alloy posts, 0.8 mm in diameter (Unimetric Dentsply, Maillefer Ins Holding, Ballaigues, Switzerland, Lot: 1356737). Group 2 contained 10 prefabricated, gold-plated, screw-type posts (#1) with a conical cross-section (Nordin, Chailly, Switzerland, Lot: 14995/263). Group 3 included 10 quartz fiber posts (Bisco DT Light Illusion X-Ro; Bisco Inc., Schaumburg, USA, Lot: 1400005973). Group 4 consisted of 10 fiberglass posts (#2) (RelyX; 3M ESPE, Neuss, Germany, Lot: 379631802).

Post spacing at 8 mm was made using each system’s post drills, leaving approximately 3 mm of gutta-percha in the apical region. Following removal of gutta-percha and preparation of the post space, posts with stoppers were placed into the canals to confirm proper length. Self-adhesive RelyX resin cement (RelyX U200; 3M ESPE, Neuss, Germany) was used to cement the posts.

CBCT imaging

Each root sample was placed in the tooth socket of the anterior region of a dry mandible. To simulate alveolar soft tissue, a 4-mm-thick layer of pink wax was uniformly applied over the mandible. The mandible was then placed inside a circular plastic box filled with water to cover the region of interest (Fig. 1). Red markers were used to ensure consistent alignment and repositioning of the mandible after modifications.

The mandible was placed in a circular plastic box, and the alveolar crest was covered with wax.

Images were obtained using a Planmeca ProMax 3D Max unit with a 5.0×5.8 cm field of view. Scans were acquired with 2 voxel sizes (100 μm and 200 μm), at 90 kVp and 96 kVp, 5.6 mA and 7.1 mA, and an exposure time of 12 s (Figs. 2 and 3). Three MAR algorithm settings were applied: none, medium, and high.

A total of 480 CBCT scans were obtained in multi-slice Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. Each of these scans had a slice thickness of 0.16 mm.

For each volumetric dataset, 2 slices were selected at distances of 2 mm (cervical third) and 5 mm (middle third) from the cementoenamel junction. These slices represented the cervical and middle thirds of the root, enabling targeted analysis of the relevant regions.

Image analysis

CBCT images were analyzed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA). For each image, 3 regions of interest (ROIs) were defined, and the minimum, maximum, mean gray values, and standard deviation were calculated.

The first ROI was a rectangle (30×60 pixels) placed in the buccal area adjacent to the root, excluding the post material. The second ROI was a rectangle (60×30 pixels) in the interproximal region, also excluding the post. The third ROI, serving as the control, was a rectangle (30×60 pixels) placed at the farthest edge of the volume, away from the tooth and artifacts (Figs. 4 and 5). Typical dark bands from beam-hardening artifacts and streak lines from scattering artifacts were observed in these regions.

Statistical analysis

Artifact evaluation was performed using analysis of variance (ANOVA). All 2-, 3-, and 4-way interactions were considered, involving the 4 material types, 2 voxel sizes, 2kVp levels, and 3 MAR settings. The tooth factor was included as a blocking variable, with the assumption that it did not interact with material type or machine parameters. To identify significant factor combinations, Tukey’s multiple comparison adjustment was applied. The analysis considered material type, voxel size, MAR, and kVp, with statistical significance set at P<0.05.

Results

When all other parameters were held constant, the average CNR value was significantly affected by tooth region, kVp, MAR setting, material, and voxel size.

A statistically significant difference in CNR values was observed between the cervical and middle thirds of the root (P<0.05), with the middle third showing higher CNR values.

Table 1 presents the findings of Tukey’s multiple-comparison test for kVp, material type, MAR, and voxel size. Among the materials, gold (Nordin) showed the lowest mean CNR value for both the cervical and middle thirds. At 96 kVp, mean CNR values were higher than at 90 kVp. Similarly, both medium and high MAR produced higher CNR values than no MAR, although no significant difference was detected between medium and high MAR. In addition, a voxel size of 200 produced higher mean CNR values than a voxel size of 100.

Across all comparisons, the combination of titanium posts with high resolution, medium MAR, and 96 kVp in the middle third of the root generated the highest CNR. By contrast, gold posts with 96 kVp, a voxel size of 100, and no MAR produced the lowest CNR in the cervical third of the roots.

When synthesizing all datasets and comparisons, the most effective configuration for CBCT volumes containing posts involved the use of low resolution, medium MAR, and 96 kVp, particularly in the middle third of the root with titanium posts, which produced the highest CNR while also achieving one of the lowest radiation dose levels. Conversely, gold posts combined with 96 kVp, a voxel size of 100, and no MAR consistently yielded the lowest CNR, especially in the cervical third of the roots.

Discussion

There are several methods for modifying CBCT settings. To provide patients with the best image at the lowest feasible dose, exposure settings should be tailored to the diagnostic purpose. High-density materials, such as metals, are well known to induce streaking and beam hardening artifacts. These obscure diagnostically important regions, reduce interpretability, and lower diagnostic quality.10 These artifacts make it difficult to evaluate ROIs and reduce contrast between closely positioned structures. Numerous studies have shown that when gutta-percha and root canal sealers are present, artifacts and poor CNR can result in false-positive findings and misdiagnoses of root fractures and voids.11,12,13,14 However, to the authors’ knowledge, no prior research has evaluated image quality and interpretation challenges associated with dental post materials in CBCT. The present study therefore investigated how the Planmeca ProMax 3D Max CBCT system’s image quality is affected by different dental post materials and settings. By separately adjusting imaging parameters, it was possible to identify strategies for maximizing image quality while keeping radiation exposure within acceptable limits Because CNR is a widely used and easily interpretable image quality metric, it was employed in this study to evaluate the effects of parameter adjustments on image quality. Increasing kVp lowers the absorption ratio and raises photon energy, thereby increasing the detector signal. Bechara et al.15 and Kim et al.16 reported that higher kVp values substantially improved CNR. Pauwels et al.17 examined optimal kVp levels in CBCT while balancing dose and CNR, using a phantom with air, aluminum, low-density polyethylene, and polytetrafluoroethylene. They concluded that at a fixed radiation dose, particularly at low dose levels, the highest kVp produced the greatest CNR. In contrast, these results showed that while higher kVp often improved image quality, it also yielded both the most and least favorable CNR outcomes, suggesting that similar image quality may be achieved, but at the cost of increased radiation exposure.

In this study, low resolution increased CNR and improved image accuracy around dental posts Klintström et al.18 reported that resolution changes had no clear effect on CNR, aligning with Neves et al.,19 who reported that different CBCT resolutions did not significantly impact image quality. The discrepancy between the present findings and those of Neves et al. may be explained by differences in dental post materials. Another study comparing voxel sizes (0.25 vs 0.30 mm) reported that higher resolution images (0.25 mm) were more accurate, and that both voxel size and metal posts negatively affected diagnostic accuracy.20

The results also showed that applying the MAR algorithm increased the CNR, likely because of reduced beam hardening and scattering artifacts from dental posts. Bechara et al.15,21 reported similar findings with metal-containing phantoms. Clinically, MAR provides clearer images for radiologists and endodontists by reducing noise and artifacts. However, its limitations include longer reconstruction times and uncertainty about whether MAR restores true surrounding anatomy or simply suppresses artifacts through interpolation or iteration.2

High-density materials are particularly problematic, as they reduce the CNR, obscure regions of interest, and reduce contrast between adjacent structures.4,21 In this study, 2 silica fiber post types were compared with titanium and gold-plated posts. The RelyX fiber post contains 30–40% epoxy resin, 60–70% glass fiber, and additional zirconia filler, while the DT-Light Post consists of 62% quartz fiber and 38% epoxy matrix. Despite similar composition and equivalent radiopacity,22,23 gold-plated posts produced the lowest contrast and mean CNR due to their high atomic number. This finding is consistent with De Rezende Barbosa et al.,24 who reported reduced CBCT diagnostic accuracy with gold posts. Conversely, fiber posts produced more uniform energy absorption and clearer images.25 Overall, titanium posts yielded the highest CNR, making them preferable when optimal image contrast is required.

Lira de Farias Freitas et al.26 reported that hyperdense artifacts and reduced image quality occurred more frequently in the cervical third than in the apical third, which they attributed to the smaller cross-sectional area of the cervical region. The findings of the present study are consistent, showing higher CNR in the middle third than in the cervical third. The CNR was measured in buccal and interproximal regions, including alveolar bone and dentin, but excluding post materials. It is concluded that differences in the proportional dimensions of the dental post relative to dentin in the middle versus cervical thirds explain these findings. Although the overall tooth area is smaller in the cervical region, the relative post area is larger, particularly in interproximal regions. This proportionally greater presence of post material in the cervical third reduces the CNR, whereas the smaller relative post area in the middle third increases the CNR, thereby improving diagnostic potential.

Exposure settings must be optimized to achieve the best possible image quality at the lowest acceptable dose, consistent with the “as low as diagnostically acceptable” principle. In this study, the most clinically effective technique for evaluating the middle third of the root with a titanium post was low resolution, medium MAR, and 96 kVp, which produced the highest CNR. Effective dose was calculated using DAP values multiplied by a conversion coefficient, according to the kilovoltage-dependent formula recommended by Batista et al.8: (E=(0.001453×{kV}+0.0118)×PKA). However, the following formula is used to determine the effective dose (E) in clinical settings: Effective dose (E)=∑absorbed dose×radiation weighting factor (WR)×issue weighting factor (WT). It is necessary to use tissue weighting factors because different organs are radiosensitive to radiation at varying degrees, even with the same equivalent dosage.

This investigation had several limitations. First, its in vitro design was a drawback. In real clinical scenarios, diagnostic accuracy may be affected by additional interferences such as prostheses, implant sites, and patient movement during image acquisition. Second, the absence of crowns or restorative fillings on the post materials’ coronal structures limited applicability. Although restorations were excluded to standardize CNR calculation, this does not reflect clinical conditions. Third, only a single CBCT unit was used, meaning results may not be generalizable across devices; further in vivo studies with different machine types are needed. Finally, while the MAR algorithm improved CNR values, its application has limitations. It remains unclear whether MAR settings truly restore surrounding structures to reflect anatomical reality or simply reduce artifacts through interpolation or iterative processing. Additional research is therefore necessary to assess the accuracy and reliability of MAR in these circumstances.

The findings of this study provide insight into optimizing image quality. The lowest radiation dose combined with the highest CNR was achieved using medium MAR, low resolution, and 96 kVp. This protocol appears to be the most clinically applicable for evaluating teeth with posts on the Plan meca ProMax, as it produced lower dose levels while improving image quality. Moreover, CNR was consistently higher in the middle third than in the cervical third. Although gold posts produced the lowest mean CNR values in both regions, overall CNR did not vary substantially across post materials when other parameters were held constant.
