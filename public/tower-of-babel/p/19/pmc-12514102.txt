The single-cell atlas of colorectal cancer reveals cellular heterogeneity and key molecular features of the tumor immune microenvironment
Authors: JunHu Li, ZhiQiang Liu, BoYu Zhang, LiuPing You, YueNan Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is a highly heterogeneous malignancy, with its tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) playing a crucial role in tumor progression, immune evasion, and treatment response. Despite insights from single-cell technologies, challenges such as small samples and inconsistent annotations have limited a comprehensive understanding. Thus, a large-scale, high-resolution single-cell atlas is needed to fully explore the heterogeneity of CRC TIME and guide personalized treatment.MethodsWe analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data from 100 CRC samples (371,223 cells). Immune, stromal, and epithelial cells in the TIME were clustered, annotated, and functionally analyzed. Transcription factor networks were inferred using SCENIC, differentiation states assessed with CytoTRACE, and cell–cell communication analyzed via CellPhoneDB. TIME subtypes were constructed from bulk RNA-seq data, followed by survival analysis. SCRN1 was further validated through in vitro experiments.ResultsThis study presents a comprehensive single-cell CRC atlas, revealing significant heterogeneity in T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells within the TIME. We defined two immune ecological subtypes: subtype 1 (enriched in metabolic and motility pathways with poor prognosis) and subtype 2 (enriched in immune response pathways, with better prognosis and greater immunotherapy potential). High SCRN1 expression in malignant epithelial cells correlated with poor prognosis and metastasis. In vitro experiments confirmed the role of SCRN1 in promoting CRC cell proliferation and migration.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive characterization of the CRC TIME, links TIME subtypes to prognosis, and identifies SCRN1 as a potential therapeutic target, offering insights for CRC precision subtyping and immunotherapy strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-025-04173-2.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most common and deadly malignancies worldwide, with its incidence increasing with age [1]. Although traditional treatments like surgery and chemotherapy have had some success, many patients still face poor prognosis due to disease recurrence and metastasis [2]. In recent years, advancements in molecular biology and omics technologies have enabled a deeper understanding of CRC’s molecular mechanisms. Tumor heterogeneity in CRC has been identified as a key factor influencing treatment response and prognosis [3]. Through large-scale omics analyses, CRC has been classified into four consensus molecular subtypes (CMSs), which exhibit significant differences in tumor biology, clinical features, and treatment response [4].

With the advancement of single-cell sequencing technology, researchers can now analyze the TIME of CRC at higher resolution. TIME consists of tumor cells and surrounding immune cells, stromal cells, endothelial cells, and others, forming a complex ecosystem that drives tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion. Single-cell RNA sequencing has revealed the high heterogeneity of the CRC TIME, identifying several functionally distinct tumor-associated cell subpopulations, such as SPP1 + macrophages promoting angiogenesis and regulatory T cells (Tregs) distributed across different CRC subtypes [5, 6]. These findings offer important insights into the mechanisms of immune evasion and immune therapy responses. However, existing studies face challenges such as small sample sizes, limited sequencing depth, and inconsistent cell classification standards, leading to significant variability in results. The universality and functions of some cell subpopulations remain to be validated. Therefore, constructing a unified and comprehensive single-cell atlas of CRC, revealing the relationship between TIME cell heterogeneity and clinical features, holds significant theoretical and clinical value.

This study analyzed 100 CRC samples and integrated single-cell transcriptomic data from approximately 370,000 cells, constructing the largest and most comprehensive single-cell atlas of CRC to date. We systematically mapped the cellular heterogeneity in CRC tissue, identified key transcriptomic patterns and cell subpopulations, and further analyzed individual differences among patients. The study revealed that the TIME cell composition in CRC patients can be divided into 33 subpopulations, providing new insights into tumor immune evasion mechanisms. These analyses offer valuable theoretical support for studying CRC pathology, developing personalized treatment strategies, and optimizing immunotherapy.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and processing of single-cell data

The CRC single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from publicly available datasets by Pelk et al., including 36 normal tissue samples and 64 tumor tissue samples, totaling 371,223 cells (GSE178341) [6]. The scRNA-seq data were converted into Seurat objects using the R package “Seurat” [7]. The harmony algorithm was employed to remove batch effects [8]. Three quality control strategies were applied: retaining genes expressed in at least 5 cells, removing cells expressing fewer than 100 genes, and excluding cells with more than 5% mitochondrial gene expression. The data were then normalized using the NormalizeData function, and the top 2000 highly variable genes were identified with the FindVariableFeatures function. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed, selecting the top 20 principal components for cell clustering analysis. Batch correction across different samples was performed using the harmony, implemented via the RunHarmony function in R. All parameters were set to the tool’s default values to ensure unbiased integration. After batch correction, we applied the FindClusters function in Seurat with a clustering resolution parameter of 0.5, which provided an optimal balance between cluster granularity and biological interpretability in our dataset. Differentially expressed genes for each cluster were calculated using the FindAllMarkers function, with thresholds set to FDR < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)|> 0.25.

Acquisition and preprocessing of bulk RNA-seq data

In this study, we obtained transcriptomic and clinical data from the UCSC website for the TCGA COAD cohort and converted the counts data to log2 (TPM + 1) format for further analysis [9]. Additionally, we downloaded three microarray datasets (GSE17537, GSE32323, and GSE44076) from the GEO website. For these microarray datasets, probes corresponding to multiple genes were removed, and for probes corresponding to a single gene, the average expression was taken and log2 transformed. In survival analysis, samples with a survival time of less than one month were excluded. To visualize survival status across different groups and generate Kaplan–Meier (KM) plots, we used the “survminer” R package. For continuous gene expression values, the optimal cutoff determined by the “survminer” package was used to divide patients into high and low expression groups. Finally, the log-rank test was applied to assess the statistical significance of survival differences. Harbin cohort comprised paired normal and tumor samples from 40 treatment-naive CRC patients who underwent surgical resection between 2009 and 2021 at Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. All patients were pathologically confirmed diagnosis of CRC. Comprehensive clinicopathological parameters were extracted retrospectively from electronic medical records. These samples were then used for RNA sequencing in present study. And the study was approved by the ethics committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University (Approval Number: YJSKY2025-198). Cell-type annotation

We annotated the six main cell types identified in the single-cell dataset based on known marker genes, including CD3D, CD3E, CD79A, MS4A1, CD14, CD68, COL1A2, COL3A1, VWF, PECAM1, and EPCAM, corresponding to T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells [10, 11]. The stromal and immune cell populations (including T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells) were then re-clustered to identify subtypes within the major cell groups. Through normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, different cell subtypes were identified and annotated. Each subtype (e.g., CD4 + T cell cluster 01) was labeled with a marker gene (e.g., IL7R), such as “CD4_01_IL7R”. Criteria for selecting marker genes included: (1) differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the cell group; (2) genes with high specificity, being highly expressed in the corresponding cell group and low in others; (3) literature support that the gene is a marker or closely related to the function of the cell type.

Cell–cell interaction analysis

We constructed the cell–cell interaction network in the CRC TIME using the Python package CellPhoneDB (v2.0) [12], mainly following the software’s default settings. Potential interaction strength between two cell subpopulations was predicted based on the expression levels of ligand–receptor pairs. The interactions were then filtered based on significance (P < 0.05). Specifically, if a cell expressed a receptor or ligand, the corresponding interaction was defined as input or output. Biologically relevant ligand–receptor pairs were analyzed between different cell subpopulations. The relative expression levels (Z-scores) and adjusted P-values of ligand-receptor pairs were visualized in dot plots or interaction heatmaps.

Predicting transcription factor activity using SCENIC

We used the SCENIC Python workflow (v0.12.1, https://github.com/aertslab/pySCENIC) [13] to analyze gene regulatory networks (GRNs) and transcription factor (TF) activity with default parameters. The input data were the normalized expression matrix of target cells. The analysis utilized the RcisTarget and GRNboost transcription factor binding site databases (Homo sapiens), sourced from the official resources (https://pyscenic.readthedocs.io/en/latest). The SCENIC workflow generated regulon-specificity scores and identified active transcription factors in a binary matrix. Based on the AUC matrix, we used the Wilcoxon test to select differentially active transcription factors, with a cutoff of FDR < 0.05 and fold change > 2. The results were visualized using heatmaps.

CytoTrace analysis

The CytoTRACE algorithm, developed by Gulati et al., is an advanced tool for analyzing scRNA-Seq data. Its core function is to capture, refine, and quantify gene expression levels highly correlated with single-cell gene counts. After the CytoTRACE computation, each cell is assigned a score that describes its stemness state in the specific dataset. As a reliable computational method, CytoTRACE accurately predicts cell differentiation states and has been validated in large-scale datasets, outperforming traditional stemness assessment algorithms [14]. In this study, we used the R package CytoTRACE v0.3.3 to calculate the CytoTRACE scores for malignant cells, with scores ranging from 0 to 1. A higher score indicates stronger stemness (lower differentiation), while a lower score suggests weaker stemness (higher differentiation).

Inference of CNV based on scRNA-seq data

In each tumor sample, the tumor epithelial cell dataset was assumed to consist of epithelial cells, while other cells (such as immune and stromal cells) were classified as the reference dataset. Using the infercnv tool (version 0.99.0), we estimated the initial CNV values for each single cell based on transcriptomic features, following the approach of Sun et al.[15]. To quantitatively analyze CNV levels in single cells and identify malignant cells, we defined a CNV_Score, which was calculated by measuring the mean squared CNV values across the entire genome.

Gene set enrichment analysis

To explore the heterogeneous expression features of different cell subtypes, we performed gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using the R package GSVA (v1.44.3) [16]. The pathway enrichment analysis was based on the Hallmark gene sets from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) [17].

Identification of TIME ecological subtypes based on single-cell transcriptomic data

To investigate the potential cellular composition of different TIME ecosystems in CRC, we analyzed the co-occurrence patterns of different TIME cell subpopulations. The correlation between the normalized frequencies of any two groups in different tumor samples was calculated using the corr.test function. These correlation values were then clustered using the pheatmap (v.1.0.12) R package with the ward.d2 clustering method and correlation distance.

Evaluation of TIME subtypes based on bulk transcriptomic data

The top 50 marker genes are critical for defining cell types and states through literature review [18, 19]. Following the methods reported in these literatures, we therefore used ssGSEA to define cell-type scores. To validate the presence of different TIME ecosystems in bulk transcriptomic data, we used the top 50 marker genes specifically expressed in immune and stromal cells and assessed the content of different immune and stromal cell subpopulations using ssGSEA. We then applied the ConsensusClusterPlus R package for unsupervised clustering of immune and stromal cell content [20]. The following parameters were used: 80% item resampling (pItem), 100% feature resampling (pFeature), a maximum k value of 9 (maxK), 1000 resamplings (reps), and the pam clustering algorithm based on Euclidean distance (clusterAlg).

Cell culture and cell transfection

The CRC cell line HCT116 was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). HCT116 cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Biyuntian, Shanghai, China) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. The sh-SCRN1 and OE-SCRN1 plasmids were purchased from Genechem (Shanghai, China). Transfection was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the efficiency of knockdown and overexpression was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot analysis.

Western blotting assay

Total proteins were extracted from CRC cells using RIPA lysis buffer containing PMSF and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA protein assay kit. The protein samples were mixed with SDS sample buffer and heated at 98 °C for 10 min. Proteins were separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane during the blotting process. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk solution for 2 h at room temperature, then incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C, and signals were detected using an ECL kit. The primary antibodies used in this study were: anti-GAPDH antibody (AC002, Abclonal, Wuhan, China) and anti-SCRN1 antibody (Abcam, ab105355, USA).

qRT-PCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from HCT116 cells using the total RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), with GAPDH as the internal control. Relative RNA levels were calculated using the 2−∆∆Ct method. All primers were purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The sequences of the primers were as follows: SCRN1 (forward: 5′-GGATGGTCTGGTGGTATTTGG-3′ and reverse: 5′-CCTTGGAACTTGGTCGATTG-3′) and GAPDH (forward: 5′-GACTCATGACCACAGTCCATGC-3′ and reverse: 5′-AGAGGCAGGGATGATGTTCTG-3′).

CCK8 assay

HCT116 cells (1 × 10^3 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate, and cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK8 kit (C0041, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h of incubation, 10 μl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. The cells were further incubated for 1–4 h, and the OD value was measured at 450 nm.

Colony formation assay

1 × 10^3 cells were seeded in a six-well plate and cultured, with media changed every 3 days. After two weeks, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (P0099, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and stained with crystal violet (C0121, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Statistical analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Wound healing assay

CRC cells were seeded in a six-well plate to form a confluent monolayer. A straight line scratch was made using a 10 μl pipette tip to create a “scratch.” After washing twice with PBS, cells were cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h. Images were captured at 0 and 24 h, and the scratch area was calculated using ImageJ software. Cell migration rate (%) = (0 h scratch area—24 h scratch area)/0 h scratch area × 100%.

Transwell Assay

1 × 10^5 cells in 200 μl serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber of an 8-μm Transwell (Corning, USA), and 800 μl fresh medium with 10% serum was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 h, cells on the underside of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. The cells on the upper side of the membrane were wiped off with a cotton swab. Five random fields were photographed under a microscope (100 ×), and statistical analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Result

Tumor immune microenvironment atlas of colorectal cancer

To comprehensively map the TIME of CRC, we performed single-cell transcriptomic sequencing on 100 samples from 64 pre-treatment patients (including 64 tumor tissues and 36 paired normal tissues). After rigorous quality control, 371,223 high-quality cells were obtained for subsequent analysis. Using principal component analysis for dimensionality reduction and graph clustering, we identified 42 cell clusters (Supplementary Fig. 10A). Based on known marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 10B), these clusters were classified into six major groups: T cells, B cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 10C). To further explore the functional implications of the identified cell types, we compared their proportional composition between tumor and matched normal samples, as well as between MSI and MSS groups. In tumor tissues, fibroblasts were significantly enriched (Supplementary Fig. 1). When comparing MSI and MSS cases, we observed that MSI tumors exhibited higher proportions of myeloid cells, fibroblasts and endothelial cells, suggesting a more immune-inhibited status (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Further annotation of each cell type revealed subpopulations: T cells included CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), γδT cells, helper T cells (Th), and NK cells (Supplementary Fig.10d; B cells were divided into naïve B cells, germinal center B cells, memory B cells, and plasma cells (Supplementary Fig. 10E); myeloid cells consisted of macrophages, dendritic cells, monocytes, mast cells, and neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 10F). In the stromal cells, fibroblasts were further classified into normal fibroblasts and four types of cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs): myofibroblast-like (myCAFs), progenitor-like (proCAFs), inflammatory (iCAFs), and matrix-like (matCAFs) (Supplementary Fig. 10G); endothelial cells included venous, arterial, immature, proliferative, and lymphatic subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 10H); epithelial cells were divided into 23 distinct subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 10I).

We compared the enrichment differences of hallmark gene sets across T cell subpopulations (Fig. 1D). In CD4 + T cells, the CD4T_04_HSPA1B subpopulation was significantly enriched in carcinogenic pathways such as the p53 and KRAS signaling pathways. HSPA1B has been linked to stress responses [21], suggesting that the CD4T_04_HSPA1B subpopulation may undergo a pronounced response during CRC development. CD8 + T cells showed significant enrichment in inflammatory responses and gamma interferon pathways, especially in the CD8T_07_GZMK subpopulation, which exhibited the strongest anti-tumor activity. NK cell subpopulations demonstrated a suppressive state in pro-tumor pathways, such as angiogenesis and IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling. Certain Th and Treg cell subpopulations were highly enriched in pro-tumor pathways, such as Treg_30_IER5L, which was enriched in epithelial-mesenchymal transition and apoptosis pathways, and Th_26_IL17A, which was enriched in hypoxia and KRAS signaling pathways, indicating that these subpopulations primarily promote tumor progression. However, Th_25_CXCL13 exhibited a silent state in most pathways, suggesting potential anti-tumor capabilities.

SCENIC analysis revealed transcriptional networks specific to T cell subpopulations (Fig. 1E). For instance, CLOCK and ELK3 showed high regulatory specificity in the CD4T_01_IL7R subpopulation, suggesting that these transcription factors play a key role in the conversion of CD4 + T cells to this subset. Additionally, the transcriptional activity of BATF and GATA3 was elevated in the Treg_27_FOXP3 subpopulation, further indicating that this subgroup may enhance Treg-mediated pro-tumor functions.

Cell–cell interaction analysis showed strong interactions between CD8 + T and NK cell subpopulations and other subgroups, indicating their association with anti-tumor functions (Fig. 1F). CytoTrace analysis revealed that the CD4T_02_CCR7 subpopulation of CD4 + T cells had the highest differentiation potential, suggesting that other CD4 + T cell subpopulations may derive from this subgroup (Fig. 1G-H). Previous studies have shown that CCR7 is a marker gene for naive CD4 + T cells [21]. Differentiation potential was also heterogeneous across other cell subtypes, further emphasizing the functional complexity of these populations.

We compared the enrichment differences of hallmark gene sets in different B cell subpopulations (Fig. 2D). The naïve B cell subgroup exhibited a silent state in most pathways, with only the angiogenesis-related pathway showing significant enrichment. Germinal center B cells and memory B cells were active in several cancer-related pathways, such as PI3K-AKT-mTOR, NOTCH, and G2M checkpoints, while the naïve B cells and plasma cell subpopulations showed lower pathway activity.

SCENIC analysis revealed specific transcriptional networks for B cell subpopulations (Fig. 2E). For example, CLOCK (regulating lymphocyte development) and LHX2 (associated with immune cell infiltration) exhibited high regulatory specificity in the germinal center B cell subpopulation, suggesting these transcription factors may synergistically promote the function and development of germinal center B cells. In the cell–cell interaction analysis, the interactions between plasma cell subpopulations were weak, indicating that they might exert their function through the secretion of different antibodies (Fig. 2F).

Additionally, CytoTrace analysis assessed the differentiation potential of different B cell subpopulations. The results showed that plasma cell subpopulations had the lowest CytoTrace score, indicating they were in a terminally differentiated state. In contrast, the naïve B cell subpopulation had the highest CytoTrace score, suggesting it has the highest differentiation potential, with other B cell subpopulations likely derived from this group (Fig. 2G-H). These findings further emphasize the complexity of B cell subpopulations and their important role in the TIME.

Subpopulation characteristics and interaction networks of myeloid cells

Through unsupervised clustering, 46,991 myeloid cells were divided into 28 clusters (Supplementary Fig. 11A), which were further categorized into 5 major subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 10F). To explore the heterogeneity of myeloid cells, we performed a detailed subpopulation analysis. The results showed that dendritic cells were divided into 3 subpopulations, macrophages into 12 subpopulations, mast cells into 3 subpopulations, monocytes into 5 subpopulations, and neutrophils formed 1 unique subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 11B). Each subpopulation had distinct molecular markers (Supplementary Fig. 4). Further analysis revealed significant differences in the properties of myeloid cell subpopulations across tissue types and patients, with macrophages being the most abundant. This highlights the significant heterogeneity of myeloid cells in the TIME, warranting further investigation (Supplementary Fig. 11C).

We first compared the enrichment scores of gene sets across different subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 11D). The results showed that most dendritic cell subpopulations exhibited a silent state in most pathways, with the γ-interferon response pathway being notably enriched, possibly related to the antigen-presenting function of dendritic cells. Some macrophage subpopulations were significantly enriched in tumor progression-related pathways. For example, the Mac_05_S100B subpopulation was enriched in oxidative stress-related pathways, while the Mac_07_MARCO subpopulation was enriched in angiogenesis pathways, suggesting that these subpopulations may play a role in promoting tumor progression. In contrast, other macrophage subpopulations were enriched in anti-tumor pathways, such as the Mac_14_CLEC9A subpopulation, which was enriched in α-interferon-related pathways. Overall, myeloid cell subpopulations exhibited significant functional differences.

SCENIC transcriptional network analysis revealed highly specific transcription factors for myeloid cell subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 11E). For instance, transcription factors such as SOX2 exhibited high regulatory specificity in the monocyte subpopulation, suggesting that these factors may drive the chemotaxis of monocytes to tumor sites. Cell–cell interaction analysis showed complex interactions between subpopulations, particularly the Mon_23_REG1B monocyte subpopulation, which had close interactions with multiple macrophage subpopulations, such as Mac_04_CXCL9, Mac_06_KLRC2, and Mac_07_MARCO (Supplementary Fig. 11F). These results partially confirm the synergy between monocytes and macrophages.

Additionally, CytoTrace analysis was used to assess the differentiation potential of different myeloid cell subpopulations. In dendritic cells, the CytoTrace scores of the 3 subpopulations showed a gradually increasing trend, suggesting that the DC_03_LTK subpopulation may differentiate into the DC_01_CCR7 and DC_02_LCNL1 subpopulations. In macrophage subpopulations, significant differences in CytoTrace scores indicated potential transformation relationships. Most monocytes are likely derived from the Mon_20_FCN1 subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 11G-H).

Heterogeneity and functional characterization of fibroblasts and endothelial cells in the tumor immune microenvironment

Unsupervised clustering analysis revealed the heterogeneity of fibroblasts and endothelial cells in the TIME. Specifically, 8,056 fibroblasts were divided into 12 clusters (Fig. 3A) and categorized into five subtypes (Supplementary Fig. 10F); endothelial cells were divided into 19 clusters and similarly categorized into five subtypes (Fig. 4A). In fibroblasts, normal fibroblasts formed one subgroup, inflammatory cancer-associated fibroblasts (iCAFs) consisted of two subgroups, stromal cancer-associated fibroblasts (myCAFs) formed one subgroup, myofibroblast-like cancer-associated fibroblasts included three subgroups, and progenitor cancer-associated fibroblasts comprised four subgroups (Fig. 3B). Each of these subpopulations had distinct molecular markers (Supplementary Fig. 5). Endothelial cells were further subdivided into arterial endothelial cells (three subpopulations), venous endothelial cells (five subpopulations), immature endothelial cells (three subpopulations), proliferative endothelial cells (one subgroup), and lymphatic endothelial cells (one subgroup) (Fig. 4B, Supplementary Fig. 6). Gene set enrichment analysis based on the hallmark gene sets (Figs. 3D and 4D) revealed that most gene sets in fibroblast and endothelial cell subpopulations were in a suppressed state, indicating that these stromal cells are less active in tumor initiation and progression compared to immune cells. However, some cancer-associated fibroblast subpopulations exhibited significant enrichment in carcinogenic pathways. For example, the myCAFs_05_RERGL subgroup was notably enriched in the NOTCH signaling and oxidative stress pathways, while the iCAFs_01_HSD17B2 subgroup was enriched in the IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling and inflammation response pathways, suggesting a role for these subpopulations in promoting tumor progression.

SCENIC analysis further revealed transcription factors that are highly specific to each fibroblast and endothelial cell subpopulation (Fig. 3E and 4E). For example, the transcription factor MSX2 displayed significant regulatory activity in the matCAFs_03_COL7A1 subgroup, while TIGIF1 showed high specificity in the arterial endothelial cell Artery_03_FOXA2 subgroup. Moreover, cell–cell interaction analysis indicated that interactions between fibroblast subpopulations were relatively weak, whereas endothelial cell subpopulations exhibited more robust interactions (Figs. 3F and 4F), suggesting that endothelial cells play a more critical cooperative role in the TIME.

CytoTrace analysis used to assess differentiation potential (Figs. 3G-H and 4G-H) revealed that most progenitor cancer-associated fibroblasts had high differentiation potential, while the proCAFs_11_S100B subgroup showed significantly lower differentiation potential compared to other subpopulations. These results further emphasize the functional and differentiation trajectory differences of stromal cell subpopulations in the TIME, providing direction for studying their specific biological roles.

Identification of TIME ecological subtypes in colorectal cancer

To investigate how different immune and stromal cell subpopulations form tightly connected “ecosystems” within the TIME, we performed unsupervised analysis to infer cell relationships and their co-occurrence patterns. Following previous research methods, we analyzed the correlation of frequencies between non-tumor cell subpopulations in the TIME and identified two highly correlated cell modules (Fig. 5A). For each patient, the frequencies of cell clusters within the same module were summed, and the most abundant module was designated as the dominant cell module for that patient, dividing patients into two TIME subtypes: TIME subtype 1 and subtype 2. We found that TIME subtype 1 had significantly poorer overall survival compared to subtype 2 (Fig. 5B), with stage IV patients exclusively distributed in TIME subtype 1 (Fig. 5C). We then validated the presence of TIME ecological subtypes in large-scale CRC cohorts using bulk RNA sequencing data. Firstly, we identified the top 50 highly variable genes for each TIME cell cluster as characteristic genes and computed feature scores for each cluster using the ssGSEA algorithm. We found that the feature scores based on TIME cell clusters also successfully grouped external bulk-sequencing patients into two clusters with significantly different prognoses (Fig. 5D, F). PCA analysis further revealed distinct cell composition profiles between the two patient clusters (Fig. 5E, G). Functional enrichment analysis showed that TIME subtype 1 was mainly characterized by pathways related to metabolism and cell motility (Fig. 5K). This cluster was significantly enriched in pathways associated with lipid, vitamin, and carboxylic acid metabolism, as well as inorganic anion and organic acid transport. Additionally, pathways related to cilium or flagellum-dependent cell motility, such as sperm motility, cilium movement, and microtubule motor activity, were also enriched. These findings suggest that tumor cells in TIME subtype 1 may have higher metabolic activity and motility, which could be related to tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential. TIME subtype 2 was characterized by immune response and inflammation (Fig. 5L). This cluster showed significant enrichment in pathways related to leukocyte migration, cell chemotaxis, cell–cell adhesion, and immune regulation, particularly functions associated with T cell activation, cytokine-mediated signaling, and immune effector processes. These results suggest that TIME subtype 2 may have more abundant immune cell infiltration and stronger immune responses. Furthermore, we found that immune-related cytotoxic genes in TIME subtype 2 were significantly higher than those in subtype 1 (Fig. 5M). These features may be associated with better prognosis, as immune system activation generally helps suppress tumor growth and spread. To further analysis the differences in the microenvironments of TIME subtype 1 and subtype 2, we inferred the abundance of tumor-infiltrating immune cells in patients of TCGA COAD cohort using ABIS, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, EPIC, ESTIMATE, and xCell algorithms [16, 22–25]. We found that TIME subtype 2 exhibited generally higher levels of infiltrating immune cells compared with TIME subtype 1, which may indicate a greater potential for response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy (Supplementary Fig. 7A). We next evaluated several previously reported biomarkers predictive of ICB therapy response, including T cell inflamed score (TIS) [26], COX-2-associated inflammatory signature (COX-IS) [27], and tumor immune dysfunction and exclusion (TIDE) [28]. High TIS levels have been associated with improved responses to ICB therapy, whereas lower COX-IS and TIDE scores also predict greater benefit. Notably, TIME subtype 2 exhibited elevated TIS levels together with reduced COX-IS and TIDE scores (Supplementary Fig. 7B-C), collectively suggesting that patients within this subtype are more likely to respond favorably to ICB therapy.

In addition, to enhance the novelty and impact of our study, we collected tumor and adjacent normal tissues from 40 CRC patients at our center (Harbin cohort). Bulk RNA-seq was performed on these samples, and the TIME subtypes identified by our integrative analysis were successfully validated in this independent in-house cohort (Supplementary Fig. 8A–C). Patients in TIME subtype 1 exhibited poorer overall survival and disease-free survival (DFS). This results provided valuable insights into the prognostic value of TIME cell types in CRC.

CNV features and subpopulation analysis of malignant cells in colorectal cancer

Malignant cells in CRC typically evolve from epithelial cells. To further explore the malignant tumor cells in CRC, we performed inferCNV analysis on 151,184 cells, revealing significant differences between malignant tumor cells and normal epithelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 12A). We then quantified the CNV scores and CNV correlations for each epithelial cell and classified them into three categories: 54,639 normal epithelial cells, 53,081 potential malignant epithelial cells, and 43,464 malignant tumor cells (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Normal epithelial cells and abnormal epithelial cells were clearly distinguished into separate subpopulations (Supplementary Fig. 12C). Moreover, malignant tumor epithelial cells were further divided into 14 clusters (Supplementary Fig. 12D). Among these, Cluster 8 showed higher CNV scores, which may indicate a higher degree of malignancy (Supplementary Fig. 12E-F).

High expression of malignant epithelial gene SCRN1 promotes CRC cell proliferation and migration

To identify genes associated with the malignant phenotype in CRC, we performed differential expression analysis to identify genes differentially expressed in each epithelial cell subpopulation (Fig. 6A). In the most malignant Cluster 8, the top 5 most differentially expressed genes were SCRN1, RAB3B, TNS3, TNRC18, and HMGCS2, with SCRN1 being the most significantly different gene. SCRN1 encodes a member of the Secernin protein family, which regulates mast cell exocytosis [29]. Previous studies have shown that downregulation of SCRN1 inhibits invasion and migration in oral squamous cell carcinoma through the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway [30]. However, the specific expression pattern and functional role of SCRN1 in CRC progression are unclear. We found that SCRN1 mRNA levels were significantly higher in CRC tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 6B, C), and high SCRN1 expression was associated with poorer prognosis in CRC patients (Fig. 6D, E). Further analysis showed that SCRN1 expression was closely related to phenotypes, such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition, invasion, metastasis, and proliferation in CRC (Fig. 6F). These findings were also validated in Harbin cohort (Supplementary Fig. 8D-F). Additionally, patients with high SCRN1 expression were associated with poorer disease-free survival (Supplementary Fig. 8G). To validate the impact of SCRN1 on CRC proliferation and metastasis, we constructed SCRN1 knockdown and overexpression HCT116 cells. We found that SCRN1 overexpression enhanced HCT116 proliferation, colony formation, and migration (Fig. 7), while SCRN1 knockdown inhibited these abilities (Fig. 8). To elucidate the potential mechanisms of SCRN1-driven metastasis, we analyzed the differentially enriched pathways between patients with high and low SCRN1 expression in the TCGA COAD cohort (Supplementary Fig. 9). The functions of SCRN1 were closely linked to the extracellular matrix (ECM) and its associated signaling networks. The focal adhesion and ECM-receptor interaction pathways regulate tumor cell adhesion, migration, and invasion. Meanwhile, extracellular structure organization, extracellular matrix organization, and collagen fibril organization are involved in ECM remodeling and collagen fiber rearrangement. These processes are critical for metastasis, as dynamic remodeling of the ECM not only creates a permissive microenvironment for tumor cell migration but also activates integrin-mediated signaling pathways that enhance cellular motility and invasiveness [31, 32]. Thus, SCRN1 may promote tumor dissemination by driving ECM remodeling and activating related signaling cascades, facilitating tumor cells to escape from the primary site and establish distant metastases.

Discussion

CRC is a highly heterogeneous malignancy, and the TIME plays a critical role in its progression and metastasis [33]. In this study, we constructed a comprehensive single-cell atlas of CRC using a large-scale dataset, providing a thorough analysis of the cellular composition and functional features of TIME. This study not only revealed the heterogeneity of different cell subpopulations but also provided a deeper understanding of their roles in immune regulation, offering new insights into personalized immunotherapy strategies.

The CRC TIME consists of various immune cells, stromal cells, and epithelial cells, each exhibiting significant functional diversity and differentiation potential. In the T cell population, we identified several subpopulations with distinct functional features. For example, the CD8 + T cell subpopulation, CD8T_07_GZMK, was highly enriched in anti-tumor pathways, especially in the interferon response pathway, suggesting its potential role in tumor immune surveillance. In contrast, regulatory T cells (Tregs) and certain helper T cell subsets (such as Th_26_IL17A) exhibited pro-tumor characteristics, likely aiding tumor immune evasion by suppressing anti-tumor immune responses. These results are highly consistent with previous studies. Both Lee et al. and Zhang et al. identified Tregs, SPP1 + macrophages, and CD8 + T cells as key players in immune evasion and immune response [6, 34]. Notably, the CD8T_07_GZMK subgroup we identified showed an enrichment in interferon signaling pathways, which aligns with Lee’s description of the “activated CD8 + T cell-enriched subgroup,” further validating its core role in anti-tumor immunity.

B cell functionality also displays diversity, particularly in germinal center B cells and plasma cells. The former is active in multiple carcinogenic pathways, suggesting its potential role in immune regulation within the tumor microenvironment. However, plasma cells represented the predominant subset within the B cell compartment, consistent with previous reports in colorectal cancer and other solid tumors. This predominance may be attributed to chronic antigen exposure and persistent immune stimulation in the TIME, which continuously drives naïve and memory B cells toward terminal differentiation into antibody-producing plasma cells. Additionally, the presence of tumor-associated tertiary lymphoid structures and local class-switch recombination may further contribute to plasma cell accumulation. Plasma cells in the TIME can exhibit a functional duality. On one hand, they may support anti-tumor immunity through secretion of tumor-specific antibodies, facilitation of antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), and presentation of antigen to T cells. On the other hand, certain plasma cell subsets may exert immunosuppressive functions, for example by secreting IL-10, modulating T cell activation, and supporting immunoregulatory networks, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion. In our single-cell transcriptomic analysis, plasma cells displayed relatively lower activity across most hallmark pathways, indicating a transcriptionally quiescent state. However, this does not preclude their potential involvement in both effective humoral immunity and suppression of anti-tumor responses, depending on their phenotypic polarization. These observations highlight the complex and context-dependent role of plasma cells within the CRC TIME and warrant further functional validation.

Myeloid cells, such as macrophages, dendritic cells, and monocytes, also exhibit strong heterogeneity. Some macrophage subpopulations (e.g., Mac_05_S100B) are enriched in pathways related to tumor progression, such as oxidative stress and angiogenesis, suggesting their potential role in promoting tumor development by enhancing angiogenesis and intercellular signaling.

Although stromal cells are often overlooked in the TIME, our study emphasizes their importance in CRC progression. We found that fibroblasts and endothelial cells in TIME exhibit high heterogeneity, particularly cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which likely play a key role in regulating tumor progression. Through systematic analysis, we identified multiple CAF subtypes, including myofibroblast-like (myCAFs) and progenitor (proCAFs), which are significantly enriched in signaling pathways associated with tumor progression and immune evasion. In particular, the myCAFs subgroup is enriched in pathways related to angiogenesis and cell migration, suggesting that it may promote tumor aggressiveness by enhancing neoangiogenesis and increasing tumor cell migration. Our findings are consistent with Ma et al.’s research on the oncogenic role of CAFs, which highlighted the strong activity of iCAFs and myCAFs in the IL6-JAK-STAT3 and NOTCH signaling pathways [35]. Furthermore, we refined CAFs into four functional subtypes and, for the first time, applied CytoTRACE to analyze their differentiation potential. This finding deepens our understanding of the functional diversity of CAFs and reveals their plasticity and dynamic evolutionary characteristics within TIME, expanding the current understanding of stromal cell biology.

Endothelial cell subpopulations in TIME also exhibit diverse functions, particularly arterial and venous endothelial cells. While their role in immune responses is less discussed, our research suggests that they may contribute to regulating tumor vasculature stability and blood flow dynamics. The complex interaction network between endothelial cells may facilitate immune evasion in the tumor microenvironment, providing a survival niche for tumor cells to thrive and metastasize.

Through unsupervised clustering analysis, we identified two main TIME subtypes: TIME subtype 1 and TIME subtype 2. TIME subtype 1 is characterized by higher metabolic activity and cell motility, which correlates closely with tumor invasiveness and metastatic potential. Patients with subtype 1 generally have a poorer prognosis and are more commonly found in advanced-stage (Stage IV) cancer. In contrast, TIME subtype 2 is enriched in immune response pathways, suggesting stronger immune cell infiltration and anti-tumor responses, and is associated with better prognosis and a higher prevalence in early-stage (stage I/II) patients. Our analysis further confirmed that TIME subtype 1 is associated with poorer overall survival, while subtype 2 exhibits stronger immune responses and lower metastatic risk. Therefore, stratifying CRC patients based on the cellular composition of their TIME not only aids in prognosis prediction but also provides a basis for selecting appropriate immunotherapies. Additionally, although the previous CMS classification emphasized the association of CMS4 with stromal activation and poor prognosis [3], our TIME subtyping analysis not only aligns well with CMS (with CMS4 significantly enriched in TIME subtype 1) but also offers quantifiable cellular composition and functional feature scores, such as the comparison of metabolic/motility and immune activity pathways, further explaining the malignant biological basis of CMS4 at the mechanistic level.

The significant overexpression of SCRN1 in CRC has important clinical implications. Our study found that SCRN1 expression levels are closely related to CRC proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, and its high expression is significantly associated with poorer prognosis. SCRN1 promotes CRC progression by enhancing cell proliferation and migration. In vitro experiments confirmed that SCRN1 overexpression enhances CRC cell proliferation and migration, while SCRN1 knockdown significantly inhibits proliferation, colony formation, and migration. This finding is consistent with previous studies on SCRN1 in other cancer types, such as oral squamous cell carcinoma [30]. SCRN1 encodes a member of the Secernin protein family and can regulate exocytosis in mast cells [36]. Downregulation of SCRN1 inhibits the invasion and migration of oral squamous cell carcinoma cells via the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway [30]. DNA methylation is closely linked to tumor metastasis [37, 38]. Previous studies have demonstrated that SCRN1 exhibits DNA methylation-mediated modification in gastric cancer [39]. Thus, epigenetic silencing via DNA methylation may represent a potential mechanism through which SCRN1 drives metastatic progression. Our study reveals the specific role of SCRN1 in CRC and provides a theoretical foundation for its use as a new therapeutic target. In conclusion, SCRN1 holds promise as a new target for CRC therapy, offering a potential direction for the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Despite the large-scale single-cell sequencing analysis of the CRC tumor immune microenvironment, this study has some limitations. Firstly, this study is based on a single dataset, which may have sample source bias. Secondly, while we have deeply analyzed the functional characteristics of cell subpopulations, the cell–cell interaction networks within the TIME still need further validation. Future research should combine clinical samples and animal models to validate these findings and explore how regulating cell types within the TIME can improve CRC treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

This study constructed a comprehensive single-cell atlas of CRC, revealing the cellular composition, heterogeneity, and functional characteristics of the TIME. Through detailed cell typing and functional annotation, several key immune and stromal cell subpopulations were identified, highlighting the close relationship between different TIME subtypes and patient prognosis. Notably, the immune-responsive TIME subtype 2 may represent a population that benefits from immunotherapy. Furthermore, SCRN1, identified as a key factor promoting tumor progression in CRC, could serve as a new therapeutic target. This study provides essential resources and theoretical support for the development of personalized immunotherapy strategies and a deeper understanding of the TIME in CRC.
