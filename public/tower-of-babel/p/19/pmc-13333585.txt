Recent advances in metal–organic frameworks for the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer
Authors: Sanket N Fulkari, Mujibullah Sheikh, Manish P Deshmukh, Rajat Mahajan, Tejas pimple, Isha Mirzapure
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer remains the most prevalent malignancy among women worldwide, presenting significant clinical challenges due to tumor heterogeneity, multidrug resistance (MDR), and the severe systemic toxicity associated with conventional chemotherapy. In the rapidly evolving field of nanomedicine, Metal–Organic Frameworks (MOFs) have emerged as a superior class of porous nanomaterials with exceptional potential for oncology applications. Distinguished by their ultra-high surface area, tunable porosity, and biodegradable nature, MOFs offer a versatile platform for encapsulating chemotherapeutic agents, genetic materials, and photosensitizers. This review provides a comprehensive overview of the recent advancements in MOF-based nanoplatforms for the diagnosis and treatment of breast cancer. We critically analyze the synthesis and functionalization strategies that enhance the stability and targeting capability of MOFs in the physiological environment. Furthermore, we highlight the development of stimuli-responsive drug delivery systems that utilize the unique tumor microenvironment (pH, redox, and ATP) for controlled release. Finally, the review discusses the dual role of MOFs as theranostic agents integrating bioimaging (MRI, CT, and optical imaging) with therapeutic modalities (chemotherapy, photothermal, and photodynamic therapy) and outlines the future perspectives for their clinical translation.Graphical abstract Keywords: Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), Breast cancer, Nanomedicine, Targeted drug delivery, Theranostics, Nanoparticles

Introduction

Female Breast Cancer is still a global health as well as economic burden and is the most frequent cancer diagnosis worldwide, more common than lung cancer (with an estimated 2.3 million = 11.6% new cases globally in year 2020). This high prevalence contributes to a tremendous health hazard; in 2020 alone nearly 10 million cancer deaths occurred worldwide [1]. Although the traditional treatments such as surgery, natural agent-based chemotherapy, and radiotherapy are the major approaches, these methods have obvious disadvantages, with respect to inhibition of metastasis and tissue toxicity [2, 3].

Current therapeutic options for breast cancer including surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy still play a central role, but they have obvious shortcomings in suppression of metastasis and tissue toxicity [4]. Surgical resection cures patients with early breast cancer but is associated with the risk of postoperative recurrence and is often inadequate for advanced malignancies [5]. Radiotherapy, especially when directed at the hypothalamic- pituitary axis, may result in growth hormone deficiency (GHD) causing growth abnormalities and metabolic co-morbidities, particularly in children [6]. Although chemotherapy is essential for advanced and metastatic disease, it often leads to myelosuppression as a result of cytotoxicity on rapidly proliferating cells, including hematopoietic system [7]. This is also complicated by problems such as drug resistance and the low aqueous solubility of many drugs. Such challenges underscore the importance of precision therapeutics [8, 9].

Novel delivery systems including liposomes, inclusion complexes, and polymeric carriers (Fig. 1) have also demonstrated potential in enhancing the bioavailability and decreasing systemic toxicity of drugs by altering the pharmacokinetics, such as utilizing the enhanced permeability of tumors [3, 10, 11]. However, these systems have limitations because liposomes have low drug-loading efficiency and are unstable in blood. And various problems including non-porosity and the weak interactions between drug and matrices contribute to the uncontrolled release of drugs, especially for breast cancer that lacks of an effective therapeutic targeting strategy [12].

Overview of novel drug delivery carriers used for advanced therapeutic delivery systems

MOFs are hybrid organic–inorganic materials constituted by metal ions or clusters coordinated to organic linkers to generate one, two, and three-dimensional frameworks with intrinsic voids in the organic ligands [13, 14]. This class of hybrid materials combines the advantages of high adsorption capacity, large surface area, and strong porosity and robust thermal and chemical stability, which collectively enable diverse applications [14–17]. Their distinctive properties make them promise in many fields, such as photocatalysis, electrochemistry, gas storage and separation, energy technologies (conversion and storage), imaging/sensing/biomedicine [13].

A significant advantage of MOFs in drug delivery, particularly compared to liposomes, lies in their superior drug loading capacities, attributable to their precisely tunable pore geometries [18]. Nevertheless, it should be noted that MOFs may show significant batch-to-batch variability in their properties [18]. The stiff structure of MOFs is also crucial to boost tumor penetration ability, particularly in the dense stromal space present in breast cancer [19, 20]. In addition, the inclusion of Active Targeting mechanisms (through attachment to specific ligands, aptamers or antibodies on surface) allows active targeting and significantly enhances anti-tumor drug concentration over the conventional carrier systems [21].

Although the presence of ligand bonds in MOFs can promote structural instability during their exposure to complicated biological environments, these nanomaterials paradoxically demonstrate exceptional promise as theranostic carriers, especially in the context of breast cancer management [19]. The controlled release kinetics is important for a precise delivery of the chemotherapeutic drugs, which is made possible by their inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability [18, 22]. This is accomplished through a combination of two targeting strategies: 1) passive targeting, in which particles accumulate in tumor tissues due to enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect (tumor vasculature that is highly leaky accompanied by poor lymphatic drainage) [23, 24]. Secondly, active targeting is performed via surface-conjugated biological moieties like ligands, aptamers or antibodies, which can attach specifically to cancer cells overexpressing particular receptors and thus augment local delivery and uptake [23]. This multi-modal approach serves to collectively enhance drug accumulation in tumors and diminish off-target toxicity, thus directly addressing key limitations commonly faced by conventional chemotherapy [24].

This review article aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the recent advances in the design and application of MOFs for breast cancer diagnosis and treatment. We will first delve into the fundamental classification of MOFs based on their structural characteristics, metal ion composition e.g., zinc-based, iron-based, zirconium-based, organic linkers, and functional properties, such as stimuli-responsiveness and imaging capabilities. Subsequently, we will explore the various synthesis and modification strategies employed to engineer MOFs with enhanced stability, biocompatibility, and targeting specificity towards breast cancer cells. A significant portion of this review will be dedicated to MOF-based drug delivery systems, detailing drug loading strategies, mechanisms of controlled and targeted release including pH-responsive, redox, and enzyme-responsive systems, and their critical roles in co-delivery of therapeutic and imaging agents. The applications of MOFs in breast cancer diagnosis, therapy including chemotherapy enhancement, photodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy, and chemodynamic therapy, and integrated theranostics will be thoroughly discussed, supported by evidence from both in vitro and in vivo studies Finally, we will address the current challenges, including toxicity concerns, reproducibility, and scale-up barriers, and outline future research directions necessary for the successful clinical translation of MOF-based nanoplatforms for personalized breast cancer management (Fig. 2).

Key categorization strategies MOFs

MOFs are porous crystalline coordination polymers obtained from metal ions (or clusters) and multitopic organic linkers, which can incorporate different guest species such as therapeutic molecules and counterions [25, 26]. Over the past two decades we have seen extraordinary increases in the architectural diversity of MOFs, driven by the targeted modification of their metal nodes and organic linkers [27]. Such structural adaptability means that they can be used for a variety of applications, including DDSs, catalysis, sensing and energy technologies [28, 29]. In this context, MOF-based DDSs have emerged as a promising platform for controlled and targeted drug delivery. Expanded classification of MOFs by metal ions for drug delivery

MOFs are characterized by their precisely synthesized molecular building blocks, offering intrinsic porosity, large surface areas, and customizable pore sizes and shapes [27]. These features are critical for their application in various fields, including carbon capture, Gas Separation, energy storage, and biomedical applications such as drug delivery and imaging [13]. The exceptional structural and chemical tunability of MOFs allows for their rational design to meet specific application requirements [30].

Classification based on metal ions

The selection of metal ions in the design of MOFs for biomedical applications, particularly in drug delivery systems (DDSs) for breast cancer therapy, is a key determinant of biosafety, biocompatibility, and clinical translatability. Metal ion choice governs three interdependent criteria: biocompatibility, biodegradation kinetics, and inherent cytotoxicity. While many transition and post-transition metals offer favorable coordination chemistry for MOF synthesis, their biological interactions are often concentration-dependent and can introduce toxicological risks. This supports the preference for physiologically endogenous metal ions such as Fe3+/Fe2+, Zn2+, Cu2+, and Ca2+, which are regulated through natural homeostatic mechanisms and provide a safer profile. However, even endogenous ions demand rigorous biosafety profiling that extends beyond mere elemental identity. Critical parameters including route of administration (intravenous, oral, intratumoral), duration of exposure, chemical speciation (free ion vs. framework-bound vs. metabolite), and pharmacokinetics (clearance half-life, organ-specific bioaccumulation, particularly in the liver and spleen) must be systematically evaluated [51]. For example, while Zr4+ is not endogenous, zirconium-based MOFs such as UiO-66 have demonstrated exceptional hydrolytic stability and low acute toxicity, making them prominent candidates for sustained drug release in the acidic tumor microenvironment of breast cancer [52]. Conversely, non-endogenous metals like gadolinium (Gd3+) and platinum (Pt2+/Pt4+) are employed for specialized functions Gd3+ as an MRI contrast agent and Pt complexes as cytotoxic payloads but their use introduces significant long-term safety concerns, including nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (for Gd) and cumulative neuro- and nephrotoxicity (for Pt), which require careful mitigation through robust framework encapsulation and controlled release kinetics [53]. Crucially, the biodegradation behavior of the MOF must align with the therapeutic timeline: overly stable frameworks may persist indefinitely, causing chronic inflammation, whereas excessively labile structures may disintegrate before reaching the target site. Therefore, rational metal ion selection must be integrated with linker chemistry and surface engineering to achieve a balanced profile of stability, functionality, and safe metabolic clearance, ensuring that the MOF platform acts as a transient, responsive vehicle rather than a persistent foreign body [51, 54].

Ca-MOFs

Calcium-based metal–organic frameworks (Ca-MOFs) have received more attention because of the multi-functionality in biomedicine, such as bone regeneration and anti-infection [59]. These materials exploit the inherent bioactivity of calcium ions (Ca2+), which play essential roles in physiological processes such as neuromuscular coordination, blood coagulation, and osteogenesis. Some research proved the excellent potential of Ca-MOFs and its composites in bone reconstruction. For example, Ca-Sr-MOFs that consist of calcium and strontium (other osteoinductive element) were prepared with organic ligands such as 1,3,5-tricarboxylicbenzene (H3BTC). These MOFs are biodegradable in aqueous environments, and they can release osteogenic Ca2+ and Sr2+ ions, inducing in vitro biomineralization and preosteoblastic cell differentiation [60]. Microstructure-remodelable scaffold could offer a micro-environment for bone healing by its osteoinductive [61]. The release of metal ions, bioactive ligands, or drug molecules (encapsulated within MOFs) in a sustained manner from the MOFs leads to their biological activity towards bone or cartilage tissue [62]. In addition, MOFs can protect drugs from degradation and stimulate the release of entrapped substances under different stimuli, for example pH, enzymes and reactive oxygen species (ROS) [13].

Beyond osteogenesis, Ca-MOFs and related MOF systems exhibit potent antimicrobial properties, which are crucial for preventing implant-related infections, a common complication in orthopedic surgery [61, 63]. The antibacterial mechanisms of MOFs are diverse and include the sustained release of intrinsic or exogenous antimicrobial components, chemodynamically catalyzed generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and direct physical interaction with bacterial cell membranes [64, 65].

For example, Vadivelmurugan et al. [66] synthesized Ca-Sr-MOF made by co-precipitation followed by aminomalononitrile (AMN) surface treatment had very high antibiotic loading capability and the levels achieved were 63% for antibiotic; loaded Ca-Sr-MOF and 57% with; Ce-Sr-AMN-MOF. These systems also presented release behavior controllable, cumulative percentage releases of 55.15 and 9.1% within a period of up to 72 h, respectively. Both formulations demonstrated extremely effective antimicrobial activity with more than 90% of the bacteria inhibited; but at the same time very high cellular viability for osteoblast cells (> 95% at day 1). This further emphasizes their biphasic capability as antibiofilm agents and bone cell health promoter [59].

Another example of MOFs with bifunctionality is ZIF-8 (Zeolitic Imidazolate Framework-8), a zinc-based MOF, has attracted considerable attention among the various types of MOFs occasionally applied to bone repair for its capability of promoting angiogenesis, loading drugs and antibacterial activity in some studies. ZIF-8 could be able to release Zn2+, The released Zn2+ Further interacting with certain receptors in the cell membrane and activating a series of signaling passageways to drive osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs followed by bone generation [67]. Zinc ions have also been reported to contribute to antimicrobial processes like disruption of bacterial metabolism and enzyme activity [68].

While Ca-MOFs offer excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and osteogenic potential, their relatively lower structural stability and limited intrinsic catalytic activity can restrict their performance in certain drug delivery applications. In contrast, other metal-based MOFs present distinct advantages. For example, zirconium-based MOFs (Zr-MOFs) exhibit superior chemical stability and high drug-loading capacity, making them suitable for long-term delivery systems. Copper-based MOFs (Cu-MOFs) and iron-based MOFs (Fe-MOFs) provide enhanced catalytic and redox properties, enabling applications in chemodynamic therapy and ROS-mediated drug release, though they may raise concerns regarding cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. Potassium-based MOFs (K-MOFs), although less explored, offer advantages in ionic conductivity and mild biological interactions.

Thus, each class of metal-based MOFs presents a unique balance between biocompatibility, stability, drug-loading efficiency, and therapeutic functionality. Understanding these differences is critical for selecting appropriate MOF systems tailored to specific drug delivery and biomedical applications.

Zr-MOFs

Zirconium-based metal–organic frameworks (Zr-MOFs) have gained significant attention due to their remarkable coordination flexibility and superior chemical stability [69]. Sun et al. [70]synthesized a carbazolyl-functionalized UiO-67-CDC through a solvothermal process, followed by methylation to obtain UiO-67-CDC-(CH3)2, achieving an impressive 56.5 wt% anticancer drug loading capacity. These electron-deficient Zr-MOFs also displayed highly sensitive fluorescence sensing behavior, driven by charge transfer mechanisms. Similarly, Jiang et al. [71] designed a Zr-cluster-based framework, ZJU-800, which showed a notable 58.8 wt% drug loading efficiency along with excellent biocompatibility, maintaining over 90% cell viability as confirmed by MTT assay.

From a comparative perspective, Zr-MOFs offer clear advantages over Ca-MOFs in terms of structural stability, higher surface area, and enhanced drug loading capacity. These properties make them particularly suitable for sustained and controlled drug delivery. However, unlike Ca-MOFs, Zr-MOFs lack intrinsic bioactivity related to tissue regeneration, such as osteoinduction. In addition, their relatively low biodegradability may limit rapid clearance from biological systems, which is an important consideration for long-term applications.

Cu-MOFs

Compared to iron-based MOFs, copper-based metal–organic frameworks (Cu-MOFs) have been less explored but show strong potential for drug delivery due to the catalytic properties of Cu2+ ions in biologically relevant reactions [72, 73]. Li et al. [43] developed a nanoscale Cu-BTC framework functionalized with diethyldithiocarbamate (DDTC), a key metabolite of the alcohol-aversion drug disulfiram. The Cu2+–DDTC coordination led to the formation of a Cu-DDTC complex with potent proteasome-inhibiting antitumor activity. However, the system exhibited limited in vivo stability, highlighting a key challenge associated with Cu-MOF-based platforms.

The engineered Cu-BTC@DDTC nanoplatform demonstrated significant anticancer efficacy, achieving 89% ± 0.4% tumor cell inhibition at a concentration of 4 μg/mL within 48 h. Mechanistic studies revealed that its therapeutic effect is mediated through ferroptosis induction via suppression of the SLC7A11/GPX4 signaling pathway. In addition, administration of Cu-BTC@DDTC (10 mg/kg) in combination with low-dose cisplatin (1 mg/kg) resulted in enhanced antitumor activity and improved biosafety, indicating a synergistic therapeutic effect.

From a comparative standpoint, Cu-MOFs offer distinct advantages over Zr-MOFs and Ca-MOFs due to their intrinsic catalytic and redox activity, which enables advanced therapeutic mechanisms such as chemodynamic therapy and ferroptosis induction. However, these benefits are accompanied by notable limitations, including lower structural stability and potential cytotoxicity associated with excess copper ion release. Compared to Fe-MOFs, Cu-MOFs often exhibit stronger catalytic efficiency but may pose greater challenges in maintaining biocompatibility and controlled ion release.

Fe-MOFs

Iron ions (Fe), which are vital trace elements involved in hematopoiesis and immune regulation, possess well-established biosafety and biocompatibility profiles. These characteristics make iron-based metal–organic frameworks (Fe-MOFs) highly attractive for biomedical synthesis and therapeutic applications. For optimal biomedical performance, Fe-MOFs must meet key criteria, including precise control over particle size, ease of synthesis, excellent biocompatibility and non-toxicity, strong chemical stability, and tunable biodegradability [74]. Iron-based materials of the Lavoisier Institute (MILs) demonstrate superior biodegradability and lower cytotoxicity compared to other MOF subclasses [75]. In vitro studies using MTT and CCK-8 assays have confirmed the biocompatibility of MIL-88, MIL-100, and MIL-101 when interfaced with both human and animal cell lines [31, 76]. Recent innovations include a litchi-like porous composite, Fe3O4@Fe-MOF@Hap, developed by Yang et al. [77], which exhibits a high drug-loading capacity of 75.38 mg/g, a saturation magnetization of 34 emu/g enabling magnetic targeting, pH-responsive drug release behavior, and excellent cytocompatibility.

From a comparative perspective, Fe-MOFs offer a balanced combination of biocompatibility, biodegradability, and functional versatility. Compared to Cu-MOFs, they exhibit lower cytotoxicity and improved safety profiles, although their catalytic activity in redox-based therapies is generally less pronounced. In contrast to Zr-MOFs, Fe-MOFs provide better biodegradability and physiological compatibility, but they often show lower structural stability under harsh conditions. When compared with Ca-MOFs, Fe-MOFs lack inherent osteogenic properties but compensate with broader applicability in drug delivery, imaging, and magnetic targeting.

K-MOFs

Potassium-based metal–organic frameworks (K-MOFs) possess unique dual functionality, combining structural versatility with biological safety. Their porous architectures enable energy-related applications such as selective ion transport, catalytic reactions, and gas adsorption, while their low cytotoxicity and strong environmental compatibility make them promising candidates for drug delivery systems. Cyclodextrins (CDs) biocompatible cyclic oligosaccharides act as natural linkers in the formation of CD-MOFs, resulting in three main variants: K-α-CD-MOF, K-β-CD-MOF, and K-γ-CD-MOF. These frameworks improve drug solubility, enhance bioavailability, maintain structural stability, and ensure excellent biosafety [78, 79]. Kristi et al. [80] successfully achieved 9–30 wt% active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) loading in K-γ-CD-MOF through crystallization optimization, demonstrating significant cytoprotective effects in both mammalian cell cultures and Danio rerio (zebrafish) models.

From a comparative perspective, K-MOFs offer advantages in terms of biocompatibility and mild biological interactions when compared to Cu-MOFs and, to some extent, Fe-MOFs, where metal ion-associated toxicity remains a concern. However, their drug loading capacity and structural robustness are generally lower than those of Zr-MOFs, which limits their application in high-capacity or long-term drug delivery systems. Unlike Ca-MOFs, K-MOFs do not exhibit intrinsic bioactivity such as osteoinduction, and compared to Fe-MOFs, they lack multifunctional properties such as magnetic targeting or redox responsiveness.

Classification by organic ligands

Theoretically, the number of possible MOF combinations exceeds thousands, enabled by the systematic variation of metal nodes and organic linkers. Experimentally validated frameworks primarily employ carboxylate, phosphonate, sulfonate, imidazolate, and phenolate ligands, with dicarboxylic or polycarboxylic acids serving as the main rigid scaffolds [81]. Ligand functionalization plays a crucial role in fine-tuning the physicochemical properties [82] of MOFs, generating highly active sites suitable for multifunctional therapeutic applications. For example, porphyrin–phthalocyanine-based MOFs containing phosphonate or azolate linkers demonstrate excellent water solubility and structural stability [83].

In drug delivery systems (DDSs), the choice of linker must carefully balance physicochemical stability, biocompatibility, and degradation kinetics. Abánades Lázaro et al. [84] showed that fumarate-ligated zirconium MOFs exhibit superior stability compared to UiO-66 analogs, attributed to lower ligand pKa values that intensify phosphate–carboxylate coordination competition. Ligand chemistry also influences drug loading and release behavior such as the semirigid 5-(4′-carboxyphenoxy)nicotinic acid (H2cpon)-derived Zn-cpon-1, synthesized via ClO4⁻-templated assembly, which achieved significantly higher loading for 5-fluorouracil (5-FU, 44.75 wt%) compared to 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP, 4.79 wt%) [85].

Bio-metal–organic frameworks (bio-MOFs) have emerged as a next-generation subclass that integrates biomolecules such as nucleobases, cyclodextrins, enzymes, peptides, porphyrins, sugars, and amino acids as linkers [86]. This design merges the tunable structural properties of MOFs with inherent biocompatibility and biodegradability. For instance, a Zn–curcumin bio-MOF has been successfully synthesized, offering permanent porosity and an exceptionally high surface area of up to 3002 m2/g [87]. Recent studies have explored bio-MOFs for diverse applications including targeted anticancer drug delivery [88], adsorption systems, and biosensing technologies. However, challenges remain, including complex synthesis procedures with poor reproducibility, high production costs, and limited stability under physiological conditions [89].

Biocompatibility and biodegradability of MOFs in biomedical applications

Biocompatibility and biodegradability are critical determinants for the successful clinical translation of MOFs, particularly in cancer diagnosis and therapy, including breast cancer. MOFs are composed of metal ions/clusters and organic ligands, and their biological behavior is strongly influenced by these constituents, as well as particle size, morphology, surface chemistry, and colloidal stability [90]. In general, MOFs constructed from endogenous or low-toxicity metals (e.g., Fe, Zn, Ca) and biocompatible organic linkers (e.g., amino acids, peptides, or porphyrins) exhibit improved safety profiles and reduced cytotoxicity [89, 91]. However, despite their promising properties, concerns remain regarding potential toxicity arising from metal ion release, accumulation in organs, and long-term immunological responses, necessitating comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations [92].

The biodegradability of MOFs is a critical feature that facilitates controlled release of therapeutic payloads and eventual clearance from the body. Degradation primarily occurs through hydrolytic cleavage of coordination bonds between metal nodes and organic linkers under physiological conditions (pH 4–7.4, aqueous milieu), with acidic endo/lysosomal environments (~ pH 4.5–5.5) significantly accelerating framework disassembly 23. This process leads to the release of metal ions and organic fragments into biological systems. While certain metal ions such as Fe2+/3+, Mg2+, and Ca2+ are endogenous and can be safely metabolized, others like Co2+, Ni2+, or Cr3+ may induce oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory responses depending on concentration and exposure duration 45. The biological fate of MOFs involves opsonization, macrophage uptake, lysosomal processing, and eventual renal or hepatic clearance, which is modulated by particle size, surface charge, and PEGylation status 6. Despite significant preclinical progress, clinical translation remains hindered by batch-to-batch variability, incomplete understanding of long-term in vivo safety, lack of standardized toxicity assays, and undefined regulatory pathways for nanoscale MOFs.

Biodegradability is equally important, as non-degradable MOFs may accumulate in biological systems and lead to chronic toxicity. Ideally, MOFs should undergo controlled degradation into non-toxic, excretable byproducts under physiological or tumor-specific conditions (e.g., acidic pH in tumor microenvironments). Studies have shown that certain MOFs exhibit pH-responsive or enzyme-mediated degradation, enabling safe clearance and controlled drug release [93]. Nevertheless, some MOFs still suffer from poor degradability and stability in aqueous environments, which can result in bioaccumulation and limit their biomedical applicability [94]. To address these challenges, various strategies such as surface functionalization, polymer coating, and the development of bio-MOFs have been proposed to enhance both biodegradability and biocompatibility [95, 96].

Further supporting evidence highlights that the biological fate of MOFs is highly dependent on their physicochemical properties, particularly particle size, surface charge, and hydrophilicity, which directly influence cellular uptake, circulation time, and clearance pathways [90, 97]. Smaller nanoscale MOFs generally exhibit enhanced cellular internalization but may also induce higher cytotoxicity due to increased surface reactivity and faster ion release [92]. In contrast, surface-engineered MOFs, such as those coated with polymers, lipids, or biomolecules, demonstrate improved hemocompatibility, reduced immune recognition, and prolonged systemic circulation, thereby enhancing their therapeutic efficacy [96].

In vivo studies further reveal that the degradation behavior of MOFs is closely linked to the coordination strength between metal nodes and organic ligands; frameworks with weaker coordination bonds tend to degrade more readily under physiological conditions, facilitating renal or hepatobiliary excretion [90]. For instance, iron-based and zinc-based MOFs are widely regarded as safer candidates due to their inherent biodegradability and the essential biological roles of their metal ions. Nevertheless, incomplete degradation or premature disintegration may compromise therapeutic performance or lead to unintended toxicity, emphasizing the need for precise control over degradation kinetics [96].

Moreover, emerging strategies such as the design of stimulus-responsive MOFs (e.g., pH-, redox-, or enzyme-sensitive systems) have shown great promise in improving both safety and therapeutic selectivity, particularly in tumor environments where acidic pH and elevated enzyme levels can trigger site-specific degradation and drug release [98]. Additionally, hybrid systems such as polymer–MOF composites and bio-MOFs have demonstrated enhanced structural stability in circulation while retaining controlled biodegradability at target sites, thereby balancing efficacy and safety [89, 94].

Beyond their physicochemical advantages, the reported drug-loading efficiencies and favorable cytocompatibility profiles of MOFs hold significant clinical implications for breast cancer therapy. Compared with conventional nanocarriers such as liposomes and polymeric nanoparticles, MOFs provide substantially higher loading capacities due to their tunable pore structures and exceptionally large surface areas, enabling efficient encapsulation of chemotherapeutic agents, photosensitizers, and immunomodulatory compounds. This feature is particularly important in breast cancer management, where maintaining therapeutic drug concentrations within tumor tissues while minimizing systemic toxicity remains a critical challenge. Moreover, the excellent cytocompatibility demonstrated by Fe-, Ca-, and Zr-based MOFs supports their suitability for further translational and preclinical investigations. The combination of controlled release behavior, tumor-targeting capability, and reduced off-target toxicity positions MOFs as promising multifunctional drug delivery systems for precision oncology, particularly for aggressive and drug-resistant breast cancer subtypes such as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). In addition, the integration of stimuli-responsive and ligand-targeted strategies may further improve therapeutic selectivity and patient outcomes in future clinical applications.

Synthesis and modification strategies of MOFs

MOFs are engineered through intricate synthesis and modification strategies, where the selection of metal ions, organic linkers, and any encapsulated drug molecules or solvents is critical for their final architecture and performance [99, 100]. The choice of synthesis method and specific experimental conditions profoundly dictates the crystallinity, structure, properties, and functionality of MOFs [101]. These highly Porous Materials, formed by coordinating metal ions/clusters with multidentate organic ligands, have garnered significant attention across various applications, including gas storage, catalysis, and separations [102].

The synthesis of MOFs follows a bottom-up approach, where molecular building blocks self-assemble into ordered crystalline structures [100]. This process is driven by the reversibility of coordination bonds, which enables error correction and promotes formation of a stable, low-energy crystalline state [103]. Various synthesis techniques have been developed to control these structural features, as summarized in Table 4, which outlines commonly used methods for MOF fabrication and highlights their relevance to tuning material properties. Modern approaches focus on sustainability, incorporating green solvents and mechanochemistry to reduce environmental impact [104]. In addition, reticular chemistry and defect engineering allow precise control over pore size and active sites, enabling customization of MOFs for specific applications[103].

Among the various synthesis strategies, solvothermal and hydrothermal methods remain the most widely employed because they produce highly crystalline and structurally stable MOFs; however, they often require long reaction times, elevated temperatures, and large solvent volumes, which limit scalability and sustainability. Microwave-assisted synthesis offers rapid crystallization and improved control over particle size, but its industrial scalability and uniform heat distribution remain challenging. Mechanochemical synthesis is considered an environmentally friendly and solvent-free alternative with short processing times, although precise control over morphology and crystallinity is comparatively difficult. Sonochemical methods enable rapid nucleation and the formation of nanosized particles, but reproducibility and large-scale production remain limitations. Electrochemical synthesis provides energy-efficient MOF fabrication under mild conditions, yet its applicability is restricted to conductive metal systems. Ionothermal and deep eutectic solvent-based methods offer unique structural tunability and greener synthesis environments; however, solvent recovery and process optimization are still under development. Continuous-flow synthesis represents a promising strategy for large-scale and reproducible MOF production, although it requires specialized reactor systems and careful parameter optimization. Finally, post-synthetic modification provides exceptional flexibility for tailoring MOF functionality without reconstructing the framework, but excessive modification may compromise structural stability and porosity. Overall, the selection of an appropriate synthesis method depends on the intended biomedical application, desired physicochemical properties, scalability, and biocompatibility requirements.

Safety is a vital issue in biomedical applications of MOFs. As MOFs are usually synthesized along with solvents, it is important to use biocompatible, non-toxic and biodegradable solvents to minimize cytotoxicity. In designing MOFs for drug delivery, the selection of synthesis protocol should be balanced in terms of biostability, pore and particle size and drug loading efficiency and controlled released behaviors to ensure biocompatibility in physiological environment. After the initial synthesis, MOF structures can be modified to enhance their susceptibility and adapt them for specific biomedical applications, particularly in targeted drug delivery. Functionalization has two usual paths; during the synthesis (i.e., insitu modification: ligand exchange) or after the synthesis (i.e., postsynthesis modification: surface grafting) [118, 119]. The MOFs are then subjected to comprehensive physicochemical studies upon synthesis to verify their efficacy for use in biomedicine. The following analyses are used: (1) Powder X-ray Diffraction (PXRD), and Electron Microscopy (TEM, SEM) for structural identification; (2) Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR), Elemental Analysis (EA) for chemical composition upon utilization of the constituent PNCs, [43] which also serve to deduce drug loading and release characteristics using a standard spectrophotometric method attached to UV–vis or fluorescence spectrometers [120]. Overall, these evaluations confirm the mechanical properties, in vitro activity and biocompatibility of the framework, which reduce concerns about potential toxicity.

MOFs for breast cancer diagnosis

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

MOFs represent a rapidly evolving class of hybrid nanomaterials exhibiting significant promise as contrast agents for advanced bioimaging in breast cancer diagnosis, owing to their tunable structures, high porosity, and diverse compositions [121–123]. Their inherent versatility allows for tailored designs that enhance image contrast and specificity across various modalities.

In MRI, MOFs have emerged as potent contrast agents (CAs), improving the visualization of breast lesions by influencing the relaxation times of water protons [124, 125]. Gadolinium-based MOFs (Gd-MOFs) are particularly effective for T1-weighted imaging, with researchers demonstrating their superior performance stemming from the high relaxivity of gadolinium ions and the tunable porosity of MOFs, which facilitates efficient water molecule exchange [125]. Gadolinium () enhances MRI images due to its powerful paramagnetism from seven unpaired electrons, which accelerates the T1 relaxation of nearby water protons [126]. For instance, Karaca et al. [127] synthesized Gd-MOFs using terephthalic acid and gadolinium (III) chloride hexahydrate, evaluating them as potential drug delivery systems with imaging capabilities. Conversely, iron-based MOFs, such as the Fe-MIL series, are extensively explored for T2-weighted imaging. Zhou et al. [124] synthesized an iron-based hydrogen-bonded organic framework (Fe-HOF) from Fe-TCPP, demonstrating its potential as a T2-weighted MRI CA with superior relaxivity. Similarly, Wang et al. [128] synthesized four Fe-MOFs based on triangular Fe3O clusters with different topologies, including MIL-101(Fe) and MIL-100(Fe), showing topology-dependent T2 relaxivity for enhanced tumor monitoring. These iron-based MOFs are favored for their biocompatibility and robust magnetic properties, which improve contrast while minimizing potential toxicity [50, 129]. The development of such iron-based agents addresses the limitations of conventional iron oxide nanoparticles, which, despite clinical approval, have faced obstacles in widespread commercial use [124]. In MRI, T1-weighted images appear bright where protons relax quickly (e.g., fat), while T2-weighted images appear bright where protons stay in sync for longer (e.g., water in a cyst); dense cancerous tissue typically has a short T2 time and appears dark. Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced (DCE) imaging with gadolinium agents leverages the paramagnetic properties of gadolinium to dramatically shorten T1 relaxation, making compromised areas, such as tumors with leaky vessels, stand out [130]. Cui et al. [131] reported a microwave-responsive gadolinium metal–organic framework nanosystem designed for magnetic resonance imaging–guided cancer thermotherapy combined with immunotherapy. In their study, a porous Gd-based MOF was used as both an MRI contrast agent and a drug carrier for anti-PD-1 antibodies, taking advantage of the strong T1 relaxation enhancement provided by paramagnetic Gd3 + ions. To address common limitations of MOFs, such as premature drug leakage and insufficient tumor retention, the authors incorporated a phase-change material, 1-tetradecanol, and further camouflaged the nanoparticles with homologous tumor cell membranes. This hierarchical design enabled controlled drug release triggered by microwave-induced heating and improved tumor accumulation through membrane-mediated homologous targeting. Computed tomography (CT)

High-Z MOFs, particularly those incorporating hafnium (Hf) and zirconium (Zr), have emerged as promising dual-function agents for breast cancer theranostics, serving both as radiosensitizers and computed tomography (CT) contrast agents. Their high atomic numbers (Z = 72 for Hf and Z = 40 for Zr) enable superior X-ray attenuation, making them effective CT contrast enhancers compared to conventional iodine-based agents. For instance, nanoscale Hf-MOFs and Zr-MOFs (such as UiO-66-type frameworks) coated with biocompatible polymers have demonstrated excellent imaging contrast and in vivo stability in CT imaging [132]. Moreover, Hf-based MOFs exhibit strong radiosensitizing capabilities by enhancing local radiation dose deposition and promoting reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, which intensifies DNA damage and apoptosis in cancer cells, thereby improving radiotherapy efficacy [133]. In preclinical cancer models, hafnium MOFs such as UiO-66-NH2 (Hf) significantly improved tumor regression following radiotherapy by increasing X-ray absorption and inducing oxidative stress [134]. Additionally, Pd-loaded Hf-MOFs have demonstrated enhanced tumor localization and superior CT imaging compared to conventional agents, highlighting their potential for imaging-guided therapy [135]. Collectively, Hf- and Zr-based MOFs offer an integrated platform for breast cancer diagnosis and treatment, providing precise imaging and effective radiosensitization, with ongoing research advancing their translation into clinical applications.

Zhou et al. [134] synthesized UiO-66-NH2 (Hf) nanoparticles as novel hafnium-based metal–organic framework (Hf-MOF) radiosensitizers designed to improve radiotherapy efficiency. Due to the high atomic number (Z = 72) of hafnium, these nanoparticles significantly enhanced X-ray absorption and secondary electron emission, thereby increasing local radiation dose deposition at tumor sites. In vitro and in vivo results demonstrated that UiO-66-NH2 (Hf) effectively induced apoptosis and DNA double-strand breaks in cancer cells through reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, validating their potential as safe and stable nanoradiosensitizers with minimal cytotoxicity to normal cells.

Similarly, Ding et al. [136] provided a comprehensive overview of hafnium-based nanomaterials (HfO2 nanoparticles, Hf-organic hybrids, and Hf-MOFs), emphasizing their dual role as CT imaging contrast enhancers and radiosensitizers. The authors highlighted that Hf-based systems like NBTXR3 and UiO-66-Hf exhibit excellent electron density and biocompatibility, making them superior alternatives to iodine- and gadolinium-based agents. Importantly, Ding et al. emphasized their translational potential in cancer theranostics, as Hf-based nanomaterials combine imaging visibility, radiotherapy sensitization, and therapeutic safety, positioning them as key candidates for clinical radiotherapy augmentation (Ding et al., 2023).

Earlier, Dekrafft et al. [132] demonstrated that both Zr- and Hf-based nanoscale MOFs (UiO-66-type) can serve as highly effective CT contrast agents due to their large metal content and strong X-ray attenuation capabilities. Hf-MOFs, in particular, provided markedly higher attenuation coefficients than Zr-MOFs, directly correlating with Hf’s greater electron density. These Hf-MOFs also displayed good biocompatibility and in vivo distribution, especially in liver and spleen imaging, establishing early proof of their diagnostic potential in high-contrast CT imaging.

Optical/fluorescence imaging

Optical and fluorescence imaging based on MOFs has emerged as a highly promising approach for near infrared (NIR) bioimaging, especially in cancer diagnostics. This strategy exploits the structural versatility of MOFs to incorporate organic fluorophores, porphyrin ligands, or quantum dots (QDs) into their frameworks or pores, enhancing both fluorescence stability and imaging depth. The porous structure of MOFs protects embedded dyes or QDs from photobleaching and quenching, leading to prolonged signal retention and improved quantum yield.

For instance, Zhao et al. [137] demonstrated that light-responsive nanoscale MOFs (NMOFs) functionalized with NIR-emitting ligands could enable deep-tissue fluorescence and photoacoustic imaging, offering superior spatial resolution and minimal photodamage compared to traditional organic dyes. Similarly, Liu et al. [138] discussed that nanoscale MOFs incorporating luminescent linkers or quantum dots serve as effective imaging probes, with tunable emission wavelengths in the NIR window (700–900 nm) that allow non-invasive imaging of tumors and real-time tracking of drug delivery.

Furthermore, integrating quantum dots (CdSe, PbS, or InAs) into MOFs enhances luminescence intensity and photostability, while also benefiting from the biocompatibility and modularity of the MOF matrix. This hybridization creates MOF–QD composites that emit in the NIR region, enabling deep-tissue tumor imaging an essential advancement for breast cancer diagnostics where tissue scattering is significant.

MOFs for breast cancer treatment

Photothermal therapy (PTT)

MOFs stand out as cutting-edge nanoplatforms for photothermal therapy (PTT) in breast cancer, thanks to their exceptional porosity, customizable structures, and versatile treatment features. In PTT for breast cancer, specialized agents absorb near-infrared (NIR) light and transform it into targeted heat via non-radiative decay, precisely destroying cancer cells [139]. This non-invasive approach offers pinpoint accuracy and boosts anti-tumor immune responses [140], often relying on noble metal nanoparticles like gold (Au), palladium (Pd), or platinum (Pt) for superior NIR uptake and heat generation efficiency [141].

To boost outcomes and tackle resistance in breast cancer PTT, researchers now blend it with other strategies. Notably, combined PTT-chemodynamic therapy (PTT/CDT) systems excel against metastatic and triple-negative breast cancers. For example, Zou et al. [142] created a Cu2-xSe@ZIF-8 nanocomposite to hit bone metastases in breast cancer, shielding Cu2-xSe inside ZIF-8 to cut toxicity, sustain strong photothermal effects, and produce cancer-killing—OH radicals through Cu+/Cu2+ Fenton-like processes. Likewise, Yang et al. [143] designed Fe3+-doped mesoporous organosilica frameworks for triple-negative breast cancer, merging Fe3+-driven Fenton reactions with gentle PTT to release toxic -OH and spark immunogenic cell death, igniting powerful adaptive immunity.

Building upon these advancements, MOF-based nanostructures such as Au@ZIF-8 have been developed to achieve synergistic chemo-photothermal therapy, offering high drug-loading efficiency and controlled release under tumor-specific conditions [144]. Similarly, multifunctional MOFs doped with metals like copper and platinum enable imaging-guided and H2O2 self-supplying photothermal and chemodynamic therapy, enhancing both therapeutic precision and tumor ablation [145]. Further innovations using graphene quantum dots and iridium oxide nanoparticles within MOFs have demonstrated improved photothermal conversion and synergistic photothermal-photodynamic effects for targeted breast cancer treatment [146, 147]. A comprehensive review confirms that MOFs not only offer high photothermal conversion efficiency and biocompatibility but also support multifunctional therapies integrating chemotherapy, photodynamic therapy, and immunotherapy for more precise and effective breast cancer management [58].

Immunotherapy

Tumor immunotherapy represents a paradigm shift in oncology by leveraging the body’s immune system to target and eliminate malignant cells with high specificity. Unlike chemotherapy or radiotherapy, which cause direct cytotoxicity to both healthy and cancerous tissues, immunotherapy enhances endogenous antitumor immunity primarily through activation of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and antigen-presenting cells (APCs). TIL-based therapies have demonstrated durable tumor regression and long-term remission across multiple cancer types, including melanoma and prostate cancer, by amplifying the cytotoxic capacity and persistence of CD8+ T cells [148, 149]. APC modulation, particularly through dendritic cell activation, further enhances adaptive immunity by improving tumor antigen presentation and effector T-cell priming [150]. Recent innovations, such as combining TIL therapy with PD-1 blockade and bifunctional antibodies targeting IL-7 and PD-1 pathways, have improved the longevity and functionality of TILs, offering durable clinical responses even in refractory cancers [151]. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that by synergistically activating TILs and APCs, tumor immunotherapy achieves tumor-specific, durable antitumor responses while minimizing off-target toxicity, solidifying its role as a transformative treatment modality in modern oncology [152, 153].

Chemotherapy

MOFs have revolutionized chemotherapy drug delivery due to their exceptionally high specific surface areas, tunable porosity, and superior biocompatibility. These properties make MOFs ideal candidates for controlled and targeted drug release systems, particularly in cancer therapy. Recent studies have demonstrated that MOF-based nanoplatforms can efficiently encapsulate chemotherapeutic agents like doxorubicin (DOX), enabling enhanced therapeutic outcomes with reduced systemic toxicity. For instance, dual pH- and magnetic field-responsive MOFs, such as FeO@PDA@ZIF-90 and Bio-MOF-13 analogs, have shown precise tumor targeting and controlled drug release, resulting in improved efficacy in breast cancer treatment [162]. Similarly, zirconium-based UiO-66 and its derivatives enable pH-dependent and folate receptor-mediated DOX delivery, significantly enhancing cytotoxicity against cancer cells while maintaining safety for normal tissues [163]. MOFs have also been successfully integrated into multifunctional nanoplatforms combining chemotherapy with magnetic hyperthermia or photodynamic therapy for synergistic tumor suppression [164]. Comprehensive reviews highlight that surface-functionalized and stimuli-responsive MOFs represent a new generation of intelligent nanocarriers that can adapt to tumor microenvironments and improve drug bioavailability [58, 165].

Multivariate modulation (MTV) strategies revolutionize metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) for advanced biomedicine, enabling seamless integration of diverse functional ligands within unified structures for co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents. Abánades Lázaro et al. [166] pioneered defect-engineered UiO-66 MOFs via modulator diversification, achieving precise co-loading of dichloroacetic acid and 5-fluorouracil (5-FU). This multidrug MOF platform delivered superior cytotoxicity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells over single-drug alternatives, showcasing MTV-MOFs as next-gen nanocarriers for combination chemotherapy.

Ferroptosis emerges as a powerful iron-driven lipid peroxidation pathway to conquer chemotherapy-resistant tumors, with MOFs amplifying this mechanism in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) treatment. Li et al. [167] developed Cu-TCPP(Fe) copper-porphyrin MOFs that dismantle antiferroptotic defenses, incorporating glucose-starving gold nanoparticles alongside RSL3 ferroptosis inducer to block glutathione synthesis. The peroxidase-like activity of this TNBC ferroptosis MOF heightened oxidative stress, shattering antioxidant barriers for breakthrough synergistic therapy.

Core–shell PCP-MOF@G@B nanostructures, engineered by Du et al. [168] drive dual-starvation therapy through pH/ROS-responsive PEG-CDM-PEI shells that trigger size and charge shifts for deep tumor penetration. Endogenous H2O2 accelerates MOF breakdown, yielding 75–94% drug release in breast cancer models. In MDA-MB-231 xenografts, this responsive MOF nanoplatform sparked 42% apoptosis-driven cell death, shrinking tumors by 89% and boosting survival in mice.

PDT

Photodynamic therapy (PDT) harnesses photosensitizers activated by precise light wavelengths to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), especially singlet oxygen (1O2), driving apoptosis in breast cancer tumors. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) excel as porous carriers that securely load these photosensitizers, concentrating ROS generation exactly where light hits for maximum tumor destruction [169]. This targeted PDT with MOFs sparks rapid immunogenic cell death (ICD), unleashing tumor antigens that ignite inflammation and supercharge immune recognition for stronger anti-cancer responses [170].

Deep tumor hypoxia cripples standard PDT, but innovative solutions like Chen et al. [171] BODIPY-Zr-MOF (69-L2) change the game. Through post-synthetic ligand exchange and perfluorocarbon hybridization, this oxygen-generating nanoplatform delivers steady O2 supply even in low-oxygen zones. LED activation unleashes powerful 1O2 production, delivering 50–70% cell killing in MDA-MB-231 triple-negative breast cancer at 25–100 μg/mL doses. Embedding this advanced MOF-PDT system in hydrogel scaffolds yields game-changing results, slashing tumor volumes by 80–85% in mouse mammary carcinoma models over 80 days. These hypoxia-busting, ROS-amplifying MOF nanocarriers position PDT as a frontline strategy for aggressive breast cancers like TNBC, combining deep tissue penetration with sustained immunity boosting.

Advanced MOFs revolutionize breast cancer treatment through synergistic photodynamic-immunotherapy combinations that surpass standalone PDT limitations. Liang et al. pioneered HA-PEG grafted PCN-224 MOFs co-loaded with PD-L1 inhibitor BMS-202, enabling precise tumor targeting and dual-modal action. In 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, this nanoplatform achieved complete primary tumor eradication while unleashing systemic antitumor immunity, slashing metastatic growth by 73% via enhanced dendritic cell maturation and cytotoxic T-lymphocyte activation positioning HA-PEG PCN-224 MOFs as breakthrough platforms for metastatic breast cancer management [172].

Challenges and future perspectives

The clinical translation of MOF-based platforms for breast cancer diagnosis and therapy, despite their immense preclinical promise, faces a constellation of interrelated scientific, engineering, and regulatory challenges that must be critically addressed to bridge the gap between laboratory innovation and patient benefit. A primary and persistent limitation is the inadequate physiological stability of many MOF formulations in complex biological milieus. Numerous MOFs, particularly those constructed from softer metal ions like Zn2+ or Cu2+, are susceptible to rapid disintegration in the presence of physiological concentrations of phosphate anions, proteins, or at the slightly acidic pH of the tumor microenvironment, leading to premature payload release, unpredictable pharmacokinetics, and potential off-target toxicity [53, 54]. This instability directly undermines the core premise of targeted delivery and necessitates a fundamental shift toward the rational design of frameworks using more hydrolytically stable metal nodes, such as zirconium (Zr4+) or iron (Fe3+), whose hard Lewis acidity forms robust bonds with carboxylate-based organic linkers, thereby enhancing their resilience in vivo [50, 52]. Concurrently, the field suffers from a pronounced lack of standardized, scalable, and Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant synthesis protocols. The batch-to-batch variability in critical quality attributes such as particle size distribution, crystallinity, surface charge, and ligand functionalization not only hinders reproducible therapeutic outcomes but also complicates the establishment of reliable structure–activity relationships, which are essential for regulatory approval [51]. Perhaps the most significant research gap lies in the comprehensive long-term toxicological and biodegradation profiling of MOFs. While acute cytotoxicity is often assessed, there is a critical paucity of data on chronic organ-specific accumulation, particularly within the reticuloendothelial system (liver and spleen), and the metabolic fate of both the organic linkers and the leached metal ions over extended periods [49, 173]. The potential for metal ion leaching, even from ostensibly stable frameworks, poses a serious safety concern that demands rigorous investigation using sensitive analytical techniques in relevant in vivo models.

Furthermore, a stark disconnect exists between the overwhelmingly positive in vitro results and the often modest or inconsistent efficacy observed in sophisticated in vivo models. A major contributor to this translational failure is the overreliance on subcutaneous xenograft models in immunocompromised mice, which fail to recapitulate the complex stromal architecture, immune cell infiltration, and vascular heterogeneity characteristic of human breast tumors, especially aggressive subtypes like triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) [174]. These simplified models do not adequately mimic the high interstitial fluid pressure and dense extracellular matrix that constitute formidable barriers to nanoparticle penetration and distribution within solid tumors. To address this, future research must prioritize the use of more clinically relevant models, including orthotopic, patient-derived xenograft (PDX), and humanized mouse systems that preserve the tumor's native microenvironment and immune contexture. Beyond biological and model limitations, the path to the clinic is obstructed by a lack of harmonized regulatory science frameworks specific to MOFs. There is an urgent need for collaborative initiatives between academia, industry, and regulatory bodies to define MOF-specific quality-by-design (QbD) principles, including standardized methods for characterizing residual solvents, endotoxin levels, sterility, and batch consistency [51]. Future perspectives must therefore be strategically focused on several key directions: (i) the development of "smart" biodegradable MOFs with hydrolytic kinetics precisely tuned to the therapeutic window of the encapsulated drug; (ii) the integration of real-time imaging modalities (e.g., MRI, PET) with therapeutic payloads to enable image-guided, adaptive dosing regimens; (iii) the establishment of robust in vitro–in vivo correlation (IVIVC) models that incorporate patient-derived organoids to predict clinical response; and (iv) the creation of a dedicated regulatory pathway that acknowledges the unique physicochemical properties of MOFs while ensuring their safety and efficacy for human use. Only through such a concerted, multidisciplinary effort can the extraordinary potential of MOFs be fully realized in the fight against breast cancer (Fig. 8).

Schematic representation of the major challenges and future perspectives of MOFs in breast cancer diagnosis and therapy

Conclusions

MOF demonstrate significant quantitative advantages as nanoplatforms for breast cancer management, with drug loading capacities reaching 56.5–58.8 wt% in zirconium-based systems and up to 625 mg/g in magnesium-based MOFs, while iron-based carriers achieve 75.38 mg/g alongside magnetic targeting capabilities. Biocompatible platforms such as calcium and iron MOFs maintain greater than 90–95% cell viability, with calcium systems showing 63% drug loading and controlled release of about 55% over 72 h. Therapeutic efficacy is consistently improved, with copper-based MOFs achieving about 89% tumor cell inhibition, responsive nanoplatforms demonstrating around 89% tumor reduction in vivo, and immunotherapy-integrated MOFs reducing metastatic burden by approximately 88% and suppressing tumor growth by more than 70%. Photodynamic and combination therapies further enhance outcomes, showing 50–70% cancer cell killing and up to 73% reduction in metastasis. These improvements are supported by stimuli-responsive release efficiencies of 75–94% under tumor-specific conditions. Despite these measurable benefits, clinical translation remains constrained by batch-to-batch variability, limited long-term safety data related to metal accumulation, and the absence of standardized large-scale synthesis protocols. Future progress requires the development of fully biodegradable MOFs with validated toxicological profiles, reproducible manufacturing methods, and standardized characterization frameworks, supported by computational modeling to predict biological interactions and optimize therapeutic performance for clinical application in personalized breast cancer therapy.
