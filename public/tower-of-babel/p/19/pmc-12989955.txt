Evaluating Generative Artificial Intelligence Models’ Responses to Questions About Scaphoid Fracture and Scaphoid Nonunion
Authors: Armaan Dhanoa, Ann-Sophie Lafreniere, Carl M Harper, Grace Y Kim, Laura Saenz, Jonathan Persitz, Kevin J Zuo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate and compare the responses of ChatGPT and Google Gemini to common patient questions about scaphoid fracture and scaphoid nonunion, and to compare responses between hand fellowship-trained orthopedic and plastic surgeons.MethodsA list of 30 common patient questions about scaphoid fracture and nonunion was developed and classified using Norman Webb’s Depth of Knowledge levels 1–4. Each question was input into ChatGPT-4o and Google Gemini 2.0 Flash. An evaluation guide was created with four domains for each response, each rated on a Likert scale from 1–5: accuracy, clarity, Artificial Intelligence Response Metric, and comparison to an in-person clinician interaction. Responses were evaluated by three orthopedic and three plastic hand surgeons. Statistical comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests to assess differences between AI platforms, domains, question complexity, and surgical specialty.ResultsThere were no considerable differences between mean Likert scale scores for ChatGPT and Google Gemini. Plastic surgeons rated responses higher than orthopedic surgeons overall and for ChatGPT. Google Gemini performed better for DOK level 2 and level 3 questions. ChatGPT’s responses had greater clarity. For both platforms, ratings for clinician comparability across all DOK levels were considerably lower than scores for all other metrics.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that ChatGPT and Google Gemini offer clinical use for patient care regarding scaphoid fracture and nonunion. However, clinician comparability was not a key strength for either platform, highlighting a key area for improvement for AI-based large language models in clinical application.Type of Study/level of evidenceDiagnostic V.Key words: AI, ChatGPT, Google Gemini, Nonunion, Scaphoid

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and natural language processing has led to the production of increasingly complex large language models (LLM).1 These AI models are trained to receive a diverse range of inputs and produce natural, human-like responses.2 Chat Generative Pre-Trained Transformer (ChatGPT) is an AI platform developed by Open AI that was introduced on November 30, 2022.3 ChatGPT is a generative AI model that uses patterns and structures learned from existing data to develop new data.1 This allows it to build on previous LLM by refining its responses and developing a unique output to each query.4 Similarly, Google Gemini is another generative AI LLM created by Google and first made available on December 13, 2023.5 Given the similar nature of these platforms, they have been compared in previous studies, including in health care in the context of patient care.6, 7, 8

The accessibility to and use of generative AI models is becoming increasingly more common. As of August 2025, there are 800 million weekly active users on ChatGPT, double the amount from February 2025.9 Google Gemini averaged 400 million monthly visitors in 2025, an increase of >100 million from 2024.10 This still is less traffic than more common search engines such as Google, which receives 8.5 billion searches daily.11 Prior studies have demonstrated that many patients consult the internet with health care queries before meeting with their doctor.

There is a limited but growing body of evidence assessing the ability of ChatGPT to respond to common medical questions from patients.2,12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 In 2023, Seth et al14 studied the responses generated by ChatGPT to three questions on scaphoid management, however, their analysis remained descriptive rather than systematic. Furthermore, few studies have evaluated Google Gemini or compared it to ChatGPT in the clinical setting.6 As patients increasingly use generative AI models for this purpose and perhaps in place of clinician consultations, it is important to evaluate their clinical use and assess the quality of their responses to medical questions.18,19

This study aims to build on prior literature by evaluating and comparing the responses of ChatGPT and Google Gemini to common patient questions about scaphoid fracture and scaphoid nonunion. Scaphoid fractures are the most common carpal fractures, and are at risk of nonunion or avascular necrosis causing wrist arthritis.20 Moreover, given the overlap in management of hand injuries between hand fellowship-trained orthopedic and plastic surgeons, we also aimed to compare the evaluation of responses between these two specialties.21

Materials and Methods

Question list

A list of 30 common patient questions about scaphoid fracture and scaphoid nonunion was developed among hand fellowship-trained surgeons (Table S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org.). The questions captured basic definitions, diagnosis, treatment plans, recovery, and long-term consequences. They were classified by the surgeons based on their complexity according to Norman Webb’s Depth of Knowledge (DOK), where the complexity increases with each level: level 1 – recall and reproduction; level 2 – skills and concepts; level 3 – strategic thinking; level 4 – extended thinking (Table S2, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org.).22

AI response generation

A new account was created on ChatGPT and Google Gemini before inputting the questions. The memory feature was disabled on ChatGPT to eliminate the ability to remember content between chats. ChatGPT-4o and Google Gemini 2.0 Flash were the standard processing models equipped at the time the responses were generated. Each of the 30 questions was input into a new chat, in the same order on each platform, generating a total of 60 responses between the two platforms. Table S3 (available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org.) provides an example of the response on each platform to question 1.

Evaluation guide

The responses were copied into a Google Form where each page displayed the response by ChatGPT and Google Gemini to the same question. The order in which the responses were displayed alternated randomly. Each response was followed by four evaluation questions on a Likert scale from 1–5, aimed at assessing different domains of the response. Question 1 asked evaluators to rate response accuracy and question 2 asked to rate response clarity. Question 3 was the AI Response Metric (AIRM), a tool developed by Anastasio et al23 to determine the understandability and reliability of an AI generated response. The tool presents four focuses with a scale of 1–5 as grading options. Question 4 asked evaluators to compare the response to an in-person interaction with a clinician. The detailed evaluation questions can be found in Table S4 (available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org.). The Google Form was distributed to three hand fellowship-trained orthopedic (C.H., J.P., and L.S.) and plastic surgeons (G.Y.K., K.J.Z., and A.S.L.).

Statistical analysis

A Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare mean Likert scale scores between ChatGPT and Google Gemini. Comparisons of scores across evaluation domains (AI platform x domain) and across DOK levels (AI platform x DOK) were performed using a Friedman repeated-measures analysis of variance, followed by Durbin-Conover pairwise comparisons. A Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare scores between orthopedic and plastic surgeons. Multiple comparisons were corrected using false discovery rate (FDR), and the threshold was set to pFDR < .05.24 Interrater reliability for both specialties was assessed using a two-way random-effects model with absolute agreement. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05.

Results

There were no significant differences between mean Likert scale scores for ChatGPT (4.1) and Google Gemini (3.9). Plastic surgeons (4.0 overall, 3.9 ChatGPT) rated responses higher than orthopedic surgeons (3.7 overall, 3.4 ChatGPT), overall (U = 209,127, P < .001) and for ChatGPT (U=40,487, P < .001). Intra-platform comparison – domain

Figure 1A–F illustrates overall and specialty-specific comparisons of domains within each platform. For Google Gemini, the accuracy, clarity, and AIRM of responses to DOK level 1 questions outperformed evaluators’ perceived comparability to an in-person interaction with a clinician (P < .05). For DOK level 2, clarity achieved significance (P < .001), and for DOK 3, clarity and AIRM reached significance (P < .001). Only accuracy was higher than clinician comparability for DOK level 4 (P = .04).

A and B depict pooled comparisons of orthopedic and plastic surgeons, C and D depict ratings from orthopedic surgeons, and E and F depict ratings from plastic surgeons. ∗P < .05, ∗∗P < .001. Clinician comparability was consistently rated lower than all other evaluation domains.

The clarity of ChatGPT responses was greater than accuracy and AIRM for DOK level 1 questions (P < .05). For DOK level 1 and 2, accuracy (P < .05), clarity (P < .001), and AIRM (P < .05) exceeded clinician comparability. Similarly, for DOK level 3, all three domains were higher (P < .001). For DOK level 4, only clarity exceeded clinician comparability (P = .04).

For Google Gemini, orthopedic surgeons scored clarity and AIRM higher than clinician comparability for DOK level 3 questions (P < .05). Similarly, for ChatGPT, accuracy (P = .004), clarity (P < .001), and AIRM (P = .031) were higher. Regarding DOK level 4, clarity outperformed clinician comparability (P = .033).

Among plastic surgeons evaluating Google Gemini, accuracy (P < .05), clarity (P < .001), and AIRM (P < .05) exceeded clinician comparability for DOK level 1 and 2 questions. Clarity and AIRM were greater for DOK level 3 (P < .05), and for DOK level 4, accuracy outperformed clinician comparability (P = .007). ChatGPT responses had higher clarity than accuracy and AIRM for DOK level 1 questions (P < .05). For DOK level 1 and 2, accuracy (P < .05), clarity (P < .001), and AIRM (P < .05) scored higher than clinician comparability, whereas for DOK 3, clarity and AIRM were higher (P < .05).

Interrater reliability

The intraclass correlation coefficient for orthopedic surgeons was 0.231 (95% CI, 0.162–0.302; P < .05), and 0.201 for plastic surgeons (95% CI, 0.114–0.287; P < .05), indicating poor interrater reliability among evaluators within specialties.

Discussion

This study posed 30 common patient questions about scaphoid fracture and scaphoid nonunion to ChatGPT and Google Gemini, and the responses were evaluated by six hand fellowship-trained surgeons (three orthopedic, three plastic). The relative strengths of each platform differed across evaluation domains and levels of question complexity. Overall, plastic surgeons rated the quality of AI responses higher than orthopedic surgeons across all four evaluation domains.

Differences between ChatGPT and Google Gemini

Across evaluation domains, there were minimal differences between the platforms regarding the accuracy, understandability, and reliability of responses, and comparability to an in-person interaction with a clinician. The clarity of ChatGPT responses outperformed Google Gemini overall and for DOK level 1 questions specifically. This is relevant for patients deciding which AI platform to use for scaphoid related questions, as both platforms were comparable to a clinician consultation. In contrast, a scoping review by Fattah et al.8 comparing how these platforms address medical inquiries found the accuracy of ChatGPT (72%) was significantly higher than that of Google Gemini (63%). While this could reflect differences in the way accuracy was assessed, their findings were variable across medical specialties, suggesting that the accuracy could depend on the clinical setting.

Google Gemini performed significantly better than ChatGPT on DOK levels 2 and 3 questions, which encompass skills, concepts, and the incorporation of strategic thinking. This highlights specific areas in which Google Gemini may guide patient care. We also found that Google Gemini performed better on DOK levels 2 and 3 questions than DOK levels 1 and 4, while there were no differences for ChatGPT. Thus, ChatGPT might be more versatile across the complexity of questions it can answer, suggesting that it could offer broader clinical use. Of note, neither platform performed better on DOK 4 questions, which is consistent with a study by Christy et al.2 evaluating the responses of ChatGPT to distal radius fracture questions. They found it was not as proficient at answering questions requiring higher-order thinking skills requiring synthesis of multiple clinical considerations and some element of judgment.25

Notably, clinician comparability consistently scored lower than the other domains for both platforms. While these models may offer use in the clinical setting, this demonstrates that their comparability to an in-person interaction with a clinician is considered their weakest attribute of the domains we assessed. Moreover, this consistent finding across both platforms delineates a specific area for improvement. This finding is especially striking for patients and clinicians as studies continue to compare the responses of these platforms to medical experts.26,27

Differences by surgical specialty

Plastic surgeons consistently gave significantly higher ratings to ChatGPT responses than orthopedic surgeons, while the findings were comparable for Google Gemini. Although it remains unclear which specialty rated responses more appropriately in the absence of a reference standard, these discrepancies are notable given the overlap in management of hand and wrist pathologies between these specialties.21 However, as noted by Testa et al,28 differences in residency pathways could result in variability in hand surgery training. They found that hand surgery residents performed more hand and wrist bony trauma cases, while plastic surgery residents had more total hand procedures, and completed more nerve repairs and amputations. The unique training pathways could manifest in different values in identifying key aspects of the management of scaphoid fracture and nonunion. Additional research should attempt to further elucidate this relationship and provide direction for refining residency and hand surgery fellowship curricula.

Clinical implications

As the use of generative AI systems in patient care grows, their use in the clinical setting must be evaluated critically. This study highlights the strengths of two LLMs and may help guide patients in choosing between them. A mean score of 4.1 for ChatGPT and 3.9 for Google Gemini out of a possible total of five indicates that these models both offer clinical use for patients with questions about scaphoid fracture and nonunion. Despite strong overall performance, respondents consistently perceived the comparison to direct clinician consultation as the lowest rated domain, indicating that in-person interactions offer benefits not yet captured by AI. Our findings suggest that Google Gemini may confer more value to patients for questions drawing on skills, concepts, and strategic thinking. Conversely, ChatGPT’s strength lies in response clarity. Additionally, it remains to be seen whether these ratings change as LLMs continue to evolve. However, although these platforms may offer clinical value, patients should exercise caution when interpreting responses until more definitive evidence is available regarding their reliability. Introzzi et al.29 proposed a framework to gage the level of trust based on the availability of information about AI confidence and reliability. Such frameworks could help guide their safe and effective integration into patient care.

While this study expands upon the understanding and application of AI models in health care, there are several limitations. With only three orthopedic and plastic hand surgeons, this study had a relatively low number of evaluators, limiting the generalizability of findings. There also is a potential response bias, as evaluators may have prior experience with either platform and prefer a specific response style. Each question was input only once, and since each prompt generates a unique output, this study could not evaluate the reliability of responses. Since the time of this study, ChatGPT released a new model, GPT-5, and it may address some of the limitations outlined. Lastly, there is an element of subjectivity in the classification of questions into DOK levels, which could influence the ability to interpret the performance of AI models across different levels of question complexity.

Building on our findings, subsequent investigations could compare different platforms, including Claude AI, an AI chatbot developed by Anthropic, and OpenEvidence, an AI platform for clinicians that synthesizes peer-reviewed medical literature.30,31 Multiple studies have evaluated responses to patient questions, so future studies could assess these platforms from a clinician’s perspective. A single blinded randomized controlled trial comparing patient interactions with a clinician in-person to an AI model could further outline the value of AI in patient care.
