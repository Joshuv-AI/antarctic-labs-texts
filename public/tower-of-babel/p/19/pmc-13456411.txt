Dupilumab's Real‐World Efficacy and Safety Across Age Groups for Children and Adolescents With Paediatric Atopic Dermatitis
Authors: Paul Sun Woo Kim, Jacqueline Tu, Kelvin Truong, Gloria Fong, William Luu, Peter Barclay, Melanie Wong, Li‐Chuen Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground/ObjectivesDupilumab is an anti‐interleukin (IL)‐4 receptor (IL‐4Rα) monoclonal antibody used to manage atopic dermatitis (AD). There is a paucity of real‐world Australian data for dupilumab in paediatric AD, especially regarding differences in clinical profiles between age groups. This study evaluated dupilumab's efficacy and safety at an Australian tertiary centre.MethodsAll paediatric AD patients (< 18 years old) treated with dupilumab at a tertiary centre with available follow‐up data were retrospectively analysed. Effectiveness was measured using the Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI), Children's Dermatology Life Quality Index (cDLQI), and Physician Global Assessment (PGA). Adverse events (AEs) were recorded. Differences in efficacy and safety between age groups (0–5, 6–11, 12–17 years old) were analysed.Results103 patients (9.2 ± 3.8 years old, 51.5% male) were analysed with a follow‐up of 495.3 ± 337.3 days. 95.1%, 90.1% and 66.7% of patients achieved a 50%, 75%, 90% drop in EASI (EASI‐50, EASI‐75, EASI‐90). 93.4% and 94.7% of patients achieved a 6‐point drop in cDLQI and a 2‐point drop in PGA. 43.2%, 48.7% and 71.1% of patients achieved EASI ≤ 1, cDLQI ≤ 1 and PGA ≤ 1 respectively. Two patients discontinued dupilumab due to AEs (psoriasis flare and lichenoid drug‐reaction). 49 (47.6%) patients experienced AEs, with the most frequent category being ocular‐related (n = 43). There were no statistically significant differences between age groups in the rate of achieving efficacy thresholds or in the rate of AEs for each AE category. Median time‐to‐effect for EASI‐50/75/90 were 130, 140 and 291 days on Kaplan–Meier analysis.ConclusionsPaediatric dupilumab patients experienced significant improvements with an acceptable safety profile, independent of age.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) affects up to 20% of the paediatric population [1]. Patients with severe AD may respond sub‐optimally to conventional therapies, including topical corticosteroids (TCS) and topical calcineurin inhibitors. Poorly controlled AD can cause long‐term negative effects on the child's physical and mental health [1, 2, 3]. Escalation of therapy to systemic immunomodulators such as methotrexate, azathioprine, cyclosporin, and mycophenolate mofetil can have significant side effects [2].

Dupilumab is a novel anti‐IL‐4 receptor (IL‐4Rα) monoclonal antibody that suppresses IL‐4 and IL‐13 signalling pathways, hence inhibiting T helper type 2 (Th2) inflammation [4]. Previous large randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have demonstrated its efficacy and safety in treatment‐resistant paediatric AD [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10].

However, as RCTs may not fully reflect the complexities of real‐world practice [11], real‐world data that captures diverse cohorts with long follow‐up may provide relevant insights for clinicians and patients. A recent Australian study investigated the real‐world clinical profile of dupilumab in paediatric AD, demonstrating significant clinical improvements with an acceptable safety profile [12]. There is still, though, a lack of data describing the differences in efficacy and safety outcomes between age groups.

In the Australian context, age‐related differences in clinical outcomes among paediatric patients are of particular interest, as dupilumab currently has Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) funding only for AD patients aged 12 years or older [13]. Children under 12 rely on a compassionate access scheme, either hospital‐funded (Individual Patient Use, IPU) or manufacturer‐funded (Sanofi). As dupilumab can cost up to $20,000 per year, depending on dosing schedules [14], real‐world data in patients under 12 years will support continued funding and future access on PBS.

Hence, this single‐centre retrospective analysis aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dupilumab in paediatric AD at a major Australian tertiary centre. Additionally, possible differences in dupilumab clinical profiles between age groups (0–5, 6–11, 12–17 years old) were considered in the analysis.

Methods

This was a retrospective analysis of all paediatric patients (< 18 years old) who were prescribed dupilumab at a major Australian tertiary centre from 14 August 2020 to 28 April 2025. Only patients with at least one recorded follow‐up visit after dupilumab initiation were included. Background data, including age, sex, comorbidities, and previous AD treatments, were collected. Efficacy measures were collected at the pre‐dupilumab baseline visit and each subsequent post‐dupilumab clinic visit. The primary efficacy measures were validated scoring tools: Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI), Children's Dermatology Life Quality Index (cDLQI) and Physician Global Assessment (PGA).

Dupilumab's effectiveness was assessed in two domains: (1) to achieve a clinically meaningful response and (2) to induce near‐remission of disease. The investigators defined a clinically meaningful response as at least a 50%, 75%, or 90% decrease in EASI from baseline (EASI‐50, EASI‐75, EASI‐90), a 6‐point drop in cDLQI [15], or a 2‐point drop in PGA [16]. Near‐remission was defined as EASI ≤ 1, cDLQI ≤ 1, or PGA ≤ 1 [16, 17, 18]. The rates of patients achieving these efficacy thresholds were reported as a total and between age groups (0–5, 6–11, 12–17 years old). For each efficacy variable, the analysis for that specific variable only included patients who had both baseline and follow‐up data for the given variable. Because data availability varied between the variables, the number of patients analysed is different for each variable (as reported in the results section).

Relevant clinical and bloodwork adverse events (AEs) were collected at each follow‐up visit. Adverse events were counted on a per‐patient basis, as the exact number of discrete episodes was not precisely quantified in the electronic medical record notes. For example, if a patient experienced multiple episodes of a specific side effect, they were counted as a single patient experiencing that adverse event. Bloodwork AEs that reached clinical significance were recorded.

Data was entered into REDCap and analysed using R Studio (version number 2025.05.1 + 513). Continuous variables were summarised using mean and standard deviation, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies. Two‐sided Fisher's Exact or Chi‐squared tests were used as appropriate to assess differences between age groups in the proportion of patients achieving efficacy thresholds and in the rates of AEs for each adverse event category. Two‐tailed paired t‐tests were used to determine if a statistically significant drop in mean EASI/cDLQI/PGA score occurred between the first pre‐ and last post‐dupilumab visit. Kaplan–Meier analysis was used to calculate median time‐to‐response graphs for the efficacy measures, and a graph of the mean EASI/cDLQI/PGA values grouped in 6‐monthly intervals was created to visualise the time course of improvement.

Low‐to‐Negligible Risk (LNR) ethical approval was granted by the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/ETH00444).

Results

A total of 111 children were recorded as being treated with dupilumab for atopic dermatitis (AD) at our tertiary centre. Of the 111 patients, 103 with available follow‐up data were included in the study.

The mean age was 9.2 ± 3.8 years, ranging from two to 17 years. The sample had a slight male predominance (53 males, 51.5%). Mean follow‐up time was 495.3 ± 337.3 days. A total of 24 patients received dupilumab through the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS) and 79 patients through non‐PBS compassionate access programs (hospital or manufacturer‐funded). A total of 20 patients were aged 0–5, 56 were aged 6–11 and 27 were aged 12–17.

26 (25.2%) of patients had the atopic triad (asthma, allergic rhinitis and atopic dermatitis). Other atopic comorbidities included food allergies (n = 52, 50.5%), asthma (n = 41, 39.8%), allergic rhinitis (n = 41, 39.8%), respiratory allergy (n = 26, 25.2%), skin allergy (n = 7, 6.8%) and eosinophilic oesophagitis (n = 2, 1.9%). Other relevant dermatological comorbidities included prurigo nodularis (n = 3, 2.9%), alopecia areata (n = 1, 1.0%), and psoriasis (n = 1, 1.0%).

Prior treatments included topical corticosteroids (n = 101, 98.1%) and topical calcineurin inhibitors (n = 43, 41.7%). Note that for the two patients who had not used topical corticosteroids, one had no data available on any previous medication usage, and the other was using topical calcineurin inhibitors at the time of dupilumab initiation but had no record of previous topical corticosteroid usage. Systemic immunomodulators were also used: azathioprine (n = 2), methotrexate (n = 6), mycophenolate mofetil (n = 1), omalizumab (n = 1), ustekinumab (n = 1), systemic steroids (n = 9), cyclosporin (n = 3), and oral tacrolimus (n = 1).

For clinically meaningful improvements (Table 1), 95.1% of patients achieved EASI‐50, 93.4% achieved at least a 6‐point drop in cDLQI, and 94.7% achieved at least a 2‐point drop in PGA. Notably, there were no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) between age‐groups for the proportion of patients achieving these thresholds, indicating that dupilumab was consistently effective across paediatric age‐groups.

For complete remission (Table 2), 43.2% of patients achieved EASI ≤ 1, 48.7% achieved cDLQI ≤ 1, and 71.1% achieved PGA ≤ 1. Again, there were no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) between age groups for the proportion of patients achieving these thresholds, indicating that dupilumab was consistently effective across age groups for achieving remission.

The investigators also examined the magnitude of the decrease in mean EASI/cDLQI/PGA between baseline and final follow‐up clinic visit (Table 3). All age groups and the combined total cohort experienced a statistically significant drop (p < 0.001) in these three measures, as assessed by two‐tailed paired t‐tests.

The most common category of AEs was ocular (n = 43), followed by injection‐site reactions (n = 8) and skin‐related AEs (n = 2). For specific AEs, dry eyes (n = 20) were the most common, followed by conjunctivitis (n = 19). No bloodwork abnormality reached clinical significance. There was no statistically significant difference in the proportion of patients experiencing any ocular, injection‐site, or skin‐related AEs across the paediatric age‐groups (p > 0.05), indicating that the risk of dupilumab‐related AEs was not correlated with age.

Discussion

In this study, 95.1% of patients achieved EASI‐50 (Table 1), 48.7% achieved cDLQI ≤ 1 (Table 2), and 47.6% experienced at least one adverse event (Table 4). This aligns with previous overseas studies that have demonstrated significant improvements, including 95.6%–100% of children achieving EASI‐50 [19, 20], 31.3% achieving near‐remission (cDLQI ≤ 1) [21] and 27.1%–58.3% reporting at least one AE [19, 20]. Similarly, in a recent Australian real‐world study, 87% of patients achieved EASI‐75, 58% achieved EASI‐90, and 2.4% discontinued dupilumab [12]. This very closely matches our 90.1% EASI‐75 and 66.7% EASI‐90 achievement rate with a 1.9% discontinuation rate.

Unlike previous studies, this study is one of the first to show that dupilumab is equally effective and safe for younger and older children. Although previous studies have generally shown that dupilumab is effective for a variety of paediatric age‐groups [5, 6, 7], this is the first Australian study to demonstrate that there was no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05) in rates of achieving efficacy thresholds or in the rate of AEs for each adverse event category for the younger age groups (0–5, 6–11 years old) compared to older (12–17 years old) children (Tables 1, 2 and 4). The real‐world implication is that this emphasises the need to improve access to dupilumab for younger children, either through easier compassionate access or a broadening of dupilumab's PBS listing to children younger than 12 years.

It is notable that these improvements occurred in the context of severe treatment‐resistant disease. 98.1% of patients had already tried the conventional standard‐of‐care (topical corticosteroids) but still had a mean baseline cDLQI of 21.3 (Table 3), which is classified as an ‘extremely large effect’ on quality‐of‐life [18] and is significantly higher than the mean baseline cDLQI of 13.0–17.5 seen in the treatment arm of major clinical trials [5, 6, 7]. By the final follow‐up, our mean cDLQI dropped to 4.0 (classified as a ‘small effect’ on quality‐of‐life) [18] (Table 3). This underscores the extensive real‐world quality‐of‐life benefits of dupilumab for severe recalcitrant disease, even for patients with worse quality‐of‐life than RCT patients.

Critically, the benefits of improving access to dupilumab for younger patients may extend to halting the atopic march (the sequential development of atopic diseases that begins with AD, with potential progression to asthma and allergic rhinitis) [22]. Indeed, an analysis of over 3000 paediatric patients showed that dupilumab lowers the risk of new‐onset asthma and allergic rhinitis, and that this effect is greater when dupilumab is commenced at a younger age (0–5 years) [23]. Using dupilumab to disrupt this atopic march early may also mitigate the development of long‐term mental health issues (e.g., low self‐esteem) prevalent in young‐onset AD patients [3]. Notably, a large proportion of our cohort had already progressed along the atopic march before dupilumab initiation, with n = 41 (39.8%) having asthma and n = 26 (25.2%) having the atopic triad. This suggests that earlier dupilumab treatment for our patients may have prevented a portion of them from developing these atopic comorbidities, potentially sparing them from significant multimorbid disease‐burden during the critical early years of life. Hence, dupilumab's benefits in young patients should be recognised and considered earlier in the treatment cascade.

Dupilumab demonstrated a rapid time‐to‐effect, with the median time to clinically meaningful responses such as EASI‐50 being 130 days (Figure 1A). However, it is notable that the median time to the more stringent thresholds (e.g., EASI‐90) was significantly longer (291 days). This is consistent with previous RCTs, where only a minority (19.0% to 41.8%) of patients achieved EASI‐90 at week 16 [5, 6, 7], but the majority (50.0% to 58.6%) of patients achieved EASI‐90 by week 52 in the corresponding open‐label extensions [8, 9, 10]. Indeed, in another retrospective study, n = 10 (3.2%) patients discontinued dupilumab before 6 months due to a perceived lack of efficacy [24]. Our findings that dupilumab may take close to 1 year to reach high‐level efficacy thresholds, such as EASI‐90, may inform patient counselling and clinician decision‐making about the importance of long‐term treatment adherence to reach a significantly disease‐free state.

A total of 49 (47.6%) patients reported at least one adverse event (Table 4). This is comparable to the range of AE rates reported in previous studies, which range from 27.1% to 58.3% depending on the investigator's definition of an AE and length of follow‐up times [12, 19, 20]. Our long follow‐up time (average 495.3 days) may account for our slightly higher rate, with more time for AEs to manifest themselves. However, the vast majority were mild with only two patients discontinuing due to safety reasons (one developing a psoriatic flare and the other with a dupilumab‐induced lichenoid eruption), both of which are linked to an upregulation of Th1 pathways in response to dupilumab's suppression of Th2 inflammation [25, 26]. The most common adverse event category was ocular (n = 43, 41.7%), which correlates with a previous real‐world registry study that showed ocular AEs occurred in 34.6% of paediatric patients [27].

The strengths of this study included our relatively large sample size, an age‐diverse cohort, and a long follow‐up (mean 495.3 days), which may have practical real‐world relevance for clinicians. Another strength is our age‐group sub‐analysis, which provides novel insights within the Australian paediatric population. Its limitations are that this was a single‐centre study at a metropolitan tertiary hospital, which may limit the demographic variety of our patient cohort. Furthermore, this was a retrospective study, and some documentation was incomplete, e.g., only 38/103 patients had a complete set of pre‐ and post‐dupilumab PGA data available. However, as PGA is the least consequential parameter for clinical decision making, this incomplete documentation is unlikely to have materially affected the study's overall findings. Other inherent limitations of a retrospective analysis include recall bias. There was also no dupilumab‐naïve control group to compare rates of improvement and adverse events.

Future studies could also look at how long dupilumab‐induced AD remission lasts based on age, which would require long‐term follow‐up. Future studies may also wish to investigate dupilumab's incremental cost‐effectiveness ratio (ICER) compared to the conventional standard of care for patients below 12 years old. A Canadian government analysis found that dupilumab had an ICER of approximately $CAD ~130,000/QALY compared to conventional treatments for patients below 12 [28]. Extrapolating this to Australia, it would be well over the $AUD 50,000/QALY general upper limit for PBS listing [29]. However, a future ICER analysis should not only account for the immediate improvements in AD‐related quality‐of‐life but also account for downstream cost savings from dupilumab's halting of the atopic march and prevention of severe asthma/allergic rhinitis.

In summary, this single‐centre experience demonstrates that dupilumab is an effective and safe treatment for paediatric AD patients in a real‐world setting, independent of age group. These findings may support continued funding for treatment of children < 12 years of age, from IPU/compassionate access schemes, as well as possible future listing on the PBS.
