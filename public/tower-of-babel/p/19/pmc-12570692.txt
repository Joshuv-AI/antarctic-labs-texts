Superior circle of Willis configuration in oldest-old patients with first-ever ischemic stroke: a retrospective clinical-radiological study on its association with delayed onset and enhanced female prominence
Authors: Shusheng Jiao, Jianxia Zhi, Miaomiao Li, Liping Chen, Zihua Gong, Haoyuan Ma, Qinghua Wang, Xiaofang Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study characterized the Circle of Willis (CoW) configuration in oldest-old (≥ 80 years) patients with first-ever acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and investigated its association with the age of AIS onset in the elderly.MethodsIn this retrospective, single-center clinical-radiological study, consecutive patients presenting with first-ever AIS at Bethune International Peace Hospital (Shijiazhuang, Hebei, China) between March 2022 and June 2024 were retrospectively enrolled and stratified into oldest-old (≥ 80) and elderly (60–79) groups. CoW morphology was assessed via brain magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) or computed tomography angiography (CTA) using CoW scores and types.ResultsA total of 276 patients were included in the final analysis based on our inclusion and exclusion criteria (188 aged 60–79; 88 aged ≥ 80). The oldest-old group (≥ 80 years) comprised a higher proportion of females (47.1% vs. 31.4%) and showed a greater prevalence of atrial fibrillation (17.1% vs. 5.3%) and coronary heart disease (26.1% vs. 10.6%) compared to the younger elderly group. The oldest-old group exhibited significantly higher anterior communicating artery (AcoA) scores, total CoW scores, and a greater prevalence of a complete CoW than the younger elderly group. A poorer CoW configuration was associated with an earlier age of AIS onset, while a higher AcoA score predicted a later onset, even after adjusting for risk factors. These associations were significantly more pronounced in females. Among females, the oldest-old had higher AcoA, posterior communicating artery (PcoA), and total CoW scores. The age of onset in females was significantly associated with AcoA score, total CoW score, and poorer configuration.ConclusionsOldest-old patients with first-ever AIS possess a more favorable CoW configuration than younger elderly patients, an association markedly enhanced in females.

Introduction

Age is the foremost non-modifiable risk factor for acute ischemic stroke (AIS). As global life expectancy rises, the proportion of very elderly individuals among AIS patients continues to grow. Data from the National Acute Stroke Israeli Survey (NASIS) reflect this trend: the incidence of first-ever ischemic stroke in patients aged ≥ 80 years increased from 18.3% in 2004 to 24.5% in 2010 [1]. This oldest-old cohort presents a distinct clinical profile, characterized by a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation, large artery atherosclerosis [2], and major cardiovascular events compared to younger elderly patients [3].

Although conventional views suggest that a greater burden of vascular risk factors predisposes individuals to earlier AIS onset [4], this pattern does not hold in a special oldest-old population, where first-ever stroke occurs later despite elevated risk profiles. This paradox suggests the potential involvement of protective mechanisms that mitigate conventional risk factors and delay stroke occurrence in advanced age.

The Circle of Willis (CoW), a critical anastomotic structure at the base of the brain [5], plays a key role in cerebral collateral circulation and hemodynamic regulation. Incomplete CoW configurations are more common in AIS patients than in healthy individuals [6] and are associated with increased stroke risk and severity [7, 8]. A recent meta-analysis further indicated that CoW variants elevate the risk of ischemic stroke by 1.38-fold [9]. These findings imply that a complete or well-formed CoW may help postpone AIS onset in the elderly—a hypothesis yet to be directly investigated in aged populations.

To address this gap, we conducted a retrospective study involving patients with first-ever AIS who underwent MRA or CTA between March 2022 and June 2024. Participants were stratified into oldest-old (≥ 80 years) and elderly (60–79 years) groups. CoW configuration was assessed using standardized scoring and typology by two independent neurologists. We compared CoW features between groups and employed regression models to examine the relationship between CoW morphology and age at first stroke onset, with additional gender-stratified analyses.

Methods

Study design and period

This was a retrospective observational clinical-radiological study conducted at a single center (Bethune International Peace Hospital) in China, with a study period of 26 months. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Bethune International Peace Hospital (2023-KY-121); given the retrospective nature of the study (which involved reviewing and analyzing medical records of patients admitted to the hospital during the specified timeframe), the requirement for obtaining written informed consent from participants or their legal representatives was waived. During the study period, clinical and radiological data of eligible patients were collected and analyzed to achieve the preset research objectives.

Consecutive patients with first-ever AIS who were admitted to the Department of Neurology, Bethune International Peace Hospital (Shijiazhuang, Hebei province) between March 2022 and June 2024 were retrospectively enrolled and included in final analysis based on our inclusion and exclusion criteria. These patients were then stratified by age at first stroke onset into two groups: the oldest-old group (≥ 80 years) and the elderly group (60–79 years). Inclusion criteria comprised: (1) age ≥ 60 years; (2) initial clinical stroke event; (3) onset-to-admission time ≤ 7 days; (4) acute cerebral infarction confirmed by diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) on brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) within 24 h of admission; (5) ability to complete required examinations (brain MRI + MRA/CTA, cervical Doppler ultrasonography, laboratory tests). Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior stroke history (cerebral infarction, transient ischemic attack, cerebral hemorrhage); (2) other intracranial pathologies (traumatic brain injury, tumors, arteriovenous malformations, venous sinus thrombosis, hydrocephalus, multiple sclerosis, congenital abnormalities).

Collection of clinical data

We collected demographic and clinical data including age, sex, current smoking status, key comorbidities (atrial fibrillation, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, hyperhomocysteinemia), relevant medication history (antiplatelets, oral anticoagulants, statins, antihypertensives, antidiabetics), admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, and time from symptom onset to admission.

Collection of CoW data

All enrolled patients had underwent standardized neuroimaging within 72 h of admission, including axial T1-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), T2WI, T2-FLAIR MRI sequences, DWI, and 3D time-of-flight (3D-TOF) MRA or CTA. Two experienced neurologists (Lianyuan Feng and Xueli Wang), blinded to clinical data, independently evaluated MRA/CTA images to assess CoW configuration, with discrepancies resolved by consensus. The inter-rater reliability prior to consensus was assessed. The fetal posterior communicating artery (PcoA) variant was defined by visible PcoA with absent P1 segment. CoW integrity was scored (0–6) per Xu et al. [10]: anterior communicating artery (AcoA) scored 0 (absent AcoA/unilateral A1), 1 (intact AcoA with ipsilateral A1 diameter ≤ 50% of contralateral), or 2 (intact AcoA with bilateral A1 diameters > 50% of contralateral); each PcoA scored 0 (absent PcoA/P1), 1 (diameter ≤ 50% of ipsilateral P1), or 2 (diameter > 50% of ipsilateral P1); total score summed AcoA + bilateral PcoA scores. Poor CoW was defined as total score 0–2. CoW variants were classified per Hartkamp et al. [11] [incomplete: vessel absent or dysplastic (diameter < 0.8 mm)]: Type I (complete: all vessels present/non-dysplastic); Type II [complete anterior half, incomplete posterior half (≥ 1 vessel absent/dysplastic)]; Type III [incomplete anterior half (≥ 1 vessel absent/dysplastic), complete posterior half]; Type IV [incomplete anterior and posterior halves (≥ 1 vessel absent/dysplastic in each)].

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses used SPSS 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Continuous variable normality was assessed via Kolmogorov-Smirnov test; descriptive statistics presented normally-distributed data as mean ± SD, non-normal as median (IQR), and categorical data as frequencies (%). Group comparisons employed independent t-tests (normal continuous), Mann-Whitney U tests (non-normal continuous), χ² or Fisher’s exact tests (categorical). Inter-rater reliability was assessed for all categorical variables (CoW types) using Cohen’s kappa and for ordinal variables (AcoA score, PcoA score, total score) using linearly weighted kappa. Linear regression analyzed associations between first-onset age and CoW parameters (total score, AcoA score, PcoA score, poor CoW, fetal PcoA, incomplete CoW); nonparametric tests examined age group-CoW variable associations. Multivariable linear regression adjusted for: Model 1 (sex, smoking, comorbidities); Model 2 (Model 1 + medications). Sex-stratified analyses repeated comparative and regression assessments. Statistical significance was set at two-sided p < 0.05. Finally, a post hoc power analysis was conducted using PASS 15.0 software to evaluate the statistical power of the study and justify the sample size utilized.

Results

Comparison of demographic and clinical data

A total of 357 patients aged ≥ 60 years with first-ever acute ischemic stroke (MRI-DWI confirmed) and available MRA/CTA were screened. After exclusions [47 due to unclear stroke/TIA/mimic history, 12 for poor image quality/motion artifacts, and 22 for comorbidities including chronic subdural hematoma (n = 3), hypophyseal adenoma (n = 2), cavernous hemangioma (n = 3), arteriovenous malformation (n = 1), venous sinus thrombosis (n = 1), hydrocephalus (n = 2), Alzheimer’s disease (n = 7), mitochondrial encephalopathy (n = 1), suspected drug abuse (n = 1), and suspected CADASIL (cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy) (n = 1)], 276 patients were analyzed. The cohort comprised an elderly group (60–79 years; n = 188, 68.1%) and an oldest-old group (≥ 80 years; n = 88, 31.9%).

Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. The oldest-old group included a greater proportion of females (47.1% vs. 31.4%, p = 0.009). Oldest-old patients exhibited a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation (17.1% vs. 5.3%, p = 0.002) and coronary heart disease (26.1% vs. 10.6%, p = 0.001). No significant differences were observed in current smoking rates or other vascular risk factors (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, hyperhomocysteinemia; all p > 0.05). Pre-admission antiplatelet use was more frequent in the oldest-old group (38.6% vs. 28.2%, p = 0.082), while statin, oral anticoagulant (among atrial fibrillation patients), antihypertensive (among hypertensives), and antidiabetic (among diabetics) use showed no significant intergroup differences. Clinically, the oldest-old group presented with higher NIHSS scores at admission [median 2.5 (IQR 2.0–5.0) vs. 2.0 (1.0–3.0), p = 0.001], but time from onset to admission did not differ significantly between groups.

Given the potential anatomical relationship between CoW configurations and the topography of cerebral ischemic stroke, as well as the possible influence of infarct location and subtype on the time of first onset [12], we compared the two groups in terms of infarct distribution across vascular territories (the middle cerebral artery, anterior cerebral artery, and posterior cerebral artery), proportion of watershed infarctions, and frequency of lacunar infarcts. No statistically significant differences were observed in the overall territorial infarct distribution between groups. However, the oldest-old group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of watershed infarctions compared to the elderly group (23.9% vs. 11.7%, p = 0.009). In contrast, the rate of lacunar infarcts did not differ significantly between the groups.

Differences in CoW configuration between age groups

Characteristics of CoW variants are presented in Table 2. The inter-rater agreement prior to consensus was excellent. The weighted kappa was 0.98 (95%CI: 0.97–0.99) for the total CoW score, 0.97 (95%CI: 0.95–0.97) for the AcoA score, and 0.97 (95%CI: 0.96–0.99) for the PcoA score. For CoW morphological types, Cohen’s kappa was 0.94 (95%CI: 0.92–0.97). Significant distribution differences were observed between the oldest-old and elderly groups for total CoW scores (0–6; p = 0.013), AcoA scores (0–2; p = 0.003), and PcoA scores (0–4; p = 0.007). The oldest-old group demonstrated significantly higher median AcoA scores [2 (IQR 2–2) vs. 2 (1–2), p = 0.001] and total CoW scores [2 (2–3.75.75) vs. 2 (2–2), p < 0.001]. Poor CoW configuration (total score 0–2) was prevalent overall (76.1%, 210/276) but significantly less frequent in the oldest-old group (64.8% vs. 81.9%; p = 0.002). While the four-category CoW classification (Types I-IV) showed no significant intergroup distribution difference (p = 0.065), the oldest-old group exhibited a higher proportion of complete CoW (Type I: 14.8% vs. 6.9%). Association between age category and CoW characteristics

Based on the observed effect sizes from our primary outcomes described above—specifically, the difference in the prevalence of poor CoW configuration between the oldest-old and elderly groups (64.8% vs. 81.9%)—a post hoc power analysis indicated that our total sample size of 276 participants (n = 88 oldest-old; n = 188 elderly) achieved a statistical power exceeding 83% (α = 0.05, χ² test). These results confirm that the study was sufficiently powered to detect clinically meaningful associations between age category and CoW characteristics.Logistic regression analyses evaluated associations between CoW parameters and first-onset age category [reference: early-onset (60-79 years); outcome: late-onset (≥80 years)]. Unadjusted analyses revealed significant associations between late-onset and higher AcoA score (OR = 0.561, 95%CI = 0.389-0.810, p = 0.002), higher total CoW score (OR = 0.709, 95%CI = 0.578-0.870, p = 0.001), complete CoW (OR = 0.429, 95%CI = 0.190-0.968, p = 0.042), and poor CoW (OR = 2.588, 95%CI = 1.462-4.583, p = 0.001) (Table 3). After adjustment for sex, smoking, and comorbidities (atrial fibrillation, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, coronary heart disease, hyperhomocysteinemia; Model 1), AcoA score (OR = 0.584, 95%CI = 0.394-0.864, p = 0.007), total CoW score (OR = 0.722, 95%CI = 0.580-0.900, p = 0.004), and poor CoW (OR = 2.692, 95%CI = 1.463-4.956, p = 0.001) remained significantly associated with late-onset. Further adjustment for medication history (antiplatelets, statins, oral anticoagulants, antihypertensives, antidiabetics; Model 2) yielded consistent results for these parameters. Association between first-onset age and CoW characteristics

Linear regression analyses assessed the relationship between continuous first-onset age and CoW parameters (Table 4). After adjusting for vascular risk factors and medications, poor CoW configuration was significantly associated with earlier stroke onset in univariable analysis (β = -2.671 years, 95% CI = -5.140 to -0.203, p = 0.034), while higher AcoA score predicted later onset (β = 1.665 years, 95% CI = 0.343-2.987, p = 0.014). These associations persisted after adjustment for sex, smoking, and comorbidities (Model 1: poor CoW β = -2.629, p = 0.032; AcoA β = 1.367, p = 0.041). However, the poor CoW association became non-significant after additional medication adjustment (Model 2: β = -2.144, p = 0.081), whereas AcoA score remained significant (β = 1.336, p = 0.045). Discussion

A key finding of this study is the observation that a more favorable CoW configuration is present in oldest-old patients (≥80 years) with first-ever ischemic stroke compared to younger elderly individuals (60–79 years), with this association being particularly pronounced in females. To our knowledge, this appears to be the first investigation to directly link a complete CoW and higher AcoA score to delayed first-ever stroke onset specifically in an elderly cohort, even after accounting for conventional vascular risk factors. These results may offer a new neuroanatomical perspective on delayed stroke occurrence in very advanced age, suggesting that an intact CoW, especially complete AcoA, could act as a protective collateral mechanism that mitigates the effects of accumulated vascular risk and aging. The marked female-predominant advantage in CoW morphology also highlights its potential relevance for sex-specific risk assessment and supports the development of more personalized stroke prevention approaches in older populations.

The occurrence and severity of ischemic stroke are strongly associated with established vascular risk factors [13]. While timely diagnosis and management of these factors remain pivotal for stroke prevention, the observed resilience of late-onset stroke patients to traditional risk profiles suggests the existence of underlying protective mechanisms in the oldest-old population. Given the documented impact of CoW variants on ischemic pathology patterns, occlusion outcomes, and treatment approaches due to their morphological and functional characteristics [14], we hypothesized that favorable CoW configuration might contribute to delayed stroke onset. Our findings may support this hypothesis: oldest-old patients (≥80 years) exhibited significantly higher CoW scores and a greater prevalence of complete CoW morphology compared to younger elderly patients (60-79 years), with this association being particularly pronounced in females. These results, validated through complementary scoring (Xu et al. [10]) and categorical (Hartkamp et al. [11]) assessment methods, suggest that CoW integrity may serve as a protective factor against early cerebrovascular events.

While stroke is a multifactorial disease involving both modifiable and non-modifiable elements [15], our data reinforce poor CoW configuration as an independent risk factor. The developmental trajectory of the CoW—demonstrating greater completeness in childhood and increasing asymmetry/incompleteness with age [16]—suggests its configuration represents a largely non-modifiable anatomical determinant. Physiological considerations regarding the limited collateral capacity of small communicating arteries further support this interpretation. Consequently, early identification of poor CoW variants warrants intensified management of modifiable vascular risks (e.g., hypertension, atrial fibrillation) to potentially delay or prevent stroke onset in susceptible individuals.

Notably, women experience worse post-stroke outcomes globally, including higher mortality [17], greater disability burden, and increased stroke severity [18]. While explanations often cite longevity, care disparities, or hormonal factors, our finding of less favorable baseline CoW configurations in elderly females with stroke offers an additional neuroanatomical perspective on this sex disparity. The detrimental impact of poor CoW in females warrants systematic investigation.

Sex-stratified analyses yielded important insights. On the one hand, females exhibited a distinct anatomical advantage: the oldest-old women with first-ever ischemic stroke demonstrated significantly higher scores in the ACoA, PCoA, and overall CoW, along with a markedly lower prevalence of poor CoW configuration compared to younger elderly women. This observation is consistent with existing evidence indicating sexual dimorphism in CoW morphology, wherein females tend to have more complete configurations across different age groups, including pediatric [19] and young adult populations (<40 years) [20]. On the other hand, women in the oldest-old age group with acute ischemic stroke represent a clinical subgroup characterized by unfavorable prognosis and elevated in-hospital mortality, often associated with abrupt symptom onset, impaired consciousness, and medical complications during treatment [21]. Taken together, these findings suggest that while women may inherently possess a more favorable CoW morphology than men, those with poor CoW integrity are at increased risk of early-onset stroke and adverse clinical outcomes. Therefore, incorporating CoW morphology into risk stratification models—particularly for female patients—could facilitate earlier and more intensive management of modifiable risk factors, supporting more personalized and effective stroke prevention strategies.

The oldest-old (≥80 years) represent the fastest-growing demographic segment and exhibit distinct clinical-pathological profiles. Our study underscores the value of analyzing this group separately to uncover unique protective or risk factors. Investigating CoW morphology's relationship with stroke onset timing provides a novel lens for understanding healthy neurovascular aging and developing targeted prevention strategies. Neuroimaging data from oldest-old stroke survivors offer a particularly rich resource for such exploration, often exceeding the availability of imaging from healthy octogenarians.

Several limitations of this study warrant thorough discussion. First, the retrospective and single-center nature of our design inherently carries the risk of selection bias and may restrict the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. Second, the composition of our cohort was restricted to stroke patients; the absence of a control group comprising healthy oldest-old individuals without stroke limits our ability to define a normative or definitively neuroprotective CoW configuration. Third, the sample size, though adequate for primary analyses, was insufficient for robust subgroup analyses—such as comparisons based on specific infarct topography or lacunar versus non-lacunar subtypes—which may have obscured further clinically relevant associations. Fourth, as previously noted, we did not evaluate potential interactions between infarct topography and CoW variation, an important avenue for understanding mechanism-specific effects. Finally, although we adjusted for major known confounders, the potential for residual confounding due to unmeasured variables, such as detailed hemodynamic parameters or other undetermined clinical factors, cannot be excluded.

Future prospective studies with long-term follow-up, beginning at earlier ages, would help clarify the dynamics of survival bias and better establish causal relationships between CoW configuration and lifespan free of stroke. Additionally, linkage with mortality registries and inclusion of non-stroke octogenarians could further contextualize these findings. These studies should aim to include neuroimaging data from healthy oldest-old controls to better define the normative CoW anatomy in successful agers. Additionally, analyses stratified by stroke etiology (e.g., cardioembolic, large artery atherosclerosis, small-vessel occlusion) and topography could provide deeper mechanistic insights. Combining CoW morphology with advanced hemodynamic imaging techniques would also help elucidate the precise physiological mechanisms underlying this neuroanatomical protection. Furthermore, given the distinct pathophysiology and clinical features of lacunar infarcts [22], a promising future line of research would be to investigate potential differences in CoW configuration between oldest-old patients presenting with lacunar versus non-lacunar stroke subtypes in a larger, specifically designed cohort.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings indicate that among patients with first-ever ischemic stroke, those aged ≥80 years exhibit a more favorable CoW configuration—reflected in higher completeness scores and improved morphology—compared to patients aged 60–79 years. This association was more evident in females. To our knowledge, this study provides initial evidence specifically focusing on the oldest-old population with first-onset ischemic stroke and examining the relationship between CoW integrity and delayed stroke onset within an elderly cohort. However, these results should be interpreted with caution and require validation in larger, independent studies. Our observations contribute to a broader understanding of potential neuroanatomical factors that may help explain delayed stroke presentation in the oldest-old. Further investigation into the clinical utility of assessing CoW morphology, especially in females, could help refine risk stratification and inform more personalized stroke prevention strategies.
