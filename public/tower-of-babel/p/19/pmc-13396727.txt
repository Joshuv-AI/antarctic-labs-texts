Interaction between systemic arterial reservoir behavior and cerebrovascular vasoreactivity during acute controlled hypocapnia
Authors: Yaren Alan, Ahmet Tas, Muhammed Ikbal Bayhan, Alp Ozcan, Irem Sezer, Türker Kundak, Sabahattin Umman, Tim P van de Hoef, Jan J Piek, Kim H Parker, Murat Sezer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebral carbon dioxide (CO2) vasoreactivity (CVR), defined as the dilatory capacity of cerebral vessels in response to CO2, has pathophysiological importance and is clinically relevant. Reservoir–excess pressure analysis (REPA) of arterial blood pressure (ABP) waveforms, which estimates the pressure-buffering (reservoir) capacity of large arteries, provides contemporary insights beyond standard ABP measures by encompassing global arterial compliance and vascular tone. We examined the correlation between the systemic arterial reservoir behavior and CVR, as well as the coupling between peripheral and cerebrovascular hemodynamics. In 26 individuals, transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD) recordings of middle cerebral artery (MCA) flow velocity, simultaneous non-invasive ABP recordings and end-tidal CO2 (etCO2) were obtained at baseline and during intentional hyperventilation to lower CO2. Reservoir–excess pressure parameters, including integral of reservoir pressure (IntPr) and diastolic rate constant (DRC) were extracted from ensemble-averaged ABP waveforms. CVR, defined as ΔMCAV/ΔetCO2, and cerebrovascular resistance index (CVRi), defined as MAP/MCAV, were calculated. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated as ABP averaged over the cardiac cycle. In participants in the below-mean CVR group, a lower DRC, reflecting slower diastolic decay, was associated with a higher CVRi (r = -0.445; p = 0.029). By contrast, in the above-mean CVR group, a lower DRC correlated with a lower CVRi (r = 0.807; p < 0.001), suggesting effective flow redistribution to support the brain when vasoreactivity is intact. REPA revealed subtle differences between the below- and above-mean CVR subgroups, despite comparable mean arterial, systolic, and diastolic blood pressures. The interplay between systemic arterial reservoir function and cerebral perfusion is modulated by the extent of CVR. When CVR is higher, increased peripheral vascular tone (reflected by a lower DRC) coincides with lower cerebrovascular resistance, which may contribute to effective redistribution of blood flow to preserve brain perfusion. Introduction

Cerebrovascular autoregulation is the ability of cerebral blood vessels to adapt to changes in perfusion pressure, thereby maintaining relatively constant cerebral blood flow1. This essential process is facilitated by structural and functional safeguards of the brain, including the Circle of Willis and a highly efficient collateral network, along with autoregulatory adjustments at the microcirculatory level1. When cerebral perfusion is threatened (e.g., by systemic hypotension), systemic adaptive mechanisms can also be engaged, redistributing blood flow to preserve cerebral perfusion even at the expense of other organ territories2–4. Thus, both local cerebral mechanisms and broader systemic responses contribute to maintaining adequate cerebral blood flow across a range of physiological challenges1,5–8.

Beyond perfusionpressure regulation, another critical determinant of cerebrovascular control is the arterial CO2 level. Increases in CO2 induce vasodilation of cerebral arterioles, a physiological response quantified as CO2 vasoreactivity (CVR). Although CVR is fundamentally considered a brain-specific index, lower CVR values have been linked to higher cardiovascular mortality and are predictive of future stroke9,10. This systemic relevance underscores that CVR reflects not only neurovascular function but also overall vascular health.

Reservoir–excess pressure analysis (REPA) offers a contemporary approach to characterizing subtle dynamic features of the arterial pressure waveform that provide pathophysiological insight beyond conventional arterial blood pressure (ABP) parameters. It quantifies: (i) the Windkessel-like reservoir pressure (Pr), which reflects the buffering capacity of elastic arteries and is assumed to be spatially uniform across the major arterial tree; (ii) the excess pressure (Pxs), representing the component above Pr and governed by local hemodynamic influences; and (iii) the diastolic rate constant (DRC) as an indicator of peripheral vascular tone and compliance11. Although differences in Pr and Pxs have been reported across the arterial tree, largely reflecting local variation in Pxs12, dynamic adaptations in peripheral arterial reservoir behavior, particularly those serving compensatory roles, remain insufficiently explored.

In this context, the aim of the present study was to investigate how peripheral arterial reservoir behavior interacts with cerebrovascular dynamics during experimentally induced vasoconstriction via controlled hypocapnia. By examining the synchrony between peripheral vascular responses and cerebrovascular reactivity, we sought to advance understanding of global vascular compensatory mechanisms that support cerebral perfusion.

Materials and methods

Study population

This study was performed retrospectively on the data from a prospective, non-blinded, non-randomized, single-center study conducted at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center13,14. The source study (accessible via PhysioNet) provides a comprehensive account of the inclusion and exclusion criteria, as well as the protocol in full detail14. In summary, the dataset comprised de-identified physiological recordings, including transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD) measurements of the middle cerebral arteries (MCA), continuous high-resolution waveforms of non-invasive arterial blood pressure and bilateral MCA flow velocities (MCAV) at rest and during hyperventilation, and clinical and laboratory data from 5 stroke patients and 27 control participants. In accordance with our study protocol, only control participants were selected for analysis. After excluding measurements with missing data or insufficient signal quality for detailed hemodynamic waveform analysis, a total of 43 vessels from 26 participants were included in the final analysis. Transcranial doppler ultrasound assessment

A transcranial Doppler ultrasound (TCD) system (Multi-Dop X4; Neuroscan, Inc, Sterling, VA) was utilized to monitor MCAV bilaterally. The right and left MCAs were insonated from the temporal windows by placing the 2-MHz probe in the temporal area above the zygomatic arch. Each probe was positioned to record the maximal MCAV and fixed at the desired angle using a three-dimensional positioning system attached to the light-metal probe holder (PMD150; Spencer Technologies, Inc. WA). Systolic, diastolic, and mean MCAV were derived from the envelope of the Doppler flow velocity waveforms. Heart rate was measured with a 3-lead electrocardiogram (Spacelabs Medical, Issaquah, Wash). Beat-to-beat blood pressure was continuously recorded with a Finapress device (Ohmeda Monitoring Systems, Englewood, Colorado) and confirmed with Dinamap blood pressure measurements. Respiration was monitored continuously using a nasal thermistor and an infrared gas analyzer (Capnomac Ultima, Ohmeda Inc.) to quantify tidal volume, etCO2, and O2 via an attached face mask. Participants rested in the supine position for 10 min to allow stabilization of cardiovascular and respiratory parameters, with continuous monitoring of heart rate, beat-to-beat blood pressure, bilateral MCAV, respiration, and end-tidal CO2 (etCO2) and O2. Following this equilibration period, participants underwent a controlled hyperventilation protocol to reduce etCO2 to approximately 25 mmHg for 3 min. After hyperventilation, participants remained in the supine position for an additional 10 min with continuous ECG, blood pressure, TCD, and etCO2 monitoring to evaluate recovery dynamics.

Hemodynamic signal analysis and cerebrovascular perfusion indices

Data from 26 participants were included in the final analysis. Simultaneously recorded bilateral MCAV signals (when available), peripheral non-invasive arterial pressure waveforms, and etCO2 were analyzed at baseline and during hyperventilation-induced hypocapnia (etCO2(h) etCO2(b)). EtCO2 was used to estimate arterial CO2 according to previous studies15. After importing the data into the MATLAB environment (v R2024a, The MathWorks, USA), the recorded peripheral noninvasive arterial pressure waveforms were ensemble-averaged into a single representative cardiac cycle to calculate conventional arterial pressure parameters, including mean arterial pressure (MAP), and to allow extraction of the reservoir–excess pressure parameters. Time windows were chosen to be as long as possible while maintaining adequate signal quality, with a minimum of 8 cardiac cycles included in each. From the averaged pressure signal, MAP, systolic blood pressure (Psys) and diastolic blood pressure (Pdias) were calculated separately at baseline and during hyperventilation. In addition, we used the core MATLAB code from our previous studies to perform the reservoir–excess pressure analysis (REPA)16–18. The subscripts (b) and (h) appended to CVRi, etCO₂ and MCAv denote values obtained at baseline and during hyperventilation, respectively. ΔMCAV was calculated as MCAV(h) MCAV(b), and ΔetCO2 was defined analogously. Although TCD itself does not measure absolute CBF, MCAV is a reliable and valid surrogate for CBF in the corresponding brain region19–23. Moreover, the potential influence of MCA diameter on TCD-derived estimates has been investigated under both hypocapnic and hypercapnic conditions23–25. A high-resolution MRI study by Verbree et al. reported that diameter changes of the insonated MCA trunk are negligible within physiologically relevant CO2 variations25, as vasomotor responses primarily occur in smaller distal resistance vessels rather than in the proximal large arteries26. Accordingly, MCAV remains an appropriate surrogate for relative changes in cerebral blood flow within the range of CO2 modulation used in our protocol. Nevertheless, during markedly elevated CO2 levels in hypercapnia, more noticeable diameter changes may occur; therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting velocity as a direct indicator of volumetric flow under those conditions25.

Because CBF depends on both cerebral perfusion pressure and cerebrovascular resistance, their relationship can be expressed analogously to Ohm’s law (Q = ΔP/R). CVR was calculated as ΔMCAV/ΔetCO2, consistent with its definition27. In addition, cerebrovascular resistance index (CVRi), a measure of the resistance to blood flow within the cerebral circulation, was calculated as MAP/MCAV28,29 (Fig. 2).

CO2 is a potent vasodilator in the arterial system30 with numerous studies demonstrating its key role in cerebrovascular regulation24,31–33. Hypercapnia (high CO2) leads to marked vasodilation of the arteries and arterioles and increases cerebral blood flow, among other flow-regulating mechanisms34,35, whereas hypocapnia (low CO2) has the opposite effect, causing vasoconstriction and reducing cerebral blood flow. We therefore used controlled hypocapnia (via hyperventilation) as the physiological challenge in this study. In this context, CVR reflects the dynamic ability of the cerebral circulation to adjust its tone in response to changes in CO2 levels. Due to the lack of an established cut-off in the literature, we used the group mean CVR value (0.95 0.71 cm s−1 mmHg−1; 95% CI: 0.73–1.17 cm s−1 mmHg− 1 to divide participants into below-mean (≤ 0.95 cm s−1 mmHg− 1 and above-mean (> 0.95 cm s−1 mmHg−1 CVR groups. The relatively large standard deviation indicates that the threshold between below and above-mean groups will vary between studies, and this should be taken into account when comparing studies.

Comparable subgrouping strategies have been implemented in similar exploratory analyses, where relatively higher CVR values in the studied population are interpreted as indicative of preserved vasoreactivity in the absence of standardized reference ranges9. Given the relatively small size of our cohort, mean-based dichotomization supported balanced group sizes and avoided the imposition of external or arbitrary thresholds, thereby enabling meaningful subgroup comparisons. Additionally, we used CVRi to quantify the resistance of cerebral vessels.

Finally, the mean absolute (Δ) changes (hyperventilation values - baseline values) and relative percentage changes (Δ%) (hyperventilation values - baseline values/ baseline values x 100) were calculated for both reservoir–excess pressure parameters and cerebrovascular variables (CVRi, MCAV). Analyses were performed on 43 vessels from 26 participants. Of these, 17 patients had both bilateral TCD channels available, whereas 9 patients had only one analyzable vessel. Based on the predefined CVR cut-off value used in this study, 4 vessels (2 left MCA and 2 right MCA) from 2 patients were classified into opposite CVR categories at the vessel-level analysis, despite originating from the same individuals. For the REPA analysis presented in Supplementary File 1, results were available for 35 vessels from 21 participants due to missing or poor-quality arterial pressure signals during hyperventilation.

Reservoir–excess pressure analysis (REPA)

This analysis decomposes the arterial pressure waveform into two components: Pr, analogous to Windkessel pressure with nuances, and Pxs, representing the additional pressure above the reservoir that propagates through the arterial system11 (Fig. 3). By capturing these physiologically distinct features, REPA provides insight into global arterial compliance and peripheral resistance beyond what is conveyed by conventional blood pressure measures36–39. The arterial reservoir capacity, a function of net arterial wall compliance, is dynamically modulated by total vascular resistance and, in turn, influences vascular tone40–42. REPA also derives the diastolic rate constant (DRC), which represents the exponential rate at which reservoir pressure declines (= release of reservoir) during diastole (Fig. 3)11. DRC is determined by the combined effects of systemic arterial compliance and resistance to outflow. A slower diastolic decay (lower DRC) reflects increased peripheral vasomotor tone and enhanced arterial compliance, whereas a faster decay (higher DRC) is associated with attenuated cumulative peripheral resistance and reduced arterial compliance, leading to a more rapid discharge of reservoir pressure11,43. Given its known dependence on compliance and systemic arterial resistance, DRC (the inverse of the time constant (tau) of the diastolic decay (1/)) was calculated to evaluate changes in vasomotor behavior. REPA was applied to ensemble-averaged arterial pressure waveforms collected both at rest and during hyperventilation. A more detailed mathematical description has been published previously40,42.

Representative reservoir–excess pressure analysis (REPA) decomposition of arterial pressure. P denotes total measured pressure; ks and kd represent systolic and diastolic rate constants, respectively; and P∞ denotes the asymptotic pressure derived from non-linear exponential fitting of the diastolic pressure decay.

Reservoir–excess pressure analysis was performed using a MATLAB implementation described by Hughes and Parker44 and validated in large-scale clinical studies, including the CAFE substudy of the Anglo-Scandinavian Cardiac Outcomes Trial (ASCOT)45. After obtaining the ensemble reservoir pressure waveform (Pr), additional transformation was performed through diastolic pressure subtraction (Pr − Pdias). Integral of reservoir pressure (IntPr) and integral of reservoir pressure after diastolic pressure subtraction (Int(Pr−Pdias)), together with the integral of excess pressure (IntPxs), were calculated as REPA-derived indices, each demonstrating strong associations with pathophysiological alterations and adverse clinical outcomes40,45. As a potential novel index, the arterial reservoir capacity (ARC), defined as either Pr/Pxs or IntPr/IntPxs, was also computed as an exploratory measure to stratify individuals according to global vascular status16.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed in a blinded fashion on a per-vessel basis (where applicable, separately for both left and right MCA vessels). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation [95% Confidence Intervals]. Normality of variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical and discrete variables were presented as numbers and relative frequencies (percentages) and compared with Fisher’s exact test. Group means for variables with normal and non-normal distributions were compared using independent samples t-tests, Welch’s tests, and Mann-Whitney U tests, respectively. Statistical analysis was performed at rest and during induced hyperventilation using either a paired samples t-test or a Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as appropriate. Correlation coefficients between reservoir–excess pressure and cerebrovascular parameters (CVRi and MCAV) were evaluated using Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients, depending on the normality of the variables. Univariate and multivariate generalized linear model (GLM) with a gamma distribution and log-link function, and a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with a gamma distribution, log-link function and a random intercept by patient ID were performed to examine the relationships in the presence of various confounding variables, taking into account the known effect on vascular hemodynamics of age, sex, cardiovascular risk factors present in the study population (hypertension, hyperlipidemia), different medications (antihypertensive, statin, antiplatelets) and smoking. Univariate GLM predictors of CVRi exhibiting p-values less than 0.1 were subsequently included in both a multivariate GLM and GLMM to further investigate their independent effects and account for potential data dependencies. Outliers were defined as measurements falling outside the range [Q1−(IQR⋅2.2), Q3+(IQR⋅2.2)] as previously utilized, and excluded from the analyses after controlling for measurement error27. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data were blindly analyzed offline using R-based JAMOVI statistical software (The Jamovi Project, Sydney, Australia) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethical review

The study protocol conforms to the Declaration of Helsinki. The original study consecutively recruited participants and obtained informed consent in accordance with the standards of their local Institutional Review Board14. The present analysis was performed retrospectively on an open-access, de-identified dataset, and thus no further review was required.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The study population included 16 females (61.5%) and had a mean age of 69 ± 5 years. No participant had a history of stroke (cerebrovascular accident), diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, heart failure, or atrial fibrillation. Hypertension was present in 30.77% [8/26] of the study population (Table 1). No statistically significant differences were observed in the prevalence of hypertension (p = 0.633), mean duration of antihypertensive treatment (p = 0.365), frequency of hyperlipidemia (p = 0.545), serum levels of cholesterol (p = 0.830) or smoking status (p = 0.118) between the below- and above-mean CVR groups. In addition, there were no between-group differences in systolic (p = 0.634), diastolic (p = 0.253), or mean arterial pressures (p = 0.762) between the below- and above-mean CVR groups.

Cerebral flow velocity response to hyperventilation

Overall, the hyperventilation maneuver induced a 20% decrease in MCAV [From 53.6 ± 15.9 cm/s to 42.1 ± 11.7 cm/s (p < 0.001)]. The above-mean CVR group, compared to the below-mean CVR group, showed a greater reduction in MCAV (29.3 ± 7.9% vs. 12.6 ± 8.1%, p < 0.001). There were no statistically significant differences between hypertensive and non-hypertensive participants regarding MCAV(b) (p = 0.957), MCAV(h) (p = 0.663) or absolute (ΔMCAV) (p = 0.478) and relative (Δ%MCAV) (p = 0.420) changes in MCAV. Notably, ∆etCO2 (p = 0.971) and Δ%etCO2 (p = 0.549) did not differ between groups during hyperventilation, indicating that the vasoconstrictor stimulus was not different in magnitude.

Reservoir–excess pressure analysis along with cerebrovascular parameters

The above-mean CVR group had a lower baseline cerebrovascular resistance index (CVRi(b)) (p < 0.001), accompanied by higher MCAV(b) (p < 0.001) and MCAV(h) (p = 0.028) values. By contrast, the below-mean CVR group exhibited significantly higher IntPr at baseline (Table 2). In addition, the concomitant presence of enhanced IntPr and elevated MCAV(b) was observed exclusively in the group with below-mean CVR (r = 0.498, p = 0.013), which may imply the presence of an additional compensatory mechanism accompanying increased reservoir pressure. No significant correlations involving the conventional blood pressure measures (MAP, Psys, Pdias) were found in either CVR group, underscoring that conventional blood pressure measurements alone did not explain the observed differences.

Cerebrovascular reactivity modified the interplay between peripheral and cerebral conductance in distinct ways across groups, reflecting potential differences in regulatory mechanisms. In the above-mean CVR group (> 0.95 cm s−1 mmHg−1), low DRC (reflecting higher peripheral vasomotor tone) was accompanied by lower CVRi (b) (r= 0.807, p < 0.001) and higher MCAV(b) (r = -0.703, p< 0.001). The coexistence of lower CVRi (b), higher MCAV(b) and lower DRC indicates a prioritization of cerebral over peripheral perfusion, in other words a more preferential flow diversion to the brain, in the group with above-mean CVR in response to vasoconstrictor stimulus. Oppositely, in the group with below-mean CVR, DRC was inversely correlated with CVRi (b) (r= -0.445, p= 0.029) (Fig. 4). This negative linear correlation observed between DRC and CVRi (b), in which low DRC is associated with higher CVRi (b) and vice versa, reflects the concomitant attenuation or enhancement of peripheral and cerebral vasomotor tone and may suggest impaired cerebrovascular–peripheral coupling, resulting in reduced preferential flow redirection to the brain. Consistently, DRC was positively correlated with MCAV in the below-mean group (r= 0.631, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Responses of reservoir–excess pressure parameters and cerebrovascular parameters to hyperventilation

Consistent with earlier findings, the above-mean CVR group exhibited a stronger vasoconstrictor response to the hypocapnic stimulus than the below-mean CVR group, as evidenced by greater Δ%MCAV (19.1 ± 5.85 vs. 5.44 ± 12.63, p < 0.001) and ΔMCAV (29.34 ± 7.85 vs. 12.63 ± 8.07, p < 0.001) compared to the below-mean CVR group. Correspondingly, the mean ΔCVRi (0.57 ± 0.33 vs. 0.19 ± 0.28, p < 0.001) and Δ%CVRi (42.66 ± 26.59 vs. 8.30 ± 10.0, p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the above-mean CVR group (Fig. 5).

Absolute and relative cerebrovascular resistance index (CVRi) changes in response to hyperventilation.

During the hyperventilation phase, i.e. after induced vasoconstriction, the above-mean CVR group maintained the significant correlations between DRC(h) and MCAV(h) (r: -0.666, p = 0.005) and CVRi(h) (r: 0.761, p < 0.001) similar to that observed at baseline. In contrast, in the below-mean CVR group, no significant correlation was present between MCAV(h) (r:0.134, p = 0.586) or CVRi(h) (r: -0.113, p = 0.646) with DRC(h), indicating a breakdown of the coordinated peripheral-cerebral response under hypocapnia.

Furthermore, the below-mean CVR group exhibited more labile hemodynamic features characterized by significant alterations in both conventional arterial parameters (MAP, Psys, Pdias) and reservoir–excess pressure parameters (Pr, PrPdias, Pxs) in peripheral vessels after induced vasoconstriction, further suggesting a fragile compensatory mechanism (Supplement 1).

Correlation between CVRi and DRC across CVR groups and interference of potential confounders

Given the known effects of certain diseases and medications on the vasculature, we employed GLM and GLMM analyses to rigorously evaluate the correlations between CVRi and DRC in the two CVR groups. Potential confounders, including age, sex, a composite cardiovascular risk factor (hypertension and hyperlipidemia), and medical treatment history, were accounted for based on the descriptive characteristics of the study population (Table 1).

Univariate GLM analysis in the above-mean CVR group identified age (exp(B) = 1.031, p < 0.001), hypertension (exp(B) = 0.827, p = 0.095), and beta-blocker use (exp(B) = 1.261, p = 0.046) as potential confounders. However, these effects did not persist in multivariate GLM and GLMM. Importantly, the significant positive association between CVRi and DRC remained robust in both multivariate GLM (exp(B) = 1.242, p = 0.011) and mixed models (exp(B) = 1.255, p = 0.014) after adjusting for these potential confounders in the above-mean CVR group.

Similarly, multivariate GLM analysis in the below-mean CVR group indicated that a unit increase in DRC was associated with a 14.9% reduction in CVRi (exp(B) = 0.851, p = 0.030), although this association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the GLMM analysis (exp(B) = 0.898, p = 0.273). Collectively, these results suggest a significant alteration in the cerebral and peripheral vascular response to the stimuli in the above- and below-mean CVR groups, potentially indicating a protective cerebral vascular response in the above-mean CVR group (Table 3).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate how cerebral CO2 vasoreactivity interacts with the dynamic reservoir behavior of the systemic arterial system during an acute vasoconstrictive challenge to cerebral circulation (controlled hypocapnia). We demonstrated that the systemic peripheral vasomotor response to reduced cerebral perfusion may follow a divergent pattern during the dynamic cerebrovascular response.

The principal finding of this study is that there is a bidirectional relationship between peripheral vascular tone (indexed by DRC) and cerebrovascular resistance (CVRi), which depends on the effectiveness of cerebrovascular vasodilatory responsiveness. In individuals with higher cerebral vasoreactivity, an increase in peripheral vasoconstriction is accompanied by a decrease in cerebrovascular resistance, effectively prioritizing cerebral perfusion. In contrast, when vasodilatory responsiveness is impaired, peripheral and cerebral vascular tone rise in tandem, resulting in reduced cerebral perfusion and a decrease in MCA flow velocity (Figs. 4, 5 and 6; Table 3).

Differential cerebro-peripheral coupling across CVR groups.

Peripheral hemodynamic response to cerebral hypoperfusion

Cerebral hypoperfusion may occur transiently, for instance, during brief orthostatic stress, but is typically compensated by intact autoregulatory mechanisms. In such instances, total peripheral resistance and cardiac index decrease, while heart rate increases46. Additionally, animal studies have shown that pharmacologically induced hypotension causes a marked reduction in systemic peripheral resistance and, consequently, diminished blood flow in other organ territories, while cerebral blood flow remained relatively constant despite a 61% reduction in mean arterial pressure47.

Novak et al. reported that middle cerebral artery flow velocity (MCAV) can be lower at baseline in patients with cerebrovascular disease, despite comparable mean arterial pressure, and may decline further with postural change14. These observations underscore the importance of intact cerebrovascular vasodilatory responsiveness in maintaining perfusion when systemic pressures fluctuate. However, conventional blood pressure parameters do not capture certain dynamic features of the arterial pressure waveform, specifically the arterial reservoir behavior and excess pressure, that have been demonstrated across a wide range of diseases and organ systems36–39,48,49 including within the cerebral circulation50.

Insights from reservoir–excess pressure analysis

Some studies have noted that systemic arterial reactivity can diminish in parallel with cerebrovascular reactivity under certain conditions51. However, to our knowledge no prior work has directly examined the interplay between peripheral reservoir–excess pressure characteristics and cerebrovascular dynamics. This is the first study to integrate reservoir–excess pressure parameters with CO2 vasoreactivity (CVR) assessment, and this approach unmasked subtle hemodynamic differences between participants with high vs. low CVR despite having comparable blood pressures. Notably, we observed opposite directional correlations between DRC and CVRi in the two groups: in the below-mean CVR group DRC was inversely correlated with CVRi, whereas in the above-mean CVR group DRC was positively correlated with CVRi. Furthermore, in the below-mean CVR group, a higher reservoir pressure integral (IntPr) was associated with a higher MCAV(b), suggesting a possible salvage effect whereby an elevated reservoir pressure helps sustain cerebral blood flow despite impaired reactivity.

Reservoir pressure functions as a dynamic capacitor within the cardiovascular system, buffering sudden fluctuations during ventricular contraction to prevent abrupt changes in blood flow and protecting distal vessels from harmful pulsatile hemodynamics. Yet, it not only operates during systole, favoring a distally protected vascular bed with more uniform perfusion, but also sustains its role throughout the cardiac cycle. By enabling the gradual release of stored volume and energy, reservoir pressure acts as a hemodynamic reserve that supports both end-systolic and diastolic coronary filling11. Theoretically, it represents the component of the arterial pressure waveform corresponding to the minimum hydraulic work required by the left ventricle to generate a given stroke volume52. In this context, reservoir pressure serves as an integrated marker influenced by ventricular output, arterial compliance, systemic vasomotor tone, and aortic impedance.

Effective reservoir function is essential for maintaining optimal hemodynamic performance, and deviations from the physiological range may indicate pathological conditions. Functionally, the reservoir component buffers sudden pressure rises, thereby mitigating potential barotrauma. Conversely, since only pressure exceeding Pr can propagate forward, an abnormally elevated Pr reflects a higher workload imposed on the left ventricle to generate forward flow53. In our study, higher IntPr values observed in the below-mean CVR group (Table 2) may suggest that the heart must exert greater hydraulic effort to achieve the same stroke volume, likely reflecting reduced arterial compliance or increased downstream impedance, as reported previously53. This finding points to a potentially deleterious hemodynamic burden in individuals with lower CVR, who may require additional cardiac effort to maintain cerebral perfusion during hypocapnic challenges. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted within their broader physiological and contextual framework.

Divergent pattern of vasomotor behavior in the cerebral and systemic vasculature

In the above-mean CVR group, peripheral blood flow appears to be redirected toward the brain through increased systemic peripheral vasomotor tone (i.e., low DRC/slower diastolic decay accompanied by higher MCAV), resulting in better maintenance of cerebral blood flow, reflecting prioritization of cerebral perfusion over peripheral blood flow. However, this protective coupling appears to break down in the below-mean CVR group, which exhibits a simultaneous rise in both cerebral and systemic vascular resistance (Fig. 6). Accordingly, individuals with above-mean CVR demonstrate a robust peripheral vasomotor response when cerebral perfusion is challenged, as reflected by a slower diastolic pressure decay (greater peripheral vascular tone) and a concomitant preservation of cerebral perfusion. Conversely, in the group exhibiting below-mean CVR, greater DRC did not result in a significant recovery of brain perfusion; however, an enhancement of cerebrovascular perfusion was observed relative to the lower DRC values, as indicated by elevated MCAV (Fig. 4). In essence, the opposite DRC-CVRi correlations in the two groups (even after accounting for confounders) underscore a fundamentally different cerebro-peripheral coupling: a protective mechanism in the above-mean CVR group versus a maladaptive one in the below-mean CVR group (Table 3).

Overall, individuals with below-mean and above-mean CVR demonstrate distinctly different relationships between cerebral perfusion and the reservoir behavior of systemic arteries. The below-mean CVR group exhibited more labile responses to hypocapnia challenge, as reflected by more pronounced changes in arterial blood pressure variables. Importantly, reservoir–excess pressure analysis revealed additional hemodynamic components that conventional parameters would have otherwise masked, allowing the identification of subtle yet consistent patterns (Supplement 1).

Potential clinical applications: using reservoir–excess pressure parameters in cerebrovascular assessment

The clinical significance of CVR9,27,51 and CVRi28,29,54 has been investigated in previous studies. Traditionally, cerebral perfusion control has been evaluated with indices based on mean arterial pressure, which reflect the ability of cerebral vessels to maintain flow during sustained changes in perfusion pressure. However, these static measures do not fully capture the rapid, beat-to-beat adjustments in cerebrovascular tone that occur in response to dynamic fluctuations in the arterial pressure waveform. Key aspects of this fast dynamic control can be missed if analysis is confined to the cerebral circulation alone. By contrast, incorporating reservoir–excess pressure analysis allows us to recognize patterns that include the systemic peripheral response to the same stimulus. In this context, it can be observed that when cerebral vasodilatory responsiveness is reduced, disturbed systemic arterial reservoir behavior may further impair cerebral perfusion, even in participants who are otherwise considered healthy. Such subtle impairment might represent an early or subclinical form of vascular vulnerability.

In summary, individuals with lower CVR tend to develop an excessive peripheral circulatory response that yields only a minimal increase in cerebral blood flow, whereas those with robust CVR can effectively redirect pressure and blood flow to the brain, while preventing peripheral hyperperfusion. The clinical implications of these contrasting patterns warrant further investigation in dedicated studies.

Conclusion

The immediate compensatory response to an acute vasoconstrictor stimulus in the cerebral circulation triggered by instructed hyperventilation is modulated by an effective crosstalk between intact cerebral CO2 vasoreactivity and a robust arterial reservoir function of the systemic circulation. Incorporating non-invasive arterial reservoir–excess pressure metrics provides a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of cardiac–cerebrovascular coupling, concealed by conventional blood pressure parameters.

Limitations

The small sample size and the presence of risk factors, albeit in a minority of the participants, are the main limitations of the present study. On the other hand, we endeavored to overcome the modifying effect of confounders by performing the generalized linear models while accounting for the cardiovascular risk factors and medication history in the study population. Given the lack of a validated clinical cut-off for CVR in the existing literature, we defined the mean CVR value (0.95 cm s−1 mmHg−1) as the threshold for exploratory group comparisons. However, this study was designed as a hypothesis-generating, mechanistic, proof-of-concept investigation, and the findings should be interpreted within this context. Future studies including clinical outcome measures and ROC-based optimization will be required to establish clinically meaningful CVR cut-offs. Notably, the study included control participants with no documented history of cerebral disease. Here, the hyperventilation stimulus, leading to a widespread metabolic change, resulted in a relatively homogeneous response. This contrasts with neurophysiological assessments involving visual stimulation and verbal challenges, which may remain localized and elicit a heterogeneous response across distinct brain areas. In addition, CVRi was calculated as MAP/MCAV. While the relationship between MAP and MCAV largely defines cerebrovascular resistance and is documented in the literature for CVRi calculation with normal intracranial pressure (ICP), this implies that it may not accurately represent resistance when ICP is not within the normal range. This was a hypothesis-generating hemodynamic study with pathophysiological observations on the coupling between peripheral and cerebral flow regulation. Further validation is necessary in a larger, prospective clinical trial.
