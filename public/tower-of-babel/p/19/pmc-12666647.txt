Home sputum collection for Aspergillus fumigatus detection in adults with cystic fibrosis
Authors: Gina Hong, Joanna Walsh, Allen Koshy, Jesse Y. Hsu, Anna L. O'Dea, Elisa M. Vesely, Warda Memon, Rebecca H. Dezube, Christopher H. Goss, Louisa B. Goss, Alicia Greene, Jane E. Gross, Alexandra Wilson, David P. Nichols, Sean X. Zhang, Robert A. Cramer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) has impacted the ability for people with cystic fibrosis (PwCF) to spontaneously expectorate sputum, leading to lower respiratory sampling rates and infection detection challenges. Home sampling may permit a potential strategy for fungal detection in PwCF.MethodsWe conducted a prospective decentralised cohort study of PwCF to test the feasibility of home sputum collection and ambient temperature transport for Aspergillus fumigatus (Af) detection. Participants collected and shipped weekly sputum samples from home to the laboratory for fungal culture and completed electronic questionnaires. Descriptive statistics were calculated for patient factors, sputum characteristics and Af-positive cultures. We used a generalised estimating equations model to determine the association between highly effective modulator therapy (HEMT) and sputum volume.ResultsWe enrolled 76 adults with cystic fibrosis (CF) with a median (interquartile range) forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) % predicted of 72.5% (53.8–86.3). 60 (79%) were on ETI and 44 (58%) had a history of Aspergillus. 70 (92%) successfully collected and shipped three or more sputum samples. Of 284 samples received, 83% arrived within one day. Sputum collection was reported as easy in 83 (29%) and somewhat easy in 114 (40%) collection events. Sputum volume from PwCF on HEMT was 36% lower than those not on HEMT (36%, 95% CI 3–58; p=0.03), adjusting for covariates. Af was detected in 205 (73%) of home sputum samples.ConclusionHome sputum collection is feasible in adults with CF. Af was detected in remotely collected sputum samples. Further work to assess the validity of home sputum samples in PwCF is necessary to determine the value of remote specimens in clinical and research settings.

Introduction

Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) modulators directly augment CFTR protein function, thereby improving mucociliary clearance and respiratory health in people with cystic fibrosis (PwCF) [1, 2]. Approximately 90% of PwCF are eligible for CFTR modulator therapies, including ivacaftor, tezacaftor/ivacaftor, and elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI), the most widely used CFTR modulator. Expectorated sputum collection has proven far more difficult with ETI use, resulting in dramatic reductions in clinical microbial testing and reported prevalence of cystic fibrosis (CF) microorganisms [3–6]. As chronic infections persist in PwCF on CFTR modulators, alternative surveillance methods are needed due to poor sensitivity of oropharyngeal swabs [4, 7–9]. Fungal culture testing requires sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage or tissue, as oropharyngeal swabs are insufficient for mould detection in the lower respiratory tract [6].

Survey data of 168 PwCF and caregivers of children with CF revealed that despite changes in spontaneous sputum expectoration, 58% expectorated sputum at home within the past 2 weeks, suggesting that remote respiratory sampling may be possible (Goss, personal communication 2021). Recognising that sputum expectoration continues to occur outside of the clinic despite difficulty obtaining sputum samples in clinic, some centres have explored the use of remote respiratory sampling with sample “drop-off” and rarely with mail transport [10, 11]. Furthermore, healthcare delivery shifted rapidly to telehealth and remote monitoring during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. Many PwCF and their families favour ongoing telehealth use. Yet, both patients and clinicians perceive the inability to perform microbiological surveillance during telehealth visits as a significant barrier for the telehealth care delivery model [12–15]. The CF Foundation recently redefined the CF care model in the United States to adapt to the changing clinical landscape. While reductions in CF clinic visit frequency can be considered in some older children and adults to every 4–6 months, respiratory sampling for infection surveillance continues to be recommended every 3 months[16]. Introduction of remotely collected and mail-in specimens would align well with the modified patient-centred practice for PwCF. Feasibility, acceptability and validity of remotely collected respiratory specimens has yet to be rigorously explored but is a leading research priority. We sought to test the feasibility and acceptability of remote sputum collection at home in adults with CF shipping samples to a centralised laboratory, with a focus on detecting Aspergillus fumigatus (Af).

Methods

Study population

We conducted a decentralised pilot study of PwCF from October 2021 to July 2023. Adults with CF 18 years and older without history of organ transplantation receiving clinical care at American CF centres at the University of Pennsylvania (Philadelphia, PA, USA), University of Washington (Seattle, WA, USA), National Jewish Health (Denver, CO, USA) or Johns Hopkins University (Baltimore, MD, USA) were remotely consented to participate. We initially enrolled adults with history of Aspergillus-positive cultures within 2 years of screening from October 2021 to September 2022. Following interim analyses demonstrating high prevalence of Aspergillus in the remotely collected samples, this inclusion criterion was removed to reduce risk for indication bias and better ascertain Af rates in a broader population (supplementary text).

Study design

Study procedures, including informed consent, training, questionnaires and respiratory sample collection were conducted remotely in a decentralised fashion by the University of Pennsylvania. Informed consent was obtained utilising a secure web-based platform and electronic forms on REDcap (Research Electronic Data Capture) hosted by the University of Pennsylvania [17]. Consented participants were provided home sputum collection kits by mail and instructed to self-collect expectorated sputum at home once per week for four occurrences to assess the feasibility of sample collection. The primary outcome was the proportion of participants collecting three or more samples. The secondary outcomes were Af-positive culture and sputum volume.

We virtually trained participants and provided written and video-recorded instructions for unsupervised sputum collection. The samples were shipped overnight in ambient temperature via FedEx from the participant's residence to one of the two reference laboratories, Johns Hopkins University clinical laboratory (Baltimore, MD, USA) or Dartmouth Geisel School of Medicine research laboratory (Hanover, NH, USA). The study team sent text message or email reminders to the participants on a weekly basis to encourage sputum collection by any method to facilitate successful sputum expectoration and sampling. For this pragmatic study, participants were not required to use specific mucoactive medications or airway-clearance devices to provoke sputum expectoration. Reported use of mucoactive medications, including dornase alfa or hypertonic saline, was considered an “induced” home sample in our analysis.

Electronic questionnaires were completed by participants for each remote collection to assess patient-reported respiratory symptoms, any airway-clearance methods to facilitate sputum expectoration, and overall experience with sample collection and shipping (supplementary materials). After the completion of study activities, short semi-structured interviews and post-study questionnaire were administered to participants (supplementary materials). We collected demographic and clinical data, including reported highly effective modulator therapy (HEMT) use, defined by reported use of ETI or ivacaftor, which are noted to have a more robust clinical improvement compared with other CFTR modulators in the CF population [18, 19]. Microbiology history of co-infection with traditional CF pathogens, such as Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus, were obtained. Positive microbiological history was determined by at least one positive culture of the pathogen within 24 months of screening.

Laboratory analysis

The specimens received underwent volume measurement. Sputum consistency was grossly assessed by the laboratory and categorised by an internally developed scale as mucous (purulent), semi-mucous (partially solid), or nonmucous (liquid). Aliquots of neat sputum were equally divided based on total sputum volume received and plated on fungal culture medium (inhibitory mould agar, Sabouraud agar with gentamicin and Czapek-Dox agar, a nitrogen-rich culture medium) [20]. We developed and implemented an a priori study-specific fungal culture protocol aimed to test the hypotheses that culture medium, mucolysis with dithiothreitol (DTT), a hypoxic incubation environment and incubation temperature (30°C versus 37°C) would affect Af growth and culture detection [20–25]. In the Johns Hopkins clinical laboratory, we analysed unprocessed (neat sputum) and sputum undergoing homogenisation with DTT or ultrasonication. Then, we incubated plates in several conditions (30°C, 37°C, 5% CO2, 21% O2, and 1% O2) up to 10 days (details in the supplementary text). In the Dartmouth laboratory, similar conditions were tested, except ultrasonication of the sputa, incubation temperature at 30°C, and ambient CO2 incubation, due to differences in laboratory equipment. We identified Aspergillus at the species complex level. Bacterial cultures were not conducted as part of the protocol.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were calculated at the participant and collection level. We used a Fisher's exact test to explore the relationship between HEMT use and the number of samples collected by participants. A multinomial generalised estimating equations (GEE) model with a time-exchangeable structure was used to explore the relationship between HEMT use and sputum texture. An adjusted Gaussian GEE model was fit to examine the relationship between HEMT and log-transformed sputum volume, and a logistic GEE model was fit to examine the relationship between Aspergillus history and Af positivity. Both models were fit with an exchangeable correlation and were adjusted for age, sex, pancreatic insufficiency, and FEV1 % predicted (ppFEV1); the Af-positivity model was also adjusted for sputum volume. We used unadjusted GEE models to determine the association between sputum induction and log-transformed sputum volume and also investigate the relationship between transport time and Af positivity. Data analyses were performed using R v.4.3.2.

The study was approved by the University of Pennsylvania institutional review board (849570). We previously reported some of these data in abstract form [26].

Results

We screened 91 pwCF; 11 declined to participate and four did not meet entry criteria (supplementary text). We enrolled 76 adults with CF with median (interquartile range (IQR)) age of 36 years (29–46) and median ppFEV1 of 72.5% (53.8–86.3). 53 subjects (69.7%) had at least one copy of F508del and 60 (78.9%) reported taking ETI. Aspergillus culture-positive history was present in 44 (57.9%) individuals (table 1). Of 76 participants, 74 (97%) successfully collected sputa, yielding 287 samples. 70 (92.1%) sent all four samples and none submitted three samples, resulting in 92% successfully collecting and shipping three or more samples (primary end-point). In addition, three (3.9%) participants sent two samples, one (1.3%) sent one sample, and two (2.6%) failed to send sputum (figure 1a). HEMT was not associated with the number of samples collected by participants (p=0.51; supplementary table S1).

Of 281 samples analysed, 123 (43.8%) samples appeared mucous, 78 (27.8%) semi-mucous, and 80 (28.5%) nonmucous. In our multinomial model, HEMT was not significantly associated with semi-mucous or mucous texture compared with nonmucous (OR 1.23, 95% CI 0.48, 3.14; p=0.67 and OR 1.75, 95% CI 0.62, 4.92; p=0.29, respectively). Median (IQR) sputum volume was 2.0 mL (1.0–4.0). However, use of HEMT impacted sputum volume. Median sputum volume was 1.5 mL (0.8–3.5) and 3.0 mL (2.0–5.0) in those on HEMT and not receiving HEMT, respectively (figure 3). Sputum volume from PwCF on HEMT was 36% lower than the sputum volume from those not on HEMT (36%, 95% CI 3–58; p=0.03), adjusting for covariates. For example, a participant not using ivacaftor or ETI with a remote expectorated sputum volume of 3.0 mL would be expected to have a sputum volume of 1.92 mL if they were on HEMT. Median sputum volume of “induced” home specimens was 2.5 mL (1.0–4.8) while the volume of non-induced specimens was 1.8 mL (1.0–3.0) (supplementary figure S1). However, the administration of mucoactive medications (“induced”) was not significantly associated with sputum volume (7%, 95% CI 21–44; p=0.68).

Median sputum volume (mL) is lower in adults with CF receiving highly effective modulator use (HEMT; defined as elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (ETI) or ivacaftor) compared with those not taking HEMT (n=281 sputum collection).

We detected Af in 205 (73%) home sputum samples and 72 (97.3%) individuals using our fungal culture protocol, including 37°C, hypoxia, and 5% CO2 incubation conditions. Sputum transport time was not associated with Af positivity (OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.89–1.44; p=0.31). Af was detected in at least 50% of samples in 64 (86.5%) people (figure 4). Whereas, clinical Aspergillus history was only seen in 44 (57.9%) in the cohort. Aspergillus history was associated with an adjusted OR of 2.87 (95% CI 1.49–5.54; p<0.01) for Af positivity. Sputum samples from individuals with non-fumigatus Aspergillus history also were found to have Af (supplementary table S2). We detected other non-fumigatus Aspergillus species, including Aspergillus niger and Aspergillus nidulans (supplementary table S3).

A post-study questionnaire was completed by 72 participants. Four participants were lost to follow-up and failed to perform the questionnaire and interview. Similar to the real-time sputum collection survey, 25 (32.9%) and 31 (40.8%) participants reported coughing up the sputum was “easy” and “somewhat easy”, respectively, whereas nine (11.8) and seven (9.2%) responded that coughing up the sputum was “somewhat difficult” and “difficult” respectively. A total of 63 (82.9%) participants described following the instructions for home sputum collection to be easy. Common themes included some difficulty with shipping coordination with FedEx (“FedEx pickup was a bit on the confusing side”), descriptions of the study as “straightforward” and easy, and low sputum yield, despite attempts due to improved health (“As someone who does not cough sputum that much, I was still able to produce sputum samples when needed”). All respondents agreed with the statement “home sputum collection is a great method to test my sputum for clinic and research studies” with 65 (85.5%) “agree” and seven (9.2%) “somewhat agree” responses.

Discussion

Our study finds that remote sputum collection in adults with CF in the HEMT era is feasible and favourable. This begins to address a key limitation in clinical care and research by demonstrating the feasibility of repeated sputum collection at home. Nearly all participants self-collected and shipped four requested sputum samples while in stable sinopulmonary health, reflecting the ability to collect at multiple time points. Over two-thirds of sputum collections were considered easy to obtain and sample shipment was perceived as easy in 93% of collections. This may be influenced by the pragmatic approach for home sputum collection and shipment, permitting participants to attempt sputum collection with or without supportive manoeuvres, medications or equipment. Although the effectiveness of ETI has reduced the readiness for spontaneous sputum expectoration in PwCF, these data suggest that home sputum for respiratory sampling and microbe detection is achievable, paving the way for future research to better understand the validity and potential limitations of home-collected specimens transported by mail.

Sputum volume observed in the study was lower than what has been described pre-HEMT, but sufficient for clinical laboratory analysis. HEMT was associated with lower sputum volume, further supporting its role in reducing mucous hypersecretion. Overnight shipping was reliable with 237 samples arriving within one day, suggesting little concern for transport as a limitation for remote sampling. Delayed processing and ambient or room temperature transport of remotely collected CF respiratory samples may have implications for CF pathogen recovery and density. Immediate processing is favoured to enhance the accuracy of microbiological culture performance. If delayed processing of CF respiratory samples is anticipated, the current recommendation is to store in 4°C or refrigeration (CF UK Trust Laboratory Standards for Processing Microbiological Samples from People with Cystic Fibrosis December 2022) [6, 27]. Yet, some studies suggest delayed processing of sputa of people with bronchiectasis may not significantly impact culture-based microbiological yield [28, 29]. Molecular methods for pathogen detection in CF sputa are considered more sensitive than culture and may be more vulnerable to microbial community changes when samples are stored at room temperature [30, 31]. Recently, Hatfield et al. [32] reported CF pathogen detection in mailed CF sputum in ambient storage that underwent delayed processing. This single-centre study mimicked remote sputum sampling by testing 93 paired expectorated sputum samples from adults with CF (none on ETI), by collecting sputum in clinic and aliquoting it for immediate processing and analysis (fresh) or ambient postage by the UK Royal Mail back to the laboratory (posted). They observed 85% concordance for Pseudomonas aeruginosa and 87% for Staphylococcus aureus positivity between fresh and posted samples, despite a median (IQR) of 5 days (2.5–7) of delayed processing of the posted samples. Aspergillus was not examined in this study. These data support the validity of remotely collected sputum in adults with CF not using ETI, but further investigation in the HEMT era is needed.

Despite lower sputum volumes and ambient transport, Af detection in the home sputum samples and prevalence were higher than previously reported Af prevalence [33–35]. The ubiquity of Af in the environment poses a risk for contamination of the sputum samples. It is possible that Af conidia sourced from the environment may have become trapped in the sputum during the collection and transport process and were, therefore, detected on culture. However, over 50% of the cohort had Af detected in 100% of the samples, making repeated contamination less likely. The observed Af prevalence may be influenced by other factors. First, our study population was enriched with those with Af history and, therefore, greater likelihood for Af detection. Our rigorous culture protocol may be more sensitive for Af compared with current clinical fungal culture methods. Our experimental use of semi-selective fungal culture medium, sputum processing and hypoxic incubation conditions may not be employed by clinical laboratories. As Af is thermotolerant and thrives in warmer temperatures, it is possible that the delayed processing and ambient temperature shipping may promote the growth and recovery of Af when conidial burden is low. However, transport time was not associated with Af positivity in the study. Furthermore, microbiological guidelines suggest that fungi can be collected and stored at room temperature for as long as 24 h due to effects of refrigeration on fungal viability [6]. Recent reports based on in-person induced sputum collection suggest reductions in Af density and mould clearance in PwCF on ETI warranting further study of the impact of remote sampling and shipping on Af recovery [36]. As a result of these data, further validation studies to compare Af detection in remotely collected compared with paired clinic-collected sputum samples must be conducted.

We recognise limitations in our study. We are unable to assess the accuracy of Af detection in home sputum without paired in-clinic sampling, which was outside the scope of this decentralised pilot study. Therefore, conclusions related to true Af prevalence in the CF lung cannot be directly drawn from our results. Our protocol used culture-based microbiological testing, which is reported to have lower sensitivity than molecular diagnostics [37, 38].The study communicated weekly reminders to the participants, which likely impacted the successful conduct of unsupervised study activities, including sputum collection and shipment. Therefore, operationalising these methods in a clinical setting may warrant additional efforts. We attempted to represent geographical diversity, but our findings may not be generalisable to all regions. We intentionally did not require ease of expectoration as entry into our study but recognise that an adult cohort may represent a more diseased population and sampling bias for individuals more readily able to expectorate may have occurred. Nonetheless, our findings demonstrate the promise for remote respiratory samples as an infection detection method [39]. Determining the role of self or parent collection of respiratory samples at home is a top priority for future infection research in PwCF [6]. We intend to validate remotely collected sputa with paired in-clinic induced sputum sampling for bacterial and fungal detection in an ongoing observational study (NCT06950892) to determine its potential to serve as a solution for infection surveillance challenges faced by PwCF.
