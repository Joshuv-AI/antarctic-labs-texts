New records of powdery mildew fungi on stone fruits from India
Authors: Sana Surma, Z. A. Bhat, M. A. Bhat, M. D. Shah, Khalid Z. Masoodi, M. Najeeb Mughal, Injla Qadri, Z. A. Dar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Powdery mildew disease is one of the major fungal diseases, affecting most of the crops including stone fruits. Due to their economical significance, the precise identification proof of the causal fungus is fundamental for their effective control. Seventy-five powdery mildew samples were collected across Kashmir valley (J&K, India) from each of the three stone fruits viz., peach (Prunus persica), plum (Prunus domestica) and apricot (Prunus armeniaca) for the taxonomic studies of associated fungi. The powdery mildew fungi were identified based on morphological features and by molecular characterization using ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region. Three species from two genera viz., Podosphaera and Erysiphe of Erysiphacea family were identified. The species identified were P. pannosa on peach, P. ampla on apricot and Erysiphe prunastri on plum. Amongst the identified fungal species, P. ampla on apricot is hitherto unreported from Asia, P. pannosa on peach and E. prunastri on plum from India.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23119-3.

Introduction

The Union Territory (UT) of Jammu and Kashmir (J&K), India offers enormous scope for cultivation of temperate fruit crops. Around six lakh families, consisting of 30 lakh people are directly or indirectly associated with horticulture in J&K1. The area in the UT under fruit production is 327.43 thousand hectares which accounts for 2355.19 thousand metric tonnes of fruit production2. Comparing with international standards the productivity of fruit crops in J&K is still low, attributable to numerous factors of which biotic stresses play an important role. Of the biotic factors, powdery mildews which affect approximately 10,000 species of angiosperms worldwide, constitute a major constraint of most of the fruit crops3–5.

The economic significance of powdery mildews necessitate the need for carrying out the systematic studies on taxonomic aspects of the associated fungi, as precise identification is the key to successful disease management. Powdery mildew fungi belonging to Ascomycetes are pleomorphic, with life cycles typically involving both sexual (teleomorphic) and asexual (anamorphic) states. The fungi were recognized and named by Linnaeus6–8 as early as 1753, when he published Mucor erysiphe for a powdery mildew fungus, now known as Phyllactinia guttata7. Since then, several workers attempted to identify the morphological characters important in taxonomy of powdery mildew fungi4. The taxonomy of powdery mildews has significantly transformed over the years, based on new molecular and morphological approaches. In practice, a rather small number of morphologic features distinguishable with light microscopy are sufficient to delimit powdery mildew genera, however, species level identification involves multitude of morphological traits of both anamorph and teleomorph4,9–11. Morphology of chasmothecial appendages was once considered as an important morphological character to delimit powdery mildew genera; however, the character is now considered a taxonomic trait to delimit species. Besides, anamorphic characteristics such as conidiogenesis, fibrosin bodies, hyphal appressoria, conidial germ tubes, conidial surface structure and parasitism have been found more important for the generic taxonomy of powdery mildews4,9,12– 13. With this backdrop, the three previous sections of the genus Erysiphe, viz., sects. Erysiphe, Golovinomyces and Galeopsidis, were raised to the genus level and the genera Microsphaera and Uncinula were reduced to morphological, non-phylogenetic sections of Erysiphe. Similarly, the genus Sphaerotheca was reduced to synonymy with Podosphaera and treated as morphological, non-phylogenetic Sects4,14–17.

Salmon18 wrote the first monograph of powdery mildews and the second monograph of the Erysiphales by Braun6 was the comprehensive taxonomic study of this significant group of plant pathogenic fungus. Taxonomical study of powdery mildew fungi from temperate conditions of India, which is typically represented by Kashmir region of UT of J&K, has not invited much attention of researchers, although most of the economically important crops and wild/weed hosts are affected by this disease. There are only few documented reports of powdery mildews from Kashmir region that were based on old conventional taxonomical system of morphological characteristics and not supported by molecular data19–24, except one recent report of Phyllactinia pyri- serotinae on pear25 which was based on morpho-molecular approach. Besides, numerous regional monographs and checklists have been published from different parts of the world, however, due to lack of any comprehensive taxonomic studies from Kashmir region, no such information has been documented. Keeping in view the lack of comprehensive studies regarding the diversity and taxonomic studies of powdery mildew species under temperate conditions of India, especially from stone fruits, the present study was therefore, carried out to characterize the powdery mildew species infecting major stone fruit using both morphological and molecular approaches.

Molecular characterization

Sequence analysis of ribosomal DNA (rDNA) region

Each morphologically identified species was further characterized at molecular level using ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer region in order to validate their morphological based identity. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region, using primer pair ITS1 and ITS4 was carried out using DNA of all the test isolates which yielded 500 bp product. The amplified products were sequenced, followed by sequence analysis using software BioEdit. The nucleotide blast analysis (BLASTn) of ITS region based on the sequence similarity of these isolates with that of sequences available at NCBI grouped these isolates into two fungal genera viz., Podosphaera and Erysiphe of family Erysiphacae. Based on the sequence similarity, two species in genus Podosphaera (P. Pannosa and P. ampla) and one in genus Erysiphe (E. prunastri ) were identified. The sequences retrieved were submitted to NCBI under accession numbers PX105193 (P. pannosa on peach), PX105191 (P. ampla on apricot) and PX105155 (E. prunastri on plum).

Phylogenetic analysis

The chromatograms obtained from the forward and reverse primers aligned using BioEdit version 7.0, yielded consensus sequences. These sequences were further compared to type species sequences retrieved from the National Centre for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (http://www.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore) for species-level identification through BLASTn (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST). A comparative analysis was performed on the pathogen sequences in relation to the ex-type sequences of Erysiphe prunastri (AB046983; AB709961), Podosphaera ampla (MK530453; MK530454), and P. pannosa (KY661141; KY661057). This study also incorporated related sequences from P. pannosa, P. ampla, and E. prunastri, all exhibiting high similarity, as well as sequences from various powdery mildew species within the genera Podosphaera and Erysiphe. Additionally, sequences from out group taxa (Venturia inaequalis and Fusarium oxysporum) were sourced from the NCBI database to enhance the analysis. The results were processed using Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis (MEGA) XI, leading to the construction of a dendrogram (phylogenetic tree). This tree effectively categorized the sequences into distinct clades corresponding to two different fungal genera: Podosphaera and Erysiphe, based on their species similarities. The resulting clades comprised Podosphaera pannosa, P. prunicola, P. clandestina, P. leucotricha, P. ampla, P. tridactyla, P. longiseta, and P. fuliginea, alongside Erysiphe necator, E. prunastri, E. arcuata, E. alphitoides, E. astragali, E. juglandis, and E. juglandis-nigrae. Additionally, outgroup taxa represented by sequences from Phyllactiniamali and Phyllactiniapyri-serotineaformed a separate clade (Fig. 7). Based on our findings, the isolate identified as Podosphaera pannosa from peach in Kashmir (Accession No. PX105193) was grouped in a distinct clade alongside other P. pannosa sequences (Accession Nos. KY661057, KY661141, PP335186, KX639822, and OR425008 ), indicating a high level of similarity. Additionally, an isolate of powdery mildew fungus on apricot from Kashmir was identified as Podosphaera ampla (Accession No. PX105191), which clustered in another clade with other P. ampla sequences (Accession Nos. MK530453, MK530454, MW364500, MW36453, and MW364541 MW364521). Finally, Erysiphe prunastri var. prunastri, identified on plum from Kashmir (Accession No. PX105155), formed a separate clade with related Erysiphe prunastri var. prunastri sequences (Accession Nos. AB046983, AB709957, OM530247, AB046984) based on their relatedness ( Fig. 7).

Discussion

Powdery mildew fungi, infecting three major stone fruits viz., peach, plum, and apricot, belonging to genus Prunus of family Rosaceae were first time characterized from temperate region of India, using morpho-molecular approaches. In our study, powdery mildew specimens from stone fruits were characterized into three species from two genera viz., Podosphaera (sect. Sphaerotheca), Podosphaera (sect. Podosphaera) and Erysiphe (sect. Uncinula) of family Erysiphaceae. Five powdery mildew genera (Podosphaera, Erysiphe, Golovinomyces, Leveillula and Phyllactinia) are reported on the Rosaceae4. Of these genera, Podosphaera and Erysiphe possess 672 and 68 hosts from the Rosaceae family, respectively29. The Podosphaera sect. Podosphaera is characterized by non-myeloid, aseptate or septate, thinto thick-walled, hyaline to pigmented appendages, arising equatorially or in the upper half of the chasmothecium with regularly dichotomously branched apices. Podosphaera sect. Sphaerotheca is distinguished from Podosphaera sect. Podosphaera by chasmothecia with unbranched or at most irregularly branched mycelioid appendages4. The chasmothecia appendages in case of Erysiphe sect. Uncinula have circinate-uncinate tips4,30.

Various Podosphaera species such as P. prunicola, P. wuyishamensis, P. longiseta, P. ampla and P. tridactyla, P. pannosa have been reported on host species from genus Prunus4 while, E. prunastri is the only species from Erysiphe (sect. Uncinula) reported on genus Prunus4,31. Of these reported species only P. tridactyla (on P. corunata) is reported on genus Prunus from India, besides Phyllactiania guttata (on Prunus spp)32. Based on the morphological observations and their comparison with authentic descriptions4,27, three species identified from our study were Podosphaera (sect. Sphaerotheca) pannosa on peach, Podosphaera (sect. Podosphaera) ampla on apricot and Erysiphe (sect. Uncinula) prunastri on plum.

In order to validate the morphological based identity, the test fungi were further characterized at molecular level using ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer region. The phylogenetic analysis based on ITS sequences grouped the isolates into distinct clades wherein isolate from peach was identified as P. pannosa, one isolate from plum as Erysiphe prunastri and one isolate from apricot as P. ampla. The molecular identification of powdery mildew fungi infecting major fruit crops under Kashmir condition using ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer region, was therefore in accordance with the identification made through morphological based approach. The taxonomy of powdery mildews based on molecular data began appearing in the 1990s. Complementing morphology with sequencing of rDNA ITS has led to more accurate efficient identification of powdery mildew fungi at species level30,33–35. The internal transcribed spacer (ITS) was proposed as an authentic barcoding region for the identification and phylogenetic analysis of fungi and has been used extensively within the Erysiphales36–41. rDNA ITS region of fungi has been used for identification of species or intraspecific taxa and to reveal phylogenetic relationships among pathogen isolates because of its high variability as it is highly conserved within the species but varies from species to species41–46. 47initiated the phylogenetic study of powdery mildews using 45 taxa from 10 different genera and they compared the morphological characteristics with the evolutionary hypothesis inferred from the analysis of the ITS nucleotide sequences.

Therefore, the final confirmation of identity of fungi causing powdery mildew disease on major stone fruits was based on combined approach of morphological and molecular characterization. The identified fungi were Podosphaera pannosa (Wallr.: Fr.) de Bary on peach, Podosphaera ampla Meeboon, S. Takam. & U. Braun on apricot and Erysiphe prunastri DC on plum. To our knowledge, P. ampla on apricot is the first record from Asia while, E. prunastri on plum and P. pannosa on peach are the first records from India.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Diseased plant parts with typical symptoms of powdery mildew disease were collected from three major stone fruits viz., peach (Prunus persica), plum (Prunus domestica) and apricot (Prunus armeniaca), for taxonomic studies of the associated fungi. Seventy-five powdery mildew samples were collected from each host which included five samples from each of fifteen locations in Srinagar, Ganderbal, and Pulwama districts of the Kashmir valley (J&K, India). The peach samples were collected in the month of July and peach whereas that of plum and apricot in August. Teleomorphic samples were collected late in the season from the same orchard.

The associated causal fungi were morphologically characterised followed by molecular characterization of representative sample from each morphologically identified species.

Powdery mildew incidence was also recorded at each location from where samples were collected. Hundred leaves were randomly selected from each of the three randomly selected trees and observed for recording disease incidence. The per cent disease incidence was calculated by the formula:

Morphological characterization of powdery mildew fungi

The fungi were morphologically characterized with respect to both anamorphic and teliomorphic characteristics, however plum samples for anamorph only as the stage was absent on this host. The characteristics recorded were compared with authentic descriptions4. Fresh material sealed in a plastic bags and stored at 5 °C, were examined within 24 h of collection. To examine the anamorphs; hyphae, conidiophores and conidia of fresh collections were stripped off from the leaf surfaces with clear adhesive tape, mounted on a microscopic glass slide in distilled water with the fungal mycelium uppermost and examined under microscope (Olympus CX 41). Anamorph characteristics with respect to morphology of mycelium (hyphal cell dimensions, diameter and shape of appressoria), conidiophores (size, number of cells, position of conidiophore on mother cell, number of following cells, number of conidium initials, arrangement of conidia on conidiophore and size and shape of foot cell), conidia (size, length/width ratio, shape, presence or absence of fibrosin bodies), germ tube (length, position and tips of germ tube) were examined for the identification of powdery mildew fungal species. Standard protocol4 was followed for recording observations related to fibrosin bodies and conidial germination. For observations of teleomorphic structures, chasmothecia were stripped off from the leaf surface with a sterile needle, mounted on 3.0 per cent sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution and observed under microscope. Teleomorph characters studied for the identification of powdery mildew species were morphology of chasmothecia (shape and size), morphology of chasmothecial appendages (shape, size, colour and septation and shape of appendage apices), morphology of ascus (size, shape and number of ascus/asci per chasmothecia) and morphology of ascospores (shape, size, septation and number of ascospores/ascus).

Molecular characterization

The morphological based identification was confirmed at molecular level using ribosomal DNA internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region.

DNA extraction

Whole-cell DNA 0f the test isolates was isolated from mycelium/chasmothecia by extraction buffer method47. The 400µL extraction buffer was added (200 Mm TrisHCl pH 8.5, 250mM NaCl, 25mM EDTA, 0.5% SDS) to the mycelium or chasmothecia crushed with a conical grinder (Roth), fitting exactly the tube and homogenized by hand or electric potter at 200 rpm for 1 min. Four microliters RNase (20 mg/mL) were added to the cell suspension and incubated for 10 min at 65 °C with intermittent mixing. Further, 130 µL of 3 M sodium acetate, pH 5.2 was added and incubated at -20 °C for 10 min. The lysate was then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4 °C for 15 min and the supernatant was transferred to a clean 1.2 ml deepwell plate-96. DNA was precipitated by the addition of 650-µl room temperature isopropanol, pelleted at 4, 000 rpm for 10 min and washed with ice-cold 70% (v/v) ethanol to remove residual contaminants. Further, DNA was re-suspended in 100 µL of 1XTris-EDTA buffer. The quantity and quality of the extracted DNA were assessed using Nano-drop (Eppendorf, Bio-spectrometer) and 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and dilutions were made by TrisEDTA buffer to maintain a concentration of 20–25 ng/µl for further PCR amplification.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification

Amplification using PCR thermocyclerfor taxonomic identification and phylogenetic analysisof Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region was performed in 0.2 ml PCR tubes in a T-Gradient thermal cycler (WhatmanBiometra, T-Gradient, Goettingen, Germany) using 2 µL of genomic DNA of each isolate in a final volume of 50 µl per reaction mixture containing PCR buffer (10X), Mgcl2 (25mM), DNTP’s (25mM), Forward and Reverse primers (10 pmol each), Taq Polymerase (5U/µl) respectively. The ITS region (5.8S) of rDNA was amplified using ITS1 (5’TCCGTAGGTGAACCTGCG3’) and ITS4 (5’TCCTCCGCTTATTTGATATGC3’) primers and the reaction mixture in PCR tubes was given short spin or vortexed in microcentrifuge (Thermo Scientific, Thermo Electron Corporation).The PCR product was processed under various thermal cycling conditions for initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min followed by 35 cycles at 94 °C for 1 min, at annealing temperature 56℃ for 1 min, 72 °C for 2 min and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The amplified product was resolved on a 1.2% agarose gel in 0.5X TAE buffer, added with Ethidium bromide at concentration of 0.5 µg/µl, and photographed using the Alfa Imager gel documentation system (Alfa Imager EC, Protein Simple, USA).

DNA sequencing

After PCR amplification using ITS primers, 5 µl of PCR product of test isolates was electrophoresed to ensure successful amplification and 20 µl of sample were sent for custom sequencing (BioKart labs, Bangalore, India). The sequences for each forward and reverse primer were retrieved from chromatograms received from BioKart labs Bangalore, India. The sequence alignment was done for forward and reverse sequences in BioEdit version 7.0 to obtain consensus sequences for each isolate and analysed through BLASTn programme (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/BLAST) to compare the present sequences with the available sequences in database of National Centre for Biotechnological Information (NCBI) (http://www.nlm.nih.gov/nuccore) for identification at species level.

Phylogenetic analysis

The consensus sequence for each test isolate was compared with ex-type and closely related sequences exhibiting high similarity from powdery mildew pathogen species belonging to the genera Podosphaera and Erysiphe. This comparison also included sequences of out group (Venturia inaequalis and Fusarium oxysporum ) obtained from NCBI database. The alignment and assembly were carried out using CLUSTAL W within the MEGA software version XI for molecular evolutionary analysis48. A phylogenetic tree was generated using the maximum likelihood (ML) method, employing the Kimura-2-parameter substitution model to ensure the best fit for the data. Additionally, to assess the reliability of phylogenetic tree, the confidence level of each branch was evaluated using 1000 bootstrap replications49. The phylogenetic relationships among the isolates were validated by their grouping into distinct clades. The sequences obtained were submitted to GenBank National Centre for Biotechnological Information (NCBI) for accession numbers.
