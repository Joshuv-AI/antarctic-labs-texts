Design, fabrication, and optical imaging performance comparison of precision microlens arrays for application in plenoptic microscopy systems
Authors: Chihoon Kim, Munseob Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on analyzing the characteristics of a microlens array produced by 3D diffusion lithography and the characteristics of the optical system based on the arrangement of the lenses. The microlens array, with an 85 μm pitch, 2.1 mm focal length, and 1 μm sag, was designed to match the numerical aperture of a 10×, 0.25 NA objective lens. Photoresist, aluminum, and photoresist layers were deposited onto a silicon substrate. After patterning with a photomask, the aluminum layer was etched to form a metal mask for subsequent diffusion lithography. The use of a metal mask improved the uniformity of the mold of the microlens array. A microlens array was replicated using polydimethylsiloxane, and the characteristics of this lens were very close to the design values, with an average pitch of 86.02 μm, sag of 1.078 μm, and substrate thickness of 1.067 mm. In addition, the Strehl ratio had a median value of 0.975, indicating excellent performance. The microlens array was integrated into two configurations: plenoptic camera module 1, in which the microlens array was directly attached to the sensor, and plenoptic camera module 2, in which it was mounted separately. A comparative analysis of the spatial resolution and depth of field of both modules revealed that both configurations achieved the same spatial resolution of 7.8 μm. However, plenoptic camera module 2 provided a wider depth of field than plenoptic camera module 1, allowing for clear imaging over a broader depth range.Subject terms: Engineering, Optics and photonics, Physics

Introduction

The plenoptic function is a 7-dimensional (7D) representation of the optical field that includes information about all dimensions of light rays, encompassing the volumetric, spectral, and temporal aspects of the input scene. In contrast, conventional image sensors measure only the 2-dimensional plenoptic function, limiting the amount of information that can be captured and resulting in suboptimal optical measurements. The theoretical definition of plenoptics implies the creation of a visually undistorted, fully immersive, and realistic 3D image by implementing the intensity and directional information of light reflected from objects1–4.

There are two main methods for acquiring plenoptic images: using multiple cameras and employing a microlens array (MLA) close to the sensor of the camera. Multicamera array imaging is a technique for obtaining high-resolution images using plenoptic imaging technology that uses multiple cameras to capture different viewpoints and computationally combines overlapping fields of view. However, this method is costly, poses synchronization challenges in dynamic scenes, is limited by spacing constraints owing to the bulk of the camera, and presents significant challenges in camera alignment. In contrast, the MLA method involves placing the MLA in front of a single camera sensor to separate and store the intensity information of the light rays passing through the main lens in multiple directions. This approach is cost-effective and resolves issues related to camera arrangement, signal gain, calibration, and synchronization5–8.

As critical components for acquiring plenoptic images, MLA has excellent optical characteristics with low aberration and minimal distortion, offering a high spatial/angular resolution and depth of field (DOF) depending on the optical design and configuration. Microlenses can be fabricated using direct and indirect methods. Among the direct methods, thermal reflow has been the dominant technique for decades, yielding very smooth surfaces, although challenges remain in controlling the contact angle and geometry, which are affected by material wettability and ambient air. Microplastic embossing is a cost-efficient, single-step method for producing plastic microlenses that is less influenced by mold surface quality9–11. Inkjet printing involves dispensing UV-polymerizable liquid droplets from a nozzle onto a substrate, which then solidify into smooth shapes upon UV exposure. This method is suitable for producing large-area MLAs at room temperature, although controlling the microlens consistency and shape size is difficult, and additional surface treatments may be required for larger-aperture microlenses, thereby increasing the process complexity12. Indirect methods, such as the microelectromechanical system (MEMS)-based approach, utilize photolithography and chemical reactions to etch the curvature of microlenses into substrates, thereby achieving smooth and uniform lens shapes. In this study, an indirect 3D diffusion lithography method was employed for MLA fabrication. This technique leverages optical diffusers in traditional photolithography to form diverse and complex 3D structures. The uniformity of the light distribution ensures consistent 3D structure formation and precise curvature control, making it a highly effective method for microlens production13,14.

This study was conducted using 3D diffusion lithography to create molds, followed by replication using flexible polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). The morphological and surface characteristics and optical properties of the fabricated microlenses were examined. Furthermore, the performances of optical systems based on MLA placement were compared to develop a plenoptic microscopic imaging system. Two configurations were considered: Plenoptic Camera Module 1 (PCM 1), in which the MLA was attached directly to the sensor, and Plenoptic Camera Module 2 (PCM 2), in which the MLA was mounted separately. The optical characteristics of the plenoptic microscope system, including the spatial resolution and DOF, were analyzed based on these configurations.

Plenoptic microscope system setup

We constructed a plenoptic microscope, as illustrated in Fig. 1. This setup utilized a 10×, 0.25 NA objective lens (Olympus Plan Achromatic) and a wide-field tube lens (Olympus, magnification: x1, FL: 180 mm). To facilitate the rotational adjustment of the camera module and seamless interchange with other modules, an extension mount (20 mm) was positioned between the beam splitter and tube lens, enhancing the degree of freedom of the system. Additionally, a beam splitter (nonpolarizing, 50:50, Thorlabs) was connected to a coaxial white-light source (3000 K, 640mW output power). This illumination was optimized for uniformity and efficiency using an aspheric condenser lens (FL: 8 mm, Thorlabs) and plano-convex lens (FL: 59.8 mm, Thorlabs).

(a) illustrates the configuration of the optical system for the plenoptic microscope, whereas (b) presents a three-dimensional assembly diagram.

The MLA was placed in two configurations: directly attached to the sensor plane of a high-speed camera (91.3fps, 25MP, VIEWORKS) and attached to a quartz plate mounted on an adjustable mount, allowing distance adjustments relative to the sensor. In each configuration, the light rays passing through the main lens were focused onto the MLA. The microlenses then directed the rays to different pixels on the image sensor, which was influenced by the incident angle of the main lens.

Microlens array fabrication and optical characterization

The fabrication process of the MLA is illustrated in Fig. 2. To support the demands of advanced semiconductor packaging—such as wafer-level chip scale packaging (WLCSP) and 2.5D/3D integration—a microlens array (MLA)–based plenoptic imaging system was developed to enable high-resolution and depth-aware inspection of fine-pitch features including micro-bumps15,16. Theoretical parameters were first calculated to achieve a horizontal resolution of 8.5 μm using a 10×, 0.25 NA objective lens, leading to the design of an MLA with a pitch of 85 μm, focal length of 2.1 mm, and sag height of 1 μm. These values ensure optimal numerical apertures (NA) matching between the objective lens (NA = 0.25) and MLA (NA = 0.020), a critical requirement for effective plenoptic imaging. To fabricate the MLA, a hexagonal mask layout was designed, and replication processes were established to maintain a pitch tolerance of ± 0.5 μm. Careful control during fabrication helped minimize deviations and ensured high surface quality. With a 2.5 μm pixel size, each microlens spans approximately 34 pixels, providing sufficient sampling for sub-image capture. The chosen focal length and geometry support plenoptic refocusing while maintaining mechanical compatibility with high-speed cameras and microscope objectives. The sag height was computed using geometric lens formulas and optimized with an index of refraction of 1.43 ensuring reproducible, high-quality lens profiles. The optical configuration yields a lateral resolution of 8.5 μm and axial resolution of 46 μm, suitable for inspecting BEOL-level features. At a wavelength of 633 nm, the calculated Airy disk size is 19.08 μm (7.63 pixels), indicating diffraction-limited performance and compliance with Nyquist sampling. The effective usable pixels per microlens (26.3) enable accurate image reconstruction and robust defect detection.

To fabricate the mold for the MLA, a thick photoresist layer, a metal thin film, and a thin photoresist layer were sequentially coated and deposited on a silicon substrate (a). The mask was then patterned using a photolithographic process (b). The patterned thin upper photoresist layer was etched and used as an etching mask to etch the intermediate metal thin film (c). Using the 3D diffusion lithography method, the thick bottom photoresist layer was exposed (d), followed by etching of the thin upper photoresist layer (e) and metal mask (f). A thick bottom photoresist layer was developed (g). PDMS was coated onto the fabricated mold (h) and thermally cured to produce the MLA (i).

The bottom photoresist chosen was AZ 40XT (EMD Performance Materials Corp.), which allowed the fabrication of thick structures exceeding 100 μm with excellent resolution. This photoresist was spin-coated to a thickness of 70 μm after the HMDS treatment and soft-baked on a hotplate at 90 °C for 7 min. Next, a 150 nm aluminum thin film was thermally deposited as the metal mask using a thermal evaporator, maintaining the process temperature below 50 °C to prevent the thermal reflow of the lower thick photoresist. For the etching mask of the middle aluminum layer, an S1800 (MICROPOSIT) positive photoresist was selected and spin-coated to a thickness of 1.5 μm without HMDS treatment. Soft baking was performed on a hotplate at 95 °C for 90 s to prevent the reflow of the lower photoresist under standard soft-baking conditions.

The upper photoresist was then exposed to UV light (80 mJ/cm²) and developed, followed by its removal from a heated NMP (N-Methyl-2-pyrrolidone) solution at 70 °C. The Al thin film was etched for 90 s using an etchant (H3PO4: HNO3: CH3COOH: H2O = 16:1:1:2) under stirring. An insufficient etching time can leave aluminum residues that cause light scattering during the diffusion lithography process and negatively affect the MLA surface quality. Conversely, excessive etching can result in lateral etching, which expands the dimensions of the original photomask pattern.

A metal mask was used to fabricate the MLA via 3D diffusion lithography to achieve a sag of 1 μm. Achieving a high fill factor is crucial for MLA fabrication. Therefore, the pattern was designed to overlap the exposures to ensure that no unexposed areas remained between adjacent microlenses, resulting in a smooth pattern with a high fill factor. The sag height was controlled by adjusting the diffuser angle (20° Full Width at Half Maximum), UV exposure dose (90–100 mJ/cm2). Accordingly, before fabricating the MLA mold via 3D diffusion lithography, the thin upper photoresist and aluminum metal patterns were removed by immersion in NMP and an aluminum etchant, respectively. After removing the metal mask, a thick bottom photoresist was developed using diluted AZ 300 MIF for 70 s (AZ Electronic Materials Ltd.). The complete development of adjacent exposed areas effectively removed the upper unexposed regions across the entire area. This process ensured the formation of a uniform concave mold for MLA fabrication, indicating the suitability of this process for producing high-quality MLA17.

To precisely transfer the photoresist mold shape to the PDMS, a self-assembled monolayer (SAM) was applied to the mold surface to prevent adhesion problems during demolding. Octadecyltrichlorosilane (OTS) dissolved in toluene at a concentration of 0.5% was used as the SAM material. After SAM coating, PDMS was spin-coated at 600 rpm for 30 s and cured at 85 °C for 1 h18. The cured PDMS was easily separated from the mold owing to the anti-adhesive effect of the coating. The fabricated MLA, which was produced via 3D diffusion lithography based on the design parameters, was measured for pitch, sag, and substrate thickness. The results of these measurements are presented in the table below.

(a) 3D profile of the MLA with measurement points P1–P3. (b) Line profile analysis showing average pitch of 86.02 µm, consistent with the design value. (c) Summary table of measured pitch values. (d) 3D profile highlighting surface roughness measurement areas 1–3. (e) Surface profiles of Areas 1–3. (f) Surface roughness results (Sa = 32 nm; Sz = 0.485 µm), demonstrating a smooth surface quality suitable for high-performance MLA fabrication.

These results indicate that the MLA fabrication process was optimized, demonstrating the potential for uniform large-area MLA production and a reduction in the standard deviation of the sag by up to six times compared with traditional diffusion lithography methods. Consequently, the measured characteristics of the fabricated MLA, as shown in Table 1, were a pitch of 86.02 μm (design value: 85 μm), sag of 1.078 μm (design value: 1 μm), and substrate thickness of 1.067 mm (design value: 1 mm). These measurements indicate a high degree of accuracy in both the design and the process. A laser microscope (VK-X1000; Keyence) was used for the measurements.

MLA module assembly and alignment

The fabrication of the MLA requires the design and production of modules that can be either directly attached to the sensor plane or mounted for flexible positioning. The module includes a linear stage to adjust the focal length of the main lens, thereby enabling precise focus control. This linear stage was integrated with a microlens mount, allowing the position of the microlens to be adjusted closer to or farther from the image sensor. This design accommodates the varying focal distances required for different microscopic observation conditions.

The primary components of the microlens-mount module are as follows: first, the linear stage, which is composed of a high-precision screw-and-rail system, allows for fine adjustments of the lens position. Second, the microlens mount was designed with a stable structure to hold the lens securely and included a sliding mechanism to freely adjust the distance to the sensor. Third, the entire module was built with a robust metal frame, which enhanced the system durability and minimized focus loss owing to vibrations.

This module offers several advantages over conventional plenoptic microscopic systems19. This enhances flexibility by allowing easy adjustment of the focal distance to suit various observation conditions. The precise positioning capability ensures optimal focus and depth settings for high-resolution imaging. Additionally, its compact design facilitates integration into existing microscopy systems without compromising performance. However, this module requires correction for angular misalignments. Therefore, the kinematic mount was adjusted to align the MLA horizontally. An autocollimator (SURUGA H400, ± 0.2°) was used for this purpose, as shown in Fig. 4d. The module with the attached MLA was mounted parallel to the autocollimator. The X- and Y-axes of the module were tilted such that the reflected laser was centered on the adjustment screen, as shown in Fig. 4f. This alignment process was repeated whenever a mechanical movement occurred in the plenoptic camera module.

Results and discussion

MLA fabrication accuracy and optical properties

In conventional optical microscopy, the ability to identify objects varies with magnification, whichrequires precisely crafted and expensive lenses for superior resolution. Similarly, to achieve high performance in plenoptic microscopy, the system must efficiently collect information on the intensity and angle of the light reflected from the object to accurately extract detailed 3D image information. In this experiment, two configurations were fabricated: PCM 1, in which the MLA was directly attached to the sensor, and PCM 2, in which the MLA was mounted separately.

For PCM 1, the direct attachment of the MLA to the sensor resulted in a shorter optical path between the lens and sensor, reducing the alignment errors. Consequently, optical distortion was minimized, covering more pixels and enabling the acquisition of higher-resolution images. Additionally, owing to the low refractive index and short focal length of PDMS, it is expected that DOF information can be obtained more accurately without the need for distance adjustment between the lens and sensor.

In contrast, PCM 2 allows an adjustable distance between the sensor and MLA, enabling the capture of rays from various angles and distances, which facilitates flexible DOF acquisition. Moreover, with the MLA mounted separately, the assembly and maintenance are simplified, and the lens position can be adjusted to suit different experimental conditions, thereby enhancing the ease of assembly and maintenance.

The Strehl ratio ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating superior optical system performance. In this study, the light detected at the focal point of the fabricated lens was measured by positioning the sensor, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. Based on these measurements, 30 microlenses were arbitrarily selected (indicated by yellow boxes). The Strehl ratios for these selected microlenses were measured, and the results shown in Fig. 5d indicate values close to 1. Typically, a Strehl ratio above 0.8 is considered indicative of excellent performance, and high-quality optical systems generally use components with Strehl ratios exceeding this threshold21.

Figure 5e shows a box plot in which the median serves as a robust measure of the central tendency, reflecting the general performance of the microlenses. The median value was 0.975, suggesting that most of the microlenses focused light effectively, indicating high performance. The edges of the box represent values of approximately 0.970 and 0.980, respectively, capturing the middle 50% of the data and indicating moderate variability in the Strehl ratios among the microlenses. This narrow range demonstrates a relatively consistent performance across most MLA. The high median value and narrow edge range suggest an excellent overall performance and consistency among the microlenses. Furthermore, the results indicate that the MLA fabrication and alignment processes do not require improvement because uniform performance has already been ensured.

Plenoptic microscopes capture both spatial and angular light information simultaneously, requiring the conversion of RAW images into 4D radiance arrays L(u, v,s, t), where (s, t) represents microlens centers and (u, v) represents angular information. After appropriate preprocessing steps, including microimage center identification and sensor-microlens alignment correction, this approach enables computational refocusing, a key advantage that allows sharp image reconstruction at various focal planes without physical focus adjustment. The refocusing parameter α controls the focal plane position: when α > 1, the focal plane moves toward the camera, and when α < 1, it moves away from the camera. This computational method enhances experimental flexibility by generating multidimensional images without complex optical devices. However, a trade-off exists between the DOF and refocusing capability: increased DOF expands the refocusing range but may reduce the depth resolution owing to limited angular sampling, necessitating careful optical design optimization22.

Based on the excellent performance of the microlenses, the spatial resolution and DOF of the plenoptic microscopy system were evaluated. To evaluate the spatial resolution, the USAF 1951 target (Thorlabs) was used, following the same assessment method used for conventional 2D images. For the DOF evaluation, a 45° tilted target (DOF 5–15 Depth of Field Target, Edmund Optics) was used. Coaxial and ring-type illuminations were applied to the microscopy system to measure each sample23–26.

For the analysis of the imaging system, targets containing various spatial frequencies were imaged, and the contrast transfer capability at each frequency was evaluated. This approach provides an objective assessment of the resolution and frequency response characteristics of the system.

This performance difference can be attributed to the fundamental characteristics of each illumination type: coaxial lighting provides uniform illumination with minimal reflection, which is ideal for detecting small defects, whereas ring lighting enhances edge visibility but may introduce glare. The adjustable MLA-sensor distance in PCM 2 enables precise optical path control, optimizes the uniformity of the coaxial illumination, and minimizes light distortion, thereby achieving a higher contrast.

A comparison of the spatial resolution and DOF analysis results of PCM 1 and 2 revealed several key differences. In PCM 1, ring illumination provided a higher contrast than coaxial illumination, resulting in a better spatial resolution. This suggests that ring illumination offers superior sidelighting, which enhances the visibility of fine details. Conversely, in PCM 2, the coaxial illumination provided a higher contrast than the ring illumination, leading to better spatial resolution. This indicates that coaxial illumination in PCM 2 offers more consistent lighting and improves image sharpness.

The DOF analysis results showed that PCM 1 had a relatively low DOF in both the 5 LP and 15 LP regions, with the number 5 appearing incomplete and only the middle-scale bar mark visible. This implies that the DOF in PCM 1 is limited, necessitating further adjustments to improve its performance. In contrast, PCM 2 exhibited a relatively high DOF in both the 5 LP and 15 LP regions, with the number 5 appearing complete and the entire scale bar being visible. This indicates that PCM 2 provided a greater DOF, allowing for clear images over a broader range of depths. Additionally, the high-frequency pattern (15 LP) contains smaller details, which can narrow the DOF owing to the relationship between the spatial frequency and DOF27. In contrast, the low-frequency pattern (5 LP) included larger details, resulting in a wider DOF. These factors contributed to the observed differences in the DOF values measured in the 5 LP and 15 LP regions.

Consequently, in PCM1, the MLA is directly attached to the sensor, resulting in a very short physical distance that makes it difficult to focus on various depths. It can only accept a narrow light angle, ultimately limiting the DOF. Owing to limitations in illumination and optical path, high spatial resolution can be achieved at specific shallow focal planes; however, image clarity cannot be maintained at various depths. For high-frequency spatial details (e.g., the 15 LP region), clear images can only be captured in the central portion, and the lack of focus adjustment flexibility narrows the DOF. Moreover, the depth is influenced by the spatial frequency of the observed pattern and the DOF formula, making it difficult to maintain a wide DOF at high frequencies because the MLA-sensor distance is fixed. In contrast, the PCM2 allows for the adjustment of the distance between the MLA and the sensor, enabling the capture of various depths and improving the DOF. By optimizing the optical path to accommodate a wider angle of light and allowing for customized alignment according to the depth characteristics of the scene and numerical aperture, clear images can be obtained over a wider depth range.

Conclusion

In this study, we fabricated a microlens array (MLA) using 3D diffusion lithography for application in plenoptic microscopy. The fabricated MLA, with an 85 μm pitch, 2.1 mm focal length, and 1 μm sag, closely matched the design specifications, demonstrating high accuracy and precision in the manufacturing process. The Strehl ratio analysis showed a median value of 0.975, which indicated excellent optical performance.

In this study, we fabricated a microlens array (MLA) using 3D diffusion lithography for application in plenoptic microscopy. The fabricated MLA, with an 85 μm pitch, 2.1 mm focal length, and 1 μm sag, closely matched the design specifications, demonstrating high accuracy and precision in the manufacturing process. The Strehl ratio analysis showed a median value of 0.975, which indicated excellent optical performance.

We developed two configurations for integrating the MLA into the plenoptic microscopy system: PCM 1, with the MLA directly attached to the sensor, and PCM 2, with the MLA mounted on an adjustable platform. Comparative analysis revealed that both PCM 1 and PCM 2 provided similar spatial resolution (7.8 μm), whereas PCM 2 offered a significantly wider DOF. Configuration 2, in particular, demonstrated superior performance in capturing sharp images across a broader depth range, making it more versatile for various microscopic applications.

These findings highlight the potential of 3D diffusion lithography for producing high-quality MLAs and its applicability in enhancing plenoptic microscopy. The ability to fine-tune the optical characteristics and modular flexibility of the system enabled the development of a robust platform for advanced imaging applications. Future research will focus on further optimizing the configurations and incorporating additional techniques to enhance the spatial resolution and DOF.
