Key risk factors for pulmonary vs. extrapulmonary tuberculosis: a comparative study in Southwest Ethiopia

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, with more than 10.7 million new cases and 1.23 million deaths per year (131 per 100,000). The incidence and death rates have declined by 12% and 29%, respectively in recent report. Over the last decade, TB decline was uneven, greater in Europe than Africa. About 78% of cases were newly diagnosed and 54% were using rapid methods [1], [2]. Globally, pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB) accounts for nearly 75%, and extrapulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB) for 15% of all TB cases, with EPTB rising, particularly in high HIV prevalent areas. [3], [4]It still ranks among the leading infectious causes of morbidity and mortality globally, with numerous factors contributing to its transmission and progression [5]. Among these factors, environmental factors such as overcrowding, poor housing, and low socio-economic status play a key role in the spread of the disease [6], [7], [8]. Vulnerable groups, particularly children under five and older adults, are at greater risk of TB progression [9]. Additionally, individuals with HIV are at an elevated risk, with HIV-positive people being 18 times more likely to develop active TB. HIV co-infection has also been strongly associated with elevated TB mortality and the emergence of multidrug-resistant TB [1], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]. Malnutrition, which compromises immunity, is another crucial factor, with nearly half of TB patients globally being malnourished [17], [18]. Co-infection with diseases like diabetes further complicates TB treatment and accelerates its progression [19], [20], [21], [22], [23], [24].

More than 95% of TB cases and deaths occur in developing countries, with Sub-Saharan Africa disproportionately affected due to the overlapping HIV and TB epidemics [9], [14], [25]. These regions face significant socio-economic challenges, including malnutrition and overcrowded living conditions, which amplify its transmission and progression [26]. Furthermore, limited resources for TB diagnosis, treatment, and prevention hinder effective control efforts and contribute to higher incidence and mortality rates. WHO reported that 5.3 million people received TB preventive therapy(58% people living with HIV,25% close contacts).The overall treatment success rates were 88% and 71% for Drug resistant TB,with 42% coverage. [1], [7]Effective control of TB in these regions requires public health strategies, including the strengthening of healthcare infrastructure and increased public awareness about TB [7]. [9], [28], [29], [30]Additionally, overcrowding in urban slums and rural areas amplifies the transmission risk, and also delayed diagnoses and limited healthcare access hinder effective disease management [31]. Barriers such as a lack of awareness and financial constraints contribute to delays in diagnosis and treatment, particularly in rural and marginalized communities. About 25% of TB-affected households experience catastrophic cost [1], [32].

PTB is highly infectious, with a single untreated case capable of transmitting the disease to 5–15 people annually. [3], [14], [33] Smoking and alcohol use, as lifestyle factors, significantly heighten the risk of contracting TB.Alcohol use disorder raises the risk by a factor of 3.3, and smoking elevates it by 1.6. More specifically, relevant risk factors such as housing conditions, unhealthy behaviors and practices, level of awareness, HIV/AIDS, and comorbidities were thoroughly examined, thereby effectively addressing significant gaps.

Methods and materials

Design and study areas

A cross-sectional study was conducted to assess risk factors for active TB among outpatients in three administrative zones in southwestern Ethiopia: Bench-Sheko, Kaffa, and Sheka, located at varying distances from Addis Ababa: Bench-Sheko (561 km), Kaffa (452.5 km), and Sheka (596.8 km).The areas have diverse demographic, geographic, and cultural characteristics, with urbanization levels ranging from 7.4% in Kaffa to 17.2% in Sheka, and a balanced gender distribution (49.4%–50.7% male).The region's varied landscapes, including lowlands, midlands, and highlands, may influence healthcare access and TB exposure risk. Ethiopia's broader cultural mosaic is reflected in the area's religious and linguistic diversity. The region's varied landscapes and cultural diversity provide a relevant context for studying TB risk factors and healthcare access (Fig. 1).

Study population and sampling techniques

A multistage sampling technique was used to select three zones in southwestern Ethiopia—Keffa, Bench-Sheko, and Masha—based on TB prevalence. Districts with higher TB rates and health facilities reporting more cases were prioritized. The study focused on patients diagnosed with active TB through smear microscopy, GeneXpert MTB/RIF, or clinical symptoms. Consecutive sampling was applied, and exclusion criteria included patients not diagnosed at MTU TB laboratory, temporary residents, severely ill patients, and those unable to provide sputum samples. ICU patients were excluded to ensure protection of vulnerable populations and ethical standards fully applied, prioritizing patient safety. Generally, those individuals who were unable to provide a sputum sample or respond to data collection tools were also excluded, as invasive procedures such as tracheobronchial lavage were not applicable in most settings, and data quality could be compromised.

Data collection process

Interview method

Data collection was conducted between December 2018 and June 2020, using standardized instruments to gather environmental, behavioral, clinical, and test data. Clinical conditions, primarily clinical features, coinfection (simultaneous infection with more than one infectious agent; HIV + TB), comorbidities (presence of one or more additional diseases alongside a primary disease; DM in TB patient), and previous history of infections were assessed and recorded. All patients with positive results for both PTB and EPTB were thoroughly interviewed. Trained healthcare providers carried out interviews, and refining the instruments were made based on their feedback. Data quality was ensured through observation, structured interviews, and medical record reviews, all adhering to strict protocols for consistency.

Routine diagnostic methods

Sputum smear microscopy (AFB), GeneXpert/NAAT, culture (LJ/MGIT), and chest imaging (CXR) were used to identify all those TB cases. Both lung parenchymal disease (PTB) and TB involving organs outside the lungs such as lymph nodes, pleura, CNS, and bones (EPTB) were diagnosed similarly. However, FNAC or biopsy with histopathology showing granulomas and caseous necrosis, AFB smear, GeneXpert/NAAT on tissue or fluid samples, and mycobacterial culture were performed, and a panel microscope was used for examination of stained smears and cytological/histopathological specimens. Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) was used for extrapulmonary TB cases to collect tissue samples from lymph nodes, pleura, or other affected areas. GeneXpert Ultra were used to detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis and assess rifampicin resistance (rpoB gene mutations) at the study sites.

Study variables

An analysis examined the associations between clinical, environmental, awareness, and behavioral factors and active TB. Severity correlated with clinical conditions, HHC, poor housing, unhealthy behaviors, and certain demographics, though no simultaneous link was found between PTB and EPTB. These relationships inform targeted risk assessments and interventions for improved TB control.

Statistical analyses

Data were entered into EpiData 3.1 and thoroughly analyzed using Stata windows version 14 or 16. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize those factors, and visual representations were presented through charts and tables. Continuous variables were analyzed with means, standard deviations, and normality tests, and categorical variables were assessed using frequency distributions. Associations with active TB were examined via Chi-square or Fisher's exact tests. A standardized odds ratio, defined as an odds ratio adjusted for covariates or potential confounding variables between groups to provide a more accurate and comparable estimate of the association between an exposure and an outcome, was estimated in this study. Spost13 package was installed into Stata and multiple logistic regression analyses was performed to estimate those standardized odd ratios (commands: listcoef and esttab), with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the ALIHR IREC and zonal health authorities. Informed consent was secured from adults, with parental assent for children under five. Confidentiality, non-invasive sampling, and strict data security were ensured.

Results

Study characteristics

The study included 410 active TB patients, with males comprising 55%. Ages ranged from 1 to 73 years (mean 27.65 ± 11.44). Newly diagnosed cases accounted for 99.27%, with treatment failure and relapse each at 0.24%. PTB was present in 80% (328), EPTB in 19.3% (79), and both in 0.73% (3). Most households used thatched roofing 73.4% (301), followed by corrugated roofing 22.7% (93) and concrete roofing 3.9% (16). Many lacked windows 62.2% (255), and most had no separate bedrooms 68.8% (282). Most individuals did not consume raw milk 58.5% (240), and a significant proportion cohabited with animals 29.5% (121). Animal stables were present in some households 28.3% (116). Awareness and behavioral factors

The study found that 93.9% (385) of participants did not cigarette smoke, and only 6.1% (25) smoked. In households, 90.7% (372) had no smokers, and 9.3% (38) had at least one. Substance abuse risk was observed in 19.0% (78) of participants, while 80.7% (331) were not at risk. Alcohol use was reported by 23.4% (96), with 76.6% (314) abstaining. Fig. 2 was used to visually illustrated with these findings. Among 410 respondents, 72% (295) had no contact with an active TB case in their family, and 28.1% (115) had contact. Data on awareness showed that 75.9% (311) of respondents were unaware of how TB is transmitted, and 24.2% (99) knew. Similarly, 75.6% (310) were unaware of how to prevent TB, and 24.4% (100) knew how to prevent it (Fig. 3).

Comorbidities and previous history

The data showed that 83.7% (343 cases) were not tested for diabetes, with 83.9% (344 cases) having an unknown diabetes status. Among those tested, 14.6% (60 cases) were identified as non-diabetic, and only 1.46% (6 cases) were confirmed to have diabetes. A total of 94.88% (389) of the 410 patients were tested for HIV. Among those tested, 85.85% (352) were HIV-negative, 9.51% (39) were HIV-positive, and 4.63% (19) had an unknown status. The HIV status of each patient was shown in Fig. 3. Of all patients, 30.49% (125) had no co-infection, pneumonia was found in 1.22% (5), and other co-infections were reported in small percentages. Of those, 28.78% (118) had no previous diseases, and pneumonia was reported in 0.73% (3). Other conditions were also reported in small percentages.

Environmental influences

The odds of PTB were significantly increased in concrete housing (OR = 37.916, p = 0.005), and the odds of EPTB were reduced by 97.4% (OR = 0.026, p = 0.005). Higher odds of PTB and lower odds of EPTB were observed in corrugated housing, but these associations were not statistically significant (both p = 0.175). The number of windows was protective against PTB but increased the risk of EPTB (Table 2). The odds of PTB were 24.2% higher in individuals with a bedroom (OR = 1.24) and 23.5% higher in those with a separate kitchen (OR = 1.24, both p = 0.632), but neither was statistically significant. Families with a bedroom or separate kitchen had 19.5% and 19.0% lower odds of EPTB, respectively, with no statistical significance. It was shown by the data that cleaner energy sources were protective against PTB but increased the risk of EPTB. Cohabitation was associated with 55.0% lower odds of PTB (OR = 0.45, p = 0.019) and 2.2 times higher odds of EPTB (OR = 2.22, p = 0.019). The presence of an animal stable around the house was associated with 7.0% higher odds of PTB (OR = 1.07) and 6.5% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.94, both p = 0.860). Unhealthy behaviors and clinical factors

The consumption of raw milk was associated with 48.9% lower odds of PTB (OR = 0.51) and 95.7% higher odds of EPTB (OR = 1.96, both p = 0.02), with both statistically significant. Boiling milk showed no effect on the odds of PTB (OR = 1.01, p = 0.98) or EPTB (OR = 0.99, p = 0.98). The smoking habit was associated with 15.0% lower odds of PTB (OR = 0.85, p = 0.82) and 18.0% higher odds of EPTB (OR = 1.18, p = 0.82). Being a passive smoker showed 2.2 times higher odds of PTB (OR = 2.10, p = 0.29) and 52.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.48, p = 0.29), but these were not statistically significant either. Alcohol consumption was associated with 4.44 times higher odds of PTB (OR = 4.44, p = 0.00) and 77.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.23, p = 0.00), both of which were statistically significant. Substance abuse had 4.0% lower odds of PTB (OR = 0.96, p = 0.93) and 4.0% higher odds of EPTB (OR = 1.04, p = 0.93).

Having active TB in the family was associated with 2.76 times higher odds of PTB (OR = 2.76, p = 0.12) and 64.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.36, p = 0.12), but neither was statistically significant. Household contacts were linked to 30.0% lower odds of PTB (OR = 0.70, p = 0.59) and 43.0% higher odds of EPTB (OR = 1.43, p = 0.59). Awareness of transmission showed 2.72 times higher odds of PTB (OR = 2.72) and 63.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.37, p = 0.05), near statistical significance. Awareness of prevention showed 57.0% lower odds of PTB (OR = 0.43, p = 0.09) and 2.35 times higher odds of EPTB (OR = 2.35, p = 0.09), both marginally significant. HIV/AIDS status was linked to 78.0% higher odds of PTB (OR = 1.78) and 44.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.56, both p = 0.07), nearing statistical significance. Similarly, coinfection was associated with 36.0% higher odds of PTB (OR = 1.36, p = 0.08) and 27.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.73, p = 0.08), with both nearing statistical significance. A previous history of TB was linked to 24.0% higher odds of PTB (OR = 1.24, p = 0.14) and 19.0% lower odds of EPTB (OR = 0.81, p = 0.14). The details of the findings were summarized in Table 3.

Discussions

Significant associations were demonstrated between key risk factors and both PTB and EPTB in the extensive analysis of TB epidemiology. The critical role of a multifaceted, integrated approach to TB control was well substantiated. PTB was found to be the most prevalent form, consistent with existing literature [39], [40], [41], [42], [43]. The burden or trends reported in this study may have been affected by differences in clinical recognition, investigation intensity, or access to diagnostic tools, leading to unequal detection of PTB and EPTB cases. Moreover, concurrent complex PTB and EPTB were rarely reported, though missing data may have influenced findings. The prevalence trend of PTB was consistently documented across multiple studies. [44] Our results suggested that environmental factors had a complex impact on the development of PTB and EPTB, affecting both their spread and progression. [45], [46] Housing type showed differing patterns between PTB and EPTB, with stronger effects in concrete structures and weaker, non-significant trends in corrugated housing. These differences may reflect variation in indoor ventilation, where more enclosed modern buildings often have reduced natural airflow compared with simpler housing types. Limited air circulation and darker indoor environments may prolong survival of airborne droplet nuclei, which could influence transmission dynamics. Additionally, the number of windows per household reduced PTB risk, suggesting a protective effect, but increased odd of EPTB was observed, highlighting the complex impact of housing structure on TB spread. These difference in TB occurrences linked to effects of ventilation systems, consistent with previous studies [47], [48], [49].

The study also revealed a protective effect of fuel quality. The use of biomass fuels, particularly firewood, emerged as a significant risk factor for PTB. [50], [51] Over 80% of participants reported using firewood as their primary energy source, and this was strongly associated with an increased risk of PTB. In contrast, cleaner energy sources, like electricity and butane gas, comparatively reduced PTB risk, supporting evidence on the health impacts of indoor air pollution in low-resource settings. Similar effects were also consistently reported across different studies. [52], [53], [54], [55], [56] The results compared dominant risk factors between the two forms and were not refutations against established epidemiological knowledge or previously documented patterns. Both charcoal and electricity users also had 81.4% and 95.2% lower odds of PTB, but 5.4 and 21.1 times higher odds of EPTB,respectively. [53], [54] The results suggested that a greater respiratory impact was likely caused by wood smoke compared to cleaner energy sources. These reports highlight the need for policies promoting cleaner cooking technologies in rural areas to reduce indoor air pollution and improve TB prevention. [53], [56], [57] Cohabitation was also protective, with 55.0% lower odds of PTB but 2.2 times higher odds of EPTB.Animal owners showed higher risk of EPTB than PTB,mainly linked to the ingestion route of spread. This contrasts with previous studies linking animal exposure to increased TB risk. Having a separate bedroom or kitchen had no effect on PTB but raised EPTB odds, contradicting earlier studies suggesting a stronger link. The presence of an animal stable increased PTB, supporting previous findings. [47], [48], [58], [59], [60], [61] These results highlight the complex and distinct effects of environmental factors on PTB and EPTB, with some factors affecting one form more strongly but less so in the other [52], [62].

The study revealed that unhealthy practices, low awareness, and risky habits were significantly linked to the prevalence of both PTB and EPTB. Consumption of raw milk was associated with a 48.9% reduction in PTB risk but a 95.7% increase in EPTB risk (both p = 0.017), suggesting that pathogens in unprocessed milk may influence TB outcomes. The higher odds of EPTB associated with raw milk consumption were likely driven by M. bovis transmission, its common ingestion-based route of spread, and its rare hematogenous dissemination to the lungs. These findings suggested that unpasteurized dairy products contributed more to EPTB than PTB. The results were also strongly consistent with previous studies. [63], [64] Alcohol use was also linked to a 4.44-fold higher risk of PTB and a 77.0% lower risk of EPTB (both p = 0.002).Alcohol adversely affects microphages and T cells functions, which impairs host defense and increase susceptibility to TB.These findings were consistently aligned with previous studies. [65] Risky habits, both active and passive smoking, also showed no statistically supported associations with PTB or EPTB. However, smoking comparatively reduced the risk of PTB by 15% and increased odds of EPTB by 18% (both p = 0.82), while passive smoking raised twofold PTB risk but lowered EPTB risk by 52% (both p = 0.29). Smoking impairs mucocilliary clearance, alveolar microphages, and T cells responses, which compromises both local and systemic defenses against both forms of TB. The results were consistently with those previous studies. [65], [66] Diabetes was associated with a 50.4% higher risk of PTB and a 33.0% lower risk of EPTB, both statistically significant, suggesting diabetes influences TB.This agreed consistently with previous findings [67].

Our data showed that both PTB and EPTB were significantly impacted by diabetes, HIV/AIDS, and other coinfections. Coinfections adversely affected both forms, however, heterogenous pattern of their effects and difference in effect magnitudes were observed. Some showed stronger influence on PTB and others on EPTB.Diabetic status strongly influenced PTB than EPTB occurrence (50.4% higher PTB and 33.5% lower EPTB, p = 0.00). The findings suggest that diabetes strongly influenced both TB forms by impairing immune defense, with comparatively great effects were observed in PTB.The effect was consistently agreed with previous studies. [68] HIV/AIDS was comparatively linked to a 78.2% increase in the odds of PTB, though this finding was marginally significant, as HIV weakens the immune system and elevates the risk of PTB. [69], [70] Coinfection was associated with a 36.3% higher risk of PTB and 26.6% lower odds of EPTB (both p = 0.08), with marginal significance, suggesting that coinfections exacerbate immune suppression, increasing the risk of PTB and affecting the likelihood of EPTB. A weak effect was found for previous TB history on either form of TB, suggesting that past infections did not influence their current conditions. [66] Overall, diabetes had comparatively the most significant impact on PTB progression, with HIV/AIDS and coinfection showing weaker adverse effects. These effects were consistently observed across different previous studies. [70], [71].

Our study was limited by its cross-sectional design and snapshot analysis, which lacked the strength to determine causal relationship. Being exposure and TB occurrences were captured at a single point in time, and consequently, temporal relationships between those risk factors and TB could not be clearly established, limiting causal inference from those observed associations. [72] Additionally, disruptions arising from ethnic conflicts, resource shortages in the two study sites, and logistical difficulties impeded the research process. Those challenges created some difficulties in consistently identifying cases and have led to an underestimation of the true burden of TB in the areas, specifically Kaffa and Sheka Zones. Therefore, the imbalance in sampling distribution, with a higher proportion drawn from Bench-sheko zone (87.3%), resulted in findings that strongly reflect the characteristics of this area. Overall, the imbalance in sample distribution may limit ability to generalize or extrapolate those results to the broader society being studied. As a result, the true prevalence of TB in these areas may not have been accurately captured, potentially leading to an underestimation of its burden and failing to reflect the actual situation. [73], [74] Bias may also have arisen from inaccurate recall of previous disease history, affecting results.

Conclusions

The findings showed that housing, environmental factors, and lifestyle choices were found to impact PTB and EPTB risks. Concrete housing was associated with increased PTB but reduced EPTB, and ventilation was linked to lower PTB and higher EPTB. Fuel types (charcoal, gas, and electricity) were shown to reduce PTB but increase EPTB, especially with electricity. Cohabitation was found to protect against PTB but increase EPTB. Raw milk consumption was associated with reduced PTB but increased EPTB, and alcohol use was linked to higher PTB but lower EPTB. Awareness of TB transmission was shown to lower EPTB but increase PTB while preventive awareness showed the opposite pattern. Limited diabetes testing may have affected the strength of associations made and their reliability. These results highlighted the need for targeted interventions on housing, lifestyle, and TB awareness.

Ethical statements

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the ALIHR Institutional Ethical Review Committee (IREC) and the respective zonal health authorities prior to data collection. Written informed consent was obtained from all adult participants after providing detailed information about the purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and confidentiality of the study. For children under five years of age, consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians.

Participation in the study was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to refuse or withdraw from the study at any stage without any consequences to their medical care or services. Confidentiality and privacy of participant information were strictly maintained by using coded identifiers instead of personal information. All collected data were securely stored and accessed only by authorized research personnel.

The study employed non-invasive and standard diagnostic procedures, including smear microscopy, GeneXpert testing, clinical evaluation, and chest X-ray findings, with minimal risk to participants. All research activities were conducted in accordance with established ethical principles and guidelines for research involving human participants.
