The ABC-Death Score for Mortality Prediction in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation Undergoing Catheter Ablation
Authors: Yu-Feng Wang, Chao Jiang, Liu He, Xin Du, Xue-Yuan Guo, Ri-Bo Tang, Cai-Hua Sang, De-Yong Long, Jian-Zeng Dong, Gregory YH Lip, Chang-Sheng Ma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundData on the performance of risk scores in predicting mortality risk after atrial fibrillation (AF) ablations are limited.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to investigate the associations of mortality with preablation biomarkers and evaluate the performance of age, biomarker, and clinical history (ABC)-death score in patients with AF undergoing catheter ablation.MethodsPatients with AF undergoing catheter ablations between 2013 and 2019 in the Chinese Atrial Fibrillation Registry were enrolled. Biomarkers associated with ABC-death score were quantified from baseline blood samples collected before AF ablation. Clinical outcomes were all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality. Discrimination, reclassification, clinical use, and calibration were further evaluated.ResultsWe identified 4,218 patients with AF undergoing catheter ablations. During a median follow-up period of 4.0 years, 119 patients died due to all causes, with 49 dying due to cardiac causes. Biomarker levels were all independently associated with an increased risk of all-cause death and cardiac death. The ABC-death score was superior to the CHA2DS2-VASc score in predicting all-cause death (C index 0.73 vs 0.63; P = 0.001) and cardiac death (C index 0.83 vs 0.71; P = 0.007). Reclassification analysis revealed significant reclassification improvements of the ABC-death score compared with the CHA2DS2-VASc (cardiac failure or dysfunction, hypertension, age ≥75 [doubled], diabetes mellitus, stroke [doubled]—vascular disease, age 65 to 74 years and sex category [female]) score. Decision curve analysis showed the greater net benefit of use of the ABC-death score. Calibration plots presented an overestimation of the observed mortality event rate by ABC-death score.ConclusionsPreablation biomarkers associated with ABC-death score were independently related to increased all-cause and cardiac mortality risk. Despite the overestimation of the event rate, the ABC-death score outperformed the CHA2DS2-VASc score in discriminating and reclassifying mortality risk, especially for cardiac mortality.Key Words: ABC-death score, atrial fibrillation, biomarker, catheter ablation, mortality

Atrial fibrillation (AF), one of the most common cardiac arrhythmias, is associated with an increased risk of mortality and morbidity due to stroke and heart failure.1 Catheter ablation is an effective option to improve the prognosis of patients with AF.2 Despite its introduction as a stroke risk stratification tool, several studies have shown that the CHA2DS2-VASc score could predict mortality in patients with AF3,4 and identify major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in those undergoing catheter ablation.5 However, data on the performance of various risk stratification scores in predicting mortality risk after AF ablation procedures are sparse.

In recent years, the correlation between biomarkers and the prognosis of patients with AF has become an area of great interest. Several biomarkers have been demonstrated to be strongly associated with clinical adverse events and enhance the predictive value of simple clinical risk scores, such as the CHA2DS2-VASc score.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

These biomarkers reflect the probable pathophysiological processes associated with AF and its associated complications, although many are nonspecific for AF-related outcomes.11 Among numerous biomarkers, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and cardiac troponin T measured with high sensitivity assays (TnT-hs), indicating myocardial damage and stress, and grow differentiation factor (GDF)-15, a marker of oxidative stress, are potential predictors of mortality risk.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 The ABC (Age, Biomarker, and Clinical history)-death score, composed of age, heart failure, and the aforementioned 3 biomarkers, has been validated in clinical trials and real-world studies for predicting all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in patients with AF.12,13

Considering the predictive performance of biomarkers and ABC-death score, first, we aimed to investigate the relation of preablation levels of GDF-15, NT-proBNP, and TnT-hs with all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality in patients with AF undergoing ablation procedures. Second, we evaluated the performance of ABC-death score in predicting 2 clinical outcomes in these patients.

Methods

All individuals in our study were included in the Chinese Atrial Fibrillation Registry, one of the largest observational studies for patients with AF in China, encompassing 31 tertiary and nontertiary hospitals around the nation. Baseline data collection included demographics, comorbidities, physical examination, laboratory tests, and medication. Each patient had a routine follow-up to obtain clinical outcomes such as mortality and cardiovascular events. All patients who participated in this registry and underwent catheter ablations would complete a consent form before the ablation procedure. The study was approved by the ethics committees of Beijing Anzhen Hospital. The baseline and follow-up data were collected by cardiovascular specialists with the relevant expertise and/or nurse practitioners in outpatient clinics or via telephone interviews and stored using a web-based data collection system. Our analysis used solely anonymized information.14

ABC-death score, which consists of age, heart failure, TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15, was calculated using the ABC-AF death equations proposed by Hijazi et al.12 This equation could provide the 1-year risk, which is equal to the probability of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality at 1 year for patients with AF. We used the version of “Troponin T” as presented by Hijazi et al12 in their supplemental data online in our study. Briefly, the predicted risk at 1 year was obtained through the estimated event-free probability calculated using baseline survival at 1 year and linear predictors, which is the weighted sum of predictors (components of ABC-death score). The detailed calculation procedure was shown in the Supplemental Appendix.

Heart failure in the equation refers to the clinical history of heart failure that was diagnosed based on the presence of symptoms and/or signs of heart failure, elevated NT-proBNP or BNP, and abnormal parameters using echocardiography, including heart failure with reduced ejection fraction and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction.

The CHA2DS2-VASc score was calculated using clinical factors including congestive heart failure, hypertension, age ≥75 years (2 points), diabetes mellitus, stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) (2 points), vascular disease, age 65-74 years, and sex category (female).15

Study population

The following inclusion criteria were presented: 1) older than 18 years; 2) underwent ablation procedures at baseline; and 3) accessible baseline blood sample. Excluded were patients having one or more of the following: 1) valvular AF, including prosthetic mechanical valves and moderate to severe mitral stenosis; and 2) unstable condition before ablation procedures, including acute coronary syndrome, stroke/TIA, and acute onset of heart failure in the preceding 3 months.

Clinical outcome and follow-up

Clinical outcomes in our study were all-cause mortality, which was defined as death from any cause, and cardiac death due to heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, sudden cardiac death, ischemic stroke/TIA, or other cardiac diseases. Patients were followed up for the first time 3 months after ablation and every 6 months thereafter to record survival information and clinically relevant events. Clinical outcomes were adjudicated by the cardiac specialists using all available data collected during the follow-up period.

Postprocedure anticoagulation strategies

The use of oral anticoagulants was left to the discretion of cardiovascular doctors at hospitals participating in the study. In relevant patients with AF, adjusted-dose warfarin (international normalized ratio: 2.0-3.0) was begun on the evening of the ablation procedure, or a non–vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulant (dabigatran 150/110 mg or rivaroxaban 20/15 mg) was resumed the day after the procedure. Anticoagulation therapy was continued for at least the first 3 months.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± SD or median (IQR) according to the distribution of variables. Categorized variables were shown as frequencies (percentages).

With the stratification according to the ABC-death score categories (low risk: <2%; intermediate risk: 2%-5%; high risk: >5%), baseline characteristics were shown and compared using the t test or Wilcoxon test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for categorical data. Cumulative event rate curves were plotted using Kaplan-Meier method, in terms of the level of biomarkers and ABC-death score categories to graphically show the difference in all-cause death and cardiac death. Cox regression was used to identify risk variables related to clinical outcomes. Considering the competing risk of mortality for other reasons, we presented the association of biomarkers with cardiac mortality by competing risk regression. C index16 was presented to show the performance of the CHA2DS2-VASc score, its combination with each biomarker, and the ABC-death score in predicting all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality. Calibration curves were plotted by comparing the observed annualized incidence rate calculated for each category with those predicted by ABC-death score in our study. Net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were computed at the median follow-up time to show the reclassification improvement of ABC-death score compared with CHA2DS2-VASc score.17 Briefly, The NRI focuses on reclassification tables constructed separately for participants with and without events, and quantifies the correct movement in categories; the IDI focuses on differences between integrated sensitivities and “one minus specificities” for models with and without the new marker. The decision curve analysis was used to display and compare the net benefit of using ABC-death and CHA2DS2-VASc score in identifying mortality events.18 Sensitivity analysis was further explored in patients without continuous anticoagulation therapy of at least 1 year.

All statistical analysis was conducted using R (version 4.1.3) and a P value <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

There were 4,240 patients who meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria. However, data on GDF-15 was lost for 22 patients. In total, 4,218 participants (mean age: 59.8 ± 11.1 years; 34.3% female) undergoing catheter ablation for AF were enrolled in this study. Patients were stratified into low-, medium-, and high-risk groups using the ABC-death score, with 3,606, 489, and 123 patients, respectively. The baseline characteristics of patients in each group are shown in Table 1. There were 107 patients with AF without estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), so the baseline characteristic of eGFR was presented by a complete dataset (n = 4,111). Those with higher ABC-death risk were more likely to be older and female, to have a lower level of eGFR and a larger proportion of persistent/long-standing persistent AF, to have more comorbidities, including coronary artery disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and stroke/TIA, and have higher CHA2DS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores. From the low- to high-risk group of the ABC-death score categories, ascending trends also exhibited in biomarker levels of TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15. Baseline characteristics stratified by biomarker levels are shown in Supplemental Table 1.

After a median follow-up of 4.0 (IQR: 3.4-5.5) years, we recorded 119 deaths from all causes, 49 (41.2%) of which were cardiac deaths. The incidence rates of all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality were 0.66% and 0.27% per person per year, respectively.

Among cardiac mortality events, there were 21 (17.7%), 11 (9.2%), 8 (6.7%), 5 (4.2%), and 4 (3.4%) deaths caused by heart failure, cardiac sudden death, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, and other cardiac reasons, respectively.

There were 51 (42.9%) deaths caused by noncardiac events, including 10 (8.4%) intracranial hemorrhage, 1 (0.9%) major bleeding, and 40 (33.6%) other noncardiac reasons, and 19 (15.9%) deaths with uncertain reasons.

Association of biomarkers with all-cause and cardiac mortality

The probability of reaching an endpoint was significantly higher with increasing levels of GDF-15, NT-proBNP, and TnT-hs for all-cause and cardiovascular deaths (Supplemental Figure 1). Because there were 107 patients with AF without eGFR, we used the completed dataset (n = 4,111) when conducting Cox analysis. After adjusting for multiple risk factors, including age, eGFR, hypertension, coronary artery disease, diabetes mellitus, heart failure, prior history of ischemic stroke or TIA, and oral anticoagulation for at least 1 year (OAC 1 year), TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15 independently predicted all-cause mortality (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02-1.28; P = 0.022; HR: 1.15; 95% CI: 1.03-1.29; P = 0.012; HR: 1.60; 95% CI: 1.21-2.11; P < 0.001, respectively) and cardiac mortality (HR: 1.27; 95% CI: 1.12-1.44; P < 0.001; HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.05-1.49; P = 0.014; HR: 1.67; 95% CI: 1.08-2.58; P = 0.022, respectively) (Table 2). The association of 3 biomarkers with cardiac mortality risk by competing risk analysis was similar with that by Cox analysis (Supplemental Table 2). Among clinical factors, age, heart failure, and prior history of stroke/TIA independently predicted all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality, coronary heart disease was independently associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, and a lower level of eGFR was independently associated with a decreased risk of cardiac mortality (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4).

Performance of biomarkers and the ABC-death score in mortality prediction

The incidence rate of all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality stratified by ABC-death score was 0.43%, 1.62%, and 3.16%/person-year, and 0.12%, 0.86%, and 2.23%/person-year in low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups, respectively. Kaplan-Meier curves (Figure 1) showed that higher risk categories were accompanied by greater cumulative all-cause death and cardiac death risk (P for log-rank<0.001). Within the CHA2DS2-VASc score categories, the ABC-death score could further stratify patients into subgroups (Supplemental Figures 2 and 3). After adjusting for coronary artery disease, diabetes, eGFR, hypertension, OAC 1 year, and prior history of stroke or TIA, the ABC-death score was significantly associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality (Supplemental Table 5).

The ABC-death score, which comprised age, heart failure, and biomarkers, including TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15, yielded a higher C index than the CHA2DS2-VASc score in predicting all-cause death (0.73 vs 0.63; P < 0.001) and cardiovascular death (0.83 vs 0.71; P = 0.007). We further compared the predictive ability of ABC-death score with the CHA2DS2-VASc score plus all 3 biomarkers, and it showed that the ABC-death score statistically outperformed CHA2DS2-VASc plus biomarkers score in predicting all-cause mortality (C index 0.73 vs 0.69; P = 0.031); There was no statistical difference between the ABC-death score and CHA2DS2-VASc plus biomarkers score in predicting cardiac mortality (C index 0.83 vs 0.80; P = 0.249).

Reclassification analysis showed a higher reclassification capacity for ABC-death score compared with CHA2DS2-VASc score, in terms of NRI (15.8%; 95% CI: 0.9%-30.7%; P = 0.037) and IDI (0.5%; 95% CI: 0.03%-0.9%; P = 0.036) at the median follow-up of 4 years. The reclassification improvement seemed to be more obvious for cardiac death, with a significant NRI of 36.8%, and IDI of 1.1% (Table 4). Decision curve analysis revealed a moderate net benefit for ABC-death score in identifying additional all-cause mortality events without increasing the false-positive rate in comparison with CHA2DS2-VASc score, whereas the net benefit was more substantial for identifying patients with cardiac death events (Figure 2).

Sensitivity analysis

We performed a sensitivity analysis in patients with AF without OAC of at least 1 year, again showing that each biomarker was also significantly associated with mortality risk after adjusting for multiple risk factors (Supplemental Table 6). Comparison of C indexes showed that ABC-stroke score still statistically outperformed CHA2DS2-VASc score for discriminating all-cause death and cardiac death than CHA2DS2-VASc score (Supplemental Table 7).

Discussion

The principal findings from our study are as follows: 1) there was a strong association of the preablation level of biomarkers including TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15 with all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality; 2) the discrimination of the prediction model significantly improved after adding each biomarker to the CHA2DS2-VASc score; 3) the ABC-death score outperformed the CHA2DS2-VASc score in predicting all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality in patients with AF undergoing ablations; and 4) there was an overestimation of the observed event rate with suboptimal calibration with the ABC-death score, especially for those with higher mortality risk. Multiple studies have demonstrated the catheter ablation procedure as an approach to rhythm strategies to lessen AF burden, relieve symptoms, and improve prognosis including reducing the risk of mortality.2,19,20 However, few focused on the evaluation of mortality risk in patients who underwent ablation procedures. One study from California reported that age older than 80 years and heart failure were independent risk factors for 30-day death after ablation procedures.21 Another study found that the CHA2DS2-VASc score was significantly associated with MACE, as well as being predictive for postablation MACE, although this was mostly driven by thromboembolism events.5 The long-term relationship between CHA2DS2-VASc score and death for patients with AF undergoing ablation procedures was further demonstrated in another study (HR: 1.28 for 5 years; P < 0.001), but the discriminative metrics such as the C index were not reported.22

Our study demonstrated moderate predictive performance of CHA2DS2-VASc score for all-cause mortality in patients with AF after ablation procedures, with the caveat that the CHA2DS2-VASc score was proposed for stroke and thromboembolism risk stratification.15 Also, a recent European Heart Rhythm Association position paper appeals for the appropriate use of risk scores for what they were designed for, and for the right outcome.23

Some biomarkers have been demonstrated to be linked with mortality risk in patients with AF. One analysis from the RE-LY (The Randomized Evaluation of Long-Term Anticoagulation Therapy) trial has shown that vascular mortality was independently related to elevated levels of troponin I (HR: 4.38; 95% CI: 3.05-6.29) and NT-proBNP (HR: 6.73; 95% CI: 3.95-11.49) in the multivariable model; the discriminative performance of the CHA2DS2-VASc score in predicting the composite outcome of stroke, systemic embolism, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, and vascular death was significantly improved when adding each biomarker to the prediction models.6 In the ARISTOTLE (The Apixaban for Reduction in Stroke and Other Thromboembolic Events in Atrial Fibrillation) trial, TnT-hs and NT-proBNP were also confirmed to have an independent relationship to cardiac mortality (HR: 1.91; 95% CI: 1.43-2.56; HR: 2.50; 95% CI: 1.81-3.45, respectively), and inclusion of TnT-hs and NT-proBNP to the CHA2DS2-VASc score significantly increased the C index for cardiac mortality from 0.59-0.71 and 0.69, respectively.7,8 Another postanalysis from ENGAGE TIMI-AF 48 (Effective aNticoaGulation with factor xA next GEneration in Atrial Fibrillation–Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction study 48) trial has further demonstrated the strong association of biomarkers including troponin I and NT-proBNP with composite events and the better performance of biomarker-based score compared with CHA2DS2-VASc score (0.71 vs 0.59).10 GDF-15, a biomarker of oxidative stress, had a substantial association with mortality in the ARISTOTLE trial, even after adjusting for NT-proBNP and TnT-hs (HR: 2.10, 95% CI: 1.62-2.42), whereby the predictive performance of all-cause mortality increased significantly from 0.64-0.71 when GDF-15 was included in the CHA2DS2-VASc score.9

Research from real-world experience similarly revealed good discrimination of GDF-15 for all-cause mortality,13 despite its promotion as a specific biomarker for bleeding. One biomarker substudy using the Random Survival Forest method further underscored the superiority of NT-proBNP and TnT-hs over all other 266 markers of inflammation and cardiovascular illnesses, as well as 13 clinical risk variables, including age. Indeed, GDF-15 was also determined as one of the biomarkers showing the strongest association with cardiovascular mortality.24

It is currently unclear how GDF-15, a marker of inflammation and oxidative stress, could contribute to mortality. AF and other cardiovascular comorbidities could cause atrial remodeling, cellular ischemia, and reperfusion that might increase the plasma level of GDF-15, which might be influenced by redox state and increase cardiovascular dysfunction and mortality.9,25 Therefore, under a variety of clinical circumstances, the level of GDF-15 can be used as an integrative indicator of the severity of cardiovascular disease. The nonspecificity of GDF-15 has little bearing on its predictions of death, as a previous study has shown an independent relation between GDF-15 and mortality,9 which is consistent with what we found in our study. But further research on the underlying biological process might be necessary.

The majority of research verifying the connection between biomarkers and mortality risk enrolled anticoagulant-treated patients with AF. However, the evidence on the use of biomarkers to predict clinical adverse outcomes in patients with AF after ablation procedures remained limited. Only one study examined the predictive value of TnT-hs levels for MACE after AF ablation, and it demonstrated that TnT-hs was independently linked with the composite outcome. Due to the relatively short duration of follow-up, no death events occurred.26 Our study demonstrates the strong association of the aforementioned 3 biomarkers with mortality risk in patients with AF undergoing ablation procedures, as well as the statistically significant improvement in predictive performance after adding them to the CHA2DS2-VASc score, which suggests that a risk model combining clinical factors and biomarkers will accurately predict mortality risk in this population.

ABC-death score, which comprised age, heart failure, TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15, was derived and well-validated with good performance in predicting all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality in trials with patients with AF on anticoagulation therapy or antiplatelet therapy.12,27 In our study, in which approximately 80% of patients discontinued anticoagulation therapy for at least 1 year after AF ablation, this score also showed superior discrimination compared with the CHA2DS2-VASc score. Moreover, the sensitivity analysis showed a similar outperformance of the ABC-death score in the subgroup that discontinued anticoagulation compared with the entire cohort of patients.

Due to the origination from AF patients on anticoagulation therapy, it was reasonable that the mortality risk by ABC-death score was overestimated in the AF population undergoing ablations. Patients with higher mortality risk might benefit more from AF ablations than those with lower mortality risk, hence we found poor calibration in patients with higher risk as predicted by ABC-death score. Nevertheless, our findings demonstrated a significantly increasing trend of mortality risk from low- to high-risk groups stratified by ABC-death score, which might still help discriminate patients with various mortality risks.

The results of our study cannot be construed to indicate that patients with greater biomarker levels or ABC-death scores should avoid ablation treatments because of higher risk. More regular follow-up and careful recommendations, including anticoagulant use, symptom control, and concomitant disease management, were necessary for patients categorized as higher risk after AF ablation. The greater reclassification improvement and net benefit in identifying cardiac death events also suggested the substantial importance of optimizing treatment strategies for cardiovascular comorbidities in patients with higher risk.

Study Limitations

First, because all patients in our research received AF ablation, the results cannot be generalized to the overall AF population. However, our results filled a gap in the use of TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, GDF-15, and the ABC-death score for predicting mortality risk in patients with AF undergoing catheter ablations. Second, it was not feasible to re-evaluate biomarkers after AF ablations in our study because we only obtained baseline blood samples. We recognize that risk is not static but can be dynamically changed with aging and incident comorbidities, so changes in biomarkers before and after AF ablations might give more useful prognostic information for patients. Third, because the ABC-death score overestimated the mortality risk in patients with AF undergoing ablation procedures, further modification of the ABC-death score was necessary for accurately identifying patients with different risk levels. Finally, we recognize that many biomarkers are nonspecific, being described by Camelo-Castillo et al11 as being “reflective of a sick patient or a sick heart.” Diurnal variation and assay variability when used in real-world practice are additional considerations.

Conclusions

Elevated levels of TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15 before AF ablation were independently related to an increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality. Each biomarker significantly enhanced the efficacy of the CHA2DS2-VASc score in predicting clinical outcomes. Despite the overestimation of the observed mortality event rate, the biomarker-based ABC-death score outperformed the simple CHA2DS2-VASc score in discriminating and correctly reclassifying mortality risk, especially for cardiac mortality.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Elevated levels of TnT-hs, NT-proBNP, and GDF-15, and increasing ABC-death score categories are significantly associated with all-cause mortality and cardiac mortality risk even in the presence of AF ablation procedures.

COMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE: ABC-death score could be a better option for discriminating patients with AF with high mortality risk undergoing AF ablations, especially the cardiac mortality risk. More regular follow-up and careful recommendations would be advocated for these selected patients.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 1: Modification of ABC-death score in patients with AF after ablation procedures requires further investigation because it overestimated the observed event rate in our study.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK 2: The value of AF management strategies guided by ABC-death score is still unknown. Exploration of the difference in benefit to patients with AF from certain management strategies (eg, ABC integrated care) among different ABC-death score categories might provide more information.
