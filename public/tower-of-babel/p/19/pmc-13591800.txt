Association between food group intakes and metabolic acidosis in patients with non-dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease
Authors: Nobuhisa Morimoto, Shintaro Mandai, Tamami Fujiki, Fumiaki Ando, Takayasu Mori, Koichiro Susa, Shotaro Naito, Eisei Sohara, Tatsuhiko Anzai, Kunihiko Takahashi, Wataru Akita, Akihito Ohta, Shinichi Uchida, Soichiro Iimori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence is limited regarding the associations between intakes of specific food groups and metabolic acidosis in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). We investigated the association between food group-based intakes and metabolic acidosis in patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD.MethodsWe used baseline data from a prospective cohort study that recruited adult patients with CKD at three hospitals in Tokyo, Japan. Dietary intake was estimated using a validated diet history questionnaire. We examined the association between intakes of individual food groups and serum bicarbonate levels using multiple linear regression, adjusted for demographic and clinical parameters. We also evaluated the association between food group intakes and metabolic acidosis, defined as a serum bicarbonate level < 22 mmol/L, by multiple logistic regression.ResultsAmong the 225 eligible patients (median age 72 years, 33.8% women), 43 patients (19.1%) had metabolic acidosis. Intakes of pulses, green/yellow vegetables (including carrots, tomatoes, and green leafy vegetables), other vegetables (including cabbage, radishes, and onions), and dairy products were positively associated with serum bicarbonate levels. Greater intake of other vegetables was associated with lower odds of metabolic acidosis (highest vs. lowest intake: adjusted odds ratio = 0.28, 95% confidence interval: 0.10, 0.79, P = 0.016); however, such associations were not observed for other food groups, including potatoes, green/yellow vegetables, and pulses.ConclusionsPatients with higher intakes of certain vegetables had lower odds of metabolic acidosis. Further longitudinal studies are warranted to confirm these associations and develop effective dietary measures that optimize the acid-base balance of patients with CKD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12882-026-05147-x.Keywords: Metabolic acidosis, Chronic kidney disease, Food group, Vegetable

Introduction

Metabolic acidosis is an important complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [1]. Metabolic acidosis has been associated with worse prognoses in patients with CKD, including protein-energy wasting, bone demineralization, CKD progression, and higher mortality risk [2–4]. While the management strategies for metabolic acidosis have included sodium bicarbonate treatment and dietary therapy, sole reliance on pharmacological treatment may not be optimal, given the potential downsides of sodium bicarbonate outlined in the most recent Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines, including elevated blood pressure, higher heart failure risk, and decline in quality of life [1]. The guidelines have suggested that the threshold for sodium bicarbonate treatment be lowered from < 22 mmol/L to < 18 mmol/L [1, 5], highlighting the importance of dietary therapy in the management of metabolic acidosis.

The primary focus of dietary therapy for metabolic acidosis is increasing the intake of fruits and vegetables, which reduce dietary acid load, and limiting animal foods, which increase it [1]. Previous randomized controlled trials involving patients with stage 3 and 4 CKD has demonstrated that increasing fruits and vegetables ameliorated metabolic acidosis [6, 7]. Several cross-sectional studies have also examined the association between dietary acid load and serum bicarbonate levels [6–10]. One cross-sectional study on 100 patients with stage 3–4 CKD reported that those with greater animal protein intake and higher potential renal acid load (PRAL), which estimates the amount of dietary acid load by taking into account the intake of acid-generating nutrients (protein and phosphorus) and base-generating nutrients (potassium, calcium, and magnesium) [11], had lower serum bicarbonate levels [8]. Another cross-sectional study in 42 patients with stage 3–5 CKD similarly showed that PRAL was associated with serum bicarbonate levels [9]. While these studies suggest the potential benefit of plant foods in acid-base management, whether different types of vegetables have distinct associations with metabolic acidosis remains largely unclear. Because vegetables contain a variety of foods with varying nutrient contents and health effects [12, 13], certain vegetables may confer greater benefit in ameliorating metabolic acidosis than others. For example, the high protein content of pulses may increase endogenous acid production and attenuate the acid load-reducing effect of certain minerals in pulses. In contrast, cruciferous vegetables are rich in glucosinolates, whose metabolite, sulforaphane, may possess anti-inflammatory and renoprotective effects [12, 13], potentially leading to improved acid-base balance. Additionally, because most previous studies on the diet-acidosis relationship were conducted in the US, Europe, and South America [8, 9, 14], the generalizability of these findings to Asian populations, whose diets generally consist of a greater amount of plant foods and a smaller amount of meat [15, 16], remains uncertain.

Given these knowledge gaps, in this cross-sectional study, we aimed to investigate the association between the intake of individual food groups and metabolic acidosis in patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD in Japan.

Subjects and methods

Study population

This study is a cross-sectional analysis of baseline data from an ongoing multicenter prospective cohort study, which aims to explore the associations between nutritional factors and complications of CKD [17]. We enrolled adult patients with CKD at three hospitals (Institute of Science Tokyo Hospital, Komagome Hospital, or Doai Memorial Hospital) in Tokyo, Japan [17]. In this study, CKD was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, a history of nephrotic syndrome or glomerulonephritis that had been treated, or persistent proteinuria or hematuria for at least three months [18]. Patients were excluded from the prospective cohort study if they were < 20 years old, were undergoing maintenance dialysis treatment, had eGFR > 90 mL/min/1.73 m2, had a recent diagnosis or treatment of malignancies within two years of study entry, or were clinically unstable.

For this cross-sectional study, participants under sodium bicarbonate treatment were excluded to minimize the effect of sodium bicarbonate on serum bicarbonate levels. Additionally, patients with eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73m2 were excluded because of the low prevalence of metabolic acidosis in this population [19]. Patients with implausible energy intake (women: <500 kcal/day or > 3500 kcal/day; men: <800 kcal/day or > 4000 kcal/day) were also excluded [20]. Lastly, patients missing data on one or more variables in our regression models were excluded, resulting in a final sample size of 225.

Details of the study have been described previously [17]. Briefly, patients completed a brief self-administered dietary history questionnaire (BDHQ), a validated diet history questionnaire on the frequency of various foods and beverages consumed over the previous month [21, 22]. Patients also answered questions about their lifestyle habits. We retrieved data on comorbidities, medication use, and past medical history from the electronic medical records.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Tokyo Medical and Dental University (approval number: M2021-355). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted according to the guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki. This study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology – Nutritional Epidemiology (STROBE-nut) guidelines (Supplementary Table 1) [23].

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes of this study were serum bicarbonate levels (continuous variable) and metabolic acidosis, which was defined as a serum bicarbonate level < 22 mmol/L [1]. Serum bicarbonate levels were calculated by pH and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2) levels measured by a venous blood gas test, using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation as follows:

pH = 6.1 + log (bicarbonate / (0.03 × pCO2)).

Venous blood samples were collected in most patients in non-fasting conditions during the outpatient visit or hospital admission. Blood samples were sent to the central laboratory of the respective hospitals within 15 min of collection and analyzed for pH and pCO2 levels using a blood gas analyzer.

Dietary methods

Participants filled out a brief self-administered dietary history questionnaire (BDHQ), a validated 4-page diet history questionnaire that inquired about the frequency of commonly eaten foods and beverages in Japan over the previous month [21, 22]. Specifically, patients were provided with BDHQs and written instructions during the outpatient visit, and were told to complete the BDHQs at home within one month before the next outpatient visit, when a blood sample was obtained for this study. For some patients, a blood sample was obtained on the day when BDHQs were distributed. These patients were told to complete the BDHQs within one month after the outpatient visit, as the BDHQs covered dietary data over the previous one-month period. This study also included a small number of inpatient participants, who were similarly provided with BDHQs and written instructions and were told to complete the BDHQs during their hospital admission. Completed BDHQs were mailed to EBNJAPAN (http://www.ebnjapan.org) for the calculation of daily food and nutrient intakes, which were estimated using an ad hoc computer algorithm [21]. Details of the estimation of food and nutrient intakes have been described previously [21]. Briefly, BDHQ inquires participants about the consumption frequency of 46 commonly consumed foods and non-alcoholic beverages in Japan; the number of bowls of rice and cups of miso soup consumed per day; drinking frequency of five alcoholic beverages and amount per drink; cooking methods; and dietary behavior. Specifically, food intakes are estimated based on the reported consumption frequency and the standard portion sizes derived from recipe books for Japanese dishes. Because the standard portion sizes were generally intended for women, portion sizes for men were estimated by multiplying the standard portion sizes by 1.14. Additionally, portion sizes for specific food items were weighted based on the previous findings of underreporting of certain food items [24] and other unpublished results [21]. Intakes of rice and miso from miso soup were estimated from the number of bowls of rice and cups of miso soup per day, respectively, and the standard portion sizes. Daily energy and nutrient intakes were calculated based on the estimated food and beverage intake and data from the Standard Tables of Food Composition in Japan [25].

The primary exposure of interest was intakes of individual food groups. Food group intakes were adjusted by total energy intake using the density method to decrease measurement errors [26]. Specifically, food group intakes in grams were divided by total energy intake and multiplied by 1000 so that the intakes were expressed in grams per 1000 kcal per day. Food groups included grains, potatoes, sugars/sweeteners, pulses, green/yellow vegetables, other vegetables, fruits, fish, meats, eggs, dairy products, oils, confectioneries, drinks, and seasonings. Specific foods in each food group have been described previously [21] and presented in Supplementary Table 2. In this study, grains, potatoes, pulses, green/yellow vegetables, other vegetables, and fruits were considered plant food groups, while fish, meats, eggs, and dairy products were considered animal food groups.

Covariates

Participants’ height and weight were obtained by self-report. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m2). Information about the underlying cause of CKD, the presence of comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia), past medical history, and medication use was collected from the electronic medical records. Data on lifestyle habits, including smoking and alcohol drinking, were collected by a self-administered questionnaire. Smoking habits and alcohol drinking habits were classified into three groups: never, past, and current. Renal function was determined by eGFR, which was calculated by the following formula for Japanese individuals [27]: 194 × serum creatinine− 1.094 × age− 0.287 × 0.739 for women and 194 × serum creatinine− 1.094 × age− 0.287 for men.

Statistical analyses

Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized according to the presence of metabolic acidosis using means and standard deviations for normally distributed continuous variables, medians and interquartile ranges for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and numbers and percentages for categorical variables. The symmetry of distributions was determined by visual inspection of histograms. The standardized mean differences for these characteristics between those with and without metabolic acidosis were calculated using the “tableone” package (version 0.13.2) in R [28].

Energy-adjusted intakes of individual food groups were summarized as medians and interquartile ranges. The median energy-adjusted intakes of these food groups in Japanese adults aged 20 years or older and those aged 65–74 years were obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan, 2019 [15].

To examine the association between the intake of individual food groups and serum bicarbonate levels, we fitted simple and multiple linear regression models, adjusted for the following potential confounders based on clinical experience and previous studies [29, 30]: age, sex, eGFR, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and the use of loop diuretics or thiazides. We excluded two food groups (sugars/sweeteners and oils) due to their low intake (median intake < 10 g/day). Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were used to examine the presence of multicollinearity. As described above, we performed the regression analyses using complete case analysis (n = 225) because the number of participants with missing covariates was small (n = 9).

To examine the association between the intake of individual food groups and metabolic acidosis, we fitted simple and multiple logistic regression models, adjusted for the same confounders as the linear regression models. For the exposure variable, we divided the participants into three groups by the tertile cutpoints of each food group intake (low-, medium-, and high-intake) and included these three groups in the regression models.

We performed subgroup analyses by stratifying participants by eGFR level (≥ 30 and < 30 mL/min/1.73m2). Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of our findings. First, logistic regression was performed after including energy and energy-adjusted protein intakes in the regression model. Second, logistic regression analyses were performed after further adding the use of renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors to the regression model, as these medications could affect serum bicarbonate levels by altering serum potassium levels.

To evaluate whether our results had adequate statistical power to detect a significant association, we calculated the minimum detectable effect with 80% power and 5% two-sided type-I error, using the following formula for logistic regression [31]:

where βaj is the effect for a one standard deviation increase in the continuous exposure variable, n is the sample size, and p is the prevalence of the outcome. Given our sample size (n = 225) and the prevalence of metabolic acidosis (p = 0.19), βaj was calculated to be 0.475, corresponding to an odds ratio of 1.61 or 0.62.

We also compared the mean PRAL between the three intake groups of each food group by one-way analysis of variance. PRAL was calculated using the following formula [11]: 0.4888 × protein + 0.0366 × phosphorus − 0.0205 × potassium − 0.0125 × calcium − 0.0263 × magnesium.

Statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA) and R version 4.1.3 for Windows [32]. In this study, a two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Of 311 participants who agreed to participate in the study, 225 participants (median age (interquartile range (IQR)) 72 (61–79) years; 33.8% women) were eligible (Fig. 1; Table 1). Among the 225 eligible participants, 177 participants (79%) and 148 participants (66%) completed BDHQs within 30 days and 7 days before a blood sample was collected, respectively (Supplementary Figure. 1). Blood and urine tests were performed within one month before or after completing BDHQs in most patients. Some patients did not follow the instructions and completed BDHQs more than one month before or after the blood sample collection. The median (IQR) eGFR level was 32.4 (23.5–42.1) mL/min/1.73m2. Among the 225 participants, 43 (19.1%) had metabolic acidosis. The mean serum bicarbonate level was 24.3 ± 2.9 mmol/L. The most prevalent cause of CKD in this study was nephrosclerosis, followed by glomerulonephritis and diabetic nephropathy. The median (IQR) BMI was 23.7 (21.3–26.4) kg/m2. A majority of patients were taking renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors and calcium channel blockers.

Patients with metabolic acidosis tend to be older, have lower eGFR, venous pH, pCO2, and hemoglobin levels, and higher serum potassium and chloride levels than those without metabolic acidosis (Table 1).

Dietary intakes

The median (IQR) energy intake was 1624.0 (1348.8–2030.2) kcal/day (Table 1). The median (IQR) protein intake was 64.6 (48.3–76.4) g/day. The median (IQR) fiber intake was 10.0 (7.3–14.1) g/day.

As for energy-adjusted food intake by individual food groups, the largest weight (median) came from drinks (377.6 g/1000 kcal/day), followed by grains (205.6 g/1000 kcal/day), seasonings (108.0 g/1000 kcal/day), other vegetables (81.4 g/1000 kcal/day), dairy products (77.9 g/1000 kcal/day), green/yellow vegetables (51.0 g/1000 kcal/day), fruits (48.2 g/1000 kcal/day), meats (38.7 g/1000 kcal/day), and fish (37.7 g/1000 kcal/day) (Table 2). As compared to the national median energy-adjusted food group intakes, participants in this study tend to consume larger amounts of dairy products, confectioneries, and seasonings, while the intakes of other food groups appeared comparable.

Association between food group-based intake and metabolic acidosis

Multiple linear regression analyses demonstrated significant positive associations of intakes from pulses, green/yellow vegetables, other vegetables, and dairy products with serum bicarbonate levels (beta coefficient (β) = 1.47, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.22, 2.71, P = 0.021; β = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.17, 1.78, P = 0.017; β = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.10, 1.45, P = 0.024; β = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.04, 0.99, P = 0.035, respectively) (Supplementary Table 3, Fig. 2). Multiple logistic regression analysis showed significantly reduced odds of metabolic acidosis in participants with higher intakes from other vegetables as compared to those with lower intakes (for one standard deviation increase in intake: adjusted odds ratio (OR) = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.34, 0.89, P = 0.015) (Supplementary Table 4). The high-intake group of other vegetables had reduced odds of metabolic acidosis than the low-intake group, reaching statistical significance (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.10, 0.79, P = 0.016) (Supplementary Table 5, Fig. 3a). Intakes of the green/yellow vegetable group, as well as other food groups, did not show statistically significant associations with metabolic acidosis (Supplementary Tables 4–5, Fig. 3a and b). VIFs were < 2 for all regression analyses, suggesting a lack of multicollinearity. The analysis of the minimum detectable effect indicated that our sample size had adequate power to detect the association between the intake of other vegetables and metabolic acidosis.

Discussion

To our knowledge, few studies have investigated the association between food group-based intakes and metabolic acidosis in patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD. We found positive associations of the intakes of pulses, green/yellow vegetables, other vegetables, and dairy products with serum bicarbonate levels, while only greater intake of other vegetables remained associated with lower odds of metabolic acidosis.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports that demonstrated the beneficial effects of vegetables on the acid-base control of patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD [7–9]. One randomized controlled trial involving patients with stage 3 CKD in the US showed that total CO2 levels increased in the group with increased fruit and vegetable intakes, while total CO2 levels decreased in the control group [7]. One cross-sectional study of 100 patients with stage 3–4 CKD in Brazil showed an association between lower dietary acid load and higher serum bicarbonate levels [8]. Another cross-sectional study of 42 patients with stage 3–5 CKD in the US also yielded similar results [9]. Our study extends these previous findings by showing the association between vegetable intake and lower odds of metabolic acidosis in an Asian CKD population, who likely have a lower risk of metabolic acidosis due to higher vegetable intake and lower meat intake. Collectively, the results from our study and these previous reports suggest that increasing vegetable intake may increase serum bicarbonate levels.

Importantly, our results showed the association between a higher intake of other vegetables, which included cabbage, radishes, and onions, and lower odds of metabolic acidosis. The intakes of other food groups were not significantly associated with metabolic acidosis. Most previous studies on the diet-acidosis relationship measured the dietary acid load of the total diet, which provided little information about which specific foods to eat to reduce the risk of metabolic acidosis [8, 9]. In the randomized controlled trials that showed the efficacy of increasing fruits and vegetables in treating metabolic acidosis, participants were given fruits and vegetables for free to reduce dietary acid load, but the results did not show how much of each food group the participants consumed [6, 7, 10], limiting the applicability of the findings to clinical practice. Our analyses of food group-based intakes and metabolic acidosis suggest the possibility that certain plant foods may be more effective in decreasing the risk of metabolic acidosis than other plant food groups. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm this possibility.

As for food group intake, the median intakes of dairy products, confectioneries, and seasonings were much greater than the national median intakes. Of note, the median intake of seasonings in our study population was more than three times greater than the national median intake. One potential reason may be reduced taste senses of patients with CKD [33, 34], which could have made them use a greater amount of seasonings to improve the palatability of their meals.

One plausible mechanism for the association of higher intakes of pulses, green/yellow vegetables, other vegetables, and dairy products with higher bicarbonate levels is the lower dietary acid load from these food groups. Theoretically, greater intake of cations, including potassium, calcium, and magnesium, and lower intake of protein and phosphorus reduce the acid load [11] and could elevate serum bicarbonate levels in individuals with impaired renal acid excretion [9]. Interestingly, however, only the other vegetable group intake was associated with lower odds of metabolic acidosis. The attenuated associations for pulses and dairy products may be related to smaller differences in PRAL between the high-intake and the low-intake groups. However, the reason for the weaker association in green/yellow vegetables compared to other vegetables could not be explained by PRAL, as the two food groups had similar patterns of association between intake levels and PRAL. While the exact mechanism of the differential findings remains unclear, one plausible mechanism may relate to the fact that the other vegetable group included various cruciferous vegetables, such as cabbage and radishes. Cruciferous vegetables, which have been associated with reduced total and cardiovascular mortality [35], contain glucosinolates, which are hydrolyzed into sulforaphane after ingestion [36]. Previous studies have shown that sulforaphane reduces inflammation and renal fibrosis [12, 13], which may help preserve the kidney’s ability to excrete acids and reduce the risk of metabolic acidosis. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to elucidate the effect of different types of vegetables on the acid-base balance of patients with CKD.

The positive association between dairy intake and serum bicarbonate levels could be explained by the lower PRAL associated with higher dairy intake. The high calcium content of dairy products could have contributed to lower PRAL. Although the association between fruit intake and serum bicarbonate levels did not reach statistical significance, the P value slightly exceeded 0.05, indicating a suggestive association. The trend could be partly explained by the lower PRAL of the high fruit intake group.

Our findings have important implications for research and clinical practice. First, our results inform future interventional studies that examine whether increasing the intake of the other vegetable group, such as cabbage, radishes, and onions, helps improve the acid-base management of patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD. While vegetables have traditionally been restricted in this population to prevent hyperkalemia [37, 38], an increasing number of studies have documented weak or no associations between plant food intake and hyperkalemia in patients with CKD [17, 39, 40], and current guidelines recommend plant-based diets and set no upper limit for potassium intake [1]. Consistent with these prior reports, our analyses showed that patients with greater intakes of other vegetables had serum potassium levels similar to those with lower intakes. Additionally, we previously demonstrated a lack of association between potassium intake from other vegetables and hyperkalemia, while potassium intake from potatoes was associated with hyperkalemia [17]. Thus, different plant food groups may not be equal regarding their effects on serum potassium and bicarbonate levels. Further prospective studies are warranted to develop effective dietary therapy that would help improve the acid-base balance, control serum potassium levels, and maintain fiber intake, which could decrease the risk of CVD and mortality in patients with CKD [41–44].

A major strength of this study is the use of food group-based intakes, which may help ease the translation of our findings to clinical practice. While several previous studies suggested the potential benefit of fruits and vegetables in the acid-base management of patients with CKD, few quantified intakes by individual food groups and examined their associations with metabolic acidosis [7–9]. Other strengths of this study include the use of a validated diet history questionnaire [21], energy adjustment of food intakes to decrease measurement errors [26], and the exclusion of implausible energy reporters [20]. Additionally, our sample size had adequate statistical power to detect a significant association between the intake of other vegetables and metabolic acidosis.

Our study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes us from establishing a causal relationship between the intakes of certain food groups and serum bicarbonate levels or metabolic acidosis. While the possibility of reverse causality exists (i.e., patients increasing their intake of vegetables after being told they have metabolic acidosis), we consider this possibility less likely because general dietary advice for patients with CKD in Japan during the study period did not recommend increasing fruits and vegetables for metabolic acidosis management owing to concern over hyperkalemia from consuming potassium-rich foods [45]. Moreover, we cannot completely exclude the effect of unmeasured and residual confounding. Second, because the dietary data were based on self-report, we cannot exclude the possibility of recall bias and misreporting of food intake [46]. Previous studies showed underreporting of food intake in patients with CKD [46, 47]. We strove to reduce the effect of misreporting by excluding patients with implausible energy intake from our analyses. Third, our findings may not be generalizable to patients with undiagnosed CKD who do not have regular visits to nephrologists. The association between food group intake and metabolic acidosis could be different in undiagnosed CKD populations because our study participants had likely been given advice on protein restriction. Moreover, the generalizability of our findings to non-Japanese CKD populations is uncertain due to differences in dietary habits and genetic backgrounds [48–50]. However, the average protein intake, an important contributor to endogenous acid production, and the mean serum bicarbonate level in this study were comparable to those of previous studies on patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD [9, 51, 52], suggesting relevance to some CKD populations. Additionally, the proportion of patients with diabetic nephropathy was lower than that of stage 5 CKD in a large-scale registry study of non-dialysis-dependent CKD patients in Japan [53], suggesting uncertain generalizability to advanced CKD populations. Fourth, the diagnosis of metabolic acidosis was based on a single measurement of serum bicarbonate. As a result, we were unable to examine the association of food group intake with persistent metabolic acidosis, suggesting the need for further studies with multiple measurements of serum bicarbonate levels. Lastly, the food items for vegetables in the BDHQ did not allow us to differentiate the intake of cruciferous vegetables from non-cruciferous vegetables specifically. Therefore, we were uncertain whether the potential benefit of the other vegetable group could be attributed to cruciferous vegetables, calling for further studies on the effect of specific types of vegetables on metabolic acidosis.

We found positive associations of the intakes of pulses, green/yellow vegetables, other vegetables, and dairy products with serum bicarbonate levels in patients with non-dialysis-dependent CKD. Only a greater intake of other vegetables was associated with lower odds of metabolic acidosis. Our findings call for further studies to examine whether certain vegetables have more favorable effects on the acid-base balance of patients with CKD.
