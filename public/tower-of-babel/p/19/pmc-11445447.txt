Lipase activated endocytosis-like behavior of oil-in-water emulsion
Authors: Youping Lin, Haixu Chen, Lei Wang, Jiaojiao Su, Junbo Li, Xin Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOil-in-water emulsion is a system with extensive applications in foods, cosmetics and coating industries, and it could also be designed into an artificial lipid droplet in recent works. However, the insights into the biophysical dynamic behaviors of such artificial lipid droplets are lacking. Here, we reveal an enzymatic reaction triggered endocytosis-like behavior in the oil-in-water emulsion lipid droplets. A thermodynamically favored recruitment of lipases onto the membrane of the droplets is demonstrated. We confirm that the hydrolysis of tributyrin by lipases can decrease the interfacial tension and increase the compressive force on the membrane, which are the two main driving forces for triggering the endocytosis-like behavior. The endocytosis-like behavior induced various emerging functionalities of the lipid droplets, including proteins, DNA or inorganic particles being efficiently sequestered into the oil droplet with reversible release as well as enhanced cascade enzymatic reaction. Overall, our studies are expected to open up a way to functionalize oil-in-water emulsions capable of life-inspired behaviors and tackle emerging challenges in bottom-up synthetic biology, revealing the unknown dynamic behaviors of lipid droplets in living organisms.Subject terms: Biophysical chemistry, Endocytosis

Introduction

Recently, bottom-up synthetic biology has emerged as a new strategy for studying the organization, function, and physicochemical behavior of living organisms1–6. By spontaneous self-assembly and integration of biomolecular building blocks, various synthetic compartmentalized systems have been created, which have been proven to be an effective approach to the design and construct the rudimentary artificial cell-like ensembles with life-like functionalities. As typical artificial cell models, the synthetic aqueous microcompartments, including liposomes7–10, polymersomes11,12, proteinosomes13–16, colloidosome17, and membrane-free liquid microdroplets (coacervate)18–22 successfully showed a wide range of biomimetic behaviors and functionalities, which include the physical and chemical basis of enzymatic catalysis in confined environments23–25, encapsulated gene-mediated cell-free protein expression26,27, selective membrane gating28, reproduction9, fusion29, division30,31, phagocytosis32, predatory33, endocytosis34, mobility35–38, etc.39–43. These studies, along with diverse investigations on alternative water-based artificial cell models, have provided a fruitful platform on the integration of functional components into individual aqueous micro-compartmentalized constructs. In contrast, given the heterogenous phases behaviors inside living organisms, like the triacylglycerol enriched lipid droplet organelles, the creation of the oil-in-water-based microcompartmental system as an artificial organelle population with life-inspired dynamic behavior and functionality has received little attention.

Lipid droplets (LDs) have a neutral lipid core shielded from the aqueous environment by a phospholipid monolayer embedded with proteins44–48. For a long time, the LDs had been recognized as intracellular oil-in-water emulsion droplets, inert existing in almost all organisms as reservoirs of lipid47,49–52. Recently, the LDs biology reveals that the LDs are playing diverse biological functions53, which are closely connected to myriad physiological processes and metabolic diseases51,52 including atherosclerosis54, obesity and diabetes55, etc.49,56–58. Despite these important cell biological properties and functions of LDs, surprisingly, little is known about the biophysical details of the processes underlying their biology45,46,50. For example, the enzymes related to LDs, which are directly involved in lipid metabolism, have a variety of cell functions49,58,59, however, the way of these enzymes located to LDs is not fully understood60,61. Moreover, some water-soluble substances, including proteins and organelles, have also been found inside LDs62,63. However, how these substances being loaded inside the lipid droplets has not been known64. Therefore, for many of these and other biological processes involving LDs, understanding their unique behavior as defined by biophysical principles is essential48,65,66. In this regard, concepts in emulsion science should be highly relevant to understand LDs cell biology67–69. Nevertheless, few paradigm has been well integrated into this field.

In this study, toward addressing the above challenges, we construct artificial LDs via the spontaneous interfacial self-assembly of amphipathic phospholipid (1, 2-dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphatidylcholine (DMPC)) and protein (bovine serum albumin (BSA)) around tributyrin/2-ethylhexyl acetate in aqueous solution. Among them, DMPC acts as one of the components of the phospholipid membrane of intracellular LDs, and BSA is used to stabilize LDs and mimic perilipin, and tributyrin/2-ethylhexyl acetate are used as the neutral lipid of the simulated oil core. Then we demonstrate the phagocytose of various biomacromolecules in the LDs community, which is triggered by the lipase-based hydrolysis reaction (Fig. 1). Accordingly, the substance transportation between the hydrophobic core of the synthetic LDs and outer aqueous solution is achieved. Specially, upon endocytosis-like behavior of glucose oxidase (GOx) and horseradish peroxidase (HRP) into the LDs, we show that an accelerated cascade enzymatic reaction could happen inside the bulk lipid phase. Overall, our results take a step towards the design and engineering of a synthetic LD community capable of having a primitive form of endocytosis-inspired behavior, which is anticipated to offer an approach to realize the functionality of oil-in-water emulsion with higher-order life-inspired behavior.

Results

Spontaneous assembly of BSA/lipases onto the membrane of synthetic lipid droplets

Lipid droplets (LDs) were prepared by Pickering emulsion with a mixture of tributyrin/2-ethylhexyl acetate (4/1, v/v, 20 μL) and 200 μL aqueous solution containing DMPC (0.5 mg mL−1) and BSA (2.0 mg mL−1). The synthetic LDs exhibited a clear oil-in-water structure by staining the tributyrin phase with Nile red (red fluorescence) and the aqueous phase with calcein (green fluorescence) (Fig. 2a). By labeling BSA with rhodamine B isothiocyanate (RBITC-BSA, red fluorescence), the successful co-assembly of BSA into the DMPC layer (stained with NBD-PE, green fluorescence) was confirmed by appearing an obvious yellow fluorescent ring around the droplets (Fig. 2b). The interfacial tension gradually decreased due to BSA assembly on the surface of artificial LDs (Fig. 2c), where an obvious decrease in interfacial tension was initially observed when the concentration of BSA was below 0.05 mg mL−1, which was also well fitted with the Langmuir–Szyszkowski model (Fig. S3) and followed by a gradual equilibrium. The assembled BSA in the membrane could still maintain mobility accompanied by a concentration-dependent lessening diffusion coefficient from 1.81 × 10−7 to 4.24 × 10−12 cm2 s−1 with increasing concentration of BSA from 0.002 to 2 mg mL−1 in the solution (Fig. 2d). Such concentration-dependent diffusion coefficient of BSA in the membrane would then provide a prerequisite for the following construction of dynamic behaviors of LDs. In addition, by calculating the free energy of the adsorption of BSA onto the oil-water interface, it showed that the interface binding energy of BSA binding onto the interface between tributyrin and water, which was much higher than the kinetic energy of 10 kBT (Supplementary Table S4), resulting in the high stability of the formed DMPC/BSA LDs for at least a month (Fig. S7).

To be of great interest, the co-assembly of lipase onto the membrane of constructed LDs widely occurred, due to the hydrophobic interaction between the lipases and tributyrin70,71. By labeling lipases with Atto 425-NHS (Atto 425-Lipase, blue fluorescence), the successful embedding of lipases onto the membrane was clearly observed by the enhancement of the blue fluorescence intensity around the LDs (Fig. 2e). Moreover, the whole assembly procedure was monitored by the time evolution of interfacial tension. When the lipase (2 mg mL−1, 200 μL) was sequentially added into the system, with the lipase assembly into the membrane of the LDs, the interfacial tension could gradually decrease from 11.7 to 9.0 mN m−1 (Fig. 2f, g), also indicating the assembly of lipase into the interface was a thermodynamically favored spontaneous procedure. Such observations were also consistent with the calculated interface binding energy value of the lipase on the interface 29.4 kBT, which was much higher than that of BSA 10.6 KBT (Supplementary Table S4). After the co-assembly of lipases into the membrane of the LDs, the building blocks aggregated more tightly in the membrane as determined that the diffusion coefficient of BSA in the membrane was reduced by an order of magnitude from 4.24 × 10−12 to 3.26 × 10−13 cm2 s−1 (Fig. 2h), which indicated that the co-assembly of lipases would also enhance the structural stability.

The high stability of lipase binding onto the interface between oil and water was investigated by volume shrinkage of the tributyrin pendant drop, which was realized by an optical surface analyzer (See method 1.2.8 for details). A compressive force was imposed by reducing the volume of the droplet at the rate of 50 μL min−1. The wrinkles appeared on the surface of the tributyrin pendant drop with increased residual volume when the lipase concentration in the solution varied from 0.001 to 5 mg mL−1 (Figs. S8, S9). The appearance of wrinkles during shrinkage indicates that the firm adsorption of lipase on the droplet surface leads to the appearance of nonequilibrium wrinkled states. Furthermore, the tributyrin pendant drop (50 μL initially, [lipase] = 5 μg mL−1) oscillated five times in volume from 50 to 5 μL due to external oscillatory compressive forces, the interfacial tension of the lipase hybrid LDs could be well maintained around 14.5 mN m−1 each time after the volume of droplets restored to 50 μL, which indicated the lipase didn’t detach from the interface (Fig. 2i). In contrast, without lipases, the persistent increase of the interfacial tension of BSA/DMPC from 10.9 to 15.7 mN m−1 was observed in each oscillation, which indicated BSA detached from the interface during shrinking and recovering processes after the volume changed back to 50 μL (Fig. S10). Overall, the volume shrinkage and oscillation experiments demonstrated the high affinity and stability of lipases at the oil-water interface.

Lipase-activated endocytic behavior in synthetic LDs community

Lipase has been recognized to be an important enzyme for the regulation of lipolysis within adipose cells, which could hydrolyze lipids, such as tributyrin, into glycerol and butyric acid. Accordingly, the entrapped lipases at the interface of the LDs would accelerate the hydrolysis of the tributyrin accompanied by the shrinking of the LDs (Fig. S11), which then triggered the occurrence of endocytosis-like behavior. By labeling BSA with RBITC (red fluorescence) and lipases with Atto 425-NHS ester (blue fluorescence), the endocytosis-like behavior of calcein (green fluorescence) and lipases (blue fluorescence) from the outer aqueous solution into the LDs was clearly observed by the co-appearance of green and blue fluorescence in the core domain of the LDs (Fig. 3a, b and Fig. S12). The whole endocytosis-like behavior process was fast and lasted 15 min until the entrapped substances reached the plateau in the core of the LDs (Fig. 3c). In contrast, without lipases, no detectable fluorescence was observed in the core of the LDs (Fig. S13). Moreover, the endocytosis-like behavior procedure was also investigated by flow cytometer based on the fluorescence intensity change of the LDs. As shown in 2D dot plots (Fig. 3d), the dots of the LDs community gradually shifted to the area of higher red fluorescence intensity after adding lipases. The enhancement of the statistics of red fluorescence intensity and side scatter further showed the successful endocytosis-like behavior of the RBITC-BSA into the LDs community (Fig. 3e).

To confirm the formation of inner water-in-oil nanodroplets, the dSTORM technique with higher lateral resolution was specially used. By labeling BSA and lipases by NHS-Cy5 (red fluorescence) and FITC (green fluorescence), respectively, the phagotrophic lipases and BSA were in the form of water-in-oil nanodroplets with sizes ranging from 100 to 200 nm distributed in the core of LDs (Fig. 3f, g), which was also consistent with the direct measurement of the collected lipid phase by the DLS measurements (Fig. S14). The inner nanodroplets were obtained by centrifugal demulsification followed by freeze-drying. The SEM image demonstrated the existence of nanodroplets (Fig. 3h). Furthermore, LDs containing endocytic nanodroplets were visually characterized by cryo-TEM (Fig. 3i). To further verify the position of the BSA and lipases in the endocytic nanodroplets, RBITC-BSA was utilized as fluorescent probe molecule, since it undergone a π → π* transition (K band) when it was excited, which would be affected by the polarity of the solvent. As the solvent polarity increased, it had a greater stabilizing effect on the excited state than the ground state. Therefore, the UV-Vis and fluorescence spectrum would move to a lower wavelength (blue-shift) as the solvent polarity decreased. Aqueous and oil phase with BSA and lipase were obtained by demulsifying the BSA-coated (2 mg mL−1) LDs (ϕw = 1), which were incubated at 25 °C for 2 h after the addition of lipase (2 mg mL−1). As shown in Fig. 3j, RBITC-BSA showed a significant blue-shift of UV-Vis maximum absorption peak from 558 nm in the aqueous phase to 552 nm in the oil phase and a blue-shift of fluorescence maximum emission peak from 588 nm in aqueous phase to 575.5 nm in oil phase, which illustrated that the RBITC-BSA should be placed in a more hydrophobic environment, i.e. on the membrane of endocytic water-in-oil nanodroplet. Similarly, for RBITC-lipase, there was also a blue-shift in the UV-Vis and fluorescence spectrum, which indicated that the RBITC-lipase was also located in the membrane of the endocytic nanodroplet. In addition, by labeling lipases and BSA with FITC and RBITC, respectively, both two proteins located in the membrane of endocytic nanodroplet was also confirmed by the appearance of the fluorescence resonance energy transfer between lipase-FITC and RBITC-BSA (Fig. S15).

Mechanism of endocytosis-like behavior

The spontaneous formation of inner water-in-oil nanodroplets was considered a self-emulsification phenomenon. Conventional self-emulsification was usually performed at the interface of incompatible bulk phases or by splitting large micron droplets into submicron droplets72–74. In this case, the new emulsion was formed in the interior of the original microdroplets, which has not been reported and needs further exploration. We attributed that lipase-catalyzed hydrolysis product butyric acid could bind the surface of BSA or lipases, thus decreasing the interfacial tension and the lipase-catalytic reaction-induced shrinking of the LDs which would result in the increase of the compressive force in the membrane, which were the two main driving forces to trigger the happening of endocytosis-like behavior. The necessity of butyric acid in endocytosis-like behavior was demonstrated by adding butyric acid and glycerol to the outer solution of RBITC-BSA and DMPC stabilized LDs in the absence of lipase. Glycerol could not induce any endocytosis-like behavior in 2 h, while butyric acid-induced obvious endocytosis-like behavior in 20 min, which demonstrated that butyric acid was the prerequisite for endocytosis-like behavior (Fig. S16). The study of isothermal titration calorimetry showed that the binding of butyric acid to the BSA via the electrostatic interaction was spontaneous and enthalpy-driven (Fig. S17). Furthermore, adding salt to the outer solution of LDs can also slow down the rate of endocytosis-like behavior, which demonstrated the electrostatic interaction between BSA and butyric acid played a key role in endocytosis (Fig. S18). With the binding of butyric acid onto BSA and exposing the hydrophobic chain of butyric acid to the solution, the increased cross-sectional area of the formed complex at the interface (Fig. S19) would then result in the change of the surface activity. As shown in Fig. 4a, the surface tension decreased at the beginning and then reached equilibrium when the concentration of butyric acid was above 500 mM. Although the formed BSA-butyric acid complex behaved similarly to butyric acid, it showed smaller cmc (25 mM) and Γmax (40.6 mN m-1) at the air–water interface (Fig. 4b). Moreover, in comparison with butyric acid (15.3 mN m−1) and BSA (11.7 mN m−1), BSA-butyric acid complex obviously reduced interfacial tension between tributyrin and aqueous solution to be 9.8 mN m−1 (Fig. 4c, d). Therefore, the decreased interfacial tension of BSA-butyric acid complex around the interface of an oil droplet in water would boost the formation of the water-in-oil droplets, especially given that the contact angle θw of BSA on the oil droplet and water interface measured in the water phase increased over 90° after the formation of BSA-butyric acid complex (Fig. 4e, Supplementary Theory and analysis 2.1). The difference in interfacial tension between BSA/butyric acid membrane-water and membrane-oil was 3.36 mN m−1, which was significantly lower than the measured interfacial tension between tributyrin droplet and aqueous solution of BSA-butyric acid (9.8 mN m−1). Therefore, the interfacial tension between the BSA-based membrane and oil was higher than that between membrane and water, leading to the bending of the interface toward the oil phase to reduce the energy of the system.

Interestingly, due to the high absorbance energy of both BSA and lipases at the interface of oil and water (EBSA, Elipase = 13.5, 61.9 kBT, respectively), it was found that during the shrinking of the LDs, most of the building blocks aggregated tightly in the membrane accompanied with the appearance of the wrinkled membrane, rather than being squeezed into the solution (Figs. S8, S20). According to the analysis of interfacial energy changes (Supplementary Method 2.2.1), for those outward-curving domains, the increased interfacial energy of the BSA-butyric acid complex would prompt it into flattening again. In contrast, those inward-curving domains would be stabilized by the BSA-butyric acid complex owing to the decreased interfacial energy. More BSA-butyric acid complex was distributed from the neck domain (curvature >0) to the top domain (curvature <0) of the inward-curving, then the increased line tension of the neck domain led to the inward-curving droplet being separated from the membrane. Moreover, based on the calculation of the ΔG of the endocytic nanodroplets, the formed endocytic nanodroplets was also a thermally favored procedure (Supplementary Table S6).

The positive force signs the force pushing the particle outside the droplet, and the negative force signs the force pulling the particle inside the droplet. For convex surface, i.e. oil-in-water droplet, R>0; for concave surface, i.e., water-in-oil droplet, R < 0 (Fig. 4g). Thus, the BSA-butyric acid complex particle has a negative resultant force Fa=Fp,a+Fγ,a<0 and pulls the particle into the interior of the droplet along with the deformation of the oil-water interface. Alternatively, as shown in Fig. 4g, the angle ω increases from negative to positive during the deformation of the interface until rsinαb=−Rbsinωb, where the radius of curvature for concave surface Rb<0, and αb=θw+ωb changes along with ωb to obey the Young’s equation γso=γsw+γwocosθw. Therefore Fp,b=−Fγ,b, BSA-butyric acid particles stop moving with the equilibrium force, and the oil-water interface stop deforming. Meanwhile, as shown in Fig. 4h, for two particles with same wetting properties, such as two BSA-butyric acid particles, the height of the oil-water interface between the two particles decreases significantly when they are close to each other, so ω∥,a,l>ω∥,a,r and ω∥,b,l<ω∥,b,r. Since FL,l=FL,r=2rγwocosω when φ=π/2, that is FL,a,l<FL,a,r and FL,b,l>FL,b,r, resulting in an attractive interaction between the two particles. While, as for two particles with opposite wetting properties in Fig. 4i, such as BSA and BSA-butyric acid particles, the deformation of the oil-water interface around two particles affect each other and hence, ω∥,a,l<ω∥,a,r and ω∥,b,l>ω∥,b,r. Therefore, FL,a,l>FL,a,r and FL,b,l<FL,b,r, there is a repulsive interaction between the two adjacent particles, which then contributes to the expansion of concave and formation of endocytic nanodroplets.

Endocytosis-like behavior mediated substance trafficking

Despite the universal presence of LDs in almost all living organisms, surprisingly little is known about the functionality of the bulky oily phase besides its storage role towards triacylglycerol or sterols, etc. In this regard, the lipase-mediated endocytosis-like behavior would then provide a pathway to realize the functionalization of the generally recognized “inert” oil-in-water emulsion community with life-inspired characteristics. We specially showed that the oily core of the LDs could be as a reservoir for localizing various biomacromolecules (Fig. 5a). Almost all the aqueous substances ranging from small molecule dyes, various proteins, DNA to tens of nanometers inorganic nanoparticles could be sequestrated into the LDs (Fig. 5b–g). As shown in Fig. 5c, with the addition of 2 mg mL−1 of lipases into the LDs solution, the sequestration of the free RBITC-lysozyme by the LD was clearly observed as increased red fluorescence intensity of the LDs core. The partition coefficients were obtained by calculating the ratio of fluorescence intensity inside and outside the LDs after engulfing processes. It showed the endocytosis-like behavior induced sequestration of the small molecular substances were non-active, such as calcein, with a low partition coefficient ca. 0.8 (Fig. 5h). However, due to the high interfacial adsorption energy of the O/W towards biomacromolecules, the LDs showed an active sequestration towards various biomacromolecules with high partition coefficient (Fig. 5h). For example, by adding 2 mg mL−1 of RBITC-BSA (10 μL) into the solution, nearly all the free RBITC-BSA could be sequestrated into the LDs with the partition coefficient as high as 2.9. Specially, by using positive charged BSA-NH2 as building blocks to fabricate the LDs, the active sequestration toward negatively charged DNA with the partition coefficient of 3.1 was also achieved (Figs. S21, S22).

To be of great interest, disintegrating the complex by heating (Fig. S23), the de-stabilized nanodroplets would release the sequestered substances into the outer solution (Fig. S24). To further modulate such endocytosis-exocytosis cycle in the synthetic LD community, Au nanoparticles was specially used and incorporated into the system (Fig. S25). After the endocytosis-like behavior of the RBITC-BSA, the whole system was exposed to near-infrared light (808 nm, 6 w). The photothermal properties of the gold NPs would break the interaction between the protein and butyric acid, then the successful release of the sequestered substances was realized (Fig. 5i, j and Fig. S26).

Endocytosis-like behavior mediated emerging of biological characteristics in LDs community

The successful sequestration of various biomacromolecules into the oil phase is undoubtedly providing a key step for extending the unknown functionalities of the LDs besides its storage role. In this regard, by selective sequestration of various enzymes into the LDs, a cascaded enzyme reaction, which has been widely studied in aqueous solution, could also happen inside the oily core domain of the LDs, even demonstrating an enhanced enzymatic reaction rate. As shown in Fig. 6a, glucose oxidase (GOx, 1 mg/mL), horseradish peroxidase (HRP, 1 mg/mL), and glucose (1 mg/mL) were added into the aqueous solution of the LDs, and the hydrophobic substrate benzene-1,2-diamine (OPD) was loaded inside the LDs. By adding lipases (1 mg/mL) into the solution, upon the happening of endocytosis-like behavior, the cascaded enzymatic reaction was triggered where the green fluorescence product (2,3-Diaminophenazine, DAP) was observed clearly from the core domains of the LDs (Fig. 6b). The endocytosis-like behavior of both enzymes into the LDs which increased the interfacial contact area between the HRP and the substrate OPD led to an obvious increase on the catalytic reaction rate compared with that of without the addition of lipases in the solution (Fig. 6c). Therefore, it is anticipated these highlighted biology-inspired functionality designs could provide a way to enrich the functionalities of the oil-in-water emulsion community.

Discussion

In conclusion, we propose an approach to generate oil-in-water emulsion by the spontaneous assembly of phospholipid (DMPC) and protein (BSA) at the interface of oil droplets in water, which being a type of artificial LDs, provides a key step towards studying the dynamic behaviors or functionalities of LDs. Specially, we demonstrate that in the presence of lipases, the lipase-based hydrolysis reaction on the interface of the oil and water phase could trigger endocytosis-like behaviors in the synthetic LDs community. We reveal a thermodynamically favored mechanism of endocytosis-like behavior that the hydrolyzed product butyric acid could interact with the entrapped proteins in the membrane, which decreases the interfacial tension in partial domains of the membrane; meanwhile, the lipase-catalytic hydrolysis reaction of tributyrin would also result in the shrinkage of the LDs that increases the compressive force of the building blocks in the membrane, which mainly contribute to triggering the endocytosis-like behaviors. Accordingly, such lipase-mediated endocytosis-like behaviors provide a way to functionalize the inert oil-in-water emulsion. In this regard, we show that the LDs could take a role as a reservoir towards localizing a wide range of biomacromolecules, including various proteins and DNA with high partition coefficient. Specially, when the gold NPs was co-sequestrated into the LDs, the exocytosis could then happen, triggered by near-infrared light accompanied by the release of the engulfed substances.

Endocytosis is an important activity by which living cells could take in cargo from the surroundings. However, given that the LDs is always considered as an inert organelle in the living organism, these highlighted advances such as the delivery and release of water-soluble biomacromolecules and the endocytosis-like behaviors mediated cascaded enzymatic reaction inside the lipid core, undoubtedly provide a key step to reveal their biological functionality as well as the related dynamic process. Moreover, considering the endocytosis-like behaviors induced formation of the water-in-oil nanodroplets with enhanced biphasic enzymatic reaction, it is also anticipated that our study could offer a technique to further engineer the oil-in-water emulsions as functional microscale systems with various dynamic behaviors and then present a paradigm to boost the development of biology-inspired functionalized emulsions, which is now recognized to be a direction towards the construction of o/w biomimetic emulsion system.

Methods

Physical methods

Optical images, videos, and fluorescence images were recorded on a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope. Measurements of the droplet diameters were determined from acquired images using Image J software (Version 1.44p, USA). Confocal fluorescence images were recorded on a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope. Ultraviolet-visible absorption spectrum (UV-vis) were detected on a LAMBDA 750S PerkinElmer UV-Vis spectrophotometer using a 10 mm path length. The fluorescence spectrum was detected by LS55 PerkinElmer fluorescence spectrometers. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum was taken via the attenuated total reflection (ATR) method on PerkinElmer Fourier transform infrared spectrometer. Surface/interfacial tensions and contact angles were determined using an optical surface analyzer (Ningbo NB Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd.). Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) was determined on MicroCal ITC200. DLS (dynamic light scattering) measurements were determined on Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK). The sample was filtered using a 0.22-μm Milli-pore filter before measurement. pH measurements were conducted in triplicate using a Mettler Toledo Seven Compact S210 pH meter. The density of the solution was determined using an electronic balance. Scanning electronic microscopy (SEM) images were recorded on a Hitachi SU-70 Schottky field emission gun SEM (FEG).

Labeling proteins by fluorescence dyes

Lipase/BSA/BSA-NH2/LYZ/GOx/HRP (4.0 mg) was dissolved in 2.0 mL of pH 8.5 sodium carbonate buffer solution (100 mM), and then 50 μL of FITC/DMSO solution (1.0 mg/mL) or 50 μL of aqueous RBITC solution (1.0 mg/mL) or 50 μL of Atto 425-NHS ester/DMSO solution (1.0 mg/mL) or 50 μL of Cy5 NHS ester/DMSO solution (1.0 mg/mL) was added dropwise. The solutions were stirred at room temperature for 5 h, purified by dialyzing against Milli-Q water, and freeze-dried.

Labeling gold nanoparticles by fluorescence dye

Add 150 ml of 2.2 mM sodium citrate solution into a 250 ml of three-neck round bottom flask, and boil the solution for 15 min under stirring. Condenser increase is conducive to inhibiting the evaporation of the solvent. After boiling, inject 1 mL of HAuCl4 (25 mM). The color of the solution changed from yellow to soft pink within 10 min. Cool the container immediately until the temperature of the solution reaches 90 °C. Then, inject 1 mL of sodium citrate (60 mM) and 1 mL of HAuCl4 solution (25 mM) sequentially (once every 2 min). By repeating this process (adding 1 mL of 60 mM sodium citrate and 1 mL of 25 mM HAuCl4 in sequence), up to 14 generations of gradually increasing gold particles can be grown. 2-amino-1-ethanethiol (40 μg) was dissolved in 2.0 mL of pH 8.5 sodium carbonate buffer solution (100 mM), and 200 μL of RBITC aqueous solution (1.0 mg/mL) was added dropwise. The solutions were stirred at room temperature for 20 h. Then, the solution was added dropwise into a stirred solution of gold nanoparticles (4 mL). The mixed solution was stirred at room temperature for 10 h, and was purified by repeated centrifugal washing for three times.

For release of gold nanoparticle experiment, near-infrared light (808 nm, 6 w) was selected to generate the photothermal effect to destroy the interaction of BSA and butyric acid. Although the absorption peak of gold nanoparticles is at 550 nm, according to our previous study, near-infrared light irradiation can still produce sufficient photothermal effect.

Preparation of lipid droplets

Oil-in-water emulsion of lipid droplets was prepared using the Pickering emulsion method. A mixture of tributyrin and 2-ethylhexyl acetate (4:1 v/v, oil phase, 20 µL) was placed in an Eppendorf tube (1.5 mL capacity) followed by addition of 200 µL of an aqueous solution containing building blocks (2.0 mg/mL). The mixture was shaken vigorously by hand for 30 s to produce a turbid oil-in water emulsion. For the experiments in which the membrane structure was observed by confocal microscopy, the lipid droplets were prepared and left for 30 min. After the lipid droplets settled, the supernatant was removed, and an equal volume of water was added.

It should be noted that tributyrin and 2-ethylhexyl acetate were used to mimic the oil phase of real LDs. Besides, to avoid the complete degradation of tributyrin by lipase during observation, 2-ethylhexyl acetate, which could not be hydrolyzed by lipase, was specifically chosen. The principle of selecting the ratio of tributyrin and 2-ethylhexyl acetate was to maintain the density of the oil droplet greater than 1 g/mL for convenient confocal observation.

For flow cytometer experiment, after preparation of lipid droplets, lipase (10 mg/mL, 10 μL) was added. Samples are tested directly at 1, 6, 11, and 18 min after adding lipase. Lipid droplets were only gated on FSC/SSC.

Sample preparation of nanodroplets for SEM

After the preparation of LDs, 200 μL lipase (2 mg mL−1) was sequentially added to generate inner nanodroplets for 30 min. Then, demulsification was performed by centrifugation at 10,000×g. Oil phase containing nanodroplets was obtained. The dry samples of SEM were obtained by freeze-drying for 2 d.

Sample preparation of LDs containing nanodroplets for cryo-TEM

Cryo-TEM images were acquired using the FEI Tecnai Spirit TEM at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. Samples were prepared by dropping 2 µL of LDs on copper grids coated with holey carbon foil. The grids were blotted with filter paper and rapidly frozen in liquid ethane at −178 °C using a Leica GP device. All images were obtained in the bright field at −172 °C.

Assessment of protein hydrophobicity

The hydrophobicity of proteins was determined based on the SDS binding capacity, as follows: SDS was added to 10 mL of 0.1% protein solution and adjusted to 0.07 mM. After standing for 30 min, the SDS-protein mixture was dialyzed against 25-fold volumes of 0.02 M phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) for 24 h. The SDS content of the internal dialysate was determined by the UV-Vis absorption spectrum. Add 10 ml of CHCl3 to 0.5 mL of internal dialysate and mix in a test tube. Then 2.5 mL of 0.0024% methylene blue solution was added to the CHCl3 layer. After mixing in the test tube, the mixture was centrifuged at 2000 g. The absorbance of the SDS-methylene blue mixture in the lower layer was measured at 655 nm. Therefore, the SDS binding capacity was determined and expressed as the micrograms of SDS bound to 500 μg protein.

Determination of surface and interfacial tension

Optical surface analyzer was utilized to determine the surface/interfacial tension. The surface tension was recorded by dropping aqueous solution to be tested into air until the system reached equilibrium. In addition, the volume of the solution was fixed during the test. The surface activity parameters were calculated by the surface tension-concentration curve.

The dynamic interfacial tension and corresponding volume was recorded by immersing a tributyrin droplet (50 μL) into aqueous solution of different blocks until the system reached equilibrium. Then, the volume was reduced at the rate of 50 μL min−1 until the wrinkles appeared on the interface, so the reduction was stopped and the wrinkled droplet relaxed quickly.

About 5 μg mL−1 of lipase solution was chosen to investigate the volume oscillation. Since the adsorption speed of 5 μg mL−1 lipase to the tributyrin droplet/water interface was low, the adsorption of lipase from the solution can be ignored during the oscillation. Lipase-based tributyrin droplet (50 μL initially, [lipase] = 5 μg mL−1) oscillated five times in volume from 50 to 5 μL at the first stage, then oscillated fourteen times in volume from 5 to 100 μL in the second stage, and the dynamic interfacial tension, corresponding volume and video was recorded.

Determination of contact angle

An optical surface analyzer was utilized to determine the contact angle. Samples were prepared by mounting an aqueous solution of BSA (2 mg mL−1, 150 μL), butyric acid (250 mM, 150 μL), or BSA-butyric acid complex (2 mg mL−1 BSA and 250 mM butyric acid incubate for 2 h at room temperature, 150 μL) onto the surface of silica wafers (1 cm × 1 cm) followed by drying. Dropped 2 μL of water or 0.5 μL of oil onto the sample surface, and the contact angle was recorded immediately. It’s worth noting that endocytosis-like behavior happened before the complete hydrolysis of tributyrin. To ensure complete binding between butyric acid and proteins, 250 mM of butyric acid, a value less than the butyric acid produced by the complete hydrolysis of tributyrin while much higher than the concentration of the used proteins (2 mg/mL), was used in this experiment.

Analysis of isothermal titration calorimetry

Drop 200 μL of 40 μM BSA solution into the sample cell, and drop water into the reference cell. Add 2 μL of 10 mM butyric acid solution (0.8 μL for the first time) to the sample solution at a rate of 2 min. Measure the heat change of the system during this process.

Measurement of FTIR spectrum, fluorescence, and UV-Vis spectrum

The FTIR spectrum of samples were taken via attenuated total reflection (ATR) method with a resolution of 4 cm−1 for 400 scans. The spectrum of lipase aqueous solution (10 mg mL−1), oily solution and lipase powder were collected under the same condition. Oil phase with lipase-based endocytic nanodroplets was obtained by demulsifying the lipase-coated (10 mg mL−1) lipid droplets (ϕw = 1), which were incubated at 25 °C for 12 h.

The fluorescence and UV-Vis spectrum were taken via fluorescence spectrometer and UV-Vis spectrophotometer, respectively. Aqueous and oily phase with BSA and lipases were obtained by demulsifying the BSA-based (2 mg mL−1) lipid droplets (ϕw = 1) which were incubated at 25 °C for 2 h after the addition of lipases (2 mg mL−1). Only RBITC-BSA or RBITC-lipase was used to avoid the confusion between two proteins.

Detection of the size distribution of building blocks

DLS (dynamic light scattering) measurement was utilized to detect the size distribution of building blocks via Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern Instruments, UK) at a fixed scattering angle of 173°. The building blocks were dissolved in 5.0 mM PBS buffer solution (pH 6.8) and then filtered by 0.45 μm of Milli-pore filters before measurement.

Direct stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy experiment (dSTORM)

Imaging buffer stock solution was prepared by dissolving 2 g KCl, 2 g KH2PO4, 80 g NaCl, and 21.6 g Na2HPO4·7H2O with H2O to 1 L and adjust to pH 7.4 with NaOH. 50% (w/v) Glucose stock solution was prepared by dissolving 0.5 g of glucose in 1 mL of H2O, then diluted to 10% with imaging buffer stock solution. Oxygen removal stock solution was prepared by mixing 2 mL of 400 mM Tris-HCl solution with pH 7.5, 2 mL of 100 mM KCl solution, 2 mL of 4 mM TCEP with pH 7, 2 mL of 20 mg mL-1 glucose oxidase solution and 2 mL of 1.6 mg mL−1 catalase solution, followed by adding 10 mL of glycerol. Fresh MEA solution with pH 8.5 was prepared by dissolving 0.77 g β-Mercaptoethylamine into 360 mM hydrochloric acid solution to 10 mL.

About 200 μL of DMPC/Cy5 NHS-BSA-based LDs were prepared and added into the mixture of 200 μL of imaging buffer stock, 200 μL of glucose solution, 100 μL of oxygen removal stock solution, 10 μL of MEA solution, 200 μL of 2 mg mL−1 FITC-lipase solution and 90 μL of H2O for 1 min, then washed three times with mixture of 200 μL of imaging buffer stock, 200 μL of glucose solution, 100 μL of oxygen removal stock solution, 10 μL of MEA solution and 490 μL of H2O. LDs were docked by mixing 10 μL of LDs solution with 10 μL of 300 mg mL−1 gelatin solution for 5 minutes. Finally, LDs was observed by Zeiss Elyra P1 super-resolution microscopy.

Cascade enzymatic reaction experiment

O-phenylenediamine (OPD, 10 mg/mL) were dissolved in the oil phase and encapsulated into the LDs. About 10 μL of DMPC/ BSA-based LDs were prepared and added into a mixture of 60 μL PBS (pH 6.5, 50 mM), 10 μL of glucose solution (5 mM), 5 μL of Atto 425-HRP stock solution (7.5 U mL−1), 5 μL of RBITC-GOx stock solution (25 U mL−1) and 10 μL of lipase stock solution (10 mg mL−1). The cascade enzymatic reaction was observed by Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope for 20 min.

Calculation of partition coefficient

The partition coefficient (K) was determined as K = Cinside/Cs, where Cinside and Cs were concentrations of the molecules inside the LDs and solution, respectively. The fluorescence intensity (FI) was proportional to the concentration of molecular. Hence, K can be roughly determined as K = FIinside /FIs.
