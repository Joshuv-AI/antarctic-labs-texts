A self-amplifying responsive nanoplatform for dual inhibition of fibroblast-like synoviocytes in rheumatoid arthritis: coupled regulation of cell cycle arrest and apoptosis
Authors: Huifang Hao, Juan Li, Jian Zhang, Lujie Yu, Mingxin Zhao, Shutong Wu, Xiaorui Song, Weiwei Kang, Weiwei Zhang, Ruiping Zhang, Ziliang Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease that features by the overproliferation of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs). Rapid proliferation and defective apoptosis of FLSs drive the pathogenesis of RA. However, it lacks effective treatment due to monotherapy strategy, adverse effects of drugs, the heterogeneity of FLSs and so on. Herein, a self-amplifying pH-responsive nanoplatform (Mn/Si-Abe@BSA) is developed to induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis for RA treatment. By loading abemaciclib (Abe) in the manganese-doped mesoporous silica nanoparticles (Mn-MSNs) and modifying with bovine serum albumin (BSA), the multifunctional nanoplatform is successfully fabricated with carbonic anhydrase (CA)-mimicking activity. The nanoplatform can effectively accumulate in the inflammed joints, after which the nanoplatform is in response to the mild acidic microenvironment to release Mn2+ and Abe, enhanced by the hydrogen protons (H+) deprived from CA-mimicking catalysis. The released Mn2+ induces arginine deprivation that cooperates with Abe to induce cell cycle arrest of FLSs. Moreover, Mn2+ catalyzes the generation of toxic hydroxyl radical (·OH) to synergize upregulated p53 to kill abnormal FLSs. In summary, a “dual pronged attack” strategy is proposed by the multifunctional nanoplatform Mn/Si-Abe@BSA with novel enzyme-mimic activities, representing a promising platform for the comprehensive management of RA.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04585-x.Keywords: Fibroblast-like synoviocytes, Self-amplifying nanoplatform, Dual inhibition, Cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, Rheumatoid arthritis

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic systemic autoimmune disease characterized by persistent synovial inflammation, progressive cartilage degradation and bone erosion, which can ultimately lead to irreversible disability and significantly decrease patient quality of life. While the precise etiology and its exact pathological mechanisms are not completely understood, previous studies have demonstrated that fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLSs), as the predominant resident cells, play a critical role in the initiation and progression of the disease by producing diverse mediators. FLSs are highly specialized mesenchymal cells and located mostly in the synovial intimal lining, the primary site of inflammation. During the pathogenesis of RA, under the consistent stimulation of chronic inflammation in synovial tissues, FLSs are activated and converted into an aggressive, inflammatory and hyperplastic phenotype, resembling tumor cells. Activated FLSs are highly able to secret a large amount of pathogenic mediators including pro-inflammatory cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and chemokines, which can recruit and activate the other effectors immune cells in the synovium of RA, thereby leading to synovitis, pannus formation and finally the deterioration of cartilage matrix and destruction of bone. Given the pivotal role of FLSs in RA progression, strategies aimed at suppressing aberrant FLSs proliferation have emerged as a key therapeutic approach for RA treatment [1–3].

Reports showed that untrollable proliferation and insufficient apoptosis of FLSs are the main causes of the aberrant proliferation of FLSs in RA. The imbalance promotes synovial hyperplasia, which, together with the chronic inflammatory environment, ultimately leads to the destruction of joints in RA. Thus, inhibiting proliferation and promoting apoptosis of FLSs are the primary focus of currrent studies surrounding RA treatment. The cell cycle serves as the core regulatory mechanism governing cellular proliferation. The mammalian cell cycle comprises four sequential stages, a growth phase (G0/G1), a DNA synthesis phase (S), a second growth phase (G2), and finally mitosis (M). Cell progression through these stages is orchestrated by cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) and their regulatory cyclin subunits, which are pivotal in catalyzing the transitions into S phase and mitosis. Among these stages, G1 phase is primarily characterizad by cellular growth and the synthesis and assembly of proteins essentisl for subsequent DNA replication in S phase. Studies have shown that during G1 phase progression, cells acutely sense all sorts of envirommental stimuli such as energy state, nutrients status, DNA damage and so on to determine cell fate on whether to commit to proliferation or block in G1 phase to repair or induce apoptosis, accompanied by the changes of the expression of p53 [4]. Therefore, G1 phase is widely recognized as one of the most environmentally sensitive stages of the cell cycle, making it the prime target for cell cycle regulation to inhibit FLSs proliferation for RA treatment. For example, Lu et al. reported that the arrest of cell cycle in G1 phase induced by L-arginine deprivation effectively reduced RA FLSs growth and suppressed arthritis development, potentially through p53-p21 pathway activation [5, 6]. Similarly, CDK inhibitors (e.g. palbociclib, a CDK4/6 kinase inhibitor), have shown efficacy in ameliorating arthritis via arresting G1 phase progression in FLSs [7]. Consequently, inducing cell cycle arrest in G1 phase of FLSs may represent a promising option for RA treatment strategies. However, the limitations of drug therapy and the reversible nature of growth arrest in FLSs fail to achieve the comprehensive elimination of pathogenic FLSs, allowing residual FLSs to drive disease progression. Based on this, novel strategies are needed for effective RA treatment to permanently eliminate abnormal FLSs through induction of apoptosisa. Fortunately, there have been studies reported that the strategy has been successfully applied in RA therapy via inducing cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in FLSs to inhibit FLSs proliferation. For instance, Bai et al. have presented that pristmerin inhibited the excessive proliferation of FLSs through G1 phase cell cycle arrest and modulation of the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway to achieve anti-RA efficacy [8]. Likewise, Zhang et al. reported that the therapeutic potential of rapamycin for addressing the abnormal proliferation of RA through comparable mechanisms [9]. However, the side effects, nonspecific biodistribution and poor efficacy of drugs may influence their clinical application in patients with RA. There is an urgent need to develop a nanomedical drug delivery system to overcome the above challenges and precisely modulate the aberrant proliferation of FLSs.

In this work, a self-amplifying pH-responsive nanoplatform was designed by Mn-MSNs integrated the CDK4/6 inhibitor Abe for RA theranostic (Scheme 1). Upon intravenous injection, the nanoplatform exhibited rapid accumulation in inflamed joints through the synergistic action of the inherent high targeting affinity of BSA and ELVIS (extravasation through leaky vasculature and inflammatory cell-mediated sequestration) effect [10]. The nanoplatform could respond to the mild acidic environment and subsequently degrade to release Mn2+ and Abe, accelerated by the intrinsic CA-mimicking activity. Subsequently, the released Mn2+ can induce arginine deprivation by arginase (ARG)-like activity and the enhanced enzyme activity of intracellular ARG in concert with Abe to inhibit CDK4/6-cyclin D1 complex for potent G1 phase arrest of FLSs. Meanwhile, Mn2+ can also catalyze endogenous H2O2 via Fenton-like reaction to generate ·OH that cooperate with upregulated p53 to destruct mitochondria, thereby promoting FLSs apoptosis. Therefore, effective cell cycle arrest and concomitant apoptosis in FLSs were successfully induced via multiple pathways. The self-amplifying pH-responsive nanoplatform Mn/Si-Abe@BSA presents a promising therapeutic strategy to inhibit FLSs proliferation for the effective management of RA.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

In this study, Mn-MSNs were first synthesized following a literature procedure with modifications. According to transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 1A and B), the as prepared Mn-MSNs were monodispersed with an urchin-like nanoflower structure and a uniform diameter of around 131.27 nm. To further determine the element composition and distribution of Mn-MSNs, area elemental mapping scanning and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was carried out to show the uniform distribution of Si, O, Mn in Fig. 1C. The results revealed a homogeneous distribution of Mn element throughout the nanoparticles with a rather high doping concentration. To evidence the mesoporous structure of Mn-MSNs, N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution analysis were further performed. As shown in Fig. 1D, the N2 adsorption-desorption patterns of Mn-MSNs showed a characteristic type IV isotherm as defined by the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) with an average pore diameter of 10.05 nm. The specific surface area of Mn-MSNs was 493.9926 m2/g, which was essential to enhance the loading capacity for drugs and sustained/controlled release potential. The results of X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern analysis in Figure S1 manifested the weak crystallinity and the amorphous structure of Mn-MSNs, which favored the framework reactive for degradation in a biological environment [11]. Furthermore, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed to investigate the elemental composition and chemical states of Mn-MSNs. As depicted in Fig. 1E, the Mn 2p XPS spectra of Mn-MSNs exhibited two characteristic peaks at 640.98 and 653.28 eV, corresponding to the binding energies of Mn 2p3/2 and Mn 2p1/2 of Mn2+ respectively [10, 12].

Based on the above results, Mn-MSNs were used as nanocarriers of the insoluble drug Abe due to the large pore and unique mesoporous structure to explore their applications in the treatment of RA. The UV–vis absorbance spectrum of Abe loaded Mn-MSNs (Mn/Si-Abe) were recorded. It was evident that Mn/Si-Abe had the characteristic peaks of Abe at 298 nm, indicating the successful loading of Abe in the Mn-MSNs (Fig. 1F) [13]. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra shown in Fig. 1G also supported the effective loading of Abe in the Mn-MSNs. Compared with the FT-IR spectrum of Mn-MSNs, the additional peaks appeared at 1435 cm− 1 caused by the existence of Abe [14]. It was also noted that the absorption peak at 1089.39 cm− 1 was attributable to the stretching vibration of –Si–O– bond in the spectrum of Mn/Si and Mn/Si-Abe. The adsorption peak at 800.98 cm− 1 and 466.88 cm− 1 resulted from the symmetric stretching vibration and bending vibration of -Si-O-Si-, respectively. Meanwhile, bending vibration and asymmetric stretching vibration of Si-OH occurred at 969.46 cm− 1 and 3445.22 cm− 1, respectively [15]. All the above results proved the successful synthesis of Mn/Si-Abe.

To further improve the dispersity and achieve active targeting function of Mn/Si-Abe, BSA was thus introduced to the surface of Mn/Si-Abe to obtain Mn/Si-Abe@BSA [16]. The success of surface modification of BSA could be proved by zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameters analysis. As shown in Fig. 1H, Mn/Si-Abe exhibited a negative charge attributed to the presence of abundant silicon hydroxyl groups on the surface, and its zeta potential was −21.92 ± 1.81 mV. Following surface functionalization with APTES, the zeta potential reversed to a positive value of 27.27 ± 0.56 mV, which was attributable to the introduced amino groups on the surface and also indicated the successful preparation of Mn/Si-Abe-NH2. After the coating of BSA, the zeta potential of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA dramatically decreased to −25.05 ± 1.57 mV, which was due to the significant reduction of the number of amino groups on the surface of the nanoplatform by the amidation reaction between the amino group in Mn/Si-Abe-NH2 amides and the carboxyl group in BSA [17, 18]. Meanwhile, the hydrodynamic diameters of different samples were slightly increasing with the modification of the functional groups (Fig. 1I). Combined with the above results, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was successfully fabricated with good biocompatibility.

CA-mimicking activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA

As previous studies reported, the Mn-O bond produced by Mn doping to MSNs is more sensitive and undergo facile degradation in a mildly acidic environment due to the changes in the structural framework of MSNs, which is fortunately the characteristic feature of RA microenvironment compared to normal tissues [19, 20]. Therefore, once the nanoplatform was exposed to the weakly acidic RA microenvironment, given the lower energy of the Mn-O bond compared to the Si-O bond, the H+ infiltration into the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA framework preferentially initiated the cleavage of Mn-O bonds, whereby Mn2+ was substituted by H+. The substitution triggered the release of Mn2+ and Abe, alongside the formation of new Si-OH groups and numerous defects, which further accelerated the degradation of SiO2 framework [21]. Thus, the degradation rate of the SiO2 framework exhibited a positive correlation with microenvironmental acidity, demonstrating accelerated breakdown under increasingly acidic conditions. It is well known that CA, a zinc-containing metalloenzyme, acts as a catalyst for the reversible hydration of CO2 to bicarbonate (HCO3ˉ) and H+, which could modulate the acidity of microenvironment. Based on the available information from the literature, ordered mesoporous materials and zeolite were good candidates for the design and development of catalysts for CO2 conversion due to their high chemical stability, high surface areas and large pore dimensions [22, 23]. Additionally, recent researchers have successfully developed transition metal-based nanomaterials exhibiting CA-mimicking activity [24]. Therefore, the CA-mimicking activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was evaluated using a commercial assay kit based on the hydrolysis of p-nitrophenyl acetate (p-NPA) as a chromogenic reaction. The resulting product, p-nitrophenol (p-NP), was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 405 nm using a UV spectrophotometer. As displayed in Fig. 2A, the absorbance at 405 nm exhibited a progressive increase with prolonged reaction time, confirming that the significant CA-mimicking activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. In addition, pH change could serve as an indicator for evaluating the CO2 capture capacity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. Compared to the blank control, the pH value of H2O + CO2 and Si-Abe@BSA + CO2 groups slightly decreased, as a result of the reaction between CO2 and H2O. Notably, the pH value of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA + CO2 group dropped significantly, indicating a marked acceleration in CO2 conversion attributed to the CA-mimetic activity of the Mn-doped nanoplatform (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the hydration product HCO3ˉ during catalysis was monitored via CaCO3 precipitation following the addition of CaCl2, providing a quantitative measure of the CA-mimicking activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. As shown in the bright field images of Fig. 2C, the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA + CO2 group revealed much more CaCO3 precipitate than the other groups, confirming its superior CA-mimicking activity. In summary, the experimental evidence collectively demonstrated that the CA-mimicking activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA, further promoting RA microenvironment acidification and thereby accelerating the degradation of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA.

Self-amplifying responsive degradation and controlled release of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA

To demonstrate the degradability of the nanoplatform, the degradation process of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was monitored via TEM images under a simulated physiological or pathological microenvironment, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2D, the morphological structures of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA still kept integrity or slightly degraded after being treated in PBS at pH 7.4 for 8 h. In comparison, the nanoplatform exhibited relatively significant degradation behavior under mild acidic conditions (pH = 6.5) during the same period. These results confirmed its high sensitivity to acidic environment, which triggered its decomposition and the consequent release of Mn2+ and Abe, thereby minimizing the side effects in neutral normal tissues. As expected, the nanoplatform exhibited a stronger time-dependent degradation-promoting effect within 8 h and even completely lost their original morphology following 8 h incubation under CO2 saturation at pH 6.5 (Fig. 2E), which could be attributed to the increase in H+ concentration in PBS mediated by CA-catalyzed reactions [11, 25–28]. The degradation behavior of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was also characterized by DLS to monitor changes in hydrodynamic size after incubation in different conditions (pH 7.4, 6.5, and 6.5 + CO2) for 8 h. The complementary results were consistent with the TEM observations and provided additional evidence supporting the self-amplifying pH-responsive degradation profile of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA (Figure S2). Driven by the self-amplifying acid-responsive degradation of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA, the in vitro release profiles of Mn2+ and Abe were systematically evaluated under different pH (7.4 or 6.5) and CO2 concentration (presence or absence) conditions. As presented in Fig. 2F and G, few Mn2+ and Abe was released from Mn/Si-Abe@BSA under neutral conditions (pH = 7.4, CO2-free) during 8 h, demonstrating that the nanoplatform was stable in physiological environment. Comparatively, the release rates of Mn2+ and Abe reached 80% under acidic RA microenvironment conditions (pH = 6.5) at the same time point and further exceeded 95% following CO2 aeration. These findings further suggested that the Mn-O bonds of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA were susceptible to mildly acidic microenvironment that caused its degradation and enabled the release of Mn2+ and Abe, which could be enhanced through additional H+ generation via CA catalysis. Based on these results, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA exhibited excellent precisely controlled drugs delivery capability, minimizing off-target toxicity in normal tissues while ensuring targeted accumulation in the articular lesion site of RA. The specific degradation of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in low pH microenvironment not only enabled the substantial release of Mn2+ for effective Fenton-like reactions and other therapeutic functions but also ensured the favorable biocompatibility and T1-weighted MRI performance of the nanoplatform [29].

Evaluation of fenton-like activity and MRI capability

As we all know, Mn2+ could trigger the Fenton-like reaction via Mn2+-mediated catalyzation of endogenous H2O2 to generate ·OH in the presence of HCO3−. Therefore, the production of ·OH was assessed by the decolorization of methylene blue (MB) in the HCO3− buffer to probe the Fenton-like activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. A marked decrease in the absorbance of MB was observed following incubation with Mn/Si-Abe@BSA and H2O2 under the acidic condition with prolonging time, while almost completely degraded after incubation for 1 h (Fig. 2H). These results confirmed that the excellent CDT performance of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA to propel Mn2+-mediated Fenton-like reaction for generating ∙OH. Moreover, as Mn2+ with five unpaired 3d electrons has been demonstrated to be an effective T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agent, the T1-weighted contrast-enhanced capability of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was evaluated under neutral and acidic (with or without CO2) conditions using a small animal MRI scanner. As presented in Fig. 2I and J, the observed T1-weighted MRI signal intensity was weaker in the physiological environment (pH 7.4), almost remaining unchanged after incubation for 4 h. However, under acidic RA microenvironment conditions (pH 6.5), a significant time-dependent T1-weighted MRI contrast signal enhancement of the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA sample was observed in the T1- weighted MR images, which was attributed to the acid-triggered release of Mn2+. With the introduction of CO2, the T1 MRI contrast signal was much higher than that only at pH 6.5. Such contrast-enhanced responsiveness could be attributed to the large amount of Mn2+ released under more acidic conditions, which was enhanced by the CA-like activity of the nanoplatform. The MRI contrast effect of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was further evaluated by measuring the longitudinal relaxation time (T1) in PBS under different conditions. With increasing release of paramagnetic Mn2+, the r1 value of the nanoplatform rose significantly from 1.412 mM−1s− 1 at pH 7.4 to 5.638 mM−1s− 1 at pH 6.5 and to 7.038 mM−1s− 1 at pH 6.5 + CO2 (Figure S3). Taken together, these results showed the potential of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA as an excellent contrast agent for T1-weighted MRI in acidic RA microenvironment [30].

Assessment of ARG-like activity

As we all know, ARGs are inducible manganese containing enzymes that mediate the terminal reaction of the urea cycle, catalyzing the hydrolysis of L-arginine to L-ornithine and urea. As previous studies reported, there is a direct correlation between ARG activity and the Mn2+ level, which mainly exerts its function on catalytic activity and structural stability of ARG. The ARG activity exhibits a positive correlation with elevated Mn2+ levels within a defined range [31]. On this basis, it was reasonable to hypothesize that the released Mn2+ of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA under acidic conditions with concomitant CO2 aeration could exhibit ARG-like activity. To verify the hypothesis, the ARG activity was indirectly evaluated by quantitatively analyzing the content of urea, the enzymatic product of the ARG-catalyzed reaction, which has a specific UV–vis absorption peak between 200 and 240 nm. As shown in Fig. 2K, the absorbance changes of urea quantified by UV–vis absorption spectra demonstrated a time-dependent increasing within 4 h, which was correlated well with the release of Mn2+ of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA under acidic conditions with concomitant CO2 aeration, indirectly indicating the ARG-like activity. During this experiment, pH 5.5 was introduced as an acidic control to demonstrate that the decrease of pH mediated by CA-mimic catalysis on the basis of mildly acidic environment did not lead to arginine denaturation. Therefore, based on experimental results and existing literature, the self-amplified acidity-responsive release of Abe and Mn²⁺ from Mn/Si-Abe@BSA enabled an in situ synergistic effect that effectively modulated aberrant cell proliferation for MRI-guided precision therapy in RA.

Evaluation of cytotoxicity and therapeutic efficacy

Considering the unique multi-enzyme activities of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA mentioned above, we further validated them at the cellular level. The biosafety of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was firstly evaluated utilizing a cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay to determine its cytotoxicity on human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) at graded concentrations. As shown in Fig. 3A, the cell viability of HUVEC consistently remained at a relatively high level across increasing concentrations and even the cell viability was maintaining approximately 85% at the maximum concentration of 1000 µg/mL, which indicated that the nanoplatform had excellent biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxicity toward normal cells. During the pathological process of the occurrence and development of RA, synovial fibroblasts (SFs), as the core cellular components related to synovial tissue formation, plays a key role. Under the stimulation of the inflammatory microenvironment of RA, SFs are activated and differentiated into an aggressive subtype, exhibiting tumor-like behaviors, such as excessive proliferation, migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis. These activated SFs further erode articular cartilage and subchondral bone, leading to structural destruction of the affected joints and subsequent functional impairment [32]. Therefore, modulating pathologically proliferative synovial fibroblasts may emerge as a viable strategy for RA treatment. Based on the theoretical research mentioned above, rheumatoid arthritis synovial fibroblasts (RASFs) were employed to explore the therapeutic efficacy of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in vitro. Before that, the cellular internalization capacity of RASFs for the nanoplatform was primarily evaluated. Currently, ICG, as the only FDA-approved NIR fluorescent dye widely used in clinical NIR-II FL imaging, has gained widespread attention due to its excellent biocompatibility, low toxicity and tailing emission within the NIR-II window [33]. Thus, the cellular uptake of RASFs toward Mn/Si-Abe@BSA were assessed through real-time monitoring of the NIR-II FL signals of RASFs following incubation with indocyanine green (ICG) modified Mn/Si-Abe@BSA for varying durations. As shown in Fig. 3B and E, the NIR-II FL signals were progressively intensifying with increasing time and reached maximal fluorescence intensity after 2 h co-incubation. This finding demonstrated the efficient cellular internalization of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA by RASFs, providing a robust foundation for its subsequent exertion of therapeutic efficacy. Based on the above experimental results, the cell proliferation inhibition of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA on RASFs was evaluated via a CCK-8 assay. As demonstrated in Fig. 3C, significant concentration-dependent cell proliferation inhibition of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA-treated cells was observed when compared to Si-Abe@BSA and Mn/Si@BSA groups, which was attributed to the synergistic effect of Mn2+ and Abe, inducing cell cycle arrest and promoting apoptosis in RASFs. The growth curve analysis further confirmed the evident cell proliferation inhibition of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 3D). To visualize cell proliferation inhibition directly, both calcein-AM/PI cell double staining and colony formation assays were employed. As presented in Fig. 3F and G, the most intense red fluorescence and the least colony formation were shown in the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group compared to the other groups, in agreement with the results from the CCK-8 assay. The quantification of colony numbers further corroborated this observation (Figure S4). In short, the results above demonstrated that Mn/Si-Abe@BSA exerted the optimal synergistic effects on proliferation inhibition of FLSs.

Induction of cell cycle arrest in RASFs by Mn/Si-Abe@BSA

The therapeutic mechanism of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was further explored in detail in vitro. Following cellular internalization, the acid-labile Mn-O bonds within Mn/Si-Abe@BSA underwent pH-responsive cleavage in the weakly acidic RA synovial microenvironment, resulting in controlled release of Mn2+ and Abe. Moreover, the CA-mimicking activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA could reversibly catalyze the hydration of CO2 into H+ and HCO3−, thus establishing a self-amplifying cycle that enhanced reactive biodegradation while maintaining sustained drug release kinetics [27]. Therefore, the CA-mimetic activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was first systematically evaluated. The pH fluorescence probe BCECF was used to detect the intracellular pH levels after different treatments to validate the CA-mimetic activity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, the green fluorescence in the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group was much weaker than that in the control group, confirming its CA-like activity through the pH-dependent fluorescence quenching. Upon uptake by RASFs, the acid-sensitive Mn-O bonds within Mn/Si-Abe@BSA responded to the mildly acidic environment of RASFs to release Mn2+. The concentration of intracellular Mn2+ was quantified using a corresponding assay kit in Fig. 4C. With prolonged incubation, the intracellular Mn2+ content rose and peaked at 4 h, mirroring the in vitro release profile and validating the successful release of Mn2+. As evidenced by the previous report, Mn2+ and Abe were released to modulate the proliferation of RASFs through arginine deprivation and CDK4/6 inhibition [34]. The mechanisms of cell proliferation inhibition mediated by Mn/Si-Abe@BSA were further investigated. Based on the in vitro results in Fig. 2K and the previous studies, the activity of intracellular ARG and the cellular L-arginine levels were measured via the corresponding assay kits. The enzyme activity relatively increased while the L-Arg concentration was decreased after treatments with Mn/Si@BSA and Mn/Si-Abe@BSA relative to the control and Si-Abe@BSA groups, which suggested the potential interaction between Mn2+ and ARG (Fig. 4D and E). Arginine deprivation could upregulate the expression of p53 and p21 to induce G1 phase arrest synergizing with Abe. To confirm the regulatory effect of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA on cell cycle progression, immunofluorescence staining and western blotting analysis were used to studied the mechanism (Fig. 4F and G). The results showed that the Mn/Si@BSA and Mn/Si-Abe@BSA groups displayed a significant upregulation of p53 and p21, demonstrating the capacity of Mn2+ to impede cell cycle progression through the induction of these key regulatory proteins. Moreover, given the intimate relationship between G1 phase arrest and the levels of cyclin D1, the expression levels of cyclin D1 in RASFs was examined. The results revealed a downregulation of cyclin D1 expression across all treatment groups, which occurred because Abe targets CDK4/6 while Mn2+ induces the deprivation of arginine, collectively inducing cell cycle arrest. The corresponding quantitative analyses also confirmed these observations (Figure S5 and S6). Flow cytometry was further performed to quantify the proportions of RASFs in G0/G1, S and G2/M phase after treatment with various samples (Fig. 4H and I). As expected, a significant increase in the G1 phase cell population was observed in all treated groups relative to the control group, and the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group showed the highest cell cycle arrest of 76.34%, indicating that Mn/Si-Abe@BSA could prevent cell division and proliferation of RASFs by prolonging the gap phase. Overall, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA exhibited the expected synergistic effect on cell cycle arrest.

Growth arrest caused by cell cycle arrest is an adaptive protective mechanism of cells in response to damage stimuli. Once the damage was reversibly repaired, the cell cycle progression of these cells in growth arrest could be resumed to continue mitosis. In contrast, irreversible damage might induce apoptosis of these cells. p53, as a critical tumor suppressor gene, modulates the cell cycle progression and apoptosis when cells are subjected to a variety of stress. There have been researches suggesting that p53 induces apoptosis by triggering cytochrome c release from mitochondria, which leads to caspase activation. Additionally, p53 also represses the transcription of specific anti-apoptotic genes, such as Bcl-2. Therefore, the upregulated p53 could synergize with Mn²⁺-catalyzed ·OH generation to induce mitochondrial damage and trigger apoptosis. To clarify the mechanism of cell apoptosis induced by Mn/Si-Abe@BSA, DCF and HPF probes were firstly used to detect the generation of intracellular ROS and ·OH after various treatments, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5A and S8, the strong green fluorescence exhibited among the latter two groups, indicating its outstanding ability to produce ROS mediated by Mn2+. Quantitative analysis of DCF fluorescence further supported this result (Figure S7). Subsequently, to verify the influence of Mn2+ and p53 on mitochondria, the mitochondrial membrane potential of RASFs after different treatments was evaluated by JC-1 staining. Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group had strong green fluorescence (Fig. 5B), indicating that the mitochondria were severely damaged. To comprehensively understand the mechanisms underlying RASFs apoptosis, the expression of key apoptosis-related proteins was further assessed via immunofluorescence and western blotting analysis. As depicted in Fig. 5C and S9, a significant attenuation of red fluorescence and an enhancement of green fluorescence were observed in the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group relative to the control, representing decreased Bcl-2 and increased Bax levels, respectively, and collectively indicating the initiation of apoptosis. Likewise, the expression of the apoptotic proteins Bax, cytochrome C and cleaved caspase-3 increased significantly in the Mn/Si@BSA and Mn/Si-Abe@BSA groups. Notably, the increase in the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group was the most striking. In contrast, the expression of Bcl-2 was lower in the experimental groups than in the control group, further supporting the above viewpoint (Fig. 5D). Quantitative analysis of band intensities additionally confirmed these expression changes (Figure S10). Flow cytometry analysis was further conducted to verify the apoptosis induced by ROS and upregulated p53. As presented in Fig. 5E and F, the proportion of apoptotic cells in both early and late stages was significantly increased in the Mn/Si@BSA and Mn/Si-Abe@BSA groups compared to the other groups. This result indicated that upregulated p53 and generated ROS deprived from Mn2+-mediated reactions could synergistically promote apoptosis. Therefore, based on the experimental results and observations, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA could specifically accumulate in pathological RASFs to suppress their proliferation through the concurrent induction of cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, providing a comprehensive and synergistic strategy for RA therapy.

In vivo imaging performance and assessment of antiarthritic efficacy

Given that the excellent biocompatibility, self-enhanced responsiveness and promising therapeutic effects of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in vitro, the potential target capability of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was further evaluated in vivo. To evaluate the in vivo targeting efficiency and MRI properties of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in inflammatory joints, the collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) model was employed in DBA/1 mice according to a repeated immunization protocol as previously described, followed by the intravenous injection of ICG-modified or unmodified Mn/Si-Abe@BSA to observe the fluorescence distribution and MR signal of mice at various time points [35]. As shown in Fig. 6A and S11, the NIR II FL signal was detected at the knee joints of CIA mice 1 h post- injection, increasing in a time-dependent manner with a peak at 4 h. These findings suggested the targeted enrichment properties of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in the inflamed joints. Similarly, the T1-weighted MRI signal in the arthritic joints of CIA mice increased progressively after injection and peaked at 4 h (Fig. 6B and S12). The time-dependent signal enhancement resulted from the self-augmented release of Mn2+ from Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in the inflammatory joints. Collectively, the imaging results confirmed the specific accumulation of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in RA joints, with an optimal imaging window 4 h post-injection, allowing for precise lesion localization and thereby enabling highly efficient MRI-guided self-enhanced dynamic therapy in RA.

Inspired by the exciting in vivo target capability, the therapeutic efficacy of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA was further assessed in the advanced stage of the CIA mice following different treatments including (1) PBS, (2) Si-Abe@BSA, (3) Mn/Si@BSA, and (4) Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. The representative photographs and infrared images of hind paws were recorded every 4 days from day 28 to day 44 during the therapy. As shown in Fig. 6C, the mice treated with PBS developed severe arthritic symptoms including paw redness, deformity and stiffness, with a high temperature of 31.7 ℃. These manifestations were moderately alleviated and the temperature was mildly decreased in the Si-Abe@BSA group. Following treatment with Mn/Si@BSA, the paw swelling of mice was relieved more substantially, despite persistent erythema and stiffness. It was worth noting that the mice treated with Mn/Si-Abe@BSA displayed much more significant remission and exhibited less redness, with the lowest temperature of 28.1 ℃, which had the most efficient anti-inflammatory effect. To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of different treatments, the clinical index and paw thickness as quantitative indicators of joint inflammation were recorded. As shown in Fig. 6D and E, the clinical index and paw thickness of PBS-treated group consistently remaining high throughout the treatment period. While after treatment with Si-Abe@BSA and Mn/Si@BSA, both the two indicators decreased at various degrees and the Mn/Si@BSA group had a significantly lower clinical index and paw thickness than the Si-Abe@BSA group. Notably, the lowest paw thickness was observed in the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group among all groups, indicating its superior therapeutic response. The clinical index also followed a comparable trend to the paw thickness reduction. Moreover, no significant body weight fluctuations were observed in CIA mice during the treatment period (Fig. 6F), indicating the high biocompatibility and minimal adverse effects of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA.

Subsequently, gait analysis was performed to assess arthritis severity in mice receiving different treatments using the collected foot gait data, and the representative gait signal images were shown (Fig. 6G). The maximum contact and maximum intensity of the right hind paw of mice were significantly reduced in the control group, whereas these parameters were effectively normalized by Mn/Si-Abe@BSA treatment. Quantitative data shown in Figure S13 demonstrated that the mean intensity and print area of the right hind paw were markedly increased across all treatment groups relative to the PBS group. Among them, the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group exhibited the highest values, demonstrating its superior therapeutic efficacy. To microscopically assess the therapeutic effect of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA, three-dimensional (3D) micro-CT analysis of hind paw bones in CIA mice with various treatments was performed (Fig. 6H). Pronounced bone loss and surface roughness were evident in PBS-treated mice, while the Si-Abe@BSA group showed only marginal improvement. However, in mice treated with Mn/Si@BSA, a partial alleviation of these degenerative changes was observed to some degree. As expected, mice receiving Mn/Si-Abe@BSA treatment exhibited minimal bone damage and more stable ankle joint structures. Quantitative micro-CT analysis further revealed the superior protective capacity of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA against cartilage erosion and joint damage (Figure S14). The above results demonstrated that the synergistic effect of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA represented a promising therapeutic strategy for advanced RA.

Histopathological analysis and biosafety evaluation

After that, the therapeutic effects of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA were further assessed by a series of histological analysis experiments on sections of the ankle joints of mice from the different groups. As shown in Fig. 7A, H&E-stained sections of PBS-treated mice exhibited extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, severe destruction of cartilage synovium and marked bone erosion. Along with treatment, limited effect in relieving these symptoms was observed in the mice treated with Si-Abe@BSA, while Mn/Si@BSA and Mn/Si-Abe@BSA strongly reduced synovial inflammation. Notably, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA demonstrated optimal performance, as evidenced by the inhibition of inflammatory cell infiltration and the restoration of synovial architecture, indicating the most robust mitigation of arthritis. Similar results were observed for safranin-O and fast green staining in Fig. 7B. The results revealed a significant loss of cartilage extracellular matrix in the PBS-treated group, indicating substantial cartilage destruction. In the Si-Abe@BSA group, there was a partial restoration of both the matrix and the cartilage layer. While the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA treated group demonstrated the most pronounced recovery, featuring a complete and smooth cartilage layer akin. These findings suggested that the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA treatment facilitated the repair of damaged cartilage in CIA mice. TUNEL staining was employed to assess synovial tissue apoptosis of mice in each group. As presented in Fig. 7C, compared with the mice treated with PBS, the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA treated group had the best ability to promote apoptosis of synovial tissue cells, owing to the synergistic effects of Mn2+ and Abe. The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in serum of mice with different treatments were further measured by ELISA to verify the therapeutic effects. As shown in Figure S15, the Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group exhibited the lowest concentrations of IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-6, consistent with the histopathological observations and further confirming its potent anti-inflammatory effect. The content of Mn2+ and the subsequent reduction in arginine levels were examined in the joints via relevant assay kits to evaluate the enrichment and performance of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. After intravenous injection for 4 h of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA, the ankle joints and main organs of healthy mice and RA mice were separated to assess the biodistribution of Mn2+. Consistent with the imaging results, the Mn2+ content in joints of RA mice significantly exceeded that measured in healthy joint samples (Fig. 7D), indicating the effective enrichment of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in the inflamed joints. The content of arginine was detected using the joints of mice treated with PBS and Mn/Si-Abe@BSA for 24 h. As expected, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA group showed obviously decreased levels of arginine (Fig. 7E), demonstrating its significant effects. Taken the above results, Mn/Si-Abe@BSA has been demonstrated to exhibit superior therapeutic efficacy for RA. Ensuring the biosafety of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA is crucial for its clinical translation. Therefore, the hemolysis assay was initially conducted to evaluate the biosafety of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA in vivo. As depicted in Fig. 7F, the hemolysis rates in all groups were less than 5% which was within the normal range, regardless of the concentration of Mn/Si-Abe@BSA. Furthermore, the blood biochemical analyses were conducted. As displayed in Figure S16 and S17, there were negligible differences observed in key liver and kidney function indicators among all groups. Apparently, the favorable biosafety rendered the nanoplatform a promising platform for RA theranostics.

Conclusion

To sum up, a self-amplifying pH-responsive nanoplatform was prepared by loading with Abe in the Mn-MSNs and modifying with BSA (Mn/Si-Abe@BSA), exhibiting CA-mimicking activity. Compared with various recently reported RA nanoplatforms, the platform offered clear advantages in facile synthesis, self-amplifying degradation and effects, theranostics, innovative arginine metabolic regulation, and multi-mechanism synergistic elimination of FLSs. Its efficacy in mitigating rheumatoid joint inflammation was demonstrated by experiments in vivo and in vitro. The in vitro results indicated that Mn/Si-Abe@BSA induced cell cycle arrest in G1 phase of FLSs by arginine deprivation and promoted inflammatory FLSs apoptosis, both of which were particularly important for inhibiting FLSs proliferation to alleviate RA inflammation. Additionally, the in vivo studies demonstrated that Mn/Si-Abe@BSA could specifically accumulate at the inflamed joints, ameliorating in vivo inflammation and reducing both bone erosion and joint swelling. Nevertheless, several limitations remained, including insufficient targeting efficiency, potential off-target damage from ·OH, lack of long-term in vivo clearance and toxicity data, and the current focus solely on FLSs without regulating other immune cells. Altogether, despite these limitations, our study extended the treatment strategies in RA through the synergistic effects of cell cycle arrest and apoptosis promotion, providing a promising theranostic nanoplatform for RA treatment and opening up new avenues to address inflammatory diseases associated with abnormal proliferation of malignant cells.
