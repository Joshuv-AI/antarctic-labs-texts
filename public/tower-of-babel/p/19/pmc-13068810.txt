Fluoroscopic radiation exposure during spinal cord stimulation procedures: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chelsie L. Jacques, Andrew R. Stephens, Ramzi El-Hassan, Adem F. Aktas, Rachel Becker, Ben L. LaPlante, Rajeev K. Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAssess the fluoroscopic exposure times of spinal cord stimulator lead placement using systematic review and meta-analysis.Study designSystematic Review and Meta-Analysis.MethodsMajor databases including PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science were used to conduct a systematic review with key terms regarding radiation (i.e. “radiation,” “fluoroscopy,” “dose area product” etc.) and spine procedures (i.e. “spinal cord stimulator,” “injections”). Initial review of abstracts and titles were performed to filter content, followed by full text review by independent authors. Studies that provided fluoroscopic exposure times for percutaneous spinal cord stimulator lead trial and implants were included. Mean and standard deviations for fluoroscopy times (seconds) were compared.Results449 patients were included in this study across all studies. Of the total number of patients, 429 received SCS trials and 20 received SCS implants. The range of fluoroscopy time averages during SCS trials was 7 ± 4.4 s to 289 ± 198 s.DiscussionThis study provided an initial reference for fluoroscopic times during spinal cord stimulator trials. The fairly limited amount of information found prompts further dialogue and investigative studies on variables that should be accounted for when discussing radiation exposure during interventional procedures.

Introduction

Epidemiology and background

Spinal Cord Stimulation (SCS) is a minimally invasive surgical treatment option for individuals with chronic neuropathic pain who have failed conservative and medical management. It involves epidural lead placement and implantation of an associated battery pack. The highest volume of SCS implantations per 100,000 people occurs in patients under 65 years old, but the highest overall increase from 2009 to 2018 was in the 85-year-old and older population (324%) [1]. The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved SCS systems for intractable chronic neuropathic pain, chronic intractable low back pain and pain of the trunk/limbs, failed back surgery syndrome (FBSS), diabetic neuropathy and Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) types I and II [2]. FBSS is a complex complication of lumbar spine surgery presenting as residual lumbar spine pain with or without radiculopathy, despite surgical procedures to relieve such pain at corresponding spine levels. Studies have shown that individuals with multilevel decompression surgery and older age are at a higher risk of FBSS, with an incidence ranging from 7% to 9.1% one year after surgery, varying with different surgical approaches and levels of fusion [3]. Other studies report incidence levels of approximately 19% for laminectomy and 46% for fusion [4]. CRPS is a chronic pain condition that often results in hyperalgesia and allodynia of extremities. Prior studies have shown that the overall prevalence of CRPS ranges from 0.07% to 1.2%, with major causes including fractures, blunt traumatic injuries, surgeries, and carpal tunnel syndrome [5].

Clinical relevance

Radiation exposure secondary to fluoroscopic procedures can have long-term effects in patients, providers and staff. A stochastic effect is when the probability of an effect parallels a radiation dose increase, such as radiation-induced cancer and genetic effects [6]. Radiation exposure during a procedure can be mitigated by pulsed fluoroscopy versus continuous fluoroscopy when using the C-arm [7]. Although no safe dose of radiation has been documented for patients or medical workers, the overall goal when performing fluoroscopy procedures is to achieve radiation exposure “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) [8]. Furthermore, proceduralists and staff can have cumulative radiation exposure throughout their professional career. Therefore, awareness of radiation dose and fluoroscopic times is beneficial for both patients and providers/staff.

Existing knowledge and study rationale

Despite the general recommendations to achieve radiation exposure “as low as is achievable,” it remains unclear what levels or exposure times are currently used in SCS studies. To this end, the overall aim of this study was to systematically review and analyze prior literature on (or about) fluoroscopy duration for SCS procedures and summarize the overall information. Awareness of what is currently happening in practice is essential not only for identifying procedural outliers but also for providing the necessary data to optimize radiation safety for both the patient and the entire interventional team, in accordance with ALARA principles. Additionally, this study demonstrated the inconsistency in the reporting of radiation dose for interventional spine procedures.

Methods

Institutional review board (IRB) requirement

As this study is a systematic review, it is exempt from IRB Approval.

Database search

In this study, a systematic review was conducted according to preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses guideline. With the aid of a medical librarian specialist (RB) multiple databases (PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane Library, Scopus, Web of Science) were queried. Key terms were used to obtain relevant articles included Fluoroscopy, Fluorography, Radiation Dosage, Dose Area Product, Radiation Exposure, Dosage, Dosing, Injections, Epidural, Radiofrequency Ablation, Spinal Cord Stimulation, Steroid, Transforaminal Epidural, RFA, Radiofrequency Ablation, MBB, Medial Branch Block, Sacroiliac Injection, Steroid, Corticosteroid, Epidural Spine, Epidural Spinal, Spinal Cord Stimulator.

Data selection and collection

Articles (2,056) obtained from the above search were uploaded to Covidence for data extraction [9]. After duplicate removal, 1922 titles/abstracts were screened by three individuals (AS, RE, AA) for topics related to fluoroscopy and spine pathologies. Titles and abstracts that did not include the aforementioned topics were excluded from the systematic review. Four hundred ninety-nine studies involving any interventional spine procedures were identified and underwent full-text review for studies related only to spinal cord stimulators. Each of the 499 texts was reviewed by two individuals (AA & CJ), and discrepancies between studies to be included/excluded were re-assessed by both individuals. The final number of studies that involved spinal cord stimulators, while also including fluoroscopic imaging and radiation doses was 5 studies (Fig. 1). Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria included reporting of fluoroscopic time and/or radiation dose for a percutaneous spinal cord stimulator trial or implant. Exclusion criteria included the use of Computed Tomography (CT scan). Upon final full text review, studies were excluded if the following were observed: not English = study was presented in another language; wrong outcomes = interventional spine procedures other than SCS were presented; wrong interventions = did not utilize fluoroscopy during an interventional spine procedure; wrong study design = primary focus of the study was not radiation exposure and did not include fluoroscopic times or radiation doses; pediatric population = included pediatric patients in study; cannot obtain full text = only abstract was available and/or unable to get access to full text; unable to differentiate interventions = unclear presentation of data for different interventions (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias

The Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions and the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool were used after selecting the final five SCS studies to assess the quality of the studies included in the systematic review.

Data items

The original goal of the study was to obtain the radiation dose of SCS procedures. Given the data from the studies extracted, the decision was made to use fluoroscopic times for SCS procedures. Four of the five studies provided data for SCS trials, while the remaining study performed an implant. All fluoroscopic times were utilized for the results.

Statistical analysis

Due to the limited number of studies identified for full review, larger pooling and statistics could not be performed. Instead, summary ranges of average fluoroscopy time (seconds) among the SCS trials were provided.

Results

Description of studies

A total of five studies met inclusion criteria. One of the studies (Zaidman) [10] reported radiation exposure for a spinal cord stimulator implant. The other four studies (D'Souza, McLean and Wininger, Wininger) [[11], [12], [13], [14]] reported results for stimulator trials. Both, Wininger et al., 2010 and Wininger et al., 2012, utilized pulsed and continuous fluoroscopy for their trials [13,14]. Additionally, Wininger et al., 2012 had both an expert and novice provider perform the SCS trials, but no statistical difference was found between the fluoroscopy times [13]. D'Souza et al., 2021 evaluated the progression of trainee fluoroscopy times and radiation doses during pain fellowship training [11]. Zaidman et al., 2015 was the only study to perform SCS implants [10].

Risk of bias

For D'Souza et al., 2021 11, the Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions was utilized to assess the risk of bias. The greatest concern for risk of bias in this study was within the domain of confounding variables. Although variables including age, body mass index (BMI), and whether the supervising attending or fellow performed the procedure were adjusted for using a linear regression model, the procedural complexity (with regard to past surgical history and degree of pathology) most likely influenced the fluoroscopic time, radiation dose exposure, and which provider performed the case based on level of training. This limited the external validity of the study.

For Wininger et al., 2012, Wininger et al., 2010, and Zaidman et al., 2015 [10,13,14], the Cochrane Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions was used to assess the risk of bias. The greatest concern for risk of bias in this study was also within the domain of confounding variables. Variables including body habitus, degree of spondylosis, age, and prior surgical interventions were not accounted for in this study. Additionally, given that Wininger et al., 2012 [13] was an observational study, it was possible that more difficult procedures were performed by the more experienced provider. With regards to Wininger et al., 2010 [14], the study separated patients based on if neural canal dual leads or single leads were utilized. Given this was an observational study, the decision to utilize one over the other was based on clinical judgement and not randomized. This can confound the degree of pathology and, therefore, procedural difficulty. For Zaidman et al., 2015 [10], there was no information provided regarding the patient cohort, with the main distinguishing factor being if shielded vs. unshielded SCS leads were utilized. The reason why certain patients received the shield vs. unshielded lead was not reported, but it could be due to co-morbidities and past medical/surgical history that may require MRI imaging in the future.

For McLean et al., 2017 [12], the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool was utilized to assess risk of bias. The most significant concern was incomplete reporting of demographics. The information provided for this patient cohort was percutaneous implantation of SCS leads for trial between January 2006 and December 2011. There was no data provided on BMI, age, gender, past surgical history, or degree of pathology.

Discussion

Spinal cord stimulators are a cost-effective management plan for neuropathic and chronic pain with well-researched associated risks [15,16]. The most common risks associated with the procedure include infection, lead migration, and lead breakage [16,17]. Although these are acute concerns after the procedure, long-term risks include radiation exposure to patients and providers/staff as well.

A standard unit of measure for x-ray beam exposure is air kerma, and, relative to fluoroscopic procedures, it is the amount of energy released by the combination of radiation with unit mass of air. This is, therefore, looking at dose intensity [18]. Wininger reported mean incident air kerma ranging from 5.8 to 13.9 mGy [13,14] (Table 1). To place into clinical context, medical workers with an occupational radiation dose of 640 mGy during their adult working life had an additional cancer mortality of 1.70% and 2.39% in men and women, respectively [18]. A major factor that can help minimize radiation exposure is the use of pulsed vs. continuous fluoroscopy during procedures. Studies have shown approximately a 6-fold dose reduction in radiation exposure when comparing low-dose pulsed fluoroscopy to standard-dose continuous fluoroscopy. A potential drawback to pulsed fluoroscopy is possible loss of diagnostic information [19]. Additional factors include projection angle of the beam, as extreme oblique angles require a greater dose, and larger body habitus of the patient, as it may require a greater skin entrance dose to provide sufficient x-ray penetration [18].

Time of fluoroscopy is one of many factors commonly used when evaluating radiation exposure. Prior studies have shown that Basivertebral nerve radiofrequency ablations have an average exposure time of 50.7 s per level [20]. When compared to less invasive procedures, including transforaminal epidural steroid injections with a median fluoroscopy time of 20.8 s [21] and cervical interlaminar epidural steroid injections with a mean fluoroscopic time of 18 s [22], SCS trials have a wide range of exposure time, 7.0 s to 289 s (Table 1).

There was heterogeneity among the studies included in the systematic review that may have attributed to the large range in fluoroscopic times. Although D'Souza et al., 2021, was the only study documented to use just trainees, it had the lowest fluoroscopic times of the five studies. Additionally, D'Souza documented using pulsed fluoroscopy, instead of continuous or mixed [11]. McLean et al., 2017 utilized experienced providers and had the highest fluoroscopic average times of 289 s [12] (Table 1). Other variables that are known to affect exposure time, such as degree of spondylosis and collimation, were not specifically reported in the five studies that were reviewed [[23], [24], [25]]. It can be inferred that although variables such as BMI and age were accounted for in D'Souza et al., 2021 with a linear regression model, the use of only trainees may have resulted in less procedurally complex cases as compared to those of experienced proceduralists in McLean et al., 2017. This can result in lower fluoroscopic times in D'Souza et al., 2021 [11,12].

The original goal of the study was to assess radiation dose exposure, but given the inconsistent reporting of radiation dose in various studies, the decision was made to focus on fluoroscopy time. Despite modifying the primary outcome assessed to fluoroscopic times, there was still a large range of reported times, given that only four studies involving trials and one study involving implants were available. Given the limited number of studies, variability in study design, and irregularity in reported confounding factors, statistical analyses would not be optimal as any outlier in results would skew the final analysis.

This study has highlighted the lack of literature on radiation exposure in SCS procedures and the deficiency in standardized reporting of outcomes. Of four hundred ninety-nine studies reviewed, only five provided fluoroscopic times and/or radiation dose values for SCS procedures. Among those five studies, there were noticeable inconsistencies in study design and reporting outcomes.

According to The American Association of Physicists in Medicine (AAPM), fluoroscopy time, cumulative air kerma, air kerma–area product, and peak skin dose (PSD) are quantifiable radiation dose indices that can aid with the estimation of radiation dose exposure. Ideally, PSD would be used as it associates well with potential tissue injury, but this index is not as readily available and has high variability in presentation. Air kerma–area product is a poor indicator of radiation induced tissue effects and is better correlated with stochastic effects as it shows the total energy deposited in the patient. Furthermore, air kerma–area product does not have standardized units which makes it difficult to implement a clinical threshold. As a result, it is recommended that cumulative air kerma be used as a primary dose index given its standardization and correlation with PSD. As for fluoroscopy time, although it is a commonly used marker, it is the least useful in predicting potential tissue effects of the radiation exposure. This index is better used for quantifying procedure complexity and efficiency. If fluoroscopic times must be used as the primary index for estimating radiation dose, it should be combined with dose rate/dose per image for specific mode of operation used and number of fluoroscopic images obtained during the procedure. Overall, the recommendation by the AAPM is utilization of cumulative air kerma as the primary dose when establishing threshold levels for fluoroscopic procedures as it uses a standard definition and is correlated with PSD. Cumulative air kerma and PSD are clinically useful for determining likelihood of tissue effects [26].

In the future, confounding variables that are known to affect fluoroscopic radiation dose and times should be accounted for to reduce bias secondary to confounding variables and participant selection. This includes age, gender, BMI, involvement of trainee and degree of spinal pathology (such as, spondylosis and scoliosis). Furthermore, outcomes should be presented as either cumulative air kerma or fluoroscopic time with dose rate/dose per image for specific mode of operation used and number of fluoroscopic images obtained during the procedure [23,26].

Conclusion

With the increase in popularity of minimally invasive procedures over time, the use of exposure times and doses can aid in advancement of more novel techniques and efficiency in fluoroscopic procedures. The goal of such advancement is to limit radiation exposure to patients, providers, and staff, while still providing good clinical care. This study highlights the lack of literature on the topic of fluoroscopic times and radiation exposure during SCS trials that deserves further dialogue. Additionally, this study emphasizes the reporting recommendations of the AAPM to utilize cumulative air kerma for future reporting of radiation dose given its standard definition and correlation with PSD [26].
