Generating polarized amplified spontaneous emission at high symmetry points of square lattices
Authors: Tianyu Wang, Yi Wang, Yuze Wu, Xiaobo Dui, Shijia He, Jiang Hu, Wenxin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlasmonic lattices offer a promising platform for overcoming the optical diffraction limit and suppressing radiative losses, which are crucial for advancing large-scale integration in nanophotonic devices. The performance of such integrated devices are strongly influenced by the arrangement and unit geometry of plasmonic lattices, highlighting the need for precise and scalable fabrication strategies. Here, we systematically design and fabricate a series of plasmonic square lattices with decreasing unit symmetries, from C∞v (O-hole) to C4v (X-hole) and Cs (OX-hole). The polarization-resolved emission intensities at high symmetry points are thoroughly investigated, revealing a 45° deflection in the polarization angle at Γ(1) as the structural symmetry decreased, while the other four high symmetry points, X(1), M(1), Γ(2), and X(2), exhibited a 90° polarization shift. This tunability provides an effective approach for modulating the polarization characteristics of plasmonic lattices. The highest polarization degree of 0.59 was observed at the X(2) point, where the energy matched the 720 nm emission of the Nile Red gain medium, resulting in directional and polarized amplified spontaneous emission under 532 nm optical pumping. This study establishes a framework for generating polarized amplified spontaneous emission at high symmetry points in plasmonic lattices, providing innovative strategies for tunable light sources in sensing and photonics communication applications. Subject terms: Nanocavities, Nanophotonics and plasmonics

Introduction

The excitation of surface plasmons (SPs) in metallic nanostructures facilitates the manipulation of light at the nanoscale1, with subwavelength-confined optical fields within plasmonic cavities strongly interacting with nearby emitters2. This provides powerful methodologies for regulating the optical characteristics of materials3 and elucidating our understanding of light-matter interactions. As interdisciplinary approaches advance, strategies relying solely on nanostructure size4 and morphology5 have proven insufficient for addressing the evolving demands of modern optoelectronics, including quantum topological optics6 and metamaterials7. The ongoing advancements in micro-nano fabrication and chemical synthesis techniques enable precise control over the spatial distribution8, geometric parameters9, and composition of structural units10. Recent research has demonstrated that plasmonic lattices lead to coupling of plasmonic with diffraction modes11. By strategically designing the structural morphology and optimizing the lattice parameters, generated resonant modes, such as surface lattice resonance (SLR)12 or Bloch-surface plasmon polariton (Bloch-SPP)13, can exhibit distinct resonance characteristics, including reduced losses14, enhanced localized field effects15, improved polarization properties16, and broader tunability17.

The Bloch-SPP mode is a key resonance mode in plasmonic nanohole arrays, where it involves the physical process of interaction between the electromagnetic field on a metal surface18, and the optical field in free space. Under the influence of periodic potential fields, the Bloch-SPP mode induces stronger localization and enhancement of the electromagnetic field compared to the SPP mode19, producing distinctive photonic band structures. The design of a plasmonic lattice allows the effective modulation of surface wave propagation, a crucial factor in integrated optics. More concretely, the dispersion of the Bloch-SPP mode, representing the correlation between frequency and wavevector, can be precisely controlled by adjusting the geometry of the plasmonic lattice20. In a fixed lattice configuration, parameters such as lattice period and size21 primarily influence dispersion frequency, while the lattice symmetry governs the distribution, localization22, and polarization state of the Bloch-SPP mode. Therefore, the characteristics of the band structure, particularly at high symmetry points, are directly linked to the symmetry of the plasmonic lattice. These high symmetry points define the topological structure of the plasmonic lattice in momentum space23, providing considerable localized density of states (LDOS) and a reduced group velocity24,25, facilitating the selective excitation of specific resonance modes between light and matter. This enables phenomena such as amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) in designated emission directions26,27, and even leads to nanolasing28. However, while much research focuses on the modes and energies at high symmetry points, less attention has been paid to their polarization properties.

Achieving optimal optical signals relies on high-quality plasmonic lattices with large, uniform structural units29. Fabrication methods for plasmonic lattices are typically categorized into two approaches: top-down and bottom-up. Traditional “top-down” etching methods, such as focused ion beam (FIB) and electron beam lithography (EBL), excel in creating sophisticated nanostructures but are limited to small areas (less than a millimeter). Complementarily, “bottom-up” techniques offer low cost, high efficiency, and scalability, enabling the construction of large-area plasmonic lattices exceeding centimeter-scale30. Despite significant progress in plasmonic lattice fabrication, achieving efficient, universal, and economical strategies for large-area assembly with superior optical performance remains a challenge. Anodized aluminum oxide (AAO) membranes, with their highly uniform nanohole structure, fulfill an ideal template for plasmonic lattices. By varying fabrication parameters such as voltage, electrolyte composition, duration, and temperature31, the holes of AAO membranes can be used to precisely control the size, shape, and symmetry of plasmonic lattices. This ability to fabricate both precise structural units and large-area lattices based on AAO membranes is a promising platform for the development of advanced plasmonic optoelectronic devices.

Here, plasmonic square lattices of 600 nm period with symmetry-evolutive units were prepared based on AAO membranes. Distribution of elements about the AAO membranes elucidated the morphology variation of the units from circle (O-hole), cross (X-hole), to circle-cross pair (OX-hole). The far-field polarization properties of these lattices at high symmetry points were verified to be correlated with the structural symmetry of the unit. Specifically, the polarization angles of Γ(1) were deflected 45° as the unit symmetries change from C∞v, C4v to Cs, while the other high symmetry points, for instance X(2), experienced a variation of 90°. Moreover, at the X(2) point of plasmonic lattice that featuring with OX-hole, the Bloch-SPP mode with the highest polarization degree of 0.59 was designed to couple with the luminescence of Nile Red, resulting in a directional and polarized ASE. This work provides an ideal platform to investigate the Bloch-SPP mode-based nanoscale light source.

Results and discussion

Preparation procedure of square lattices with centrosymmetric units of decreasing symmetry

AAO membranes exhibit long-range ordered hole arrangements with square lattice structures and tunable unit sizes, yet precise control of spatial symmetry remains challenging. This necessitates detailed potential distribution analysis to investigate their structural modulation capabilities. During the initial anodization stage, the potential distribution on the patterned Al foil (Fig. 1a) is illustrated in the inset of Fig. 1a. The strongest potential is observed inside the holes (location P1), followed by the regions between nanoholes (location P2). Since a higher potential corresponds to a faster anodization rate, the holes grow steadily at location P1, and shallow holes form at location P2 even in the absence of predefined patterns (Fig. 1b). On the bottom side of the AAO membrane, the concaves (location P2’) resulting from hole growth align precisely with location P2 (Fig. 1c). At this stage, the potential is elevated not only within the holes but also in the concave regions (the inset of Fig. 1c). In contrast to the circular hole (O-hole, C∞v symmetry) on the top side, the concave features on the bottom side exhibit C4v symmetry (Fig. 1d), with the high potential distribution forming a distinct cross-shaped pattern (the inset of Fig. 1d). Consequently, a strategy focusing on the bottom side of AAO membrane is proposed for creating a series of lattices with evolving symmetries.

The first morphology is a classic AAO membrane with O-hole, fabricated through anodization in a citric acid solution and subsequently transferred to a glass substrate. The unit represented by the purple circle with a diameter of 200 nm (Fig. 2b) corresponds to the O-hole located at high potential region labeled P1 in the inset of Fig. 1a. The side-view of the O-hole is presented in the inset, where the purple area indicates the porous channel of AAO membrane. After removing the residual Al substrate, the bottom of the AAO membrane is etched by phosphoric acid solution, resulting in the second morphology, a cross-shaped hole (X-hole, shown in green area in Fig. 2c), which originates from the location of P2′ as indicated in Fig. 1c. The detailed parameters of the X-hole are labeled in Fig. S1. The symmetry of the unit decreases from C∞v (O-hole) to C4v (X-hole). By extending the time of the hole widening process, a third morphology of the AAO membrane can be obtained (Fig. 2d). This resulting unit is a combination of circle and cross-shaped holes (OX-hole), with symmetry of Cs. The O-hole and X-hole are alternately arranged in a square lattice, as shown in the inset of Fig. 2d. The three morphologies of units with different symmetries can be evolved from the same AAO membrane, and their spatial relation in bottom view and side view are illustrated as the right inset of Fig. 2a. Additionally, the sizes of the O-hole and X-hole can be further modulated by increasing the time of the hole widening process. The curve showing the varying sizes of O-hole and X-hole as a function of hole widening time is presented in Fig. S2. All AAO membranes are transferred to a glass substrate to maintain uniformity across the samples.

The morphologies of the nanoholes evolve during the hole widening process due to differences in the element distribution within the AAO membranes. Element mapping using transmission electron microscope (TEM) was utilized to explore the formation mechanism of the X-hole. The primary elements monitored are Al and O, as the electrolyte used in the anodization process is citric acid. The distribution of these elements aligns with the morphology of the AAO membranes, with the O element occupying less space than the Al element at position Ⅰ, as shown in Fig. 2e, f. The higher proportion of Al indicates a faster corrosion rate, which leads to preferential hole formation at position Ⅰ in the barrier layer. Furthermore, the formation of the cross shape involves two key factors. First, the four corners of the hole are generated since the fluctuating morphology of the barrier layer (inset of Fig. 2b). Second, it is related to the gradual composition of the AAO membranes. During anodization, the anion in the electrolyte penetrates from the nanohole to the boundary between adjacent units, resulting in a gradual variation in the hole-wall composition. Citrate contaminates in the hole-wall, while alumina near the boundary is relatively pure. The denser alumina shows the lower corrosion rate, which leads to the retention of boundaries marked by the dashed ovals (position Ⅱ) in Fig. 2g, forming the X-hole at position Ⅰ, as shown in Fig. 2h. The corrosion process is uniformly regulated, and the sample area reaches the centimeter level (Fig. 2i), achieved using nano-imprint lithography technology. The large-area SEM images of these nanoholes with symmetry evolution are presented in Figs. 2j and S3. Lattices with engineered structural uniformity enhance plasmonic system performances by effectively minimizing the disorder-induced random scattering and boundary losses32. Such uniform patterning is essential for improving the Bloch-SPP modes that underlie the unique properties of plasmonic lattices, which promote the realization of well-defined directional ASE and increase overlap volume with gain medium, collectively boosting light-matter interaction efficiency. The optical images of the samples before and after depositing 20 nm Ag are shown in Fig. S4.

Bloch-SPP modes of plasmonic square lattices and the polarization properties at high symmetry points

Figure 3a presents the primitive vectors (a1 and a2) of the plasmonic O-hole array with 20 nm Ag in real space. The SEM images of the X-hole and OX-hole after depositing Ag are shown in Fig. S5. Using the orthogonal correlation between real space and reciprocal space, the propagation path of the square lattice is depicted in Fig. S6. In the case of normal incidence, where the in-plane wave vector k// = 0, the four lowest vectors of the Bloch-SPP modes have equal magnitude and are degenerated at the Γ(2) point, with an energy of 1.91 eV in the reflection dispersion (E-k//), as demonstrated in Fig. 3b. In the off-angle condition, a non-zero k// has to be considered. For instance, under oblique incidence along the Γ-M orientation (left panel of Fig. 3b), the quadruple-degenerate mode splits into two dual-degenerate modes: (0, 1)/(1, 0) and (0, -1)/(-1, 0). However, the M(2) point cannot be measured in the visible and near-infrared shortwave ranges. The dashed lines represent numerically calculated diffraction orders (DOs) using the empty lattice approximation (ELA) method (Fig. S6c). Similarly, under oblique incidence along the Γ-X orientation (right panel of Fig. 3b), the quadruple-degenerate mode splits into one dual-degenerate mode (0, ±1) and two singlet modes (−1, 0) and (1, 0), which shift to lower and higher energy branches, respectively. The X(2) point (2.24 eV) marks the intersection of the (0, ±1) and high-order (−1, ±1) Bloch-SPP modes. The reflection dispersions of the plasmonic X-hole and OX-hole are plotted in Fig. S7. Additionally, the polarization of incident light is a key factor in generating effective radiative coupling. The Bloch-SPP modes show similar distribution along the Γ-M direction under both transverse magnetic (TM) and transverse electric (TE) polarization. However, only the (1, 0) and (-1, 0) modes can be excited in the Γ-X direction under TM polarization (Fig. S8), while the (0, ±1) mode is observed under TE polarization (Fig. S9).

The reflection dispersion illustrates the energy variation of the Bloch-SPP modes as a function of angle, while the momentum space imaging system offers a more direct visualization of the distribution of high symmetry points, as shown in Fig. 3c. The high symmetry points of the plasmonic O-hole array within the first Brillouin zone (1st BZ, indicated by the white dashed square) and the second Brillouin zone (2nd BZ, indicated by the green dashed square) can be observed in the iso-frequency image with incident light at 450 nm. The central bright spot corresponds to the Γ(1) point, the four spots adjacent to it are Χ(1) points, the four light spots at the oblique corners represent M(1) points, and the outermost points in Fig. 3c correspond to Γ(2) points. The numerical aperture (NA) of the objective lens is 0.45, and the optical path of the home-built momentum space imaging system is shown in Fig. S10. To enable a broader and more comprehensive exploration of high symmetry points, an angle variation method is adopted to enhance the optical path of the momentum space imaging system. This method addresses the limitation of high-order optical information caused by the restricted NA of the objective lens. Consequently, the X(2) point can be measured by adjusting the incident light angle. The schematic diagram of this method is shown in Fig. S11.

In addition to the distribution of high symmetry points, the far-field polarization characteristics of the Bloch-SPP mode at each point can also be detected through the momentum space imaging system. Figure 3d–f denote the polarization directions of the plasmonic O-hole (Fig. 3d), X-hole (Fig. 3e) and OX-hole (Fig. 3f) arrays at the Γ(1), X(1), M(1), Γ(2) and X(2) points. The gray dashed lines, from dark to light, mean 1st BZ, 2nd BZ and the third Brillouin zone (3rd BZ), respectively. For the same nanohole array, the polarization directions of X(1) and Γ(2), as well as M(1) and X(2), remain consistent in pairs, but they differ from the polarization direction of Γ(1), indicating the regulation effect of high symmetry points on polarization. For the same high symmetry point from different nanohole arrays, the polarization directions adhere to a specific trend as the unit symmetry decreases from C∞v, C4v to Cs, as shown in Fig. 3g–k. For illustration, consider the positions of the five high-symmetry points indicated in Fig. 3b, the polarization direction at the Γ(1) point rotates from 135° to 180°, the X(1) and Γ(2) points deflect from 90° to 0°, and the M(1) and X(2) points vary from 135° to 45°, respectively. The original data for the polarization of the three nanohole arrays are presented in Fig. S12. Furthermore, the degree of polarization is influenced by the symmetry of the structural units, as demonstrated in Table 1. Notably, the degree of polarization at X(2) point in the OX-hole array is maximized to 0.59. In the plasmonic lattices, the progressive symmetry reduction from C∞v, C4v to Cs induces more pronounced structural anisotropy, which simultaneously modifies the Bloch-SPP mode degeneracy33 and near-field coupling efficiency34. These synergistic effects improve the lattices’ optical response along specific polarization directions and yield a high polarization degree at X(2) point, thereby facilitating the regulation of light-matter interactions.

ASE of Nile red modulated by the Bloch-SPP mode at X(2) point on the plasmonic OX-hole array

As a typical light emission phenomenon, ASE is characteristic of the state preceding the laser oscillation threshold35,36. It becomes progressively important as a competitor to or even a substitute for pulsed source in optical amplifiers. The Bloch-SPP mode offers a vital solution for generating high quality ASE with stronger emission intensity, narrower linewidth, polarization selectivity and tunable emission direction37. An angle-resolved spectral system is utilized to measure the reflection dispersion of varying environmental refractive index (RI) and the ASE of Nile Red, supported by the Bloch-SPP mode of the plasmonic OX-hole array. As illustrated in the schematic diagram of Fig. 4a, θin represents the incidence angle, θout denotes the emission angle, both of which lie within the same plane, and φ is the azimuth angle. The optical path and the encapsulation method of Nile Red are presented in Figs. S13, S14, respectively. The wavelength of the Bloch-SPP mode at X(2) point (green curve in the right panel of Fig. 4b) falls within the luminescence range of Nile Red (purple curve in the right panel of Fig. 4b) falls within the incident angle of 37°. This is because the energies of the resonance modes are redshifted when the environmental RI of the plasmonic OX-hole array changes from air (n0 = 1.00) to CH3OH (n1 = 1.33), as presented in Fig. 4b. Specifically, the valley corresponding to the gray dashed line indicates the exact location of the resonance mode at the X(2) point (720 nm), while the purple curve shows the emission spectrum of the fluorescent dye Nile Red, with its luminous wavelength ranging from 600 nm to 820 nm.

Under the effect of the Bloch-SPP mode, the ASE of Nile Red is enhanced four-fold compared to that on a glass slide (Fig. 4c), with an amplification occurring at the X(2) point. This enhancement can be elucidated by the optical feedback mechanism shown in Fig. 4d. Nile Red functions as a typical four-energy level system that can be excited from the ground state S0 to the S3 energy level by a 532 nm pump source. The electrons in the S3 state undergo non-radiative decay, transitioning to S2 state. Spontaneous emission then occurs as the system transitions from S2 to S1. The enhancement effect is maximized when the Bloch-SPP mode is optimally coupled with the emission of Nile Red derived from S2 to S1 energy level transition. Concurrently, the photons generated by the ASE are directed towards the metal surface, further exciting the Bloch-SPP mode, which results in effective resonance feedback and optical amplification. The gain medium-coated plasmonic lattice exhibits two distinct light-matter interaction pathways. The first interaction is direct excitation of Nile Red molecules by incident radiation, and the second involves resonant coupling between Nile Red’s emission and the plasmonic cavity modes. Although the spontaneous emission of Nile Red covers the range of 600–820 nm, the cavity mode couples only with emission near 720 nm, provided that both energy and spatial overlap conditions are satisfied.

As illustrated in Fig. 4e, the intensity of ASE progressively increases with the incident power density, and the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the ASE narrows to 56 nm upon surpassing the threshold power density (Pth) of 196 W/cm². The inset depicts the variation in ASE spectra as power density increases. Furthermore, the influence of the Bloch-SPP mode on ASE is also evident in its polarization characteristics. Figure 4f shows the polarization plot of the emission intensity at a wavelength of 720 nm as a function of the polarization angle. The plasmonic OX-hole array presents significant elliptically polarized coherence, with extreme values at 30° and 120° under 1.13 Pth (purple squares), whereas the emission from the glass slide demonstrates circularly polarized coherence (gray circles). The polarized emission of ASE can be well fitted using a typical electric dipole resonance angular function (purple fitted line in Fig. 4f), expressed as Acos4(δ-δ0) + Bcos4(δ-δ1), where δ represents the polarization angle, and δ0 and δ1 are fitting constants. The ASE characteristics of the plasmonic O-hole and X-hole arrays are also presented in Fig. S15 and Table S1, further illustrating how the enhanced polarization degree of the Bloch-SPP mode strengthens the interaction between light and matter. In addition to the unit morphology of the square lattice, the environmental RI is also a factor that modulates the polarization of the Bloch-SPP mode and its corresponding ASE. The polarization characteristics of the three plasmonic nanohole arrays at the X(2) wavelength are examined under varying RI conditions. The polarization angles exhibit variability with changes in RI, as illustrated in Fig. S16. As a result, the polarization angle of ASE on the plasmonic OX-hole array differs from that of the Bloch-SPP mode at the X(2) point when the environmental RI changes from 1.00 to 1.33.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have systematically investigated the polarization characteristics of Bloch-SPP mode at high symmetry points in plasmonic square lattices with a 600 nm period, featuring symmetry evolutive unit cells transitioning from C∞v (O-hole) to C4v (X-hole) to Cs (OX-hole). Our findings reveal that as the unit symmetry decreases, the polarization angle at the Γ(1) point undergoes a 45° deflection, while the polarization states at the other four high-symmetry points (X(1), M(1), Γ(2) and X(2)) experience a 90° shift. Notably, the OX-hole array exhibits the highest polarization degree of 0.59 at the X(2) point, enabling enhanced coupling with the Bloch-SPP mode. The emission intensity of Nile Red is amplified by a factor of four compared to that on a glass slide, exhibiting elliptically polarized coherence when the excitation power density exceeds the Pth of 196 W/cm2. These results underscore the critical role of Bloch-SPPs mediated polarization control in plasmonic lattices and provide a robust platform for manipulating light-matter interactions at the nanoscale. Our findings establish a foundation for future explorations into Bloch-SPPs based active nanophotonic systems, with potential implications for integrated photonics and quantum optical applications.

Materials and methods

The preparation procedure of the plasmonic nanohole arrays

Nanoimprint lithography is utilized to create the predefined pattern of square lattice on Al foil, which has been polished using a mixture of perchloric acid and ethanol (volume ratio = 1:7). The imprint pressure applied is 5 MPa. Electrochemical anodization in an aqueous solution of citric acid (0.3 M) is performed to fabricate the AAO membrane. During the anodization process, regions corresponding to the predefined pattern exhibit a high growth rate due to the concentrated electric field, resulting in nanoholes arranged in square lattice. A layer of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), dissolved in dichloromethane at a concentration of 0.25 g/ml, is spin-coated onto the top surface of the AAO membrane as a protective layer. The residual Al substrate is removed by the aqueous copper chloride solution (0.25 M). The PMMA adhered to the AAO membrane is dissolved in acetone. Then, the AAO membrane can be transferred to the glass slide. The O-hole array can be achieved when the top side of AAO membrane is faced upward. For the X-hole and the OX-hole arrays, following removal of the aluminum substrate, samples are immersed in 5 wt% phosphoric acid at 55 °C for etching away the barrier layer. As corrosion time increases, X-hole and OX-hole appear sequentially. The methods for removing PMMA and transferring AAO membranes remain consistent with those described above. Subsequently, Ag with a thickness of 20 nm is deposited on AAO membranes via magnetron sputtering (JCP 2000, Technol). The morphologies of these nanohole arrays are observed using scanning electron microscopy (Gemini 360, Zeiss).

The momentum space imaging system

This system facilitates the information conversion between the real space and the momentum space of the sample through a number of optical components, and uses the photoelectric detection equipment to carry out the momentum space imaging and measurement. A 450 nm femtosecond laser (PHAROS-ph2-10W, Light Conversion) is focused onto the sample via a beam splitter and a 50× objective lens (MXPLFLN50×, NA 0.8). The optical signal is successively captured by the two-dimensional charge-coupled device (Axio Cam ERc 5 s Rev. 2.0, Zeiss) after passing through the beam splitter mirror, lens 1, aperture, lens 2, polarizer and filter. With the exception of the light source, all optical components are arranged along the same optical axis.

The measurement of angle-resolved reflection/emission spectra

A white-light source from a halogen lamp (iDH2000, Ideaoptics) is utilized for measuring reflection spectra. Reflection light is captured by optical fibers and transmitted to a fiber spectrometer (PG 2000, Ideaoptics). To acquire the angle-resolved reflection spectra, an automatic rotating stage is utilized, adjusting from 0° to 60° in 1° increments while maintaining the azimuthal angle at a constant value of 0°. For the measurement of ASE, the angle range is from 0° to 50°, utilizing a 532 nm laser (Lasever, LSR532HX-5W) as the excitation light source.

Simulation of potential distribution

The potential distributions of the AAO membranes are simulated by the software of COMSOL Multiphysics. The AC/DC module and relative permittivity model are adopted in the simulation. Zero charge boundary condition. The electric potential is 240 V, and the dielectric constant of alumina is 5.7.
