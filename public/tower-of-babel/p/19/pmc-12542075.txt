NR2F6 regulates Temozolomide resistance in glioma via the E2F2-PARP1 pathway
Authors: Bo Wang, Pengfei Xue, Rongrong Li, Yaxin Liang, Zhaohao Wang, Lili Yan, Qianqian Zhang, Conghui Tian, Lili Yi, Cuncun Yang, Xin Li, Juan Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioma is the most common primary malignant brain tumor in adults. Temozolomide (TMZ) represents a standard-of-care chemotherapeutic agent in glioblastoma (GBM). However, the development of drug resistance constitutes a significant hurdle in the treatment of malignant glioma. Elucidating the mechanisms of temozolomide (TMZ) resistance in glioma is of critical clinical importance for improving patient prognosis and developing novel therapeutic strategies.MethodsWe obtained RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data of 648 glioma samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and 325 samples from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) as study cohorts. Additionally, we validated the expression characteristics of the NR2F6 gene in our in-house cohort of glioma patients. Furthermore, we investigated the potential mechanism of NR2F6 in TMZ resistance in glioma by constructing TMZ-resistant cell lines in vitro. Statistical analyses and graphical work were primarily performed using R language and GraphPad Prism software.ResultsWe observed a significant upregulation of NR2F6 expression in high-grade gliomas, which is associated with an unfavorable prognosis in patients. Concurrently, our findings revealed a significant upregulation of NR2F6 in drug-resistant cells, which induced TMZ resistance in glioma cells via the E2F2-PARP1 axis.ConclusionIn brief, NR2F6, as a nuclear transcription factor, enhances the transcription of E2F2.The increased expression of E2F2 enhances PARP1 expression, which in turn facilitates TMZ-mediated DNA damage repair, thereby diminishing glioma sensitivity to TMZ.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-03986-6.

Introduction

Gliomas, primary brain tumors, are hypothesized to originate from neural stem or progenitor cells harboring tumor-initiating genetic mutations. These tumors are classified based on histological and molecular features according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of central nervous system (CNS) tumors, which categorizes them into CNS WHO grades 1–4 [1], reflecting an increasing degree of malignancy. Despite advancements in aggressive multimodal therapies, the median overall survival (OS) for patients with glioblastoma (GBM), the most malignant glioma subtype, remains approximately 12–15 months. Notably, China ranks among the top three countries with the highest incidence and mortality rates for CNS cancers [2, 3].

Similar to other aggressive malignancies, GBM exhibits a pronounced anti-DNA damage phenotype, which contributes to its chemoresistance [4]. The introduction of the alkylating agent temozolomide (TMZ) has modestly improved median survival in GBM patients. However, TMZ, the first-line therapeutic agent for GBM, is associated with inevitable resistance in the majority of patients [5, 6], significantly limiting its clinical efficacy. This resistance, whether inherent or acquired, underscores the critical need to elucidate the molecular mechanisms governing DNA damage repair pathways to overcome TMZ resistance in GBM. Concurrently, identifying novel therapeutic targets remains a priority for improving clinical outcomes.

Nuclear receptors (NRs) constitute a superfamily of transcription factors that mediate hormonal and nutrient signaling to regulate diverse biological processes, including development, reproduction, and metabolism [7]. NR2F6 (nuclear receptor subfamily 2, group F, member 6), also known as Ear-2 or COUP-TFIII, is an orphan nuclear receptor. Emerging evidence suggests that NR2F6 is overexpressed in several human malignancies and may influence cellular differentiation, apoptosis, and survival [8, 9]. For instance, NR2F6 expression is elevated in breast cancer compared to normal breast tissue, and its lower expression in the NCI-60 cancer cell panel correlates with increased sensitivity to microtubule-targeting chemotherapeutic agents [10, 11]. RNA interference (RNAi)-mediated knockdown of NR2F6 induces apoptosis in colon cancer cells by suppressing the expression of the X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis protein (XIAP) [8]. These findings collectively suggest that NR2F6 may exert context-dependent roles in various cancers.

The E2F family of transcription factors plays a pivotal role in regulating cell cycle progression and cellular proliferation [12]– [13]. Accumulating evidence suggests that dysregulated expression or activation of E2F2 is frequently observed in various malignancies. Glioblastoma, an aggressive and prevalent form of primary brain tumor in adults, originates from glial cells within the brain. Emerging research indicates that E2F2 may be implicated in glioblastoma pathogenesis, as aberrant expression of E2F2 has been detected in glioblastoma cells, suggesting its potential contribution to tumor development and progression [14]. RNA interference-mediated knockdown of E2F2 has been shown to suppress the tumorigenic potential of human glioblastoma cells, underscoring its involvement in gliomagenesis and its potential as a therapeutic target in malignant gliomas [15]. E2F2 is also integral to various biological processes, including cell cycle regulation, differentiation, and apoptosis. During the S phase, E2F2 forms a complex with DP1, facilitating DNA synthesis and ensuring proper cell cycle progression [16].

PARP1, a key member of the poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase family, plays a critical role in modulating DNA repair pathways [17]. It catalyzes the addition of ADP-ribose units during DNA repair processes. PARP cleavage, a hallmark of apoptosis and caspase activation, is often associated with impaired DNA damage responses [18]. Consequently, inhibition of PARP1 induces DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) in tumor cells [17], and PARP inhibitors (PARPi) have emerged as promising therapeutic agents for cancer treatment [19].

In this study, we identified NR2F6 as a negative regulator of temozolomide (TMZ) treatment in gliomas. Suppression of NR2F6 enhances the sensitivity of glioma cells to TMZ therapy, highlighting its potential role in modulating therapeutic responses.

Results

NR2F6 expression was upregulated in GBM and demonstrated a negative correlation with patient prognosis

As a tumor-promoting regulatory factor, NR2F6 has been implicated in the progression of various cancers, including breast cancer [20], liver cancer [21], and melanoma [22]. However, its specific role in glioma remains unexplored. To investigate the involvement of NR2F6 in glioma, we analyzed its expression in 648 RNA-sequenced samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and 325 RNA-sequenced samples from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database, stratified by glioma grade. Our analysis revealed a significant upregulation of NR2F6 in GBM (Fig. 1A-B). This finding was further corroborated by Western blot (WB) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) of clinical glioma tissue samples, which also demonstrated elevated NR2F6 expression in GBM (Fig. 1C-D). Notably, NR2F6 expression was markedly higher in high-grade gliomas, suggesting a potential association with disease severity. To assess the prognostic value of NR2F6, glioma patients were stratified into low- and high-expression groups. Analysis of overall survival (OS) revealed that patients with lower NR2F6 expression exhibited significantly longer OS compared to those with higher expression in both the TCGA and CGGA cohorts (Fig. 1E-F). These findings indicate that elevated NR2F6 expression is associated with reduced overall survival and progression-free survival, underscoring its role as a prognostic marker for poor outcomes in glioma patients. Collectively, these data demonstrate that NR2F6 is upregulated in high-grade gliomas and negatively correlates with patient prognosis.

The DNA repair signaling pathway was enriched in the NR2F6-High group

To elucidate the functional role of NR2F6 in glioblastoma (GBM), we analyzed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) databases. Comparative analysis between NR2F6-High and NR2F6-Low groups in the TCGA dataset identified 591 upregulated and 67 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A). Similarly, in the CGGA dataset, 378 upregulated and 103 downregulated genes were observed (Fig. 2B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the gene expression profiles demonstrated a significant enrichment of DNA repair-related genes in the NR2F6-High group across both TCGA and CGGA datasets (Fig. 2C-D). And we share the top pathways that changes in high-low NR2F6 groups in TCGA and CGGA(S2 and S3). These findings suggest a potential involvement of NR2F6 in mediating temozolomide (TMZ) resistance in glioma.

The expression of NR2F6 was found to be up-regulated in LN229R, which was associated with altered cellular proliferation

The development of resistance to temozolomide (TMZ) represents a critical obstacle in the treatment of glioblastoma (GBM) [23]. To investigate the potential role of NR2F6 in TMZ resistance, we established a TMZ-resistant LN229 cell line-LN229R(figure S3). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) and Western blot (WB) analyses demonstrated a marked increase in NR2F6 expression in the resistant cell line (Fig. 3A-B). Immunofluorescence assays further confirmed elevated NR2F6 expression, as evidenced by higher fluorescence intensity inLN229R compared to LN229 (Fig. 3C). In the LN229R, cellular proliferation was markedly reduced following the downregulation of NR2F6 compared to the control group (Fig. 3D). These results indicate that NR2F6 is involved in the drug resistance of glioma cells.

The expression of E2F2 was elevated in LN229R

To elucidate the regulatory mechanism of NR2F6 in TMZ resistance in glioma, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis was conducted on NR2F6-High and NR2F6-Low groups derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) databases. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of the gene expression data revealed significant enrichment of the E2F signaling pathway in the NR2F6-High group (Fig. 4A-B). Subsequent analysis demonstrated that E2F2, a key member of the E2F family, was significantly up-regulated in the NR2F6-High group across both TCGA and CGGA datasets (Fig. 4C-D). To assess the potential role of E2F2 in TMZ resistance in glioma, we utilized LN229R. Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis confirmed a significant up-regulation of E2F2 expression in these cells (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, upon NR2F6 knockdown in the cell line, a notable reduction in E2F2 expression was observed (Fig. 4F). This research result indicates that E2F2 is involved in the drug resistance of glioma, and NR2F6 could regulate the expression level of E2F2.

PARP1 expression was upregulated in LN229R

As a key member of the poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase family, PARP1 plays a critical role in regulating biological processes, particularly in DNA repair pathways [24], through its catalytic activity in ADP-ribose binding. Bioinformatics analysis using the JASPAR database predicted that NR2F6 possesses a binding site within the promoter region of E2F2 (Fig. 5A), while E2F2 was identified to have a binding site in the promoter region of PARP1 (Fig. 5B). However, analysis of TCGA and CGGA datasets revealed no significant difference in PARP1 expression between NR2F6-high and NR2F6-low groups (Fig. 5C-D). To investigate the potential role of PARP1 in TMZ resistance in glioma, we utilized LN229R. qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of PARP1 expression in these resistant cells (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, knockdown of NR2F6 in the LN229R resulted in a marked reduction in PARP1 expression (Fig. 5F). These results indicate that PARP1 is involved in the drug resistance of glioma, and NR2F6 could regulate the expression level of PARP1.

NR2F6 modulates TMZ resistance in glioma via the E2F2-PARP1 signaling axis

As a transcription factor, NR2F6 plays a pivotal role in regulating the expression of multiple genes. To elucidate the mechanistic interplay among NR2F6, E2F2, and PARP1, we observed a significant reduction in the expression levels of E2F2 and PARP1 following NR2F6 knockdown in the LN229 glioma cell line (Fig. 6A). Then we overexpressed E2F2 while down-regulating NR2F6 in LN229 cell line, we found that the expression of PARP1 was higher than that of NR2F6 knockdown alone, which further proved that NR2F6 regulated PARP1 expression through E2F2 (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, we confirmed that E2F2 overexpression could rescue the reduction in cell proliferation induced by NR2F6 knockdown as demonstrated by CCK-8 assays in both LN229 and LN229R (Fig. 6C-D). These findings suggest NR2F6 may modulates TMZ resistance via the E2F2-PARP1 signaling axis in glioma.

Discussion

Temozolomide (TMZ) is the preferred chemotherapy drug for glioblastoma multiforme (GBM); intrinsic or acquired resistance to temozolomide significantly limits the efficacy of this drug [25]. Therefore, to find the mechanism of TMZ resistance is urgent scientific problem.

In our experiments, we found that downregulation of NR2F6 reduced the expression of E2F2 and PARP1 in LN229 cell line, while downregulation of NR2F6 and overexpression of E2F2 increased the expression of PARP1, Which proves that the NR2F6-E2F2-PARP1 axis has the potential to exist. Although this axis is inferred, but further experiments proved that NR2F6 was involved in the drug resistance of glioma through the E2F2-PARP1 axis.

Therefore, Our study uncovers a novel regulatory axis centered on NR2F6 that drives temozolomide (TMZ) resistance in glioma through coordinated regulation of E2F2 and PARP1. The elevated NR2F6 expression observed in TMZ-resistant gliomas aligns with recent findings demonstrating nuclear receptor family members as key mediators of neural development [26]. In non-small cell lung cancer, NR2F6 also promotes the progression of tumor cells, which is consistent with our research findings [27]. The conserved upregulation of NR2F6 across clinical specimens and LN229-derived resistant models strengthens its biological significance in glioma progression [28].

Mechanistically, our data reveal that NR2F6 simultaneously activates E2F2-mediated cell cycle progression and PARP1-dependent DNA repair, creating synergistic resistance mechanisms. The E2F2 overexpression we observed corroborates studies linking cell cycle dysregulation [29], while elevated PARP1 levels mirror findings in other PARP inhibitor-resistant cancers [30]. This pathway activation provides a molecular rationale for the limited efficacy of TMZ monotherapy in recurrent glioblastoma [31]. Our results directly support ongoing clinical efforts combining TMZ with PARP inhibitors [32], suggesting NR2F6 expression could serve as a predictive biomarker for such combination therapies.

Several limitations merit consideration. While our in vitro models recapitulate key resistance mechanisms, they cannot fully model blood-brain barrier effects on drug penetration [33]. The inflammatory tumor microenvironment’s potential influence on NR2F6 activity also requires investigation using co-culture systems with microglia and astrocytes [34]. Furthermore, NR2F6’s role in maintaining glioma stem cell populations remains unexplored—a critical gap given these cells’ therapeutic resistance [35]. Future studies should evaluate NR2F6 inhibitors in orthotopic models and explore synergistic combinations with emerging modalities like tumor-treating fields [36].

These findings redefine therapeutic resistance mechanisms in neuro-oncology by identifying NR2F6 as a central regulator connecting transcriptional reprogramming and DNA repair pathways. The clinical-translational potential of targeting this nuclear receptor is underscored by recent advances in developing blood-brain barrier-penetrant NR2F6 modulators [37]. Our work establishes a framework for overcoming TMZ resistance through coordinated inhibition of cell cycle progression and DNA repair pathways, offering a potential novel therapeutic target for glioma treatment.

Return to this topic, we have not yet received direct support from mechanism experiments such as chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) or reporter gene assays for the NR2F6 - E2F2 - PARP1 axis. We will examine the binding of NR2F6 to the E2F2 promoter and E2F2 to the PARP1 promoter by CHIP assay or dual luciferase assay in future experiments, which will demonstrate the existence of the NR2F6-E2F2-PARP1 axis. In addition, the lack of in vivo data and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models remains a limitation of the current study. We will construct a NR2F6 knockdown lentivirus and this lentivirus transferred into LN229 drug-resistant cell line to obtain a stable NR2F6 drug-resistant cell line. These two groups of cell lines were injected subcutaneously into nude mice. Finally, the tumor size of nude mice was observed to further prove that NR2F6 is involved in the drug resistance of glioma at the vector level.

Methods details

Gene set enrichment analysis

Hallmaker gene set v7.0 collections were downloaded from Molecular Signatures Database as the target sets with which GSEA performed using the R software. The whole transcriptome of all tumor samples was used for GSEA, and only gene sets with NOM P < 0.05 and FDR q < 0.05 were considered as significant.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human glioma cell line LN229 was procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Clinical glioma specimens

Transcriptomic and clinicopathological data from glioma patients were obtained from two publicly available databases: The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) [28, 38]and the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) [39]. From the TCGA dataset, RNA sequencing data and clinical information were analyzed for 648 glioma cases, comprising 144 glioblastoma (GBM) and 504 low-grade glioma (LGG) samples. Similarly, RNA sequencing data and clinical characteristics from 325 glioma cases were analyzed from the CGGA database.

Development of TMZ-Resistant cell lines

LN229 cells were plated in 96-well plates, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of temozolomide (TMZ) was quantified using the CCK-8 assay. Over a five-month period, the TMZ concentration was systematically escalated from an initial low dose of 10 µM to a final high concentration of 200 µM. Upon achieving stable cell growth, the drug concentration was incrementally doubled. Each concentration gradient was maintained for 15 days until the establishment of the 200 µM-resistant cell line.

The resulting TMZ-resistant cells were designated as LN229R(S 1).

Quantitative Real-Time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells or frozen glioma tissues using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). RNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 260 nm using a NanoDrop instrument (Wilmington, DE, USA). Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis was performed using 1 µg of total RNA with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Quantitative PCR was conducted using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, UK). Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the internal control. Primer sequences were as follows: GAPDH (forward: 5’-GGAGCGAGATCCCTCCAAAAT-3’, reverse: 5’-GGCTGTTGTCATACTTCTCATGG-3’), NR2F6 (forward: 5’-AGGTGGACTGCGTGGTGTG-3’, reverse: 5’-GTTGCGGCGGATGCTTCG-3’), E2F2 (forward: 5’-GCCCGTCGTCCCTGAGTTC-3’, reverse: 5’-CAGCGAAGTGTCATACCGAGTC-3’), and PARP1 (forward: 5’-CAGAGTATGCCAAGTCCAACAGAAG-3’, reverse: 5’-CAGCGGTCAATCATGCCTAGC-3’).

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer, and protein concentrations were quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Samples containing 30–50 µg of protein were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with NR2F6 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and GAPDH (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) (at 4 °C overnight. After washing with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China).

Cellular Immunofluorescence staining

Cells cultured in 24-well plates were washed with cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 10 min at room temperature. After blocking with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin(BSA)for 1 h, cells were incubated with a primary rabbit monoclonal antibody against human NR2F6 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 4 °C overnight. Following PBS washes, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 1 min, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (FV-1000, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

CCK-8 assay

Glioblastoma cells (LN229 and LN229 TMZ-resistant cell lines) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000–10,000 cells per well and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. Test compounds were added, and cells were incubated for 48 h. Subsequently, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well containing 100 µL of culture medium, and plates were incubated for 2 h (incubation time was optimized based on cell density). Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Plasmid transfection

LN229 and LN229R were seeded in 6-well plates at 60% confluency in antibiotic-free medium 24 h prior to transfection. For each transfection, 2 µg of plasmid DNA was diluted in 100 µL of Opti-MEM and mixed with 5 µL of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at a 1:2.5 ratio. The DNA-lipid complexes were incubated for 15 min at room temperature before being added dropwise to the cells. After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C, the transfection medium was replaced with complete growth medium. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for RNA analysis.

SiRNA transfection

LN229 and LN229R were cultured in 6-well plates until reaching 60% confluency in antibiotic-free medium 24 h before transfection. siRNA (20 µM stock) was diluted in Opti-MEM and mixed with Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The siRNA-lipid complexes were incubated for 20 min at room temperature before being added dropwise to the cells. After 6 h of incubation at 37 °C, the transfection medium was replaced with complete growth medium. Cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for analysis.

The sense strand of si-NR2F6 was 5’-GCUACUGCUACCUACUACA-3’, and the antisense strand was 5’-UGUAGUAGGUAGCAGUAGC-3’.

The sense strand of si-NC was 5’-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGU-3’, and the antisense strand was 5’-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAA-3’.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis and scoring

Tissue specimens were fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin. Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through sequential immersion in the following solutions: 100% xylene I (10 min), 100% xylene II (10 min), 100% ethanol I (5 min), 100% ethanol II (5 min), 95% ethanol (5 min), 85% ethanol (5 min), 80% ethanol (5 min), 75% ethanol (5 min), 70% ethanol (5 min), and double-distilled water (10 min). Antigen retrieval was performed by boiling the slides in 0.01 mol/L citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 99 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubating the slides in 0.3% H2O2 in methanol for 30 min. Non-specific binding was blocked with Bovine Serum Albumin(BSA)at room temperature for 10 min. The slides were then incubated with NR2F6 (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) chromogen system. Staining intensity was quantified by a trained investigator using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics), with the mean density calculated as the integrated optical density divided by the area of interest. The IHC intensity for each slide was determined as the average mean density derived from three representative images. Protein expression levels were categorized as follows: “weak” (IHC intensity 0–0.15), “moderate” (IHC intensity 0.16–0.25), “strong” (IHC intensity 0.26–0.35), and “very strong” (IHC intensity > 0.35).

Statistical analysis

Grayscale values from immunoblotting were quantified using ImageJ software. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.2) and GraphPad Prism 8 (version 8.0.1). Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (S.E.M.) and represent results from at least three independent experiments. Differences between two groups were assessed using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Significance levels were defined as follows: ns, not significant (P > 0.05); *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.
