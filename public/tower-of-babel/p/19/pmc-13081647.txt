Past diseases history: an important predictor of persistent learning disability in US children
Authors: Song Mao, Liangxia Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe aimed to analyse the role of past diseases history (PDH), including developmental delay (DD), autism, intellectual disability (ID), attention deficit disorder (ADD) / attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and asthma in persistent learning disability (PLD) risk in children.Methods1952 children were recruited from the US National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) from 2019 to 2023 according to a predefined criteria. Logistic regression analysis was performed to detect the association between PDH and PLD risk. Threshold effect analysis was used to detect the threshold age point. Interaction and Joint effects analyses between age/sex and PDH with PLD risk was conducted. Receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis was used to investigate the single and incremental predictive value of PDH on PLD risk. Population attributable fraction (PAF) of PDH in PLD risk was computed. Sensitivity analyses were performed by sub-age/sex group analyses, repeated analyses after propensity score matching (PSM), and E-values to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounders.ResultsHistory of DD, autism, ID, ADD/ADHD, and asthma were all significantly associated with increased PLD risk. ID history resulted in 13.25 more times of PLD risk. Increase of PDH numbers led to an elevating trend of PLD risk. Age over 8 years showed 6% decreasing trend of PLD risk (odds ratio (OR):0.94, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.89–0.99), while age over 12 years showed a lower PLD risk (OR < 1). 3 past diseases and 2–11 years old group had 10.78 more times PLD risk (OR: 11.78, 95% CI: 3.50, 39.68) compared with that in 2–11 years old children without PDH. 2 past diseases and female group had 2.24 more times PLD risk (OR: 3.24, 95% CI: 1.61, 6.50) compared with that in female without PDH. 2–11 years old and female showed a stronger joint effect with past diseases numbers for predicting PLD. PDH numbers had a good discriminatory power for predicting PLD risk. Combined five past diseases history accounted for over 10% of PAF for PLD risk. ID and ADD/ADHD, and autism and asthma history were the top contributors to PLD risk in female and male, respectively. Sensitivity analyses yielded similar results.ConclusionsPDH showed good prediction, discriminatory power and certain contribution to PLD risk, while lower than 12 years old, female, and increased PDH numbers may be indicators of PLD susceptibility. Early warning of PDH may be needed in specific populations with learning disability (LD).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13052-026-02225-6.Keywords: Developmental delay, Autism, Intellectual disability, ADHD, Asthma, Learning disability, Children

Background

Learning disability (LD), a significantly reduced ability to understand various new knowledge, usually started from childhood [1]. Children with LD are likely to present many medical and psychological problems, affecting neurodevelopment and even resulting in psychiatric and developmental disorders [2]. People with severe disabilities often show more challenging types of behavior [2]. Some LD cases may lead to a lasting effect on development [2]. In this sense, LD is a comprehensive disorder which need extra and special care, early and integrated intervention seems imperative.

LD causes are numerous and attributed to either genetic or environmental factors [3]. chromosomal aberration is an important cause of LD in children [4, 5]. It is reported that LD presented with a slight male preponderance and three times more prevalent in the Asian community [6, 7]. Of note, LD is not usually obvious at birth, mild LD is less likely to have a clear cause, and 40–80% of LD children have no identifiable cause [8]. Hence, to identify the possible risk factors for LD is very important. Many factors were proved to be associated with LD risk. Previous studies also focused on the risk factors of new-onset LD. For example, extremely low birth-weight children have a 50% chance of demonstrating some level of LD and a dose-response association between maternal smoking in pregnancy and LD severity existed [9, 10]. Male had a higher likelihood of having LD compared with females [11]. Health insurance, poverty level, and food situation were also significantly associated with LD in children [12]. A variety of factors contributed to the reading development with different aspects of the reading process [13]. Many individuals with autism often suffer from LD, autism also impacts learning, while some disorders overlapped with LD [14]. Strenuous efforts had been made to early predict and intervention of LD. However, some children with LD continued to adulthood. About 5% of school-aged children had difficulties with reading, writing or mathematics, while an estimated of 1-2.5% of general population were LD, which proved that many LD children persisted [6, 15].

It is traditionally regarded that LD outcome is likely to be dependent on the LD severity at school age, intelligence, socioeconomic status of parents, and the presence or absence of neurological impairment [1]. However, the detailed investigation regarding the prognostic trend of children with LD was rare. Considering the likely comorbid psychological and medical problems, we aimed to identify the association between past diseases (developmental delay (DD), autism, asthma, intellectual disability (ID) and attention deficit disorder (ADD) / attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and persistent LD (PLD) risk. Piecewise linear regression was used to assess the association between age and PLD risk. Receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis was performed to detect the single and additional predictive value of past diseases history (PDH) in PLD risk in children. Joint effects of PDH and age category/sex on PLD risk was also investigated. Population attributable fraction (PAF) of PDH for PLD risk was studied.

Methods

Study design and population

We used the data of National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) from 2019 to 2023, which enrolled a representative sample of US households. NHIS is conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. NHIS collected self-reported data on health topics. To obtain population-based estimates, each child was assigned a weight, which was composed of a base sampling weight and adjustment of both screener and topical nonresponse, selection of a single child, and various characteristics of children in different states. The children were defined as less than 18 years old. A standardized questionnaire gathered information on sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, along with PDH data. Inclusion criteria for this analysis required participants to be aged less than 18 years old, with complete data on the past and present status of LD. PLD was defined as that the children with LD history still have LD now. The recruited covariates included age, gender, race, region, poverty ratio, and general health. The past diseases/disorders history included asthma, ADD/ADHD, ID, autism, and DD. The patients without complete covariates data were excluded.

Our study was exempt from the Institutional Review Board due to the use of publicly available deidentified data. This investigation followed the Strengthening the Reporting of observational studies in Epidemiology (SROBE) reporting guidelines.

PDH and age category

The PDH included asthma, ADD/ADHD, ID, autism, and DD. For example, A standardized questionnaire “Has a doctor or other health professional ever told you that sample child had an asthma? If the answer is yes, the child was regarded as with asthma history. The disease history numbers were assigned 0 to 4, no relevant 5 diseases history observed was assigned to “0”, 1 of 5 diseases history observed was assigned to “1”, 2 of 5 diseases history observed was assigned to “2”, 3 of 5 diseases history observed was assigned to “3”, 4 and 5 of 5 diseases history observed was assigned to “4”. Age category was classified into “2 to 11 years” and “12 to 17 years”.

Covariates

During the data collection and analysis, we gathered sociodemographic and health-related data via a structured questionnaire, including self-reported age, sex, district, race, family structure, highest level of education of children’ parents, region, health insurance, poverty ratio, general health status, hearing status, vision status, diabetes history, prediabetes history and special education ever received.

Statistical analyses

We calculated nationally representative estimates by including strata, cluster, and weight in our analyses. We reported the sociodemographic characteristics and disorders history of children by PLD status. We used survey-weighted mean (95% CI) and survey-weighted percentage (95% CI) for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Survey-weighted linear regression was used to detect the association between continuous variables and PLD risk, and survey-weighted Chi-square test was applied to investigate the association between categorical variables and PLD risk. Logistic regression analysis was performed to detect the association between disorders (difficulty hearing sounds, difficulty seeing, DD history, autism history, ID history, ADD/ADHD history, diabetes history, prediabetes history and asthma history) and PLD risk (Model I: no adjusted factors, Model II: adjust for social and demographic factors, Model III adjust for social, demographic and disease history factors). We also investigated the association between PDH numbers and PLD risk, as well as single past disease with PLD risk. Threshold effect analysis was used to detect the threshold point of age for its association with PLD risk. The association between age and PLD risk before and after the threshold point was also investigated. Piecewise linear regression was used to assess the association between age and PLD risk. Additive and multiplicative interactions between age category (2–11 years, 12–17 years)/sex (female, male) and PDH numbers (0: without any 5 PDH, 1: with at least one of 5 PDH) were quantified. We made joint effects analysis of PDH numbers and age category/sex on PLD risk. ROC analysis was used to detect the predictive value of single PDH and PDH numbers on PLD risk. Net reclassification index (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) index of comparing single PDH with PDH numbers were computed. ROC analysis was used to investigate the additional predictive value of single PDH and PDH numbers on PLD risk. NRI and IDI of comparing social demographic characteristics with social demographic characteristics + single PDH/PDH numbers were also calculated. PAF of PDH in PLD risk was investigated.

In the sensitivity analyses, to test the robustness and potential variations in different subgroups, we repeated the analyses stratified by sex (male and female), and age subgroups (2–11 years, and 12–17 years). And, sensitivity analyses were performed as follows: (1) the characteristics of ever received special education and its association with PLD risk in terms of PDH numbers; (2) repeated the analysis of the association between disorders and PLD risk in subgroups, and ROC analysis of singe PDH and PDH numbers for PLD risk in subgroups; (3) association between PDH numbers/single PDH and PLD risk, joint effects analysis, ROC analysis of single PDH and PDH numbers for PLD risk and PAF analysis by using the data after the propensity score matching (PSM); (4) calculate E-values to assess the potential impact of unmeasured confounders on conclusions in the association between special education received/PDH and PLD risk.

The data analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2, provided by the R project for statistical computing, EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions, Inc., Boston, MA) and Free Statistics analysis platform (Version 2.0, Beijing, China, http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/ freestatistics). IRAP 2.2.0 (https://dceg.cancer.gov/tools/risk-assessment/irap) for PAF calculation. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participants characteristics

Out of 38400 children in the NHIS from 2019 to 2023 with available data, 1952 children who had complete data related to social, demographic characteristics, and PDH were included in this study. A total of 1656 PLD and 296 non-PLD children were involved. The weighted mean age was 11.18 years. 38.36% of the participants were female. Figure 1 showed the flowchart of participants recruitment. The baseline characteristics of enrolled children according to PLD status were in Table 1. Survey- weighted linear regression showed that no significant association between age and poverty ratio and PLD risk was observed. Survey-weighted Chi-square test indicated that no significant association between interview year, age category, sex, district, race, family structure, highest level of education of children’ parents, region, health insurance, hearing status, seeing status, diabetes and prediabetes history and PLD risk was observed. Significant associations were noted between general health status, DD history, autism history, ID history, ADD/ADHD history, asthma history and special education ever received and PLD risk (Table 1).

Association between PDH with PLD risk in children

The increase of PDH numbers (DD, autism, ID, ADD/ADHD, and asthma) resulted in obviously rising PLD risk in all models (model I, II and III). 4–5 of PDH yielded 28.07 more times of PLD risk (OR:29.07, p = 0.0009) than those without any of 5 PDH (model III, eTable 1). 2–5 of PDH yielded 1.51 more times of PLD risk (OR:2.51, p < 0.0001) than those with 0–1 of PDH (model III, eTable 1), while 3–5 of PDH yielded 5.86 more times of PLD risk (OR:6.86, p < 0.0001) than those with 0–2 of PDH (model III, eTable 1). For the association between single PDH (exclusion of co-occurrence of other 4 PDH) and PLD risk, all five PDH showed significantly increased PLD risk in all models (model I, II and III, eTable 1). ID showed a highest risk of PLD in children (model III, OR:14.25, p = 0.0096, eTable 1).

Threshold effect and curve fitting logistic analysis of age and PLD risk

The threshold point of age for its non-linear relationship with PLD risk was 8 years in all models (model I, II, III and IV, eTable 2). No significant association between age and PLD risk before 8 years in all models (model I, II, III and IV, eTable 2). Age was significantly associated with PLD risk after 8 years in all models (model I, II, III and IV, eTable 2).

Curve fitting logistic analysis indicated a significantly non-linear relationship between age and PLD risk (p = 0.047), the reference age point (OR = 1) was 12 years, which showed that age after 12 years was associated with a lower risk of PLD (Fig. 2).

Interaction between age category/sex and PDH numbers with PLD risk

Interaction analysis showed that the values of multiplicative scale, relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP) and synergy index (SI) in all models (model I, II and III) were not significant, which indicated that no significant multiplicative or additive interaction between age category (2–11 vs. 12–17 years) and PDH numbers (0 vs. 1–5) existed for PLD risk (eTable 3). Similar results were observed regarding the interaction between sex and PDH numbers with PLD risk (eTable 4). Joint effects analysis of PDH and age/sex on PLD risk

Joint effects analysis of PDH and age showed that 3 PDH and 2–11 years group had the highest PLD risk (OR: 11.78, 95% CI: 3.50, 39.68) compared with other groups after adjusting for confounding factors (model III). Joint effects analysis of PDH and sex showed that 4–5 PDH and male group had highest PLD risk (OR: 16.13, 95% CI: 2.15, 120.82) compared with other groups after adjusting for confounding factors (model III). Of note, female groups with 0, 1 and 2 PDH all yielded higher PLD risk compared with male groups with 0, 1 and 2 PDH, respectively (eTable 5, eFigure 1).

Model performance for predicting PLD risk

The predictive performance of PDH for PLD risk showed that PDH numbers had significantly higher area under the curve (AUC) value (0.6858, 95% CI:0.6564–0.7152) compared with other 5 PDH (eTable 6, eFigure 2). Meanwhile, PDH numbers yielded significantly higher NRI and IDI than that in other 5 PDH (eTable 7), which showed that PDH numbers had a better risk reclassification and discriminatory power performance for PLD risk.

PDH numbers also showed a better additive role for predicting PLD risk compared with single social and demographic characteristics group (AUC value: 0.7530, 95% CI:0.7259–0.7792, p < 10− 4, eTable 8, eFigure 3). The PDH numbers and social and demographic characteristics model yielded higher NRI and IDI value than single social and demographic characteristics model (eTable 9), which showed that PDH numbers had a better additive risk reclassification and discriminatory power performance for PLD risk.

PAF of PDH for PLD risk in children

eTable 10 and eFigure 4 presented the PAF of 5 PDH for PLD risk in children. The highest PAF of PLD risk attributed to single PDH was ADD/ADHD (3.28%, 95% CI: 2.03%-5.25%). Of note, the combination of 5 PDH (DD, autism, ID, ADD/ADHD, and asthma) yielded a PAF of 12.07% (95% CI: 9.04%-15.94%) for PLD risk in children.

Subgroup analysis in terms of sex showed that ADD/ADHD was associated with highest PAF for PLD risk in both male and female (male: 3.33%, 95% CI: 1.68%-6.51%; female: 2.89%, 95% CI: 1.45%-5.65%). Combination of 5 PDH yielded a PAF of 12.97% (95% CI: 8.97%-18.39%), 10.34% (95% CI: 6.30%-16.53%) for PLD risk in male and female, respectively.

Subgroup analysis in terms of age also showed that ADD/ADHD was associated with highest PAF for PLD risk in both 2–11 and 12–17 years old groups (2–11 years old: 3.29%, 95% CI: 1.84%-5.82%; 12–17 years old: 2.99%, 95% CI: 1.33%-6.58%). Combination of 5 PDH yielded a PAF of 12.28% (95% CI: 7.91%-18.59%), 11.31% (95% CI: 7.50%-16.71%) for PLD risk in 2–11 and 12–17 years old groups, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

Survey chi-square test showed that increased PDH numbers had higher ratio of ever received special education (eTable 11), which indicated that children with more PDH were likely to receive special education. It may explain the finding that children who ever received special education had higher PLD risk. Meanwhile, logistic regression analysis showed that children with 0 and 1 PDH who ever received special education all had significantly higher PLD risk compared with those without ever receiving special education. However, the E values of ORs of ever received special education and PLD risk in 0 and 1 PDH were less than the corresponding ORs, which indicated that the findings regarding the association between ever received special education and PLD risk in 0 and 1 PDH was not robust, certain confounding factors may influence the conclusion. Of note, the E value of OR for ever received special education and PLD risk in 3 diseases were higher than the corresponding ORs, which supported the finding that no association between ever received special education and PLD risk in 0 and 1 disease was comparatively robust (eTable 12). The E value of OR for the association between ID history and PLD risk was 7.01, which was less than the original value of 14.25 indicating other factors may be involved in this relationship (eTable 1). The E values of ORs for the threshold effect analysis of age and PLD risk were all more than the original values (eTable 2).

Subgroup analyses showed that no associations between vision status, diabetes history and prediabetes history and PLD risk were observed across all age and sex groups (eTable 13). Some difficulty hearing showed 2.11 more times of PLD risk in 12–17 years group than those without difficulty hearing. Hearing status was significantly associated with PLD risk in 12–17 years group (OR:3.11, 95% CI: 1.02–9.51, p = 0.0469, eTable 13). DD, ID and ADD/ADHD histories were significantly associated with PLD risk across all age and sex groups (eTable 13). Autism and Asthma histories were not associated with PLD risk among female and 12–17 years groups (eTable 13).

PDH numbers had significantly higher AUC value for predicting PLD risk compared with other 5 single PDH across all age and sex groups (eTable 14). PDH numbers yielded significantly higher NRI and IDI than that in ADD/ADHD and asthma across all age and sex groups (eTable 15). No marked difference of NRI and IDI between DD/ID and PDH numbers was observed among female and 12–17 years groups (eTable 15). Autism showed no differences of NRI and IDI with PDH numbers in 12–17 years group (eTable 15).

PSM yielded a matched 582 participants (291 PLD vs. 291 non-PLD). The SMDs of all variables between non-PLD and PLD were all less than 0.1, which showed a good match of covariates between PLD and non-PLD (eTable 16). Logistic regression analysis showed that all 5 PDH yielded a significantly higher PLD risk. PLD risk increased with the growing of PDH numbers (eTable 17).

Joint effect analysis showed that 1, 2 and 3 PDH groups in 2 to 11 years had higher PLD risk than corresponding groups in 12 to 17 years (eTable 18). Meanwhile, 0, 1 and 2 PDH groups in female had higher PLD risk than corresponding groups in male (eTable 18).

PDH numbers had significantly higher AUC value for predicting PLD risk than all other 5 single PDH (eTable 19). PDH numbers yielded significantly higher NRI and IDI than that in all other 5 single PDH (eTable 20).

eTable 21 and eFigure 5 presented the PAF of 5 PDH. The highest PAF of PLD risk attributed to single PDH was DD (9.43%, 95% CI: 5.14%-16.65%). Of note, the combination of 5 PDH (DD, autism, ID, ADD/ADHD, and asthma) yielded a PAF of 35.00% (95% CI: 25.95%-45.28%) for PLD risk. Subgroup analysis showed that combination of 5 PDH yielded a PAF of 36.59% (95% CI: 25.06%-49.89%), 24.89% (95% CI: 11.82%-45.04%), 41.45% (95% CI: 27.53%-56.88%), and 30.91% (95% CI: 19.05%-45.95%), for PLD risk in male, female, 2–11 and 12–17 years old group, respectively.

Discussion

LD, a public health problem across the world, are likely to show poor outcomes in children, even lasting to adulthood. PLD exerts great burden to children due to its long-term period. Based on the high prevalence of PLD in many diseases, we focused on the association between five PDH (DD, autism, ID, ADD/ADHD, and asthma) and PLD risk in children.

We found that DD, autism, ID, ADD/ADHD, and asthma histories increased PLD risk in children. Increase of PDH numbers yielded higher PLD risk, while ID history showed highest PLD risk in children among 5 PDH. Age after 8 years showed a decreasing trend of PLD risk, while age over 12 years was associated with a lower risk of PLD. Three PDH and 2–11 years group had the highest PLD risk compared with that in other groups. Female groups with 0, 1 and 2 PDH all yielded higher PLD risk compared with that in male groups with 0, 1 and 2 PDH, respectively. PDH numbers had a good discriminative and additive role for predicting PLD risk. ADD/ADHD showed a highest PAF for PLD risk among the 5 PDH. Combination of 5 PDH yielded an over 10% of PAF for PLD risk. These findings indicated that PDH played an important role in the persistence of LD in children.

Several mechanisms may account for our findings. First, LD itself is an important kind of developmental disabilities [16]. Pre-school DD was positively associated with subsequent LD [17]. Hence, DD may stimulate the progress of LD. The behavior-emotional problems are likely to co-occur in children with LD [18]. Meanwhile, communication difficulties were aggravated in children with LD [19]. Autism itself is likely to have social communication disorder, language developmental delay and loss of interest [20]. In this sense, autism may promote the development of LD indirectly. ADD/ADHD children often presented with social skills deficits, while ADHD symptoms affect learning of non-social information and may affect adequate learning of social and emotional information, which may contribute to LD [21]. ID leads to profound changes in the developmental trajectories for children, ID is also characterized by impairments in general mental abilities as well as in adaptive functioning across conceptual, social, and practical domains [22]. Children with ID are more likely to experience sleep disorders of insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness and sleep breathing disorders than typically developing children [23]. Insufficient and disrupted sleep can have negative impacts on a child’s physical and mental health, as well as influencing behavior, social competence, and learning [24]. Asthma in childhood is associated with an increased risk of school problems, including grade failure and LD, and this risk increases with rising severity of diseases [25]. Children with asthma are too vulnerable to participate in certain school activities due to the acute exacerbations of symptoms and stress [26]. All the above-mentioned data supported that asthma may induce and worsen LD.

Second, the five kinds of diseases are not fully overlapped, the increase of PDH numbers led to higher PLD risk. ID showed highest PLD risk, which may be due to that ID is likely to be comorbid with many behaviour problems [27]. Meanwhile, the E value (7.01) was lower than the original OR (14.25), which indicated that confounding factors was involved in the association between ID and PLD risk. A decreasing trend of PLD risk after 8 years old may be due to that more attention was paid to children after school age, special intervention may also be started around the school age. Joint effects analysis of five PDH and age in PLD also showed that 2 to 11 years old with 3 PDH group had higher PLD risk compared with that in 12 to 17 years old with 3 PDH group. Female with 2 or 3 PDH group had higher PLD risk compared with that in male with 2 or 3 PDH group, which may be due to that girls are more prone to somatic disorders, depression, and anxiety. These diseases are closely associated with LD risk. We also found that no multiplicative and additive interactions between PDH numbers and sex/age for PLD risk were observed, which indicated that these factors may independently affect PLD risk.

Finally, ROC analyses showed that a significantly higher AUC, NRI and IDI values were observed for both single and additive PDH numbers for predicting PLD risk, which further clarified that PDH numbers had an independent predictive value for PLD risk. It may be due to the facts that these PDH themselves were closely associated with LD symptoms. PAF calculation showed that total five PDH accounted for over 10% of PLD risk in children, and ADD/ADHD was the top cause, which may be due to that the symptoms of ADD/ADHD were mainly lack of concentration, easily distracted and forgetting [28]. These symptoms were somewhat overlapped with LD. Notably, sensitivity analyses showed that total five PDH accounted for at least 20% of PLD risk in children after PSM of social, demographic and other disorders, which indicated that exclusion of the imbalance of covariates increased the five PDHs’ contributions to PLD risk. It indicated that many other factors were involved in PLD development.

The interesting finding was that history of ever receiving special education was associated with high PLD risk, which may be due to that children with severe LD were more likely to receive special education. Our study also found that the increase of PDH numbers yielded a higher ratio of history of receiving special education, which supported the speculation that past history-induced PLD was likely to lead to more special education. On the other hand, we found that history of receiving special education was significantly associated with higher PLD risk in 0 and 1 past disease group, while history of receiving special education was not associated with PLD risk in 2 PDH group. The finding may be due to that children with fewer PDH may have other more severe symptoms leading to receive special education. PAF outcome showed that PDH numbers account for about 10% of PLD risk. Hence, it is sensible to speculate that children with fewer PDH still receiving special education may have other risk factors and present with more obvious symptoms, which easily results in PLD.

Subgroup analyses showed that female children with DD, ID and ADD/ADHD demonstrated higher PLD risk compared with that in male children with DD, ID and ADD/ADHD, respectively. Meanwhile, male children with autism and asthma had higher PLD risk compared with female children with autism and asthma, respectively. These findings may be due to the different prevalence of PDH between male and female. Previous studies also showed increased asthma prevalence in boys and male bias of autism prevalence [29, 30]. 2–11 years old children with autism, ADD/ADHD, and asthma had higher PLD risk compared with that in 12–17 years old children with autism, ADD/ADHD, and asthma, respectively, and 12–17 years old children with DD and ID had higher PLD risk compared with 2–11 years old children with DD and ID, respectively, which may be due to that autism, ADD/ADHD, and asthma are likely to remit with the increase of age while DD and ID at higher age means longer developmental and intelligence disorders.

Subgroup ROC analyses further supported our speculations. We found that PDH numbers in female and 2–11 years old children group yielded higher AUC values for predicting PLD risk than those in male and 12–17 years old children group, respectively. Meanwhile, DD and ID in 12–17 years old children group showed higher AUC values for predicting PLD risk than those in 2–11 years old children group. Female children with autism and asthma yielded higher ACU values or predicting PLD risk than male children with autism and asthma. These subgroup ROC analyses were consistent with the logistic regression results.

Notably, after PSM, PAF of PDH for predicting PLD risk increased a lot, which may be due to that other factors were involved in PLD risk. More in-depth studies should be performed in the future. In the past, many previous studies focused on LD risk and progress. Young and advanced maternal ages at childbirth were associated with the risk of ADHD and LD [31]. Pediatric atopic dermatitis was associated with greater odds of learning and memory difficulties, which was mainly limited to children with neurodevelopmental comorbidities, such as ADHD or LD [32]. Pediatric atopic dermatitis may exacerbate the symptoms of LD [33]. For children with traumatic brain injury, increased risk for developing ADHD and LD by adulthood was observed in the cases with least-severe injuries, which indicated that other factors are likely to contribute to the risk of ADHD and LD [34]. On the other hand, higher serum selenium concentration was associated with a lower risk of LD in US children [35]. Meanwhile, fluoride exposure was associated with increased risk of ADHD and lower intelligence quotient (IQ) in children, while not associated with LD risk [36]. Very low birth weight and fetal growth restriction may pose a risk for LD among adolescents with very low birth weight [37]. A high risk for LD in children was observed in low socioeconomic strata [38]. ADHD and LD had shared familial risk factors. Improper teaching, low educational level of the parents, and children’s social relationship contribute to LD risk. LD is also strongly associated with the risk of affective disorder, persisting well into midlife [39]. In terms of the above-mentioned evidence, LD risk and progress resulted from many factors, and mutual influence between LD and many risk factors may exist. Our study is a further and in-depth understanding of PLD risk factors.

Our study had several major strengths. First, analyses of the association between PDH numbers and types and PLD risk adjusted for social, demographic and other disorders factors can comprehensively reflect the role of PDH in PLD risk. ROC analysis further clarified the predictive and additional predictive value of PDH in PLD risk, which can yield a more robust result. Second, use of E-value and PSM can increase the accuracy of results. Analysis of joint association of PDH and age/sex is helpful for identifying the mostly risky group for PLD development. Finally, the calculation of PDHs’ PAFs for PLD risk can precisely detect the extent that PDH affect PLD risk.

Meanwhile, we should also pay attention to the limitations. First, the study was a population-based cross-sectional analysis, the recall bias may be inevitable. Future prospective studies may be needed.

Second, we mainly investigated the association between five PDH and PLD risk. Other kinds of diseases may also be involved in the association analysis with PLD risk. Finally, the comparatively small number of participants limited the statistical power, we may need to enroll more participants to increase the robustness.

Conclusions

ID, autism, DD, ADD/ADHD and asthma were all associated with increased risk of PLD in children, while ID showed the top risk. More PDH yielded higher PLD risk than fewer PDH. Age over 8 years showed a decreasing trend of PLD, while age over 12 years showed a lower PLD risk. 2–11 years old and female showed a stronger joint effect with PDH numbers for predicting PLD. Female with DD, ID and ADD/ADHD showed a higher PLD risk, while male with autism and asthma showed a higher PLD risk. DD, ID and ADD/ADHD in female were the top three PDH, while autism and asthma in male were the top two PDH for PAF of PLD risk. These findings indicated that demographic factors, including age and gender showed different degree of PLD risk in different PDH, which revealed the need of future in-depth study regarding the association between various demographic factors and PLD risk.
