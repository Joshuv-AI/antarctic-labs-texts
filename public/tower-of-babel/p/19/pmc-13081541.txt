Snacktivity in older women: accelerometer-derived step counts, cadence, and bout patterns associated with adiposity
Authors: Yanhua Qi, Si Chen, Ziwen Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSnacktivity—brief, high-frequency bouts of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) integrated into daily routines—may interrupt prolonged sitting and help accumulate total activity. Step count is a practical proxy for this pattern, yet the cadence thresholds that map short-bout stepping to MVPA and the relevance of bout–cadence patterns to adiposity remain unclear. This study aimed to examine the associations between accelerometer-derived step metrics and adiposity and to identify pragmatic step-based thresholds in older women.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study of 1,109 community-dwelling older women in Yantai, Shandong Province, China, with a mean age of 64.93 years (SD = 2.82). Step-based metrics (daily steps, MVPA and light-intensity physical activity (LPA) steps, cadence, and bout patterns) were derived from a waist-worn triaxial accelerometer. adiposity was defined using body-fat-ratio (BFR) categories assessed by multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. Multiple linear regression estimated associations with progressive adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and health-related covariates, with additional adjustment for total sedentary time. Sensitivity analyses replaced BFR with BMI and examined visceral fat mass (VFM) using linear regression. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses identified pragmatic step and cadence cut-points.ResultsMVPA step counts and cadence were consistently and inversely associated with adiposity (p < 0.001), whereas LPA-related metrics were largely non-significant. ROC analyses indicated modest discrimination, with higher performance for MVPA-based metrics than for LPA-based metrics. The optimal Youden index–derived thresholds were 1,846 MVPA steps/day (AUC = 0.625), 94.3 steps/min for MVPA cadence (AUC = 0.626), and 6,287 total steps/day (AUC = 0.600), while LPA steps showed near chance-level discrimination (AUC = 0.515). Accumulating ≥ 1,846 MVPA steps/day was consistently associated with lower adiposity-related outcomes across bout definitions (≥ 3, ≥ 5, ≥10 min), supporting a short-bout “snacktivity” pattern.ConclusionsAmong older women, MVPA-oriented step metrics—particularly ~ 1,846 MVPA steps/day and ~ 94.3 steps/min cadence—showed inverse associations with adiposity and outperformed LPA metrics. These thresholds may serve as pragmatic, low-barrier activity targets, but causal relationships require confirmation in longitudinal and experimental studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26912-5.Keywords: Snacktivity, Step count, Cadence, Bout duration, MVPA, Overweight, Obesity, Older women

Introduction

In 2022, an estimated 2.5 billion adults globally were classified as overweight, accounting for 43% of individuals aged 18 years and above [1]. Older women represent a particularly vulnerable subgroup, as obesity prevalence is generally higher among women and increases with age [2]. Excess body weight increases the risk of mortality from cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and other non-communicable conditions [1]. In this context, public health requires doable daily activity prescriptions that minimize equipment needs, fit within routines, and can be communicated in plain terms. However, despite increasingly clear public health recommendations, many older women continue to encounter unique barriers to achieving guideline-recommended levels of physical activity. Postmenopausal hormonal changes may contribute to declines in physical performance, including muscle strength and power [3], potentially reducing tolerance for sustained moderate-to-vigorous exercise. In addition, psychosocial factors—such as caregiving responsibilities [4]—may contribute to a tendency toward more fragmented, incidental daily movement rather than prolonged, structured exercise. Within this context, public health efforts should prioritize feasible, lifestyle-integrated activity prescriptions that minimize equipment requirements, fit seamlessly into daily routines, and are easily understood and translated into real-world practice [5].

Increasing moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) is acknowledged in public health as a fundamental strategy for obesity management. The proliferation of wearable devices have made it possible to capture movement at high temporal resolution, enabling quantification of brief, accumulated MVPA bouts—often termed “snacktivity”—that can be interspersed across the waking day [6]. While such patterns align with the pragmatic needs of older adults with limited time or functional capacity [7, 8]. Yet, debate persists about whether brief accumulated MVPA episodes (e.g., short bursts of brisk walking embedded within daily routines, often shorter than traditional ≥ 10-minute bouts) produce meaningful health gains. From a physiological perspective, short bouts of higher-intensity activity can elicit transient elevations in heart rate and metabolic demand and may improve postprandial metabolism, including reductions in blood glucose and triglyceride levels [9]. Emerging evidence further links these intermittent activity patterns to lower risks of all-cause mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events, suggesting that fragmented physical activity may still provide clinically meaningful physiological stimuli [8]. Accordingly, establishing empirical evidence linking short-bout MVPA to adiposity levels is essential for determining whether lifestyle-integrated activity patterns (i.e., “snacktivity”) offer sufficient physiological stimulus for obesity prevention.

Beyond MVPA, increasing light-intensity physical activity (LPA) has been proposed as another route to reduce sedentary time (ST) and increase daily energy expenditure. However, the LPA literature is equivocal [10]. Some studies report that substituting sedentary time with LPA is unrelated to body composition [11], others have demonstrated that reallocating 30 min of sedentary time to LPA is associated with significant reductions in body mass index (BMI) [12–14], waist circumference (WC) [12, 13] and body fat [14]in older adults. Still other analyses suggest that higher proportions of LPA may even correspond to greater adiposity under certain compositional trade-offs [15, 16]. Clarifying how step-based indicators anchored in different intensity bands relate to adiposity is therefore a priority.

Steps are an intuitive and widely adopted unit for communicating physical activity, outperforming more technical indicators such as heart rate or metabolic equivalents (METs) in terms of public understanding and self-monitoring. Yet, daily steps alone fails to reflect the complexity inherent in physical activity patterns. Two parameters—cadence (steps/min, an intensity proxy) and bout duration (temporal continuity)—represent distinct yet interacting dimensions of behavior. The same total step count may arise from short, high-cadence bursts or from prolonged, low-cadence walking, with potentially different physiological implications. Existing research has primarily emphasized daily step volume in relation to weight management [17]. However, this aggregate focus leaves three critical gaps that remain insufficiently addressed in older women—namely, the translation of cadence thresholds to free-living conditions, the comparative role of MVPA versus LPA metrics, and the physiological relevance of cadence–bout accumulation patterns.

First, most cadence cut-points used to map minute-level walking to MVPA in older adults have been derived under laboratory conditions rather than in free-living settings [18–20], limiting their real-world applicability and hindering the translation of “intensity” into actionable, minute-by-minute walking guidance. Second, given inconsistent findings regarding LPA, the comparative discriminative capacity of MVPA-based versus LPA-based step metrics for identifying obesity remains undetermined. Third, although short bouts of brisk walking and longer bouts of slower walking may differ physiologically, how combined bout duration and cadence patterns relate to adiposity remains underexplored. Specifically, it is unclear whether short, high-cadence walking (“snacktivity”) or longer, low-cadence walking more effectively discriminates adiposity-related outcomes.

Addressing these unresolved questions requires translating cadence- and bout-resolved activity patterns into practical, behaviorally interpretable walking targets for older adults. Within the field of physical activity interventions, the establishment of explicit and specific walking goals has been identified as an effective starting strategy [21]and the formulation of practical, flexible plans facilitates successful goal achievement [22]. Accordingly, this study examines how step count, cadence, and bout duration relate to adiposity among older women and applies receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses to derive pragmatic step- and cadence-based cut-points for discriminating adiposity status. These findings aim to inform lifestyle-integrated, low-barrier walking strategies for obesity prevention in populations unable to sustain prolonged exercise.

Methods

Participants

This analysis used baseline data from an ongoing cohort—the Physical Activity and Health in Older Women Study (PAHIOWS)—initiated between March and June 2021 in Yantai, Shandong Province, China. Investigators received standardized training from the principal investigator to collect self-reported and objective data. Eligibility for the parent cohort included community-dwelling women aged 60–70 years who were able to communicate, had no cognitive impairment, and provided written informed consent. A total of 1,370 older women were enrolled in PAHIOWS. Data were collected in person at community clinics/centers by trained staff, and a double-checking protocol was implemented to reduce data-entry errors. After excluding participants with missing covariates or missing outcome data for adiposity indices, and those without valid accelerometer data (per the predefined wear-time criteria), 1,109 participants were included in the analysis. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Rehabilitation, Shandong University (Approval No. 2021-R-001).

Measurements

Response variable

Adiposity indices were indexed by BMI, body fat ratio (BFR, expressed as a percentage), and visceral fat mass (VFM). BMI offers a near-zero marginal-cost screening tool for weight management (BMI = weight (kg) / height (m)²). BFR quantifies the proportion of fat mass relative to total body weight, and VFM captures metabolically high-risk ectopic adiposity linked to diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and atherosclerosis [23]. All indices were obtained with a segmental, eight-electrode, multi-frequency bioelectrical impedance analyzer (Tanita MC-180, Tanita Corp., Tokyo, Japan). The method has demonstrated acceptable validity for assessing adiposity in older women [24], supporting its use in field-based evaluations. BFR and BMI were categorized using prespecified thresholds to characterize obesity phenotypes and to support stratified and sensitivity analyses. Specifically, BFR was classified as ≤ 32% (normal), > 32% to ≤ 37% (overweight), and > 37% (obesity). Visceral adiposity was assessed as visceral fat mass (kg), with higher values indicating greater visceral fat; this variable was modeled continuously.

Predicting variable

Step counts, cadence, and bout patterns were recorded with a triaxial accelerometer (ActiGraph wGT3X-BT, Pensacola, FL, USA). The ActiGraph wGT3X-BT has demonstrated criterion validity for step counting in adults and older adults [25]. To maximize step-count accuracy, the device was worn on the left side at the waist, and secured with a belt for 7 consecutive days. Waist-worn placement has been shown to improve step-count accuracy compared with wrist-worn devices [26], likely because sensors positioned near the pelvis better capture whole-body movement and vertical displacement of the center of mass while reducing misclassification from non-ambulatory arm movements. Participants were instructed to wear the device during waking hours, removing it only for sleep and water-based activities (e.g., bathing, swimming). During the wear period, two follow-up telephone calls were made to remind participants to wear the accelerometer and to assess wear-time compliance and data quality. The sampling frequency was set to 30 Hz. Raw data were aggregated into movement counts in 60-s epochs using ActiLife (version 6.13.4). Intervals with ≥ 90 consecutive minutes of no movement counts were classified as non-wear and excluded from analyses. A valid day was defined as ≥ 10 h of wear time; participants with ≥ 4 valid days were included in the analytic sample. Daily steps (steps/day) were calculated as the average number of steps across valid days (total steps on valid days divided by the number of valid days). Cadence (steps·min⁻¹) was calculated as steps per 60-s epoch. Bout patterns of 3, 5, and 10 min were exported using ActiLife’s bout-detection feature.

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics included age, monthly income and living arrangement (living alone: yes/no). Health-related characteristics included alcohol consumption (yes/no); chronic disease status (yes/no); nutritional status [Mini Nutritional Assessment—Short Form (MNA-SF) score [27]; cognitive function [Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score] [28]; depressive symptoms [Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) score] [29]; anxiety symptoms [2-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale (GAD-2) score] [30]; health-related quality of life assessed using the EuroQol 5-Dimension (EQ-5D) index [31]; and insomnia severity [Athens Insomnia Scale (AIS) score] [32]. These covariates were selected a priori as potential confounders because sociodemographic factors, psychological status, sleep disturbances, and cognitive function may influence both habitual physical activity behaviors and adiposity-related outcomes through behavioral, functional, and neuroendocrine pathways [33, 34].

Statistical analysis

Continuous measures appear as mean (standard deviation) and categorical measures as frequency (percentage). Normality was checked via skewness and kurtosis tests. Ordered trends were tested using the Jonckheere–Terpstra procedure for continuous outcomes and multi-level ordinal categorical variables, whereas the Cochran–Armitage trend test was used for dichotomous categorical variables. We employed multivariable linear regression to assess how step count, cadence, and activity-bout patterns relate to adiposity. Linearity was assessed using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) plots, and model assumptions were evaluated through residual diagnostics. Robust standard errors were applied to account for potential heteroscedasticity, and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors. To control for confounders, we employed three models. Model 1 included age and daily accelerometer wear time. Model 2 further controlled for insomnia score, Total MNA, Total GAD, Total PHQ, EQ-5D, living-together status, disease status, income, and Total MMSE. Model 3 was fully adjusted by also adding total ST. Collinearity diagnostics used the variance inflation factor (VIF). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of step-based metrics for discriminating obesity defined by BFR. The primary analysis treated BFR as the outcome and examined its associations with step-related metrics. To assess robustness, we conducted two sensitivity analyses using the same sample, model specification, and covariate set, replacing BFR with BMI and VFM as alternative outcomes. Every variable in the model yielded a VIF < 5, suggesting no concerning intercorrelation. Analyses were run in Stata 18 (Windows), and figures were created in GraphPad Prism 8.0. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Discussion

Among 1,109 older women, higher total daily step counts were associated withlower adiposity-related outcomes. After adjusting for potential confounders, intensity-oriented metrics—MVPA steps, MVPA cadence, and bout-accumulated MVPA steps—showed strong and consistent inverse associations adiposity, whereas LPA step–based metrics were not statistically significant. Taken together, the results suggest that accumulating short bouts of MVPA (“snacktivity”) is associated with lower adiposity-related outcomes in older women.

A key contribution of this study is the bout-resolved analysis. Meeting step targets within MVPA bouts—regardless of whether those bouts were defined as ≥ 3, ≥5, or ≥ 10 min—was consistently associated with lower adiposity-related outcomes. Meta-analyses [35] show that accumulated-bout and single-bout exercise confer comparable benefits for cardiorespiratory fitness, blood pressure, lipid profiles, and glycemic/insulinemic markers, with some evidence favoring accumulated activity for body-weight and LDL-cholesterol changes. In a randomized crossover design [36], accumulating short bouts of moderate-intensity walking throughout the day improved mood, attenuated fatigue, and modulated appetite. From a physiological perspective, several mechanisms may help explain why brief MVPA bouts were associated with lower adiposity-related outcomes. Short episodes of higher-intensity walking can impose relatively high energetic demands because repeated movement initiation increases oxygen uptake and metabolic cost compared with longer steady-state activity [37], thereby amplifying total energy expenditure when accumulated across the day. Acute MVPA may also transiently suppress appetite through elevations in satiety-related hormones such as peptide YY and glucagon-like peptide-1 without consistently inducing compensatory increases in energy intake [38], supporting the potential for short bouts to generate a net energy deficit. These responses may explain why brief MVPA bouts influence adiposity-related outcomes in older women.

In contrast to the consistent inverse associations observed for MVPA-oriented metrics, LPA step-based indicators were not significantly related to adiposity in our models. One potential explanation is that light-intensity physical activity may not consistently generate sufficient metabolic stimulus to meaningfully influence energy balance, as higher-intensity activity appears to produce disproportionately greater health benefits per unit time compared with LPA [39]. Consistent with this interpretation, isotemporal substitution studies show that reallocating sedentary time to MVPA—but not to LPA—is associated with lower adiposity-related outcomes [40], and systematic reviews of device-measured activity report stronger associations between MVPA, daily steps, and adiposity than for LPA [41]. Measurement-related factors may also contribute, as waist-worn accelerometers can classify incidental low-amplitude movements as LPA, potentially diluting associations with purposeful locomotor activity [42]. In contrast to our findings, Zhu et al. reported inverse associations for both LPA and MVPA in women, which may reflect differences in age structure, activity composition, or analytical approaches [43]. Together, these observations suggest that while LPA may contribute to general health, higher-intensity walking patterns appear more strongly linked to adiposity-related outcomes in older women.

The optimal MVPA cadence observed in the present study (~ 94–95 steps/min) is slightly lower than the widely cited ≥ 100 steps/min [18] heuristic derived from laboratory-based studies in healthy middle-aged adults, as well as the ≥ 105 steps/min [19] relative-intensity threshold reported in the CADENCE-Adults investigation. In contrast, recent free-living studies have suggested lower cadence requirements, with approximately 80 steps/min [44] linked to cardiometabolic-relevant intensity and ~ 70–81 steps/min [45] reflecting moderate-intensity stepping in older adults during daily living. The intermediate cadence identified here may therefore represent a pragmatic balance between laboratory-derived heuristics and ecologically valid free-living walking patterns, particularly among older women who may achieve moderate physiological strain at slightly lower cadences. Our MVPA threshold of 1,846 steps/day corresponds to approximately 20 min/day of brisk walking (1,846 ÷ 94.3 ≈ 19.6), broadly consistent with distributing the recommended weekly total of ~ 150 min of moderate-intensity activity across days. Rather than replacing existing time-based guidelines, these findings suggest that step-anchored thresholds may provide a clinically interpretable translation of current recommendations into daily behavioural targets. In particular, accumulating roughly 1,800–2,000 MVPA steps per day at a cadence of ~ 94–95 steps/min may represent a feasible and intuitive benchmark for older women who experience barriers to sustained exercise.

From a translational perspective, accumulating roughly 1,800–2,000 MVPA steps per day through brief bouts of brisk walking (“Snacktivity”) may represent a feasible strategy for older adults who experience barriers to sustained exercise. Integrating cadence-informed step goals into wearable-device feedback, community health promotion programs, or primary care counseling could enhance adherence by providing clear, quantifiable targets. Notably, behavioral intervention trials show that step-goal prescriptions predict weight loss [39], supporting the translational potential of cadence- and step-based guidance for clinical counseling and community health programs. However, given the cross-setional design, longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether adopting cadence-based snacktivity patterns can causally reduce adiposity-related risk.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. Physical activity was objectively assessed using a waist-worn accelerometer under free-living conditions, enabling detailed evaluation of step counts, cadence, and bout patterns. Integrating cadence, bout duration, and intensity-specific steps allowed a nuanced characterization of walking behavior beyond total step volume and facilitated identification of interpretable step- and cadence-based thresholds relevant to adiposity-related outcomes. Several limitations should also be considered. First, our cross-sectional analysis cannot support causal claims and is susceptible to reverse causation (e.g., lower adiposity enabling sustained brisk stepping). Future research should examine dose–response patterns linking cadence-defined MVPA to longitudinal changes in adiposity, identify population-specific thresholds for weight management, and evaluate whether cadence-informed snacktivity strategies can be effectively translated into scalable clinical and community interventions. Second, model performance was modest (AUC approximately 0.60–0.63): suitable for population screening, monitoring, and intervention feedback, but inadequate for individual diagnostic or exclusionary use. Third, selection bias cannot be excluded: participants willing to wear accelerometers may be more health-conscious or physically active than the broader population, potentially attenuating variability in lower-intensity behaviors. Finally, external validity is limited by the sampling frame—community-dwelling older women in Yantai, Shandong—and warrants replication in more diverse populations and contexts.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of community-dwelling older Chinese women, higher accumulation of short-bout MVPA steps was associated with lower adiposity-related outcomes. These findings support snacktivity as a feasible, low-barrier target for step-based guidance, and encouraging brief bouts of brisk walking embedded within daily routines (e.g., short outdoor walks or active errands) may represent a practical strategy for clinicians and community health programs. However, its effectiveness should be evaluated in longitudinal and experimental studies, and generalizability beyond this population requires confirmation.
