A guided ungrading example for independent research projects in an upper-division ecosystem ecology STEM course
Authors: Christine S. O'Connell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUngrading is a pedagogical approach that, in some iterations, emphasizes formative feedback and student self-assessment over traditional point-based grading. While increasingly popular in the humanities and social sciences, ungrading remains uncommon in STEM courses, particularly those that are content-intensive. This article presents a structured example of ungrading implemented in an upper-division ecosystem ecology course through a 7-week independent research project. Students used public or previously collected ecological data to investigate a scientific question, culminating in a short-format, manuscript-style research paper and a 10 min oral presentation. The project was scaffolded with multiple checkpoints, including structured reflections, peer review, and instructor feedback. Students were invited to define personal growth goals, determine how they wished to be evaluated, and reflect on their progress throughout the project. While the rest of the course retained traditionally graded assessments, this ungraded project accounted for 40% of the final grade. Students reported high levels of engagement and ownership, with ~86% of students opting to receive both an instructor grade and self-grade, and a further ~9% of students opting to entirely self-grade, with only ~5% of students preferring a traditional grading schema. The model was particularly effective in a small, seminar-style course with students specializing in environmental science and may require adaptation for larger courses, lower-division settings, or to accommodate generative artificial intelligence usage guidelines. This case study offers a replicable framework for integrating ungrading into STEM curricula and highlights the importance of in-class support and iterative feedback when using an ungrading approach. By emphasizing student agency and identity “as a scientist,” this approach aligns with the goals of authentic research experiences and provides a flexible alternative to traditional grading in content-heavy STEM courses that could include independent projects.

INTRODUCTION

Ungrading refers to a pedagogical approach where traditional grades are minimized or eliminated in favor of formative feedback, self-assessment, or other forms of qualitative assessment (1, 2). This term incorporates many kinds of self-regulated learning and emancipatory pedagogies (3–5), but in one conception can be thought to emphasize students’ understanding, reflection, and personal engagement with the material rather than quantified, performance-based evaluations alone (3, 4, 6–10). In a content-driven field like biology, ungrading can foster deeper learning and critical thinking by shifting students’ focus from achieving high grades to understanding complex concepts or developing core skill sets (4, 11, 12). Research has shown that ungrading promotes intrinsic motivation, reduces anxiety, and enhances students' self-assessment skills (1, 2, 9, 10).

Despite these benefits, ungrading presents challenges, particularly in STEM fields (6), where, depending on a course’s goals (13), content mastery may be crucial. Some educators note the increased workload associated with providing detailed feedback (13, 14), as well as potential student resistance in adapting to an ungrading approach and the need for “buy-in” (7, 14). STEM educators may also question how best to assess skill-based learning outcomes without traditional grades (6). Given the limited examples of ungrading in higher education STEM courses, particularly those that are content-intensive, practical insights on how to apply this approach are valuable (6–8, 14).

In this case study, it is outlined how ungrading was integrated into an upper-division ecosystem ecology course through a structured 7-week independent research project. The timeline and materials were designed to guide students through each phase of the independent project, encouraging reflection and iterative learning. Students engaged in self-evaluation at each project stage, received formative feedback from both the instructor and peers rather than grades, and participated in regular reflections to assess their understanding and progress.

This example provides a replicable framework for STEM educators interested in experimenting with ungrading in content-heavy courses. By emphasizing a project-based, feedback-oriented model, this approach allows for a meaningful learning experience that could be adapted to similar contexts in higher education.

PROCEDURE

Course context

This ungrading approach was implemented in an upper-division undergraduate course entitled Ecosystem Ecology, cross-listed in the Environmental Studies and Biology departments. The course is designed for juniors and seniors, and class size ranges from 10 to 14 students. The course has a strong emphasis on independent research, data analysis, and engagement with primary literature in addition to traditional content delivery via lectures and textbook readings.

Students are required to have completed an introductory environmental science/ecology course and an introductory statistics course prior to enrolling. An introductory chemistry course is recommended but not required. The class is designed to be rigorous and is described to students as being comparable to an early graduate-level seminar. The primary audience is students considering graduate study in ecology, earth science, or environmental science.

Independent project overview

The centerpiece of the course is a 7-week independent research project (Table 1) designed to simulate the process of conducting and communicating original scientific research in ecosystem ecology. Students are tasked with identifying a scientific question related to ecosystem structure, function, or response to change and answering that question using either their own data (e.g., from summer research experiences) or, more commonly, publicly available ecological, environmental, or earth science data sets (Assignment Description document available as Appendix 1).

The project emphasizes both scientific inquiry and professional development skills. By the end of the project, students synthesize their findings in a written report modeled after short-format manuscripts published in journals aimed at broad scientific audiences (e.g., in the style of “Nature” or ”Science”). In addition, each student delivers a 10 min oral presentation (Table 1).

While the assignment document (Appendix 1) does not explicitly use the term “ungrading,” the structure of the project is intentionally aligned with ungrading principles. The rubric provided to students does not include point values. Instead, students are prompted to reflect on which aspects of the rubric align most closely with their personal goals for growth and success as scholars and either assign their own point structures or qualitative “importance” levels to each section of the rubric (Fig. 1). This approach encourages students to take ownership of their learning and to focus on developing skills most relevant to their academic and professional trajectories. Instead of being referred to as “ungrading,” this assessment approach is described to students as defining their own “growth goals” or conducting “self-assessment.” The project is scaffolded with multiple checkpoints and opportunities for self, peer, and instructor-driven feedback, which are detailed in the following section.

Ungrading structure, timeline, and checkpoints

The ungrading framework for this project is built around three structured reflection checkpoints, two peer-review feedback sessions, regular in-class feedback from the instructional team, and a flexible grading model that emphasizes student agency. The goal is to shift the focus from point accumulation to meaningful engagement with the research process and personal growth as a scientist.

The project spans the final 7 weeks of the semester (Table 1). Each class meeting includes a 1-hour “lecture” block followed by a 1.5 h “lab” block, which is often used as in-class work time. This structure allows students to make steady progress on their projects while receiving real-time support from the instructor.

The ungrading process is scaffolded through the following checkpoints (see also Table 1, Supplemental materials):

Checkpoint 1 (Week 1 of the project): students propose their research question and identify personal growth goals using a shared rubric (Fig. 1; Appendix 1 and 2). They are asked to reflect on which aspects of the rubric they want to prioritize in their development (e.g., experimental design, data analysis, visualization, interpretation, etc.). They also indicate an initial, non-binding preference on how they prefer to be graded: by the instructor, by themselves, or both.

Throughout the project, students receive extensive formative feedback during in-class work time. The instructor (and, during some iterations of the course, a student teaching assistant) circulates to provide individualized support on experimental design, data cleaning, statistical analysis in R, figure creation, interpretation, and scientific writing. Regular in-class work time, structured reflections, and multiple feedback opportunities help ensure that students feel supported, maintain momentum and confidence, and have a clear understanding of expectations.

Implementation tips

Students anecdotally report that the iterative feedback project structure, with multiple rounds of peer, instructor, and self-feedback, fosters a strong sense of ownership and scientific identity. In-class work time is essential for providing real-time feedback and for limiting the amount of written feedback the instructor must provide outside of class. Instructor framing is also critical: students are encouraged to think like researchers, embrace uncertainty, and use feedback to guide their decisions, which appeared to enhance student buy-in of the ungrading approach. Across two sections of this course, 86.4% of students (19/22) chose to receive both an instructor grade and self-grade, 9.1% of students (2/22) chose to entirely self-grade, and only 4.5% of students (1/22) asked to be solely graded by the instructor, suggesting widespread student enthusiasm for self-assessment as well as concurrent widespread student desire for instructor-level grading.

Across multiple iterations of the course, one early written reflection was removed to avoid overloading students, while a peer review session for oral presentations was added. The ungrading model is applied only to the research project (which comprises 40% of the course grade), while other course components (e.g., quizzes, midterm) remain traditionally graded. After year one, the instructor began facilitating a brief discussion before final self-grading about how identity factors (e.g., gender, language background) can influence self-assessment, with the aim of giving students an opportunity to reflect critically on their evaluations and sense of belonging in science; this change was based on the instructor’s anecdotal evidence that certain student demographics were grading themselves more harshly than others (4). This model was successfully used once during online instruction.

Students varied in how they approached their growth goals, with most deciding to focus on areas in which they felt they needed improvement but a notable subset of students with data science or computer science backgrounds deciding to improve in their subspecialty of interest. This distinction is apparent in how two different students justified prioritizing their growth in the “statistical approaches” category. One student, who was concurrently taking a biostatistics course, opted to focus on statistical approaches in their rubric breakdown in order to practice skills they were learning in another classroom environment, suggesting that they wanted to double down in an area of relative strength. In contrast, a second student in the same section also opted to focus their rubric on statistical approaches, but explicitly because they did not feel strong in this area and thus wanted to invest time in it. This desire to improve on self-perceived weaknesses was common, with students often justifying their prioritized growth areas by saying that they lacked confidence in a given skill set (e.g., data visualization skills) and wanted to invest in building those skills or reduce the amount that they struggle with a skill set they considered a roadblock (e.g., scientific writing). Self-setting goals anecdotally appeared to increase student motivation, with students reporting on end-of-semester anonymous student evaluations that they valued the course being tailored to their preferred areas of learning.

There are several potential pitfalls or areas for constructive change to the project and assessment structure. While students generally embraced this ungrading model, most still sought instructor validation and were hesitant to rely solely on self-assessment, an effect documented elsewhere (7, 11). This dynamic, mirrored in students’ explanations for their ambivalence, suggests that students, especially those early in their scientific training, value what they perceive as “expert” feedback as part of their learning process. This ungrading model worked particularly well in this upper-division course, where students were highly motivated and many were preparing for graduate study, but would likely require adaptation for lower-division or larger courses, where, e.g., intrinsic motivation and feedback logistics could pose greater challenges (7, 9). It also remains to be seen how the proliferation of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) may impact either student motivation (which is critical in this model) or students’ decisions about where to focus their energy within such a project. Though, alternatively, appealing to students’ self-desired growth goals may combat some of the more mercenary reasons why students over-rely on GenAI in writing-heavy courses and instead create opportunities for best practices when integrating GenAI explicitly as a writing tool (15). Finally, scalability may be limited in larger courses unless peer feedback is more structured or instructor feedback is streamlined (8).

Safety issues

There are no safety issues associated with this activity.

CONCLUSION

This ungrading model for an independent research project and associated written report and oral presentation supported student ownership, reflection, and scientific identity in an upper-division, content-driven ecosystem ecology course. Students reported feeling deeply invested in their projects and several times cited this project unprompted as a highlight of their undergraduate experience. The iterative structure, combined with frequent feedback and in-class work time, helped maintain momentum and fostered high-quality outcomes. While the model was highly effective in this small, seminar-style course with students focused on this area of academic study, it may require adaptation for larger classes or lower-division settings. Framing the project in terms of “growth goals” and scientific practice helped students engage meaningfully with self-assessment, though most still sought instructor validation. Overall, this approach offers a flexible, student-centered alternative to traditional grading that aligns well with the goals of authentic scientific inquiry over the course of an independent research project that flows from an otherwise content-driven course.
