From awareness to action: Millet-based recipe talks improve iron status and reduce anemia severity in Andhra Pradesh women
Authors: Arti Gupta, Joe Amalan, Sirisha Pulla, B Venkatashiva Reddy, Rajeev Aravindakshan, R Barath, Shanmukh Krishna Kopuru, Bhukya Kamal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Millets are nutrient-rich, ability to thrive in challenging climate were integral to traditional Indian diets. However, their intake has reduced in diet. This study aims to assess the prevalence of anemia among women in Andhra Pradesh and to determine the association between millet-based local recipe talk in the mother’s kitchen and maternal anemia.Methods:A community-based cohort study enrolled 345 mothers across three regions of Andhra Pradesh, India, in 2024-2025. Baseline data included sociodemographic characteristics, 24-hour dietary recalls (3 nonconsecutive days), anthropometry, and hemoglobin estimation of mothers. A 12-week intervention delivered weekly millet recipe talks in mothers’ kitchens/Anganwadi centers, with hands-on cooking using nine millets and two pseudo millets. Endline assessments of 24-hour dietary recalls (3 nonconsecutive days), anthropometry, and hemoglobin estimation in mothers were conducted.Results:Most mothers were predominantly aged 21–30 years. Tribal groups had higher rates of early marriage (under 18 years), reaching 18 (32.73%) in Uttar Andhra. Most mothers were housewives, and husbands were daily laborers. Rural participants had a medium standard of living, 159 (88.33%), whereas tribal participants had a low standard of living 41 (74.55%) in Coastal Andhra. Anemia was present among 266 (77.1%) mothers at baseline, with higher maternal/husband education linked to lower risk (P = 0.014, P = 0.047). Postintervention, mean daily intake increased significantly for protein (P = 0.009), energy (P = 0.007), calcium (P < 0.001), and iron (P < 0.001). Among the 265 mothers with anemia at baseline, excluding attrition, the prevalence of anemia decreased to 239 (90.2%) with P =<0.001. Severe anemia cases fell from 10 to 4 (P = 0.007), moderate from 46.9% to 34.2% (P < 0.001), and mild anemia also decreased (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Culturally tailored, hands-on millet recipe interventions significantly enhance nutrient intake and reduce anemia severity. Scaling such community-led approaches through Anganwadi networks offers a sustainable strategy to combat maternal anemia.

Introduction

Traditional Indian diets are deeply rooted in plant-based foods such as whole grains, legumes, vegetables, and spices, which together provide substantial nutritional value and environmental benefits. These diets offer essential nutrients and bioactive compounds that support long-term health. In recent years, public health programs have increasingly promoted these traditional food systems as part of sustainable dietary practices, recognizing their role in improving well-being while preserving ecological balance and cultural heritage.[1]

Among these traditional foods, millets stand out as nutrient-dense grains that thrive in warm and semiarid climates.[2] They are classified into major millets like sorghum, pearl millet, and finger millet; minor millets such as foxtail, kodo, barnyard, and little millet; and pseudo-millets like buckwheat and amaranth. India is one of the largest producers of millets, contributing over 170 lakh tonnes annually—accounting for 80% of Asia’s and 20% of the world’s production.[3] Andhra Pradesh, with its long-standing cultural use of millets, offers a unique perspective on how women, as key custodians of household nutrition, understand and incorporate these grains in daily diets.[4] Millets are rich in essential vitamins, minerals, fiber, and antioxidants, and offer several health advantages. They support weight management due to their low-calorie and high-fiber content, help regulate blood sugar levels because of their low glycemic index, and contribute to heart health by lowering cholesterol.[3] In Andhra cuisine, millets such as ragi, bajra, foxtail, sorghum, and barnyard millet have been traditionally consumed by women and children, who are often the most affected by nutritional deficiencies.[5] Their gluten-free nature and slow digestion rate make them a healthier alternative to polished rice and refined wheat, especially for individuals with diabetes, chronic diseases, or gluten intolerance.[67] This renewed global and national interest has been reinforced by initiatives like the UN’s International Year of Millets, (2023) the National Food Security Mission, and regional projects such as the Andhra Pradesh Drought Mitigation Project.[8]

Beyond macronutrients, millets contain essential micronutrients like calcium, magnesium, zinc, B vitamins, and polyphenolic antioxidants.[2] Studies have shown that millet-based diets and recipes improve hemoglobin levels, metabolic health, and child growth indicators among women and children.[9] Traditional household practices such as soaking, sprouting, and fermenting millet are increasingly recognized for enhancing nutrient absorption and reducing anti-nutrient content, particularly in maternal and child nutrition programs across Andhra Pradesh.[10]

Despite their benefits, millet consumption has declined due to changing food preferences, westernized diets, limited awareness, higher costs, supply chain issues, and the government’s historical focus on rice and wheat production.[2] Women often report challenges such as longer cooking times, unfamiliar tastes, and difficulty in adapting modern recipes using millets.[11] Accessibility also varies—urban women prefer packaged millet products, while rural women rely on local, unbranded grains.[12] While socioeconomic and educational factors influence purchasing behavior, family traditions, advice from health professionals, and community-based kitchen talks strongly encourage millet usage.[313]

Generational differences are also evident—older women possess more traditional knowledge of millets, whereas younger women and children tend to consume them primarily through home-cooked meals or school programs.[1314] This study aims to assess the prevalence of anemia among women in Andhra Pradesh and to determine the association between millet-based local recipe talk in the mother’s kitchen and maternal anemia.

Materials and Methods

A community-based cohort-based study was conducted in three regions of Andhra Pradesh: Uttar Andhra (Guntur District), Coastal Andhra (Visakhapatnam and Anakapalle), and Rayalaseema (Annamayya District) in 2024-2025. The Institute Ethics Committee approved the study; letters no. AIIMS/MG//IEC/2023-24/42, dated 12/10/2023, and AIIMS/MG/IEC/2024-25/145, dated 29-11-2024. Eligibility Criteria for recruitment of women in the study was a mother with a child aged 2 to 6 years, an elderly individual aged 60 years or the eldest person in the family. The woman should have resided in the study area for the past 6 months and be living in the selected household at the time of data collection. The sample size was calculated based on an estimated incidence of 15%, a precision of 5%, and a 95% confidence level. The sample size was calculated to be 340 mothers, with 115 from each district. Five mandals were selected from each district. Within each mandal, one rural and one tribal Anganwadi was identified. The list of eligible mothers was retrieved from the identified Anganwadi. All the eligible mothers were given a participant information sheet for those willing to participate, and a written informed consent was obtained and enrolled in the study.

A team of staff was trained for data collection, 24-hour dietary recall, anthropometric measurement, and hemoglobin estimation. A questionnaire comprising socio-demographic characteristics, dietary recall, anthropometric measurements, iron supplement intake, antenatal history, knowledge of millets and their use, and morbidity-related data was developed. All mothers’ anthropometric measurements, along with blood hemoglobin, were estimated. Hemoglobin was measured using a calibrated Hemocue 301. Weight was measured using SECA 213 and Height was measured using SECA 813. A 24-hour dietary recall of the mothers’ dietary intake for three nonconsecutive days was also obtained. A weekly local Millet recipe talk was conducted in the Anganwadi/mother’s kitchen for 12 weeks. In a session of Weekly local recipe talk in the mother’s kitchen, 5 to 10 participants were enrolled. Anganwadi workers and helpers were also invited to participate in the discussion. During the Weekly local Millet recipe talk in the mother’s kitchen, raw ingredients were provided for demonstration. Mothers were supported by project staff through step-by-step recipe preparation covering nine millets (finger millet, pearl millet, foxtail millet, little millet, kodo millet, proso millet, barnyard millet, brown top millet, and sorghum), and two pseudo cereals (amaranth and buckwheat). Three months later, after baseline data collection, anthropometric measurements of the mother were repeated, along with repeat hemoglobin estimation and a 24-hour dietary recall.

The exposure variable was defined as mothers who attended at least seven weekly local millet recipe talks held in mothers’ kitchens. Continuous variables were summarized using either mean and standard deviation. Normality was assessed to determine the appropriate test of significance: paired t-tests were used for normally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions. The chi-square test was used to assess associations when sample sizes were sufficient, and Fischer exact test was applied when expected cell counts were less than five. Differences in the proportion of millet consumption between the baseline and endline surveys were evaluated using McNemar’s test for a paired sample of proportions to assess statistical significance. Attendance at the weekly local millet recipe talks was categorized into two groups: mothers who attended seven or more sessions and those who attended fewer. Relative risk was calculated to assess the association between attendance and anemia prevalence among mothers. The data were analyzed using both bivariate and multivariate logistic regression techniques. Initially, bivariate logistic regression was performed to assess the association between each independent variable and the outcome variable. Variables with P-values < 0.2 in the bivariate analysis were subsequently included in the multivariate logistic regression model to control for potential confounding and to identify independent predictors. Adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported, and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for the multivariate analysis. Micronutrient intake in diet was calculated using DietCalc software. The latest version of DietCalc, incorporating the Indian Food Composition Tables (IFCT 2017) by Longvah et al.,[15] was used for this purpose. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 29.

Discussion

This study examined the sociodemographic and health profiles of mothers in rural and tribal Andhra Pradesh, providing essential context for understanding their diets and their effects on anemia. The cohort primarily comprised mothers in the 21–30 age group, a demographic consistent with regional analyses identifying young women as key decision-makers in household nutrition.[16] We observed a concerning persistence of early marriage (18–33% married before 18), particularly in tribal areas.[17] The symptoms indicative of anemia—such as fatigue, weakness, and dizziness—were reported by 30–60% of respondents. This symptom-based finding strongly suggests a high, under-diagnosed burden of anemia, a conclusion supported by NFHS-5 data showing high regional prevalence.[18]

This study demonstrates significant improvements in key nutritional parameters following the millet-based local recipe talk intervention. The intervention resulted in increase in iron, calcium, energy, and protein intake, with corresponding improvements in anemia prevalence. These findings align with systematic reviews demonstrating millet’s efficacy in combating iron deficiency anemia. A comprehensive meta-analysis of 30 studies found that regular millet consumption increased hemoglobin levels by 13.2% in anemic individuals, compared with 2.7% in control groups.[6] Pearl millet intervention trials in Rajasthan demonstrated a 2.24 g/dL increase in hemoglobin among adolescent girls, comparable to iron-folic acid supplementation.[19] Similarly, finger millet supplementation studies in India across various locations have shown significant improvements in calcium levels and bone mineral density among premenopausal women, with 500 mg of calcium per day from 150 g of finger millet.[20]

Calcium improvements were particularly notable, with millet providing naturally bioavailable calcium. Research confirms finger millet contains 5-30 times more calcium than other cereals, making it especially valuable for women of reproductive age who require 600-1000 mg calcium daily.[21] The study documented that hemoglobin levels increased from moderate anemia (10.8 ± 1.4 g/dL) to normal (12.2 ± 1.5 g/dL) over 21 days to 4.5 years of regular millet consumption.

Pearl millet interventions have shown positive results. A randomized clinical trial among Indian adolescent girls demonstrated that pearl millet-based iron-rich laddu (providing 15 mg iron) increased mean hemoglobin levels by 2.24 g/dl, matching the efficacy of iron-folic acid tablets (2.28 g/dl) over 90 days.[22] Similarly, biofortified high-iron pearl millet studies in Maharashtra showed that 64% of iron-deficient children became iron-replete compared with 40% consuming regular pearl millet, with bioavailability rates of 7.5 ± 1.6% providing 1 ± 0.4 mg bioavailable iron per meal.[2324]

The bioavailability of iron from millets is enhanced through traditional processing methods. Research demonstrates that germination increases bioavailable iron content from 0.4 to 1.3 mg/100 g while reducing tannin content by 50.4%.[11] Fermentation and germination can improve bioavailable iron by 3.4 and 2.2 times, respectively, contributing to 143% and 95% of physiological iron requirements for women.[25] A natural low-phytic acid finger millet accession study showed almost 3-fold increased iron absorption compared with regular varieties.[26]

Clinical trials consistently demonstrate millet’s therapeutic potential. A community-based intervention in Telangana involving 120 adolescent girls showed significant hemoglobin improvements from 8.40 ± 0.9 g/dL to 8.45 ± 0.6 g/dL following nutrient-dense millet bar supplementation.[27] Another study reported that iron-rich millet biscuits significantly improved hemoglobin levels in anemic adolescent girls over 90 days.[28]

The mechanisms underlying millet’s anti-anemic effects include high iron content (2-8 mg/100 g), enhanced bioavailability through processing, and synergistic nutrients. Finger millet provides substantial calcium (300-350 mg per 100 g) which aids iron absorption, while pearl millet offers balanced amino acid profiles that enhance mineral bioavailability.[2930] Studies confirm that one meal of biofortified pearl millet can meet 50-100% of the daily iron allowance, making it particularly valuable for women of reproductive age who require 29 mg of daily iron.[31]

The findings highlight millets as a culturally acceptable dietary strategy for addressing micronutrient deficiencies, particularly among vulnerable populations, including women. Moreover, promoting locally sourced millets supports agricultural biodiversity and food security, aligning nutrition goals with environmental sustainability. The findings also underscore the potential for millet-based dietary recommendations as part of primary care management for anemia and related micronutrient deficiencies. Millet-based interventions offer a practical adjunct to standard iron supplementation. The findings advocate for incorporating food-based strategies into patient education, emphasizing prevention and holistic care. The primary limitation of the study is that it could not capture seasonal variation in anemia prevalence, and recall bias may remain despite three nonconsecutive 24-hour dietary recalls.

To enhance the benefits of millet-based nutrition, we recommend scaling up hands-on recipe sessions in Anganwadi centers and through the National Food Security Mission, using traditional and culturally acceptable methods. Integrate millet education into school curricula, maternal health programs, and health worker training through multimedia and peer-led, generation-specific discussions. Link millet promotion to women’s economic empowerment through farming and value-added processing, paired with personalized nutrition counselling and iron supplementation. Future longitudinal studies monitoring anemia, intervention cost-effectiveness, and advanced biomarkers such as serum ferritin should be conducted to examine the causal association.

Conclusion

This study in Andhra Pradesh found that the 12-week millet recipe talk intervention significantly increased protein, energy, calcium, and iron intake, reduced anemia severity among mothers who attended more than or equal to seven local millet recipe sessions, highlighting community-based, culturally tailored approaches as effective for improving maternal nutrition in vulnerable rural and tribal populations.
