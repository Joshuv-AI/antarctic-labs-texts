Gut microbiota alterations in Neonatal Intensive Care Unit extremely preterm infants: a Chinese tertiary center study
Authors: Weijuan Han, Peipei Fu, Hong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly‑life gut microbiota shapes neonatal health. Extremely preterm infants frequently experience gut dysbiosis, while relevant Chinese longitudinal data remain scarce. This study aimed to explore early gut microbiota dynamics and identify potential microbial markers in Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) extremely preterm infants.MethodsInfants with gestational age ≤32 weeks admitted to the NICU of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University from June 2019 to December 2021 were enrolled, with full-term breastfed infants as controls. Clinical data and stool samples at postnatal <3 days, 2 weeks and 4 weeks were collected for 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (16S rRNA) sequencing to analyze gut microbiota composition and temporal variations.ResultsA total of 34 extremely preterm and 17 term infants were included. Baseline characteristics including gender, delivery mode and prolonged rupture of membranes (PROM) (>18 h) were comparable between groups. Alpha diversity remained stable over time in term infants, while it was markedly higher within 3 days after birth than at 2 and 4 weeks in extremely preterm infants. Extremely preterm infants had higher early alpha diversity than term infants, with no intergroup differences thereafter. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed obvious beta diversity temporal disparities in both groups. Compared with term infants, extremely preterm infants showed enriched colonization of Enterococcus, Streptococcus and Sphingomonas, along with delayed colonization of Enterobacter, Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides. Random forest analysis screened Sphingomonas as a candidate characteristic microbial genus of NICU extremely preterm infants.ConclusionsNICU extremely preterm infants presented distinct gut microbiota structure at multiple taxonomic levels relative to term infants. Their microbial profiles tended to converge within 4 weeks but remained unique. Sphingomonas was identified as an unvalidated candidate marker needing further verification.Keywords: Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU), extremely preterm infants, gut microbiota, 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (16S rRNA), Sphingomonas

Highlight box

Key findings

• Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU)‑hospitalized extremely preterm infants exhibited distinct gut microbiota dynamics compared with full‑term infants. Sphingomonas was identified as a potential discriminatory bacterial genus for this patient population.

What is known and what is new?

• Existing data show extremely preterm infants suffer disturbed gut microbiota under NICU‑related stressors. However, few Chinese longitudinal cohorts with healthy term infant controls have been reported.

• This study characterized serial gut microbiota shifts across three neonatal time‑points and screened Sphingomonas as a candidate microbial marker.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings advance understanding of gut microbial maturation in Chinese NICU extremely preterm infants. Sphingomonas deserves further validation as a microbial biomarker for gut dysbiosis in this vulnerable population.

Introduction

The early colonization and development of gut microbiota in newborns are critical for infant metabolism, immune programming, and potentially long-term health outcomes (1,2). Gut microbiota, a key component of the host immune system, influences the body’s response to external antigens and immune tolerance (3). Dysbiosis in early gut microbiota is associated with an increased risk of developing immune and metabolic disorders, such as asthma and obesity, which may lead to long-term health complications (4-6). Therefore, understanding the changes in gut microbiota in extremely preterm infants in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) is essential for improving clinical interventions and health outcomes.

The immature gut and immune systems of extremely preterm infants are susceptible to factors that alter gut microbiota, including mode of delivery, gender, breastfeeding, antibiotics, and parenteral nutrition. Traditional views suggest that delayed gut colonization and early antibiotic use in the NICU significantly increase the risk of colonization by antibiotic-resistant bacteria in extremely preterm infants (7). Pathogenic bacteria can spread through NICU equipment, environment, and care practices (8). Studies indicate that the NICU environment impacts the gut microbiota of newborns (9). Feeding methods also directly influence gut microbiota establishment. Breast milk, rich in probiotics and bioactive substances, promotes the growth of beneficial bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium (10,11). Recent research shows that breastfed preterm infants have lower levels of alpha-Proteobacteria and beta-Proteobacteria but increased gamma-Proteobacteria compared to mixed-fed infants (12). These factors increase the risk of sepsis, infection, and neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) (13), exacerbating gut dysbiosis.

The establishment of early gut microbiota in extremely preterm infants is a complex, dynamic process influenced by regional and cultural factors. Studies have found that the gut microbiota of NICU newborns is more complex in resource-limited environments (14). Although China has seen improvements in preterm infant care, research on gut microbiota in NICUs remains incomplete, and the dynamic shifts of gut microbiota in extremely preterm infants during NICU hospitalization remain poorly characterized. In particular, longitudinal studies with healthy full-term infant controls are scarce. This study, conducted in the NICU of a tertiary hospital in China, aims to explore the characteristics of early gut microbiota establishment in extremely preterm infants and attempt to identify potential gut microbiota markers of these infants. By conducting this study, we aim to better understand the changes of early gut microbiota in extremely preterm infants, aiding in the improvement of both short-term and long-term health outcomes.

Methods

Participant recruitment

This study involved preterm infants with a gestational age of ≤32 weeks, who were admitted to NICU at Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, between June 2019 and December 2021. The final cohort comprised 34 extremely preterm infants. A control group comprised 17 full-term infants born in the obstetrics department of the same hospital. These control infants did not require neonatal care and were exclusively breastfed.

Inclusion criteria: (I) admitted to the NICU immediately after birth; (II) gestational age ≤32 weeks. Exclusion criteria: (I) severe congenital malformations or inherited metabolic diseases; (II) discharged against medical advice with an unknown outcome.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (No. 2018-P2-203-02). Written informed consent was obtained from all parents.

Stool sample collection and 16S ribosomal ribonucleic acid (16S rRNA) sequencing

Stool samples were collected within 3 days of birth, at 2 weeks, and at 4 weeks. Samples were collected from diapers using disposable stool collection tubes, immediately stored at −20 ℃, and then transferred within a week via cold chain to a −80 ℃ freezer until analysis. High-throughput 16S rRNA sequencing was conducted on the stored stool samples (Beijing Novogene Technology Co., Ltd.).

DNA extraction, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, and sequencing

The 16S rRNA gene regions (16S V3-V4) were amplified using specific primers with barcodes. PCR reactions were performed with 15 µL of Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs), 0.2 µM of forward and reverse primers, and approximately 10 ng of template DNA. Thermal cycling included initial denaturation at 98 ℃ for 1 minute, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 ℃ for 10 seconds, annealing at 50 ℃ for 30 seconds, elongation at 72 ℃ for 30 seconds, and a final elongation at 72 ℃ for 5 minutes.

PCR products were mixed with an equal volume of 1× loading buffer containing SYBR Green and subjected to electrophoresis on a 2% agarose gel for detection. The PCR products were pooled in equal ratios, purified, and used to generate sequencing libraries with added indexes. The library was quantified using Qubit, real-time PCR, and a bioanalyzer, then pooled and sequenced on Illumina platforms based on the effective library concentration and required data volume.

Bioinformatics analysis pipeline

Sample data were demultiplexed based on barcode sequences and PCR amplification primer sequences. Denoising was performed using QIIME2 software (QIIME2-202006) to generate initial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). ASVs with total abundance less than 5 were subsequently filtered out (15). Taxonomic classification was conducted within QIIME2 with the SILVA (SSU rRNA gene) 138 reference database. Alpha and beta diversity analyses were performed on normalized data. Relative abundance at various taxonomic levels (phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) was plotted as histograms using the SVG function in Perl. Venn diagrams were used to visualize common and unique features among different samples or groups. Differential species between groups were analyzed using LEfSe (Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size) and random forest methods.

For random forest classification modeling, genus-level relative abundances were adopted as predictive features, and only taxa present in over 10% of samples were retained to reduce feature noise. Model hyperparameters were set as ntree =500, with mtry equal to the square root of all valid genera. Models were built with the top 3, 5, 10, 50, 70, 100, 150 and 200 discriminatory genera, and 10-fold cross-validation was applied to select the optimal feature set based on minimal cross-validation error. Internal validation relied on the algorithm’s built-in out-of-bag (OOB) resampling without external cohorts. Taxon discriminatory power was quantified by Mean Decrease in Accuracy and Mean Decrease in Gini, and the top 10 genera ranked by Mean Decrease in Accuracy were identified. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were generated for training and cross-validation test sets.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 for clinical baseline comparisons and R software (v4.0.3) for microbiota differential abundance analyses. The two groups were matched for mode of delivery, infant gender, and prolonged premature rupture of membranes [prolonged rupture of membranes (PROM) >18 hours] to eliminate confounding clinical factors. Categorical clinical variables including stratified gestational age, gender, delivery mode, and feeding pattern were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test; Fisher’s exact test was adopted for comparisons with small sample sizes. For microbial differential abundance analyses via MetaStat, LEfSe and t-tests, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction based on the Benjamini-Hochberg algorithm was applied to adjust q-values for multiple comparisons and reduce type I error inflation. A corrected q-value <0.05 was defined as the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the neonates in both groups

No significant differences were found between the two groups in gender ratio, mode of delivery, or incidence of PROM >18 hours. Extremely preterm infants in the NICU had an average hospital stay of 38.38 days, with extended use of antibiotics (12.38 days) and parenteral nutrition (21.05 days) (Table 1). All 34 extremely preterm infants in this study received mixed feeding consisting of maternal breast milk and formula. Only 4 out of 34 infants successfully initiated maternal milk feeding, and all breast milk used was exclusively from their own mothers.

Analysis of gut microbiota in neonates

Temporal changes in gut microbiota of full-term neonates

The number of unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) within the first 3 days, at 2 weeks, and at 4 weeks for full-term neonates was 1,257, 385, and 327, respectively (Figure 1A). Alpha diversity (Shannon Index) showed no significant differences across these time points (Figure 1B). Beta diversity analysis using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) indicated that clustering at 2 and 4 weeks was similar but distinct from clustering within the first 3 days (Figure 1C).

At the genus level, the top 10 most abundant genera in full-term neonates were Staphylococcus, Clostridium, Enterococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Salmonella, Bifidobacterium, Collinsella, Klebsiella, Bacteroides, and Enterobacter. Notably, Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides were significantly colonized within the first 3 days of life. Over time, the relative abundance of Staphylococcus and Enterococcus decreased markedly (Figure 1D). Comparison of gut microbiota composition at the genus level revealed a gradual decline in the colonization of Enterococcus, Delftia, Bacillus. These changes primarily occurred within the first 2 weeks (Figure 1E-1G).

Temporal changes in gut microbiota of extremely preterm infants in the NICU

For extremely preterm infants, the number of unique OTUs within the first 3 days, at 2 weeks, and at 4 weeks was 356, 198, and 106, respectively (Figure 2A). Alpha diversity (Shannon Index) decreased significantly at 2 and 4 weeks compared to the first 3 days (Figure 2B). Beta diversity analysis (PCoA) revealed distinct clustering at each time point (Figure 2C).

At the genus level, the top 10 most abundant genera in extremely preterm infants were Enterococcus, Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Staphylococcus, Citrobacter, Rothia, Clostridium, Sphingomonas, Veillonella, and Streptococcus (Figure 2D). Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, and Streptococcus exhibited higher relative abundances at 2 and 4 weeks. Among the bacilli, opportunistic pathogens such as Escherichia-Shigella and Klebsiella predominated, while beneficial taxa such as Bacteroides and Bifidobacterium were not prominently colonized. We compared the abundance of gut microbiota at the genus level across the three time points and found that from birth to 4 weeks, the colonization of Enterococcus, Klebsiella, Staphylococcus, Rothia, Clostridium, Veillonella, Streptococcus, Serratia, Enterobacter, and Lactobacillus significantly increased. This colonization process was largely completed within the first 2 weeks, with only Clostridium showing increased colonization by 4 weeks. Additionally, Sphingomonas, Bacteroides, Methylobacterium, Clostridium, and Bacillus exhibited significant decreases at both 2 weeks and 4 weeks (Figure 2E-2G).

Comparison of gut microbiota composition between the two groups

We compared the gut microbiota of extremely preterm infants in the NICU with that of full-term infants at the first 3 days, 2 weeks, and 4 weeks, focusing on alpha diversity, beta diversity, and differential species analysis.

Alpha diversity: the Shannon index was significantly higher in extremely preterm infants at the first 3 days compared to full-term infants. However, no significant differences were observed between the groups at 2 and 4 weeks (Figure 3A).

Beta diversity: PCoA revealed distinct clustering patterns and trajectories between the two groups. By 4 weeks, the preterm group was trending towards the full-term group (Figure 3B).

At the phylum level, the gut microbiota of both groups was predominantly composed of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, which together accounted for over 80% of the total microbiota. The relative abundance of Firmicutes was notably higher in extremely preterm infants at 2 and 4 weeks compared to full-term infants. At the order level, full-term infants exhibited higher enrichment of Enterobacteriales, Bifidobacteriales, and Bacteroidales, while extremely preterm infants had a greater relative abundance of Lactobacillales, Enterococcales, and Micrococcales. At the genus level, Enterococcus and Staphylococcus were more dominant in extremely preterm infants. Differences in the relative abundance of Escherichia-Shigella, Klebsiella, Serratia, and Clostridium between the two groups decreased over time. Meanwhile, beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides remained more abundant in full-term infants (Figure 3C-3E).

At the genus level, LEfSe analysis identified differential species between the two groups at three time points. In the first 3 days postpartum, full-term infants had a higher relative abundance of gamma-Proteobacteria, notably Enterobacteriaceae, within the phylum Proteobacteria. In contrast, extremely preterm infants had a higher abundance of alpha-Proteobacteria, specifically Sphingomonadaceae. Additionally, Pseudomonadales within gamma-Proteobacteria were more prevalent in extremely preterm infants. Within the phylum Firmicutes, full-term infants showed greater abundance of Clostridiales, while extremely preterm infants had elevated levels of Enterococcaceae and Streptococcaceae. At 2 weeks postpartum, new differential taxa included Actinobacteria, particularly Micrococcaceae, and Firmicutes, notably Veillonellaceae, in extremely preterm infants. By 4 weeks postpartum, full-term infants displayed significant accumulation of Bacteroidaceae and Enterobacteriaceae. In contrast, extremely preterm infants continued to exhibit predominant clustering of Enterococcaceae, Streptococcaceae, Staphylococcaceae, Micrococcaceae, and Veillonellaceae (Figure 4).

Further analysis using random forest identified the top 10 variables contributing to gut microbiota differences between the two groups at each time point. Key bacterial groups included Brevundimonas, Herbaspirillum, Sphingomonas, Clostridium, Pseudomonas, Methylobacterium, and Delftia. Notably, Sphingomonas, Methylobacterium, and Delftia were consistently identified at all three time points, with Sphingomonas showing relatively high abundance in extremely preterm infants (Figure 5).

Discussion

Our study reveals significant differences in gut microbiota composition and dynamics between NICU extremely preterm infants and term infants receiving normal breastfeeding across three neonatal time points.

For term infants, gut microbiota establishment begins with facultative anaerobes adapting to the oxygen-rich postnatal gut environment. Analysis of meconium collected within 3 days of birth indicated colonization by abundant Enterococcus, Enterobacteriaceae, Clostridium, and Staphylococcus. By 4 weeks, while alpha-diversity did not significantly increase, there was a notable decrease in Enterococcus and Staphylococcus, coupled with an increase in Clostridium and Klebsiella. Beneficial anaerobes such as Bifidobacteria and Bacteroides were detected within the first 3 days and persisted through 4 weeks, aligning with previous studies (16).

In NICU extremely preterm infants, gut microbiota changes were more pronounced at 3 days, 2 weeks, and 4 weeks. Initially, there was a clear dominance of cocci over bacilli, although the relative abundance of cocci decreased over time while bacilli and Clostridia increased. NICU extremely preterm infants’ gut microbiota initially colonized with facultative anaerobes, but the colonization of beneficial anaerobes like Bifidobacteria and Bacteroides was significantly delayed. Bacteroides showed a decreasing trend, while opportunistic pathogens such as Enterococcus, Staphylococcus, Klebsiella, and Enterobacter were more prevalent. Previous research indicates that certain Bacteroides species modulate the immune system by affecting Th1/Th2 balance (17) and degrade complex polysaccharides [e.g., human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs)], which are crucial for carbohydrate metabolism. Thus, the reduced capacity for carbohydrate metabolism and immune function in extremely preterm infants may be related to the early absence of Bacteroides. Gestational age is a fundamental determinant shaping gut microbiota maturation in preterm infants, while probiotics serve as an auxiliary intervention that partially modulates microbial composition (7,18,19). Gestational age-associated shifts in gut microbiota are primarily driven by enteral feeding. HMOs, complex carbohydrates that serve as substrates supporting the growth of Bifidobacterium and Bacteroides within the infant gut. The delayed establishment of gut microbiota in extremely preterm infants, due to delayed enteral nutrition, likely contributes to reduced colonization of Bifidobacteria.

Many studies suggest that gut microbiota diversity increases with age. However, for NICU extremely preterm infants, their specific condition and external factors contribute to unique gut microbiota development and dynamics (20,21). Our study found that the alpha-diversity of gut microbiota in extremely preterm infants at 3 days was significantly higher than that in term infants. Although several studies proposed that intrauterine microbial exposure might partly account for this phenomenon (22), previous work has reported that maternal gut microbiota disturbances caused by bacterial vaginosis, inflammatory bowel disease, or oral disorders are linked to elevated preterm birth risk (23-25), which may alter microbial transmission between mothers and newborns (16). Some investigations further hypothesized that Escherichia coli and Enterobacteriaceae detected in meconium derive from the placenta and amniotic fluid, and fetal swallowing of contaminated amniotic fluid could trigger intrauterine infection and preterm delivery (26-28). Nevertheless, these intrauterine microbial transmission theories remain debated and cannot fully explain the elevated alpha diversity observed in our preterm cohort. Multiple more consistent explanations center on perinatal and NICU environmental factors: extremely preterm infants are widely exposed to various hospital-derived microbes after admission, receive early broad-spectrum antibiotics, and rarely achieve exclusive breastfeeding-conditions that normally drive healthy full-term infants to develop a low-diversity gut microbiota dominated by facultative anaerobes (29-31). Notably, the elevated early alpha diversity seen in extremely preterm infants does not indicate a more mature or healthier microbial community; instead, it may signal an abnormal deviation from the standard developmental trajectory, where microbial diversity starts low and rises steadily with postnatal age. At 2 and 4 weeks, NICU extremely preterm infants showed significant decreases in alpha-diversity. Long-term antibiotic use in the NICU has been linked to decreased levels of Lactobacillus and Enterococcus (32), while enrichment of Enterobacteriaceae is associated with prolonged intravenous nutrition (33). Our study observed an average antibiotic use of over one week and three weeks of intravenous nutrition, potentially contributing to reduced alpha-diversity in extremely preterm infants during their NICU stay.

Interestingly, the development of early gut microbiota in NICU extremely preterm infants lags behind that of term infants, particularly in the colonization of Enterobacteriaceae, Bacteroides, Bifidobacteria, and Clostridia. However, this development is not straightforward but involves complex adaptive changes. Our beta-diversity analysis supports this, showing that although NICU extremely preterm infants’ gut microbiota trends towards that of term infants by 4 weeks, unique characteristics persist. For instance, while gamma-proteobacteria predominates in term infants, alpha-proteobacteria are more abundant in extremely preterm infants. Among gamma-proteobacteria, Enterobacteriaceae dominate in term infants, whereas Pseudomonas is more prevalent in extremely preterm infants. Thus, although NICU extremely preterm infants’ gut microbiota trends towards that of term infants, distinctive features and patterns remain. Recent studies have shown that preterm infants’ gut microbiota is initially simple and dominated by microorganisms such as Micromonospora, Acinetobacter, and Citrobacter. Over time, these are replaced by Enterococcus, Klebsiella, and E. coli, which are opportunistic pathogens. Similarly, Bifidobacteria colonization in preterm infants is relatively delayed and does not rapidly become a major component of the microbiota (34). These findings are consistent with our results.

Given the distinct composition and dynamics of gut microbiota in NICU extremely preterm infants compared to term infants, we identified the top 10 important taxa at the genus level through random forest analysis. Notably, Sphingomonas, Flavobacterium, and Methylobacterium appeared at all three time points. Sphingomonas, an alpha-proteobacterium, is a Gram-negative obligate aerobe. Previous studies have reported the enrichment of Sphingomonas in the gut microbiota of preterm infants (35). Elevated intestinal Sphingomonas abundance has been consistently detected across multiple independent cohorts of patients with active ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD) (36,37). Similarly, intestinal Sphingomonas abundance is markedly elevated in patients with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) (38,39), implying its involvement in disease pathogenesis. However, research on the role and significance of Sphingomonas in the gut microbiota of extremely preterm infants remains limited. We hypothesize that Sphingomonas could be a candidate microbial marker for the gut microbiota of extremely preterm infants. Its relative abundance might reflect the maturity of the gut microbiota and could be linked to the susceptibility of these infants to specific diseases. Further, more detailed studies are needed to explore these possibilities.

Early changes in extremely preterm infants’ gut microbiota may have long-term implications. The relationship between gut microbiota and immunity is an area of active research. Extremely preterm infants’ immune development is complex, with slower maturation of T and B cell responses and lower antibody levels compared to adults, extending into school age (40). This makes them more susceptible to infections but less prone to excessive immune responses to new antigens. The gut microbiota colonizes the neonatal intestine dynamically, stimulating and interacting with the immune system (41-43). The unique immune characteristics of newborns may better adapt to early microbial challenges (44). Our observation of higher alpha-diversity in extremely preterm infants’ early gut microbiota, compared to term infants, might reflect their immune system development. Although this increased diversity may be associated with early infections, it could also be an essential aspect of immune system maturation. Understanding the characteristics of extremely preterm infants’ early gut microbiota and their role in immune system development may offer new insights for preventing and treating preterm-related diseases.

Limitations of the present study

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may reduce statistical power to detect minor gut microbial differences and weaken result robustness. All participants were enrolled from a single tertiary hospital, limiting the generalizability of our findings to other centers with distinct clinical protocols. Second, we did not perform stratified adjustments for key clinical confounders (antibiotic exposure duration, parenteral nutrition course, in-hospital feeding patterns and probiotic use). While our cohort reflects local NICU practice, the results cannot be widely extrapolated. We plan to launch stratified follow-up cohort studies to adjust these confounders and refine the analysis of gut microbiota development in extremely preterm infants. Finally, the random forest model identified Sphingomonas as a candidate marker for extremely preterm infants, yet no external independent cohorts were available to validate its predictive reliability. Large multi-center longitudinal multi-omics research is required to verify our microbial markers and their clinical predictive value.

Conclusions

In summary, our study of gut microbiota composition and changes in NICU extremely preterm infants reveals that compared to term infants, NICU extremely preterm infants exhibit higher abundance of cocci and delayed colonization of Clostridia, Bacteroides, and Bifidobacteria. Despite some trends towards the gut microbiota of term infants by 2 and 4 weeks, extremely preterm infants’ microbiota display unique characteristics and patterns. Notably, Sphingomonas shows higher relative abundance in NICU extremely preterm infants, distinguishing their gut microbiota from that of term infants. These findings may offer new possibilities for understanding the time-dependent dynamics of extremely preterm infants’ gut microbiota and could inform strategies for infection prevention and immune-related therapies.
