Hypoxia-inducible factor 2α overexpression in podocytes ameliorates lipid metabolism disorders in diabetic kidney disease by inhibiting S1P
Authors: Xue Yang, Qimeng Wang, Huibin Nie, Mingming Wang, Yinghui Wang, Shan Li, Qingzhen Liu, Gang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aims: Lipid accumulation in podocytes is a major driver of diabetic kidney disease (DKD). Hypoxia-inducible factor 2α (HIF-2α) plays an important role in regulating metabolism. The function of HIF-2α in lipid metabolism in podocytes and the progression of DKD remain unclear.Methods: We investigated the effects of HIF-2α on podocyte damage and lipid metabolism using immunofluorescence, flow cytometry, ELISA, and Western blotting. In order to characterize the regulatory effects of HIF-2α, we also used ChIP and dual-luciferase reporter assays to investigate the role of sphingosine kinase 1 (SPHK1), a crucial enzyme in sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) synthesis. In vivo, the effect of HIF-2α on lipid metabolism disorders in db/db mice was investigated using the HIF-2α inhibitor PT-2385.Results: Our results revealed that HIF-2α overexpression improved lipid metabolism in DKD by enhancing cholesterol efflux via reduced S1P synthesis in podocytes by 25.69%. Inhibition of HIF-2α expression in the mouse model of diabetes exacerbated podocyte damage and proteinuria. Inhibition of SPHK1 expression rescued HIF-2α knockdown-mediated lipid disorders in podocytes. HIF-2α inhibited the transcription of SPHK1 by binding to the promoter region of SPHK1 and reduced S1P synthesis. Furthermore, we found that FG-4592, a HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, reduced the total cholesterol level in DKD by activating HIF-2α, thereby protecting against DKD.Conclusion: HIF-2α ameliorated lipid metabolism disorders and podocyte damage in DKD by downregulating S1P, providing a novel insight for HIF-2α against DKD.Keywords: Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), lipid metabolism, hypoxia-inducible factor 2α (HIF-2α), sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a common complication of diabetes. According to estimates, kidney damage is a consequence of diabetes in nearly 30% of patients with type 1 diabetes and 40% of patients with type 2 diabetes [1]. The primary clinical manifestations of DKD are proteinuria, the gradual worsening of renal function, and reduced glomerular filtration rate, which finally lead to renal failure [2]. It is well known that several factors affect the development and progression of DKD, including metabolic disorders, hemodynamic dysregulation, genetic susceptibility, inflammation, and fibrosis [1]. Lipid metabolism disorder plays a crucial role in the development and progression of DKD [3–5]. Dyslipidemia is a central lipid metabolism disorder, and the resulting lipotoxicity has been implicated in the progression of DKD [6]. DKD patients are characterized by increased plasma concentration of cholesterol, triglycerides, and apolipoprotein B (ApoB)-associated lipoproteins (very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and lipoprotein(a) (Lpa)), along with the decreased levels of high-density lipoproteins (HDL) [7]. Damage to the structure and function of podocytes is the primary cause of proteinuria, as the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) relies on podocytes, which sustains normal renal filtration function [8,9]. Notably, podocytes are particularly sensitive to lipid accumulation [10–14]. Lipid abnormalities can be commonly observed in DKD and contribute to podocyte lipotoxicity, exacerbating renal injury [15]. Given the central role of lipid dysmetabolism in the progression of DKD, identifying the molecular pathways regulating lipid accumulation in podocytes is critical for therapeutic innovation.

Cellular and tissue hypoxia have been common findings in DKD [16]. Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) are a family of transcription factors that maintain oxygen homeostasis and contribute to adaptation to hypoxic conditions. They consist of α and β subunits. Under normoxic conditions, prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) hydroxylates the prolyl residue of HIF-α, and then, the proteasome degrades the hydroxylated HIF-α. Instead of degrading under hypoxic environments, HIF-α is transported to the nucleus, forming a heterodimer with HIF-β and binding to hypoxia-responsive areas to trigger the transcription of target genes [17,18]. HIF-2α is crucial for maintaining metabolism and affects metabolic diseases, such as obesity, type 2 diabetes, and NAFLD [19,20]. However, the role of HIF-2α in these metabolic diseases remains controversial. Adipocyte HIF-2α prevents insulin resistance associated with high-fat diet (HFD) [21]. Activation of HIF-2α in the liver inhibited glucagon signaling and ameliorated hyperglycemia in the animal models of streptozotocin-induced diabetes and acute insulin-resistance [22]. In contrast, HIF-2α activation led to significant hepatic steatosis in the liver tissue [23]. However, the role of HIF-2α in DKD remains unclear.

Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) is a key signaling sphingolipid molecule. It is produced by phosphorylation of sphingosine through the function of sphingosine kinases (SPHKs) [24]. Studies have shown that S1P plays an important role in renal diseases, such as DKD [25–27], renal fibrosis [28], nephrotic syndrome [29], IgA nephropathy, and lupus nephritis [30]. Activation of SPHK1 and production of S1P are associated with renal hypertrophy and increased levels of fibronectin [31]. S1P also plays an important role in lipid metabolism [32–34]. It has been shown that SPHK1/S1P promotes hepatic steatosis and inflammation. SPHK1 expression was found to be upregulated in the livers of mice on a high-fat, high-sugar diet and in patients with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis. SphK1-/- mice were found to suffer from less severe high-fat, high-sugar diet-induced hepatic inflammation and lipid accumulation [35]. Therefore, we hypothesized that HIF-2α affects lipid metabolism in DKD podocytes through S1P.

To address the gap in understanding the role of HIF-2α in lipid metabolism in DKD, this study investigated whether HIF-2α modulates lipid accumulation in podocytes by regulating S1P signaling. In addition, we explored the effects of the HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor FG-4592 on lipid metabolism in podocytes.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Conditionally immortalized human podocytes, generously donated by Dr. Fan Yi of Shandong University, were generated by introducing temperature-sensitive simian virus 40 T antigen. Podocytes were maintained in proliferation in RPMI 1640 medium containing 20 U/mL human recombinant interferon-γ (IFN-γ), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 1% antibiotics (streptomycin and penicillin) at 33 °C. Next, to stimulate differentiation, the culture conditions were adjusted to 37 °C without IFN-γ, and the cells were cultured for at least two weeks prior to in vitro experiments. Before beginning the experiments, all cells were grown in serum-free media for 6 h to synchronize them into a quiescent state. The cells were then exposed to different stimuli for 72 h in the following manner: (1) normal glucose (NG): the final concentration of glucose in the medium was 5.6 mmol/L; (2) normal glucose + mannitol (NG + MA): medium containing 5.6 mmol/L glucose was supplemented with 24.4 mmol/L of mannitol as an osmolality control for high glucose; (3) high glucose (HG): the final concentration of glucose in the medium was 30 mmol/L; (4) high glucose + 0.5 μmol/L of S1P (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA, HG + S1P); (5) high glucose + 0.5 μmol/L of S1P + 10 μmol/L of CAY10444 (S1PR3 inhibitor, MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA, HG + S1P + CAY10444); (6) high glucose + 1 μmol/L of PF-543 (SPHK1 inhibitor, MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA, HG + PF-543); (7) high glucose + 50 μmol/L FG-4592 (HIF prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, Selleck, Texas, USA, HG + FG-4592). Moreover, the conditional 293 T cells were graciously supplied by Dr. Chengyun Zheng of Shandong University; 293 T cells were cultured in DMEM (high glucose) medium containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics (penicillin and streptomycin) at 37 °C.

Cell transfection

Antibiotic-free medium was utilized to cultivate the cells. The cells were transfected with short interfering RNA (siRNA) for target genes using the Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, California, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. siRNA with a non-targeting sequence was employed as the control. All siRNAs were obtained from Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (China). The sequences of siRNA are shown in Table S1. CMV promoter-driven HIF-2α-expressing lentivirus and empty lentivirus were designed and purchased from Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (China) to achieve stable overexpression. Podocytes were infected with lentivirus with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 for 48 h, followed by selection with puromycin.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining and confocal microscopy

Nephrin (1:100, Santa Cruz, California, USA) and synaptopodin (1:100, Santa Cruz, California, USA) primary antibodies were incubated with podocytes overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with FITC-coupled secondary antibodies (1:100, Zsbio, Beijing, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, podocytes were incubated with DAPI (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 5 min. The images were captured with an LSM780 laser scanning confocal microscope (ZEN, Germany).

Detection of lipids and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P)

Total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured in cells, serum, and kidney tissues using commercial kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, China) via enzymatic methods. Briefly, podocytes were seeded into a six-well plate, and 200 μL of the lysis solution was added to lyse the cells and release intracellular components. Approximately 20 mg of kidney tissue was weighed and homogenized in the lysate. Next, the supernatant was collected after centrifugation. Cholesterol, triglyceride, HDL-C, and LDL-C levels were quantified following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, these levels were also corrected based on protein concentration. S1P levels in cell supernatant, serum, and renal tissue were detected following the instructions of the ELISA kits (Sbjbio, Nanjing, China).

Podocyte apoptosis

Podocytes received treatment with or without HG (30 mmol/L) for 72 h. Apoptosis was detected by flow cytometry with Annexin V-FITC/PI double labeling (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Cells that tested negative for Annexin V-FITC and PI were considered viable. Flow cytometry was used to measure the percentages of Annexin V-FITC+/PI- (early apoptotic) and Annexin V-FITC+/PI+ (late apoptotic) cells.

Oil Red O staining

After culturing on coverslips, podocytes were fixed with 4% PFA. Frozen kidney pieces were returned to room temperature. Following PBS washing, Oil Red O dye (Solarbio, Beijing, China) mixed in double-distilled water at an 3:2 ratio was used to stain frozen sections or coverslips for 30 min after incubating 60% isopropyl alcohol for 2 min. Following a 1-min wash with 60% isopropanol, frozen sections or coverslips were counterstained for 10 s with hematoxylin. Under a microscope, lipid droplet deposition in tissue or podocytes was detected.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR assays

Total RNA was obtained from cells utilizing the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (Shanghai Yishan Biotechnology, China). The cDNA was composited according to the instruction of the Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and quantified using the SYBR Green Master Mix kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). β-actin was used as the internal reference. Relative expression of RNAs was quantified using the 2−△△Ct method. All primer sequences were synthesized by Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd. (China). The primer sequences used for quantification are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Protein was extracted from kidney tissues and podocytes using RIPA lysis buffer. The protein concentration was measured by the BCA assay (Epizyme, Shanghai, China). Western blot analysis was performed to determine the level of protein expression. Each lane was loaded with 15 μg of protein. After incubating the membrane overnight at 4 °C with anti-β-tubulin (1:3000, Cohesion, Suzhou, China), anti-HIF-2α (1:500, Abcam, Massachusetts, USA), anti-Nephrin (1:500, Santa Cruz, California, USA), anti-ABCA1 (1:250, Abcam, Massachusetts, USA), anti-SPHK1 (1:1000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-SPHK2 (1:4000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), anti-S1PR1 (1:2000, Abways, Shanghai, China), anti-S1PR2 (1:2000, Abclonal, Wuhan, China), and anti-S1PR3 (1:2000, Abways, Shanghai, China), the secondary antibodies (1:10000, goat anti-rabbit/mouse, Zsbio, Beijing, China) were used to incubate the membrane for 1 h at room temperature. As an internal control, β-tubulin was utilized.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays

ChIP assays were used to measure HIF-2α binding to the SPHK1 promoter. Podocytes were fixed in formaldehyde at 37 °C for 10 min to cross-link chromatin after 72 h of hyperglycemic stimulation, followed by quenching with glycine for 5 min. The collected chromatin DNA was sheared into fragments (approximately 200–1000 bp) using a non-contact ultrasonic disruptor (Diagenode, Belgium). The disruptor was operated at 4 °C for 30 s with 30-s intervals for 10 cycles. The sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated overnight using 1 μg of HIF-2α antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Massachusetts, USA) and anti-IgG control antibody (Abclonal, Wuhan, China). DNA immunoprecipitated with these antibodies was analyzed using qPCR. The regions located 2000 bp upstream of the SPHK1 gene were identified as the promoter regions. Primer sequences are provided in Table S1.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The potential HRE region of the SPHK1 promoter was cloned into the pGL3-basic vector (Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd., China) to measure the effect of HIF-2α on SPHK1 expression. SPHK1 reporter plasmid, phRL-TK Renilla luciferase control vector, HIF-2α overexpression plasmid, and the corresponding empty plasmid were transfected into 293 T cells. After 48 h, luciferase experiments were conducted using the dual-luciferase assay kit (Promega, Wisconsin, USA). The samples were quantified using relative luciferase activity (Firefly/Renilla luciferase ratio) and reported as fold change compared to the control.

Animal experiments

Animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong University and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Animal experiments also followed the guidelines of The Animal Research: Reporting of in vivo Experiments (ARRIVE). All mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment (humidity: 30–60%; temperature: 22 ± 1 °C), with a 12-h light–dark cycle. Bedding, cages, and drinking water were all sterilized using high-pressure high-temperature. Male db/db and db/m mice aged 6 weeks were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Jiangsu, China). Model mice were diagnosed with DKD when fasting blood glucose levels were more than 16.7 mmol/L in the presence of proteinuria. Db/db mice with 16 weeks of age were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6, each): the DKD group subjected to intraperitoneal injection of PBS every other day; the DKD+PT-2385 group subjected to intraperitoneal injection of the HIF-2α inhibitor PT-2385 (0.4 mg/kg) every other day; the DKD+PF-543 group subjected to intraperitoneal injection of the SPHK1 inhibitor PF-543 (1 mg/kg) every other day; the DKD+FG-4592 group subjected to intraperitoneal injection of the prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor FG-4592 (20 mg/kg) every other day. And db/m mice as the normal control group subjected to intraperitoneal injection of PBS every other day (n = 6). Mice at 20 weeks of age were euthanized, and their blood and urine samples and kidney tissues were harvested for histological and biochemical analyses. Using a glucometer, fasting blood glucose was measured every other week from the tail vein of mice fasted for more than 8 h. Urine samples were collected from mice that were individually housed in metabolic cages for 24 h before treatment and at the end of the experiment. The mouse urinary microalbumin ELISA kit (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) was employed to measure urine albumin following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histological analysis of kidneys

Periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining was performed on paraffin sections of the kidneys according to the instructions provided with the PAS staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China) to assess kidney injury.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The kidney tissues were divided into 1 mm3 sections and preserved in the cold electron microscope fixation solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China). The kidney tissues were detected using TEM by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. TEM scans were examined utilizing ImageJ software to calculate the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) thickness, foot process breadth, and the number of foot processes per μm of GBM.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The paraffin-embedded slices were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was then carried out over a 10 min boiling process in EDTA buffer, pH 9.0 (Solarbio, China). Next, the endogenous peroxidase blocker was used to block hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 min. After being incubated with goat serum for 20 min at room temperature, the slices were incubated with anti-ABCA1 (1:200, Abcam, Massachusetts, USA) and anti-SPHK1 (1:200, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) overnight at 4 °C. The slices were treated with the enzyme-labeled sheep anti-mouse/rabbit IgG polymer for 40 min after being cleaned in PBS. Next, the sections were shown stained with DAB solution (Zsbio, Beijing, China), and the slices were restained with hematoxylin. Lastly, the Hamamatsu NanoZoomer S60 digital biopsy scanner scans the biopsy data (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Comparisons between the two groups were performed by t-tests. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to analyze the differences among multiple groups. Differences were assessed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Results

HIF-2α overexpression protected podocytes subjected to high glucose stimulation

We overexpressed or knocked down HIF-2α in podocytes (Fig. S1B and C) and subsequently stimulated them with high glucose for 72 h (Fig. S1A) to investigate whether HIF-2α protects high glucose-stimulated podocytes. First, we conducted immunofluorescence staining to observe the effects of high glucose stimulation and HIF-2α on the rearrangement and structure of cytoskeletal proteins in podocytes. Laser confocal microscopy was conducted to photograph the podocyte microfilament protein F-actin, which showed that high glucose stimulation led to podocyte cytoskeletal protein breakdown and rearrangement, while HIF-2α ameliorated cytoskeletal derangement in podocytes (Figure 1(A)). High glucose stimulation reduced the expression of marker proteins nephrin and synaptopodin in podocytes, although HIF-2α increased their levels (Figure 1(B) and (C)). Furthermore, we used flow cytometry to investigate podocyte apoptosis. High glucose stimulation induced apoptosis in podocytes. HIF-2α overexpression and HIF-2α knockdown with siRNA prevented and enhanced podocyte apoptosis, respectively (Figure 1(D)). Many clinical and basic studies have shown that abnormal lipid metabolism in renal podocytes can lead to the development of glomerular diseases, such as DKD [36]; therefore, we measured the levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, and LDL-C in podocytes. High glucose stimulation increased the levels of total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL-C, and LDL-C in podocytes (Figure 1(E)). HIF-2α reduced the levels of total cholesterol and LDL-C (Figure 1(E)). Oil Red O staining revealed lipid deposition in podocytes treated with high glucose (Figure 1(F)). HIF-2α overexpression suppressed high glucose-induced lipid deposition, while HIF-2α knockdown enhanced lipid deposition in podocytes (Figure 1(F)). These results suggest that HIF-2α upregulation ameliorates high glucose-induced lipid metabolism disorders in podocytes and protects podocytes to some extent.

HIF-2α downregulated S1P expression, while S1P exacerbated lipid metabolic disorders in podocytes

Previous studies have shown that the elevated levels of S1P are associated with kidney injury [37,38]. To explore whether HIF-2α exerts protective effects through S1P, we conducted ELISA to measure S1P levels in cell supernatants and cells. HIF-2α overexpression reduced S1P expression by 25.69% in the supernatant of podocytes stimulated with high glucose, but did not significantly affect S1P level in podocytes (Figure 2A).

In addition, we stimulated podocytes with 0.5 μmol/L exogenous S1P (Fig. S2A) and used flow cytometry to detect podocyte apoptosis. Stimulation with exogenous S1P enhanced the apoptosis of podocytes (Figure 2(B)). S1P increased total cholesterol levels and lipid deposition in podocytes (Figure 2(C) and (D)), suggesting that S1P acts as a key mediator of abnormal lipid metabolism in DKD podocytes, whereas HIF-2α reduces S1P levels.

HIF-2α regulated lipid metabolism in podocytes by enhancing ABCA1 expression via S1P-S1PR3

HIF-2α predominantly affected total cholesterol level in podocytes (Figure 1(D)). Using RT-qPCR, we measured the expression levels of genes involved in cholesterol synthesis, uptake, and efflux. The results showed that HIF-2α mainly increased the mRNA levels of ABCA1, a gene involved in cholesterol efflux, but did not significantly affect the expression of other genes involved in cholesterol metabolism (Figure 3(A)). Western blotting also showed that HIF-2α increased the expression of ABCA1 (Figure 3(B)). In addition, stimulation with exogenous S1P reduced ABCA1 expression in podocytes compared to the HG group (Figure 3(G) and (H)).

S1P is a powerful activator of cell signaling pathways. In addition to serving as an internal messenger, S1P binds to five G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) on the cell surface, which plays a key role in various cellular processes. S1PR1-3 is mainly expressed in the kidney [39,40]; therefore, we measured the expression of S1PR1-3. RT-qPCR and Western blotting showed that HIF-2α reduced the expression of S1PR3 (Figure 3(C) and (D)). Thus, we stimulated podocytes with 10 μmol/L of the S1PR3 inhibitor CAY10444 (Fig. S2B). Western blotting indicated that CAY10444 decreased S1PR3 levels (Figure 3(E)). Treatment with CAY10444 reduced S1P-induced increase in total cholesterol levels (Figure 3(F)). Both qPCR and Western blotting revealed that CAY10444 stimulation reversed S1P-induced decrease in ABCA1 expression (Figure 3(G) and (H)). It has been reported that ABCA1 expression is affected by ERK1/2 signaling [41,42]. By measuring ERK1/2 phosphorylation in S1P-treated podocytes, we investigated whether S1P/S1PR3 signaling downregulates ABCA1 expression via ERK1/2 activation. Treatment with S1P significantly enhanced ERK1/2 phosphorylation compared to stimulation with HG, an effect substantially reversed by CAY10444 (Figure 3(H)). Therefore, we concluded that S1P-S1PR3 may inhibit ABCA1 expression through ERK1/2.

HIF-2α in podocytes regulated S1P synthesis by modulating SPHK1 expression

S1P is synthesized by sphingosine kinases (SPHKs)-mediated phosphorylation of sphingosine. SPHK1 and SPHK2 are the two primary isoforms of SPHKs. We measured the expression of SPHK1 and SPHK2 to investigate how HIF-2α reduces S1P levels in high glucose-stimulated podocytes. High glucose stimulation increased SPHK1 expression but did not affect SPHK2 expression (Figure 4(A) and (B)). The effect of high glucose stimulation on SPHK1 was reversed by HIF-2α overexpression (Figure 4(A) and (B)). We stimulated podocytes with 1 μmol/L of the SPHK1 inhibitor PF-543 to elucidate the role of SPHK1 in high glucose-stimulated podocytes (Fig. S2C). Podocytes treated with PF-543 showed a significant decrease in SPHK1 expression, but no effect on SPHK2 expression (Figure 4(F)). In addition, we measured S1P levels in cells and cell supernatants. We found that PF-543-stimulated podocytes decreased S1P levels in cell supernatants (Figure 4(C)). Compared to high glucose-stimulated podocytes, stimulation with PF-543 downregulated total cholesterol level in podocytes (Figure 4(D)). RT-qPCR and Western blotting showed that treatment with PF-543 attenuated high glucose-induced decrease in ABCA1 expression in podocytes (Figure 4(E) and (F)). In summary, these results suggest that inhibition of SPHK1 expression can reduce S1P synthesis, thereby ameliorating lipid disorders in podocytes.

To investigate how HIF-2α affects SPHK1 expression in podocytes, we analyzed the promoter of SPHK1 and discovered three hypothetical HIF-responsive elements (HREs) in the promoter region of SPHK1, namely, HRE1, HRE2, and rHRE3 (Figure 4(G)). Therefore, we conducted a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) test and found that in high glucose-stimulated podocytes, HIF-2α overexpression enhanced HIF-2α binding to HRE2 but not to HRE1 and rHRE3 (Figure 4(H)). Dual-luciferase reporter assay also revealed that HIF-2α overexpression inhibited the transcriptional activity of the SPHK1 promoter. However, this inhibitory effect disappeared after HRE2 mutation (Figure 4(I)), suggesting that HIF-2α downregulates the expression of SPHK1 by binding to HRE2 in the SPHK1 promoter.

Inhibition of SPHK1 expression in high glucose-stimulated podocytes mitigated abnormal lipid metabolism induced by HIF-2α knockdown

We conducted rescue experiments to investigate whether SPHK1 downregulation is necessary for HIF-2α to alleviate abnormal lipid metabolism in podocytes. In rescue experiments, podocytes were first transfected with siRNA-HIF-2α and then treated with the SPHK1 inhibitor. Compared to the siRNA-HIF-2α group, the expression of SPHK1 was significantly reduced in the siRNA-HIF-2α + PF-543 group, whereas SPHK2 expression remained unchanged (Figure 5(A)). S1P levels in cell supernatants were also downregulated in the siRNA-HIF-2α + PF-543 group (Figure 5(C)). siRNA-HIF-2α upregulated total cholesterol levels compared to the HG group, but treatment with the SPHK1 inhibitor effectively attenuated this effect (Figure 5(D)). Subsequently, we measured the expression of ABCA1 and found that it showed a significant decrease in the siRNA-HIF-2α group compared to the HG group. However, treatment with the SPHK1 inhibitor effectively reversed the decrease in ABCA1 expression (Figure 5(A) and (B)). The increased mRNA levels of S1PR3 after HIF-2α knockdown in podocytes were also reversed by inhibiting SPHK1 in podocytes (Figure 5(B)).

HIF-2α attenuated lipid metabolism disorders in the kidneys of DKD mice via S1P-S1PR3

Using db/m mice as normal controls, we treated db/db mice with PT-2385 (an HIF-2α inhibitor) and PF-543 (a SPHK1 inhibitor) during the experimental period to investigate the protective effects of HIF-2α against DKD (Fig. S3A). The results showed that the kidney weight/body weight ratio was reduced (Figure 6(A)), but blood glucose (Fig. S3B), blood creatinine (Figure 6B), and urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) (Figure 6(C)) were increased in the db/db group compared to the db/m group. The db/db + PT-2385 group showed increased blood creatinine and UACR levels with similar blood glucose levels. In contrast, the db/db + PF-543 group showed decreased blood creatinine and UACR levels. PAS staining showed more severe renal injury in the db/db + PT-2385 group compared to the db/db group, while renal injury was attenuated in the db/db + PF-543 group (Figure 6(D)). TEM analysis showed morphologic changes in podocytes. Podocytes were structurally intact and exhibited a well-arranged foot process in the db/m group. Thicker GBM, wider foot process, and fewer foot processes on the GBM were found in the db/db group (Figure 6(D)). Podocyte damage was aggravated in the db/db + PT-2385 group. However, these changes were mitigated by PF-543 (Figure 6(D)). The podocyte marker protein nephrin was downregulated in the kidneys of mice in the db/db group and was further downregulated in the kidneys of mice in the db/db + PT-2385 group, but PF-543 increased its expression (Figure 6(E)).

Next, we measured S1P and lipid-related indices in mice. Compared to the db/m group, the levels of S1P, total cholesterol, triglyceride, HDL-C, and LDL-C were elevated in kidney tissues of the db/db group. The db/db + PT-2385 group showed significantly elevated levels of S1P (33.11% higher than that of the db/db group), total cholesterol (74.12% higher than that of the db/db group), and LDL-C levels (23.97% higher than that of the db/db group), but no significant changes were observed in triglyceride and HDL-C levels (Figure 6(F) and (G)). In contrast, treatment with PF-543 significantly downregulated S1P (27.82% decrease compared to the db/db group), total cholesterol (41.74% decrease compared to the db/db group), and LDL-C levels (20.27% decrease compared to the db/db group) in mice (Figure 6(F) and (G)). The expression levels of S1P and lipid-related indicators were identical in the serum samples of the four groups of mice (Fig. S3C and D). Oil Red O staining revealed that treatment with PT-2385 aggravated lipid deposition in the db/db group, but PF-543 mitigated lipid deposition (Figure 6(H)). The results of Western blotting were consistent with those of in vitro experiments, showing that ABCA1 expression was decreased in the db/db group, while the expression of SPHK1 and S1PR3 was increased in this group. The expression of these proteins showed a more severe alteration in the renal cortex of mice in the Db/db + PT-2385 group, but these changes were reversed in the db/db + PF-543 group (Figure 6(I)). The results of immunohistochemical staining regarding the expression of ABCA1 and SPHK1 were consistent with those of Western blotting (Figure 6(J)).

FG-4592 attenuated lipid metabolism disorder in the podocytes of mice with DKD

Furthermore, we investigated the role of FG-4592, a specific HIF-2α agonist, on lipid metabolism disorders in DKD. We administered 50 μmol/L of FG-4592 to podocytes (Fig. S2D). FG-4592 enhanced the expression of HIF-2α (Figure 7(A)) and suppressed podocyte apoptosis (Figure 7(B)). FG-4592 decreased total cholesterol levels, cellular lipid deposition, and S1P concentrations in cell supernatants (Figure 7(C)–(E)). RT-qPCR and Western blotting revealed that FG-4592 increased ABCA1 expression and decreased the expression of SPHK1 and S1PR3 (Figure 7(F) and (G)). Mice with DKD treated with FG-4592 (Fig. S4A) had reduced kidney weight/body weight, blood creatinine, and UACR compared to the db/db group (Figure 8(A)). However, FG-4592 did not significantly affect blood glucose levels in mice (Fig. S4B). Additionally, PAS and TEM revealed that FG-4592 attenuated kidney and podocyte injury (Figure 8(B)). FG-4592 increased the expression levels of nephrin, a podocyte marker (Figure 8(C)). FG-4592 reduced S1P (26.00% decrease compared to the db/db group) and total cholesterol levels (53.65% decrease compared to the db/db group) in kidney tissues (Figure 8(D) and (E)). Furthermore, FG-4592 reduced LDL-C levels in kidney tissues (Fig. S4C). S1P and lipid-related indicators showed the same expression trend in the serum of mice from three groups (Fig. S4D and E). FG-4592 also prevented lipid deposition in the kidney (Figure 8(F)). In addition, FG-4592 increased the expression of ABCA1 and decreased the expression of SPHK1 and S1PR3 (Figure 8(G) and (H)).

Discussion

In the present study, HIF-2α overexpression ameliorated abnormal lipid metabolism in podocytes subjected to high glucose and protected podocytes stimulated by high glucose. HIF-2α knockdown exacerbated lipid metabolism disorder in podocytes stimulated with high glucose. Similarly, in the mice model of diabetes, inhibition of HIF-2α expression exacerbated kidney damage and proteinuria. Mechanistic studies showed that HIF-2α overexpression reduced S1P-S1PR3 expression in high glucose-stimulated podocytes and improved lipid metabolism disorder induced by high glucose by promoting ABCA1 expression. SPHK1, which synthesizes S1P, was a target gene of HIF-2α. HIF-2α inhibited SPHK1 transcription by binding to the SPHK1 promoter region, thereby reducing S1P synthesis. In addition, we found that FG-4592 alleviated lipid metabolism disorder in DKD by activating HIF-2α. HIF-2α may be a potential target for ameliorating lipid disorder in DKD.

Over the past decades, DKD has become the leading cause of end-stage renal disease worldwide [43]. Several studies have shown that podocyte density in the glomeruli is the best predictor of proteinuria and DKD progression [44–46]. In animal models, loss of more than 20% of podocytes led to irreversible glomerular damage characterized by glomerulosclerosis and proteinuria, which progressed to tubulointerstitial fibrosis and end-stage renal disease [47,48]. Changes in key signaling pathways in podocytes can significantly worsen or improve the progression of proteinuria in DKD [49,50]. Excessive lipid accumulation in podocytes can lead to lipotoxicity, which triggers podocyte hypertrophy, dedifferentiation, mesenchymal transition, actin cytoskeleton disorganization, and death [51]. A prospective study of a large Danish cohort found that patients with familial hypercholesterolemia suffer from a considerably higher risk of chronic kidney disease (CKD), implying that lipotoxicity plays a significant role in the development of CKD [52]. Several studies reported that the podocyte cholesterol efflux route plays a critical role in the development and progression of DKD [53,54]. Cholesterol efflux in DKD is significantly affected by ATP-binding cassette transporter proteins, including ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 1 (ABCA1) and ABC subfamily G member 1 (ABCG1). Compared to podocytes cultured with the serum of diabetic patients without albuminuria, podocytes cultured with the serum of DKD patients exhibited higher cholesterol buildup and actin remodeling. The serum of DKD patients decreased the expression level of ABCA1, a protein-regulating cholesterol efflux. In addition, cyclodextrins reduced the accumulation of cholesterol by inducing cholesterol efflux, which protects against DKD [53]. ABCA1 expression is decreased in mice with type 1 diabetes [55]. Likewise, downregulation of ABCA1 and ABCG1 in mice with type 2 diabetes led to lipid deposition in the kidneys and kidney injury [10,56]. In the present study, high glucose stimulation reduced ABCA1 expression in podocytes, which resulted in cholesterol accumulation and exacerbated renal injury.

There are three known isozymes of HIF-α, including HIF-1α, HIF-2α, and HIF-3α. HIF-1α and HIF-2α are the most extensively studied HIF transcription factors, with similar structure and unique target genes [57]. Studies have indicated that HIF-1α is linked to acute hypoxia reactions, promotes glycolytic genes, and reduces oxygen consumption and ROS production [58,59]. HIF-2α plays a critical role in response to chronic long-term hypoxia, promotes erythropoietin synthesis and iron metabolism, and affects lipid metabolism by modulating inflammation and fatty acid synthesis and uptake [58,60]. The kidneys in DKD are in a state of chronic hypoxia due to hyperglycemia-induced microvascular damage, which lead to abnormal oxygen delivery, glomerular hyperfiltration, and upregulation of the sodium-glucose cotransporter, which in turn increases oxygen demand [61,62]. HIF-2α may play a more critical role in DKD. Previous studies have indicated that HIF-2α in certain organs improves glucose and lipid metabolic disorders. Inflammation and insulin resistance are more severe in mice with low HIF-2α expression in their adipocytes [63]. Adipose tissue macrophage HIF-2α attenuates adipose tissue inflammation and improves insulin resistance in obese individuals [64]. Adipocyte HIF-2α ameliorates insulin resistance, adipose tissue inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and brown fat dysfunction in obesity [65]. Furthermore, adipocyte HIF-2α prevents atherosclerosis by accelerating ceramide degradation, which promotes the elimination of hepatic cholesterol [21]. Here, we found that HIF-2α in podocytes reduced lipid accumulation and ameliorated renal injury in DKD by promoting cholesterol efflux. However, Xie et al. found that HIF-2α signaling in intestinal tissues is positively correlated with body mass index and hepatotoxicity. High-fat diet-induced hepatic steatosis and obesity were considerably mitigated in mice with intestine-specific HIF-2α loss [66]. These differences may be due to differences in animal models and experimental conditions. In the present study, we found that treatment with FG-4592 ameliorated DKD-induced lipid metabolism disorders and attenuated renal injury, which supports the protective effect of HIF-2α.

Sphingolipids play a key role in preserving renal function. Accumulation of sphingolipids and sphingolipid metabolites has been found in experimental and clinical models of kidney disease. The composition of intracellular sphingolipids in the kidney is a significant factor in determining renal function. In a high-coverage targeted lipidomics study conducted in a Chinese cohort, sphingolipids, particularly ceramides, sphingomyelins, and S1P, were found to be significantly associated with the development of type 2 diabetes [67]. Likewise, another clinical study employing large-scale lipidomics reported a correlation between sphingolipids and the development of type 2 diabetes. A higher sphingolipid concentration was associated with a higher risk of type 2 diabetes [26]. Compared to healthy controls, plasma sphingomyelin and S1P levels are elevated in patients with lupus nephritis. Similarly, plasma eS1P levels were found to be considerably higher in individuals suffering from ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV). In addition, the plasma levels of eS1P were found to be significantly greater in individuals with active AAV than in those in remission [38]. Serum and urinary concentrations of S1P are positively correlated with renal injury in patients with IgA nephropathy [68]. Elevated plasma and renal S1P levels can be detected in streptozotocin-induced diabetes models [69–71]. In tetracycline-induced diabetic mouse models, increased glomerular SPHK1 expression led to S1P accumulation [31]. Similarly, single-nucleus RNA sequencing indicated that podocytes of patients with DKD have higher levels of SPHK1 expression than normal controls [72]. Schumann et al. revealed that the elevated levels of S1P can lead to renal and podocyte damage, increase UACR levels, upregulate serum cholesterol concentrations, and elevate triglyceride concentrations [37]. In addition, SPHK deficiency or its pharmacological suppression protects against insulin resistance and prevents HFD-induced hepatic inflammation, NASH, and hepatic steatosis in mice [35,73]. The effects of S1P are mainly mediated through the S1P receptor family, which include the widely expressed S1PR1, S1PR2, and S1PR3. After binding to S1PRs, S1P can activate various cellular signaling pathways. In this study, we found that S1P was upregulated in the serum sample and kidney tissue of mice with DKD. S1P activates the ERK1/2 signaling pathway via S1PR3, thereby inhibiting ABCA1 expression and exacerbating cholesterol accumulation. Inhibition of S1P expression alleviated lipid metabolism disorder and kidney damage in DKD. By inhibiting SPHK1 transcription, HIF-2α reduced S1P synthesis by 25.69%, thereby decreasing S1P levels and enhancing ABCA1 expression in podocytes. In addition, S1PR3 levels were decreased in podocytes overexpressing HIF-2α, while S1PR1 and S1PR2 levels were not significantly affected. This effect may be associated with the specific tissue distribution of S1PRs and disease status. Despite high sequence homology, the structures of S1PR subtypes widely differ [74]. The expression of S1PRs is context-dependent and cell type-dependent, varying not only between different tissues but also during the development and aging of organisms [75]. The specific underlying mechanisms necessitate further studies and exploration. Consistent with our findings, the expression level of SPHK1 was found to be significantly increased in steatotic hepatocytes. Treatment with S1P or overexpression of SPHK1 markedly increases hepatic lipid accumulation. In contrast, siRNA-mediated suppression of SPHK1 or S1P receptors, S1P2, and S1P3 markedly suppresses lipid accumulation in hepatocytes [33]. Inhibition of S1P cleavage enzymes significantly downregulates ABCA1 and ABCG1, exacerbates atherosclerosis, and induces plaque rupture in ApoE-/- mice [76]. Altogether, these results suggest that S1P is an important factor contributing to lipid metabolism disorders and renal injury in DKD.

FG-4592, a prolyl hydroxylase inhibitor, increases the protein levels of HIF-2α by inhibiting HIF-2α degradation [77]. It is used to treat renal anemia in clinical settings. Several randomized phase II and phase III studies showed that FG-4592 greatly decreases the plasma levels of total cholesterol in individuals with chronic renal disease [78–80]. However, how FG-4592 reduces cholesterol levels remains to be clarified. In this study, we discovered that FG-4592 improved lipid accumulation in DKD by stabilizing HIF-2α expression and reducing S1P levels. These findings suggest that FG-4592 can be repurposed for the treatment of DKD by targeting lipid metabolism disorders. However, clinical trials are needed to evaluate its efficacy and safety in patients with DKD, considering potential off-target effects, such as erythropoietin overproduction.

This study outlined the critical role of HIF-2α in lipid metabolism disorders in DKD podocytes and provided a new theoretical basis for HIF-2α in the treatment of DKD. However, some limitations remain to be addressed. Firstly, although this study briefly explored how S1P/S1PR3 regulates ABCA1 expression, the specific regulatory mechanisms need further studies. Secondly, changes in kidney morphology and function after systemic administration of HIF-2α inhibitors in mice may be due to the systemic effects of HIF-2α. Therefore, in vivo studies of podocyte-specific HIF-2α overexpression/knockdown mice are needed to elucidate the role of HIF-2α in DKD. Thirdly, we cannot exclude the possibility that other mechanisms are involved in improving lipid disorders in DKD by HIF-2a, and these potential mechanisms deserve further studies.

Conclusion

Our findings indicated that HIF-2α improves lipid metabolism disorder in DKD and alleviates podocyte damage by inhibiting the SPHK1/S1P/S1PR3 axis, thereby upregulating ABCA1 and promoting cholesterol efflux. FG-4592 protects against DKD to a certain extent by activating HIF-2α. HIF-2α may provide a new strategy for the treatment of DKD (Figure 9).
