Stretching vibration driven adiabatic transfer kinetics for photoexcited hole transfer from semiconductor to adsorbate
Authors: Min Zhou, Dingming Chen, Ying Liu, Haifeng Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterfacial hole transfer from a photoexcited semiconductor to surface adsorbates is pivotal for initiating solar-to-chemical energy conversion, yet the atomic-level transfer kinetics remains elusive. Using the methoxy/TiO2(110) system as an archetype, here we elucidate the hole transfer mechanism from hole-trapping lattice oxygen to the methoxy adsorbate at gas/solid and liquid/solid interfaces through molecular dynamics simulations and static minimum energy path calculations. Instead of direct nonadiabatic hopping, we uncover an adiabatic migration pathway adapted to local substrate relaxation, driven by a bond-stretching mechanism supported by stronger Ti-O stretching vibrations. Notably, this mechanism persists at the aqueous methoxy/TiO2(110) interface, albeit hindered by interfacial water and coadsorbates. Surprisingly, the hole transfer barriers across various photoexcited adsorbate/TiO2 interfaces correlate more closely with the vertical excitation energies of the adsorbates rather than their redox potentials, indicating an early-type transition-state nature. These insights deepen our understanding of elementary hole transfer kinetics in surface photochemistry.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Electron transfer, Reaction mechanisms

Introduction

The transfer of photoexcited electrons/holes across the interface between a semiconductor and surface adsorbates is prevalent in the field of surface science and heterogeneous photocatalysis and has been reported to be kinetically essential in several key photocatalytic steps1–5. This process usually triggers the generation of surface reactive radical species6–8, such as CH3O• and OH•, which have been demonstrated to be crucial for driving the subsequent carrier-consuming reactions in methanol and water photolysis9–11. Kinetically, the rate of interfacial electron/hole transfer would profoundly influence the overall photo-conversion efficiency12. In this regard, our first-principles microkinetic analyses on the photocatalytic oxygen evolution reaction over rutile TiO2(110) have quantitatively proved the beneficial effect of lowering the hole diffusion barrier11. Therefore, understanding the inherent mechanisms of interfacial electron/hole transfer is of fundamental importance in both theory and application.

Unfortunately, it is challenging to get the atom-resolved kinetic aspect on electron/hole transfer in experiment given the ultrafast nature of this process13–18 and the metastability of the radical intermediates2. Despite the sensitivity of transient spectroscopy, experimental observations have been limited to determining the timescales of hole transfer from TiO2 to the surface adsorbates (Dads−), like thiocyanate (SCN−) or alcohols (e.g., methanol, ethanol and isopropanol), ranging from femtosecond to nanosecond19–26, and this kinetic process was identified to be promoted by the environmental temperature1,2,15,16. Theoretically, such a fundamental task is equally difficult, and current typical theoretical studies of the kinetics of electron/hole transfer process (e.g., Dads−/TiO2(h+)→Dads•/TiO2) have largely relied on the framework of two-state Marcus theory or Fermi’s Golden rule23,24. This theory describes the transfer process in terms of three key parameters: the reorganization energy (λ), the electronic coupling matrix element (VAB) and the free energy of reaction (ΔG), and its application has led to considerable progress in estimating the kinetics of carrier transfer in bulk catalysts25,26. More generally, a range of theoretical studies focused on the thermodynamic properties of adsorbate/semiconductor interface through the energy level alignment, or the geometric/electronic nature of the adsorbate upon trapping the photo-hole and the localization degree of photoexcited states27,28. Taking methanol as an example, which as an important model molecule has attracted tremendous attention in surface photochemistry, photocatalytic selective conversion of carbon-based compounds, and enhanced water splitting29–32, it has been revealed that hole transfer thermodynamically tends to occur at the CH3O−/TiO2 interface33, and the CH3O− is the hole-trapping site rather than intact CH3OH molecule. Notably, with the time-domain ab initio nonadiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD) simulation, Chu et al. 17. revisited the photoexcited-hole transfer (HT) at the CH3OH/rutile-TiO2 interface and statistically pointed out that only when CH3OH was deprotonated to chemisorbed CH3O−, will ~15% of the free hole be trapped. Qualitatively, they also pointed out that three factors, i.e., the molecule/TiO2 energy level difference, thermal excitation of a phonon, and electron-phonon coupling, influence the interfacial hole transfer dynamics. Despite these significant advances, less is known about how the hole is explicitly transferred from the TiO2 surface to CH3O− at the atomic level, and the quantitative explanations for experimental observation of hole transfer (e.g., the ease and temperature effect) require further elucidation.

We emphasize that there is a lack of direct calculations of HT process across the adsorbate/semiconductor interface that can explicitly present the kinetic landscape and transfer mechanism. In a previous study, Selloni and coworkers34 performed calculations of the one-hole transfer process from a surface lattice oxygen on anatase TiO2(101) to surface-adsorbed OH−, in which they considered the possibility of hole hopping behavior when the lattice oxygen and OH− are close to each other (referred to as the bond-bending mechanism (BBM) in this study), and determined the barrier for this hole transfer mechanism. Nevertheless, it is important to explore whether the BBM is the sole transfer pathway and whether it remains valid for other systems, such as the CH3O−/TiO2 system or the other extended adsorbates (Dads−/TiO2). Meanwhile, it is crucial to determine the transfer mode, whether it tends to follow an adiabatic or non-adiabatic mechanism. These questions highlight the need for a comprehensive understanding of the transfer pathways and barriers at the atomic level, considering different adsorbates with specific characteristics. Furthermore, it is essential to identify if there exists a general indicator to predict the inaccessible hole transfer barrier in the photoexcited Dads−/TiO2 system. Such an indicator would provide insight into the fundamental processes underlying solar energy conversion on TiO2 surfaces.

In this work, we utilized the photoexcited CH3O−/rutile-TiO2(110) system as a proof of concept to address these issues by probing the transfer kinetics of localized hole from a photoexcited surface bridging Obr•− radical to an adjacent CH3O− adsorbate (CH3O− + Obr•− → CH3O• + Obr2−); this model system exhibits rich chemistry and can be cross-validated with the existing experimental results under vacuum conditions22,27,35,36. Our results, in the light of real-time AIMD monitoring and static MEP calculations for adiabatic or non-adiabatic hole transfer modes, unequivocally demonstrated that the hole is preferentially transferred via a new mechanism rather than the previously suggested direct hopping mode in BBM. This mechanism, referred to as the bond-stretching mechanism (BSM) thereafter, is driven by the Ti-O bond stretching vibration and assisted by the relaxation of catalyst substrate. Importantly, it remains appliable, albeit at a slower pace, even in the presence of interfacial water and coadsorbates. Extending the studies to the Dads−/TiO2 systems (D = CH3O, CH3, NH2 and OH), this preference could be rationalized by the stronger Ti-O bond stretching vibration in BSM, except for the OH− adsorbate where both BBM and BSM could be plausible owing to the presence of hydrogen bond. Furthermore, an unexpected linear relationship between the hole transfer barrier (Ea) and the vertical electronic excitation energy in Dads− was found, which can allow us to predict Ea inaccessible in both experiment and theory.

Results

AIMD simulation of hole transfer at the CH3O−/TiO2(h+) interface

First, we conducted an ab initio molecular dynamics simulation with a small step size of 0.2 fs at room temperature (300 K) to investigate the interfacial hole transfer from the photoexcited TiO2 to the CH3O− adsorbate (see method details in the Methods), where the photo-hole was initially located at the Obr site nearest to the CH3O− species (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 1). Noticeably, the first-principles calculation results revealed that the Obr site on TiO2(110) surface was the most favorable site for photo-hole trapping, consistent with experiments that the trapped holes are localized at or near the surface of particles10,14,21. This was further confirmed by the observation of a magnetic moment of 0.90 μB over a period of 2 ps (Supplementary Fig. 2a). As the simulation time was extended, we observed an oscillation behavior in the magnetism of the Obr atom, with its magnetic moment alternating between spin up (0.90 μB) and spin down (−0.80 μB). This oscillation behavior persisted even when the simulation step size increased to 1 fs and the simulation time extended to nearly 5 ps, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2b.

To accelerate the atomic motions and possibly enhance the photo-hole mobility at the CH3O−/TiO2 interface22, we increased the simulation temperature to 500 K. Figure 1 displays a statistical analysis of the electronic and geometric structures of the CH3O−/TiO2(h+) system in which the temperature was initially increased before reaching equilibrium (500 K) at ~0.35 ps (Supplementary Fig. 3). Figure 1b shows the magnetic moment changes of the Obr and CH3O species during the AIMD simulation with a small step size of 0.2 fs/step to ensure capturing the transient interfacial hole transfer event. From 0.35 ps to 0.60 ps, the magnetic moments of Obr and CH3O are ~1 μB and 0 μB, respectively, which indicates that the photo-hole is mainly located at the Obr site. Interestingly, at 0.63 ps, the magnetic moments of both Obr and CH3O change to ~0.50 μB. After ~0.65 ps, the magnetic moments of Obr and CH3O are inverted, implying the completion of hole transfer. The transient HT event had a lifetime of ~0.5 ps, and the representative electronic structure snapshots of the CH3O−/TiO2(h+) model captured at approximately 0.60 ps are shown in Fig. 1c. This interface transfer process is not an accidental event and is also captured in the simulation with a larger step size of 1 fs/step (Supplementary Fig. 4).

On observing the geometries from 0.54 to 0.65 ps, we found that upon completion of the hole transfer process, the Obr-Ti bond shortens to 1.861 Å and the OCH3O-Ti bond stretches to 2.267 Å, indicating that Obr/CH3O are passivated/activated and this whole process is accompanied by the substrate relaxation. This methoxy activation mode is consistent with the OCH3O-Ti bond stretching observed in the scanning tunneling microscope images with UV light irradiation on the methanol-adsorbed TiO2(110) surface35, and demonstrates that the hole transfer could occur with the participation of the substrate media, as depicted in the bond-stretching mechanism shown in Fig. 2. Noticeably, we also observed that the CH3O− species, being restricted by a single OCH3O-Ti bond and exhibiting a high degree of freedom, swing left and right to some extent during the AIMD simulation. This freedom of movement could lead to the direct hopping of the hole between Obr•− and the neighboring CH3O− via the bond-bending mechanism shown in Fig. 2, despite that the hole transfer via the bending mode has been not directly observed in the current AIMD simulation. Therefore, we have also carried out a comparative study of the possibility of BBM with BSM.

Next, we accordingly analyzed the amplitude changes for the representative distances in the BBM and BSM (d(Obr–OCH3O) and d(Obr–Ti)/d(OCH3O–Ti), respectively). Here, the distance change is defined as d = [dT − d0] / Δdmax, where dT is the length at the simulated temperature T during the AIMD simulation, d0 is the length at 0 K (3.376, 2.061, and 1.795 Å for d(Obr–OCH3O), d(Obr–Ti), and d(OCH3O–Ti), respectively), and Δdmax is the maximum difference in bond length at 0 K after hole transfer (1.098, 0.200, and 0.472 Å for Δd(Obr–OCH3O), Δd(Obr–Ti), and Δd(OCH3O–Ti), respectively; Fig. 2). As can be seen in Fig. 1d, d(Obr–OCH3O), which is the key structural parameter for the possible BBM route, changes slowly with values of less than 50%. By contrast, d(Obr–Ti) and d(OCH3O–Ti) fluctuate intensely in BSM; in particular, the Obr–Ti bond length change degree reaches a value of 100% at ~0.55 ps. In addition, the lengths of the Obr–Ti and OCH3O–Ti bonds in BSM oscillate more frequently than that of the Obr–OCH3O bond in BBM, indicating that BSM could be more likely to occur rather than BBM.

Further, we quantitatively calculated the eigenfrequencies of the stretching and bending vibrational modes, as well as the corresponding intensities, using the density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) calculations37. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the intensity of the bending vibration for the CH3O–/TiO2(h+) system is 0.008 at 186.7 cm−1; however, the stretching vibration has a higher intensity of 0.100 at 570.2 cm−1, consistent with the higher vibrational frequency and amplitude obtained for the stretching route shown in Fig. 1d. Overall, the stronger vibrational mode in the stretching route contributes to HT from the Obr•− site to the CH3O− species via BSM, which is also captured in the spin density plot in Fig. 1c at 0.63 ps. Therefore, we can make an initial judgement that the interfacial hole transfer from Obr•− to CH3O− may proceed mainly via the substrate in BSM, despite that BBM could be plausible and cannot be ruled out by the current finite-time AIMD simulations.

Notably, we considered the effect of aqueous TiO2(110) interface environment and coadsorbates on this interfacial hole transfer process in the context of practical application (see model in Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6 and calculation details in the Supplementary Information). In comparison to the gas/solid interface, the hole transfer is somewhat suppressed at the water/TiO2(110) interface. Specifically, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 7, no discernible hole transfer event was observed within the same timescale of ~1 ps (indicated by no significant changes in the OCH3O-Ti and Obr-Ti bond lengths), even with an extended simulation time of 2.5 ps under identical calculation settings (T = 500 K, 0.2 fs/step). Upon analyzing the trajectory of this AIMD simulation, we noted that, triggered by hydrogen bond interactions between CH3O– and adjacent H2O molecules, a proton hydrogen in the H2O molecule shuttles between CH3O− and H2O (CH3O− + H2O → CH3OH + OH−, Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 8). As a result, these events would retard hole transfer from nearby Obr•− to the CH3O− adsorbate to some extent, due to the relative hydrophobicity of CH3O• radical than its anion counterpart38.

To accelerate the molecular dynamics simulation for capturing the possible hole transfer event and identifying the transfer mechanism at the intricate water/TiO2(110) interface, including the presence of coadsorbates from H2O dissociation, a neural network potential (NNP) was trained for this TiO2-CH3O-H2O system within the active learning framework integrating with the popular neural network force field generator—DeePMD39 (see the detailed training information and validation results in the Supplementary Information, Supplementary Table 2–5, Supplementary Fig. 9–13). With a prolonged MD simulation accelerated by NNP, we successfully observed this interfacial hole transfer event (CH3O− + Obr•− → CH3O• + Obr2−) under the aqueous interface environment. As illustrated in Fig. 3b, the OCH3O-Ti bond length increases dramatically at around ~116 ps. It corresponds to the direct up-shift of CH3O from 1.761 Å to 2.480 Å (concurrent with the CH3O− → CH3O• transformation), while the Obr−OCH3O distance changes little (Fig. 3c). This indicates that the hole transfer process still preferentially follows the BSM pathway; in particular, when we expanded the dataset for training NNP (Supplementary Table 3-5), the hole transfer process from Obr•− → CH3O− can be observed more clearly, which explicitly shows the hole-sharing electronic state between Obr•− and CH3O− (see Supplementary Fig. 13 and more details in the Supplementary Information). In other words, the mechanism found at the gas/solid interface has effectively captured the key aspects influencing the interfacial hole transfer process. Notably, such a mechanism preference is also evidenced by the higher vibration intensity of the stretching vibrational mode of CH3O-Ti bond (0.550 at 462.6 cm−1) than that of the bending vibrational mode (0.138 at 179.4 cm−1) at the water/TiO2(110) interface. Overall, we can conclude that the presence of aqueous solution environment, including the hydrogen bond effect, water splitting event, and coadsorbed species, does not change the reaction mechanism but retards the efficiency of this hole transfer process.

Potential energy surfaces of hole transfer in BSM and BBM

We then calculated the energy barriers of hole transfer in the stretching and bending vibration mode, respectively (see method details in Methods). The potential energy surface (PES) for interfacial hole transfer in BSM was calculated using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) algorithm40. The route is defined as q(x) = xqA + (1−x)qB, where the x of each image in this BSM was obtained by linearly interpolating the geometries of all atoms in the substrate from IS (x = 0) to FS (x = 1) with their mass centers aligned. As the image climbs rigorously up the elastic band, a saddle point (i.e., the transition state, TS) appears. In this case, the electronic state of the system changes with the movement of the nucleus, implying an adiabatic process. The PES and corresponding magnetic moments in BSM are shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 14a, respectively. The TS is located at x = 0.46, indicating an early-type TS. The barrier for hole transfer in the BSM was calculated to be 0.44 eV, with CH3O and Obr carrying charges of −0.30 |e| and −0.86 |e | , respectively. Thus, the hole is shared by the Obr and CH3O species (see the spin charge densities in Fig. 4a).

To identify the dynamic characteristics of the BSM (adiabatic or diabatic process), we calculated the electronic coupling matrix element (VAB), which is given as the energy difference between the TSs in the adiabatic and diabatic states. When VAB << RT, the probability of hole transition is close to 0, and the process is diabatic; by contrast, when VAB >> RT, the probability of hole transition approaches 1, and the process is adiabatic24. For the simulated diabatic energy profile (Fig. 4b), a linear interpolation scheme was used in combination with a wavefunction-based quantum chemistry method41, using the electronic wave function from the previous step as an initial guess, which maintains the energy profile as close as possible to be diabatic, and we used the advanced HSE06 hybrid functional to guarantee the electron localization feature. Consequently, the diabatic energy barrier was calculated to be 0.61 eV. By comparison, for the adiabatic landscape, the VAB term was estimated to be 0.17 eV, which is higher than the RT threshold, indicating strong electronic coupling in the BSM route. Therefore, HT at the CH3O−/TiO2 interface in BSM should be adiabatic with a barrier of 0.44 eV. Such an adiabaticity in the BSM pathway essentially accords with the experimentally observed temperature-dependence that the hole transfer can be promoted by the ambient temperature1,2,15.

As the CH3O− adsorbate is only restricted by a single OCH3O–Ti bond, it has a degree of freedom that leads to the swinging of adsorbate as shown in the AIMD simulation. Consequently, the hole hopping may directly occur between Obr•− and neighboring CH3O− (BBM in Fig. 2). A similar mechanism has been proposed by Chen et al. for HT in the OH−/anatase-TiO2 system34. In this mechanism, when the calculated energy profiles along the two reaction directions (i.e., the PES is scanned from the IS or FS structure) are identical, HT is adiabatic; otherwise, it is diabatic.

For the CH3O−/TiO2(h+) system, the Obr atom and CH3O species should approach to each other firstly to realize hole transfer, and then return to their respective matrices. To obtain the entire PES landscape, we used the OCH3O–Obr distance as the reaction coordinate to monitor the hole transfer process. The diabatic energy profiles scanned with the hole transferred along IS → FS (Obr•− + CH3O− → Obr2− + CH3O•) or FS → IS (Obr2− + CH3O → Obr•− + CH3O−) were shown in Fig. 5a and c, respectively. Each point on the curve was obtained by linear interpolation of the OCH3O–Obr distance and optimization of the starting geometries using a constrained optimization minimization scheme combined with the wavefunction-based quantum chemistry method41 at the HSE06 level (see more details in Methods). The electronic state of the systems in each image was checked by the magnetic moment analysis in Fig. 5b and d, and the structures and spin charge densities of the representative images are illustrated in Fig. 5e.

At large OCH3O–Obr distance (from IS to I-01 state, Fig. 5a), the hole is mainly located at the Obr site. This observation was confirmed by the magnetic moment changes in Fig. 5b, as the magnetic moments of 1.00 and 0.00 μB for Obr and CH3O, respectively, are nearly constant. When the OCH3O–Obr distance approaches 2.400 Å (I-02), the energy increases gradually and the hole remains mainly located at the Obr site with a magnetic moment of ~0.90 μB. Intriguingly, when the OCH3O–Obr distance decreases to 2.272 Å and go through such a high-energy point (TS1), the energy abruptly declines and arrives at the I-03 state (Fig. 5a), and the dominant magnetic moment changes from the Obr site to the CH3O species, indicating the occurrence of hole transfer (along I-02 → TS1 → I-03, Fig. 5b). Notably, I-03 state does not completely fit in the diabatic PES from FS → IS, and when the OCH3O–Obr distance further decreases to 2.000 Å, it intersects with the PES (FS → IS) and reach the secondary high-energy point (TS2). Then, the OCH3O–Obr distance increases until Obr and CH3O return to their respective matrices (FS) once the hole transfer process is completed.

Conversely, when CH3O moves to approach Obr with the photo-hole initially located at the CH3O species (FS), i.e., following the PES (FS → IS, Fig. 5c), CH3O retains most of the photo-hole until entering in the transition-state region forming the CH3OObr intermediate, where the chemical bonding between CH3O• and Obr begins to appear with a CH3O–Obr bond distance around 1.750 Å. In this region, the hole is transferred from CH3O• to Obr2− and at the TS3 state there is a maximum hole transfer from CH3O• to Obr2− with a magnetic moment of 0.29 μB, and the magnetic moment of CH3O• decreases to 0.64 μB; the energy cost from FS to TS3 is 0.92 eV. When the CH3O–Obr bond distance further shortens at 1.700 Å, it reaches the transition state for forming CH3OObr− intermediate with an energy cost of 1.76 eV (Supplementary Fig. 15), where the magnetic moments of CH3O and Obr species are 0.58 and 0.15 μB, respectively. Alternatively, instead of the energy-consuming O–Obr bond formation, the photo-hole could be preferentially transferred obeying the PES of inverse IS → FS process from the CH3O adsorbate to the Obr site. This process could be realized through adjustment of the internal structure and achieved by increasing the CH3O–Obr distance until the IS structure (see Fig. 5c). This energy profile is inconsistent with that obtained for the IS → FS process. As the energy profiles along the two reaction coordinate directions are disparate, the HT in BBM is non-adiabatic based on the Marcus theory42. Comparably, Ea in BBM was estimated to be 0.54 eV, which is relatively higher than that in the BSM (0.44 eV).

Notably, comparing the transfer routes under adiabatic and diabatic conditions (see Fig. 5a, c), we found that the transfer behaviour is largely affected by the conformation of the CH3O adsorbate. Taking the I-03 and II-03 structures with the same OCH3O–Obr distance of 2.200 Å but different electronic states as an example, in comparison to the I-02 → I-03 process by surmounting TS1 state, which is energetically decaying and electronically transferring abruptly, the I-02 → II-03 process remains diabatic, where the charge is not obviously transferred and the energy continues to increase. Observing the structures (Fig. 5e), we found that when one of the hydrogen atoms (Ha) in CH3O is directionally close to Obr•−, the electronic state of CH3O favors the formation of the CH3O− anion (corresponding to II-03) rather than the CH3O• radical (I-03). This result could be rationalized by the specific orientation of the methyl group relative to Obr•− resulting from the tetrahedral structure of a sp3-hybridised carbon. In contrast to the I-03 configuration, the long-range electrostatic repulsion between the Haδ+ and the O-2p holes (shared in CH3O−/Obr•−) in II-03 configuration results in the relatively difficult hole transfer from Obr•− to CH3O−. Geometrically, to maintain the hole-sharing state via direction-specific p-p interaction, the CH3O species would only be adjusted by tilting and moving down to the Ti5c site to form a shorter OCH3O–Ti bond (1.939 vs. 2.129 Å in Fig. 5e), which would benefit CH3O− formation and hinder the hole transfer.

General indicator of hole transfer kinetics in extended Dads−/TiO2(h+) system

With the insight gained in the case of CH3O−/TiO2(110) system, we can conclude that BSM is the more favorable route for interfacial hole transfer. To uncover the general indicators affecting the hole transfer kinetics, we then studied the characteristics of hole transfer in three other acceptors (Dads−, i.e., NH2−, CH3−, and OH−), which are derived from the photooxidation process of NH3, CH4, and H2O, respectively. First, we calculated the vibrational frequencies of Dads− in the stretching and bending modes, as well as their corresponding intensities on TiO2(110) surface (Fig. 6a). For NH2− and CH3−, the intensity of the stretching vibrational mode is evidently stronger than that of the bending mode (0.121 vs. 0.009 and 0.071 vs. 0.003, respectively); however, for OH−, the intensities of both vibrational modes are close, and the intensity of bending mode is slightly stronger than the stretching one (0.031 vs. 0.028). Structural observations revealed the hydrogen-bond interactions between OH− and nearby Obr sites (Supplementary Fig. 16), which may contribute to the stronger bending mode intensity for OH−/TiO2(h+) system. Therefore, hole transfer via the BBM is statistically possible for the OH−/TiO2(h+) system, which was considered for the OH−/TiO2 (anatase) system in Chen’s study34. Generally, in most Dads−/TiO2(h+) systems including CH3O−, the interfacial hole transfer process statistically favors BSM (see details in Supplementary Figs. 17–19). Notably, we also calculated the hole transfer on CH3O−/anatase-TiO2(101) system, which shows the preference of BSM mechanism (see Supplementary Fig. 20 and the related discussions in the Supplementary Information).

Second, we calculated the hole transfer barriers in BSM, and the corresponding PESs are shown in Fig. 6b. The barriers for different Dads− show the trend NH2− <CH3− < CH3O− < OH− with values of 0.02, 0.18, 0.44, and 0.64 eV, respectively. Quantitatively, a Brønsted–Evans–Polanyi (BEP) relationship was examined between Ea and ΔG (the reaction energy change for Obr•− + Dads− → Obr2− + Dads•, essentially depending on the redox potential of Dads−/Dads•) with a relatively unsatisfactory fit of R2 = 0.87, with two points apparently out of fit and disordered (CH3− < NH2− < CH3O− < OH−, Fig. 6c).

As illustrated in Fig. 6d, when the electron in Dads− is excited directly from the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) of Dads− to the unoccupied energy level of Obr•−, the hole transfer process would accomplish (Obr•− + Dads− → Obr2− + Dads•). Therefore, the ease of hole transfer may be directly related to the vertical excitation energy (ΔGv), which is defined as the energy level difference between the HOMO of Dads− to the unoccupied orbitals of Obr•−. The smaller the ΔGv is, the lower the Ea of hole transfer process would be. Therefore, we calculated the local density of states (LDOSs) of four photoexcited Dads−/TiO2(h+) systems. As shown by Fig. 6e, the ΔGv of Dad−/TiO2(h+) obeys the trend: NH2− < CH3− < CH3O− < OH−, in line with the trend of the corresponding Ea. More interestingly, we found a strong linear relationship between Ea and ΔGv that the Ea for hole transfer at the Dads−/TiO2 interface increases with increasing ΔGv (R2 = 0.95, Fig. 6c). In other words, ΔGv is a better indicator for predicting Ea in Dads−/TiO2 systems. Noteworthily, upon incorporating the corresponding data point of CH3O−/anatase-TiO2(101) system into this linear relationship, we found negligible deviation (Supplementary Fig. 21) and this further supports the generality of ΔGv as a good indicator to predict Ea.

The parameter ΔGv, instead of ΔG, to predict Ea in the Dads−/TiO2 systems could be clarified for the following reasons. First, Fig. 6b shows that the reaction coordinates, x, of the TSs in Dads−/TiO2(h+) system are all less than 0.5, indicating that this process belongs to the early type, which is also evidenced by the slope of the BEP relationship being less than 0.50 (0.38 in Fig. 6c). This early type of reaction process is unfavorable for establishment of the relationship between the Ea and ΔG, leading to the relatively poor fit.

Second, according to the two-state Marcus theory24, the reaction barrier Ea is approximately given by Ea = (λ + ΔG)2/4λ, where λ is the reorganization energy, corresponding to the energy required to move an electron to the target energy level in the FS while maintaining the IS geometry, and numerically, λ is equal to ΔGv–ΔG (Fig. 6d). Therefore, Ea is equal to ΔGv/4(1-ΔG/ΔGv). Fitting the linear relationship between ΔG/ΔGv and ΔGv, we found that it gives a very good R2 of 0.98, and the slop is less than 1 (ΔG/ΔGv = 0.87ΔGv/eV – 2.29, Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating that the change of ΔGv is gentler than the ΔG. Therefore, using the mathematical approximation with the Taylor expansion, we can roughly simplify Ea as a linear function of ΔGv (Ea ≈ 0.5ΔGv – 0.67, R2 = 0.92) within a usual and limited range of [1.4, 3.0]/eV of ΔGv (Fig. 6c). Obviously, this result is close to the fitting in Fig. 6c (Ea = 0.53ΔGv – 0.78). In other words, we can also rely on the Marcus-theory framework to demonstrate why the kinetic ease of interfacial hole transfer can be well described by ΔGv, explaining the good linear relationship uncovered in Fig. 6c.

Taken together, a simplified model for interfacial hole transfer can be proposed based on the above findings. The vibrational mode intensity of surface adsorbates determines the probability of the corresponding interfacial hole transfer behavior. Specifically, the transfer route with a stronger vibrational intensity will be more favorable than the route with a weaker vibrational intensity. In addition, for the hole-activated Dads−/TiO2 systems, the donor state corresponds to the Obr, and the acceptor state corresponds to Dads−. The ability of an adsorbate to capture and retain a trapped hole can be expressed by the energy level difference between the HOMO of Dads− and the unoccupied orbitals of Obr•− (Fig. 6d). The photo-hole is transferred from a high energy level of Obr•− to the HOMO of Dads− by a band-to-band excitation event. The lower the energy level of Dads−, the more difficult the transfer of holes to the adsorbate.

Discussion

In summary, this study revisited the kinetics of one-hole transfer from TiO2 to CH3O− in detail by combining the real-time AIMD simulations and static minimum energy path calculations. The preferential interfacial hole transfer route via the newly found bond-stretching mechanism (BSM) was ascribed to the stronger intensity of the corresponding stretching vibrational mode of the CH3O− on the surface. Hole trapped at the surface Obr site prefers transferring adiabatically to the CH3O− via the BSM with a lower barrier of 0.44 eV than the bond-bending mechanism (BBM). With prolonged MD simulation driven by the neural network potential, we demonstrated that BSM is also favored at the aqueous TiO2(110) interface, albeit retarded by the interfacial water and coadsorbates. Notably, the general interfacial hole transfer behaviour in Dads−/TiO2(h+) systems with distinct Dads− species (i.e., NH2−, CH3−, CH3O−, and OH−) revealed that the ease of interfacial hole transfer barrier directly related to the vertical excitation energy of electron from the highest occupied molecular orbital of Dads− to the unoccupied orbital of Obr•−. In a broader context, these findings derived from our investigation on the rutile TiO2(110) system can be extrapolated to other TiO2 surfaces, including the anatase (101) surface (refer to the Supporting Information for more details). This quantitative study could bridge the gap between hole transfer from catalyst surfaces and surface-adsorbed radical species in photocatalysis.

In addition, it is worth discussing that we have also performed the non-adiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD) simulation to further understand the hole transfer process from Obr•– to CH3O– on TiO2(110) surface. We note that this process (CH3O– + Obr•– → CH3O• + Obr2–) corresponds to a transfer of localized hole from Obr•‒ polaron to CH3O‒ (Supplementary Fig. 22), which is different from the energy level compatible or allowed free/hot hole transfer previously discussed with NAMD simulation17,43. There is a large thermodynamically forbidden energy gap from Obr•‒ to CH3O‒, and the time-dependent Kohn–Sham energy levels calculated at 300 K and 500 K confirm that there are no obvious energy level crossings (Supplementary Fig. 23), thus inhibiting the occurrence of direct hole hopping from Obr•− to CH3O−. At 300 K, the NAMD results show that there is almost no hole transfer from Obr•− to CH3O− (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Even at a higher temperature of 500 K, only ~3% of the holes migrate from Obr•− to TiO2-VBM and CH3O− due to the slight energy level crossing (Supplementary Fig. 23b). The decoupled adiabatic (AD) and nonadiabatic (NA) contributions indicate that the overall hole transfer from Obr•− to CH3O− is via the AD pathway, and there are some probabilities for backward hole transfer from CH3O− (HOMO) to TiO2-VBM via the NA pathway. Overall, these results are largely different from the cases of interfacial transfer of free hole or hot hole (Supplementary Fig. 24d). This can be understood from the fact that the HOMO energy level of CH3O‒ is far below that of Obr•‒; only when the atomic nucleuses of the CH3O‒ relax into the configuration of CH3O• radical, can the photo-hole be thermodynamically favorably transferred. This corresponds to the PES crossover/transition from IS (Obr•‒ + CH3O‒) to FS (Obr2‒ + CH3O•) via an electron-nucleus correlated adiabatic process (see IS → FS in Supplementary Fig. 22). More relevant details can be found in the Supplementary Information.

Methods

DFT calculations

All the spin-polarized calculations were performed within the VASP code44,45 using the plane-wave basis sets with an energy cutoff of 450 eV. The project-augmented wave method was used to describe the core-valence electron interaction46. Rutile TiO2(110) surface was modeled with a four-layer p(3 × 2) slab for potential energy surface calculations (Supplementary Fig. 1). The vacuum between slabs was 15 Å, and a 2 × 1 × 1 k-point mesh was used47. All atoms were fully relaxed until the Hellman-Feynman forces on each ion were less than 0.05 eV/Å. The van der Waals interactions were considered using the DFT-D3 method48. To address the tendency of photoexcited holes to delocalize with the pure Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional due to the self-interaction error, the HSE06 hybrid functional and DFT + U method including the on-site Coulomb correction were employed49. To balance the accuracy and efficiency, the DFT + U method was used for structure optimization and the HSE06 functional was for energy and electronic structure calculations. U values of 4.2 and 6.3 eV were used for Ti-3d and O-2p orbitals, respectively, as suggested in the previous studies10,11. This strategy and setting has been verified and successfully used to describe the photoexcited TiO2 systems9–11,50,51. The electronic structure analysis further confirmed the localization of photoexcited holes. To model the hole transfer process between Obr•− and adsorbate Dads− (Dads− + Obr•− → Dads• + Obr2−), we note that the entire system maintains neutrality without removing an electron; specifically, the electron held by Dads− can be supplied by Obr2− (it changes into Obr•−) in the initial state (IS) and then returns to Obr•− to form the final state (FS) via controlled charge redistribution9–11.

Vibrational intensity analysis

Vibrational analyses on Dads− species were performed using the density functional perturbation theory (DFPT) implemented in the VASP 5 version and above, and vibration intensities were extracted from the Born-effective charges (BEC) through linear response calculations with the VASP-infrared-intensity script52. AIMD simulation

In the real-time AIMD simulations to explore the dynamic evolution of photo-hole at the CH3O−/TiO2 interface, the canonical ensemble (NVT) with the Nosé-Hoover thermostat was used53, and a small step size of 0.2 fs was adopted at the initial stage to ensure capturing the transient interfacial carrier transfer event. Notably, a stepwise pre-equilibrium strategy was used to minimize the unphysical influence on hole transfer before the entire system reached equilibrium (see details in Supplementary Information). To accelerate the atomic motions and enhance the hole mobility22, an elevated temperature 500 K and an increased time step size of 1 fs were tested and used; the calculated vibrational spectra confirmed the reasonableness of the used step size. More details can be seen in the Supplementary Information. Also, we investigated the effect of the water/TiO2(110) interface and coadsorbates on the hole transfer in the context of practical application. To this end, a specified number of water molecules were explicitly introduced in the photoexcited CH3O–/TiO2 system at ρ = 1 g/cm3 to simulate the solid-liquid interface environment (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6). Notably, the initial configuration of water solution underwent pre-relaxation until quasi-equilibrium at 500 K to accommodate the fixed surface reaction region, minimizing the unphysical influence before reaching quasi-equilibrium on hole transfer.

Machine learning accelerated MD simulations

To accelerate the MD simulation for monitoring the hole transfer at the aqueous interface for prolonged time, a neural network potential (NNP) for this TiO2-CH3O-H2O system was trained. This NNP was trained with the active learning framework integrating with the popular neural network force field generator—DeePMD39. The training data was iteratively generated via trajectories of AIMD and structure optimization at the DFT level and contains 232, 529 structures, demonstrating its diversity and robustness (Supplementary Table 2). The se_e2_a descriptor is implemented aiming to discern the local chemical environment of every atom (see detailed settings in the Supplementary Information). As Supplementary Fig. 9 shows, the training accuracy of NNP is sufficient and close to the DFT method. The mean absolute error (MAE) and root-mean-square error (RMSE) for the energy prediction are 1.4 and 0.75 meV/atom, respectively, and RMSE and MAE for the force prediction are 0.085 and 0.058 eV/Å, respectively. The NNP-driven MD simulation, also known as deep potential molecular dynamics (DPMD), was performed with the LAMMPS package54,55. Notably, to demonstrate the generalization ability of our NNP, ~100 trajectories were selected from the DPMD simulation as the validation set. The performance of NNP in the validation set is compatible with that in the training set (Supplementary Fig. 9c). We note that we have also trained an alternative NNP based on a larger data set (containing 67,8175 and 20,9621 structures for the training set and validation set, respectively), which further increases the quality of NNP is describing the hole transfer; the details are given in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Table 3–5, Supplementary Fig. 10–13).

Construction of potential energy surface

With the hole localized on IS and FS that exhibit lattice distortions, we used a linear interpolation scheme to define a hole polaron transfer pathway. The starting geometry of reaction route was obtained by interpolating the geometries of the IS and FS with their mass centers aligned, which is expressed as q(x) = xqA + (1 − x)qB, where q is the coordinate of all atoms in the CH3O−/TiO2(h+) system, qA/qB represents the equilibrium structure of IS/FS, and x is the reaction coordinate, varying between 0 and 1. This approach has been demonstrated to give a good representation of the polaron transfer coordinates in earlier works25,34,56. For the bond-stretching mechanism, x of each image was obtained with linear interpolation of all atoms in the substrate from IS to FS. The adiabatic potential energy surface (PES) was calculated using the climbing image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) algorithm40. In this case, the electronic state changes with the movement of the nucleus, implying an adiabatic process. In addition, we used the wavefunction-based quantum chemistry method proposed previously41 to simulate the diabatic energy profile, where each diabatic point was obtained using the electronic wavefunction from the previous step as the initial guess, maintaining the energy profile as closely diabatic as possible. For the bond-bending mechanism, x of each reaction coordinate was obtained by linear insertion of the OCH3O–Obr distance. The starting geometries were generated using a constrained optimization minimization scheme57,58, ensuring the total energy is minimized at the specific reaction coordinate x and avoiding the artificial construction resulting from irrationality in other dimensions. Subsequently, each optimized structures were further treated using the wavefunction-based quantum chemistry method41. By varying the reaction coordinate for IS → FS or FS → IS, the adiabatic and diabatic PESs can be structured. We note that the coordinates of the key structures involved can be found in the Supplementary Data 1.

NAMD simulation

The ab initio non-adiabatic molecular dynamics (NAMD) calculations were carried out using the Hefei-NAMD package interfaced with the Vienna ab initio simulation package59. The time evolution of the excited hole state was implemented within time-dependent DFT (TD-DFT) in the Kohm–Sham (KS) framework, using the fewest-switches surface hopping (FSSH) scheme based on the classical-path approximation (CPA). This method is computationally tractable and has been widely utilized in different systems for the charge transfer and delocalization dynamics60. Specifically, the electronic structure is firstly calculated using the optimized substrate structure at 0 K. After the geometry optimization, we used velocity rescaling to bring the temperature of the system to either 300 K or 500 K, during which a 5 ps microcanonical ab initio molecular dynamics trajectory is then generated with a time step of 1 fs. Finally, in the NAMD calculations, statistical averages were obtained using 25 different initial configurations and 1000 surface hopping trajectories.
