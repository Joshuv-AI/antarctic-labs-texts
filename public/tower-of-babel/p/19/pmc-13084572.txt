Building data capacity for maternal health equity: Introduction of a novel dataset
Authors: Brayton Amidon, Isaac Washburn, Karina Shreffler, Leremy A Colf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article describes a new, publicly available data resource linking numerous existing datasets and analyses at the county/FIPS code level. Linked datasets include publicly available data on county demographic makeup (e.g., race, population, etc.), social vulnerability factors, and pregnancy-related hospital utilization. It also includes a new dataset, measuring travel time from each county to the nearest hospital for each of four levels of maternal care services. A wide variety of datasets, including population-level health data, are published by the United States Government for public use. While these data sets are immensely valuable for research and can be used to improve healthcare delivery and outcomes, it can be challenging to find, access, and link the data. Maternal health, population health, and demography researchers as well as healthcare providers and health policy experts, can use this dataset to better understand the causes of maternal morbidity and mortality and to improve health outcomes. By linking these varied data sources, this new data resource allows examination of individual contributing factors as well as the interplay between and relative strengths of multiple correlated factors. Additionally, this dataset allows researchers to add any additional data, simply by using FIPS codes for linking.

Background

Maternal morbidity and mortality (MMM) is a critical public health issue. The maternal mortality rate in the U.S. has nearly tripled over the past three decades and is the highest among other high-income countries [1]. MMM rates and disparities have been caused by or correlated with a number of risk factors, including poverty level, social vulnerability, race/ethnicity, and geographic distance to care [[2], [3], [4]]. Yet vulnerable and underserved populations are often geographically co-located, and individual patients are often members of more than one group [1]. Due to the co-occurring and/or co-located risk factors, it is challenging to identify which factors are most strongly correlated/penetrant. Interventions targeting a co-occurring risk will likely not significantly impact causal issues [5]. To identify and understand the causal factors in the high MMM rates, it is necessary to analyze risk factors together rather than independently. However, this type of analysis requires comprehensive, nationwide, linked datasets that include all potential risk factor variables in MMM. Due to the disparate nature of health data in the U.S., this has not previously been possible. National data that incorporate maternal health, sociodemographic, and geographic variables are essential to examine multiple predictors of maternal health outcomes [6].

Data Description

This dataset contains linked data from three sources, the United States Census Bureau, Department of Health and Human Services (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project), and Centers for Disease Control and Prevention/Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry’s Social Vulnerability Index, plus a new measure created with the ArcGIS Pro software utilizing data from the Census Gazetteer and the American Hospital Association’s Annual Survey of Hospitals. The complete linked data resource described here contains 598 variables and 2395 observations. A high-level overview of these variables, including the related page numbers in the codebook, can be found in Section 3.2

Structure of dataset

The Maternal Health Equity Linked Datasets is housed in the DesignSafe-CI repository [7]. Within the folder uploaded to DesignSafe-CI, there are nine files. The dataset is available in .csv, .sav, .dta, .rdata, and sas7bdat formats. In addition to the dataset, a codebook, table of variables, and two example syntax files are included in the deposited dataset.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data description and preparation

We created an aggregate, national level geographic dataset of maternal health hospital utilization, demographics, social vulnerability, and travel time to hospitals with various levels of maternal care services, at the county level. We leveraged diverse, publicly available data to create a comprehensive data source identifying the county-level geographic distribution of pregnancy-related hospital visits across the nation, matched with social and demographic data on the populations in each of those counties. In addition, we created and linked a new dataset measuring the driving time from each county to the nearest hospital(s) providing basic care, obstetric services, and levels of advanced obstetric services. Until this point, such a dataset was not available in the United States. The release of this dataset expands the scope and intersectionality of multidisciplinary research into maternal research for the general research community.

The dataset integrates sources that span multiple release years. Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) hospital discharge data are aggregated across 2017–2019 (aggregating years minimizes data suppression). The CDC/ATSDR Social Vulnerability Index data corresponds to 2018, and U.S. Census county level and population data were drawn from 2018 to 2020 releases. We harmonized these data by anchoring the dataset to the HCUP 2017–2019 reporting period and selecting the temporally closest version available for each of the other datasets.

Healthcare utilization data, sourced from HCUP, were aggregated across the 3-year period of 2017–2019 to limit data suppression due to small numbers in counties with lower occurrences. Federal privacy protection guidance generally results in suppression of patient counts below 10, even in deidentified or limited data sets to prevent reidentification. This suppression, while valuable and appropriate, could negatively impact the value, integrity, and comparability of a nationwide data source. More information on the handling of data suppression can be found in the combined codebook, housed in the DesignSafe-CI repository [7]. To limit bias and suppression, data were aggregated across the 3-year period. In order to ensure data integrity, all additional datasets were matched to this 2017–2019 time period. 2017–2019 was specifically chosen to avoid complications due to COVID-19 [11].

All data were collected at the county level utilizing a Federal Information Processing System (FIPS) code. These codes serve as unique numeric identifiers for geographic areas at the state and county/county-equivalent level. For most states, FIPS codes identify counites. The following states or districts utilize county-equivalents: Alaska (boroughs and Census Areas), Louisiana (parishes), Nevada (counties and an independent city), Maryland (counties and an independent city), and Washington, D.C. For a five-digit FIPS code, the first two digits identify the state, and the remaining three digits identify the county/county-equivalent [12]. FIPS codes were used as the primary key to link and combine all datasets.

Sources of data

Census

County-level demographic data were downloaded from the United States Census Bureau website [13]. Race and ethnicity variables consist of population counts sourced from Decennial Census (DEC) Redistricting data. An advanced search for race and ethnicity was conducted with the following filters: Race and Ethnicity, geographies (Counites, All Counties in the United States and Puerto Rico, 2020 DEC Redistricting Data (PL 95–171)). Age variables represent population count estimates, and sex variables represent percentage estimates, both sourced from the 2018 American Community Survey (ACS). An advanced search for age and sex was conducted with the following filters: Age and Sex, Geographies (Counties, All Counties in the United States and Puerto Rico), 2018 American Community Survey 1 Year Estimates Subject Tables.

Social vulnerability index

Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) data and documentation were downloaded from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and Agency for Toxic Substance and Disease Registry’s website [14]. The downloaded dataset included FIPS codes, overall SVI percentile ranking, percentile rankings for each of the four SVI themes, and percentile rankings for all underlying variables [10]. The following filters were applied prior to downloading the data: Year: 2018; Geography: United States; Geography Type: Counties; File Type: CSV File.

Healthcare cost and utilization project

Inpatient hospital discharge data for pregnancy related visits were downloaded from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project website [15]. Data were downloaded from the HCUPNet Community Inpatient Statistics. The following selections were made: Diagnoses & Conditions: Diagnoses – Clinical Classification Software Refined (CCSR), 2016+; State: All available states (downloaded one at a time to obtain county-level data rather than nationwide data); Time Period: 3-year; Years: 2017–2019; Principal Diagnoses: All available PRGs; Measures: Number of Discharges. Once these selections were made, data were downloaded for all available states and PRG codes.

Geographic measures

The geographic centroid of each county for 2019 was obtained from the National Counties Gazetteer File found on the United States Census Bureau website [16]. County centroids were mapped by latitude and longitude within the ArcGIS software. Then, individual layers were created, and locations were mapped for OB Level 0, OB Level 1, OB Level 2, and OB Level 3 hospitals. OB service levels were obtained from the American Hospital Association’s (AHA) 2019 Annual Survey of Hospitals. AHA data also included gelocations for each hospital. County geographic centroids were used to calculate the driving time to the nearest hospital with each obstetric service level using the Origin-Destination Cost Matrix function of the ArcGIS Pro software. The travel time in minutes from the geographic centroid of each county was calculated to the nearest hospital that provides obstetric care for serious illnesses and abnormalities (OB Level 3; time3), uncomplicated and most complicated cases (OB Level 2; time 2), uncomplicated maternal and newborn cases (OB Level 1; time1), and the nearest hospital that provides no maternal care services (time0) [8,17]. American Hospital Association data are available for-purchase but not redistribution. Therefore, while a novel measure of travel time to hospitals with varying levels of maternal care services was included in the dataset, the underlying proprietary data are not included in the dataset. In some cases, driving from the centroid was not possible (e.g., the centroid was on an island with no roads or in an inaccessible area). In these cases, a negative travel time (−1) was recorded in the dataset. This negative travel time represents that the route was not available rather than a true negative value.

The shortest travel time was recorded for each FIPS code, for each level of obstetric care services. It was assumed that facilities providing higher levels of OB services were also capable of providing lower-level services (e.g., if the closest facility was OB Level 3, that travel time was also reported for Level 1, and Level 2). However, if a Level 1 facility was closer, the shorter travel time was reported for Level 0 and Level 1, etc..

Limitations

There are several limitations to note regarding this new dataset. Although a three-year timeframe was selected for HCUP data (2017–2019), values were suppressed when fewer than 11 cases were observed within the date range. Data include a three-year period (2017–2019); however, not all sources of data were available for this date range. As the goal of this project was to provide a publicly available dataset, only states and PRG codes that were publicly available on HCUPNet were included in this dataset. This could limit the dataset’s ability to represent a comprehensive national perspective. Nonetheless, 37 states were included, making this the only dataset that combined these data elements at this scale. Additionally, we assume that the overall demographic makeup of a county reflects the likelihood of expectant mothers within that county, but actual numbers may differ by county and demographic group. Finally, these data look at utilization, not outcomes, which limits the ability to make inferences related to maternal and infant health status or quality of care.

Ethics Statement

This project involves the linking of previously de-identified public datasets. The authors have read and followed the ethical requirements for publication in Data in Brief. Additionally, this work was reviewed by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences and determined to be exempt as it did not meet the criteria for human subjects research (IRB reference number: 765,009; July 31, 2024).
