Postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion for pain management in pelvic bone tumor surgery patients: a randomized, double-blind, controlled trial
Authors: Fei Huo, Ran Zhang, Yaqing Wu, Xue Tian, Yi Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have demonstrated that perioperative use of lidocaine effectively reduces opioid requirements in various surgical procedures. Our objective was to evaluate the effect of postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion on opioid consumption following pelvic bone tumor surgery.MethodsThis single center randomized controlled trial in a tertiary teaching hospital containing a total of 70 patients, aged 18 to 65 years, with American Society of Anesthesiologists(ASA) physical status classifications of I to III, who were scheduled to undergo pelvic bone tumor surgery. Participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the lidocaine group(L Group) or the control group(C Group). The L Group received lidocaine for postoperative analgesia, whereas the C Group was administered a placebo saline infusion.The primary outcome was postoperative opioid consumption. Secondary outcomes included the time to first bowel movement, the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting(PONV), and pain scores at rest and during movement on postoperative days.ResultsA significant difference was observed in the cumulative 72-h morphine equivalent consumption between the L Group (n = 33) and the C Group (n = 33). Patients in the L Group required significantly less morphine equivalent consumption compared to the C Group (13 (0-52) mg vs. 25 (14-85) mg, P = 0.043). Additionally, the proportion of patients experiencing breakthrough pain was significantly lower in the L Group compared to the C Group (33.33% vs. 75.76%, P = 0.001).No significant differences were noted between the groups in secondary outcomes, including the incidence of PONV, numerical pain scores at rest or during movement, time to first bowel evacuation, or time to first oral intake.ConclusionPostoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion is effective in reducing opioid consumption and the incidence of breakthrough pain following pelvic bone tumor surgery.Trial registrationThis single-center, prospective RCT was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100051207, 15/09/2021). The first research participant was enrolled in September 20, 2021.

Introduction

Pelvic bone tumor surgery is a highly invasive procedure that often leads to significant severe pain and stress responses [1]. In addition to the common occurrence of neuropathic pain, previous studies have shown a high rate of persistent somatic pain [2]. Depending on the tumor’s location, disease severity, and patient preferences, individuals with sacral and pelvic tumors may benefit from treatments such as ultrasound-guided joint corticosteroid injections, dorsal column stimulator leads, and/or implantable neuraxial drug delivery devices [3]. Although various methods are available for managing postoperative pain, intravenous analgesia remains the most commonly used approach [4].

Multimodal analgesia, as the most commonly employed method for intraoperative pain management, has achieved notable outcomes in various surgical procedures. However, since opioids remain the primary analgesic in multimodal analgesia, surgeries involving significant trauma are still associated with adverse effects due to the substantial opioid dosages required [5–7]. Therefore, reducing opioid consumption in pain management has become a major focus of research in recent years.

Intravenous lidocaine has shown distinct advantages in managing postoperative pain following abdominal surgeries. In major abdominal surgeries, it has been found to be noninferior to thoracic epidural analgesia for controlling acute postoperative pain within the first 24 h postoperatively [8–12]. However, its effectiveness in pelvic surgeries remains insufficiently studied.This study aims to investigate whether postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion can reduce opioid consumption and the occurrence of related adverse events in patients undergoing pelvic bone tumor surgery.

Methods

Study design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University People's Hospital (Approval No. 2020PHB318; Chairperson: Professor Shuang Mu) on 10 December 2020 and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2100051207). All participants, or their legal guardians, provided informed consent, and the study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting of this study complies with the most recent version of the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT).

Participants

The study included patients aged 18 to 65 years who were scheduled to undergo pelvic bone tumor surgery under general anesthesia. Exclusion criteria were as follows: pregnancy; emergency surgery; cardiac conduction block (atrioventricular block, sinus node block, or intraventricular conduction block); sinus bradycardia (resting heart rate < 50 bpm); preoperative hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg), a history of otolithiasis, Ménière’s disease; cerebrovascular disease; contraindications to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs(NSAIDs); or known allergies to anesthetic agents.

Study protocol

Participants were randomly allocated to the L group and the C group. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random sequence, and each participant was assigned a unique patient number. Allocation concealment was ensured by placing the allocation code in a sealed, opaque envelope, managed by a study assistant who was not involved in anesthesia administration or follow-up. Data collection was meticulously handled by a blinded investigator who was not involved in patient care to ensure unbiased and accurate recording.

Upon arrival in the operating room, venous catheter placement and vital sign monitoring were performed. Anesthesia induction included the administration of propofol (2 mg kg−1), rocuronium (0.6 mg kg−1), and sufentanil (0.3 μg kg−1). Endotracheal intubation was performed using 7.5 mm tracheal tubes for males and 7.0 mm tubes for females. Both groups received total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA). Dexmedetomidine was infused at 0.4 μg kg−1h−1, and propofol was titrated at 3–4 mg kg−1h−1 to maintain the bispectral index (BIS) within a target range of 40–60. Remifentanil infusion started at 0.1–0.2 μg kg−1min−1. Sufentanil (0.1 μg kg⁻1) was administered as needed by the attending anesthesiologist. Vasopressors were used to maintain the mean arterial pressure (MAP) within 80–120% of baseline, while atropine was administered to manage bradycardia (heart rate < 45 beats/min). The patients were administered a 5 mg intravenous dose of tropisetron hydrochloride prior to the end of the surgical procedure.

In L Group, postoperative analgesia included a patient-controlled intravenous analgesia pump (hydromorphone 0.2 mg with a 10-min lockout interval, maximum 1.2 mgh⁻1) for postoperative 72 h combined with a separate intravenous infusion pump delivering lidocaine at 1 mg·kg⁻1h⁻1for postoperative 48 h. The lidocaine infusion was initiated following a bolus dose of lidocaine (2 mg kg⁻1) administered at the end of surgery. C Group received only the hydromorphone pump and a 0.9% normal saline pump as placebo.

Extubation was performed based on the following criteria: spontaneous breathing, tidal volume ≥ 5 mLkg⁻1, respiratory rate < 20 breaths/min, oxygen saturation ≥ 95%, cooperative patient status, and body temperature of 36°C. Muscle relaxant antagonists (atropine 0.5 mg + neostigmine 1 mg) were administered prior to extubation. Patients were transferred to the general ward when their Aldrete score exceeded 9.

Pain intensity was assessed using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) at rest and during movement at 24, 48, and 72 h after surgery.

The breakthrough pain was defined as a patient reporting an NRS > 4 at least once at any assessed time point within the first 72 h. Rescue analgesia was administered when the NRS score was ≥ 4 at rest or upon patient request. Rescue analgesia was treated with intravenous fentanyl (0.01–0.05 mg) or morphine (1–5 mg). Cumulative opioid consumption was recorded in morphine equivalents (0.01 mg fentanyl = 1 mg morphine, and 1.5 mg hydromorphone = 10 mg morphine) [13].

Postoperative ambulation was not scheduled at a fixed time point due to the wide surgical extent of pelvic bone tumor procedures and the considerable variability in individual recovery. Instead, ambulation was initiated based on a comprehensive clinical assessment that considered hemodynamic stability, adequate pain control, and the surgeon’s evaluation of wound and limb stability. Patients were encouraged to ambulate when these conditions were met, and the progression of activity was individualized according to tolerance.

Outcome measures

Perioperative side effects, including PONV and dizziness, were closely monitored to assess patient tolerance and recovery profiles. PONV were assessed using a validated 4-point scale as follows: 0 = no nausea and no vomiting; 1 = nausea present without vomiting; 2 = nausea present with a single episode of vomiting; 3 = nausea present with two or more episodes of vomiting or continuous retching. Rescue antiemetic therapy (ondansetron 4 mg IV intravenously, administered at 4-h intervals) was provided to patients with a score of ≥ 2 on the nausea and vomiting scale, indicating clinically significant nausea or vomiting.

In addition to these adverse effects, other key postoperative outcomes were systematically documented, such as the time to first bowel evacuation, an indicator of gastrointestinal recovery, and the time to first oral intake, which reflects the resumption of normal physiological functions. Detailed records of intraoperative drug use, including propofol, sufentanil, and remifentanil doses, were maintained to evaluate their potential impact on postoperative outcomes and analgesic requirements.

The primary outcome of interest was cumulative opioid consumption during the postoperative 72 h, a critical measure of analgesic efficacy and overall pain management. Secondary outcomes included NRS pain scores at rest and during movement, providing insight into pain dynamics over time. Additionally, the incidence of breakthrough pain, a benchmark for evaluating pain control strategies, was recorded alongside the frequency of PONV and dizziness, which are common opioid-related side effects.

Recovery metrics, such as the time to first bowel movement and time to first oral intake, were also included to offer a comprehensive overview of postoperative recovery trajectories.

Statistical analysis

A preliminary experiment was conducted to inform the design of the current study. During this phase, 7 patients received conventional anesthesia with standard analgesia, while 5 patients received conventional anesthesia followed by postoperative intravenous lidocaine analgesia. The preliminary results revealed that the 72-h cumulative morphine equivalent consumption (mg) was 145.0 ± 47.9 in the C Group, compared to 109.8 ± 45.3 in the L group. These findings demonstrated a reduction in opioid consumption associated with lidocaine administration. Based on these preliminary data, with a significance level of 5% and a statistical power of 80%, allowing for a 20% dropout rate, it was calculated that each group would require a minimum of 33 participants to detect a meaningful difference. We planned to recruit 35 patients in each group.

The statistical analysis for the study was designed to accommodate the nature of the data and ensure robust conclusions. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test to determine normality. For variables with a normal distribution, comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-test, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For variables not following a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied, with results presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on the distribution and expected frequencies. A two-sided P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 21.0 (IBM, New York, USA), ensuring adherence to rigorous statistical standards and reproducibility of results. Opioid consumption was converted into intravenous morphine-equivalent doses using standard equianalgesic conversion factors, and comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test due to non-normal data distribution.

Cumulative morphine equivalent consumption and NRS Scores

There was a significant difference in cumulative morphine equivalent 72-h consumption between the L Group (n = 33) and the C Group (n = 33) (13 (0-52) mg vs. 25(14-85) mg, P = 0.043) (Table 3). The proportion of patients who experienced breakthrough pain was markedly lower in the L Group, compared with the C group (33.33% vs. 75.76%, P = 0.001). Specifically, 11 of 33 patients in the L group (33.33%) had at least one episode of breakthrough pain, whereas 25 of 33 patients in the C group (75.76%) experienced such episodes (Table 3). The L Group and C Group showed a comparable number of patients requiring rescue analgesia, resting and moving NRS scores on postoperative day (POD) 1, 2, and 3 (Table 3). Discussion

Our randomized controlled trial demonstrated that postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion significantly reduces the need for postoperative analgesics and the incidence of breakthrough pain in patients undergoing pelvic bone tumor surgery. This finding is consistent with other studies that reported a reduction in postoperative morphine consumption and pain scale following systemic lidocaine use in patients undergoing surgeries [14–16]. No increase in the incidence of postoperative adverse events was noted in the L Group compared to the C Group.

The analgesic effects of intravenous lidocaine have been extensively documented, with studies showing its intraoperative administration significantly reduces postoperative opioid consumption [17]. Consistent with these findings, our study demonstrated a notable reduction in cumulative 72-h morphine-equivalent consumption in L Group compared to C group. The molecular mechanisms underlying intravenous lidocaine’s analgesic effects have been widely explored. Basic research indicates that lidocaine influences numerous molecular targets involved in acute and chronic nociception. Lidocaine’s mechanisms align well with this mixed pathophysiology. At the peripheral level, systemic lidocaine blocks voltage-gated sodium channels (VGSCs), suppressing ectopic discharges from injured or compressed sensory fibers that underlie neuropathic pain and spontaneous firing. Centrally, lidocaine attenuates hyperexcitability and central sensitization through modulation of VGSCs and other targets (including NMDA-mediated pathways), and it has anti-inflammatory and antihyperalgesic effects that reduce peripheral nociceptive input and spinal sensitization. Collectively, these actions plausibly explain why perioperative intravenous lidocaine can reduce both neuropathic-type and somatic pain after extensive bone surgery such as pelvic tumor resection [18].

Orthopedic surgeries, particularly those involving pelvic and sacral tumors, are among the most invasive and are associated with severe acute pain [19]. Pelvic tumors, especially malignant ones, often present asymptomatically and may extend into the sacrum, complicating margin resections. Despite advancements aimed at reducing perioperative complications, these surgeries remain extensive and challenging [20]. Beyond neuropathic pain, breakthrough somatic pain is reported in approximately 30% of cases following pelvic bone tumor resection [2]. Consequently, optimizing postoperative pain management has become as critical as achieving favorable oncologic outcomes in these surgeries.

While opioids are frequently used, their efficacy in managing neuropathic pain is inconsistent in short-term studies [21]. Intravenous lidocaine, however, has shown promise in reducing postoperative pain intensity in various procedures, such as spine surgery [22]. The study highlights the analgesic potential of systemic lidocaine in improving postoperative pain outcomes.

Although no significant differences were found in rescue analgesia use or resting and moving NRS on POD 1, 2, and 3 between the L group and C group (P > 0.05) (Table 3), the proportion of patients experiencing breakthrough pain was significantly lower in the L Group compared to the C Group (33.33% vs. 75.76%, P = 0.001) (Table 3), indicating that lidocaine still conferred notable benefits in mitigating severe pain episodes. This may reflect the limited sensitivity of NRS, particularly under an effective analgesic protocol where a ceiling effect may occur. Additionally, NRS measures pain intensity only and may not capture qualitative differences in pain perception. These factors may partly explain the lack of observable differences in NRS scores.

The incidence of PONV in L Group was numerically higher than in C Group (21.21% vs 6.06%, P = 0.149), despite the lower opioid consumption observed in L Group—a factor that would typically be expected to reduce PONV. Several factors may explain this apparent inconsistency. First, the study was not powered to detect small differences in PONV incidence, so the observed variation may simply reflect random chance. Second, PONV is influenced by multiple baseline patient factors, such as female sex, history of motion sickness or PONV, and non-smoking status. Variability in these inherent risk factors between groups may have outweighed the potential PONV-reducing effect of decreased opioid use, resulting in the numerically higher—but not statistically significant—PONV incidence in L Group. Importantly, the incidence of clinically significant PONV requiring rescue antiemetic therapy did not differ between groups. Rescue antiemetic therapy was required in 1 (3.03%) patient in C Group and 2 (6.06%) patients in L Group (Fisher’s exact test, P = 1.000), indicating that the observed difference in overall PONV incidence did not translate into a difference in clinically meaningful outcomes.

Larger doses of lidocaine have been associated with enhanced analgesic effects; however, they also result in a significantly higher incidence of dizziness [23]. Iacob et al., in a retrospective study on neuropathic pain, highlighted that prolonged intravenous lidocaine infusion typically causes mild and transient side effects, such as perioral tingling and dizziness [24]. In a recent study investigating patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) for open hepatectomy, dizziness was reported in 17.7% of patients on POD 1 [25], a figure comparable to the 15.15% incidence observed in the L Group in our study (Table 4). While our study did not find a statistically significant difference in dizziness between the lidocaine and placebo groups, the frequency of dizziness in the L Group remains notable. In a previous study, a 1.5 mg/kg loading dose followed by a continuous infusion at 2 mg·kg⁻1·h⁻1 resulted in plasma lidocaine concentrations that remained within the established therapeutic range [26]. In our study, postoperative intravenous lidocaine was administered at 1 mg·kg⁻1·h⁻1 for 48 h, and the predicted plasma concentrations also fell within the accepted safety margin. However, plasma lidocaine levels are influenced by hepatic metabolism, protein binding, age, and concomitant medications. Taken together, these considerations underscore the potential value of incorporating lidocaine blood concentration measurements in future studies to better define exposure–response relationships and further ensure patient safety. Reducing opioid consumption not only alleviates common adverse effects, such as opioid-induced bowel dysfunction, but also facilitates faster postoperative recovery and better overall patient outcomes. Intravenous lidocaine has been shown to accelerate bowel recovery in certain surgeries, such as radical retropubic prostatectomy [27]. Although the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, animal studies suggest that lidocaine may reduce postoperative paralytic ileus by blocking inhibitory reflexes in the myenteric nerve plexus, thereby promoting gastrointestinal motility. Its opioid-sparing effects may also play a role by mitigating opioid-induced bowel dysfunction. Interestingly, lidocaine demonstrates synergistic effects when combined with opioids, enhancing their efficacy [28]. This synergism highlights the potential for optimizing multimodal analgesic strategies to improve pain management outcomes.

Due to concerns regarding perioperative bleeding, as pelvic bone tumor surgeries are typically associated with substantial blood loss (approximately 1000 mL on average) (Table 2), the use of NSAIDs was limited in routine clinical practice in this surgical context for prophylaxis of potential bleeding risk. In the current study, multimodal analgesia mainly consisted of opioid-based patient-controlled analgesia combined with postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion.

However, no significant differences in bowel recovery were observed between the L group and C group. The time to first bowel evacuation was similar [22(15.2- 42) hours in the L group vs. 22 (15.5- 38.5) hours in the C group, P = 0.901], as was the time to first oral intake [9 (6-16.5) hours vs. 12 (6.2-16) hours, P = 0.371] (Table 4). This lack of difference may be influenced by intraoperative manipulation of the pelvic nerve plexus, which can disrupt neural pathways involved in bowel motility. Additionally, the high intraoperative opioid use could have contributed to delayed gastrointestinal recovery, counteracting lidocaine’s potential benefits. Other factors, such as variability in postoperative ambulation practices, might also have affected the outcomes.

Limitations

Before interpreting our findings, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Although postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion significantly reduced opioid consumption within 72 h, the absence of a difference in pain scores between groups may be attributed to the effectiveness of our current postoperative analgesic protocol, which maintained comparable pain control regardless of opioid use. Nevertheless, incorporating intraoperative lidocaine infusion might further decrease perioperative opioid requirements. In addition, the absence of frequent early postoperative pain assessments(2-12h) may limit the ability to detect short-term differences in analgesic efficacy. However, the infusion of lidocaine during surgery could potentially lead to hypotension and an increased need for vasoactive drugs [29]. Furthermore, sacral pelvic tumor surgeries are often associated with significant blood loss, which itself can result in hypotension and a higher requirement for vasoactive medications. This complicates the use of intraoperative lidocaine infusion in anesthetic management. Further research is needed to evaluate the role of intravenous lidocaine within a multimodal analgesic strategy for pelvic bone tumor surgeries. Expanding on our findings and integrating them with prior research [30] could provide stronger evidence to support the incorporation of lidocaine into comprehensive pain management protocols. Apart from this, we did not measure objective biomarkers of recovery (e.g., inflammatory cytokines) or functional metrics such as time to ambulation. Incorporating these endpoints in future studies may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the effects of intravenous lidocaine on postoperative recovery.

Conclusion

Postoperative intravenous lidocaine infusion is effective in reducing opioid consumption and the incidence of breakthrough pain following pelvic bone tumor surgery.
