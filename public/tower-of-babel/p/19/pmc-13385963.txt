Green synthesis of copper oxide nanoparticles with Calendula officinalis plant extract and investigation of its toxicity
Authors: Ali Mohammadi, Fatemeh Hosseinabadi, Amirmahdi Danafar, Alireza Yazdinezhad, Hossein Danafar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO-NPs) were synthesized using a green, eco-friendly method employing the ethanolic extract of Calendula officinalis. The synthesis leveraged the plant’s rich phytochemical profile to act as both reducing and capping agents, resulting in nanoparticles with enhanced biocompatibility and stability. The CuO-NPs were characterized using UV–Vis spectroscopy, FT-IR, X-ray diffraction (XRD), dynamic light scattering (DLS), and Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM), confirming their formation, functional groups, crystalline structure, and nanoscale dimensions. The average particle size was ~ 6.66 nm, with a negative zeta potential indicating good colloidal stability. Hemocompatibility and cytotoxicity were evaluated via hemolysis and MTT assays on normal HFF-2 human fibroblast cells. Results showed less than 5% hemolysis and relatively low cytotoxicity compared to chemically synthesized counterparts, highlighting the safety of the green synthesis method. This study demonstrates the potential of-mediated CuO-NPs as promising candidates for biomedical applications Calendula officinalis such as drug delivery and cancer therapy.Keywords: CuO-NPs, Green synthesis, Calendula officinalis, Biocompatibility, Cytotoxicity, Hemolysis assay, Plant-mediated nanoparticlesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Cancer, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Nanotechnology has become a big deal in science, helping develop new materials and better tools for spotting and treating diseases. Because tiny particles have special traits—like more surface area, adjustable light responses, or stronger links with cells—they’re now used a lot in health care, especially for finding and fighting cancer1. Since how these particles behave depends heavily on their size, form, and outer layer, making them the same way every time matters a whole lot2. Regular lab methods can create many kinds of small structures; however, they usually need harmful chemicals, risky liquids, lots of energy, and end up polluting the environment. That’s why folks are slowly moving to cleaner ways of building nanoparticles, using stuff from plants, microbes, or organic sources instead of harsh ingredients3–5. One kind of tiny material getting lots of focus for fighting cancer is metal-based nanoparticles—they stand out thanks to unique electrical, chemical, and light-responsive traits6,7. Copper particles draw interest since they’re cheap, naturally fight tumors, and take part in key biological processes involving electron shifts. Instead of just adding effects together, these copper bits boost chemodynamic treatment by triggering reactions similar to Fenton’s within tumor areas full of hydrogen peroxide—this ramps up toxic ·OH radical creation(Fig. 1). Rather than leaving cells unharmed, such aggressive oxygen forms cause major cellular damage, break mitochondrial function, and then push malignant cells toward self-destruction8. Besides that, copper structures convert light into heat efficiently when hit with visible or near-infrared rays—opening doors for thermal treatments using lasers or lamps while lifting total healing impact9.

Mechanism of cytotoxicity of CuO-NPs.

To keep things safe for living systems, steady over time, plus eco-friendly when made, researchers are turning to plant-based ways to create copper nanoparticles. Marigold—known scientifically as Calendula officinalis extract has been shown in numerous studies to have significant anticancer effects due to its bioactive compounds such as flavonoids, terpenes, and phenolic acids10. These compounds can exhibit antitumor activities by inducing apoptosis, enhancing the ability of cancer cells to proliferate, and regulating the immune response. These substances carry parts such as –OH, C=O, or aromatic rings; these take part in building tiny particles4,11. When making CuO-NPs using marigold extract, it mainly happens in two stages: changing metal ions into atoms, then holding them stable afterward. In the initial stage, active molecules—particularly polyphenols and flavonoids—donate electrons from their –OH groups or ring structures to reduce Cu²⁺ to elemental copper (Cu⁰), facilitating the formation of copper nanoparticles12–14. During stabilization, these natural molecules attach to the particle surfaces, acting as protective caps. Through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic interactions, and weak molecular forces, they prevent aggregation, control particle size, and protect against oxidation. The natural antioxidants present in the system further enhance durability by preventing oxidative damage and maintaining uniform dispersion in liquid media over time15,16. Moreover, due to the bioactive compounds with anticancer properties in marigold extract, it can function as an anticancer carrier and a stable platform for the preparation of copper nanoparticles. These features make the resulting system a promising platform for future studies in nanomedicine and the evaluation of anticancer effects. Overall, the synthesis of copper nanoparticles using Calendula officinalis represents a green, cost-effective, and biocompatible strategy for producing CuO-NPs with significant potential in cancer diagnosis and therapy. In this project, we employed this green synthesis approach to prepare copper nanoparticles and subsequently evaluated their cytotoxicity, in comparison with chemically synthesized counterparts, on the normal HFF-2 cell line to assess their relative safety and biocompatibility17–19.

Method and materials

Materials

FBS, acetone, PBS, CuCl2, ethanol, DMSO and 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide) (MTT) were acquired from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). All solvents were of analytical grade, purchased from Merck. HFF2 cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute in Iran.

Methods

Plant collection and identification

The plant Calendula officinalis was purchased from reputable sources and identified and confirmed by the Pharmacognosy Department at the School of Pharmacy, Zanjan University. The petals were washed with clean water to remove any dirt. They were then stored in the shade, away from direct sunlight, at room temperature for two weeks to dry.

Extraction

Dried plant material of marigold (Calendula officinalis) was ground and powdered using a mortar and pestle. The resulting powder was then placed in a separatory funnel, the bottom of which was covered with a piece of cotton. 70% ethanol solution was added to the funnel until it was approximately 1.5 cm above the contents. After three days, extraction was performed and the concentrated extract was collected. This extract was subsequently used for the green synthesis of CuO nanoparticles using marigold.

Green synthesis of CuO -NPs with Calendula officinalis extract

A round-bottom flask containing 25 mL of deionized water was placed in an oil bath maintained at 80 °C. The magnetic stirrer was set to 50 rpm. Subsequently, 250 mg of the plant extract and an appropriate amount of NaOH were added to the flask, and the reaction mixture pH was adjusted to 9. Then, 5 mL of a CuCl₂ solution (50 mg) was added dropwise under continuous stirring. The system was equipped with a reflux condenser, and the reaction mixture was stirred and heated under reflux for 24 h(h).

Identification of nanoparticles by UV–visible spectrophotometry (UV–Vis)

UV–Vis spectrophotometry is a technique used to measure the absorbance wavelengths of organic and some inorganic compounds and to identify the nature of the materials. The absorption peak of each substance depends on its internal electronic transitions. First, the spectrophotometer was calibrated using deionized water as a blank. Then, the absorbance of the nanoparticle solution was recorded in the wavelength range of 200–800 nm.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectroscopy was performed to identify functional groups, chemical bonds, and vibrational modes present in the synthesized nanoparticles. For this analysis, a potassium bromide (KBr) pellet containing the sample was prepared. Dried sample powder was mixed with KBr in a 1:100 ratio (w/w), and the mixture was pressed into a thin transparent pellet using a hydraulic press. The FT-IR spectra were recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ at room temperature.

Structural analysis using X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structure of the synthesized CuO-NPs was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker AXS D8 Advance, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.542 Å). The diffraction pattern was recorded in the 2θ range of 10°–80°. XRD is one of the most widely used techniques for identifying crystalline phases and determining structural properties. The technique involves directing X-ray beams at different angles onto the sample and analyzing the resulting diffraction pattern.

Particle size measurement using dynamic light scattering (DLS)

The surface charge of nanoparticles is an important parameter in the recognition by the immune system, the rate of metabolism of nanoparticles and in general in the duration of blood circulation. The surface charge of nanoparticles was determined by DLS.

Structural characterization of particles using atomic force microscopy (AFM)

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) is complementary techniques widely employed for the structural characterization of nanoparticles. AFM offers three-dimensional surface topography with nanometer precision, enabling the analysis of particle shape, aggregation, and surface roughness under ambient, vacuum, or liquid conditions without the need for extensive sample preparation. The use of AFM allows a comprehensive understanding of both the internal and external features of nanoparticles, ensuring accurate evaluation of their structural properties for applications in nanoscience and nanotechnology20.

Hemocompatibility assay

To evaluate the hemocompatibility of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract, a hemolysis test was conducted. Fresh whole blood was collected from a healthy volunteer and stored in a heparinized tube to prevent coagulation. The sample was used within 2 h. The hemolytic activity was measured following the protocol below: 5 mL of human red blood cells (HRBCs) were prepared and divided equally into two test tubes. The tubes were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet containing RBCs was washed with normal saline, repeating the wash 5 to 6 times. Different concentrations of CuO-NPs coated with Calendula officinalis extract were prepared and incubated with the red blood cells at 37 °C for 5 h. The contents of each microtube were then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. From each tube, 1 mL of the supernatant was collected, and hemoglobin absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

Cytotoxicity evaluation on normal cells using the MTT assay

The HFF-2 cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute Cell Bank (Tehran, Iran) and stored at − 80 °C until use. HFF-2 is a normal cell line derived from human fetal fibroblasts (14–18 weeks gestation). The appropriate culture medium for this cell line is DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS.

General cell culture conditions All mammalian cell culture procedures were performed in a sterile laboratory environment. Preventing contamination was ensured by washing hands, wearing lab coats and gloves, disinfecting tools with 70% ethanol, working under a laminar flow hood, and regularly disinfecting the hood.

MTT assay Cell viability was assessed after treatment with an external agent using a colorimetric assay. In viable cells, yellow tetrazolium crystals are reduced and cleaved by the enzyme succinate dehydrogenase, leading to the formation of insoluble purple formazan crystals. The formazan precipitate was dissolved in DMSO, and the intensity of the resulting color was measured using a spectrophotometer. The intensity of this color is directly related to the quantity of viable cells present. In this investigation, the HFF2 cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute in Iran. These cell lines were stored at -80 °C for use in experiments throughout the study. The suitable culture medium for it consists of DMEM and 10% FBS. To accomplish the MTT assay, 7,000 HFF-2 cells were deposited into each well of a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 100 µL of the specific culture medium. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After the incubation period, the growth medium was discarded, and three concentrations (50, 100, 200 and 400 µg/mL) of CuO-NPs, synthesized using both green and chemical methods, were introduced into the wells. The volume was adjusted to 100 µL with the specific culture medium. The plate was placed in a shaking incubator set at 37 °C for 24 h to allow for proper cell growth. After this incubation period, we carefully removed the culture medium and washed the cells with PBS to ensure a clean environment. Next, we added 100 µL of fresh culture medium to each well to maintain optimal conditions for the cells. Following this, we introduced 10 µL of MTT indicator into each well, and once again placed the plate in the shaking incubator at 37 °C, this time for 4 h. After the incubation, we added 100 µL of DMSO to each well and shook the plate for 10 min to ensure thorough mixing. Finally, we measured the absorbance in all wells at a wavelength of 570 nm using an ELISA reader to assess the results.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate, and statistical analysis was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were done using GraphPad Prism software.

Results

UV–Vis spectrum of the synthesized nanoparticles

UV–Vis spectroscopy is an indirect method for identifying particles. Each substance has its own characteristic maximum absorption wavelength, which can be used to some extent for its identification. The UV–Vis spectrum of the CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract is shown in Fig. 2. As the results indicate, the extract exhibits peaks at 268, 290, and 330 nm (magnified view). The peaks at 268 and 290 nm are likely related to π→π* electronic transitions, while the peak at 330 nm is characteristic of phenolic compounds present in plants, attributed to localized transitions within the aromatic ring system (n→π*)21,22. However, the CuO-NPs display two relatively distinct signals at 292 and 366 nm, which are attributed to Cu–O bond transitions23–25.

UV–Vis spectrum of Calendula officinalis extract and CuO NPs.

FT-IR analysis

This spectroscopy technique reveals the surface chemical bonds of molecules. To confirm the successful capping and synthesis of the desired nanoparticles, the chemical structure was analyzed using FT-IR. Accordingly, the spectra of both the synthesized CuO-NPs and the plant extract were compared. Figure 3 shows the FT-IR spectra of CuO-NPs synthesized with Calendula officinalis extract and the extract alone. The presence of a broad absorption band at 3407 cm⁻¹ in the extract and at 3428 cm⁻¹ in the CuO-NPs indicates O–H stretching vibrations (associated with phenolic groups) in the structure of both the nanoparticles and the extract22. Weak absorption bands at 2950 cm⁻¹ and 2890 cm⁻¹ suggest the presence of sp³-hybridized hydrogen atoms in both structures. These bands appear with slightly reduced intensity and shifted positions in the CuO-NPs. The absorption band observed at 2357 cm⁻¹ corresponds to CO₂ and is considered an instrumental artifact. Additionally, the absorption bands at 1617 cm⁻¹ and 1412 cm⁻¹ in the extract likely correspond to C = C aromatic ring vibrations and C–N stretching vibrations, respectively (related to phenolic and amine groups). Shifts in these bands are observed in the CuO-NPs spectrum, indicating interactions between the extract and the nanoparticles. Furthermore, absorption bands in the range of 1000–1200 cm⁻¹ may be attributed to ether group vibrations present in the extract. These bands are still present in the nanoparticle spectrum, albeit with reduced intensity. A new absorption band at 658 cm⁻¹ in the nanoparticle spectrum corresponds to Cu–O stretching vibrations, confirming the formation of CuO-NPs26. Although the spectra of the extract and the CuO-NPs appear similar in general, the shifts in absorption bands clearly indicate interactions between the plant extract and the nanoparticles.

FTIR spectrum of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract.

Structural analysis of CuO-NPs synthesized from Calendula officinalis extract using XRD

The diffraction pattern of each compound is unique; thus, XRD analysis is used to qualitatively identify compounds based on their characteristic patterns. Additionally, this method provides information about the structural nature of the material, such as whether it is crystalline, amorphous, etc. The structural type, which reflects the chemical properties of the particles, can influence physical characteristics such as solubility or dispersion in a liquid medium. XRD patterns are essential tools for confirming the crystalline nature of green-synthesized CuO-NPs (CuO-NPs). As shown in Fig. 4, the XRD profile of CuO-NPs exhibits eight distinct peaks at 2θ values of 32.22°, 35.65°, 38.51°, 48.76°, 58.09°, 61.81°, 66.04°, and 67.8°, which correspond to the (110), (–111), (111), (–202), (202), (113), (311), and (221) planes, respectively. The obtained XRD pattern matches well with the standard data from JCPDS card no. 01-080-007626. These results are consistent with those reported by Gopinath et al., who synthesized CuO-NPs using Tribulus terrestris blue fruit extract27. Similarly, Amr Fouda and his research team reported comparable findings for copper nanoparticles synthesized via green methods26.

XRD Pattern of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract.

Particle size analysis

DLS was used to measure the zeta potential of the synthesized nanoparticles. The CuO-NPs exhibited a surface charge of − 23.7 mV. Zeta potential is a key parameter for assessing the colloidal stability of nanoparticles. Regardless of whether the surface charge is positive or negative, a higher absolute value implies greater repulsive forces between particles, which helps prevent aggregation and promotes stability24. Due to their negative zeta potential, the synthesized CuO-NPs are expected to exhibit high colloidal stability. Furthermore, the negative surface charge is likely to minimize interactions between the nanoparticles and blood cells.

Structural analysis of nanoparticles using AFM and TEM

Figure 5 presents the surface morphology of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract. The size and morphology of the green synthesized copper oxide nanoparticles were investigated using AFM. AFM images showed a relatively uniform particle distribution and good dispersion of nanoparticles without significant aggregation. Particle size analysis indicated that the average diameter of the nanoparticles was about 50 nm, indicating that the green synthesis process effectively controlled the growth of nuclei and prevented excessive particle aggregation. AFM results also confirmed these findings and showed a uniform surface topology along with a quasi-spherical morphology of the nanoparticles. The agreement of AFM data indicates the formation of nanoparticles with nanometer dimensions, narrow size distribution, and relatively homogeneous structure. Such features can lead to increased specific surface area, better colloidal stability, and improved performance of nanoparticles in biological and catalytic applications.

AFM images of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract.

Hemocompatibility assessment of the synthesized nanoparticles

Damage to blood cells can impair physiological functions and lead to organ damage; therefore, a hemolysis assay was conducted to evaluate the cytotoxicity of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract. The results of the hemolysis test are presented in Fig. 6. In this assay, different concentrations (50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL) of the plant extract and the CuO-NPs synthesized with the extract were evaluated. The results showed that the hemolysis percentages for both the extract and the nanoparticles remained below 5%, which is considered the internationally accepted safety threshold for biomedical materials. Hence, both materials demonstrated no significant hemolytic toxicity toward red blood cells. At concentrations of 50 and 100, 400 µg/mL, the plant extract exhibited slightly lower hemolysis percentages compared to the nanoparticles. However, at concentration of 200 µg/mL, the nanoparticles showed lower hemolysis than the extract. Overall, the hemolysis percentages for both the extract and the CuO-NPs remained below the safety limit of 5%, and no statistically significant difference was observed between the two, indicating good hemocompatibility.

Hemolysis assay of CuO-NPs synthesized using Calendula officinalis extract on red blood cell membranes.

Cell viability assessment using cell culture and MTT assay

One of the key considerations in biomedical applications is the biocompatibility of nanoparticles, especially when they are intended for internal use. To evaluate the biocompatibility and ensure minimal or no cytotoxicity of the synthesized CuO-NPs, an MTT assay was performed. Figure 7 presents the results of the cytotoxicity evaluation of the synthesized nanoparticles on HFF2 normal human fibroblast cells. The cells were cultured in 96-well plates, and after 36 h of incubation, cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. As shown in the Fig. 8, the horizontal axis represents various concentrations of the nanoparticles (50, 100, 200, and 400 µg/mL), while the vertical axis indicates the percentage of viable cells. An increase in cell viability indicates lower toxicity. With increasing nanoparticle concentration from 50 to 400 µg/mL, a decrease in cell viability was observed, reaching approximately 65%, suggesting dose-dependent cytotoxicity. A comparison with chemically synthesized CuO-NPs at a concentration of 400 µg/mL, which showed only about 11% viability, demonstrates the reduced toxicity of green-synthesized nanoparticles. This reduction in toxicity may be attributed to the biological capping of the nanoparticles during synthesis, a phenomenon scientifically supported in the literature, where surface modification of nanoparticles with biocompatible agents such as plants or BSA reduces their cytotoxic effects.

Cytotoxicity assessment of Calendula officinalis extract and CuO–NPs on HFF2 cell line.

Discussion

The way tiny particles behave—like their size, shape, crystal structure, and energy gap—influences how they act in living stuff, affecting both cell damage and blood response. Size stands out as a key factor because it shapes how cells take them in, whether they clump together, or how fast the body removes them. Smaller ones usually pack more surface compared to volume, making them more active on the outside and likely to create harmful oxygen molecules. These changes can strongly impact if cells survive while shifting how proteins stick to particles in blood—an aspect that directly shapes blood safety. On top of being small, how nanoparticles are shaped affects how they act around cells and blood parts. Take rod-shaped or sheet-like ones—because of their form and how they touch surfaces, they might stick around longer in the bloodstream or inside cells, which could boost chances of triggering inflammation or changing how platelets behave. Besides these factors, how much a nanoparticle is crystallized—and what kind of crystal form it has—strongly shapes how living systems react to it. Structural flaws or changes in surface activity might boost its power to make reactive oxygen species, affecting cell harm levels. Take titanium dioxide particles: the anatase type acts differently than rutile when hit by light, leading to varied health impacts. The size of the energy gap matters just as much—it decides if the particle can soak up light and drive chemical shifts. Particles with narrow gaps could spark ROS production even in normal lighting, possibly breaking down cell membranes, messing up fats in tissues, or raising red blood cell rupture. These physical and chemical traits together shape how well nanoparticles work with cells and blood, also influencing harmful side effects28. So checking their size, form, crystal structure, plus energy gap helps grasp cell damage risks alongside blood safety—assessments like these matter most when using tiny materials safely in medical uses. In spite of their physical traits, how nanoparticles are made greatly affects how safe, stable, and effective they are in living systems. Lately, eco-friendly methods have drawn more interest because they’re less harmful to nature. Instead of chemicals, these techniques use things like plant juices, sugars, or natural compounds to form and protect particles—cutting down waste while keeping results consistent and body-safe. What’s more, quite a few of the ingredients used in such processes already show potential in fighting cancer cells. So, these molecules give a good base for forming tiny particles—on top of that, they help them work better as medicine. Natural stuff fights cancer by itself; when it teams up with nanoparticles, the mix hits harder at tumors but causes fewer problems during therapy. Green-made nanoparticles aren’t just safer and eco-friendly—they could also boost cancer-fighting treatments when combined with other therapies. These combos might lead to better results without harsh side effects.

Conclusion

The primary objective of this study was to reduce the toxicity of CuO-NPs by synthesizing them via a green method using Calendula officinalis extract, and subsequently evaluate their characteristics and biological compatibility. Initially, CuO-NPs were synthesized following the protocol. The nanoparticles were characterized using UV–Vis, FT-IR, and XRD techniques to confirm successful synthesis and evaluate their structural and chemical properties. Further characterization was performed using DLS and AFM to assess particle size and morphology. The synthesized nanoparticles exhibited a spherical morphology. Next, the in vitro biocompatibility of the synthesized nanomaterial was evaluated using hemolysis assay and MTT cell viability assay. According to hemolysis data, both the Calendula officinalis extract and the CuO-NPs coated with the extract demonstrated less than 5% hemolysis up to a concentration of 400 µg/mL, indicating no significant hemolytic toxicity. The MTT assay results revealed dose-dependent cytotoxicity, with higher concentrations of nanoparticles resulting in decreased cell viability. However, the green-synthesized system still showed significantly lower toxicity compared to chemically synthesized counterparts, likely due to the protective plant-based coating.
