Towards precision vibration therapy for poststroke balance: a domain-specific systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Jung-Dae Kim, Se-Ra Park, Jin Mi Chun, Ji-Woo Seok, Jeong-Woo Seo
Published: 2026

Vibration therapy (VT) has been increasingly investigated as a non-invasive intervention to improve postural control and balance in individuals with stroke. Introduction

Stroke is a leading cause of long-term adult disability globally, imposing a significant burden on individuals and healthcare systems due to its high prevalence and functional sequelae.1 2 Among the various impairments following a stroke, balance dysfunction is one of the most debilitating, affecting approximately 83% of individuals with stroke.3 Post-stroke sensorimotor deficits including hemiplegia, somatosensory loss and muscle weakness significantly degrade postural control, thereby hindering independent activities of daily living (ADL) and increasing the risk of falls.4 Falls are reported in up to 73% of individuals with stroke within 6 months of discharge, often leading to fractures, fear of falling and further restriction of mobility.5 Consequently, restoring balance capability is a pivotal goal in stroke rehabilitation to mitigate fall risks and enhance the quality of life.

Recently, vibration therapy (VT), encompassing both whole-body vibration (WBV) and focal muscle vibration (FMV), has emerged as a promising non-invasive intervention to facilitate functional recovery.6 7 Mechanical vibration delivered to muscles and tendons activates proprioceptive pathways, specifically Ia afferent signals from muscle spindles, and facilitates motor unit recruitment via the tonic vibration reflex (TVR).8 Previous studies have demonstrated the clinical application of vibration stimuli for alleviating spasticity,9 improving ADL10 and enhancing upper extremity function.11 Furthermore, vibration has been suggested to modulate cortical excitability and promote neuroplasticity, potentially leading to better sensorimotor integration necessary for maintaining balance.

Despite these theoretical benefits, clinical evidence regarding the quantitative efficacy of VT for poststroke balance recovery remains highly inconsistent, with meta-analyses reporting conflicting effect sizes. For instance, a recent meta-analysis by Yin et al12 reported a strong positive effect of WBV on balance, demonstrating a significant mean difference (MD) of 4.08 points (95% CI 2.39 to 5.76, p<0.001; CI) on the Berg Balance Scale (BBS) compared with controls.12 Similarly, Yang and Butler13 found a statistically significant standardised mean difference (SMD) of 0.27 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.51, p=0.03) for balance outcomes immediately after intervention.13

In stark contrast, earlier systematic reviews have failed to identify such benefits. Lu et al14 analysed data from 298 individuals with chronic stroke and found no significant difference in BBS scores between WBV and control groups, reporting a weighted MD of −0.23 (95% CI −1.54 to 1.09, p=0.74).14 Consistent with this, Yang et al15 also reported a pooled MD of −0.08 (95% CI −1.35 to 1.19, p=0.91) for balance, concluding that there was no evidence to support the use of WBV for improving balance or gait performance.15 Furthermore, a broad meta-analysis by Park et al16 indicated that while VT had a moderate effect size on spasticity (1.24), its effect on balance was relatively small (0.28),16 suggesting that the therapeutic efficacy may vary significantly depending on the specific domain of motor function being targeted.

These discrepancies in pooled estimates are likely driven by substantial methodological heterogeneity and limitations in previous reviews. First, significant statistical heterogeneity has been observed (I2 values often exceeding 50%), which may stem from the lack of differentiation between vibration parameters such as frequency and amplitude.12 17 Optimal dosing remains unclear; for example, Wei and Cai18 and Divita et al have highlighted the differential effects of high versus low frequencies on functional outcomes, yet many reviews pool studies regardless of these parameters.18 19 Second, the amalgamation of acute, subacute and chronic stroke stages in previous reviews may have diluted the results, as neural recovery potential varies significantly across these phases.20 Third, balance is a multidimensional construct comprising static stability, dynamic control and functional mobility. However, existing reviews have often relied heavily on a single clinical scale like the BBS or aggregated diverse sensory stimulation outcomes,21 failing to capture subtle changes in dynamic stability or postural sway.

To address these gaps and resolve the conflicting evidence, this protocol outlines a comprehensive systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression analysis to verify the effectiveness of VT on poststroke balance recovery. Recent studies on spasticity have begun to employ more rigorous subgroup analyses,22 23 and this study will apply a similar rigorous methodology to balance. Unlike previous studies that aggregated heterogeneous outcomes, this review will categorise outcomes using a construct-based analytical framework that distinguishes between static postural control, dynamic balance or composite postural control, limits of stability or anticipatory postural control, functional mobility and gait speed or gait performance. This approach is intended to ensure that outcomes are synthesised only when they reflect conceptually comparable underlying constructs, thereby improving the clinical interpretability of pooled estimates. Furthermore, we will perform exploratory meta-regression analyses to investigate potential associations between parameters, including frequency, amplitude and intervention dose on the effect size. This approach aims to move beyond simple efficacy verification and inform future protocol development by identifying potential parameter–effect patterns, while avoiding inappropriate pooling of conceptually distinct balance-related outcomes and explicitly addressing the mixed findings reported in previous randomised controlled trials (RCTs).24 25

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis will be conducted in strict adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) guidelines. The overarching objective of this review is to evaluate the clinical efficacy of VT for balance-related recovery in individuals with stroke and to examine whether intervention effects differ according to conceptually distinct balance-related outcome constructs and vibration parameters. The protocol has been prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database (Registration Number: CRD420251110102). Any significant deviations from this protocol during the review process (eg, modifications to the analysis plan) will be documented in PROSPERO and transparently reported in the final publication.

Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcome (PICO) Question

Outcomes will focus on balance-related function and postural control after stroke. To address conceptual heterogeneity, outcomes will be categorised using a single construct-based framework rather than by the type of measurement instrument alone. The predefined outcome constructs will include: (1) static postural control or quiet-standing stability (eg, centre of pressure (CoP) sway area, sway velocity or path length during standing tasks); (2) dynamic balance or composite postural control assessed using clinical balance scales (eg, BBS, Postural Assessment Scale for Stroke (PASS) or Mini-Balance Evaluation Systems Test (Mini-BESTest)), where conceptual comparability is judged to be appropriate; (3) limits of stability or anticipatory postural control (eg, Functional Reach Test or instrumented limits-of-stability measures); (4) functional mobility (eg, Timed Up and Go test (TUG)) and (5) gait speed or gait performance related to balance function (eg, 10-Meter Walk Test (10MWT) or gait velocity).

Outcomes will not be pooled solely because they are commonly described as balance-related outcomes or because they are assessed using similar types of instruments. Quantitative synthesis will be performed only when outcomes are judged to measure the same underlying construct and have comparable task demands and clinical interpretation. When conceptual comparability cannot be established, findings will be summarised using a structured narrative synthesis.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Studies will be included if they meet the following criteria: (1) RCTs, non-randomised controlled trials (nRCTs) or crossover trials that allow for pre- and postintervention comparisons; (2) studies involving adult human participants diagnosed with ischaemic or haemorrhagic stroke; (3) trials evaluating the effects of mechanical vibration applied to the whole-body or specific muscles, tendons or plantar regions and (4) studies reporting quantitative outcome corresponding to at least one predefined balance-related construct, such as postural control, dynamic balance, limits of stability, functional mobility or gait speed/performance. Studies combining vibration with other therapeutic modalities (eg, vibration plus treadmill training) will be included only if the control group received the identical co-intervention without vibration, thereby allowing the isolated effect of vibration to be assessed.

Exclusion criteria

We will exclude studies that (1) involve paediatric populations or individuals with non-stroke-related neurological conditions (such as Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis or traumatic brain injury) unless stroke-specific data can be extracted separately; (2) use vibration as a sensory cue rather than a therapeutic stimulus, or employ non-mechanical vibration modalities (such as acoustic or ultrasound therapies); (3) employ vibration solely as an external cueing strategy without a therapeutic vibration protocol; (4) lack a suitable control condition or baseline data; (5) are non-original research such as reviews, editorials, case reports or conference abstracts without full datasets or (6) fail to report extractable quantitative data (such as means and SD) even after contacting the authors.

Search strategy

To identify relevant literature, we will systematically search comprehensive electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science and Scopus, for articles published from inception to 31 March 2026. The search strategy will use a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords related to the condition (eg, ‘Stroke’, OR ‘Cerebrovascular accident’, OR ‘Hemiplegia’), the intervention (eg, ‘Vibration therapy’, OR ‘Whole-body vibration’, OR ‘Focal muscle vibration’), and the outcomes of interest (eg, ‘Balance’, OR ‘Postural control’, OR ‘Gait stability’ OR ‘postural stability’) (see online supplemental appendix 1). Additionally, we will perform manual screening of the reference lists of included articles and relevant systematic reviews (snowballing) to identify any further eligible studies not captured by the electronic search.

Study selection process

All retrieved records will be imported into reference management software (eg, EndNote; Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for deduplication. Two reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the identified records to exclude clearly irrelevant studies. Subsequently, the full texts of the remaining potentially eligible articles will be retrieved and assessed against the detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies regarding study eligibility will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third independent reviewer. The entire selection process will be documented and presented in a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram to confirm transparency and reproducibility (figure 1).

Data extraction

Data extraction will be performed independently by two reviewers using a standardised, prepiloted form. Extracted information will include study characteristics (author, year, country and design), participant demographics (age, sex, time since stroke onset and baseline functional status) and detailed intervention parameters. To address heterogeneity in vibration protocols, we will extract data based on the ‘Body Vibration Big Five’ framework,26 recording specific parameters such as frequency (Hz), amplitude (mm), application method (eg, WBV vs FMV), session duration and the total intervention period.

Outcome data will be extracted for balance-related outcomes and categorised according to their underlying functional constructs rather than the type of assessment instrument. Specifically, outcomes will be grouped into static postural control (eg, CoP sway metrics), dynamic balance or composite postural control assessed using clinical scales (eg, BBS, Mini-BESTest, PASS), limits of stability or anticipatory postural control (eg, Functional Reach Test), functional mobility (eg, TUG) and gait performance or gait speed (eg, 10MWT or gait velocity). This approach ensures that outcomes are classified based on the construct they measure rather than being grouped under broad categories of functional task-based assessments. Accordingly, measures such as the TUG, Functional Reach Test and 10MWT will be treated as distinct outcome constructs rather than pooled together. Similarly, clinical scales such as the BBS, PASS and Mini-BESTest will not be pooled indiscriminately and will be synthesised quantitatively only when their conceptual comparability is judged to be sufficient. This classification will be used to guide subsequent analyses, including construct-specific quantitative synthesis where appropriate. For outcomes where lower values indicate better performance (eg, TUG, Sway Area), data will be inverted to align the direction of effect with clinical scales where higher scores indicate improvement. In cases where data are presented only in graphical format, digital extraction software (eg, WebPlotDigitizer; Pacifica, CA, USA) will be used to estimate numerical values.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and potential bias of the included studies will be independently evaluated by two reviewers. For RCTs, we will use the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool. This instrument assesses bias across five distinct domains: the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result. For crossover trials, particular attention will be paid to potential carryover effects and period effects using the appropriate RoB 2 variant. In the case of nRCTs, the Risk of Bias in Non-randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) tool will be employed to evaluate confounding factors and selection bias. Each domain will be graded as having a ‘low risk’, ‘some concerns’ (or ‘moderate risk’), or ‘high risk’ (or ‘serious risk’). Any discrepancies in the assessment process will be resolved through discussion or by seeking the opinion of a third adjudicator. The overall RoB profile will be summarised using graphical representations (eg, summary plots) to provide a transparent overview of the internal validity of the included studies.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data synthesis will be conducted using R software (V.4.3.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the metafor package or JASP software (JASP Team, Amsterdam, The Netherlands). To estimate the magnitude of the therapeutic effect, the SMD and 95% CI will be calculated for continuous outcomes. SMDs will be applied only within outcome groups that are judged to be conceptually homogeneous and will not be used to justify pooling outcomes that measure different underlying constructs. Where sufficient studies use the same measurement instrument, pooled analyses using MDs will also be considered to improve clinical interpretability. Crucially, to ensure consistency in the direction of the effect, data from outcome measures where lower values indicate better performance, such as the TUG test, Sway Area and Sway Velocity, will be inverted (multiplied by −1) prior to synthesis. This standardisation ensures that for all analysed metrics, positive effect sizes represent an improvement in balance function.

Given the anticipated clinical and methodological diversity among the included studies, a random-effects model using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood estimation method will be applied for all meta-analyses. Statistical heterogeneity will be quantified using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test, with I2 values of 25%, 50% and 75% interpreted as low, moderate and substantial heterogeneity, respectively.

Meta-analyses will be conducted separately within each predefined outcome construct, rather than pooling outcomes across conceptually distinct constructs. Quantitative pooling will be performed only when outcomes share comparable underlying constructs, task demands and clinical interpretation; otherwise, findings will be synthesised using a structured narrative approach.

To explore the sources of heterogeneity in an exploratory manner, we will conduct prespecified subgroup analyses and meta-regression analyses. Subgroup analyses will stratify the data based on: (1) predefined outcome constructs reflecting different aspects of balance function, (2) the chronicity of stroke (acute/subacute vs chronic) and (3) the vibration modality (WBV vs FMV). Furthermore, meta-regression analyses will be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and performed to explore associations between intervention parameters and effect sizes, using independent variables such as vibration frequency (Hz), amplitude (mm), session duration and total intervention volume. To minimise potential collinearity among related variables, each parameter will be examined in separate univariate models, and closely related variables (eg, session duration and total intervention volume) will not be included simultaneously. If sufficient data are available, a parsimonious multivariable model including non-redundant variables may be considered; otherwise, findings will be interpreted cautiously as associations rather than independent effects. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the findings, including (1) sequential exclusion of studies with a high RoB or statistical outliers, (2) restriction to studies using the same assessment instrument and (3) exclusion of non-randomised studies to evaluate the influence of study design.

Publication bias will be assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically via Egger’s regression test if 10 or more studies are included in a specific analysis. If evidence of funnel plot asymmetry is observed, the trim-and-fill procedure will be employed to estimate the potential impact of publication bias. In addition, sensitivity analyses will be performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the robustness of the pooled effect estimates.

Finally, the certainty of evidence for each outcome will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach,27 classifying the quality of evidence into high, moderate, low or very low levels. The certainty of evidence will be assessed independently by two reviewers using the GRADE approach. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Final certainty ratings (high, moderate, low or very low) and their justifications will be presented in Summary of Findings tables and evidence profiles generated using GRADEpro GDT (McMaster University and Evidence Prime, Hamilton, ON, Canada).

Ethics and dissemination

This study does not require ethical approval because it is based exclusively on the analysis of previously published data and does not involve the collection or use of individual-level participant information. As such, no additional ethical considerations or informed consent procedures are applicable.

The findings of this systematic review and meta-analysis will be disseminated through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at national and international scientific conferences. The results are expected to provide a structured and transparent synthesis of the current evidence and to inform future research directions and clinical decision-making in stroke rehabilitation.

Discussion

Rationale and significance of the study

Poststroke balance impairment is a multidimensional problem involving sensory integration deficits, motor output limitations and altered cortical control. While previous systematic reviews have investigated the effects of VT on general motor function, the results regarding balance remain inconclusive due to the aggregation of heterogeneous outcome measures. For instance, Lu et al14 reported no significant effect of WBV on the BBS in individuals with chronic stroke,14 whereas a recent meta-analysis by Yin et al12 demonstrated a significant improvement in balance and walking function.12 Furthermore, Yang and Butler13 highlighted a strong correlation between vibration dosage and fall risk reduction, suggesting that efficacy is highly parameter-dependent.13 To address the inconsistency arising from the aggregation of heterogeneous outcome measures, this systematic review applies a construct-based analytical framework rather than an instrument-based classification. Outcomes will be analysed within predefined constructs, including static postural control, dynamic balance or composite postural control, limits of stability, functional mobility and gait speed or gait performance, only when conceptual comparability is judged to be appropriate. This approach is intended to avoid pooling outcomes that differ in underlying construct, task demand or clinical interpretation. Building on previous systematic investigations of VT in stroke rehabilitation, including studies on spasticity and ADL,9 10 this study aims to extend the evidence base by providing a more structured evaluation of balance-related outcomes.

Potential mechanisms of vibration therapy

The therapeutic efficacy of VT on poststroke balance recovery can be attributed to a complex interplay of peripheral neurophysiological responses and central neuroplastic changes.

Mechanical vibration enhances proprioceptive feedback by activating large-diameter group Ia afferent fibres associated with muscle spindles, leading to the TVR and increased motor unit recruitment.6 7 12 This mechanism may contribute to improved activation of paretic lower limb muscles involved in postural stability.

VT may also modulate spinal excitability and reduce spasticity through mechanisms such as reciprocal and presynaptic inhibition.9 22 Supporting evidence indicates that focal vibration can reduce H-reflex amplitude and improve joint mobility.28

In addition, VT has been associated with supraspinal plasticity and cortical reorganisation.7 8 Neuroimaging studies have demonstrated increased activation in sensorimotor and premotor cortical regions following vibration stimulation,29 30 suggesting enhanced integration of sensory input for postural control.

Furthermore, stimulation of plantar mechanoreceptors may enhance somatosensory feedback required for postural orientation,21 improving the detection of support surface changes and postural adjustments.31 Emerging evidence also suggests potential anti-inflammatory effects of WBV through cytokine modulation,32 although this mechanism requires further investigation in stroke populations.

Categorisation of balance outcomes: clinical and physiological perspectives

A major limitation of prior reviews is the pooling of disparate balance-related metrics as if they represented a single construct. In the present protocol, outcome measures will be distinguished according to their underlying functional constructs rather than by assessment instrument type alone. The predefined constructs include static postural control or quiet-standing stability, dynamic balance or composite postural control, limits of stability or anticipatory postural control, functional mobility and gait speed or gait performance related to balance function. Xu et al33 used instrumented measures like the Romberg Test Area to detect subtle proprioceptive changes that clinical scales might miss.33 This distinction is critical because commonly used outcomes may differ substantially in what they measure. For example, the TUG test, Functional Reach Test and 10MWT should not be pooled together solely as functional task-based assessments, because they reflect functional mobility, limits of stability and gait speed or gait performance, respectively. Similarly, clinical scales such as the BBS, PASS and Mini-BESTest will only be pooled when their conceptual comparability is considered sufficient.

Accordingly, results will be interpreted within each predefined construct rather than as a single unified balance outcome. Quantitative synthesis will be restricted to outcomes that share comparable underlying constructs, task demands and clinical interpretation. When such comparability cannot be established, findings will be summarised narratively. This approach aims to provide a more nuanced understanding of intervention effects while acknowledging the inherent heterogeneity of balance-related outcomes.34 35

Addressing parameter heterogeneity via meta-regression

The heterogeneity in vibration parameters (frequency, amplitude and dosing) is a significant barrier to clinical standardisation. Wei and Cai18 found that different frequency settings (eg, high vs low) yield varying effects on physical performance, yet optimal settings remain undefined.18 Similarly, Zeng et al17 noted that the duration of intervention and the type of vibration (vertical vs side-alternating) could act as effect modifiers.17 However, these intervention parameters may be interrelated; for example, session duration and total intervention volume are closely connected, and frequency and amplitude may also covary across protocols. This review will employ prespecified meta-regression analyses to explore associations between selected intervention parameters and balance-related outcomes. These analyses will be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Closely related variables will not be entered simultaneously in the same model, and a parsimonious multivariable model including only non-redundant moderators will be considered only if the number of included studies is sufficient. If such analyses are not feasible, univariate meta-regression findings will be interpreted cautiously as associations that may be confounded by correlated intervention characteristics, rather than as independent parameter effects.

Limitations and future directions

While this protocol is rigorous, potential limitations exist. First, balance-related outcomes are inherently multidimensional, and measures that are frequently grouped together may reflect distinct underlying constructs. Although SMDs can account for differences in measurement units, they do not resolve conceptual heterogeneity. Therefore, quantitative synthesis will be restricted to conceptually comparable outcome constructs, and structured narrative synthesis will be used when appropriate pooling is not feasible. Second, the inclusion of both RCTs and nRCTs may introduce selection bias, although we will mitigate this by using the ROBINS-I tool and conducting sensitivity analyses excluding non-randomised data. Additionally, the variability in stroke chronicity (acute vs chronic) across studies may influence the magnitude of neuroplastic changes. Third, meta-regression analyses may be constrained by the number of eligible studies and by interdependence among intervention parameters. As a result, moderator analyses will be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, particularly when only univariate models are feasible. Future research directions identified from this review will focus on establishing standardised reporting guidelines for vibration parameters to facilitate more precise cross-study comparisons.
