Economic evaluation of liraglutide vs dulaglutide or oral semaglutide in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus: a systematic review
Authors: Lu Wang, Fei Wang, Yinglin Wang, Quan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe management of type 2 diabetes not only requires effective medications to regulate blood glucose levels, but also needs to consider the economic implications. Liraglutide, dulaglutide, and semaglutide, three widely-used glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, have shown significant efficacy in diabetes treatment. This review aimed to systematically assess the cost-effectiveness of liraglutide in comparison to dulaglutide or oral semaglutide for treating type 2 diabetes.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, and Cochrane. Studies published up to December 31, 2024 were retrieved. Two independent reviewers carefully screened the titles, abstracts, and full-text articles, and any disagreements were resolved with the involvement of a third reviewer. Data extraction was carried out following a pre-designed form.Results12 studies were included, evaluating liraglutide vs. dulaglutide (n = 8) and liraglutide vs. oral semaglutide (n = 6). The minimum consolidated health economic evaluation reporting standards score for the studies was 0.75. The included studies exhibited similar results in cost-effective. Oral semaglutide would be more effective and cost-saving in the US, Netherlands, Spain, and the UK. According to the available studies, liraglutide vs. dulaglutide or oral semaglutide for the treatment of type 2 diabetes is considered not to be cost-effective.ConclusionCost-effectiveness also plays a vital role in the inclusion of these drugs in healthcare reimbursement policies. The literature suggested that dulaglutide or oral semaglutide may be more cost-effective than liraglutide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01927-x.

Introduction

Diabetes is a chronic metabolic disease characterized by insufficient insulin secretion or poor cellular response to insulin [1]. The prevalence of diabetes is continuously rising [2]. Diabetes poses a severe threat to human health and exerts profound impacts on social and economic development [3]. The number of adult diabetes patients worldwide has surged from 200 million in 1990 to 828 million in 2022 [4, 5]. In 2022, China had approximately 148 million adults with diabetes, comprising 18% of the global adult diabetes population and making it the country with the second-highest number of cases, following India [4]. The economic burden of diabetes is substantial, with healthcare costs associated with the disease skyrocketing. These expenses encompass treatment, management, and the prevention of complications, as well as losses in productivity due to illness and premature death [6, 7]. The economic burden of diabetes on the healthcare system in China is significant, with the costs projected to increase from $250.2 billion in 2020 to $460.4 billion in 2030, including both direct and indirect costs [8]. Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is the predominant type of diabetes, accounting for over 90% of cases worldwide, according to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) [5]. Data from China’s National Health Commission indicates tha T2D represents 93.7% of diabetes, while type 1 diabetes (T1D) accounts for 5.6%, and other types make up only 0.7% [9].

One of the primary mechanisms of action of Glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) in T2D management is their ability to enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion. In the presence of elevated blood glucose levels, these agonists bind to GLP1 receptors on pancreatic beta cells, stimulating insulin release [10]. Several GLP-1RA are available, with different pharmacokinetic profiles. The first clinically approved GLP-1RA, exenatide, was introduced in 2005, followed by other agents such as liraglutide, semaglutide, dulaglutide, and tirzepatide [11]. In clinical trials, GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated superior efficacy compared to traditional diabetes medications. For example, in the SUSTAIN-11 trial, semaglutide showed better glycemic control and weight loss outcomes compared to insulin aspart [12]. The therapeutic applications of GLP-1RA are expanding to include oral formulations, such as oral semaglutide, which further enhance patient convenience and adherence.

The main objective of this paper was to conduct a systematic review of the most current evidence regarding the pharmacoeconomic evaluation of liraglutide compared to dulaglutide or oral semaglutide for treating patients with T2D.

Methods

The systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered in the PROSPERO (CRD42025641158) [13].

Search strategy

The literature was searched on MEDLINE (via PubMed), Web of Science, Embase, Scopus, and Cochrane, covering the period from the establishment of these databases until December 31, 2024.

The search focused on pharmacoeconomic evaluations of liraglutide for T2D published in English and Chinese. The search strategy, which included medical subject headings (MeSH) and text words, was presented in Tab. S1.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with T2D; (2) comparison of interventions, liraglutide versus dulaglutide or liraglutide versus oral semaglutide; and (3) economic evaluations, including cost-utility analyses, cost-effectiveness analyses, cost-benefit analyses, and cost-minimization analyses. The review articles, editorials, letters to editors, meeting abstracts, and protocols were excluded. Studies not published in English or Chinese were also excluded.

Two authors examined the title and abstract independently, followed by a comprehensive review of the full texts of the included studies. In cases where disagreements arose, a third researcher was necessary.

Data extraction

Two authors extracted the data independently, which included the following information: the author, year of publication, country, costing year, patients, compared intervention, type of economic evaluation, type of model, perspective, time horizon, willingness-to-pay (WTP) threshold, discount rate, the outcome of the analysis (total cost, life years gained (LYG), quality-adjusted life year (QALY), and incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER)), as well as sensitivity analyses.

Assessment of quality

The Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) checklist was used to assess the quality of the literature by two authors independently [14]. The checklist includes 28 items, encompassing four categories: completely satisfied (Y), rated as “1”; not fulfilled (N), rated as “0”; partially fulfilled (PA), rated as “0.5”; and not applicable (NA) [14–16].

Data synthesis

The WTP, total cost, and ICER were converted to 2022 U.S. dollars (USD, $) according to the Campbell and Cochrane Economics Methods Group Evidence for Policy and Practice Information and Coordination Centre (CCEMG-EPPI-Centre) cost converter (version 1.7, last updated January 2024). This converter employs the International Monetary Fund (IMF) method, which adjusts costs based on purchasing-power-parity (PPP) [17]. The results of the included studies were analyzed and summarized.

Results

Overview

1109 articles were yielded. (Fig. 1) After the removal of duplication (n = 507), 602 articles were screened for titles and abstracts. Of these, 46 articles proceeded to full-text screening. Ultimately, 12 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in the study [18–29].

All the studies reported the time horizon. Most studies used a long-term time horizon, which included a lifetime time horizon, 40-year horizon, or 50-year horizon (n = 10) [18–23, 25, 27–29]. In contrast, two studies conducted short-term time horizons of 5 years or 10 years [24, 26].

Out of the 12 included studies, 3 used the IQVIA CORE Diabetes Model (version 9.0) [20, 22, 23]. Three studies applied the UKPDS OM2 [19, 21, 29], while one study adopted a state-transition model based on the UKPDS OM1 equations [18]. Additionally, two studies utilized a 5-state Markov model structure [24, 26]. The remaining studies were conducted using the Cardiff Diabetes Model [25], the CHIME simulation model [27], and the IMS CORE Diabetes Model [28].

Regarding the comparisons made in these studies, 2 focused on different GLP-1RA (liraglutide vs. dulaglutide and liraglutide vs. oral semaglutide) [18, 19]. Four studies compared liraglutide vs. oral semaglutide [20–23], while six studies compared liraglutide vs. dulaglutide [24–29].

Primary outcomes

Liraglutide vs. oral semaglutide

Six studies estimated the economic evaluation of liraglutide versus oral semaglutide for T2D, and the results were consistent across all studies [18–23]. All the studies indicated that oral semaglutide was cost-effective compared to liraglutide (Table 3).

Four out of the six studies showed that oral semaglutide was not only less costly but also more effective. Oral semaglutide was considered a cost-saving and dominant treatment for T2D patients in the US [18], the Netherlands [20], Spain [22], and the UK [23]. The incremental QALY gained ranged between 0.009 and 0.09, and the cost savings varied from $1642.38 to $6074.74 [18, 20, 22, 23]. In another study in the US, oral semaglutide was also found to be cost-effective compared to liraglutide, with an ICER of $44836.55 per QALY [21]. Although the ICER and total costs differed between the two studies from the perspective of payers and healthcare systems in the US, oral semaglutide was consistently deemed cost-effective compared to liraglutide [18, 21]. In a study conducted in China, oral semaglutide was determined to be cost-effective by gaining an additional 0.03 QALYs and 0.02 LYGs at an increased cost of $742.87, resulting in an ICER of $33041.06 per QALY with the WTP of $36528.3 per QALY [19]. Liraglutide vs. dulaglutide

Studies compared the economic evaluations of liraglutide vs. dulaglutide for T2D. (Table 3)

Dulaglutide was shown to be dominant versus liraglutide in Colombia, Iran, and Spain by gaining 0.082, 0.157, and 0.022 additional QALYs while saving $2579.94, $1084.37, and $1926.58, respectively [24, 26, 28]. In the three studies conducted in China, dulaglutide demonstrated cost-effectiveness versus liraglutide, with varying ICERs [19, 27, 29]. In both Feng et al.’s and Yuan et al.’s studies, liraglutide was found to be more expensive and more effective than dulaglutide, with total costs of $29480.23 for liraglutide versus $26516.60 for dulaglutide, and $47,026 compared to $42,763. The QALYs were 10.36 for liraglutide versus 10.28 for dulaglutide, and 12.58 versus 12.50, respectively. The ICER was $37045.38 per QALY and $53287.5 per QALY, both exceeding the WTP of $36528.3 per QALY and $12,728 per QALY, respectively [19, 27]. Another study in China by Xie et al. indicated that dulaglutide was more expensive and more effective compared to liraglutide, yielding an ICER of $15419.01 per QALY under the WTP of $38,223 per QALY [29]. Due to the ICER of $599852.16 per QALY and the WTP of $169906.87 per QALY, liraglutide was not cost-effective compared to dulaglutide under the US payer perspective [18]. In the study by Chuang L.H. et al., neither the 1.2 mg nor the 1.8 mg daily doses of liraglutide were found to be cost-effective compared to dulaglutide from the UK healthcare system perspective [25]. With an incremental QALYs of 0.056 and incremental cost of $132.51, dulaglutide was cost-effective compared to the 1.2 mg daily dose of liraglutide, resulting in the ICER of $2366.30 per QALY [25]. While the 1.6 mg daily dose of liraglutide is more effective and more expensive than dulaglutide, it was not cost-effective due to the high ICER of $122872.18 per QALY [25]. (Figures 3 and 4).

Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive assessment of the pharmacoeconomic profile of liraglutide compared to dulaglutide and oral semaglutide in the treatment of T2D. The findings suggest that dulaglutide or oral semaglutide is generally cost-effective compared with liraglutide.

In terms of efficacy, GLP-1RA has demonstrated significant blood-glucose-lowering effects. They not only reduce HbA1c levels but also positively impact body weight and blood pressure in patients with T2D [30]. These multi-dimensional therapeutic effects are crucial for the comprehensive management of diabetes and its associated complications. For instance, the weight-reducing impact can be particularly beneficial for overweight or obese diabetic patients, as it improves insulin sensitivity and reduces the risk of cardiovascular diseases associated with diabetes.

Liraglutide has been available on the market for a long time and is supported by extensive clinical trial data that demonstrate its efficacy. It is available in an immediate-release formulation, which allows for more rapid control of blood glucose levels compared to some other drugs that may have a delayed onset due to their formulation or mode of absorption [31]. However, liraglutide must be administered through subcutaneous injections, which can be uncomfortable and inconvenient for some patients. Additionally, fear of needles may negatively affect treatment adherence. Dulaglutide is administered once a week, making it more convenient than daily injection drugs like liraglutide in some cases. This less frequent administration may enhance patient compliance. In addition to improving adherence, such dosing regimens may also positively affect patient satisfaction and utility. Improving compliance means that patients are more likely to take medication as prescribed by doctors, leading to better treatment outcomes. Additionally, patients might feel that their quality of life has improved since they no longer need to receive injections daily, and thus hold a more positive attitude toward treatment. This positive attitude, in turn, may further increase the patient’s adherence to therapeutic protocols, creating a virtuous cycle [32]. The most prominent advantage of oral semaglutide is its oral form, eliminating the need for injection. This is more acceptable to patients who are needle-phobic or have difficulty with self-injection. However, due to the first-pass effect in the liver, oral semaglutide has relatively lower bioavailability compared to its injectable form. As a result, higher doses may be required to achieve the same therapeutic effect.

The reviewed studies consistently demonstrate that oral semaglutide is a cost-effective option for patients with T2D when compared with liraglutide. Several analyses indicated that oral semaglutide also improved health outcomes with acceptable incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) in comparison to liraglutide. Studies conducted in various countries, including the US, Netherlands, the UK, and Spain, showed that oral semaglutide led to lower mean total costs and more QALYs than liraglutide.

It’s also worth noting that in the study by Feng et al. in China, for shorter time horizons (10 years), liraglutide was more cost-effective than oral semaglutide. This difference may be attributed to variations in drug pricing and the specific patient characteristics in the Chinese population. The BMI among diabetic patients in China is 24 kg/m2, which is significantly lower than that in Europe [33]. This may limit the short-term cost-effectiveness of oral semaglutide, as semaglutide could be more effective than liraglutide in weight management [34]. In the Chinese population, the proportion of T2DM with cardiovascular complications is relatively high. The long-term cardiovascular protective effect of oral semaglutide becomes more substantial in long-term follow-up, making it more cost-effective over time [35]. The incidence of gastrointestinal adverse reactions to GLP-1RA in East Asian populations is higher than in Caucasians. Liraglutide, having been used clinically for over 10 years in China and benefiting from physicians’ extensive experience in dose adjustment and managing adverse reactions, may influence the short-term cost-effectiveness of oral semaglutide [36]. The average daily cost of oral semaglutide exceeds that of liraglutide. Short-term economic evaluations show that semaglutide’s higher cost presents a significant disadvantage, impacting its short-term cost-effectiveness [19]. The shorter-term analysis in this study highlights the importance of considering regional and time-specific factors when evaluating cost-effectiveness.

The review showed that dulaglutide is also a cost-effective option for patients with T2D when compared to liraglutide. The QALYs associated with liraglutide versus dulaglutide varied in different studies. Some studies suggested that liraglutide may offer more QALYs but fail to meet cost-effectiveness criteria, as indicated by ICER greater than the WTP threshold. In some studies, liraglutide was simultaneously more expensive and less effective in certain scenarios. As noted, the once-weekly administration of dulaglutide enhances patient compliance over the daily injection schedule of liraglutide. Morieri M.L.’s study showed that the continuous medication rate one year after duraglutide treatment was 6.3% higher than that of duraglutide, and the reduction of HbA1c was greater with dulaglutide than with liraglutide (-0.24 ± 0.08%; p = 0.003) [37]. Beyond adherence, studies consistently indicate that less frequent injections correlate with higher treatment satisfaction, attributed to reduced treatment burden, fewer disruptions to daily routines, and decreased anxiety associated with daily self-injection [38]– [39]. In pharmacoeconomics, enhancing patient satisfaction translates into higher health utility value [40]. These utility values directly impact cost-effectiveness analyses by improving quality-adjusted life years. Consequently, dulaglutide enhances patient compliance-it contributes to improving patient satisfaction and utility, may potentially enhancing cost-effectiveness.

However, it is important to note that the cost-effectiveness can be influenced by various factors. Healthcare system differences among different regions, such as variations in drug prices, reimbursement policies, and healthcare resource utilization patterns, can lead to different cost-effectiveness outcomes. The results of economic analysis results have significant implications for clinical practice. Healthcare providers must consider not only the clinical efficacy of these drugs but also their cost-effectiveness when making treatment decisions. For patients with limited financial resources, choosing a more cost-effective drug can help ensure continuous treatment and improved disease management. In regions with different healthcare reimbursement policies, the cost-effectiveness of liraglutide and dulaglutide can also influence the inclusion of these drugs in the reimbursement lists. If a drug is proven to be highly cost-effective, it is more likely to be covered by insurance, benefiting a larger number of patients.

All economic evaluations in this systematic review were based on models. Three studies used the IQVIA CORE Diabetes Model, which consists of 17 interconnected Markov submodels that simulate diabetes-related complications [20, 22, 23]. One study employed the IMS CORE Diabetes Model, which has been rebranded as the IQVIA CORE Diabetes Model since 2017 [28]. The UKPDS OM2 is a microsimulation model, which considers the complications of myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, stroke, ischemic heart disease, amputation, end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or renal failure, blindness, and ulcer [19, 21, 29]. One study used a state-transition model predicted by the UKPDS OM1 equations, which include 5 macrovascular complications and 3 microvascular complications [18]. The Cardiff Diabetes Model is a fixed-time-increment stochastic simulation model, and long-term micro- and macrovascular complications were estimated [25]. The CHIME model is the first validated tool for predicting outcomes in Chinese patients with prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, developed using Chinese data. Thirteen risk equations were used, and eleven micro- and macrovascular complications were estimated [27]. The Markov model in the other two studies only considered the effects of nephropathy, retinopathy, and both, with a shorter time horizon, which lacked some diabetes complications and could affect the generalizability of their findings [24, 26]. Compared with cohort Markov models, microsimulation models can capture long-term complications more accurately and fully account for individual heterogeneity [41].

The quality assessment of economic research is important. The CHEERS statement checklist was used to assess the quality of the included studies. The overall quality of the included health economic studies was good, but there is scope for improvement. Partial articles do not appropriately specify the title. More attention should be placed on the health economic analysis plan, subgroup analyses of heterogeneity, and characterization of distributional effects [42]. Researchers should have a correct and standardized understanding of the CHEERS checklist to improve the standardization and scientificity of economic evaluations.

One of the advantages of GLP-1RAs is their potential to assist in weight loss. In addition to this, they also provide cardiovascular and renal benefits [43]. It would be valuable to conduct economic evaluations of subgroup analyses for individuals with T2D who are overweight or obese or maybe have diabetic complications (such as diabetic nephropathy, retinopathy, cardiovascular diseases, etc.) in future studies. Such subgroup analyses could provide more accurate evaluation of the cost-effectiveness in this population and more evidence for clinical decision-making, and enhance the applicability of research results to the complex real-world patient population.

This review had some limitations. Firstly, only full articles were included, while the economic evaluations published as conference abstracts were excluded. Second, there was a language limitation, as articles not published in English or Chinese were not considered. Third, different time horizons in various studies may lead to variations in the results. Fourth, the majority of the studies were conducted in high-income countries, which may limit the generalizability of findings to low- and middle-income countries. Finally, most of the results of economic evaluations were based on the clinical trials and may not fully reflect real-world scenarios. Long-term real-world observational studies are needed.

Conclusion

This paper presents a systematic review of economic evaluations comparing liraglutide with dulaglutide or oral semaglutide in the treatment of T2D. The literature suggested that dulaglutide or oral semaglutide may be more cost-effective compared to liraglutide. More economic evaluations based on real-world scenarios are needed.
