Genomic insights into the mangrove-associated cultivable bacteria and their ecological function
Authors: Xiaofang Huang, Puzi Jiang, Xi Yang, Junde Dong, Tongyin Liang, Juan Ling, Haixin Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryMicrobes are indispensable players in sustaining mangrove plant health and ecosystem productivity. However, studies concerning the isolation and function of mangrove-associated bacteria remain limited. Detailed genomic analysis could provide some insights into their ecological functions. In this study, we isolated 163 bacterial strains from the mangrove sediments of Sanya River Mangrove Nature Reserve, including 51 putative novel species and 2 novel genera. The genomes of 75 isolates were sequenced, combined with 160 publicly available genomes from NCBI, resulting in an integrated gesnomic characterization of 235 mangrove-associated isolates. Genomic analyses revealed that most isolates possess putative functions (e.g., siderophore production and phosphate solubilization) in supporting mangrove plant health. Furthermore, key bacterial taxa such as Pseudomonadales and Rhizobiales were identified as critical biogeochemical linkers connecting the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles. Our findings provide valuable microbial resources and a theoretical foundation for developing strategies to enhance plant health and restore mangrove ecosystems.

Introduction

Mangrove wetlands are one of the most productive marine ecosystems. Because of unique environmental conditions such as high salinity and high nutrient contents, low pH, and low oxygen, the mangrove ecosystems harbor diverse and active microbial communities.1 Bacteria are key components of the microbial communities associated with mangrove wetlands, accounting for 91% of the total microbial biomass in mangrove habitats.2 Mangrove ecosystem-associated bacteria affect mangrove plant growth, health, and nutrient absorption, especially plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR).3 For example, diazotrophs are vital PGPR that can convert N2 into plant-available ammonia and enhance plant growth, are already widely used as microbial biofertilizers in mangrove restoration4; phosphate-solubilizing bacteria present in the mangrove sediments can also act as biofertilizers and increase phosphate availability for plant growth5; sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) and cellulose-degrading bacteria are dominant in the mangrove ecosystem and play major roles in the sulfur and carbon cycles.6 Therefore, the composition and abundance of PGPR can be further developed as an indicator for evaluating the biological status of mangrove forest health and nutrition.7,8

In recent years, a variety of molecular biology techniques, such as high-throughput amplicon and metagenomic sequencing, have provided opportunities to study microbial communities in mangrove wetlands.4,9 Such methods mainly focused on identifying difficult-to-culture bacteria that may enhance mangrove plant health and understanding how they modify their community structure and function in response to various environmental conditions.1,10,11 However, the bacterial metabolic activity and products and their functional verification can be addressed only by culture-based methods.2,12 The isolation of key microorganisms from plant roots is essential for revealing the bacterial functions and the plant-microbe interaction.13,14 Such cultivated bacterial strains from the mangrove rhizosphere microbiota can provide available microbial resources for further functional and mechanistic studies.15

Although the mangrove ecosystem had a highly diverse microbial diversity, there were no more than 5% of microbial species that had been cultivated and described; this phenomenon was known as the “great plate anomaly.”6,12,16 Many bacterial species found in mangrove wetlands were difficult to isolate and culture because their various growth conditions and nutrient requirements were largely unknown. In addition, the plant-microbe interaction was complicated, making it challenging to provide a suitable in situ environment to isolate efficiently.17 The plant host might alter the plant-microbe and microbe-microbe interactions in response to unfavorable environmental conditions,18 increasing the recruitment of PGPR to improve the plant nutrient supply of mangrove plants.19 The bacterial genomes contained numerous genes associated with plant growth-promotion (PGP) functions by regulating carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles. These genes were well-known genomic indicators reflecting the nutrition status of mangrove plants, and the presence of indicator gene clusters related to PGP functions has been confirmed in previous studies.20,21

Various bacterial strains have been isolated from the mangrove ecosystem, and many of them were identified as novel taxa, such as the type strains of Mangrovibacterium diazotrophicum, Mangrovibacter plantisponsor, Mangrovibacterium lignilyticum, Marinobacterium mangrovicola, and Maribellus sediminis.22,23,24,25,26 However, all of these studies focused only on the isolation of culturable bacteria from the mangrove ecosystem, and the functional genomic characterization of the culturable bacteria has not been measured. As far as we are aware, there have been limited genomic studies focusing on culturable bacteria in mangrove ecosystems. In this study, we isolated 163 culturable bacterial strains from mangrove sediments, including 51 putative novel species and 2 novel genera identified through 16S rRNA gene sequencing. The whole genomes of 75 bacterial isolates, combined with 160 publicly available genomes of mangrove-associated bacteria retrieved from the NCBI database, were used to generate a comprehensive genome catalog of 235 mangrove-associated bacteria. To further explore the ecological functions of these bacterial isolates, we analyzed the genomic indicators and experimentally verified the growth-promoting function of 11 strains through genome mining. This study provided novel insights into the potential ecological roles of culturable bacterial groups in mangrove ecosystems and offered valuable microbial resources for the development of strategies aimed at mangrove restoration and rehabilitation of degraded wetlands.

Results

Culturable bacteria from the mangrove habitats reveal novel diversity

A total of 163 isolates were obtained from the mangrove sediments by using the 2216 marine broth agar (MA), 10% 2216 marine broth agar (10% MA), and actinomycetes isolation agar (AIA). It is worth noting that most of the isolates (72 isolates) were obtained with 10% MA medium, followed by MA (51 isolates) and AIA (40 isolates) (Table S1). These isolates were classified into 44 genera across eight classes based on 16S rRNA sequence similarity. These include Alphaproteobacteria (50 isolates), Gammaproteobacteria (66 isolates), Betaproteobacteria (9 isolates), Actinomycetia (29 isolates), Bacilli (4 isolates), Cytophagia (3 isolates), Flavobacteriia (1 isolate), and Chitinophagia (1 isolate). The most abundant bacterial taxa belonged to Pseudomonadaceae (30 isolates) and Sphingomonadaceae (28 isolates) (Figure 1A). Based on 16S rRNA sequence similarity, putative novel species were assigned using an identity threshold of 98.7% sequence similarity cutoff.27,28 Among these isolates, we identified 70 different bacterial species by taxonomic assignments based on the EzBioCloud database (Table S1). Fifty-one putative novel species and two novel genera were detected, which together made up approximately 33% of all bacterial isolates. Genome characteristics of culturable bacteria from mangrove wetlands

Based on 16S rRNA sequence similarity, some bacterial isolates affiliated with the highly recurring genera were excluded for whole-genome sequencing, such as Novosphingobium (11 isolates), Pseudomonas (14 isolates), Streptomyces (18 isolates), and Acinetobacter (8 isolates). Besides, 37 assembled genomes did not meet the criteria of high quality (completeness >95% and contamination <5%). Therefore, we obtained whole-genome sequences for 75 cultivated isolates from mangroves. These assemblies were combined with the genomes of 160 mangrove-associated isolates collected from the NCBI database (see STAR Methods). A total of 235 of these assemblies met the criteria of the high-quality genome, which were defined as having a completeness of at least 95% and a contamination level of no more than 5%, the average completeness was 99.37%, while the average contamination level was 0.85%, and was used to generate the mangrove isolates catalog. The genome sizes of these mangrove microbial genomes (MMGs) ranged from 2.37 Mb to 9.72 Mb, and GC% varied from 29.76% to 74.59% (Table S2). In addition, 44 isolates contain 2 to 11 16S rRNA gene copies (Table S2). Taxonomic classification using the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) (release v207_r2) suggested that the MMGs represent 7 phyla, 9 classes, 27 orders, 53 families, 106 genera, and 118 species (Figure 1B). The MMGs dataset was dominated by Proteobacteria (147/235), Actinobacteriota (43/235), Firmicutes (25/235), and Bacteroidota (17/235), whereas Desulfobacterota (1/235), Planctomycetota (1/235), and Verrucomicrobiota (1/235) were identified as rare phyla. Furthermore, 8 genera and 87 species (34 species from this study by isolation) were not assigned to representative classification in the GTDB (release v207_r2) using an average nucleotide identity threshold of 95%,29 therefore representing potential novel lineages (Figure 1B).

Distribution of functional genomic indicators in culturable bacteria from mangrove wetlands

We assessed the metabolic pathways of MMGs using Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) level 2. Metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, as well as cofactors and vitamins, was abundant in all these isolates (Figure 2 and Table S3). We noticed that certain KEGG pathways exhibited specific distribution patterns among different classes or genera. Genomic indicators involved in cell motility were abundant in the classes Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and Bacilli, and the genera Pseudomonas and Shewanella. The genera Klebsiella and Agrobacterium show a relatively higher abundance of genes involved in membrane transport. We further evaluated the potential of mangrove-associated bacteria to utilize carbohydrates. A total of 12,543 potential glycoside-degrading enzymes (GDEs) were identified across 229 isolates, including 50 auxiliary activities (AAs), 756 carbohydrate-binding modules (CBMs), 799 carbohydrate esterases (CEs), 5,994 glycoside hydrolases (GHs), 4,700 glycosyl transferases (GTs), and 244 polysaccharide lyases (PLs) (Table S5). We discovered that the GDE content varied across different phyla, with the average frequency of GDE sequences spanning from 0.19 to 42.92 per sequenced genome equivalent (SGE, Mb). Compared with Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Bacteroidota, the phylum Actinobacteriota held higher content of GDE contents of AA and GT (Figure 3B, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p-value <0.05). The contents of GDEs, especially CEs, GHs, CBMs, and GTs, were abundant in the genera Streptomyces, Bacillus, Klebsiella, and Vibrio, whereas the genera Novosphingobium and Acinetobacter had relatively lower GDE content of AAs, GHs, GTs, and CBMs (Figure 3C, Wilcoxon rank-sum test, p-value <0.05).

The indicator genes associated with carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycling in culturable bacteria

To summarize the functional potential roles of mangrove-associated bacteria in biogeochemical cycling, we analyzed the genomic indicators for the pathways of CH4, nitrogen (N), and sulfur (S) cycling in the MMGs, and these metabolic patterns displayed significant divergence across classes and genera. Besides, intra-genome (number of one replicated function gene within one genome) and inter-genome (number of genomes containing one replicated function gene with respect to all the genomes being analyzed) redundancy were calculated, respectively, for each function gene, to analyze gene redundancies in dominant genera.(1)CH4 cycle. The annotation results showed that 234 mangrove-associated isolates held at least one indicator gene associated with aceticlastic methanogenesis (Table S6). A total of 146 isolates had the potential for the hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (metF), especially the class Gammaproteobacteria and dominant genera Novosphingobium, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Streptomyces, Shewanella, Agrobacterium, and Klebsiella (Figure S2 and Table S6). The indicator gene families involved in the methylotrophic methanogenesis (torACDYZ and mtbABC) were identified in 51 MMGs (Table S6), including the genera Klebsiella and Shewanella with high intra-genome redundancies and high inter-genome redundancies of torACDYZ (Figure 4). Besides, the whole MMGs showed the potential to oxidize methane. Streptomyces, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Klebsiella, and Shewanella were the major taxa involved in the anaerobic oxidation of methane. These dominant genera also had high intra-genome redundancies in narGZHYB (Figure 4), which is known to couple with nitrate reduction and sulfate reduction. These results indicated that mangrove-associated isolates were enriched in pathways related to aceticlastic and hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis and generally possess the ability to utilize methane as a carbon source.Figure 4The point plot shows the redundancies of indicator genes associated with carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles in dominant generaThe point sizes indicate intra-genome redundancies (number of one replicated function gene within one genome), and the point colors indicate inter-genome redundancies (number of genomes containing one replicated function gene with respect to all the genomes being analyzed). A total of 3,308 genomic indicators involved in the N cycle were predominantly to the nitrate reduction process, with 12.2% for denitrification, 10.2% for dissimilatory nitrate reduction (DNRA), and 66.5% for organic N degradation and synthesis (Table S6). The distributions of genomic indicators were consistent with metagenomics analysis of mangrove sediment in a previous study.30 The gene families involved in the denitrification (nap, nos, nar, nor, nik) were detected in 104 isolates, and the major denitrifying taxa included Streptomyces, Agrobacterium, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Shewanella, and Klebsiella (Figure S3 and Table S6). The indicator gene families of organic N degradation (ureABC, nmo), organic N synthesis (gs, glnA), and DNRA (nirBD) were detected in most MMGs (Figure S3 and Table S6), while intra-genome and inter-genome redundancies of these indicator genes varied across dominant genera (Figure 4). Besides, we identified the necessary indicator gene family for nitrogen fixation (nifDHKW) in 48 MMGs, 15 of which were affiliated with Novosphingobium and 4 of which were affiliated with Klebsiella. High intro-genome redundancies of Novosphingobium and Klebsiella in nifDHKW indicated their potential for promoting plant growth (Figure 4). However, no gene associated with anammox was detected in MMGs. These results suggest that mangrove isolates showed high functional potential in nitrate reduction and nitrogen fixation.(3)S cycle. A total of 13,561 genes involved in the S cycle were identified in a majority of MMGs (Table S6), largely mapped to organic sulfur transformation (30.3%), the link between inorganic and organic sulfur transformation (22.0%), and assimilatory sulfate reduction (ASR, 20.4%). Especially, the complete pathway of ASR was identified in the genera Klebsiella, Bacillus, and Kosakonia (Figure S4). Besides, key genes for SOX systems of oxidizing thiosulfate to sulfate were mainly affiliated with class Alphaproteobacteria and Gammaproteobacteria, 11 of which had the potential to participate in full pathway of SOX systems (Figure S5). Notably, the indicator gene family phsABC for sulfur disproportionation was only detected in 9 mangrove isolates, including Klebsiella, Shewanella, and Kosakonia (Figures 4 and S4). We further observed that genes for sulfur oxidation and sulfur reduction were dominated by Streptomyces, Klebsiella, and Shewanella (Figure S4 and Table S6). MMGs also have abundant genes involved in the link between inorganic and organic sulfur transformation, including cysEKMO and metABCXYZ (Figures 4 and S4). Considering the major taxa involved in CH4, N, and S cycles, it is noted that the denitrifying taxa Streptomyces, Pseudomonas, Shewanella, Klebsiella, and Agrobacterium play a vital role in coupling these three geochemical cycles in the mangrove ecosystem (Figure S5).

The indicator genes for plant growth-promoting properties of bacterial isolates

We observed that 20.42% (48/235) of mangrove-associated isolates possess the key indicator gene nif for nitrogen fixation, including Novosphingobium, Klebsiella, Shewanella, Draconibacterium, and Mangrovibacter, indicating their potential to be PGPR. We further investigated other plant growth-promoting traits in mangrove isolates by a genome-based approach, including indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) synthesis, phosphate solubilization, and siderophore production. Firstly, 765 indicator genes related to four major tryptophan (Trp)-dependent IAA synthesis pathways were detected in MMGs, 14 of which could potentially de novo synthesize IAA from Trp through the tryptamine pathway or indole-3-pyruvate (IPA) pathway (Table S7). Novosphingobium, Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and Klebsiella owned the key genes coding aminotransferases (Figure S6), which function to convert Trp to IPA (IPA-ipdC|step1) (Table S7). Besides, the indicator gene ipdC was detected in Acinetobacter and Klebsiella, indicating their ability to decarboxylate IPA to indole-3-acetaldehyde (IAAld) (IPA-ipdC|step2), while genes involved in transforming IAAld to IAA were detected in Microbacterium, Agrobacterium, and Klebsiella (Table S7). Notably, two Alphaproteobacteria were capable of transforming IPA to IAA through the YUCCA pathway (IPA-YUCCA|step3) that existed in plants. These findings indicated that the vast majority of mangrove isolates were capable of synthesizing IAA despite the presence of genes for Trp utilization. Secondly, the indicator gene family ent for siderophore synthesis was majorly identified in Streptomyces, Pseudomonas, Agrobacterium, Acinetobacter, and Klebsiella, while most of the MMGs contained indicator gene families related to iron-siderophore transport (fhu, fep, ycl, flu) and iron (III) transport (afu) (Table S8 and Figure S6). Thirdly, indicator gene families related to P-starvation response regulation (pho), phosphate transport (pst), and inorganic P solubilization (ppa and ppk1) were found to be common in MMGs, especially in classes Alphaproteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, and Bacteroidia, suggesting their high potential to mobilize inorganic P in conditions of P starvation (Table S8 and Figure S6). Alkaline and acid phosphatases could catalyze phosphomonoester hydrolysis (which accounted for up to 90% of the organic P in soil) and are frequently characterized by their activity at optimal pH.31 We observed that the indicator gene family pho encoding alkaline phosphatases was widely detected in MMGs, likely due to the typically acidic nature of the mangrove sediments (Figure S6). Additionally, gene family phn capable of lysing P-C bond were mainly identified in Proteobacteria, including genera Agrobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Klebsiella, suggesting functional diversity of mangrove isolates in mineralizing organophosphate.

We then investigated the intersection of PGP-related genes in 235 MMGs, aiming to identify isolates with multiple PGP potentials. All MMGs contained at least three PGP traits (Figure S6). Notably, 48 MMGs harbored genes for nitrogen fixation and also carried genes related to phosphate transport, phosphate response, and P solubilization, owing to the nitrogen fixation process, which requires cellular ATP.32 Among the 75 mangrove isolates we obtained and cultured, 22 isolates contained genes involved in nitrogen fixation, 66 isolates had indicator genes related to IAA production, and 58 isolates carried indicator genes associated with siderophore synthesis (Figure S7). By integrating the PGP-related genes analysis of the 75 mangrove isolates, the strains that showed at least three or more PGP traits were recommended, and only strains with relatively faster growth rates were considered for the subsequent selection. From the 75 strains, we selected 7 strains from the dominant genera Agrobacterium, Pseudomonas, and Klebsiella, and 4 strains from rare genera Pseudacidovorax, Rhizobium, Allorhizobium, and Kosakonia to test PGP traits by traditional physiological function analysis. The results revealed that 11 isolates showed the physiological function for nitrogen fixation, followed by 6, 2, and 4 strains with physiological and genotypic potential for IAA production, siderophore synthesis, and solubilizing inorganic P, respectively (Figure 5 and Table S9). Discussion

Diverse and novel bacteria cultured from mangrove sediments

Bacteria inhabiting mangrove sediments are pivotal for the biogeochemical cycles of nitrogen, carbon, and sulfur in the mangrove ecosystems.33,34 The present study based on the culturable approach identified Gammaproteobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria as the dominant classes in mangrove sediments, which was relevant to our previous study based on the culture-independent method.7 In the present study, 163 bacterial strains were isolated from the mangrove sediments, and there were 53 putative novel species, thus reflecting highly diverse and novel bacteria harbored in the mangrove ecosystem with extreme environments such as high salinity, low oxygen, and periodic tides.34 It was well worth it that most putative novel species were isolated from the MA or 10% MA medium; this finding was not surprising as many previous studies have indicated that MA was more efficient in isolating and selecting putative novel species from marine environments.27,35,36 Moreover, traditional cultural analysis in this study could provide more available bacterial strains to understand their putative functions and be further used for plant growth in mangrove restoration.37 Recently, many studies have been carried out to explore the culturable bacterial diversity and detect a vast number of bacterial genera and species from mangroves, such as Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Halomonas, Mangrovibacter, and Mangrovibacterium.2,23,38,39,40

To further investigate mangrove-associated isolates at genome-scale resolution, we sequenced and assembled 75 microbial genomes of culturable mangrove-related bacteria, along with 160 genomes of mangrove-associated bacteria downloaded from the NCBI database, finally generating the mangrove microbial catalog. A total of 235 mangrove isolates were characterized by the predominance of the classes Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, Actinomycetia, Bacteroidia, and Bacilli, representing 106 bacterial genera and 118 species. Previous studies have reported bacterial isolates from other typical blue carbon ecosystems, including seagrasses and corals.27,41 The seagrass-related isolates were affiliated with 49 genera, mainly belonging to the classes Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, and the phylum Actinomycetota,41 while coral-related isolates were affiliated with 58 genera, mainly belonging to the classes Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, and Flavobacteria.27 These results suggested that dominant species vary across blue carbon ecosystems, with isolates from mangrove ecosystems exhibiting high phylogenetic diversity.

Ecological function of mangrove-associated bacteria based on genomic indicator

The mangrove sediment was characterized as carbon (C)-rich, sulfur (S)-rich, and nitrogen (N)-limited properties, and the microbes in mangrove sediment have substantially contributed greatly to the C, N, and S cycles.33 Several studies have put efforts into deciphering the microbial functionality related to the mangrove biogeochemical cycles by culture-independent methods.42,43 However, the function of culturable bacteria from the mangrove ecosystem remains unclear. Here, the putative metabolic functions of these isolates assigned by KEGG suggested that the metabolisms of carbohydrates, amino acids, cofactors, and vitamins were ubiquitous in all isolates from mangroves. These results support the crucial role of microbes in carbon cycling and the supply of small molecules, such as vitamins and amino acids.33 Besides, the genera Novosphingobium and Acinetobacter showed a unique capacity to degrade environmental pollution. It has been reported that most of the genus Novosphingobium enables degradation of a wide range of industrial pollutants and environmental contaminants such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons,38,44 and the genus Acinetobacter was well known for its capacity to biodegrade hydrocarbons.45 Notably, a large number of indicator genes involved in methanogenesis and methane oxidation were found in these isolates. Methanogenesis (CH4 production) and methanotrophy (CH4 oxidation) are two key microbial processes determining methane emission from soil to the atmosphere in mangrove wetlands.46 It is worth noting that we found Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, and Klebsiella may be involved in methane-cycling,30,47 and this finding needs follow-up exploration.

Besides, our results found that mangrove isolates had high functional potentials related to nitrate reduction and nitrogen fixation. These isolates contain indicator genes for nitrate reduction that could avoid large accumulations of artificial nitrogen inputs to pollute the mangrove environment,48 while nitrogen fixation by these bacteria contains essential indicator genes that could contribute available nitrogen for mangrove plant growth in a nitrogen-limited environment.49 Notably, 15 strains affiliated with Novosphingobium contain a complete nif-gene cluster, suggesting that Novosphingobium may be a key diazotrophic taxon in mangrove wetlands, which was consistent with our previous study.19 Moreover, our study also revealed a large number of bacterial isolates involved in the sulfur cycle in the mangrove ecosystem. Especially, 11 of these isolates may participate in thiosulfate oxidation via a complete SOX system. Mangrove sediments are anoxic and sulfidic zones, and the sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB) could oxidize the toxic sulfide (H2S) produced by sulfur-reducing bacteria (SRB), playing important roles in driving sulfur transformations in the mangrove wetlands.50 Our previous study51 and previous reports11,52 consistently found that the SOB and SRB groups were the dominant diazotrophic community, which were able to use nitrite, thiosulfate, and organic C compounds as electron donors, with sulfate, nitrate, and thiosulfate serving as electron acceptors, driving a strong coupling between the cycling of C, N, and S.33 In the present study, the bacterial isolates, including Streptomyces, Pseudomonas, Shewanella, Klebsiella, Vibrio, and Agrobacterium, acted as vital species in coupling the CH4, N, and S cycles, consistently revealing the compact connection among these geochemical cycles in the mangrove ecosystem. However, it should also be acknowledged that this study is currently limited to the functional genomic indicator level and was only a preliminary functional study, which needs further work by integrating bacterial physiology and metabolism in the future.

PGPR as indicators for evaluating the health condition of mangrove plants

PGPR in mangrove ecosystems was observed in various types, such as nitrogen fixers, phosphate solubilizers, siderophore synthesizers, phytohormone producers, and anoxygenic photosynthetic bacteria.37 The study of PGPR in mangrove ecosystems is still in its infancy, especially since their genomic indicator, potential function, and application in mangrove restoration are rarely known. Our previous study provided important insight into the application of keystone PGPR (a diazotrophic strain, Novosphingobium sp.) for microbiome manipulation in mangrove restoration.19 In this study, we provided further comprehensive insights into the PGP functions (physiological and genotypic indicators) of these isolates from the mangrove ecosystem. Firstly, almost 20% of these isolated bacterial strains showed potential nitrogen fixation capability by detecting the indicator gene nif cluster encoding nitrogenase, as well as the physiological ability for nitrogen fixation, suggesting that nitrogen fixers were widely distributed in the nitrogen-deficient mangrove ecosystem and contributed substantially to the nitrogen uptake of mangrove plants.3,53 Secondly, our study found that organic phosphorus mineralization or hydrolysis-related indicator genes (phn and pho gene family) and phosphate transporter (pst gene family) were abundant in the mangrove-associated bacteria. As soluble phosphate is an essential nutrient for plant growth and health, mangrove plants could benefit from sediment microbes that have strong organic phosphorus utilization ability.54 For example, the genus Klebsiella exhibited a great ability to transport iron, nitrate, and phosphate, which were key characteristics reflecting the good plant-microbe symbiotic relationships.55 Moreover, other indicator genes for PGP traits, such as IAA and siderophore production, were also widely distributed in bacteria, playing important roles in plant growth regulations.56 Notably, almost all of these isolated bacterial strains showed at least two or more PGP traits by both physiological tests and genotypic analysis, such as Novosphingobium, Klebsiella, and Agrobacterium. These PGPR that exhibit several different activities simultaneously have great potential for utilization as biofertilizers in mangrove restoration.32,57 Thus, PGPR (e.g., nitrogen fixers) could be suggested as important bacterial indicators of the health condition of mangrove plants and functional restoration in mangrove wetlands.8

In summary, our study expanded the number of culturable bacteria by 163 isolates from the mangrove sediments, 51 of which were putative novel species, and two novel genera were detected. Furthermore, we outlined 75 genomes of the bacterial isolates and also collected 160 genomes of mangrove-associated isolates from the NCBI. This study could provide an in-depth functional genomic view into the culturable bacteria from the mangrove ecosystem. To our knowledge, this may be the widest study about culturable bacteria and their functional genomic indicators from mangroves so far. The beneficial interaction between these culturable bacteria and the mangrove plants was fully clarified in the study, as shown in Figure 6. Almost all these mangrove-associated isolates were involved in various ways that promote mangrove plant growth, including carbohydrates, amino acids, vitamins, siderophores, and phosphate solubilization. Notably, some bacterial species, such as Enterobacterales, Pseudomonadales, and Rhizobiales, may be the dominant putative biogeochemical linkers driving the coupling of the C, N, and S cycles in the C-rich, S-rich, and N-limited mangrove sediments (Figure 6). Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the ecological function of mangrove-associated cultivable bacteria from a genomic indicator perspective. The number of described culturable bacteria from mangroves was increased by 163 isolates, 51 of which were putative novel species and two from novel genera. Furthermore, an integrated genomic catalog of 235 mangrove-associated bacteria was created by both culturing techniques and collection from the database. The genomic indicators analysis suggested that almost all of these 235 isolates had their own putative ecological functions to support mangrove plant growth. The key bacterial groups, such as Pseudomonadales and Rhizobiales, were vital biogeochemical linkers connecting the carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur cycles in mangrove ecosystems, which indicated the ecological health of mangrove wetlands. In short, bacterial isolation combined with functional genomic indicator analysis should be a practical pattern for screening beneficial bacterial taxa as biofertilizers in mangrove restoration.

Limitations of the study

Our findings provide evidence suggesting that the mangrove-associated cultivable bacteria play irreplaceable key roles in maintaining the ecological functions of the mangrove wetlands. Although bacterial isolation and genomic analysis demonstrated that mangrove-associated bacteria can benefit mangrove plants through various strategies, such as nitrogen fixation and phosphate solubilization. However, the genomic analysis is still limited in confirming their function. Further studies concerning the bacterial inoculation effects on mangrove plant growth will be necessary to reveal the underlying mechanisms connecting the culturable bacteria to mangrove plants, and ultimately evaluate the ecological function of these mangrove-associated bacteria in mangrove wetlands.

Method details

Isolation and identification of bacterial strains

The mangrove sediment samples were collected from the riverbank of Sanya River (18.9455 N, 109.3048E) in October 2020, which was a riverine mangrove located at the southernmost Hainan Island. To broaden the diversity of the isolated strains in mangrove sediments, we collected 16 surface sediment (depth of 0–10 cm, volume of 10 cm3) samples from four representative mangrove plants, including one exotic species (Sonneratia apetala) and three native species (Aegiceras corniculatum, Lumnitzera racemosa, and Rhizophora apiculata). The mean temperature, pH, and salinity for the sampling location were 26.5°C, 7.05, and 5.16‰, respectively, which were reported in detail in our previous studies.7

Three types of culture media were used: 2216 marine broth agar (MA) and 10% 2216 marine broth agar (10% MA) for cultivating the copiotrophic and oligotrophic bacterial groups, respectively, and actinomycetes isolation agar (AIA) for selecting the marine actinomycetes. The sediments were thoroughly mixed to ensure a representative sample, and then 1.0 g of fresh sediments was diluted to 10−1, 10−2, and 10−3 in sterile artificial seawater, and sediment dilutions (100 μL) were spread onto agar plates.67,68 These plates were incubated at 28 °C for up to 7 days, and colonies were selected based on morphology and color. Then the single colony was selected and purified by plate-streaking methods. The bacterial isolates were frozen in a 20% glycerol solution and stored at −80 °C for long-term storage.

The bacterial DNA was extracted by the E.Z.N.A. Bacterial DNA Kit (Omega, USA). The bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer sets 27F/1492R. The obtained 16S rRNA gene sequences were compared with related sequences in the EzBioCloud database. Then, a phylogenetic tree was created using MEGA-X software with the neighbor-joining (NJ) method to further confirm the taxonomic status of these isolates. The phylogenetic trees were constructed with the bootstrap resampling method, and the trees were uploaded to iTOL for visualization and formatting.66

PGP properties of 11 selected mangrove strains, including nitrogen fixation, IAA synthesis, phosphate solubilization, and siderophore production, were analyzed as described in our previous study.19 The nitrogen fixation rate of these isolates was obtained using the acetylene reduction method, IAA production was determined using the colorimetric method, phosphate solubilization was measured by molybdenum antimony resistance colorimetry, and siderophore production was analyzed by quantitative chrome azurol S assays.

Mangrove-associated isolates collection

We collected mangrove-associated microbes in the NCBI database using the keywords “(mangrove isolated microbes OR mangrove derived microbes)”. A total of 167 available genomes with genome lengths between 1 Mb and 10 Mb were downloaded from the NCBI database using the NCBI Datasets command-line tools.69 The detailed information of 167 genomes is listed in Table S10.

Whole-genome sequencing and assembly of isolates

Bacterial genomes used for analysis were sequenced or downloaded from NCBI. The genomic DNA was extracted using a TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen) and then sequenced by BGI using the DNBSEQ-T7 platform with 150-bp paired-end reads. Low-quality raw reads, with consecutive bases covered by fewer than five reads, were discarded. The sequenced reads were assembled using the unicycler (v0.5.0) software.58 Genome data were stored in the CNSA (CNP0006757) database.

Genomic and phylogenetic analysis

The completeness and contamination of 167 available genomes and in-house genomes were evaluated using the ‘lineage_wf’ module of CheckM (v1.2.1).59 A total of 235 assemblies with completeness ≥95% and contamination ≤5% were considered high-quality genomes and retained, including 160 available genomes and 75 in-house genomes. The rRNA genes were identified using Infernal (v1.1.5)60 with models from the Rfam database (v14.10),70 and tRNA genes were identified using tRNAscan-SE (v2.0).61 Taxonomy annotation of the 235 isolates was performed using the ‘classify_wf’ module of the Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk, v2.0.0) with reference to GTDB release r207_v2.29 Isolates were assigned at the species level if the average nucleotide identity (ANI) to the closest GTDB-Tk representative genome was ≥95%. The isolates that were not given a species assignment were considered candidate novel taxa. The phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the 120 bacterial universal single-copy proteins using the ‘denovo_wf’ module of GTDB-Tk (v2.0.0) with the default parameters. The resulting tree was visualized with the ggtree (v3.13.0) package in R software (v4.2.1).

Gene function annotation

Genes of 235 assemblies were predicted using Prokka (v1.14.6).62 Genes were annotated against Non-Redundant Protein Database (NR) (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/db), Swiss-Prot,71 Uniref. 50,72 NCycDB,73 MCycDB,74 and SCycDB databases75 using the ‘blastx’ module (identity threshold of 85%) of DIAMOND (v2.1.8).63 The genes were annotated against the carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZy) database (v07262023)76 using DIAMOND (v2.1.8) with an e-value threshold of 1e-5. The predicted genes were searched against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway) using kofamscan (v1.3.0)64 with an e-value threshold of 1e-5. These genes were also annotated using the eggNOG-mapper65 and the eggNOG Orthologous Groups database (v5.0.2).77 The eggNOG database integrates functional annotations collected from several sources, including COG categories78 and GO databases.79

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (v4.2.1). Exact sample sizes (n) and what n represents are referenced in the corresponding subsections of the results section. All of the pairwise comparisons in this study were performed using two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum tests. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05 and annotated as ns (p ≥ 0.05), ∗ (p < 0.05), ∗∗ (p < 0.01), ∗∗∗ (p < 0.001), and ∗∗∗∗ (p < 0.0001). For data visualization, boxplots display the median (center line), interquartile range (box), and 1.5×IQR whiskers; individual points represent samples.
