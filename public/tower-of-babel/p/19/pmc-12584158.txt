Introduction

For patients with end stage renal disease (ESRD), kidney transplantation (KT) is consider the optimal form of renal replacement therapy because it is associated with extended life expectancy, improved quality of life, and is cost‐effective [1, 2]. As of August 2025, the United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) national waiting list for kidney transplantation had >91,000 candidates awaiting KT [3]. In 2024, 27,759 KTs were performed in the United States, including 21,341 from deceased donors. In the past 10 years, the total number of deceased donors KTs (DDKTs) performed in the United States has nearly doubled. Unfortunately, during this same period, the number of discarded kidneys has more than tripled. In 2024, 9275 kidneys recovered with the intent to transplant were not utilized, representing a nonutilization rate of 29.3% [3]. For high Kidney Donor Profile Index (KDPI) donor kidneys (KDPI > 85%), the nonutilization rate in 2024 was 74%.

The ongoing shortage of donor organs coupled with the escalating kidney nonutilization rate are two of the major challenges in organ transplantation today. With an increasing number of older donors, anatomic considerations have become an important cause of kidney nonuse [4, 5, 6, 7]. Kidneys from older donors that exhibit severe aortic atherosclerosis with hard ulcerative occlusive plaque extending into the renal artery may represent a contraindication to organ acceptance at many centers. There are a few reports describing eversion endarterectomy (EE) as a salvage procedure to permit successful DDKT in this setting [8, 9, 10]. Moreover, deceased donor kidneys with other negative features such as marginal renal functional capacity, abnormal biopsies, poor pump parameters, or delayed allocation represent a large proportion of organs doomed to nonutilization [4, 5, 6, 7, 11, 12, 13]. One approach to offset this dilemma has been the application of dual kidney transplantation (DKT), which has been shown to be effective when appropriate donors and recipients are chosen [11, 14, 15, 16]. However, this procedure may involve greater inherent anesthetic and surgical risks and is usually reserved for donor kidneys with favorable vascular anatomy. Herein we report our initial monocentric experience and short‐term outcomes with DKT from adult donors in which dual renal endarterectomy was performed prior to transplantation.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective chart review of all adult deceased donor DKTs performed at our center from 10/1/2001 to 3/1/2025. During this 23.5‐year study period, a total of 99 DKTs were performed, including five undergoing dual renal EE and two undergoing single EE (the latter two cases were excluded). Standardized donor and recipient selection and management algorithms were followed during the period of study and have been previously reported [10, 12, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Definitions

Expanded criteria donors (ECDs) were defined by the UNOS definition and standard criteria donors (SCDs) were defined as all donors not meeting ECD criteria. Donation after circulatory death (DCD) donors were defined as organ recovery following declaration of death by cardio‐circulatory arrest. Delayed graft function (DGF) was defined as the need for dialysis (for any reason) in the first week post‐KT. Renal allograft loss was defined as death with a functioning graft, allograft nephrectomy, resumption of dialysis, kidney retransplantation, or return to the pretransplant serum creatinine (SCr) level in a patient transplanted preemptively. KDPI was determined based on the UNET calculation at the time of kidney offer.

Indications for Dual Kidney Transplant

First and foremost, both kidneys need to be available, which usually implies refusal by multiple centers. Second, the host organ procurement organization has reached a decision that these are “hard‐to‐place” single kidneys and are willing to consider both an open offer and dual utilization in lieu of discard. There is no single factor that leads to this determination, but multiple factors are involved including donor age and quality, renal function, KDPI, type of donor (donation after brain death vs. circulatory death), kidney anatomy and biopsy findings, warm and cold ischemia times, pump parameters, logistics, and having the right recipient. Despite UNOS policies specifying criteria for expedited dual kidney allocation, in our experience very few donor kidneys are offered primarily as dual kidneys because most OPOs prefer to offer kidneys for single use initially to maximize utilization. Because it may take 4–6 h or longer post cross‐clamp to obtain serology, biopsy results, and pump parameters (at which time the allocation process proceeds), in most cases dual kidney allocation is usually not considered for at least 12 h following cross‐clamp. Contraindications‘’ for DKT at our center include the presence of disseminated intravascular coagulation or rhabdomyolysis in the donor, extremely large kidneys (>13 cm in length), presence of complex vascular or urologic anatomy (multiple vessels or ureters), glomerulosclerosis >35%, and projected prolonged cold ischemia time (CIT) because of logistical issues. Whenever possible, both kidneys were accepted by us on “full waivers,” which prevents incurring an organ acquisition charge if either of the kidneys were deemed unusable for any reason and appropriate documentation was provided. Many of these kidneys were targeted for discard in the absence of our utilization for DKT as a last resort. It is our current practice that when we are initially offered a single kidney from a marginal donor that is not appropriate for our patients high on the waiting list, we will inform the host organ procurement organization that we are interested in out‐of‐sequence allocation for either a single or dual kidney transplant.

Kidney Preservation

Whenever possible, donor kidneys were placed on machine preservation to minimize preservation injury, maintain functional reserve, and provide another means of assessment [12, 19]. Although pump parameters were not exclusively used to discard kidneys, a flow rate >60 mL/min and a resistance <0.50 mm Hg/mL/min after a minimum of 6 h on machine preservation were considered thresholds for DKT. If the kidneys were pumping well, CITs up to 30+ hours were considered acceptable. Because most of the kidneys were not initially offered as “2 for 1”, long CIT was common. Consequently, ex vivo assessment of the kidneys based on pump parameters was an important part of the management algorithm.

Histological Assessment

Donor kidney biopsy was used to assist in the evaluation of preexisting and terminal renal parenchymal injury. Renal cortical wedge biopsies for frozen section were performed at the time of organ recovery and evaluated for the presence and degree of glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, chronic interstitial inflammation, tubular atrophy, and vascular hyalinosis or sclerosis [20, 21, 22]. Moderate to severe vascular or interstitial changes, tubular atrophy, or glomerulosclerosis ≥35% were contraindications to kidney utilization. For paired kidney biopsies displaying disparate histological results, an average of the two biopsies was calculated to assist in the determination of dual kidney utilization or refusal.

Recipient Evaluation and Selection

At our center, no specific upper age limit was an absolute contraindication to DKT; the oldest DKT recipient in this case series was 72 years. With dual kidneys, recipient selection was usually out‐of‐sequence allocation based on older age (>50 years), smaller size (body mass index—BMI < 30 kg/m2), and identifying low immunological risk patients such as primary KT, human leukocyte antigen matching, low panel reactive antibody level (usually 0%), and informed consent [16, 18]. Age‐matching between donor and recipient was a consideration as we tried to avoid age mismatches >15 years. In addition, the predicted ability to tolerate a longer and more complicated operative procedure with possibly a higher incidence of DGF were important considerations. Other recipient characteristics unique to dual kidney consideration were adequate space in the pelvis (extremely small or short stature or presence of large polycystic kidneys were relative contraindications), favorable vascular anatomy (no significant iliac atherosclerosis), adequate bladder capacitance, no chronic anti‐coagulation (warfarin or clopidogrel) or history of thrombophilia, adequate cardiac function and reserve (ejection fraction >40%–50%, no atrial fibrillation or significant valvular disease), absence of either significant pulmonary hypertension or systemic hypotension, and no history of prior pelvic surgery or irradiation [16, 18].

Technique of EE and DKT

EE was performed by subintimal dissection of the Carrel aortic patch surrounding the renal artery followed by complete eversion and removal of the cast (Figures 1, 2, 3) [10]. Indications for EE were stenotic plaque involving the ostium with extension into the renal artery such that resecting the area of involvement would result in an extremely short artery (<1.5 cm in length) that would be difficult to implant. Patients received localized heparin intra‐operatively and aspirin 81 mg daily post‐operatively. KT duplex ultrasonography was performed on post‐operative Day #1 and whenever clinically indicated for monitoring purposes.

The atheroma is identified in the main renal artery distal to the Carrel aortic patch.

A plane is developed on the aortic patch for subintimal dissection.

The artery is everted. Dissection is continued circumferentially until the plaque is completely freed. The vessel is then carefully flushed with heparinized saline to wash out debris and check for any leaks or vessel wall disruption.

Dual kidneys were procured in the usual fashion, separated at the time of procurement, preserved as mentioned previously, and were then prepared separately on the back table and transplanted sequentially either through a bilateral extraperitoneal or unilateral extraperitoneal approach [16, 18]. Standard end‐to‐side vascular anastomoses to the iliac vessels were performed followed by separate dual anterior extravesical ureteroneocystostomies over two separate ureteral stents. Following confirmation of vascular patency and hemostasis, the kidneys were then positioned to avoid any torsion, twist, or tension to any of the vascular anastomoses. The DKT procedure usually lasted 1–2 h longer than a single KT.

Immunosuppression and Post‐Transplant Management

DKT recipients received depleting antibody induction with multi‐dose rabbit antithymocyte globulin (n = 1) or alemtuzumab 30 mg (n = 4, either intravenous or subcutaneous) as a single intra‐operative dose [10, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Maintenance immunosuppression consisted of tacrolimus, mycophenolic acid (360 mg twice daily in recipients 60 years and older), and tapering doses of steroids to 5 mg/d by 1‐month post‐transplant. Target 12‐h tacrolimus trough levels were 6–9 ng/mL for DKT recipients in the first 3 months and then 4–6 ng/mL thereafter in the absence of rejection.

All patients received surgical site prophylaxis with a first‐generation cephalosporin for 24 h, anti‐fungal prophylaxis with fluconazole for 1 month, and anti‐Pneumocystis prophylaxis with sulfamethoxazole‐trimethoprim (Dapsone if allergic to sulfa) for at least 12 months. Antiviral prophylaxis consisted of oral valganciclovir for 3–6 months, depending on donor and recipient cytomegalovirus serologic status. Specifics regarding drug dosing and duration have been published previously [10, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Because of two ureteral implantations into the bladder, the urethral catheter usually remained in place for a minimum of 5 days. Post‐transplant renal allograft function was evaluated by measuring SCr levels as well as calculating glomerular filtration rate (GFR) using the abbreviated Modification of Diet in Renal Disease formula.

Statistical Analysis

Data were compiled from both prospective and retrospective databases, with confirmation by medical record review in accordance with Institutional Review Board guidelines and approval. Categorical data were summarized as proportions and percentages, and continuous data were summarized as means and standard deviations. Endpoints included patient survival as well as uncensored graft survival. Other study endpoints included DGF and 6‐month renal allograft function (based on SCr level and estimated GFR).

Results

From 11/01/2001 to 3/01/2025, a total of 99 DKTs were performed at our center, including five undergoing dual renal artery EE. All five cases were accepted on anatomic waivers, technically successful, and resulted in DKT. Of the five cases, three were from ECDs, three from DCD donors, and two from SCDs. Case #1: The patient underwent unilateral DKT to the right iliac fossa in April 2019. The right kidney had a single artery, and the left kidney had two arteries spaced 2.5 mm apart on a common patch. Both kidneys exhibited stenotic plaque extending beyond the ostia for which EEs were performed prior to implantation. Given the proximal bifurcation of the right common iliac vessels, both kidneys were anastomosed to the right external iliac vessels, with the left kidney being implanted first proximally and the right kidney distally. Both kidneys reperfused slowly with excellent renal arterial pulses but the parenchyma appeared swollen. Given the prolonged CIT and overall appearance, dual capsulotomies were performed to improve cortical perfusion. Operating time was 5.5 h and estimated blood loss was 750 mL. The patient tolerated the procedure well, did not require any transfusions, and experienced immediate graft function with an excellent diuresis. He was discharged on post‐operative Day 3 with a SCr level of 2.6 mg/dL, averaging 4.6 L of urine output daily. SCr level had further decreased to 1.4 mg/dL at the time of his first post‐transplant clinic visits on post‐operative Day 6. By post‐operative Day 10, SCr level had further decreased to 1.1 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 61 mL/min/1.73m2. At 1‐year post‐DKT, the SCr level was 1.5 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 45 mL/min/1.73m2. At 3‐year follow‐up, the patient's kidney function gradually declined, and he was placed back on hemodialysis at 41 months post‐DKT, secondary to acute tubular necrosis. Shortly thereafter he was diagnosed with invasive squamous cell carcinoma of the scalp and experienced an episode of cardiac arrest secondary to unstable atrial fibrillation and electrolyte disturbances. He was eventually transitioned to comfort care and died at 43 months post‐DKT and 2 months after resuming dialysis.

Case #2: The patient underwent unilateral DKT to the right iliac fossa in October 2020. The right kidney was not pumped due to lack of an adequate aortic patch and hard renal artery plaque that prevented straight cannulation. The right kidney had two arteries, and the left kidney had a single artery. Both kidneys were noted to have moderate stenotic plaque that extended beyond the arterial orifices for which EEs were performed on both kidneys prior to implantation. The left kidney was implanted first and anastomosed to the right common iliac vessels, and the right kidney was implanted second and anastomosed to the right external iliac vessels. Both kidneys reperfused well with excellent arterial pulses. Operating time was 4.0 h and estimated blood loss was 300 mL. The patient tolerated the procedure well but did experience DGF and received a single hemodialysis treatment on post‐operative Day 3 for uremia. By post‐operative Day 4, the patient's SCr level had decreased to 5.0 mg/dL, and he was averaging 2 L of urine output daily. He was discharged on post‐operative Day 7 and the SCr level was 3.2 mg/dL at his first post‐transplant clinic appointment on post‐operative Day 9. The SCr level steadily declined and was 1.1 mg/dL at 1.5‐month follow‐up with an estimated GFR of 79 mL/min/1.73m2. SCr level at 1‐year post‐transplant was 1.4 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 55 mL/min/1.73m2, and the patient remains stable at 57 months post‐DKT.

Case #3: The patient underwent unilateral DKT to the right iliac fossa in July 2021. Both kidneys had single arteries and were noted to have moderate stenotic plaque that extended beyond the arterial ostia for which EEs were performed on both kidneys prior to implantation. The left kidney was implanted first and anastomosed to the right common iliac vessels, and the right kidney was implanted second and anastomosed to the right external iliac vessels. Both kidneys reperfused well with excellent arterial pulses. Operating time was 4.0 h and estimated blood loss was 150 mL. The patient tolerated the procedure well. By post‐operative Day 1, the patient's SCr level was decreasing but she experienced DGF and received a single hemodialysis treatment for hyperkalemia. By post‐operative Day 4, the patient's SCr level had dropped to 3.1 mg/dL, and she was averaging 1.6 L of urine output daily. She was discharged on post‐operative Day 5 and the SCr level was 2.1 mg/dL the following day in clinic. Six months post‐transplant, the SCr level was 1.97 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 28 mL/min/1.73m2. At 1‐year post‐transplant, the SCr level was 1.5 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 40 mL/min/1.73m2 and the patient continues to do well at 48 months follow‐up.

Case #4: The patient underwent bilateral DKT in January 2025. Both kidneys had single arteries with hard stenotic plaque extending beyond the arterial orifices, for which EEs were performed on both kidneys prior to implantation. Given the patient's small body habitus and inability to fit both kidneys in the right pelvis, the decision was made to perform bilateral DKT implantation by anastomosing the right kidney to the right external iliac vessels and then the left kidney to the left external iliac vessels through bilateral Gibson incisions using an extraperitoneal approach. Both kidneys reperfused well with excellent arterial pulses. Operating time was 5.7 h and estimated blood loss was 300 mL. The patient tolerated the procedure well. By post‐operative Day 1, the SCr level was decreasing, and he was averaging 2.2 L of urine output daily. He was discharged on post‐operative Day 6 with a SCr level of 2.5 mg/dL. At his 2‐month post‐transplant clinic visit, the SCr level had dropped to 1.1 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 72 mL/min/1.73m2. At 6 months follow‐up, the SCr level was 1.36 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 58 mL/min/1.73 m2.

Case #5: The patient underwent bilateral DKT in February 2025. Both kidneys had single arteries with stenotic plaque that extended beyond the arterial ostia for which EEs were performed prior to implantation. Given the patient's narrow pelvis and inability to implant both kidneys in the right pelvis, the decision was made to perform bilateral DKT by anastomosing the right kidney to the right external iliac vessels followed by the left kidney to the left external iliac vessels through bilateral Gibson incisions using an extraperitoneal approach. Both kidneys reperfused well with excellent arterial pulses. Operating time was 7.0 h and estimated blood loss was 300 mL. The patient tolerated the procedure well but experienced DGF and oliguria requiring hemodialysis treatments on post‐operative Days 2, 3, and 5. She was discharged on post‐operative Day 5 with a SCr level of 7.2 mg/dL. At her 2‐month post‐transplant clinic visit, her SCr level had dropped to 1.1 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 51 mL/min/1.73m2. At 6 months post‐transplant the SCr level is 1.65 mg/dL with an estimated GFR of 34 mL/min/1.73m2.

Discussion

The persistent disparity between organ supply and demand challenges the transplant community to maximize and optimize the use of organs from all consented donors. In the new millennium, the recovery of kidneys from “marginal” and older donors has become increasingly accepted as a method to augment the organ supply [1, 4, 23]. Unfortunately, because of concerns raised regarding inadequate renal functional capacity and the projected limited life span of some of these kidneys, the kidney nonutilization rate has steadily increased and proportionately has outpaced the rate of increase in kidneys recovered with the intent to transplant [1]. Projected poor renal function in a donor is second only to kidney biopsy findings as the main reasons given for kidney non‐use and represents the number one reason given for kidney non‐recovery [4, 5, 6, 7, 11, 13, 24, 25].

Growing acceptance and use of donor kidneys with limited renal functional capacity have been tempered by concerns that these kidneys have an increased susceptibility to ischemia‐reperfusion injury leading to higher rates of primary nonfunction, DGF, and acute rejection, all of which may impact resource utilization, kidney graft survival and projected life span [24, 25, 26]. In addition, kidneys from older donors have a reduced repair capacity and display increased immunogenicity [27, 28]. These concerns have fueled initiatives to qualitatively assess marginal donor kidneys prior to KT by examining associated risk factors, refining selection criteria, and using ex vivo biopsies and machine preservation parameters to predict outcome [12, 16, 19, 20, 21, 22, 24]. An unintended consequence of these initiatives has been an increased risk of kidney discard associated with either the use of kidney biopsy (because of histopathological findings) or machine preservation (because of suboptimal pump parameters) assessments. In an effort to utilize donor kidneys that are not considered suitable for single KT, the application of DKT has evolved in lieu of organ discard [11, 14, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21, 22]. Multiple studies of short and long‐term success with DKT have been reported since the mid‐1990's, at which time the primary indication for DKT was insufficient nephron mass. At present, the decision to perform a DKT versus either a single kidney or discard both kidneys is multifactorial and usually occurs when both kidneys have been refused for single KT by multiple centers and have accrued prolonged CIT. In our experience, only a minority of DKTs actually occur in the setting of intentional dual kidney allocation according to UNOS criteria. Another confounding factor is that many ECD or high KDPI donor kidney offers meet the UNOS criteria for dual kidney allocation, yet the optimal use of kidneys from a utilitarian perspective is to perform two single KTs instead of one DKT.

In addition to donor organ renal functional capacity, biopsy findings, and poor pump parameters, another risk factor for kidney nonutilization is abnormal kidney anatomy, including the presence of severe renal artery atherosclerosis. In 1992, Nghiem and Choi reported on 34 donor kidneys that required backbench EE prior to engraftment [8]. This study noted an acceptable graft survival rate of 76.4% with a mean SCr level of 2.3 mg/dL at a mean follow‐up of 4 years. Utilization of these grafts expanded the center's KT volume by 18% per annum. There was no increase in the risk of hypertension, and no vascular complications were noted. The procedure was shown to be safe and effective in this single center study cohort.

In 2006, Sagban and colleagues reported on renal artery thrombo‐endarterectomy in 10% (115/1134) of KT cases because of intrinsic atherosclerotic plaque causing renal artery stenosis [9]. In an additional 23 cases, the length of plaque precluded safe EE, and a saphenous vein interposition bypass graft was placed after resecting the involved segment of renal artery. Mean donor age was 61 years, and the 5‐year graft survival rate was 84.3%. In 2018, we reported our initial experience with EE as a method of graft salvage in 17 patients undergoing KT [10]. All cases were technically successful and resulted in KT. Most of the kidneys were imported from other donor service areas, suggesting that multiple centers had refused these kidneys prior to acceptance at our center and probably would have been discarded in the absence of our rescue utilization. Many transplant programs regard severe donor aortic and renal artery atherosclerosis as a surrogate marker for intra‐renal atherosclerosis and intrinsic renal parenchymal disease. Consequently, in this setting, centers are reluctant to consider these kidneys for transplantation.

The majority of these kidneys were from ECDs or donors with a high KDPI (mean 81 ± 14%), suggesting a burden of atherosclerosis related to advanced age, longstanding hypertension, or diabetes that may influence renal recovery and outcomes.

In cases with isolated ostial stenosis (without significant plaque extension into the renal artery), the preferred reconstruction technique at most centers is to merely cut the renal artery off the aorta and perform the anastomosis similar to a living donor KT. We reserve EE only for those cases in which the involved segment of renal artery is such that resection distal to the plaque would leave a short artery, which would then be difficult to implant. This is particularly relevant in the setting of DKT, in which longer vessel length provides more options for safe and successful implantation of both kidneys. It is interesting to note that in our original study, the 3‐year patient and graft survival rates (76.5%) were near‐identical to the outcomes reported by Nghiem and Choi [8, 10].

In summary, the critical shortage of donor kidneys compounded by the increasing rate of kidney nonutilization defines two of the major challenges in organ transplantation today. Dual renal artery EE in the setting of DKT appears to be a safe and under‐utilized procedure that may prevent discard of marginal donor kidneys and is associated with acceptable short‐term outcomes. The presence of severe atherosclerosis with partially occlusive plaque extending into the renal artery should not be considered an absolute contraindication to DKT, even in the setting of unacceptable biopsy findings and pump parameters. Appropriate patient selection is paramount to optimally match the functional capacity of the kidneys with the estimated demand in the recipient. Long‐term follow‐up is needed to determine the utility of this unconventional technique in expanding the donor pool.
