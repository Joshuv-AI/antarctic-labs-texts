Associations of nutritional status and oral health-related quality of life with early childhood caries of children aged 4–5 years: a hospital-based cross-sectional analytical study
Authors: Harshani Nadeeshani, Malinda Narada, Sadhisha Gunawardena, Sudam De Alwis, Chathumini Dias, Sanath Thushara Kudagammana, Chandra Herath, Ruwan Jayasinghe, Ruvini Liyanage
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegular growth and development during early childhood can be hindered by different communicable diseases and chronic severe early childhood caries (S-ECC). Comprehensive studies on the factors associated with ECC, nutritional status, and oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) are scarce. Thus, this study aimed to investigate dietary, oral hygiene, and socio-demographic factors associated with these conditions and their impact on OHRQoL in a hospital-based cohort of Sri Lankan children.MethodsThis cross-sectional analytical study included 545 children aged 4–5 years attending the Pedodontic Clinic at the Dental Teaching Hospital, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka. After obtaining parental consent, data were collected using a pre-validated questionnaire, and oral examinations were conducted by three trained and calibrated dentists. Binary logistic regression analysis and negative binomial regression analysis were used to examine the effects of independent variables on nutritional status, ECC status, and OHRQoL.ResultsWithin this clinical sample, higher S-ECC severity assessed via the defs index and the consumption of some sugary snacks and sweets were significantly associated with poor nutritional status (P < 0.05). Conversely, higher weight-for-age, BMI-for-age, and maternal education levels were significantly associated with a lower likelihood of S-ECC. Adverse dietary habits (frequent consumption of sweetened snacks), prolonged breastfeeding (> 2 years), bottle-feeding, and inadequate oral hygiene practices (infrequent or unsupervised brushing and use of non-fluoridated toothpaste) were identified as key behavioral indicators significantly associated with increased S-ECC (P < 0.05). Furthermore, lower nutritional status and higher defs indices were strongly associated with poorer OHRQoL scores (P < 0.001).ConclusionsS-ECC and poor nutrition were prevalent among 4-5-year-olds, strongly associated with dietary habits, oral hygiene practices, and maternal education, while both conditions correlated with lower oral health-related quality of life. Importantly, higher ECC and poor nutrition were interrelated, highlighting the potential utility of integrated oral health and nutritional interventions in clinical pediatric settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41043-026-01407-6.

Background

Early childhood caries (ECC) is identified as the presence of one or more decayed teeth, missing teeth due to caries, or filled tooth surfaces in any primary tooth in a child aged 72 months or younger. Severe early childhood caries (S-ECC) is identified when a child aged 4 years has ≥ 5 decayed, missing, or filled tooth surfaces, and a 5-year-old child has ≥ 6 such surfaces [1]. Serious complications of ECC include tooth sensitivity to thermal changes, pain, fever, dental abscesses, difficulty chewing, prolonged food retention, and impaired brushing [2]. Oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) is defined as “a multidimensional construct that reflects people’s comfort when eating, sleeping, and engaging in social interactions; their self-esteem; and their satisfaction concerning their oral health” [3]. The Early Childhood Oral Health Impact Scale (ECOHIS) is a validated tool that captures oral health problems and treatment experiences that may affect the daily lives and overall well-being of children and their families [4].

ECC is increasingly recognized as a multifactorial condition influenced by biological, behavioral, dietary, and socioeconomic determinants. Dietary practices such as frequent sugar consumption, inadequate nutritional intake, and poor oral hygiene behaviors contribute to ECC development, while socioeconomic disadvantage may further exacerbate both poor oral health and malnutrition [5]. At the same time, ECC itself may negatively affect children’s nutritional status through pain, difficulty chewing, and impaired eating behaviors. These consequences may ultimately interfere with growth and development [6]. In addition, ECC-associated issues adversely affect children’s and families’ oral health-related quality of life. Therefore, ECC, nutritional status, and OHRQoL can be considered interconnected dimensions of child well-being rather than isolated health outcomes [7, 8].

Borderline nutritional status and ECC remain major health issues in low- and middle-income countries [9, 10]. In Sri Lanka, 60.7% of five-year-old children have been reported to have active dental caries, of which 96.2% remain untreated [11]. At the same time, stunting, wasting, and underweight affect 17.3%, 15.1%, and 20.5% of children under five years of age, respectively [12]. Globally, ECC affects a substantial proportion of preschool children, with reported estimates ranging from 20% to over 70%, reflecting considerable variability across regions and socioeconomic groups [13]. Poor nutritional status, including underweight, stunting, and wasting, contributes to increased morbidity, delayed growth, and impaired cognitive development [14, 15]. Published research indicates that ECC not only directly affects children’s oral health but also has broader implications for their quality of life and nutritional well-being [7]. Systematic reviews and population studies consistently show that greater ECC severity is significantly associated with poor OHRQoL in preschool children, affecting functional, psychological, and social domains measured by validated tools such as the ECOHIS, and intensifying negative impacts as caries increases in severity [16, 17].

Despite growing recognition of these interrelationships, relatively few studies have examined ECC, multiple anthropometric indicators for nutritional status, OHRQoL, dietary factors, oral hygiene behaviors, and sociodemographic variables together in preschool children [8, 18–20], and such comprehensive evidence is particularly limited in Sri Lanka [7, 21–23]. Furthermore, while many studies have focused on the impact of poor nutritional status on ECC, limited evidence is available on how ECC may, in turn, influence the nutritional status of children [24–26]. This study offers a novel approach by simultaneously evaluating all of these interconnected health domains within a single cohort, providing the first comprehensive analysis of how dental caries, nutritional status, behavioral factors, and quality of life concurrently impact preschool children in Sri Lanka.

The gaps in the literature also highlight the importance of the present study. Therefore, we hypothesized that higher ECC status is significantly associated with poor nutritional status among Sri Lankan children aged 4–5 years, as assessed by height-for-age (HA), weight-for-age (WA), weight-for-height (WH), body mass index-for-age (BMIA), mid-upper arm circumference-for-age (MUACA), head circumference-for-age (HCA), and triceps skinfold thickness-for-age (TSFTA), with the corresponding null hypothesis stating that no such association exists. In addition, we hypothesized that higher ECC status is significantly associated with poorer OHRQoL in this age group, with null hypotheses indicating no association. Overall, the primary objective of this study was to investigate the complex associations among nutritional status, S-ECC, and OHRQoL among Sri Lankan children within an integrated framework. Furthermore, this study aimed to identify the behavioral, environmental, and structural determinants affecting these primary outcomes, focusing on how dietary habits, socio-economic factors, and oral hygiene practices concurrently influence child health within the study sample.

Methods

Selection of the sample

A cross-sectional analytical study design was employed. The study was conducted at the teaching hospital of the Faculty of Dental Sciences, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka. The study population comprised children aged 4–5 years with severe, moderate, or no ECC who attended the dental clinic during the study period.

Children were excluded if they were taking medications known to affect salivary flow; had diagnosed systemic, genetic, or metabolic diseases causing secondary malnutrition (such as celiac disease, congenital heart defects, Type 1 diabetes, or severe chronic infections); had psychological impairments; exhibited congenital dental anomalies; had erupted permanent teeth; or were uncooperative during the clinical examination. The required sample size for assessing the incidence of the disease/condition was calculated using the following Cochran’s formula [27].

where n is the sample size, Z is the standard normal deviation (1.96 for 95% CI), P is 68.8% [28], and D is 5%. An additional 10% was added to account for potential non-response. Since the study was conducted in a hospital-based setting rather than through population-based random sampling, a conservative design effect of 1.5 was applied during sample size calculation to account for potential homogeneity among participants attending the same institution and to minimize underestimation of the required sample size [29]. Therefore, the final sample size was 545 ([330 + 33] × 1.5). Furthermore, the final sample size was sufficient for regression analyses, as the number of outcome events relative to the number of predictors met established recommendations for events-per-variable (EPV ≥ 10), supporting the robustness of the fitted models [30].

A systematic sampling technique was used for sampling. Every other child attending the Dental Teaching Hospital, Faculty of Dental Sciences, University of Peradeniya, for routine check-ups or dental treatment and fulfilling the inclusion criteria was enrolled in this cross-sectional analytical study during the period of January 2022 to November 2022. The Faculty of Dental Sciences was selected as the study site because it manages nearly all ECC conditions and offers a wide range of treatment modalities. Therefore, only a limited number of case scenarios were likely to be missed at enrollment, and almost all relevant cases were expected to be captured. Children registered at the Dental Teaching Hospital were subsequently enrolled in the study. This study was not pre-registered with the journal, however the study was pre-registered at the Faculty of Dental Sciences, University of Peradeniya, Sri Lanka, as a Master’s study. The study protocol was developed prior to data collection, and all planned objectives and analyses were followed as described, with no changes to the primary research questions after the study commenced. This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for observational studies. Obtaining informed consent

This study was designed and conducted following ethics review committee guidelines, following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki [31] and standards for professional conduct. The ethical clearance certificate (ERC/FDS/UOP/I/2021/45) was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee, Faculty of Dental Sciences, University of Peradeniya, on 12th October 2021. All information regarding the research was conveyed to parents or guardians. The consent was obtained only after parents or guardians had received and sufficiently understood all important information. Given that intraoral photographs were obtained, parents provided explicit, separate written consent authorizing the publication of clinical photographs in scientific journals. Additionally, formal child assent was obtained verbally from each preschooler before any clinical examination or photographic documentation, ensuring the child’s voluntary cooperation and comfort.

Clinical examination of the oral cavities

Clinical examination of oral cavities was carried out by only three trained and calibrated dentists, according to the World Health Organization criteria [32] of the four-point scale caries lesion extension [33]. The cavitated carious lesions were classified according to a 4-point scale: D1- clinically detectable enamel caries with a sound surface, inclusive of white and brown spot lesions, D2 - cavitated enamel caries, D3 - carious lesion involving dentin, D4 - lesions involving pulp [33].

Before data collection, examiner calibration was performed. Inter-examiner and intra-examiner reliability for caries diagnosis were assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic calculated in IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 25; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Kappa values ranged from 0.81 to 0.85, indicating substantial agreement. Dental examinations were carried out using a sterile plane dental mouth mirror and a blunt-tipped probe/explorer under white LED light, with participants seated upright, in accordance with World Health Organization diagnostic criteria. The caries status of the primary teeth of children was recorded using the deft (sum of decayed, extracted due to caries, and filled teeth) and the defs (sum of decayed, extracted due to caries, and filled surfaces) indices [32, 34]. According to the WHO diagnostic criteria, as decayed teeth, only cavitated teeth were counted, meaning that non-cavitated white or brown spot lesions are explicitly excluded from this category.

Determination of the nutritional status of children through anthropometric data

Anthropometric measurements of patients were taken using standard techniques. Each measurement was taken twice, and the mean was used for calculations. Data such as HA, WA, WH, BMIA, MUACA, HCA, and TSFTA of children were obtained using standard normative charts. The body weights were measured using a portable calibrated scale (T-79–100; Tsutsumi, Sanjo, Niigata, Japan) with 100 g precision, with the child standing barefoot in minimal clothing, and height was measured using a tape measure. TSFTA was measured using a skinfold caliper (Slim Guide; Body Care, Kenilworth, UK). The WHO child growth standards were used to evaluate the nutritional status of selected children. The child’s nutritional status was determined using Z-scores, where a Z-score below − 2 was considered indicative of poor nutritional status in terms of HA, WA, WH, BMIA, MUACA, HCA, and TSFTA [35, 36].

Collecting information through questionnaires

All enrolled children were interviewed using parent/guardian-administered questionnaires. Information about children’s demographics, oral and general health status, and eating habits was collected using a previously validated and published questionnaire [37], which was translated into Sinhala (the main language of Sri Lanka) and verified by an expert panel within the Ethics Review Committee. The dietary component operated as an adapted Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) designed to assess the habitual intake of core food groups and cariogenic items. To capture established dietary patterns, the FFQ structured food consumption frequencies into five standardized categorical responses: ‘more than 1 time/day’, ‘6–7 times/week’, ‘3–5 times/week’, ‘1–2 times/week’, and ‘fewer than 1 time/week or never’. While the tool was originally validated and implemented to assess socio-behavioral caries risk factors in preschool cohorts within a project framework [37], its application in the current study was adapted to align with the regional dietary habits of Sri Lankan preschoolers. Content and face validity were verified by a panel of local pediatric dental experts before administration. The validated ECOHIS was used to assess the OHRQoL of children [22].

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 25; IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses. The first primary outcome was dental caries, which was subdivided into the following three levels of severity: mild (scores from 1 to 3), moderate (scores from 4 to 5), and severe ECC (scores similar to or above 6) [38]. Based on the defs index, the severity of dental caries was subdivided into ECC (defs < 6) and S-ECC (defs ≥ 6) [39]. The term S-ECC refers to the atypical, progressive, acute, or rampant pattern of decay, which is taken as defs ≥ 6 in this study [39]. The associations between caries experience and each of the independent variables were evaluated.

The second primary outcome was nutritional status assessed by HA, WA, WH, BMIA, MUACA, HCA, and TSFTA. The associations between nutritional status and each of the independent variables were evaluated. The third primary outcome was OHRQoL assessed by the child impact section (CIS), family impact section (FIS), and overall ECOHIS scores. The associations between OHRQoL and each of the independent variables were evaluated.

The independent variables in each dependent variable category included nutritional status, ECC status, demographics, eating habits, oral health, and general health. The statistical analyses included descriptive information and frequency distributions. Binary logistic regression analysis (using the enter method) was used to evaluate the association of independent variables on dichotomous dependent variables (e.g., presence of dental caries, dichotomized as “yes” or “no”). Dental caries severity was measured using two levels of severity of deft and defs: defs < 6 and defs ≥ 6. This single, conservative cutoff was selected to maintain statistical homogeneity and prevent the artificial inflation of S-ECC cases, given that the study sample was tightly clustered and chronologically advanced within the target range, with a mean age of 55.8 ± 5.2 months (representing children predominantly approaching or entering their fifth year). A minor proportion of records (27 children) containing missing or incomplete questionnaire responses, dental examination data, and anthropometric data were excluded from the final multivariable models, which is reflected in the provided participant flow diagram in Fig. 1.

To evaluate the ECOHIS counts (CIS, FIS, and overall scores), data were first tested for overdispersion. Due to significant overdispersion, negative binomial regression analysis was performed instead of a standard Poisson regression to estimate associations with the independent variables. The independent variables were entered into the regression analysis if they exhibited P values < 0.05. The level of significance was set as α ≤ 0.05. To account for the inflated risk of type I errors associated with evaluating multiple dietary habits simultaneously, a multiple-testing correction was implemented across the dietary panel. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled at 5% (Q = 0.05) utilizing the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Raw P-values for dietary variables were ranked, and adjusted P-values were computed to identify robust statistical associations. Model stability and sample size adequacy across the three regression models were verified using events-per-variable (EPV) metrics (Additional file 1: Table S3).

Demographics

The mean (± SD) age of children was 55.78 (± 5.21) months; 282 (51.7%) were male, and 263 (48.3%) were female. Most children were Sinhalese (87.5%), followed by Muslims (9.6%) and Tamils (2.9%). Nearly half (45.3%) had one sibling, while only 0.4% had four siblings. The largest proportion of children (41.1%) was second-born, and only 1.8% were fourth-born. A small percentage (1.1%) lived with grandparents, although 27.3% were cared for by grandparents during parents’ working hours. Among mothers, 50.8% had a collegiate education and 13.6% had a tertiary education, although 62.2% were unemployed. The mean (± SD) maternal age was 33.83 (± 4.98) years. Among fathers, 46.9% had collegiate, and 11.6% tertiary education, and only 0.4% were unemployed. The highest proportion of children (43.1%) lived in households earning < 50,000 LKR per month.

Factors associated with the nutritional status of children

To statistically analyze the association between nutritional status (BMIA as the dependent variable) and the other independent variables studied, factors were grouped into four categories: ECC status, demographics, eating habits, and general health status. Dietary patterns and the FFQ structured food consumption frequencies are summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1. For each level, unconditional logistic regression analysis was performed to exclude variables with a P-value of ≥ 0.20. Explanatory variables were selected for the final binary logistic regression analysis model only if they had a P-value of ≤ 0.05 after adjustment for variables from the same or prior levels of determinants. This stepwise approach allows researchers to quantify the contribution of each level of adjustment and to understand the model-building strategy, as well as to interpret the independent associations.

The regression analysis for the first horizontal level selected the defs index as the ECC-related factor associated with poor nutritional status (Z-score < −2) of children (P < 0.20). Among demographic factors, the person responsible for childcare, maternal and paternal education up to secondary and collegiate levels, and household income were associated with poor nutritional status (P < 0.20). Consumption of bakery items, fruit juice, ice cream, candy, and tea with sugar ≤ 2 or > 2 days/week were identified as the eating habits associated with the poor nutritional status of children (P < 0.20). General health–related factors associated with poor nutritional status (P < 0.20) included birth weight, gestational age at birth, and breastfeeding duration.

The results of this final regression analysis indicated that there was a significant positive correlation between the defs index (OR = 1.546, CI: 1.022–2.352) and the incidence of poor nutritional status (BMIA Z-score < −2) (P = 0.042). Children whose mothers had been educated up to grade 13 were less likely (P = 0.047) to experience poor nutritional status (BMIA Z-score < −2) as evidenced by the odds ratio of 0.698 (CI: 0.411–0.917). Among eating habits, frequent consumption of ice cream (OR = 1.689, CI: 1.031–2.772, P = 0.047) and tea with sugar (OR = 1.965, CI: 1.056–3.655, P = 0.042) was significantly and positively associated with the occurrence of poor nutritional status. In addition, the birth weight of children was negatively associated with the poor nutritional status of children (OR = 0.459, CI: 0.285–0.739, P = 0.010).

According to that, HAZ (RR = 1.131, CI: 1.042–1.227, P = 0.004), WAZ (RR = 1.791, CI: 1.338–1.951, P = 0.002), and BMIAZ (RR = 1.204, CI: 1.086–1.336, P = 0.003) Z-scores were significantly and positively associated with the overall ECOHIS scores. Among the ECC status-related factors, the defs index (P = 0.001), D1 (P = 0.001), D2 (P = 0.002), D3 (P = 0.003), and D4 (P = 0.003) caries were positively associated with the overall ECOHIS scores. Among demographics, birth order (P = 0.019), maternal unemployment (P = 0.002), maternal age ≤ 30 years (P = 0.002), paternal unemployment/daily-paid employment (P = 0.001), and household income ≤ 50,000 LKR (P = 0.003) were more likely to have higher overall ECOHIS scores, as evidenced by rate ratios of > 1.000. Under oral health-related factors, toothbrushing only once daily (P = 0.009), toothbrushing by the child alone (P = 0.001), and using non-fluoridated toothpaste (P = 0.024) appeared to result in higher overall ECOHIS scores as demonstrated by RR of 1.063, 1.104, and 1.057, respectively.

Discussion

ECC status, poor nutritional status, and OHRQoL

Few studies have examined the prevalence of ECC across different districts in Sri Lanka [40–42]; however, research utilizing the detailed D1–D4 caries classification is particularly limited in Sri Lanka. The mean deft score in the present sample was higher, likely due to the hospital-based nature of the study population [40–42]. For instance, a study in the Kegalle District reported that 71.9% of 4–6-year-old children had dental caries, with a mean dmfs score of 6.4 [22]. The mean defs score in the current clinic-based study was considerably higher (15.76 ± 15.61), reflecting that most participating children were seeking treatment at the dental hospital. Similarly, a study conducted in a dental hospital in the Colombo District found that 66.53% of children aged 0–3 years had ≥ 6 carious teeth [43]. The present study showed a comparable defs proportion (65.5%) among the assessed 545 children aged 4–5 years.

Dietary patterns in the present study revealed high consumption frequencies of sugary foods: 70.5% consumed sweets, 61.1% chocolate, and 47.2% ice cream more than three days per week. According to WHO guidelines, free sugar intake should be < 10% of total energy intake; it is likely that most children in this sample exceeded this threshold (10). Although a substantial proportion consumed pulses (63.3%), green vegetables (94.3%), fruits (54.3%), and cheese (15.4%) frequently, the intake of cariogenic foods was also high. For instance, 90.8% consumed biscuits, 47.3% bakery items, 61.1% chocolates, and 70.5% other sweets more than three days per week.

Factors associated with the nutritional status of children

The null hypothesis, which stated that there is no significant association between higher ECC status and poor nutritional status among Sri Lankan children aged 4–5 years, was rejected based on the observed strong positive association between the defs index and the occurrence of poor nutritional status. It was further confirmed by a study that mentioned each tooth of children with deep decay increased an additional 10% odds of having undernutrition, measured by HA, WA, and BMIA [44]. In addition, a few studies have reported that the higher incidence of dental caries increased malnutrition status among children [45, 46]. This nutritional deficit can be driven by caries-related pain and discomfort, which impairs masticatory function (chewing difficulty) and subsequently reduces overall dietary intake [47].

Maternal education showed a negative correlation with poor nutritional status, consistent with findings from numerous international studies [48–50]. The observed positive associations between the intake of ice cream and tea with sugar and poor nutritional status align with published evidence indicating that frequent consumption of sweet snacks and tea with sugar is a significant predictor of undernutrition [51]. High intake of low-nutrient carbohydrates may reduce dietary diversity; children consuming fewer than four food varieties have been shown to have a higher risk of being underweight [52]. The negative association between birth weight and poor nutritional status was also in line with earlier findings [48, 53, 54].

Factors associated with the ECC status of children

Although many reports would call for references in declining ECC rates in other countries, high prevalence persists in parts of Asia, including Sri Lanka [55]. In this study, S-ECC was significantly associated with WAZ and BMIA, supporting evidence that children with borderline or poor nutritional status are more prone to ECC [24, 25, 56]. These results further support the rejection of the null hypothesis that there is no association between higher ECC and poor nutritional status among children aged 4–5 years in Sri Lanka. Maternal educational level was marginally associated with higher untreated caries, consistent with previous findings [43], although one study in Kegalle reported that lower maternal education was linked to better OHRQoL [22]. Globally, lower maternal education is strongly associated with higher ECC occurrence [57–59].

Frequent consumption of sugary snacks, sweets, and sweetened beverages was associated with S-ECC, similar to findings from Sri Lankan studies [60–62] and international studies [19, 63–65]. Frequent consumption of free sugars creates a sustained acidic microenvironment within dental plaque, favoring the proliferation of aciduric bacteria over beneficial oral microbiota. This continuous acid exposure exceeds the natural remineralizing capacity of saliva, leading to rapid, unchecked destruction of primary tooth structure [66]. Long-term breastfeeding [63, 64, 67, 68] and bottle-feeding [20, 69, 70] were very strongly and significantly associated with ECC, with ORs of 6.258 and 2.051, respectively, which is consistent with several previous reports. Toothbrushing less than twice daily [71, 72], brushing without supervision [73, 74], and the use of non-fluoridated toothpaste [75, 76] significantly increased ECC abundance, supporting evidence from numerous studies.

Factors associated with the OHRQoL

The null hypotheses of no association between poor OHRQoL and poor nutritional status and no association between poor OHRQoL and higher ECC were both rejected by the observed strong positive associations. The observed strong associations are in agreement with published literature, as given in Table 4 [6, 77]. Many authors have observed an association between ECC and OHRQoL assessed by ECOHIS [78]. This association exists because the symptoms of ECC, such as severe dental pain, functional chewing difficulties, and altered sleep patterns, directly compromise daily well-being, functional performance, and psychological development [79]. Mean ECOHIS scores were found statistically significantly higher in children with caries compared with caries-free children below 5 years [80–85].

Some studies in the published literature further confirmed the observed association between ECOHIS scores and demographic and socioeconomic factors, as illustrated in Table 4 [82]. Age, ethnicity, and parents’ education of some Sri Lankan children living in the Kegalle district were significantly associated with ECOHIS scores/OHRQoL [22]. Higher ECOHIS scores were found in parents with lower education or income levels [86]. Among other factors, ECC status assessed by defs index was very strongly associated with OHRQoL, while many other factors showed modest associations. The present finding was aligned with some studies that demonstrated OHRQoL was positively associated with dental caries experience, age of parents/caregivers, and monthly household income [87, 88].

Long-term breastfeeding was also modestly associated with an increased impact on OHRQoL, as illustrated in the present study, and this finding was further confirmed by several published studies [17, 89]. The finding of the association between bottle-feeding and increased scores of ECOHIS was also in accordance with one study [8]. Poor oral hygiene practices, such as brushing less than twice daily, using non-fluoridated toothpaste, and unsupervised brushing, were significantly associated with poorer OHRQoL, reaffirming published evidence [6, 77].

Limitations and strengths

This study has certain limitations that should be acknowledged. Information on dietary habits, oral hygiene practices, and general health status was obtained through parental interviews, which are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. In addition, some potential confounding factors, such as broader dietary diversity, were not captured. Detailed data on certain behaviors, snacking habits, including the flossing assistance, frequency of bedtime feeding, and cariogenic microbial flora, were also not collected, despite being reported as associated with ECC. Moreover, nutritional status was assessed using anthropometric measures only, which may not fully reflect biochemical or clinical indicators of malnutrition.

Furthermore, as a hospital-based study, these findings cannot be generalized to the entire child population in Sri Lanka or the region. However, the primary purpose of this study was to investigate the associations between ECC status, nutritional status, and their associations with OHRQoL, rather than to produce population-wide estimates. These associations are relevant beyond the local context for several reasons: first, the mechanisms linking ECC, nutritional status, and OHRQoL are universal and applicable across populations. Second, evidence from regional or local settings fills critical gaps in global knowledge, especially from underrepresented countries where such data are scarce. Third, the findings may help inform public health strategies and interventions in similar socio-economic settings worldwide. Finally, our study is limited by the lack of data on several unmeasured confounding variables that heavily influence cariogenic risk. Specifically, we did not account for baseline plaque index, oral microbial load, or protective exposures such as water fluoride levels. Additionally, nuanced dietary behaviors, including the exact quantity of sugar intake and bedtime feeding habits, were not captured, which may introduce residual confounding to our observed associations.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It used standardized, pre-validated tools and clinical assessments conducted by calibrated examiners from the Dental Teaching Hospital, University of Peradeniya, ensuring data quality and reliability. Furthermore, the novelty of this study lies in exploring the associations between ECC and nutritional status in Sri Lankan children, an area that has been scarcely investigated as a comprehensive study, accounting for other confounding independent variables such as OHRQoL, oral hygiene practices, general health status, eating habits, and demographic factors.

Conclusions

This hospital-based study demonstrates a significant, interconnected relationship between ECC, poor nutritional status, and diminished OHRQoL among Sri Lankan children aged 4–5 years. The findings emphasize that nutritional status, ECC, and OHRQoL are interconnected and influenced by a combination of socio-economic, dietary, and oral hygiene-related factors. Given the clear association found among ECC, poor nutritional status, and OHRQoL, as public health interventions, authorities should integrate oral health screenings and nutritional counseling into routine maternal and child health clinic visits. Additionally, sugar consumption reduction targets and parental education should be incorporated into sustainable preventive strategies.
