Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) is an established therapy for reducing mortality and morbidity in advanced heart failure when compared with optimal medical therapy in a select patient group (left ventricular [LV] ejection fraction <35%, left bundle branch block with QRS duration >120 ms, and NYHA functional class II-IV).1,2 The positioning of the LV pacing lead in the latest activated region of the LV (most commonly, the lateral wall) is an important factor for obtaining a successful CRT response.3 In a large meta-analysis by Gamble et al4 that reviewed 29,503 patients, the overall rate of failed LV lead placement was 3.6%, including inability to cannulate the coronary sinus (CS), unsuitable target vein, and phrenic nerve stimulation.

Surgical epicardial lead placement via thoracotomy or thoracoscopy is considered the alternative for patients with difficult CS anatomy, but it may not be applicable in some patients with very low LV ejection fraction, previous history of sternotomy, and high anesthesia risk.5 Conduction system pacing in the form of left bundle branch area pacing has emerged as an optimal therapy for CRT nonresponders as well as the first-line therapy for CRT-eligible patients with difficult CS anatomy.6 However, the presence of significant scarring in the interventricular septum poses some difficulty in penetrating the septum with the pacing lead.Take-Home Message•Atrial trans-septal endocardial pacing with a thinner, lumenless lead is a feasible option of cardiac resynchronization for patients with challenging CS anatomy.•Conventional trans-septal puncture and use of a 260-cm coated guidewire (either end of which was snared to position the sheath and advance the lead to the desired location) made our technique simple and effective.

Case Summary

A 68-year-old man, a chronic smoker with diabetes (for 10 years) and hypertension (for 5 years) who had undergone coronary artery bypass graft 2 years ago for triple-vessel disease, presented with recurrent heart failure symptoms (NYHA functional class III) for the past 8 months and a history of 2 heart failure hospitalizations. His electrocardiogram revealed complete left bundle branch block with QRS duration 145 ms (Figure 1A). Echocardiography revealed an LV ejection fraction of 30%, with mild to moderate mitral regurgitation (Figure 1B, Videos 1 and 2); coronary computed tomography angiography showed native triple-vessel disease, with all grafts patent. Under local anesthesia and intravenous antibiotics, 3 venous access approaches were performed to the left subclavian vein. Thereafter, right atrial pacing and right ventricular defibrillation leads were implanted in a conventional manner by advancing the leads by way of peel-away sheaths through the subclavian accesses to the right atrial appendage and the right ventricular apex, respectively. Next, cannulation was attempted to the CS using a decapolar electrophysiology catheter, followed by an Amplatz left diagnostic catheter with a coated 0.035-inch guidewire. However, even with multiple attempts, the CS ostium could not be negotiated. A left coronary angiogram in the left anterior oblique 40° view showed a narrow-caliber CS ostium with early branching (Figure 2, Video 3). This patient was a post–coronary artery bypass graft candidate with a thinned-out and scarred septum, thus he was not an ideal candidate for left bundle branch area pacing. Surgical epicardial lead placement and trans-septal LV endocardial lead placement was discussed with the patient and his relatives, but they refused to undergo surgical epicardial lead placement. We therefore planned for trans-septal LV endocardial lead placement.Figure 2Levophase Imaging of Left Coronary Artery Showing Coronary Sinus AnatomyCoronary angiography shows a narrowed ostioproximal coronary sinus with its aberrant anatomical location (red arrow).

Procedural Steps

Trans-septal puncture was performed through the right femoral venous route using an 8-F Mullins dilator and a Brockenbrough needle. Intravenous unfractionated heparin 100 units/Kg was administered. Then, a 260-cm coated 0.035-inch guidewire (Terumo) was advanced into the left atrium (LA), and the Mullins dilator was removed (Figure 3A). Using a 5-F internal mammary artery catheter, the floppy end of the Terumo wire was guided from the LA to the aorta through the LV into the descending thoracic aorta (Figure 3B, Video 4) and was subsequently snared from the right common iliac artery using a 20-mm snare (Figure 3C, Video 5). The externalized floppy end of the wire was gently pulled, while the stiff end was allowed to advance into the inferior vena cava, still within a catheter. Next, a 9-F sheath was inserted over the third wire from the left infraclavicular pocket. Through this sheath, a 6-F JR 3.5 catheter and the same 20-mm snare were introduced. The stiff end of the Terumo wire was captured and externalized via the left subclavian vein (Figure 3D, Video 6). A C315 His catheter with dilator (Medtronic India) was then advanced over the Terumo wire into the aorta. While pulling out the wire, the catheter was allowed to fall toward the LV free wall (Figure 3E, Video 7). A lumenless 4.1-F bipolar lead (SelectSecure 3830, Medtronic) was advanced through the sheath and screwed into the LV free wall. The sheath was gradually withdrawn from the LV to the LA, and then into the right atrium. Lead parameters were assessed and were found to be within satisfactory limits. The sheath was slitted and removed. Anticoagulant therapy with apixaban 5 mg twice daily was initiated on postoperative day 2; the patient was already on aspirin 75 mg. Postimplant echocardiographic examination showed no increase in mitral regurgitation. There was no trans-septal shunt, pericardial fluid, or any other complications. The patient was followed up at 2 and 6 months. Sensing and pacing parameters remained stable, with no clinical signs of thromboembolic events. The patient also showed improvement in 2 NYHA functional classes. Electrocardiogram demonstrated narrow complex biventricular pacing (Figure 4A). Discussion

Constrained CS anatomy (non-negotiable ostium or absence of suitable tributaries in the latest activated LV region) is an important factor for failed CRT or a CRT nonresponder. Although surgical epicardial LV lead placement remains a well-established alternative treatment in patients with unsuitable CS anatomy for CRT, it carries significant periprocedural surgical risk, acute and possible long-term increase in adverse outcomes, as well as concerns about lead failure rate. Behar et al7 recently showed that, in severe ischemic cardiomyopathy with poor CRT response, endocardial pacing was superior to epicardial pacing. The mechanism of benefit may be due to the ability to access the more optimal sites that cannot be reached by the constraints of the CS anatomy. Although the ALSYNC trial (a large prospective multicenter trial recruiting 132 patients who had failed CRT) showed successful LV endocardial lead implantation of about 90%,8, 9 Elencwajg et al8, 9 showed a 100% success rate of LV endocardial lead implantation (the Jurdham procedure) in 88 patients at 15 centers in 8 countries.

LV endocardial lead implantation can be achieved by atrial trans-septal puncture or transventricular septal puncture. In transventricular septal puncture, a 91-cm deflectable 9-F sheath (Agilis; St Jude Medical) with a tapered dilator to reach the right ventricle, used a left ventriculogram in the right anterior oblique view to identify the LV borders and to position the tip of the dilator as close to the mid septum as possible, and angiography of the left coronary artery to ensure the puncture site was not adjacent to a major septal perforator. Then, the ventricular septum was punctured by using a Brockenbrough needle, radiofrequency needle or wire, or a 0.032-inch guidewire and diathermy pen. After entering the LV, a 6-F bipolar active fixation lead was implanted into the LV endocardium. The Jurdham procedure used femoral venous access for atrial trans-septal endocardial LV lead (standard pacing leads) placement, followed by percutaneous snaring of the proximal lead tip to translocate it to a subclavian device pocket for connecting to a standard CRT device. In our case, we used a 260-cm coated 0.035-inch guidewire to enter the LA after atrial septal puncture, which was advanced into the aorta through the LV and was snared from the right iliac artery. The other end of the wire was snared out from the left infraclavicular pocket through the left subclavian vein. Over this, the delivery sheath was advanced into the LV lateral wall, and subsequently the pacing lead was implanted into the LV lateral wall. In our case, we used a thinner lead (4.1-F), which may be less thrombogenic compared with standard pacing leads.

Potential Pitfalls

The 2 most important concerns in this atrial trans-septal LV endocardial approach are the risk of thromboembolism and worsening mitral regurgitation. However, in our patient we were able to avoid both risks using a thinner pacing lead and oral anticoagulant. Another limitation to this study is that we report a single patient experience with short-term follow-up. Larger case series with longer term follow-up is required, as is comparative analysis with a conventional pacing lead. Finally, we implanted the lead empirically in the lateral wall of the LV without searching for the latest activated LV wall segment by cardiac magnetic resonance imaging or electroanatomic mapping.

Conclusions

Atrial trans-septal LV endocardial pacing using a thinner, lumenless pacing lead is feasible in patients with difficult CS anatomy. At short-term follow-up, it was safe (no evidence of thromboembolism or worsening mitral regurgitation) and effective (improved LV function).
