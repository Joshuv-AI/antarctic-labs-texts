A Case Report of Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor‐Associated Pneumonia Complicated by Invasive Pulmonary Aspergillosis in a Patient With Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease
Authors: Shifeng Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case reports a patient with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease who developed immune checkpoint inhibitor‐associated pneumonia following immunotherapy, complicated by invasive pulmonary aspergillosis. Such cases are rare and pose diagnostic challenges in clinical practice. The successful diagnosis and treatment of this patient provide a valuable reference for clinical management.Keywords: Aspergillus fumigatus, immune checkpoint inhibitor‐associated pneumonia, interstitial pneumonia, invasive pulmonary aspergillosis

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitor‐associated pneumonia (CIP) is an adverse reaction caused by antitumor immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), with studies reporting an incidence rate of 5%–10% [1]. CIP can not only disrupt subsequent antitumor immunotherapy but may also lead to respiratory failure or even death [2]. Invasive pulmonary aspergillosis (IPA) is an infectious disease caused by aspergillus species, where fungal hyphae invade lung tissue, leading to bronchitis or pneumonia. Traditionally, IPA has been commonly reported in immunocompromised populations, such as patients with solid organ transplantation, hematologic malignancies, or neutropenia [3]. However, recent findings indicate an increasing incidence among non‐neutropenic patients, including those with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [4]. The clinical presentation and imaging features of IPA in these patients are often atypical, making it prone to missed or misdiagnosis [5]. IPA is closely associated with high mortality rates in patients with acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) [6]. The co‐occurrence of CIP and IPA is rare, and large‐scale studies on its specific incidence are currently lacking. Their clinical and imaging manifestations are non‐specific, posing significant challenges for early identification. The successful diagnosis and management of the patient in this case provide a valuable reference for clinical practice.

Case History

A 71‐year‐old male patient was admitted to the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine, Putian First Hospital, Fujian Province, in November 2025, with the chief complaint of “recurrent cough, expectoration, and wheezing for over 10 years, aggravated in the past 10 days.” The patient had a history of COPD with recurrent symptoms over the past decade. Upon this admission, his weight was measured at 52 kg. He had previously undergone radical total gastrectomy under general anesthesia for gastric cardia adenocarcinoma in April 2024 at our hospital's Department of Gastrointestinal Surgery. Postoperative pathology confirmed moderately‐to‐poorly differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma on the lesser curvature of the gastric body. Postoperatively, the patient received immunotherapy with the immune checkpoint inhibitor cadonilimab, combined with chemotherapy (oxaliplatin and tegafur, gimeracil, and oteracil potassium [S‐1]) for five cycles, followed by continued monotherapy with cadonilimab for immunotherapy. In August 2025, the patient's chest CT scan revealed emphysema and pulmonary bullae, with no inflammatory lesions observed in either lung (Figure 1). Ten days prior to admission, the patient presented to our department with worsening cough, sputum production, and shortness of breath. A chest CT scan performed at that time revealed newly developed inflammatory lesions in both lungs, with some areas exhibiting interstitial inflammation (Figure 2). Admission diagnoses included acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and immune checkpoint inhibitor‐associated pneumonia. It is recommended that the patient undergo a bronchoscopy, but the patient refuses. Laboratory tests revealed the following: Complete blood count: white blood cell count 8.82 × 109/L, absolute neutrophil count 6.48 × 109/L, red blood cell count 3.30 × 1012/L, platelet count 447 × 109/L. Procalcitonin, fungal (1,3)‐β‐D‐glucan, serum galactomannan assay (GM test), blood gas analysis, sputum culture and drug sensitivity test, influenza A and B virus nucleic acid test, SARS‐CoV‐2 nucleic acid test, B‐type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and troponin levels were all within normal limits.

Treatment

After admission, the patient was administered intravenous methylprednisolone sodium succinate (40 mg/day). During treatment, the patient's wheezing symptoms improved, suggesting that the treatment for CIP was effective. However, two weeks after the start of treatment, the patient suddenly developed respiratory failure, with pulse oximetry showing an oxygen saturation of 80% (without supplemental oxygen). A repeat complete blood count revealed: white blood cell count 11.81 × 109/L, absolute neutrophil count 9.75 × 109/L. Chest CT indicated partial resolution of inflammation in the right lung but progression in the left lung (Figure 3). Further evaluation with electronic bronchoscopy showed hyperemia and edema of the tracheal and bronchial mucosa, with white, thread‐like, viscous sputum observed in some lumens (Figure 4). On the 23rd day after the initiation of glucocorticoid therapy, the metagenomic next‐generation sequencing (mNGS) report of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) detected Aspergillus fumigatus (sequence count 4836, relative abundance 27.15%). BALF culture also confirmed the presence of Aspergillus fumigatus (+++). The patient was subsequently placed on high‐flow oxygen therapy, and intravenous isavuconazole was initiated for anti‐Aspergillus fumigatus infection treatment. Results

After two weeks of treatment, the patient's condition improved significantly, with marked alleviation of wheezing symptoms. Pulse oximetry increased to 94% (without supplemental oxygen), and respiratory failure was corrected. A follow‐up chest CT showed significant resolution of bilateral lung inflammation (Figure 5). Subsequently, oral itraconazole was initiated to continue antifungal therapy.

Discussion

Risk factors for CIP include COPD, interstitial lung disease, and history of chest radiotherapy, among others [2]. The clinical presentation of CIP is non‐specific [1]. Currently, there are no characteristic serological tests for diagnosing CIP. High‐resolution computed tomography (HRCT) of the chest is the key examination for diagnosing and assessing the severity of CIP. CIP is a drug‐induced interstitial pneumonia, with imaging manifestations including ground‐glass opacities (GGO), interstitial pneumonia, and hypersensitivity pneumonitis, among others [1]. According to severity, CIP is classified into five grades (CTCAE [7] or NCCN [8]): Grades 1–2 involve mild symptoms and limited lung involvement, while Grades 3–4 indicate severe disease with extensive lung involvement affecting over 50% of the lung parenchyma. Glucocorticoids are the first‐line treatment for CIP [9]. For patients with Grade 3–4 CIP or rapidly progressing disease, initial treatment is recommended with high‐dose intravenous steroids, equivalent to at least 1 mg·kg−1·d−1 of prednisone [10]. Given that the patient weighs 52 kg, initial anti‐inflammatory therapy was initiated with Methylprednisolone Sodium Succinate for injection (40 mg/day).

The clinical manifestations of IPA are also non‐specific [11]. Imaging findings of IPA include multiple pulmonary nodules, bronchopneumonia, and GGO [12]. BALF and lung tissue biopsy are the preferred specimens for diagnosis [13]. In 2016, the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) stated that “fungal culture from sterile sites and histopathology” are the gold standards for diagnosing aspergillosis [14]. However, CT‐guided percutaneous lung biopsy or bronchoscopic biopsy for histopathological examination carries significant risks. Bronchoalveolar lavage offers a safer, non‐invasive alternative with high sensitivity and specificity [15]. A study by a research team from Huashan Hospital affiliated with Fudan University in Shanghai evaluated BALF mNGS in non‐neutropenic patients and found a sensitivity of 92.31% and a specificity of 92.73% for diagnosing IPA [16]. Due to the challenges in confirming IPA, diagnosis should be based on a comprehensive consideration of the patient's underlying conditions, clinical symptoms, imaging findings, and microbiological culture results to develop an individualized treatment plan. Antifungal agents for IPA include triazoles (e.g., itraconazole, voriconazole, isavuconazole) [14].

Diagnosing CIP combined with IPA in COPD patients is challenging. Since the symptoms of COPD itself can mask the clinical manifestations of both CIP and IPA, and the imaging features of CIP may obscure signs of pulmonary aspergillus invasion. Long‐term or high‐dose use of glucocorticoids is an independent risk factor for IPA, particularly in patients with COPD [17]. Pulmonary inflammation associated with IPA typically does not respond effectively to corticosteroid therapy alone and may even exacerbate lung inflammation. However, failure to administer timely glucocorticoid treatment for CIP may also lead to disease progression. Cadonilimab, an immune checkpoint inhibitor with anti‐PD‐1/CTLA‐4 bispecific antibody [18], is used for the treatment of patients with advanced solid tumors. According to the drug label, the incidence of CIP is 5.4%. The patient had COPD, a risk factor for CIP, and developed interstitial pneumonia after multiple cycles of ICI therapy. During the initial treatment with glucocorticoids alone, the patient's wheezing improved, and a follow‐up chest CT showed partial resolution of the pulmonary inflammation. Combined with laboratory tests that ruled out other common causes of interstitial lung disease, a diagnosis of CIP was considered. However, after two weeks of treatment, the patient's symptoms worsened, and follow‐up imaging revealed new interstitial pulmonary inflammation. BALF mNGS detected a high sequence count of Aspergillus fumigatus, and BALF culture also grew Aspergillus fumigatus (+++). Although histopathological confirmation was not obtained, the patient's symptoms significantly improved, and imaging findings showed marked resolution after treatment with isavuconazole, strongly supporting the diagnosis of IPA.

However, this study has certain limitations. Due to patient compliance issues and the medical constraints of our hospital, bronchoscopy was not performed at the time of admission. After the patient's condition worsened, bronchoscopy was performed. However, the BALF galactomannan (GM) test could not be conducted, nor was the serum GM test repeated. This had a certain impact on the diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis in this patient. However, some studies have reported that the positivity rate of the serum GM test in non‐neutropenic patients is only 55.4% [5]. In retrospect, it is considered that the patient may have had early localized Aspergillus colonization or infection that was not detected by initial tests. The subsequent administration of corticosteroids then promoted rapid fungal proliferation. The transient improvement observed after the initiation of corticosteroids was likely due to the temporary suppression of CIP‐related inflammation. When the patient's condition worsened, a follow‐up chest CT revealed aggravated pulmonary inflammation. After adding isavuconazole treatment, imaging showed resolution of the pulmonary inflammation. Combined with laboratory findings, a clinical diagnosis of invasive pulmonary aspergillosis was therefore considered confirmed.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that the clinical presentation and imaging features of CIP combined with IPA are non‐specific. The patient's condition progressed rapidly and was prone to respiratory failure. Clinicians need to maintain a high index of suspicion and promptly initiate targeted diagnostic investigations.
