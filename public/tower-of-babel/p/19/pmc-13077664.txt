Introduction

This Perspective examines Thailand's role in cell and gene therapy, focusing on regulatory development and early clinical experience. These therapies are used in Thailand for β‐thalassemia, or blood cancer, together with the evolving ATMP regulatory framework and the ATMP Sandbox Project. Rather than cataloguing global indications, we analyze how selected advances intersect with Thailand's health system, highlighting opportunities and constraints for its role as a regional site for ATMP research, manufacturing, and access in Southeast Asia.

Cell and Gene Therapy Overview

Cell and gene therapy encompass complementary strategies that directly correct or compensate underlying molecular defects (Table 1). Gene replacement delivers functional copies of defective genes, often via adeno‐associated viral (AAV) vectors, to restore protein expression [1]. Genome editing platforms such as CRISPR‐Cas and base editors enable precise modification of pathogenic DNA sequences while cell‐based immunotherapies, notably CAR‐T cells, engineer immune cells to recognize and eliminate disease‐causing targets [2]. Together, these modalities constitute a mechanistic precision with clear translational potential.

Globally, clinical translation now spans monogenic, oncologic, neurologic, and regenerative indications, with advanced programs in β‐thalassemia, sickle cell disease, hemophilia, and CD19‐directed CAR‐T cells [3, 4]. In Thailand, however, most of these products are still accessed via overseas referral; meanwhile, domestic efforts concentrate on hemoglobinopathies and hematologic malignancies, supported by a maturing regulatory framework [5] (For more information: https://drug.fda.moph.go.th/atmp). In this context, rigorous safety oversight and fit‐for‐purpose regulation will be essential as Thailand and the broader ASEAN region integrate these innovations into equitable clinical practice.

Geopolitics and Global Outlook of Gene Therapy: The USA–China Ecosystem

For Thailand, the evolving U.S.–China biotechnology relationship actively shapes strategic choices in ATMP regulation, clinical development, and manufacturing. Thai regulators and developers must operate across two partially incompatible systems—U.S. FDA–centric pathways and China's rapidly evolving dual‐track model [6, 7]—while preserving optionality for global partnering and market access. This requires “dual‐compatible” development programs and documentation that anticipate both FDA and NMPA expectations, minimizing duplicative bridging and time‐to‐approval risks as decoupling accelerates [6, 7]. In this context, the divergence between U.S. and Chinese standards places a premium on predictable, internationally legible Thai oversight. Consequently, aligning core technical requirement, including CMC for vectors and cell substances, GCP for decentralized trials, pharmacovigilance, with ICH‐based standards as implemented by the FDA and the EMA is essential. By maintaining this alignment while retaining flexibility to accommodate China‐generated datasets, Thailand can position itself as a credible site for multi‐regional trials seeking acceptance across both regulatory blocs [7]. Clear guidance on cross‐border sample and data transfer, genetically derived data handling, and use of foreign real‐world evidence—interoperable with both U.S. and Chinese rules—reduces friction for sponsors and CROs selecting Thailand for pivotal oncology and rare‐disease programs [7].

At clinical‐development level, increasing U.S.–China friction has made direct cross‐border collaboration more difficult, prompting sponsors to look to neutral, well‐regulated environments in Southeast Asia for first‐in‐human and registration studies. Within this ecosystem, Thailand can capitalize by (i) offering expedited scientific advice for ATMP protocols that map to both FDA and NMPA expectations, (ii) enabling adaptive designs that facilitate global data pooling while respecting data‐sovereignty constraints, and (iii) institutionalizing independent data monitoring and audit trails that meet U.S. standards without breaching Chinese data‐export limits. Such positioning lowers coordination costs created by regulatory and political mistrust and makes Thailand an attractive anchor for regional CAR‐T and gene‐editing trials [6, 7].

Concurrently, tighter Chinese genetic‐data controls and U.S. concerns about data security complicate cross‐border bioinformatics, cloud use, and secondary data analytics that are integral to cell and gene therapy [7, 8]. Thailand can de‐risk participation in this polarized data environment by adopting tiered data‐localization options (on‐premises primary processing with controlled federated learning), strong de‐identification standards, and contract templates for material and data transfer that allocate cybersecurity and provenance obligations consistent with both regimes [7, 8]. Such infrastructure enables Thai sites to host high‐value analytics (e.g., vector integration profiling, MRD tracking) without jeopardizing collaboration with either side [7, 8].

Moreover, as many U.S. biotechnology companies in‐license late‐stage Chinese assets and Chinese companies seek Southeast Asian trial and commercialization footholds, Thailand will increasingly evaluate dossiers built on China‐generated evidence or mixed China/U.S. packages [6, 8]. A transparent Thai framework for accepting foreign clinical data, calibrating bridging requirements, and recognizing validated external manufacturing and quality systems will shorten timelines and make Thailand a preferred entry point for regional launches, while hedging against sanctions‐ or export‐control‐driven disruptions in either direction [6, 8].

In summary, the U.S.–China dynamic pushes Thailand to pursue regulatory credibility aligned with global gold standards, operational flexibility to accommodate Chinese data and manufacturing realities, and resilience in supply chains and data governance. Policies that institutionalize dual‐compatible development pathways, trusted‐supplier programs, and robust compliance will allow Thailand to convert geopolitical headwinds into a comparative advantage as a Southeast Asian hub for ATMP regulation, clinical research, and early manufacturing scale‐up [6, 7, 8].

Thailand in the Southeast Asian ATMPs Landscape

Against this U.S.–China backdrop, the Southeast Asian regulatory landscape for ATMPs is also diversifying. Within the region, Singapore remains the regulatory benchmark for ATMPs, with a dedicated framework in force since 2021 and 2024 guidance operationalizing a two‐tier scheme (Class 1 notification; Class 2 registration; for more information: https://www.hsa.gov.sg/ctgtp/regulatory‐overview). Malaysia has moved into a “near‐Singapore” tier with its second‐edition national guideline for cell and gene therapy products (2025), while Indonesia formalized an ATMP pathway via a new BPOM (Badan Pengawas Obat dan Makanan) regulation in 2025 (For more information: https://www.pom.go.id/) Thailand sits between these dossier‐centric regimes and later‐formalizing markets, having issued ATMP‐specific instruments, including a conditional approval route announced in late 2024, to accelerate access while keeping dossiers intelligible to the expectations of both the FDA and the EMA, and more broadly to other ICH‐aligned regulators. The Philippines has introduced an ATMP definition within broader biologics reforms, and Vietnam's specific ATMP rules remain emergent alongside initiatives such as a Japan–Vietnam MoU to establish a cell/stem‐cell quality‐control center.

Singapore also concentrates regional GMP‐enabling infrastructure through specialist CDMOs (e.g., CellVec for lentiviral vectors; Esco Aster for cell‐therapy and plasmid manufacturing), whereas Thailand is expanding hospital‐ and university‐based facilities (e.g., Chulalongkorn University's Excellence Center for Advanced Therapy Medicinal Product, EC‐ATMP; https://kcmh.chulalongkornhospital.go.th/), improving readiness for early‐phase programs. The establishment of Genepeutic Bio (https://www.gnptbio.com) in 2020—the first Thailand‐based CDMO dedicated to cell and gene technologies, founded by scientists under the Thailand Center of Excellence for Life Sciences (TCELS) and private partners—marks a major milestone. This GMP‐certified facility initially focuses on CAR T‐cell manufacturing, improving access for Thai patients and positioning Thailand as a potential manufacturing hub for the wider Asia‐Pacific region. Overall, Thailand offers a balanced option—more flexible than Singapore for early access (via conditional approval), more mature than Vietnam and the Philippines in ATMP‐specific oversight, and broadly comparable to Malaysia's dossier architecture—making it an attractive base for multi‐regional trials and regional launches originating from both U.S.‐ and China‐oriented development pipelines.

Navigating the Regulatory Landscape Through ATMPs Sandbox Model

The safe and effective implementation of gene therapy requires a robust regulatory framework. The Thai Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has been proactive in addressing the unique challenges posed by Advanced Therapy Medicinal Products (ATMPs), including gene and cell therapies. At present, Thai FDA applies existing biological‐product guidelines to ATMPs; while dedicated ATMP guidance is under development, this framework already supports approval of clinical trials and marketing authorization. Figure 1 summarizes the current use of cell and gene therapy in Thailand. To date, three ATMP products have been formally registered with the Thai FDA for clinical trial or commercial use, reflecting early implementation in hemoglobinopathies, hematologic malignancies, and spinal muscular atrophy. Notably, a domestically manufactured CD19‐directed CAR T‐cell product from Genepeutic Bio has progressed to a Phase II trial, with more than 10 Thai patients treated so far, marking a transition from concept to routine delivery of ATMPs in tertiary centers.

In 2025, the Thai government announced a national policy to foster ATMP development through the ATMP Sandbox Model (More information: https://drug.fda.moph.go.th/atmp). This special program aims to accelerate ATMP registration and approval via a one‐stop service that integrates multiple regulators, including Thai FDA, the Medical Council of Thailand, and the Department of Health Service Support under the Ministry of Public Health. The sandbox is designed to streamline approvals for investigator‐initiated trials (IITs), investigational new drugs (INDs), and Phase I–III clinical studies of ATMP products and services.

The Thailand ATMP Sandbox Model targets registration of at least two ATMP products by 2026. Leading candidates include mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) for lung fibrosis and genetically modified CD34+ cells for β‐thalassemia using lentiviral gene addition (HGI‐001‐C01) (https://drug.fda.moph.go.th/atmp) and CRISPR‐Cas9–mediated gene editing (RM‐001‐02, under IND applications). This evolving regulatory landscape underscores Thailand's commitment to balancing patient safety with active promotion of innovation, research, and development in gene therapy.

The Road Ahead: Challenges and Opportunities

Despite significant milestones, Thailand's transition to a regional leader in gene therapy faces structural hurdles. The high cost of the ATMP treatments remains a primary barrier to accessibility. This is exemplified by the recent case of a pediatric patient with a rare genetic disorder requiring over 100 million baht for treatment, which underscores the acute financial burden on families and the current lack of sustainable funding models for high‐cost therapies [9]. Strategic industrial partnership between Thailand and China may mitigate these barriers. Specifically, the collaboration between Reforgene and Nouvogenes Therapeutics utilized localized clinical‐grade manufacturing and established CRISPR gene‐editing platforms to streamline the registration of gene therapies in Thailand [10]. Leveraging China's manufacturing economies of scale through such public‐private frameworks could reduce procurement costs and improve domestic and regional reach.

Although government support exists, inconsistent R&D financing continues to impede long‐term self‐reliance. Realizing the nation's potential requires a shift from sporadic funding to sustained investment in the national biotechnology ecosystems. Nevertheless, Thailand possesses significant structure advantages. Its established Universal Health Coverage (UHC) system and extensive tertiary‐care hospital network provide a robust platform for the delivery of advance therapies.

Nevertheless, opportunities for Thailand are immense. By integrating a transparent, ICH‐aligned regulatory framework with strong healthcare infrastructure, a skilled workforce, and a strategic location, Thailand can transition from a trial site to become a regional hub for advanced therapies in Southeast Asia. Continued investment in research, clinical infrastructure, and a transparent regulatory framework would not only improve health outcomes for Thai patients but also enable meaningful contributions to global advances in genetic medicine.

In conclusion, Thailand's journey is marked by pioneering research, growing clinical expertise, proactive and dual‐track regulatory approach. While challenges remain, the nation's commitment to evidence‐based policy and strategic international partnerships positions it to transform its healthcare landscape. With strategic vision and sustained investment, Thailand is poised to become a vital regional hub for genetic medicine.
