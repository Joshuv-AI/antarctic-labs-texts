Different sagittal cutting plane orientations do not affect posterior tibial slope in open wedge high tibial osteotomy: a Sawbone model study
Authors: Elvin Gurbanov, Blaise Cochard, David E. Bauer, Hermes H. Miozzari, Philippe M. Tscholl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeControl of the posterior tibial slope (PTS) is crucial in high tibial osteotomy (HTO), since unintended changes can alter the normal knee kinematics and ligament loading. This study investigated the influence of sagittal cutting plane orientations on PTS in bi-planar medial open wedge HTO (MOWHTO) on a Sawbone model.MethodsSixty Sawbone® left-tibia models (A Pacific Research Company, USA) were used to perform biplanar MOWHTO with three sagittal orientations: (1) parallel to the medial tibial plateau (PO); (2) 10° anterior-inclined osteotomy (AIO); (3) 10° posterior-inclined osteotomy (PIO). The biplanar cut was performed distal to the tibial tuberosity in half the specimens and proximal in the remainder. Customized 3D-printed cutting guides ensured reproducibility. PTS and valgus correction were measured pre- and post-osteotomy using a navigation system. Group- and subgroup-specific ΔPTS were analyzed using a Bayesian multilevel model. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was set at 2.5°. Bayesian “power” analysis (assurance) was used to assess the sensitivity of ΔPTS and pairwise differences relative to the MCID.ResultsAcross the three sagittal orientation and their distal/proximal subgroups, ΔPTS and pairwise ΔPTS did not exceed the predefined MCID of 2.5°. Bayesian assurance of equivalence exceeded 86% in every unit and pairwise comparison, indicating a high confidence that the evaluated HTO configurations are clinically equivalent with respect to ΔPTS.ConclusionIn this experimental study, different sagittal osteotomy inclinations in MOWHTO showed no clinically relevant changes in posterior tibial slope. No significant differences were observed across sagittal cut orientations or ATT levels.Level of evidenceIn vitro biomechanical study.

Introduction

Medial opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy (MOWHTO) is widely used to treat medial compartment osteoarthritis (OA) with concomitant varus malalignment of the knee joint [1]. Compared to a lateral closing wedge, MOWHTO have the advantage of an easier alignment correction, does not cause leg shortening and spares the fibula, therefore lowering the risk of peroneal nerve injury [2]. However, unintended increase of the posterior tibial slope (PTS) of up to 3–4° is a frequently reported consequence of MOWHTO [3–5]. Unless deliberately performed to reduce the tibial slope in a posterior cruciate ligament (PCL)-deficient knee [6], this might be detrimental in other patients. Indeed, increasing PTS causes an anterior shift of the tibia while standing under axial load, thus increasing the tension on the anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) [7] and leading to possible loss of extension. Therefore, control of the PTS is crucial. Various factors influencing PTS in high tibial osteotomy (HTO) have been described in the literature, such as hinge position [8, 9], the direction of the wedge insertion [10], the relationship between anterior and posterior gap opening [11] and tibial plate position [12]. In order to control and prevent any change on PTS, Pape et al. suggested the plane of the osteotomy to be parallel to the medial tibial plateau [13].

Eliasberg et al. and Moon et al. using CT-based (postoperative) and virtual simulations respectively [8, 15], showed that osteotomy orientation significantly impacts PTS, highlighting the need for precise surgical planning.

Despite these insights from virtual modeling to the best of our knowledge, in vitro validation has not yet been reported. Therefore, the aim of this experimental study was to investigate the influence of different sagittal osteotomy orientations (anteriorly inclined, parallel to the native slope or posteriorly inclined) on the PTS in MOWHTO, using a Sawbone model.

Materials and methods

Sixty Sawbone left-tibia models (A Pacific Research Company, Washington, USA), made of solid foam (reference number: 1116) were used to produce a biplanar MOWHTO with 10 mm correction in the coronal plane, with a constant hinge position. Three groups of 20 Sawbones each were defined by a different osteotomy orientation in the sagittal plane: (1) parallel to the medial tibial plateau (PO), (2) 10° anteriorly inclined osteotomy (AIO) and (3) 10° posteriorly inclined osteotomy (PIO). In each group, 10 osteotomies were performed with the coronal plane running proximal to the anterior tibial tuberosity (ATT), and 10 distal to it (Fig. 1).

Schematic representation of the three sagittal osteotomy orientations (anterior-inclined, parallel, and posterior-inclined) and the two anterior tibial tuberosity (ATT) cut levels (proximal and distal)

To achieve the best accuracy and reproducibility, MOWHTO were performed using customized 3-D printed PSI cutting blocks made for each group, based on ACTIVMOTION guides (Newclip Technics Nantes, France), created according to the anatomy of the given Sawbones model.

Osteotomy protocol

The Sawbones tibia diaphysis was firmly held by a metal clamp, attached to a table. The custom osteotomy cutting guide allowed for a temporary hold on the proximal tibia (Fig. 2) and was then fixed by 3 pins through predisposed drill-holes.

Fig. 2Positioning of the patient-specific cutting guide on the proximal tibia for standardized biplanar medial open-wedge high tibial osteotomy

Positioning of the patient-specific cutting guide on the proximal tibia for standardized biplanar medial open-wedge high tibial osteotomy

To obtain a reproducible strictly lateral hinge position, a depth “stop” was built in the cutting block, to always allow full penetration of the 90.0 mm High Precision Falcon saw blade (Stryker, Portage, USA: Reference number 6725-127-090) up to its broader base (Fig. 2). To ensure a consistent hinge position and achieve symmetric coronal correction, the hinge was planned strictly perpendicular to the main axis of the tibia in the axial plane, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Once the biplanar osteotomy performed, the cutting block was replaced by a custom-made, group specific, wedge-guide bloc and a constant 10 mm opening was achieved by inserting the according metal wedge through the guide (Fig. 4).

Fig. 4Three-dimensional design of the wedge-guide block used to achieve a standardized 10-mm medial opening

Three-dimensional design of the wedge-guide block used to achieve a standardized 10-mm medial opening

Changes in PTS and valgus correction were quantified using the fast protocol of the Brainlab knee 3 navigation system (Brainlab, Munich, Germany) with measurements obtained pre- and post-osteotomy.

Data acquisition and evaluation

To standardize the data acquisition procedure, all reference points necessary for the “Tibia fast protocol” of the Brainlab knee 3 navigation system (Brainlab, Munich, Germany) were identically marked on each bone model before measurements. The following landmarks were needed: the medial malleolus, the distal A/P center of the tibiofibular joint, the proximal tibia mechanical axis point and the proximal tibia A/P direction.

A standardized slot in the proximal tibia was created through a custom-made cutting guide parallel to the PTS and to the joint line in the coronal plane, to be able to place the tibial array for the knee plane reference tool (Fig. 5).

Fig. 5Standardized anterior slot for reproducible positioning of the navigation tibial array

Standardized anterior slot for reproducible positioning of the navigation tibial array

Data acquisitions were performed, before (for the “native” PTS and the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle (mMPTA)) and after the osteotomy, to assess the coronal correction and the PTS. The change in PTS before and after the osteotomy was calculated. A negative Δ PTS was considered as a change into a more horizontal, anterior verted, tibial plateau.

Statistical analysis

A posterior distribution of group-specific and subgroup specific PTS changes was estimated with a Bayesian multilevel model (normal likelihood, weakly informative priors), using Monte Carlo posterior sampling to approximate the distributions, with pre/post operative time as the within-subject factor, and slope (3 levels) × ATT level (2 levels) as between-subject factors. This approach was selected because it is naturally robust against small variances, allows to explicitly model measurement error, provides automatic multiplicity control via partial pooling and delivers natural probabilistic statements for clinical relevance based on effect magnitude. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) [16] was established at 2.5° PTS change [6, 17]. Bayesian prospective “power” analysis (assurance) was used to evaluate the sensitivity of ΔPTS in groups and subgroups as well as of pairwise ΔPTS differences to the MCID level and sample size Fig. 6.

Results

In the coronal plane, the intended 10 mm opening (mean of 8.7°± 0.8° valgus correction) after MOWHTO was consistent throughout the groups, with an mMPTA of 92.7°± 1.0° (“native” mMPTA with an average of 84° ± 1.0°), without any statistical difference.

All sub-groups showed slope reductions. Figure 7 displays the subgroup-specific Monte-Carlo probability densities for ΔPTS along the lower diagonal and the group densities (i.e. pooling proximal and distal levels) along the upper diagonal. The posterior probability that |ΔPTS| is comprised within our predefined MCID = 2.5 ° remains above 99.3% for all groups and subgroups.

More relevant to our study, to compare the different surgical configurations, we calculated the posterior density of the difference in ΔPTS between any two subgroups, denoted i and j (with i and j representing the six subgroup configurations). This is expressed as P(ΔPTS_i – ΔPTS_j | data). Unfilled curves below the diagonal in Fig. 7 reflect all 15 contrasts across subgroups; the above diagonal unfilled curves compare the three sagittal cut groups. In all cases, configurations turn out to be equivalent to each other within an MCID of 2.5 °. The probability of a clinically important ΔPTS difference between the most distant configurations (AIO-distal and PIO-proximal) is only 0.2% (bottom-left panel).

For a study with N = 10 tibiae per subgroup and an MCID of 2.5°, assurance was 1.0 and 0.86 for groups’ and subgroups’ ΔPTS compared to no effect, and 1.0 and 0.91 for groups’ and subgroups’ pairwise ΔPTS differences, respectively. All values exceeded the 0.8 benchmark, validating the adequacy of the chosen sample size. Figure 8 further illustrates that doubling the sample size would provide evidence of equivalence even at lower MCID thresholds.

Discussion

In this experimental study, the different orientations of the sagittal cut resulted in small but systematic reductions of the PTS. Although small group and subgroup level differences were observed (e.g., the AIO group showing the smallest ΔPTS, Tables 1 and 2), these did not reach statistically clinical significance. Importantly, the posterior probability that /ΔPTS/ remained within our predefined MCID (2.5°) exceeded 99% across all configurations, confirming that slope changes induced by biplanar MOWHTO with 10 mm medial opening are clinically irrelevant.

Our findings are consistent with those of recent studies, such as that of Lee et al., in which no statistically significant PTS changes were detected with an AIO cut (mean increase of 0.2°, SD ± 3.2) [14]. Similarly, in our study, the AIO group showed the smallest mean ΔPTS, The observed changes remained below the 2.5° threshold and are therefore unlikely to be of practical clinical consequence; however, they may still be biomechanically meaningful in selected populations, particularly in ACL-deficient knees and high-demand athletic patients.

In an experimental study based on a square column model, Moon et al. demonstrated that an osteotomy cut oriented parallel to the native posterior tibial slope (PO; 10° parallel) resulted in the least alteration in PTS, whereas posteriorly inclined osteotomies (PIO; 10° posterior inclination) led to an increase in PTS and anteriorly inclined osteotomies (AIO; 0° and 10° anterior inclination) produced a reduction in PTS [15]. Consistent with our study, the AIO produced a neglectable reduction in the PTS, < 1°, and < 2° with an angle correction up to 15° (anterior inclined 0° and 10°) and 30° opening respectively. Our findings align with these observations, as we detected small slope reductions across all groups with a mean 8.7° valgus correction. The maximal reduction in PTS observed in our study remains below the threshold generally considered to induce clinically relevant alterations, as shown by Giffin et al. [6]. It is therefore safe to say that by having a cut parallel to the native PTS or anterior inclined (AIO), with a lateral positioned hinge, symmetric from anterior to posterior, MOWHTO with 10 mm medial opening should allow for a negligible postoperative change in PTS.

When comparing 6 groups, AIO with the osteotomy distal to the ATT showed the least change in ΔPTS (-0.5 ± 0.6°). AIO with osteotomy proximal to the ATT had a change of (-1.3 ± 0.5°). On the other hand, AIO might imply some technical problems during surgery, such as the risk of a too thin posterior proximal bony fragment, with a higher risk of Takeuchi type III fracture [18] and potentially fixation problems, depending on the plate. The results of our present study demonstrate an insignificant reduction of PTS, regardless of the biplanar cut running proximal or distal to the ATT (Fig. 6). The decision to perform the osteotomy proximal or distal to the anterior tibial tuberosity (ATT) is largely guided by surgical considerations and does not appear to meaningfully affect posterior tibial slope.

Regardless of the sagittal cut performed, obtaining a perfect lateral hinge is not obvious. The study was conducted in ideal conditions using standardized procedure with PSI cutting blocks and osteotomy depth control. The strict lateral position of the hinge was probably the most important factor which led to minor clinical changes of the PTS between the groups. Since, antero-lateral or posterolateral hinge position would lead to variation in PTS, the anterolateral position should be prioritized in order to avoid major changes of the PTS, as demonstrated by mathematical models [11, 19] and simulated surgeries [20].

Another factor affecting the PTS is the height of the hinge within the “safe zone” in the coronal plane. The safe zone lies at the proximal tibio-fibular joint, and it helps prevent hinge fracture in the lateral cortex, in particular unstable fractures such as Takeuchi type II and III [18, 21]. In a cadaveric study, Joe et al. showed that a lower hinge significantly increases the PTS, compared to the standard group (11.2 ± 3.0° and 5.6 ± 2.5°), respectively; p < 0.001) [9]. Clearly, obtaining a well-placed, symmetrical lateral hinge seems to be paramount to avoid complications such as hinge fractures or changes in PTS. Still, this is not easy to reproduce in vivo.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, Sawbones® models do not reproduce the same elasticity properties as of human bone and lack soft-tissue structures, which may influence osteotomy behavior and hinge mechanics in vivo.

Use of 4th generation composite Sawbones models would have been an alternative to achieve an adequate elasticity modulus, though much more expensive and with potential inter-sample heterogeneity [22].

Second, the exclusive use of sponsor-provided cutting guides and instrumentation may introduce potential unconscious bias despite the absence of sponsor involvement in data analysis or interpretation. Third, only a single correction magnitude (10-mm medial opening, corresponding to approximately 8–9° valgus correction) was investigated. Larger corrections, which are more prone to posterior tibial slope changes and hinge-related asymmetry, were not evaluated. Consequently, the present results cannot be extrapolated to severe varus deformities, larger correction angles, or double-level osteotomy procedures. Finally, the strictly controlled experimental conditions with patient-specific cutting guides and an idealized hinge position may limit the generalizability of the findings to routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

In this experimental study with a 10 mm opening, different sagittal osteotomy inclinations in MOWHTO did not result in statistically significant PTS changes above the predefined threshold for clinical relevance of 2.5°. There were also no clinically relevant differences across sagittal cut configurations or sagittal-ATT cut level subgroups.
