Saccadic Eye Movement as a Harbinger of Gaucher's Disease in Children: A Case Report of Diagnostic Difficulty
Authors: Ramesh Khadayat, Sucharita Tuladhar, Sri Ram KC, Bijesh Shresth, Sailesh Shrestha, Tilak Gautam, Ramesh Basnet, Shreya Thapa, Dipendra Magrati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGaucher disease type 3 (GD3) is a rare disorder causing neurological decline, including saccadic eye movement defects. A 2‐year‐old boy, initially treated for anemia, later developed gaze slowness, highlighting the need for careful assessment in children with eye movement issues. Early diagnosis and management are crucial despite costly treatment.

Introduction

Gaucher disease (GD) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterized by the defective function of the catabolic enzyme β‐glucocerebrosidase (GBA), leading to an accumulation of its substrate, glucocerebroside, primarily in the liver, spleen, and bone marrow [1, 2]. A total or partial deficiency of this enzyme results in severe lysosomal dysfunction in addition to the accumulation of glucosylceramide (GC) in macrophages of the reticuloendothelial system [3]. It is the most common lysosomal storage disease with highly variable clinical manifestations. It has three types: Type 1 (GD1) is the most common non‐neuropathic form that involves the liver, spleen, bone marrow, or may also involve the lungs and kidneys [1]. Type 2 (GD2) is an acute neuropathic form that manifests in early childhood with rapid neurological deterioration, and children hardly survive beyond age 2. Type 3 (GD3) is a subacute neuropathic form with slower neurological development [1]. In type 3 GD, the most common neurological features are horizontal saccadic initiation failure (SIF) and saccadic slowing; however, they are very hard to detect clinically due to the uncooperativeness of children [4]. Clinical ocular assessment, saccadometry using video‐oculography, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) are important ophthalmic evaluations for detecting saccadic eye movement abnormalities in all suspected cases. Here we are going to present the case of a 2‐year‐old male child with saccadic eye movement finally being diagnosed as type 3 Gaucher's disease.

Case History and Examination

History and Examination

A 2‐year‐old, developmentally normal, fully immunized male presented with a 1‐month history of decreased appetite and a 10‐day history of irritability and progressive pallor. No fever, cough, GI symptoms, jaundice, bleeding, bone pain, rash, lymphadenopathy, or transfusion history and no TB contact were noted. He had a family history of the death of a 1st cousin due to metabolic syndrome at the age of 11. On examination, he was active and alert, with stable vitals (Temperature‐98 F, RR‐24 breaths/mins, HR‐104 bpm, BP‐90/60 in the right arm (50th centile for age and sex), CRT of < 2 s). He was pale, but there was no icterus, lymphadenopathy, edema, cyanosis, dehydration, or clubbing. Anthropometric parameters are shown in Table 1. On systemic examination, there was hepatomegaly, with a liver span of 8 cm and a palpable spleen 2 cm below the costal margin. The rest of the physical examination was unremarkable.

Follow‐Up

On follow up, his appetite had improved and his irritability had decreased. The hemoglobin electrophoresis showed a normal report. Then we planned to go for a genetic test to rule out the inborn error of metabolism, but the patient's party initially refused due to financial issues.

Readmission (History and Examination)

The child was again admitted after 1 month to the hospital for the complaint of insidious onset nonprogressive slowness of gaze when the head moves in the horizontal direction, as noticed by the mother. It was not associated with abnormal body movement, uprolling of eyes, frothing from the mouth, or urinary or stool incontinence. Other history was also not significant. On examination, we noticed the slowing of the right eye when the head was moved horizontally (slowing of saccadic eye movement). However, there were no focal neurological deficits and no features of raised intracranial pressure (ICP). The rest of the physical examination was unremarkable except for the persistence of hepatosplenomegaly that was not changed from the last time. Repeated ophthalmologic examination did not show evidence of cherry red spots and ophthalmoplegia.

Treatment and Follow Up

Anemia was managed with iron, parents were counseled regarding the nature of the disease, treatment options, and possible complications and management. However, the child lost follow‐up.

Discussion

GD is an autosomal recessive lysosomal storage disorder due to pathogenic variants in gene GBA1, resulting in a deficiency of the enzyme glucocerebrosidase. Deficiency of glucocerebrosidase leads to accumulation of glucocerebrosides and other glycolipids within the lysosome of macrophages, which are called Gaucher cells. The disease mainly affects the liver, spleen, and bone marrow. GD is a universal disease, with a worldwide prevalence of around 1/75,000 newborns, but it's much more frequent in populations such as Ashkenazi Jews (prevalence between 1/400 and −½/500) [3]. The frequency of the neuronopathic form is estimated to be 1 per 40,000 live births. It occurs much more frequently in the Ashkenazi population, with an incidence being 1 per 1000 live births, where N370S (57%–70%) and 84GG (10%) are the most frequent mutations [5]. Based on neurological involvement, it has been classified into three types: type 1 (GD1, non‐neuropathic), type 2 (GD2, acute neuropathic), and type 3 (GD2, chronic neuropathic).

The clinical presentation of GD is highly variable, depending upon the type and age of the patient. However, the major clinical presentations across all types of GD include splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, thrombocytopenia, anemia, bleeding, osteopenia, growth retardation, bone pain, and fracture [6]. The most common signs and symptoms in children with GD1 are splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, thrombocytopenia, epistaxis, bruising, anemia, delayed growth, delayed puberty, and acute and chronic pain associated with bone disorders [7, 8, 9]. Children with neuronopathic Gaucher disease (GD2) typically present perinatally or within the first year of life and are characterized by rapid neurological decline leading to death in infancy [9, 10, 11]. Patients with GD3 often present within the first year of life with massive organomegaly, anemia or thrombocytopenia, and a horizontal supranuclear gaze palsy [10]. The most common manifestation is horizontal supranuclear gaze palsy, and in some individuals, which, in some individuals, may be the only neurological symptom [4, 12, 13]. In our case, the child was initially presented with features of anemia, hepatosplenomegaly, and later developed horizontal saccadic initiation failure. Since the presentation was vague and the initial evaluation was normal, only his eye movement problem caught our attention.

In a rare subset of patients with GD3, severe cardiac valve involvement may develop; however, this was not the case in our patient. The diagnosis of GD3 is most often made by a neuro‐ophthalmologist when abnormal eye movements are observed [10]. Therefore, a careful ocular examination and thorough investigation are crucial for detecting any ocular abnormalities. This includes clinical ocular assessment, saccadometry using video‐oculography, and optical coherence tomography (OCT) [14]. However, in our case, due to limited resources, we were unable to perform the aforementioned investigations.

Various treatment goals have been proposed in the literature for managing the clinical manifestations of GD in pediatric patients, including addressing anemia, thrombocytopenia, hepatosplenomegaly, growth retardation, and bone disease [9]. Prevention, early detection, and management of complications are the primary goals of treatment to improve the quality of life for patients. The only approved curative option recommended for all symptomatic pediatric patients is enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) [9]. Early intervention with ERT in symptomatic children may be beneficial to prevent the development of various irreversible pathologies, such as osteonecrosis. Additionally, treatment is crucial for improving growth, enhancing bone mineralization, and reducing the disease's impact on physical and psychosocial development [15]. Substrate reduction therapy (SRT) is another treatment option, though it is currently limited to certain adult patients and not approved for use in children. However, next‐generation SRTs designed for pediatric use are currently in development in addition to gene therapy, chaperone therapy, and histone deacetylase inhibitors [9, 15]. However, due to the child's financial constraints, we were unable to treat his condition with ERT. Nonetheless, we managed his anemia and provided counseling regarding possible complications and their management.

Conclusion

Saccadic eye movements are a common neurological manifestation of type 3 GD, but detecting them in children can be challenging due to their uncooperative behavior during examinations. A thorough history from parents, combined with meticulous physical examination, is crucial for identifying abnormalities in eye movements in children suspected of having GD. Any child presenting with saccadic eye movements should be thoroughly evaluated for underlying GD to ensure early diagnosis and timely management of complications associated with the condition.
