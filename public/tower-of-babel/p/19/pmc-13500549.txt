In-tandem electrocatalysis via Ni single atoms and reconstructed Cu single clusters for CO2-to-acetone reduction
Authors: Tonglin Yang, Fangqi Yang, Haoming Yu, Xuanzhao Lu, Wenlei Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrochemical conversion of CO2 into multi-carbon (C2+) products offers a promising route toward sustainable energy and carbon neutrality, but is hindered by inefficient C–C coupling, especially for C3 products. Here we show a Ni single atom/Cu single cluster catalyst (Ni SAC/Cu SCC) that achieves a total Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 84.1% for C2+ products (ethanol, ethylene glycol, and acetone) in a flow cell. The FE for acetone (a C3 product) reaches 48.8%. The high acetone selectivity on Ni SAC/Cu SCC is attributed to the synergistic interplay among in-tandem catalysis, the confinement effect, and in situ-formed Cu0/Cu+ sites. Mechanistically, CO spills over from Ni sites to adjacent Cu sites and is confined within the hierarchical pore structure of the reduced graphene oxide (rGO) support, resulting in high local CO coverage on Cu sites. Concurrently, the in situ-formed Cu0/Cu+ sites enhance adsorption of the key *CO intermediate. Together, this synergy lowers the energy barriers for C–C coupling. This work provides insights into the electrosynthesis of multi-carbon products.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis, Materials for energy and catalysis

Introduction

Electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) has emerged as a promising and environmentally friendly strategy for mitigating CO2 emissions and storing renewable electricity as chemical energy in fuels1,2. A variety of products can be produced from the CO2RR process. To date, C1 products (e.g., CO and HCOOH) are the most prevalent, with Faradaic efficiencies (FE) approaching 100%3,4. However, multi-carbon (C2+) products (e.g., ethanol, ethylene, n-propanol, acetone) are more desirable due to their higher energy density and commercial value5,6. Despite this advantage, only a few C2 products, such as ethylene and ethanol, have achieved significant activity7,8. For C3 products, the more complex reaction kinetics, which involve not only C1–C1 coupling but also C1–C2 coupling, make achieving high activity and selectivity is more challenging9,10. For instance, the reported FE for acetone in the electrochemical CO2RR has been limited to below 40% (Supplementary Table 1).

During the electrochemical conversion of CO2 to C2+ products, the reaction pathway typically involves a multistep proton-coupled electron transfer process with diverse reaction intermediates and transition states11. Consequently, modulating the interactions between reaction intermediates and catalyst active sites represents a promising strategy for steering the reaction pathways. It is widely established that *CO serves as the pivotal intermediate for the formation of C2+ products via C–C coupling12. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the adsorption behavior of *CO, such as binding strength and surface coverage, significantly impacts the C–C coupling step, thereby modulating the selectivity for C2+ products13,14. Copper (Cu) is capable of effectively converting CO2 into C2+ products due to its distinct electronic properties15. However, the sluggish C–C coupling kinetics on pure Cu surfaces significantly hinder the efficient production of C2+ products16,17. Accordingly, various strategies—such as defect engineering, doping modifications, tuning the oxidation states, and surface microenvironment modulation—have been developed to improve the selectivity of C2+ products on Cu-base catalysts by modulating the adsorption energy and surface coverage of *CO18,19. Among these, tuning the oxidation state of copper, particularly the formation of Cu0/Cu+ sites, has been shown to be thermodynamically favorable for C2+ formation11,20. The synergistic interaction between Cu0 and Cu+ active species facilitates electron transfer during CO2 activation, promotes the adsorption and activation of key intermediates such as *CO, and stabilizes the transition states required for C–C bond formation, thereby enhancing the C–C coupling process21,22. Furthermore, the enhanced adsorption of these critical intermediates in turn stabilizes the Cu+ species against reduction23. Nanoconfinement strategies have shown significant advantages in suppressing catalyst agglomeration and leaching, stabilizing the valence states of metal species, increasing the surface coverage of intermediates, and promoting C–C coupling24–26. Additionally, in-tandem catalysis has emerged as an efficient strategy for promoting C2+ production27,28. Compared to single-active-site catalysts, in-tandem catalysts feature two types of active sites, allowing for substantial adjustments in the interaction between reaction intermediates and catalytic sites, and thus showing considerable promise for improving the selectivity of C2+ production29,30. In-tandem catalysis for CO2–to–C2+ process can be decoupled into two individual steps of CO2–to–CO and CO–to–C2+, where the CO intermediate connects the two steps31,32. To date, several Cu-based in-tandem catalysts have been developed for CO2RR to C2+ products, such as Ag@Cu2O33, AgI-CuO34, Au-bipy-Cu35, and Au/Cu NRs36. However, these catalysts all suffer from the use of high-cost noble metals (Au and Ag) to achieve an efficient CO2–to–CO process. Besides, in most cases, C2 products such as ethanol and ethylene are mainly obtained with high selectivity. Developing in tandem catalysts with high selectivity for C3 products is still challenging. Therefore, it is urgent to develop in-tandem catalysts by combining non-noble-metal active sites for producing CO with Cu active sites for promoting C–C coupling kinetics, particularly for enhancing the selectivity towards C3 products.

Downsizing metal active sites to the few-atom scale has been demonstrated to be effective in boosting their electrocatalytic performances37. Compared with conventional metal nanoparticles/bulks, atomic-scale catalysts, such as single-atom catalysts (SACs) and single-cluster catalysts (SCCs), offer advantages such as high atomic utilization efficiency, well-defined coordination environment, and reliable durability37,38. Ultrafine nanoclusters provide the combined advantage of maximize active site exposure while retaining high atomic utilization, leading to substantially enhanced activity37. SACs with transition metal atoms (e.g., Co, Fe, Ni) have been proved to be active substitutes for noble metal catalysts for CO2RR to CO39. In particular, nickel single atom catalyst (Ni SAC) exhibited high activity toward CO2–to–CO conversion within a broad potential range, making them one of the promising low-cost catalysts for CO2–to–CO conversion40,41. Low-coordination Ni SAC has been demonstrated to be more conducive to the efficient electrochemical reduction of CO2 to CO40. Additionally, copper single cluster catalyst (Cu SCC) has been suggested to be an effective platform for CO2RR to C2+ products. The electronic behavior of the Cu SCC can be effectively regulated by varying the cluster size, coordination geometry, etc., resulting in distinctive CO2RR properties42–44. For example, Peng et al. reported that a Cu cluster and a partially reduced O-containing hexaphyrin ligand are highly potent in converting CO2 into alcohols, with ethanol and n-propanol FEs of up to 32.5% and 18.3%, respectively43. The Xu group prepared Cun clusters (n = 3 and 4) and achieved an ethanol FE of up to 91% at a low potential of −0.7 V vs. RHE (reversible hydrogen electrode)44. Hence, incorporating Ni SAC with Cu SCC as an in-tandem catalyst is anticipated to enhance CO2RR performance and offer promising selectivity for C3 production.

In this study, we report a synergistic strategy that integrates in-tandem electrocatalysis, confinement effect, and in situ-formed mixed-valence Cu0/Cu+ sites. In this system, Ni single-atom sites selectively reduce CO2 to CO, which then spills over to adjacent in situ reconstructed Cu sites (Cu0/Cu+) for subsequent C–C coupling. The confinement effect of the hierarchical pore structure in the reduced graphene oxide (rGO) support facilitates the enrichment of intermediates. Meanwhile, the in situ-formed Cu0/Cu+ sites enhance the adsorption of the key *CO intermediate. This synergy lowers the energy barriers for C–C coupling and thereby facilitates acetone formation (Fig. 1a). In a flow cell with 1.0 M KOH electrolyte, a total FE of 84.1% for C2+ products (ethanol, ethylene glycol, and acetone) was achieved at −0.6 V vs. RHE, with stable operation for over 20 h. The FE for acetone (a C3 product) reached 48.8%.

Results

Synthesis and characterization of catalysts

The Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalyst was synthesized through a two-step annealing process, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. The process began with the annealing of a graphene oxide (GO)/NiCl2 precursor at 900 °C to form Ni SAC, followed by the introduction of CuCl2 and a subsequent annealing step at 550 °C, whereby Cu SCC was incorporated to construct the final Ni SAC/Cu SCC composite. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images (Supplementary Fig. 3) showed that the Ni SAC catalyst comprised thin, crumpled sheets without any large metal-based nanoparticles. After the second annealing step, which loaded the Cu SCC onto this Ni SAC-modified support, the material retained its thin and crumpled sheet-like morphology (Fig. 1b). Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image illustrated the coexistence of Cu SCC and Ni SAC on the support without the formation of large nanoparticles (Fig. 1c). The graphitic carbon layers around the Cu SCC are enriched with atomically dispersed Ni sites, which ensures close spatial proximity between the two catalytic components, thereby forming the key structural foundation for their in-tandem catalysis. The average size of Cu SCC was determined to be ~2.6 nm (Supplementary Fig. 5). The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis indicated that C, Ni, and Cu elements were uniformly dispersed on the support (Fig. 1d). Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) results revealed that the Ni and Cu contents in Ni SAC/Cu SCC were 0.19 and 0.26 wt%, respectively (Supplementary Table 2).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of all samples show only two broad diffraction peaks at ~23.7° and ~43.4°, which were assigned to the (002) and (100) planes of the rGO substrate, respectively (Fig. 2a)45. Additionally, no diffraction peaks associated with Ni or Cu nanoparticles were detected, consistent with the TEM results and further ruling out the presence of large metallic aggregates on the rGO matrix. Raman spectra exhibited the characteristic features of the rGO substrate for all samples (Supplementary Fig. 6), where the D-band located at ~1347 cm−1 and the G-band located at ~1589 cm−1 were attributed to the disorder-induced defects and the symmetry-allowed graphite band, respectively46. The broad peak centered at ~2800 cm−1 was attributed to the 2D band of rGO46. To further investigate the surface area and porosity of the as-prepared samples, N2 adsorption-desorption measurements were carried out. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, all isotherms exhibit a typical type-IV curve with a hysteresis loop, originating from the presence of mesopores47. Meanwhile, the rapid increase in N2 uptake at relative pressure P/P0 < 0.01 indicates the coexistence of abundant micropores46. The hierarchical pore structure can promote mass diffusion and transfer during the CO2RR process48. Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas of Ni SAC, Cu SCC, and Ni SAC/Cu SCC were determined to be 486, 427, and 395 m2 g−1, respectively, all lower than that of the rGO support (512 m2 g−1). This indicates that Ni and Cu species are confined within the hierarchical pores of rGO, which facilitates the stabilization of the metal species and promotes the enrichment of reaction intermediates23–26. In addition, a water contact angle of 117° was measured for Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Supplementary Fig. 8), demonstrating hydrophobicity, which can create abundant gas-filled gaps and facilitate the accumulation of gaseous CO2 at the electrode/electrolyte interface, thereby enhancing the CO2RR performance49. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was conducted to investigate the chemical state of samples. The Ni 2p3/2 binding energies for Ni SAC (855.0 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 9) and Ni SAC/Cu SCC (854.8 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 10) are situated between those of metallic Ni0 (853.0 eV) and Ni2+ (855.7 eV), indicating a valence state between 0 and +2 for Ni species48. The Cu 2p XPS spectra of Cu SCC (Supplementary Fig. 11) and Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Fig. 2b) show characteristic peaks of Cu0 at ~932.5 eV (Cu 2p3/2) and ~952.5 eV (Cu 2p1/2)21. The high-resolution C 1 s XPS spectra of all samples consistently showed three peaks at ~284.8, ~285.8, and ~288.9 eV (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13), which were attributed to C–C/C = C, C–O, and O–C = O, respectively50. Additionally, a weak peak at ~283.9 eV was observed in Ni SAC and Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 13), corresponding to C-Ni bonding51. To further probe the local coordination environment and electronic structure of Ni SAC/Cu SCC, X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) measurements were conducted. For the Ni species, the absorption edge in the Ni K-edge XANES spectrum (Fig. 2d) is positioned between those of Ni foil and NiO, indicating an oxidation state between 0 and +2 for the Ni species in Ni SAC/Cu SCC, consistent with the aforementioned XPS results. To reveal the coordination environment of Ni species in Ni SAC/Cu SCC, the wavelet transform (WT) analysis of the EXAFS spectra was performed (Supplementary Fig. 15). The WT contour plot exhibits a single intensity maximum at ~4.2 Å−1, which is attributable to Ni–C scattering. The absence of a Ni–Ni coordination peak indicates the atomic dispersion of Ni species. Furthermore, the Fourier transform of the EXAFS (FT-EXAFS) spectra was also performed (Fig. 2e). Like NiPc, which only displayed a characteristic peak at ~1.45 Å arising from the first coordination shell of Ni–N, Ni SAC/Cu SCC exhibited a predominant peak at ~1.57 Å, which is associated with the scattering path of Ni–C bond. Moreover, the Ni–Ni peak at ~2.17 Å was not observed, confirming the atomic dispersion of Ni species. Additionally, a least-squares EXAFS fitting was conducted to study the quantitative structure of Ni species in Ni SAC/Cu SCC. According to the fitting results (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 16, and Supplementary Table 3), it can be concluded that each Ni atom is coordinated to three C atoms with an average Ni–C bond length of 1.99 Å, suggesting the Ni–C3 moiety. For the Cu species, the Cu K-edge XANES spectrum (Fig. 2g) exhibits an absorption edge position and spectral line shape nearly identical to those of Cu foil, confirming its metallic state (Cu0). The corresponding FT-EXAFS spectrum (Fig. 2h) displays a dominant peak at ~2.24 Å, attributable to Cu–Cu metallic bonding, thereby verifying the existence of Cu clusters. The WT analysis (Supplementary Fig. 18) shows a distinct intensity maximum at ~7.8 Å−1, corresponding to the Cu–Cu coordination. Quantitative EXAFS fitting (Fig. 2i, Supplementary Fig. 19, and Supplementary Table 4) further refines the local structure, yielding a Cu–Cu coordination number of 10 with a bond distance of 2.54 Å.

Electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction

The CO2RR performance was assessed using a flow cell with 1.0 M KOH electrolyte to overcome the low solubility of CO2 and suppress the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) to favor C–C coupling (Supplementary Fig. 20). All potentials in this work are reported versus RHE. As depicted by linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves in Fig. 3a, all catalysts demonstrated higher current densities when CO2 was fed to the gas diffusion layer than when using Ar, manifesting their CO2 reduction ability. Ni SAC/Cu SCC delivered much higher current densities than either Ni SAC or Cu SCC alone, achieving a current density of 357 mA cm-2 at -1.2 V under CO2 atmosphere (Fig. 3a). To investigate the products from CO2 reduction, the gas- and liquid-phase counterparts at different applied potentials were detected by gas chromatography (GC) and 1H nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR), respectively. As shown in Fig. 3b, only CO and H2 products could be detected for the Ni SAC catalyst. The FE of CO exceeded 90% over a broad potential range from −0.5 to −1.1 V, and reached its maximum value of 99.4% at −0.7 V. For Cu SCC (Fig. 3c), the CO2 reduction process yields not only CO and methanol (CH3OH) but also C2+ products, including acetone (CH3COCH3), ethanol (C2H5OH), and ethylene glycol (C2H6O2), with the maximum FE of C2+ products reaching 63.2% at −0.6 V. Among these products, ethanol exhibited the highest selectivity, achieving an FE of up to 40% at −0.5 V. Acetone, a C3 product, was formed with a maximum FE of 22% at -0.6 V. For comparison, a commercial Cu nanoparticle (Cu NPs) was used as a control sample, with detailed structural information provided in Supplementary Fig. 21. The Cu NPs displayed numerous reduction products (formate, ethylene, ethanol, methanol, and CO) but with poor activity and selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 22). The maximum FE for C2+ products was only 26% at -0.6 V, and it quickly dropped with more negative potentials. Additionally, no C3 products were detected across the entire potential range. These results highlight the enhanced C2+ production capability of Cu SCC. The performance for C2+ product formation was enhanced on the Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalyst, as shown in Figs. 3d–f and Supplementary Fig. 23. The maximum FE for C2+ products (Fig. 3e) and the corresponding partial current density (Supplementary Fig. 23) reached 84.1% at -0.6 V and 119 mA cm−2 at -0.8 V, respectively, which are 1.3 and 1.7 times higher than those of Cu SCC. The formate and ethylene observed on commercial Cu NPs were not detected on Ni SAC/Cu SCC. The selectivity for acetone was improved on Ni SAC/Cu SCC, with the highest FE reaching 48.8% at −0.6 V, representing a 2.2-fold increase compared to Cu SCC (Fig. 3d). Subsequently, as the applied potential was further negatively shifted, the acetone FE gradually decreased due to competing HER. The acetone partial current density on Ni SAC/Cu SCC was 53.9 mA cm-2 at −0.6 V and reached a maximum of 68.7 mA cm-2 at -0.9 V, both substantially surpassing the values obtained on Cu SCC (Fig. 3f). Concurrently, the acetone yield for Ni SAC/Cu SCC attained 143 μmol h-1 cm-2 at −0.6 V and peaked at 184 μmol h-1 cm-2 at -0.8 V, compared with 44 and 62 μmol h-1 cm-2 on Cu SCC at the respective potentials (Supplementary Fig. 26). These results suggest that the high activity and selectivity of Ni SAC for CO generation promote CO spillover to Cu SCC, achieving high *CO coverage on Cu SCC that enhances subsequent C–C coupling, thereby boosting C2+ product generation on Ni SAC/Cu SCC. The FE values for acetone and C2+ products on Ni SAC/Cu SCC are competitive with those of reported catalysts (Supplementary Table 1). The stability test was carried out at −0.6 V. As shown in Fig. 3g, within 20 h of continuous electrolysis, both the current density and FE of products remained nearly constant. In situ inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) measurements revealed no significant dissolution of Ni and Cu from the Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Supplementary Fig. 27). This is associated with the confinement effect of the hierarchical pore structure in the rGO support, which stabilizes the catalyst and suppresses the dissolution of metal species (Supplementary Fig. 7)24. TEM, XRD, and XPS analyses of Ni SAC/Cu SCC after electrolysis showed that the catalyst maintained its overall bulk structure, although partial surface oxidation of Cu0 to Cu+ species was observed (Supplementary Figs. 28–30). Additionally, the Ni and Cu contents in the post-electrolysis Ni SAC/Cu SCC, as determined by ICP-AES, were 0.17 and 0.23 wt%, respectively, showing no significant change compared to the pristine sample (Ni: 0.19 wt%, Cu: 0.26 wt%) (Supplementary Table 8). To verify the origin of the generated products, a control experiment was conducted on Ni SAC/Cu SCC using Ar as the feed gas; under these conditions, CH3COCH3, C2H5OH, C2H6O2, CH3OH, and CO were not detected, demonstrating that these generated products indeed originated from the reduction of CO2 (Supplementary Figs. 31 and 32). The 13CO2 isotope labeling experiment further confirmed that the produced CH3COCH3 originated from the CO2 reactant (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34)52.

Mechanism analysis

To track the valence state and structural evolution of the Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalyst during the CO2RR process, in situ XPS and in situ XAS characterization were performed. For the Ni species, in situ Ni 2p XPS spectra revealed no shift in the binding energies of the Ni 2p3/2 and Ni 2p1/2 peaks during the CO2RR process (Supplementary Fig. 35), indicating that the Ni species maintained their initial oxidation state. Furthermore, Ni K-edge XANES (Fig. 4a) and FT-EXAFS spectra (Fig. 4b) at various electrolysis times were nearly identical to those before electrolysis, confirming that the Ni species retained the Ni-C3 coordination environment under CO2RR conditions. For the Cu species, in situ Cu 2p XPS spectra revealed no characteristic peaks of Cu2+ (Supplementary Fig. 36), confirming that no Cu2+ species were formed. Given that the Cu 2p binding energies of Cu+ and Cu0 are similar, precluding clear discrimination between the two species, we further analyzed the Cu LMM Auger spectra (Fig. 4c). A new characteristic Cu+ peak at a binding energy of 570.2 eV emerged within 20 s and stabilized within 60 s, indicating rapid partial oxidation of surface Cu0 to Cu+ species under CO2RR conditions21. This finding is further corroborated by in situ Raman spectroscopy (Fig. 4d), which revealed characteristic Cu2O vibrational modes at ~515 and ~623 cm-1. An additional band at ~712 cm-1 was observed, attributed to the Cu–OH vibration likely arising from •OH radicals53. This suggests that the observed Cu+ formation is driven by oxidative •OH radicals, consistent with recent reports53,54. Generally, Cu+ species are thermodynamically unstable under reducing potentials and tend to be reduced back to Cu0. However, as shown in Fig. 4c, the Cu+ signal, once formed, stabilizes within 60 s, indicating that Cu species reach a dynamic equilibrium between Cu0 and Cu+ during the CO2RR process, driven by the competing effects of cathodic reduction and oxidation by •OH radicals (Supplementary Fig. 37). The confinement effect of the hierarchical pore structure in the rGO support is also beneficial for stabilizing Cu+ species (Supplementary Fig. 7)23,24,26. Furthermore, in situ XAS was employed to track the structural changes of Cu sites during the CO2RR process (Fig. 4e, f, Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39, and Supplementary Table 9). The Cu K-edge XANES spectra recorded within the first 10 min of electrolysis reveal that the average valence state of Cu lies between 0 and +1, and this state remains stable throughout the subsequent electrolysis (Fig. 4e). The corresponding FT-EXAFS spectra show the emergence of a peak at ~1.38 Å assigned to Cu–O scattering in Cu2O within the initial 10 min, indicating the partial oxidation of metallic Cu to Cu2O under CO2RR conditions (Fig. 4f, Supplementary Fig. 38, and Supplementary Table 9). No marked spectral changes are observed thereafter, suggesting that the structure of the Cu sites remains stable throughout the subsequent electrolysis. Wavelet transform analysis of the Cu K-edge was further performed (Supplementary Fig. 39). In addition to the intense signal centered at ~7.6 Å-1 attributable to Cu–Cu scattering, a weak signal originating from Cu–O contributions is detected at ~5.7 Å-1, which further confirms the in situ formation of Cu2O during electrolysis. To further identify the active sites of Cu species, selective poisoning experiments were performed. According to the literature, SCN⁻, as a strong ligand for Lewis acidic sites, can selectively adsorb onto Cu+ sites in a system where Cu0 and Cu+ coexist, forming an SCN⁻–Cu+ complex that poisons the Cu+ sites and thereby prevents them from participating in the reaction55,56. Therefore, SCN⁻ was used as a selective inhibitor to identify the active sites of the Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) catalysts. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 40, upon adding KSCN to the electrolyte, the current density of CO2RR and the FE of C2+ products decreased, indicating that Cu+ sites play a pivotal role in the reduction of CO2 to C2+ products. C2+ products were still obtained with an FE of 22.6% in the presence of KSCN, suggesting that Cu0 sites also contribute to the CO2RR to C2+ products. These results confirm that the Cu0/Cu+ constitutes the active sites for CO2RR.

To gain insight into the reaction intermediates, in situ Raman spectroscopy was performed. The distinct signals at ~286 and ~354 cm-1 were observed, corresponding to the frustrated rotational mode [ρ(Cu–C–O)] and the stretching mode [ν(Cu–CO)] of adsorbed CO, respectively (Fig. 4d)53. Their intensity increased with more negative potentials and peaked at −0.6 V. In the Raman region of 800–3000 cm-1, we conducted isotopic labeling experiments using 13CO2 and D2O (Supplementary Fig. 42). The band at ~2038 cm-1, associated with the C ≡ O stretching vibration of *CO57, exhibited a red shift to ~1988 cm-1 upon 13CO2 substitution. As the reducing potential became more negative, the band intensity gradually decreased, suggesting the consumption and subsequent conversion of *CO. The band at ~1668 cm-1 shifted to ~1639 cm-1 with 13CO2 substitution, but remained essentially unchanged under D2O, allowing its assignment to a C = O stretching mode, likely originating from acetone58. Similarly, the band at ~2863 cm-1 shifted to ~2120 cm-1 with D2O, supporting its assignment to the C–H stretching of –CH3 groups associated with the production of methanol, acetone, ethanol, and ethylene glycol59. The bands at ~886 and ~1050 cm-1 displayed red shifts under 13CO2, suggesting a contribution from carbon-containing adsorbates. Specifically, the band at ~886 cm-1 may originate from either the C–C–O stretching of ethanol/ethylene glycol or the out-of-plane bending of CO32- formed by the reaction of CO2 with KOH59,60. Likewise, the band at ~1050 cm-1 may arise from either the C–O stretching vibration of CO32- or the C–OH stretching of methanol59,61. In the Raman region of 1300–1500 cm-1, reliable identification of intermediates is difficult owing to the strong carbon background interference (Supplementary Fig. 6). To further capture the reaction intermediates, in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was conducted on the Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalyst. As shown in Fig. 4g, as a reducing potential was applied, a band at ~1608 cm-1 emerged, corresponding to the asymmetric stretching of the *COOH intermediate, a key species for CO formation62. Simultaneously, a peak at ~2062 cm-1 appeared and gradually intensified with prolonged electrolysis, which is assigned to the C ≡ O stretch of *CO, a crucial intermediate for subsequent C–C coupling63. With prolonged electrolysis, new vibrational features emerged at ~1570, ~1543, and ~1481 cm-1, assigned to *COCO, *COCOH, and *COCH3 intermediates, respectively64. These species are critical intermediates for the formation of C2+ products, including acetone, ethylene glycol, and ethanol. Furthermore, characteristic peaks corresponding to acetone (C = O stretch at ~1716 cm-1) and methanol (C–OH stretch at ~1132 cm-1) were observed and progressively intensified65,66. The bands observed at ~1408 cm-1 and ~1514 cm-1 are attributed to HCO3⁻ and CO32- species, respectively, originating from the reaction of KOH with CO267. Furthermore, to gain deeper insights into the CO2 reduction process, we employed in situ DRIFTS to monitor the differences in reaction intermediates on Cu SCC, Cu NPs, and Ni SAC (Supplementary Figs. 43–45)66. The in situ DRIFTS spectrum of Cu NPs (Supplementary Fig. 43) showed a peak at ~1471 cm-1, corresponding to the *C–CH2 intermediate, a key species for ethylene formation. A peak at ~1371 cm-1 was also observed, attributed to the *OCHO intermediate associated with formate production68. Additionally, weak signals for *CO and *COCO were also detected on Cu NPs. In contrast, on Cu SCC (Supplementary Fig. 44), the *OCHO and *C–CH2 intermediates were not detected, consistent with the absence of formate and ethylene formation on this catalyst. Instead, enhanced peaks for *CO and *COCO, along with an additional vibrational feature assigned to COCOH—all of which are key intermediates for the formation of multi-carbon products—were observed. This is associated with the confinement effect of the hierarchical pore structure in the rGO support (Supplementary Fig. 7). These findings suggest that Cu SCC favors the formation of multi-carbon products. By comparison, on Ni SAC (Supplementary Fig. 45), *COCO and *COCOH were not observed; only *COOH and *CO, key intermediates for CO production, were identified. These results demonstrate that in the Ni SAC/Cu SCC system, Ni SAC primarily facilitates CO generation, whereas Cu SCC promotes C–C coupling, collectively accounting for the enhanced acetone selectivity. Subsequently, we evaluated the influence of solvation and cathodic potential on both *CO–*CO and *CO–*COCH3 coupling. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 52, the ΔG for both *CO–*CO and *CO–*COCH3 coupling decreases with more negative cathodic potentials, confirming that cathodic potential thermodynamically favors C–C bond formation. Additionally, compared to vacuum-based calculations, KOH aqueous solution also reduces the ΔG for both *CO–*CO and *CO–*COCH3 coupling (Supplementary Fig. 53 and Supplementary Data 1–3). We further explored the role of the local CO concentration in *CO–*CO and *CO–*COCH3 coupling. As depicted in Supplementary Fig. 54, higher CO coverage significantly reduced the energy barriers for both *CO–*CO and *CO–*COCH3 coupling. Based on in situ Raman, in situ DRIFTS, DFT results, and the literature5, a plausible pathway for CH3COCH3 formation was proposed as: *CO2 → *COOH → *CO → *COCO → *COCOH → *COC → *COCH → *COCH2 → *COCH3 → *COCOCH3 → *COHCOCH3 → *CCOCH3 → *CHCOCH3 → *CH2COCH3 → *CH3COCH3.

To further verify the existence of in-tandem electrocatalysis between Ni sites and Cu sites in the Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) catalyst and explore the influence of the Cu0/Cu+ sites on CO adsorption, we performed CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) measurements on Ni SAC, Cu SCC (Cu0), and Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) (Fig. 4h). Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) exhibited a CO desorption temperature of 219 °C, higher than that of Ni SAC (130 °C), demonstrating the stronger CO adsorption affinity of Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+). This provides a thermodynamic driving force for the spillover of CO generated on Ni sites to the adjacent Cu sites. Furthermore, Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) exhibited a higher CO desorption temperature (219 °C) than that of Cu SCC (Cu0) (188 °C), indicating that the Cu0/Cu+ sites enhance CO binding strength, a critical factor for subsequent C–C coupling. To further confirm the CO spillover from Ni sites to Cu sites in the Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) catalyst, we conducted selective poisoning experiments monitored by in situ Raman spectroscopy. We first compared the in situ Raman spectra of CO adsorbed on Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) and Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) in a CO-saturated electrolyte at open-circuit potential (Supplementary Fig. 56). The nearly identical Cu–CO peak intensities indicate that the introduction of Ni sites does not alter the intrinsic CO adsorption capability of the Cu sites. To establish the selectivity of KSCN poisoning, we separately treated Ni SAC and Cu SCC with KSCN solution. The FECO of Ni SAC dropped sharply (Fig. 4i), whereas the activity of Cu SCC remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 4j), confirming that KSCN selectively poisons the Ni single-atom sites with negligible effect on the Cu nanoclusters. Having established these controls, we selectively poisoned the Ni single-atom sites in Ni SAC/Cu SCC with KSCN solution and subsequently monitored the Cu–CO signal under CO2RR conditions (Fig. 4k). After poisoning the Ni sites, the Cu–CO peak intensity decreased to a level equivalent to that of pure Cu SCC, which can be attributed to the interruption of CO supply from the Ni sites to the Cu sites, thereby reducing the Cu–CO signal. These results provide strong evidence for the spillover of CO generated at Ni sites to Cu sites. The high selectivity for acetone on Ni SAC/Cu SCC (Cu0/Cu+) is attributed to the synergy of in-tandem electrocatalysis, confinement effect, and in situ-formed mixed-valence Cu0/Cu+ sites. Specifically, Ni single atom sites catalyze the highly selective reduction of CO2 to CO, which subsequently spills over to the adjacent in situ reconstructed Cu sites (Cu0/Cu+), where C–C coupling occurs (Fig. 4l). The confinement effect of the hierarchical pore structure in the rGO support facilitates the enrichment of intermediates (Supplementary Fig. 7). Consequently, the in-tandem effect and confinement effect significantly elevate the local *CO concentration on the Cu0/Cu+ sites. Meanwhile, the in situ reconstructed Cu0/Cu+ sites enhance the adsorption of the key *CO intermediate. This synergy collectively lowers the energy barriers for C–C coupling, thereby promoting acetone production.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalyst for CO2 electroreduction, attaining an FE of 84.1% for C2+ products (including acetone, ethanol, and ethylene glycol) at -0.6 V vs. RHE. In particular, it exhibits an FE of 48.8% for acetone (a C3 product). The high acetone selectivity arises from the synergy of three factors: in-tandem catalysis between Ni sites and Cu sites, the confinement effect of the hierarchical porous rGO support, and the in situ-formed Cu0/Cu+ sites. Together, these factors increase local CO coverage via CO spillover and confinement, enhance adsorption of the key *CO intermediate through Cu0/Cu+ sites, and lower the energy barriers for C–C coupling, thereby favoring acetone formation. This work presents an integrated design strategy combining in-tandem electrocatalysis, confinement effects, and dynamic valence engineering, offering a promising route for electrosynthesizing high-value multi-carbon products from CO2.

Methods

Chemicals and materials

All the chemicals were used as received without further purification. Nickel (II) chloride (NiCl2), copper (II) chloride (CuCl2), potassium hydroxide (KOH), potassium permanganate (KMnO4), graphite powder were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethanol (C2H5OH), sulfuric acid (H2SO4), phosphoric acid (H3PO4), hydrochloric acid (HCl) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Carbon dioxide gas (CO2, 99.999%) and argon gas (Ar, 99.999%) were purchased from Nanchang Guoteng Gas Co., Ltd. Deionized water (resistivity > 18.2 MΩ cm) was used in all experiments.

Preparation of graphene oxide (GO)

GO was synthesized using a modified Hummers method69. Specifically, 3 g of graphite powder was uniformly dispersed in a mixture of 360 mL concentrated H2SO4 and 40 mL concentrated H3PO4 under continuous stirring in an ice bath for 4 h. Subsequently, 18 g of KMnO4 was gradually added over 1 hour while maintaining the temperature below 20 °C. The reaction was then allowed to proceed in an oil bath at 50 °C with stirring for 12 hours. The resulting mixture was poured into 400 mL of deionized water containing 3 mL of H2O2 to reduce residual oxidants, resulting in a bright yellow solution. The obtained GO suspension was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm and sequentially washed with 5% HCl solution and deionized water until the supernatant reached a pH of ~7. The final product was collected and freeze-dried to yield GO.

Preparation of Ni SAC, Cu SCC, and Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalysts

To prepare the Ni SAC, an aqueous GO dispersion (30 mL, 2 mg mL-1) was mixed with NiCl2 (0.3 mmol) and ultrasonicated for 1 h to form a homogeneous suspension. The solvent was removed by rotary evaporation, and the resulting solid was annealed under an argon atmosphere by heating to 900 °C at a ramp rate of 2 °C min-1, followed by a 2-hour hold at the target temperature. After cooling to room temperature ( ~ 25 °C), the product was successively washed with 1 M HCl and deionized water, then dried to obtain the Ni SAC catalyst. The Cu SCC and Ni SAC/Cu SCC catalysts were synthesized through a unified procedure with different solid precursors. Pure GO was used to prepare the Cu SCC, whereas the as-prepared Ni SAC served as the precursor for the Ni SAC/Cu SCC. In a typical synthesis, the respective solid precursor was dispersed in ethanol (30 mL, 2 mg mL-1). Subsequently, CuCl2 (0.3 mmol) was added to the mixture, which was then ultrasonicated for 30 min. After removing the solvent by rotary evaporation, the resulting solid was annealed under an argon atmosphere. The thermal protocol involved heating to 550 °C at a ramp rate of 2 °C min-1 and then holding at this temperature for 2 hours. Finally, the system was cooled to room temperature ( ~ 25 °C) under argon, obtaining the final catalyst product.

Characterizations

The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images were obtained by using a FEI Talos F200X microscope. High-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images were collected on a JEOL ARM 200 F microscope. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were taken on a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were tested on a Thermo Fisher Scientific Escalab 250Xi using a monochromatic Al-Kα source. Raman spectra were acquired using a Confocal LabRam HR800 spectrometer equipped with a laser wavelength of 532 nm. The in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy was obtained using a Nicolet IS50-FTIR spectrometer equipped with an MCT-A detector. The CO temperature programmed desorption (CO-TPD) tests were conducted on a Micromeritics Autochem 2920 equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). The in situ dissolution of Ni and Cu was monitored using a PerkinElmer NexION 2000 ICP-MS. The loading of Cu and Ni was measured on an inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometer (ICP-AES) on an Optima 7300 DV. X-ray absorption spectra, including the X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS), were attained on the BL01C1 beamline at National Synchrotron Radiation Research Center. The radiation was monochromatized by a Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. All spectra were obtained in the fluorescence mode. The N2 adsorption/desorption curves were tested using a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 adsorption apparatus at 77 K.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical measurements were conducted in a custom-designed flow cell connected to an electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E). The cathodic and anodic chambers were separated by an anion exchange membrane (Fumasep FAA-PK-130, 130 μm, 1.5 × 1.5 cm2). The membrane was pretreated by immersion in a 0.5 M KCl aqueous solution for 48 hours, followed by immersion in a 1.0 M KOH aqueous solution for 48 hours. The IrO2/Ti mesh and Hg/HgO electrode served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The reference electrode was calibrated using a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) before measurements. The working electrode was prepared as follows: 10 mg catalyst was mixed with 100 μL Nafion solution (5%) and 1 mL ethanol; the mixture was sonicated for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous catalyst ink; then, 100 μL of the ink was loaded onto a gas diffusion layer (Sigracet 29 BC, 1 × 1 cm2) with a mass loading of ~1 mg cm−2. The resistance (R) for Ni SAC, Cu SCC, and Ni SAC/Cu SCC is 3.61 ± 0.18 Ω, 3.39 ± 0.15 Ω, and 3.10 ± 0.26 Ω, respectively. These values were determined via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) using a 1 cm2 working electrode. A 100% iR compensation was applied manually to each potential. All potentials were then converted to the RHE scale and corrected according to the equation: E (V vs. RHE) = E (V vs. Hg/HgO) + 0.098 V + 0.0591 × pH - 100%iR. The electrochemical experiments were performed at room temperature ( ~ 25 °C) and atmospheric pressure. During electrolysis, CO2 gas was delivered into the cathodic compartment at a rate of 20 sccm, controlled by a mass flow controller. 1.0 M KOH solution was used as electrolyte (pH 13.9 ± 0.2), and circulated through the cathode compartment at a rate of 10 mL min-1. The fresh electrolyte was prepared using a volumetric flask before the testing. The gaseous and liquid products from CO2 reduction were quantified by a gas chromatograph (GC, 7890B) and a Bruker 400 MHz 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrometer, respectively. DFT calculations

All DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)70. The exchange-correlation effects were described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)71. The core-valence interactions were described by the projected augmented wave (PAW) method72. The energy cutoff for plane wave expansions was set to 450 eV, and a 3 × 3 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack grid k-points was used to sample the Brillouin zone. The vacuum space of 15 Å was introduced above the surfaces to avoid periodic interactions. The structural optimization was completed for energy and force convergence set at 1.0×10-4 eV and 0.02 eV Å-1, respectively.
