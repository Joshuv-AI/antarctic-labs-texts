Use of AI agents to assess preoperative frailty in cancer patients
Authors: Denise T Lee, Steven Yen, Georgia Smits, David S Matteson, Noah Cohen, Eyal Klang, Girish N Nadkarni, Michael L Marin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssessment of preoperative frailty is essential for risk stratification and optimization of patients undergoing major abdominal surgery for hepatobiliary (HPB) and gastrointestinal (GI) malignancies. Large language model (LLM)-based agents may facilitate automated frailty scoring, thereby supporting targeted prehabilitation efforts to improve surgical outcomes. We compared the performance of an agentic LLM system with a single LLM system in calculating frailty scores and classifying frailty status. Using real-world preoperative notes documented within 60 days prior to surgery, we evaluated both approaches. The agentic LLM system outperformed the single LLM system in 4 out of 6 models for a binary frailty classification task and in 5 out of 6 models for generating patient Risk Assessment Index (RAI) scores. The greatest improvements were observed among lower-parameter models (Llama 3.1 8b, Qwen 2.5 7b). These findings suggest than an agentic workflow may enhance frailty assessment performance. Automating frailty score calculation from Electronic Health Record (EHR) data may enhance clinical efficiency and enable targeted prehabilitation strategies for patients with cancer undergoing surgery.Subject terms: Cancer, Health care, Medical research, Oncology

Introduction

Frail patients are well-known to experience higher rates of post operative complications such as delirium, prolonged hospitalization, readmission and short and long-term mortality1–3. Improved risk stratification of frailty prior to surgery allows for targeted prehabilitation strategies to reduce adverse outcomes4. However, despite the development of up to eleven frailty assessment tools validated in surgical populations, routine administration of frailty surveys is commonly overlooked in clinical practice5. The automation of frailty assessment from clinical electronic health records (EHR) is an opportunity to recognize and intervene on a vulnerable surgical population to improve postoperative outcomes.

Frontier LLMs have demonstrated impressive performance across a wide range of traditional health care tasks that can be automated, such as medical question and answering6,7, clinical report summarization8–10 and data extraction from narrative reports11–15. However, LLMs face challenges that limit their clinical deployment. With increasing task complexity and density of data, LLMs are prone to hallucinate—or produce seemingly coherent, evidence-based output that on closer inspection contains erroneous information16. Prompt engineering techniques such as chain of thought (CoT) prompting and retrieval augmented generation (RAG) are used to mitigate LLM hallucinations17.

Surprisingly, despite these mitigation strategies, some LLM models still perform poorly on tasks such as simple arithmetic18. A lack of quantitative proficiency is especially problematic as LLMs become increasingly incorporated into clinical decision support tools where accurate calculations are critical to surgical decision making. For example, frailty assessment tools such as the Risk Analysis Index (RAI) require evaluation and summation of deficits across multiple physiologic domains (functional status, cognition, nutrition and medical co-morbidities), which are often documented in EHR5,19,20. The aptitude for current LLMs to interpret complex medical data and calculate clinical scores from EHR remains unclear.

To address these challenges, larger and newer generation LLM models utilizing tens of billions of parameters are increasingly tasked to achieve higher performance and accuracy metrics. An alternative solution exploits the use of smaller, more efficient models that distribute multiple cognitive subtasks across multiple LLM agents. To overcome limitations of single LLMs systems that respond to tasks instructed from a single prompt, agentic LLM systems are capable of executing multiple prompts and tasks concurrently21. LLM agents are poised to maximize the use of LLMs in clinical medicine beyond standard natural language processing (NLP) tasks and may provide meaningful improvement in performance while remaining accessible to healthcare systems.

In this study we evaluate the ability of an agentic LLM to evaluate and utilize 60-day preoperative clinical notes extracted from the EHR to calculate RAI frailty scores in patients undergoing surgery for an abdominal malignancy. We compared the performance of an agentic LLM system to traditional single LLM system approach and tested multiple open source LLMs of different generations and model sizes. We hypothesized that LLM agents would demonstrate improved performance over single system approaches— presenting an opportunity for LLM agents to provide clinicians preoperative frailty alerts that may allow for targeted prehabilitation interventions aimed at improving outcomes in cancer patients undergoing surgery.

Results

Patient Demographics

204 patients were included in this study. Based on patient reported frailty scores, 149 (73.04%) patients were classified as frail (RAI score > = 21) and 55 (26.96%) patients were classified as not frail. Compared to not frail patients, frail patients were older (median age 63 versus 57, p < 0.001), more likely to be male (female 31.54% versus 96.36%, p < 0.001) and with lower BMI (25.58 vs. 27.26, p = 0.05). Frail patients also scored higher in the “age, sex, cancer status” (25 vs. 20, p < 0.001) and “medical co-morbidities” (4 vs. 0, p < 0.001) subcategories of the RAI survey than non-frail patients. There were no significant differences in race, tumor origin or in the cognitive and ADL RAI subcategory scores (Supplementary Table 1).

Assessment of LLM failure rate

The 6 LLM models were tasked with calculating RAI scores for all patients (N = 204) patients using two workflow systems (agentic and single). This resulted in 12 pairs of different model and workflows (Tables 1, 2). Five of these model-workflow pairs (Llama 3.1 8b-single, Llama 3.1 8b-agent, Llama 3.3 70b-single, Qwen 2.5 7b-single, Qwen3 30b-single) were unable to calculate RAI scores for all patients (denoted as LLM failure rate). The highest failure rate was the Llama 3.1 8b model using the single agent workflow (N = 41, 20.1%). The reasons for failed outputs included missing output scores and JSON errors. In order to effectively compare performance, missing outputs were imputed using a bootstrap plus multiple imputation approach. Further details on the failed outputs and imputation can be found in Supplementary Table 12 and Supplementary Appendix.

LLM Performance on frailty classification

Figure 1 demonstrates that the agentic LLM system outperformed the single LLM system for all models except Qwen2.5 72b when given a binary frailty classification task (Fig. 1). Accuracy was used to evaluate how well the models performed at this classification task. The differences in accuracy were significant between single and agentic for Llama 3.1 8b and 72b, Qwen 2.5 7b and Mistral-small 3.1 24b. In the single LLM system, accuracy ranged from 0.58, 95% CI 0.52–0.65 (Mistral-small 3.1 24B) to 0.84, 95% CI 0.79–0.89 (Qwen 2.5 72b). In the agentic LLM system, accuracy ranged from 0.74, 95% CI 0.68–0.79 (Llama 3.1 8b) to 0.85, 95% CI 0.80-0.90 (Qwen 3 30b). Additional metrics of performance (precision, recall, F1-score, PR-AUC) are detailed in Supplementary Table 8. Confusion matrices for all models can be found in Supplementary Tables 2–7.

When comparing single and agentic systems within each model, there were significant differences in Spearman’s ρ for all models except Qwen 2.5 72b. For RMSE, there were significant differences in all models except Qwen 3 30b (Supplementary Table 10).

Comparison of qwen2.5 7b versus 72b parameter models

To evaluate the impact of parameter scaling within LLM systems (single and agentic), we compared the performance metrics (accuracy, Spearman’s ρ and RAI RMSE) of Qwen2.5 7b to Qwen 2.5 72b (Table 3). For the binary frailty classification task, Qwen 2.5 72b demonstrated higher accuracy than Qwen 2.5 7b using both single (0.84 vs. 0.69, p < 0.05) and agentic LLM systems (0.82 vs. 0.77, p < 0.05). Discussion

This study evaluated the capability of an agent to assess preoperative clinical notes and calculate RAI frailty scores of cancer patients undergoing surgery. The agent outperformed the single LLM when tasked with providing a frailty classification in 4 of 6 LLM models. The agent also demonstrated greater proficiency with generating RAI scores that more closely aligned with patient-reported scores in 5 of 6 models. These results suggest that agents possess improved capability over traditional LLM approaches with increased task complexity such as interpretation of EHR, calculations and multi-step integration of separate tasks. Lower weight models (Llama 3.1 8b, Qwen 2.5 7b) demonstrated the greatest improvement in performance when utilizing the agentic approach. This distinction underscores the importance of weighing the combination model selection and LLM architecture to optimize the performance of tasks requiring multi-step functions such as clinical decision support tools.

While researchers using current frontier LLM APIs are limited to single systems that respond to individual prompts, agentic AI systems add an additional design layer. These systems leverage chains of LLM calls to integrate multiple agents, each capable of independently executing complex tasks and communicating with one another to synthesize and interpret data22. Such elevated capabilities in autonomy, reasoning and problem solving, especially with longer reasoning chain prompts, distinguishes agentic AI as a tool well-suited for the complexities of clinical reasoning23. Investigators have begun to explore their use in multi agent conversations (MAC) to simulate multidisciplinary discussion to formulate diagnosis and evaluation and work up24. And recently, AI agents have been highlighted as partners to scientists in virtual labs that resulted in the identification of new nanobody binders for SARS-CoV-2 variants, and as independent biomedical AI agents that can perform tasks across different subfields25,26. As the novel use of AI agents are being increasingly explored in theoretical frameworks, we present this study as a practical use case for agentic LLMs to improve the performance of clinical decision support tools, such as frailty surveys.

The utilization of LLMs to assess clinical frailty presents an important opportunity that is being newly explored. Early investigations, however were limited by the use of hypothetical clinical scenarios as the input data27 or the use of older LLM models to provide a frailty prediction alone28. Although the publication of LLM studies has increased rapidly, until 2024 only an estimated 5% have evaluated real patient data29. Most studies have relied instead on clinical vignettes or simulated case scenarios for task-specific queries. Yet, performance metrics of LLMs trained on real patient data better reflect the outcomes that may result from clinical implementation of models. We specifically utilized accessible clinical notes to develop a simpler pipeline that may better reflect real world outcomes from developed models.

We tested open-source LLM models from different generation to provide insight into the performance of both smaller and larger models across generations. We also developed multi-step prompts where the LLMs were asked to both interpret clinical data and to calculate clinical scores. Interestingly, we found that the lower weight models (Llama 3.1 8b, Qwen 2.5 7b) demonstrated improved performance in frailty classification and RAI score calculation by using the agentic LLM system. This finding suggests that the agentic workflow may benefit lower complexity models by splitting a complex task into sub tasks. We can consider a clinical decision support tool as a series of complex tasks and sub-tasks. By breaking down the complex problems into smaller pieces, we aim to enhance the problem-solving capabilities of our LLMs30. However, for higher complexity models, performance varied. Qwen 2.5 72b and Qwen 3 30b were found to have no significant differences in accuracy between single and agentic systems and varying significance in spearman’s ρ and RMSE. These results suggest that higher complexity models may not derive as much performance improvement from an agentic approach. Such insights are relevant when considering the accessibility and affordability of LLM models for healthcare systems. Open source, smaller models may be more feasible for medical centers with more financial constraints to deploy LLMs in a privacy preserving fashion behind institutional firewalls. However, increasingly complex healthcare tasks may be impractical for smaller models to deploy without significant error. Our study aimed to better understand the capability of different model performance using an agentic LLM system. The results suggest that smaller models using an agentic architecture hold comparable performance to larger models.

Our study contains several limitations. Our study population was limited in size due to the inclusion criteria of only patients who had conducted the RAI survey. The capture of 60-day preoperative notes from our EHR may not have been inclusive of information relevant to the RAI survey, given many patients were referred from outside the healthcare system. Furthermore, patients were excluded if their notes exceeding the context window. Future studies may consider using LLM summarization of notes to avoid this limitation. However, additional safeguards would be required to ensure essential clinical details are not inadvertently omitted. LLM failure rates were considered and imputation was performed for missing outputs. Prompt engineering is also an important factor in performance of LLMs and may have been used to improve performance. LLMs can be further improved using full supervised fine-tuning, Reinforcement Learning (RL), or Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)31,32. Future studies may incorporate autonomous agents capable of independently determining which tasks to perform.

It is important to note that, although our study demonstrated modest improvements in agentic performance for 4 of the 6 LLM models tested, the error rates remain too high to support an autonomous approach to LLM-based frailty classification using EHR data. Specifically, the best RMSE was approximately 6 RAI points, which is clinically meaningful given that a score of > 21 denotes frailty. An error of this magnitude could result in misclassification at the patient level. While these findings demonstrate promise, further work is needed before these models can be made available for patient-level clinical decision making. This includes improving prompt engineering, testing further models and agentic architectures, and including external validation to confirm generalizability, as different EHR systems across institutions may influence LLM performance. We are also considering to exploration various techniques to handle missing outputs for LLMs such as GAN modeling33.

In conclusion, this study shows that agentic LLMs improve frailty assessment, particularly in smaller models, while performance in larger models is variable. Despite limitations, the findings support further exploration of agentic architectures in larger datasets and across both open and closed source models to advance their use in preoperative care.

Methods

Patient population and data collection

Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Mount Sinai School of Medicine, in accordance with Mount Sinai’s Federal Wide Assurances (FWA#00005656, FWA#00005651) to the Department of Health and Human Services approved the study ID 18-01293. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection, which involved completion of the RAI survey and prospective collection of their medical data for frailty-related research. We conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data. The study cohort included adult patients ( > 18 years) diagnosed with an abdominal malignancy (colorectal, gastric, pancreatic, peritoneal mesothelioma), who underwent curative intent surgery between 2019 and 2022. All patients provided informed consent for the use of their data in frailty-related research and completed the Risk Analysis Index (RAI) frailty survey20 prior to surgery. Patient demographics, surgery date, and patient-reported RAI scores were securely stored in a REDCap database. An IRB amendment was obtained to authorize the use of retrospective analysis of the database, and for additional data collection from the electronic health record (EHR) utilizing Mount Sinai Data Warehouse (MSDW)’s Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) Common Data Model (CDM) repository. Exclusion criteria included patients with missing clinical notes and with clinical notes that exceeded the context window (Supplementary Fig. 1). Using direct structured query language queries, all clinical notes from 60 days prior to surgery were extracted. Clinical notes included all progress notes, emergency-related notes, operative-related notes, general care notes, communication notes, and pathology reports. It is unlikely RAI scores were recorded in any collected notes, as the RAI assessments were collected and stored in a REDCap database according to study protocol. The notes were then concatenated into one registry per patient. No further preprocessing was done to preserve clinical context. A token counter, tiktoken from OpenAI, was used to estimate the token count for each concatenated registry. A conservative limit of 100k tokens was set as the context window to limit loss of accuracy for the LLM models.

LLM systems

Agentic LLM system architecture

The RAI frailty survey comprises four separate sections: (1) Age, Sex and Cancer, (2) Medical Co-Morbidities, (3) Cognition, Residence and Activity of Daily Living, and (4) Activities of Daily Living and Cognitive Decline. Each section comprises multiple questions that are assigned a point value. The points are added to calculate a score for each section, which are then added to provide a final RAI frailty score20. In this study, we defined our agentic architecture to be a fixed-path pipeline to mirror agentic goal-directed behavior. To design the agentic architecture, separate prompts were created for each section of the RAI survey. LangGraph by LangChain was utilized for the agentic workflow development34. The tasks were to calculate scores for each separate section in the RAI survey and then independently store the scores as shared states (Supplementary Fig. 2). Once the sectional scores were generated, a simple function within the workflow was used to calculate the RAI frailty score using previously annotated states. The output of the workflow includes the RAI frailty score and the generated sectional scores. The workflow allowed for multiple entry points for each RAI sectional score for parallelization and prompt chaining. This permits separate tasks to be independently processed in parallel. All branches converged into one node tasked to sum the sectional scores into a final RAI frailty score.

Single LLM system architecture

To best compare the use of single prompt system versus an agentic workflow system, a stitched version of the agentic workflow was used in this single prompt development. Examples can be seen in Supplementary Appendix. One zero-shot prompt was performed by the LLMs to calculate the sectional scores, and a final RAI score was generated for each patient.

LLM model selection and analysis of RAI scores

To demonstrate usability across different model types with different parameter counts, we selected 6 open-source models that can be deployed behind institutional firewalls. In the month of May 2025, the following LLMs were queried: Llama 3.1 8b, Llama 3.3 70b, Qwen 2.5 7b, Qwen2.5 72b, Qwen 3 30b and Mistral-Small 3.1 24b. These model families (Llama, Qwen, Mistral) represent a release window between early 2024 and mid 2025, and parameter counts between 7b and 72b. Dense models include Llama 3.1, Llama 3.3, Qwen 2.5, and Mistral-Small 3.1. Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model include Qwen 3. Two sizes of Qwen 2.5 (7b and 72b) were selected to evaluate the impact of parameter scaling on performance. Model details can be found in Supplementary Table 10.

For each LLM model, both LLM systems (agentic and single) were tasked to calculate the RAI score of all patients using both clinical notes and age at the date of operation. Preliminary analysis demonstrated that lighter weight models such as Llama 3.1 8b were unable to consistently calculate age from clinical notes. LLMs without reasoning techniques such as chain of thought (CoT) have been shown to have poor performance on arithmetic tasks35. In order to have consistent analysis, age was inserted in the prompt across all models. After the RAI score was generated for each patient, a binary classification of frail/not frail was assigned to each patient (with RAI > = 21 denoting frail20). All computing was done on Mount Sinai’s High Performance Computing (HPC)36.

Evaluation of LLM performance and Statistics

We evaluated LLM performance in two ways. First, RAI scores were converted to a binary frailty classification with the aforementioned threshold of RAI > = 21 denoting “frail.” Second, continuous model-produced RAI scores were compared to patient-reported, ground truth RAI scores using root mean square error (RMSE) and Spearman’s ρ. Spearman’s ρ were also calculated between model RAI scores to detect any substantial discrepancies in model outputs. Classification performance was evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and PR AUC. For each performance metric, bootstrap methods were used to construct 95% confidence intervals as well as perform paired two-sided permutation tests to compare performances between LLM systems. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Adjusted p-values were calculated using the Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction to control the False Discovery Rate37. All statistical analyses were performed in Python 3.12.4.
