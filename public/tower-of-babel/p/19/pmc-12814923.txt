LGBTIQA+ Inclusion in Aged Care: Perspectives From Providers and Community Stakeholders
Authors: Daniel Tissot, Sara Karacsony, Sharon Andrews, Russell James
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesExperiences across aged care services for older LGBTIQA+ individuals vary, with some receiving affirming care and others having unmet needs. Given the growing demand from this population, it is essential to examine both effective care aspects and ongoing challenges. This study aimed to explore the perspectives of aged care providers, carers and community stakeholders on opportunities and barriers to the consistent implementation of culturally safe care for older LGBTIQA+ Australians.MethodsAdopting an interpretive descriptive methodology, a thematic analysis using Braun and Clarke's six‐step framework was conducted on LGBTIQA+‐specific transcripts from the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety Diversity Hearing (October 2019) in Melbourne, Australia.ResultsThematic analysis identified two main themes: Inclusive and Culturally Safe Care and Robust Governance for Inclusive Care. Findings revealed that engagement with aged care is severely undermined by historical trauma and a profound mistrust of formal services by some LGBTQIA+ older people. Systemic barriers persisted due to inconsistencies in governance and a lack of accountability for diversity standards. Stakeholders highlighted the critical necessity of mandatory, identity‐affirming LGBTIQA+ workforce training and the vital role of advocacy and community connections in bridging access gaps and ensuring cultural safety.ConclusionsWhile policy frameworks and ‘special needs’ recognition exist, genuine inclusion for older LGBTIQA+ Australians hinges on operationalising cultural safety through consistent governance, stronger compliance mechanisms and a mandatory, well‐resourced training regimen for the aged care workforce. These findings provide critical implications for policy reform and practice improvement under Australia's evolving aged care standards.Keywords: aged, clinical governance, cultural sensitivity, health workforce

Introduction

Australia's aged care sector faces growing pressure from a rapidly ageing population. By 2066, people aged 65 years and older are projected to comprise 21%–23% of the total population [1, 2]. This will significantly increase demand for aged care—services.

Amid rising demand, older LGBTIQA+ Australians were formally recognised as a ‘special needs’ group under the Aged Care Act 1997 via the 2012 National LGBTI Ageing and Aged Care Strategy and 2013 legislative amendments [3, 4, 5]. Despite this recognition, attention may still be needed to ensure aged care practices are inclusive of older LGBTIQA+ Australians.

LGBTIQA+ refers to people who are lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, intersex, queer, asexual and others with diverse sexual orientations and gender identities [6, 7]. While variations such as LGBT or LGBTI+ appear in quoted statements, LGBTIQA+ is used throughout for consistency and alignment with Australian guidance, including the Australian Human Rights Commission and the Department of Health and Aged Care.

Within this diverse population, some individuals faced lifelong discrimination, exclusion and persecution, often concealing their identities, which created barriers in aged care as providers struggled to understand their emotional, psychological and social needs [8, 9]. This concealment increased isolation and loneliness, compounded by societal marginalisation [8, 10]. Consequently, they often remained hesitant to engage with care systems, showing limited trust and openness with providers [8]. Although research has expanded [11], further exploration is needed to better understand the evolving needs, challenges and improvements of older LGBTIQA+ Australians in aged care [11].

Drawing on the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety (RCACQS) Diversity Hearing transcripts, this study aimed to explore the perspectives of aged care providers, carers and community stakeholders on opportunities and persistent challenges for advancing inclusive care for older LGBTIQA+ Australians.

The context and data source for this study derive from the Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety Diversity Hearing (October 2019). The RCACQS, established in October 2018, examined key issues in Australia's aged care sector, highlighting challenges such as cultural safety, diversity and workforce needs [12, 13]. A prominent Diversity Hearing in Melbourne (October 2019) specifically focussed on the needs of diverse groups, including older LGBTIQA+ individuals accessing or requiring aged care [14]. The panel—comprising aged care service users, LGBTIQA+ older adults, their partners and carers, and providers—varied in number and level of involvement. Drawing on the transcripts, this study explored opportunities to advance inclusive care and address systemic barriers for these individuals within Australia's aged care system, specifically through the lens of providers, carers and community stakeholders. The term ‘speakers’ was used in this study, rather than participants, to describe contributors who spoke to the panel and whose input varied in length and detail. The numeric designations (S1–S11) correspond to the consecutive order of the Royal Commission transcripts and are cited throughout the results chapter.

Methods

Study Design

This study used an interpretive descriptive methodology to examine the perspectives of providers, carers and community stakeholders on inclusion and challenges in aged care for older LGBTIQA+ Australians. Interpretive description is well‐suited to social and practice‐based inquiries, to support flexible, in‐depth analysis without imposing predefined categories, allowing findings to emerge flexibly from the data [15].

Data Collection and Analysis

The data for this study were derived from the RCACQS public diversity hearing in Melbourne, Victoria (7 October 2019–11 October 2019), previously described. Although the hearing included various culturally diverse groups, this study specifically focussed on older LGBTIQA+ individuals. Only 11 transcripts containing statements pertinent to this population were included. Selection was guided by the study's objectives, prioritising content relevant to older LGBTIQA+ experiences within aged care from the lens of their carers, service providers and other stakeholders. These 11 transcripts provided rich data for exploring perspectives, experiences and insights regarding access to, and quality of, aged care services for older LGBTIQA+ Australians.

Braun and Clarke's six‐step Thematic Analysis framework [16] was used for data analysis. This structured process systematically identified themes in the qualitative data, involving: (i) familiarisation with data, (ii) generating initial codes, (iii) searching for themes, (iv) reviewing themes, (v) defining and naming themes and (vi) producing the report. All coding was conducted by the first author, who systematically reviewed each selected transcript. For consistency, the coding process and discrepancies were discussed in team meetings with other authors. Reflexive notes were maintained throughout, enabling the team to consider how personal perspectives may have shaped interpretation, and an audit trail documented the coding and theme development process. This process utilised a colour‐coded framework that organised codes into five distinct themes: needs of older LGBTIQA+ people (Orange), workforce and training issues (Green), advocacy and partnerships (Yellow), governance and compliance (Blue) and access to services (Red). Finally, codes within each cluster were refined and synthesised into higher order themes, establishing the definitive thematic framework for presenting the findings.

Ethical Considerations

All transcripts used in this study were publicly accessible via the RCACQS website. As such, formal ethics approval was not required, given that only publicly available secondary data were used [17, 18]. Nevertheless, all data were handled with sensitivity, and the privacy of hearing participants was protected in line with ethical research standards. Although the transcripts and accompanying public recordings (e.g., YouTube videos) were available for a limited period, speaker identities have not been disclosed. Inclusive and Culturally Safe Care

Some speakers reflected on both the aspirations and realities of aged care for older LGBTIQA+ individuals. While inclusive, welcoming environments were noted, gaps remained when staff lacked understanding of diverse identities. A key theme, Inclusive and Culturally Safe Care, was identified with two subthemes: access, advocacy and community connections, and inclusive care workforce training.

Some speakers shared examples of positive experiences that reflected this ideal:

When organising home care, the staff ensured our needs were understood and respected, and we felt comfortable and supported as a Lesbian couple… (S2, Carer + Partner)

Providers also highlighted the vision of an inclusive aged care system:

The vision is to have an aged care system that truly values everyone in how we support, welcome, and engage each LGBTIQA+ person […] (S3, Provider)

However, these positive experiences coexisted with ongoing challenges. Systemic discrimination and past trauma could make aged care settings feel unsafe:

Being subject to ongoing systemic discrimination… leaves them with concerns about their aged care… (S3)

Inconsistent care practices also further impacted individuals:

Several home‐care workers who came to our home dampened my partner's spontaneity and this frustrated me as she withdrew into herself… (S2)

Speakers emphasised the importance of culturally safe spaces, particularly for those with past trauma:

Rebuilding a sense of place for LGBTI elders is through understanding how LGBTI older people have built culturally safe spaces for themselves—in the safety of their own homes, in intimate relationships and friendships… (S11, Policy Manager)

Overall, these accounts illustrate that while Inclusive and Culturally Safe Care is achievable, its consistent implementation remains a critical challenge for aged care services.

Access, Advocacy and Community Connections

Access to timely and culturally safe aged care was identified as essential for older LGBTIQA+ individuals. Speakers highlighted that overcoming systemic inconsistency and historical discrimination—the primary barriers to access—fundamentally relies on effective advocacy and robust community connections:

The frustration and anxiety older LGBTI community members experience in trying to navigate their way through the aged care system. (S3, Provider)

The urgent need for inclusive and affirming service models, particularly for those lacking strong support systems, was emphasised by the same speaker:

People from these communities are often excluded…disadvantaged financially, educationally and socially… have no advocate or ally… (S3)

Past discrimination, including experiences of homelessness and trauma, was seen to impact older people's confidence and willingness to access care:

For many older [LGBTIQA+] people who experienced disadvantage and homelessness… there is a fear of services…due to concern for losing their independence and becoming ‘institutionalised’ in residential aged care. (S4, Housing Advocate Organisation)

This emotional and social disconnection often extended to family relationships, underscoring the need for external support networks:

My siblings never coped with me being a lesbian, and by my thirties we had lost contact… Now, after 40 years, I am trying to rebuild relationships with my brothers. (S7, Partner)

Speakers also stressed the need for improved system design, increased funding and stronger government frameworks. Collaboration and meaningful partnerships were highlighted as necessary to address access barriers:

…[having] appropriate support is required to assist people with diverse needs to access aged care services; such support may include providing information in accessible formats that address common issues for this group. (S5, Diversity Manager)

Government initiatives, such as the Aged Care System Navigator Measure, were discussed to support special needs groups. However, broader gaps were noted in the systematic collection of disaggregated data and in this group's access to aged care, highlighting the importance of rigorous evaluation, particularly of sexual orientation and gender identity (SOGI), to ensure initiatives evolve alongside sector needs.

The services being trialled are available to all people [including older LGBTIQA+ persons] who need information about aged care options or help accessing aged care supports. (S8, Department of Health [DoH])

Data is not available to assess access for care for LGBTI people. The Department recognises that further work is needed to understand the experience of these groups… (S8)

Ultimately, to ensure equitable access to care, community connections and peer‐led organisations were seen as vital for bridging service gaps, fostering inclusion and building trust, often providing primary advocacy and driving cultural change:

In recent years, I found comfort and support through several people and organisations such as COTA SA, ECH, BFriend, Catalyst and Alice's Garage that are involved in supporting LGBTIQ elders. (S6, Partner)

…formal and informal advocates should be available to support people receiving aged care services as necessary. (S5, Diversity Manager)

Speakers emphasised the dual role of these connections: providing belonging and validation while simultaneously acting as essential navigators and advocates, thereby shaping service provision, policy and cultural safety sector‐wide.

LGBTIQA+ Specific Workforce Training

Speakers emphasised the need for a culturally competent workforce capable of meeting the diverse needs of older LGBTIQA+ individuals. They highlighted the importance of targeted training to deliver affirming care, supported by broader improvements in governance, data and funding.

While some general diversity training exists across the sector, speakers noted a lack of specific education focussed on the needs of older LGBTIQA+ people.

Assessors are supported with relevant training including in respect of cultural diversity in their induction and through the [Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission] ACQSC's CPD Program. (S1, ACQSC)

Some providers had incorporated inclusive practices during staff onboarding, though implementation remained broad and inconsistent. Therefore, the need for mandatory, sector‐wide training was repeatedly emphasised:

I encourage mandatory training for all aged care staff on providing care that is sensitive to LGBTI peoples' experience. (S2, Partner)

Others noted that the absence of funded and available training requirements limited uptake, even for low‐cost options, and also risks implementation failure by neglecting practical policy integration. This concern was voiced by one provider:

Training on diversity available for staff has to be translated into practical policies …and linking to performance management within the workplace. (S3, Provider)

The provider further stated that while low‐cost training is positive, there is no requirement for providers to take up this training, this is critical as staff attitudes and behaviours can be driven by overarching beliefs and social values… (S3)

This call was echoed by government representatives:

Agencies are hindered by a lack of fundamental resources such as funds and program training and support. (S4, Housing Advocate Organisation)

There should be mandatory training on caring for LGBTIQ elders… for all staff at all levels of organisations and facilities. (S6, DoH)

Collectively, these findings indicate that mandatory, funded and systematic training is essential for delivering respectful, affirming and culturally safe care.

Robust Governance for Inclusive Care

An overarching theme was governance in aged care, particularly the extent to which services support LGBTIQA+ inclusion. Central to this was how ACQSC assesses compliance with the Aged Care Quality and Safety Standards [19] to integrate diversity and inclusion initiatives.

Assessors consider how services identify care recipients' individual beliefs, and cultural backgrounds, and ensure care reflect and value these in practice. (S1, ACQSC)

The approved provider must demonstrate how they will comply with their obligations to continuously improve against the Quality Standards 2019 …including that each consumer is treated with dignity and respect, and that care and services are culturally‐safe. (S1)

However, some speakers questioned providers' readiness, and highlighted that the standards were relatively new, represented an improvement over previous standards, and their impact on inclusivity for older LGBTIQA+ individuals will depend on ongoing evaluation:

Standard 1 is open to interpretation, with some providers, viewing it as treating people equally rather than recognising and responding to their differences…The current system relies on the good intent of providers to respond to the needs of diverse communities and does not require it as mandatory. (S3, Provider)

It is unclear at this point how ACQSC will measure compliance with this aspect of the Standard and what consequences will flow for non‐compliance. (S3)

The Standards have only been in operation recently and will require ongoing evaluation in relation to how the increased scrutiny on diversity is measured and evaluated. (S5, Diversity Manager)

Speakers also highlighted the classification of LGBTIQA+ people as a group with ‘special needs’ may not be adequate:

The Aged Care Act 1997 presents ‘people with special needs’ as a list of equals. This is not the case; some groups have been subject to ongoing, systemic discrimination promoted by government and law… (S3)

Overall, according to some speakers, while the ACQS standards are a positive step for LGBTIQA+ inclusion, ongoing evaluation and clearer compliance are needed to meet the needs of older LGBTIQA+ individuals.

Frameworks for LGBTIQA+ Inclusion

Speakers highlighted the Rainbow‐Tick Accreditation [20] and the Aged Care Diversity Framework [21] as initiatives supporting inclusive care for older LGBTIQA+ Australians. The Rainbow‐Tick was recognised for promoting measurable, culturally safe and responsive care:

The Rainbow‐Tick Accreditation identifies quantifiable measures that could be incorporated into the ACQSC accreditation process. (S3, Provider)

Rainbow‐Tick guides providers in delivering culturally‐safe care by fostering understanding of staff values, historical discrimination, and risks of re‐traumatisation. (S11, Policy Manager)

Rainbow‐Tick accreditation has enabled our service to provide to older Australians with diverse characteristics and life experiences on a number of levels. (S10, Provider)

Challenges included resources, staff capacity and organisational buy‐in:

The biggest challenge was gaining and maintaining the trust of the LGBTI community… Rainbow‐Tick accreditation is challenging but if led from the top down and with buy‐in from staff, residents and families, it is achievable. (S10)

It requires providers to demonstrate resource capacity to meet the needs of older LGBTI Australians, which may not be present in all providers. (S11)

Some speakers also acknowledged the Diversity Framework, as it embeds diversity into governance, improving outcomes and addressing barriers to inclusive care.

The overarching goal of the Framework is to embed diversity in the delivery of aged care, and support action to address barriers… (S8, DoH)

This Framework's overarching imperatives and strategies would result in major improvements in supporting older LGBT Australians. (S9, Policy Director)

Yet, despite these benefits, some speakers noted challenges in operationalising this Framework, including the dilution of other LGBTIQA+‐specific strategies, risks of inconsistent implementation and limited linkage to compliance:

There are difficulties in taking the action plans…into something operationalised, implemented and maintained across an organisation. (S5)

…the Diversity Framework fails to recognise the needs of those who historically experienced ongoing, systemic discrimination and, whilst articulating good practice, may have limited impact as it is not linked to provider compliance and accreditation… (S3)

Overall, speakers recognised both initiatives as valuable for promoting inclusive care, while also highlighting practical challenges in implementation, sustainability and linkage to compliance standards.

Discussion

Historical Trauma and Trust in Aged Care

This study highlights that older LGBTIQA+ Australians' engagement with aged care services is profoundly influenced by systemic discrimination and historical trauma [22, 23, 24]. Some speakers described long histories of concealment, shame and marginalisation, experiences that reflect minority stress and internalised caution identified in prior research [25, 26]. These experiences contributed to fears stemming from past legal and social exclusion and reluctance to disclose identities, which in turn fostered mistrust towards providers [22, 23, 24]. Participants also reported concerns about mistreatment, loss of independence or becoming institutionalised, further limiting access to care [22, 23].

These findings align with broader evidence that historical discrimination influences service engagement and poses challenges to self‐disclosure among older LGBTIQA+ individuals in Australia [22] and internationally [23]. To address these barriers, strategies must go beyond awareness training and embed trauma‐informed, identity‐affirming principles into workforce practice, governance and policy [24, 25]. Although trauma‐informed care was not explicitly mentioned in the transcripts, participants' narratives reflect its core elements: safety, trust and empowerment [24, 25].

Effectively addressing these historical barriers requires providers to recognise the long‐term impacts of discrimination and minority stress, consistently demonstrating respect, affirmation and reliability to rebuild trust with older LGBTIQA+ individuals [22, 23, 24].

Access, Advocacy and Community Connection

Despite increasing recognition of the unique needs of older LGBTIQA+ Australians, significant barriers to accessing inclusive and culturally safe aged care services persist [27]. Findings from the transcripts reveal profound frustration with navigating complex systems and encountering provider inconsistencies. This echoes recent Australian literature showing that older members of this group perceive major barriers to care due to concerns about receiving respect and recognition, inadequate staff training and limited overall inclusiveness [28, 29]. Furthermore, the challenge differs by care setting. The transcripts highlight that in home care, inconsistent understanding from multiple workers often hindered consumers' spontaneity and sense of self. Conversely, in the context of residential care, speakers repeatedly described a significant fear of institutionalisation and loss of independence [29].

To counteract these systemic barriers, our findings also highlighted the vital role of advocacy and community connections in safeguarding and supporting the older LGBTIQA+ group. Peer‐led organisations and informal networks frequently bridge service gaps, building trust and providing essential pathways to care. To ensure equitable access, stronger governmental frameworks, increased funding and meaningful partnerships with LGBTIQA+ advocacy organisations are crucial [30].

Governance and Compliance

During the 2019 diversity hearing, several speakers questioned the sufficiency of existing governance, particularly the then‐current Aged Care Quality Standards [31]—to support meaningful inclusion. Standard 1 (Consumer Dignity and Choice) was often critiqued as being interpreted generically (‘treat‐everyone‐equally’) rather than allowing sensitivity to difference, risking frameworks becoming symbolic rather than substantive [32]. These critiques highlighted the need for stronger legislative and operational mechanisms to ensure that inclusivity was meaningful rather than merely procedural.

Crucially, LGBTIQA+ Australians were formally recognised as a ‘special needs’ group under the Aged Care Act 1997 in 2012 through amendments to the Allocation Amendment (People with Special Needs) [3, 4, 33]. In response, initiatives such as the Rainbow‐Tick Accreditation [20], the Aged Care Diversity Framework [21] and the Silver‐Rainbow training program—funded until 2026—were introduced [33]. However, the study's findings highlighted inconsistencies in practical implementation and on‐the‐ground impact. These insights align with broader critiques that, while designed with good intentions, diversity frameworks may lack sufficient enforcement and accountability [32].

Despite these policy initiatives, significant challenges persist. Our findings align with existing literature, indicating that the limited and inconsistent collection of comprehensive SOGI data continues to hinder effective needs assessments, access monitoring and evaluation of diversity initiatives [34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Addressing these systemic barriers and closing the gap between policy and practice will be critical as the sector transitions to the Aged Care Act 2024, effective 1 November 2025 [38]. While the reforms promise strengthened regulatory standards, their success will depend on embedding robust workforce training, inclusivity and enforcement mechanisms. Ultimately, cultural safety requires operationalising LGBTIQA+ inclusion through core accreditation and governance, backed by sufficient resourcing and consistent standard enforcement.

Workforce Training

A finding from this study, consistently emphasised in the dataset, is the pressing need for a culturally competent aged care workforce capable of meeting the diverse needs of older LGBTIQA+ individuals [39]. Despite some existing efforts and voluntary training programs, a considerable gap remains in the consistent provision and uptake of targeted, mandatory LGBTIQA+ specific diversity training [40]. This lack of awareness and limited capacity directly impact the delivery of person‐centred, inclusive and identity‐affirming care [39, 40].

Our findings strongly reinforce the call for mandatory, interdisciplinary training that covers respectful language, pronoun usage and an understanding of the historical and social contexts influencing older LGBTIQA+ individuals' experiences [39, 41]. Mandating LGBTIQA+ training across all levels of the aged care workforce, from direct care workers to management, is paramount to fostering a truly inclusive culture and ensuring the consistent application of respectful and affirming practices. This aligns with extensive previous research consistently supporting the critical role of comprehensive education in improving healthcare professionals' understanding and sensitivity towards LGBTIQA+ populations [39, 40, 41].

Moreover, addressing funding limitations and varying provider and staff values, as indicated in the transcripts, has likewise been identified as crucial factors for successful implementation and sustained impact of these educational initiatives.

Strengths and Limitations

This study's primary strength was being the first to utilise RCACQS transcripts for an in‐depth, foundational analysis of inclusion and challenges in aged care for older LGBTIQA+ Australians. This approach yielded rich insights from various perspectives, including providers, carers, and community stakeholders. Furthermore, while much existing research explores these topics from the consumer/resident perspective, this study offered a valuable, though limited, insight into the status quo as shared by providers, carers and regulatory bodies (ACQSC).

However, as a secondary analysis, the research was constrained by a single public hearing, providing only a snapshot in time. Due to the composition of this diversity hearing, where contributors were primarily providers, the findings inherently reflected their views. This reduced the depth of consumers' viewpoints from this group and potentially limited the balance of all voices pertinent to the topic in question. Future research should build upon these insights using primary data collection and broader mixed‐methods research across diverse Australian aged care settings to capture more representative perspectives.

Conclusions

This research offered critical insight into the issues and perspectives affecting older LGBTIQA+ Australians in aged care. Our findings revealed that persistent trust‐related barriers were deeply rooted in a history of discrimination and systemic inconsistency, profoundly shaping perceptions of the aged care sector.

The research highlighted the pivotal role of advocacy and community networks in bridging service gaps and enhancing access to care and services. Crucially, improving inclusivity also hinged on developing a culturally competent workforce through compulsory, identity‐affirming LGBTIQA+ training that addressed respectful language and historical context.

While existing diversity frameworks and initiatives were valuable, their impact depended on practical implementation by providers and a necessity for stronger regulation and adequate funding. Given that many aged care providers in Australia are faith‐based organisations, future research should explore how organisational values influence the inclusivity of services for LGBTIQA+ Australians [1, 4, 32].

Further investigation should also engage with middle‐aged LGBTIQA+ individuals to anticipate future care needs and develop long‐term strategies that ensure responsiveness to Australia's diverse ageing population [11, 39].

Looking forward, continuous commitment is required to bridge the gap between policy goals and the lived realities of this group, thereby ensuring the new or strengthened Aged Care Standards succeed in genuinely enhancing their inclusivity and cultural safety. This research provided essential implications for practice improvement and inclusive policy development, supporting the push towards a genuinely responsive and person‐centred aged care system.
