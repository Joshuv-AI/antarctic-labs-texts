Bias and precision in true‐score estimation
Authors: L Andries van der Ark, Wilco H M Emons, Jules L Ellis, Klaas Sijtsma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe discuss two approaches to estimating the true score from classical test theory, each with a corresponding measure of uncertainty due to measurement error: the classical method with the standard error of measurement (SEM) and Kelley's method with the standard error of estimation (SEE). For both approaches, we examined the bias and sampling variability of the true‐score, SEM and SEE estimators, as these properties were largely unknown. For each estimator, we derived analytic expressions for the bias and proposed approximations for practical bias assessment by omitting cumbersome terms. We also proposed a standard error estimator for each estimator. Simulations were used to assess the accuracy of the bias approximations and standard error estimators and to examine the impact of bias and sampling variability on both true‐score estimation methods. Results indicate that both the bias approximations and standard error estimators are sufficiently accurate. Whereas the classical true‐score estimator is unbiased, Kelley's estimator may exhibit substantial bias for extreme true scores. The bias of the estimated SEM and SEE may also be substantial when reliability is high and the reliability estimator is negatively biased. The impact of sampling variability is typically small.Keywords: classical test theory, estimation bias, reliability, standard errors, true‐score estimation

INTRODUCTION

Classical test theory (CTT, e.g. Gulliksen, 1950; Lord & Novick, 1968) is the most frequently used framework for estimating test‐score reliability. CTT assumes that any observed test score can be decomposed into a true score and a random measurement error. Ideally, the error‐free true scores rather than error‐laden test scores should be used for measurement. CTT provides two methods to estimate true scores. Both methods provide a point estimate and an uncertainty measure. The classical method (e.g. Lord & Novick, 1968, section 7.2) takes the person's observed test score as the estimate of the true score, and the standard error of measurement (SEM) as a measure of uncertainty. Kelley's method (Kelley, 1947) uses linear regression to estimate the true score as a mixture of a person's observed test score and the mean test score and uses the standard error of estimate (SEE) as an uncertainty measure.

The psychometric literature does not unequivocally recommend one method for practical use, and the discussion has been going on for along time (e.g. Campbell & Kenny, 1999, p. 27; Charter & Feldt, 2001; Dudek, 1979; Gulliksen, 1950; Kelley, 1947, p. 411; Patterson, 1955; Stanley & Spence, 2024). As a linear regression estimator of the true score, the Kelley estimator provides the most accurate linear prediction of the true score on average (Holland & Hoskens, 2003). However, it does not improve accuracy uniformly across individuals. Assuming homoscedastic measurement errors, it can be shown that for true scores near the mean, the Kelley estimator is typically more accurate than the classical estimator, whereas for more extreme true scores the reverse is true. Whether the gain in overall accuracy outweighs the local reduction of accuracy is a question that does not admit a general answer, as it may depend on the location of the cut‐off and the context (e.g. Maassen, 2000).

The two measures of uncertainty are also fundamentally different. When homoscedasticity is assumed, the SEM is the estimated standard deviation of the test‐score distribution for a group of individuals with the same true score. When true scores depend linearly on the test scores, the SEE is the estimated standard deviation of the true‐score distribution for a group of individuals with the same test score (e.g. Stanley & Spence, 2024). The debate between the two true‐score estimation methods is conducted at a theoretical level, under the assumption that all relevant parameters are known. In practice, however, true scores and their accompanying uncertainty measures are estimated from finite samples where estimation‐induced bias and sampling variability may obscure the theoretical properties. In this study, we examine these issues.

This study has two goals. First, we derive analytical expressions for the bias of the true‐score estimators, the SEM and the SEE. The resulting expressions have limited practical use, however, because they involve quantities that are typically unknown prior to data collection and difficult to specify a priori (e.g. sampling covariances between estimators and higher‐order aspects of sampling variability). To obtain practically useful approximations, we therefore consider reduced expressions—referred to as bias approximations—in which such terms are omitted, as they are expected to have limited impact on the bias. These bias approximations depend primarily on quantities, such as reliability and its bias, for which researchers often have substantive expectations, and therefore have greater practical applicability. We evaluate their accuracy in a simulation study. Second, we provide standard error estimators for the true‐score, SEM and SEE estimators, to assess the impact of sampling variability on true‐score estimates and their associated measures of uncertainty. We assume that test scores are obtained from a single administration of the test to a sample of N persons.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. First, we discuss the two true‐score estimation methods. Second, we derive analytical expressions for the bias and the proposed bias approximations. Third, we derive the standard error estimators. Fourth, we examine the accuracy of the bias approximations and standard error estimators in a simulation study. Fifth, we assess the accuracy of the true‐score, SEM and SEE estimators. Sixth, we provide plots giving insight into the magnitude of bias. We conclude with a discussion.

STANDARD ERRORS

For a generic parameter λ, the extent of sampling variability can be expressed by the sampling variance, Varλ^ or standard error SEλ^=Varλ^. Precision is defined as 1/Varλ^. As the classical true‐score estimator (Equation 7) is affected only by measurement error, and not by sampling variability, its standard error is zero by definition. In the remainder of this section, we derive analytic standard errors for Kelley's true‐score estimates (Equation 12), the SEM estimator (Equation 10) and the SEE estimator (Equation 16) under the multinomial sampling scheme.

Three comments are in order. First, persons having the same test score also receive the same estimated true score, τ^vK. Therefore, the number of estimated true scores equals the number of observed test scores in the data. Assume that L≤N test scores have been observed in the data, denoted X1,…,XL. Then τ^K=τ^KX1τ^KX2…τ^KXL′ is the vector containing the L true‐score estimates in the sample, where the apostrophe denotes the transposition. Collecting these L true‐score estimates in a vector is convenient as it allows simultaneous estimation of the standard errors for all true‐score estimates in the sample.

Second, for deriving the standard errors we used a two‐step procedure involving recursive computation, which has been described in detail elsewhere (e.g. Koopman et al., 2020; Kuijpers et al., 2013; Oosterhuis et al., 2017; Van der Ark, 2025). Therefore, we have diverted the full explanation of the two‐step method to the Data S1 (Section 1) and describe the procedure only briefly here.

Third, τ^K, σ^E and σ^ϵ depend on the particular reliability estimator. Therefore, their standard errors depend this estimator as well. To keep the task manageable, we derived these standard errors only for the case in which test‐score reliability is estimated using Cronbach's alpha. However, they can also be derived for other reliability estimators with known standard errors.

VALIDITY OF THE BIAS APPROXIMATIONS AND STANDARD ERRORS

Under several conditions, we compared the bias expressions (Equations 19, 22 and 25), the bias approximations (Equations 20, 23 and 26) and the bias obtained from simulated data, which we refer to as the empirical bias. If the bias approximations are close to both the bias expressions and the empirical bias, this supports the idea that these approximations can be used to examine bias in practice. We also compared the estimated standard errors (Equations 28, 29 and 30) with empirical standard errors obtained from replicated simulated data to assess their accuracy.

Method

Data‐generating model

A two‐parameter logistic item‐response theory (IRT; Birnbaum, 1968) model for J dichotomous items served as the data‐generating model. We selected item‐parameters sets that approximately produced prespecified values for both the reliability (ρX) and the bias of the reliability estimator (see, Data S1, Section 2). The difference between the prespecified and produced reliability values was < .00001. The discrete latent variable Θ, whose realizations are denoted by θ, took 10,001 equally spaced values between −5 and 5 with a step size of .001. The distribution of Θ, PΘ=θ, approximated a standard normal distribution. Cronbach's alpha (α) was used as the reliability estimator.

The following relevant parameters were computed from the data‐generating model: τθ (i.e. the true score for a person with Θ=θ; θ=−5,−4.999,…,4.999,5; 0<τθ<J), σT2, μX, σX2 and α (for details, see Appendix F). From these parameters, the reliability (Equation 6), the SEM (Equation 9), the SEE (Equation 15) and the bias of the reliability estimator (α−ρX) were computed. A true standard error was approximated by the empirical standard error: the standard deviation of the estimated statistic over the replications.

Dependent variables

First, we examined the magnitude of the terms that were omitted from the bias expressions. Second, for the parameter estimators of interest (τ^−2, τ^0, τ^2, σ^E, σ^ϵ) we compared the exact bias, the bias approximation and the empirical bias (i.e. bias obtained from the simulation study), by examining their correlations across all 96 conditions, and the differences between the bias and its approximation. Furthermore, for the same parameter estimators, we compared the estimated standard errors with the empirical standard errors by examining their correlations across all 96 conditions, as well as the root mean squared error (RMSE) and coverage rate within each condition.

Procedure

For both bias and standard errors, and for each parameter estimator of interest, we used a fully crossed design with 2×4×4×3=96 conditions. Each condition was replicated 5000 times, resulting in 5000 datasets per condition. The main results are presented in the paper, additional results are provided in the Data S1 (Section 3).

Conclusion

Based on these results, we found no evidence against the use of the proposed bias approximations and standard error estimators. In the absence of alternative bias approximations or standard error estimators for these parameters, we therefore recommend their use.

BIAS PROJECTION

The analytic bias approximations in Equations 20, 23 and 26 allow graphic display of the bias in the estimators for Kelley's true scores, the SEM and the SEE. Such graphs may help to assess the bias in samples. Figure 2 shows approximations (Equation 20) of the finite‐sample bias (right‐hand panel) and the total bias (i.e. finite‐sample bias and asymptotic bias; left‐hand panel) of Kelley's true‐score estimator. It follows from Equation 20 that unless τv*=τv, which occurs when ρX=1 or ρX=0, the asymptotic bias will always be present and does not vanish as the sample size increases. The finite‐sample bias, Biasρ^Xτv−μX, depends on both the magnitude of the bias of the reliability estimator, and the discrepancy between a person's true score and the mean test score. In general, it is difficult to assess the bias of a reliability estimator without making assumptions about the data beyond CTT. For lower bounds to reliability, such as Cronbach's alpha, Biasρ^X<0 (unless items or test parts would satisfy unrealistic conditions no data set would satisfy; Sijtsma & Pfadt, 2021), which makes Biasρ^Xτv−μX positive for persons with true scores below average, and negative for persons with true scores above average.

DISCUSSION

CTT provides two approaches for estimating the true score, each accompanied by a measure of uncertainty. In the comparisons and discussions of these methods, the extent to which these methods are affected by estimation bias and sampling variability has been largely ignored. While the classical true‐score estimator is unbiased, we derived expressions for bias in Kelley's true‐score estimates and in the estimated SEM and SEE. By omitting covariances between parameter estimates and higher‐order variance terms, we derived bias approximations that allow researchers to examine the bias of the true score, SEM and SEE estimators across a range of plausible values for the reliability and its bias. Using simulations, we showed that these bias approximations can be used safely to examine bias prior to a study.

Two points should be noted regarding our definition of bias of Kelley's true‐score estimator. First, several authors (e.g. Holland & Hoskens, 2003) noted that Kelley's true‐score estimator is biased. However, these authors assumed known reliability values and therefore considered only the asymptotic bias, without taking into account the additional finite sample bias. Second, we defined its bias as Eτ^vK−τv, (Equation 19), whereas one could argue that it could also be defined as Eτ^vK−τv*, which eliminates the asymptotic bias. However, using this alternative definition of bias has two disadvantages. First, the two methods would no longer estimate the same parameter, and more importantly, Kelley's true‐score estimator would lose its property as BLP.

To investigate sampling variability, we provided estimators for the standard errors of Kelley's true‐score estimate, the SEM estimate and the SEE estimate under a multinomial sampling scheme. As a multinomial sample scheme is very flexible, these estimators can be applied irrespective of the distribution of the test data. These estimators treat each unique item‐score pattern observed in the data as a separate category in a multinomial distribution. On regular laptops, memory problems may occur if the number of categories exceeds 1,000,000. Because unobserved item‐score patterns do not contribute to the standard errors and can therefore be ignored (e.g. Kuijpers et al., 2013), the number of categories cannot exceed N. Consequently, estimation remains feasible even for a large number of items. However, if the sample size is too small to accurately estimate the variances and covariances of the sample covariance matrix, which are required to estimate the standard errors of the lower bound reliability estimates, the resulting standard errors may be inaccurate. Also, if the number of response categories becomes large (e.g. semi‐continuous responses), the procedure becomes slow (e.g. see Van der Ark, 2025).

Simulations showed that the estimated standard errors were accurate under wide variety of conditions and even for reasonably small samples (N=200). Simulations further indicated that the effect of sampling variability is usually small. It should be noted that in our simulation study, we used item parameters of the two‐parameter logistic model that yielded specific values of both ρX and Bias(ρ^X). However, there may be other sets of item parameters that yield the same values of ρX and Bias(ρ^X), but different values for μX and σX2, and subsequently different values for σΤ2, τ~v, σE2 and σϵ2 and their estimates. Hence, the values the true scores, SEM, SEE are test specific. The results of our simulations should therefore primarily be regarded as a validation of the analytical results, and as providing insight into general trends in bias and standard errors one may expect.
