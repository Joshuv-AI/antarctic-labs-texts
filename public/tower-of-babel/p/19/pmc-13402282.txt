Unraveling the Relationship Between Respiratory Metabolism and Post-priming Deterioration in Rice
Authors: Jiayi Xu, Xiaowei Duan, Xing Chen, Lianlian Yan, Xiang Dan, Fangbo Cao, Jiana Chen, Huabin Zheng, Min Huang, Weiqin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeed priming has emerged as an effective and low-cost option to enhance rice seed vigor. However, the fast post-priming deterioration limits its adoption in rice production. The deterioration rate of primed rice seeds was varied across different priming methods, but whether this variation was associated with changes in seed respiratory metabolism remains unknown. In the present study, seeds of rice (Oryza sativa L.) cv. Chuangliangyou627 were subjected to hydro-priming (HP) and osmo-priming with 5% (OP5), 15% (OP15) and 25% (OP25) polyethylene glycol, non-primed seeds were used as control (NP). Subsequent artificial accelerated aging was conducted for 0 h, 48 h and 96 h. Seed deterioration rate, oxygen consumption rate (OCR), respiratory control ratio (RCR), enzyme activities involved in glycolysis and TCA cycle were measured. Results showed that HP accelerated seed deterioration as compared with NP, while OP significantly reduced post-priming deterioration compared to HP, and this protective effect became more pronounced as the OP concentration increased. Similarly, respiratory metabolism was greatly reduced in HP seeds during aging with respect to OCR, RCR and enzymatic activity, whilst OP partially mitigated those negative effects. Furthermore, partial least squares path modeling (PLS-PM) indicated that glycolysis exerted a negative effect on post-priming deterioration, whereas aerobic respiration showed a positive effect. These findings were validated using respiratory inhibitors. Suppressing the TCA cycle during priming significantly retarded deterioration by lowering reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and lipid peroxidation, whereas inhibiting glycolysis accelerated aging. The present study denotes the important role of respiratory metabolism in regulating primed rice seeds deterioration. However, further research is required to elucidate changes in mitochondrial ultrastructure and specific ETC function, as well as to precisely characterize the spatiotemporal patterns of mitochondrial ROS generation during the aging of primed rice seeds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12284-026-00925-5.Keywords: Rice, Seed priming, Seed deterioration, Respiratory metabolism

Introduction

Seed vigor is a critical seed quality trait that determines crop establishment and is essential for uniform seedling emergence and stable yield in crop production (Yang et al. 2024). The seed vigor could be enhanced by genetic modification, seed production technique and seed processing technique (Cheng et al. 2023; Groot 2022). However, such strategies are cost-intensive and technically demanding, posing challenges for widespread adoption by farmers. Consequently, seed priming has emerged as an effective and low-cost option to enhance seed vigor (Reed et al. 2022). The procedures of seed priming include the controlled seed hydration to induce various metabolic processes during the early phase of germination, and the drying of primed seeds to an initial moisture content before radicle emergence (Heydecker and Gibbins 1977). This technology has been widely reported to enhance seed vigor across various crops (Paparella et al. 2015). Rice (Oryza sativa L.), one of the most significant food crops globally, requires continuous yield improvements to ensure food security(Mueller et al. 2012; Propper et al. 2024). The use of seed priming technique in modern rice production is critical to maintain or increase yield because the transition from the traditional manual rice establishment to simplified and mechanized methods requires lower seed sowing rate and thus need higher seed vigor (Farooq et al. 2019; He et al., 2022). However, primed rice seeds are susceptible to rapid deterioration and loss of storability, which severely limits the extension of seed priming techniques in rice production.

Rapid deterioration of seeds following priming treatment has been reported in rice and various crops (Sano and Seo 2019; Schwember and Bradford 2011; Wang et al. 2018). However, the deterioration rate of primed seeds varies across studies and is influenced by different factors. Wang et al. (2018) reported that increasing the storage temperature did not reduce the viability of primed rice seeds stored under vacuum conditions, whereas seeds stored in the absence of oxygen but with high RH showed reduced longevity, which indicated that air humidity might be a key environmental factor causing rapid deterioration. Furthermore, the deterioration of primed seeds was varied across distinct seed lots and genotypes, and these variations are likely attributed to differences in initial seed traits, such as viability, weight, and chemical composition (Butler et al. 2009; Fabrissin et al. 2021; Powell et al. 2000). Additionally, our previous work identified that the priming method greatly affects post-priming deterioration; specifically, compared to hydro-priming, osmo-priming significantly mitigates the deterioration rate of primed rice seeds (Ren et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying the differential deterioration rates governed by storage conditions, seed traits, and priming methods remain poorly understood.

The rapid deterioration of primed seeds is associated with changes in various metabolic processes including DNA double-strand break (DSB) repair activities, Heat shock protein expression, and the pre-consumption of storage matrix (Batista et al. 2020; Hussain et al., 2015; Waterworth et al. 2025). Furthermore, several studies observed fast accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and decreased antioxidant enzyme activity in primed rice seeds during storage (Guha et al. 2021; Wang et al. 2018; Yalamalle et al. 2019). Priming rice seeds with the ROS scavenger significantly retarded the deterioration process (Ren et al., 2023), indicating a strong correlation between ROS metabolism and the rapid deterioration of primed seeds.

The majority of ROS is primarily produced via seed respiration during storage (Zhang et al. 2021). Significant reduction in respiratory rates and associated metabolic activities have been observed following artificial accelerated aging or natural storage (Jiang et al. 2018), resulting in marked declines in seed vigor and germination percentage (Ebone et al. 2020). Furthermore, reduced respiratory metabolism during seed deterioration is accompanied by alterations in reactive oxygen species (ROS) dynamics. Mitochondria function not only as the sites of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and oxidative phosphorylation but also as the primary source of intracellular ROS (Jiang et al. 2018). Structural damage to mitochondria during deterioration accelerate ROS production and membrane lipid peroxidation, ultimately causing cell death (Ebone et al. 2019). On the other hand, it has been suggested that the benefits of seed priming to enhance seed vigor was strongly associated with the repair and biogenesis of mitochondria (Nie et al., 2020), and the enhanced oxygen consumption rate and ATP level (Wang et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the relationship between respiratory metabolism and the rapid deterioration of rice seeds after priming remains unclear.

Our previous research established a negative correlation between the concentration of priming agents and the rate of post-priming deterioration in rice seeds. Building on this finding, the present study generated primed seeds exhibiting distinct deterioration rates by modulating priming concentrations to evaluate the changes in respiratory metabolism during deterioration. Furthermore, experiments using respiratory inhibitors were conducted to validate the specific contributions of respiratory pathways to post-priming deterioration. The objective of this study was to elucidate the relationship between respiratory metabolism and post-priming deterioration in rice.

Materials and Methods

Seed Sources

The rice (Oryza sativa L.) seeds of a local mega variety Chuangliangyou627 (CLY627, hybrid) were used in present study. The initial germination (83.5%, n = 4) of the seeds was determined before experiment.

Experimentation

Two experiments were conducted in the laboratory of Hunan Agricultural University, Changsha, China in 2024. In experiment 1 (Exp.1), the rice seeds were primed with four different priming solutions, viz., distilled water (HP), 5% polyethylene glycol (OP5), 15% polyethylene glycol (OP15), and 25% polyethylene glycol (OP25), the none-primed seeds (NP) were used as control. After priming treatment, the seeds were aged for 0, 48 h and 96 h by artificial accelerated aging method (Goel et al. 2003). The seeds were then transferred to a growth chamber to conduct standard germination test, and the germination traits and physio-biochemistry attributes of the seeds were determined. In experiment 2 (Exp. 2), the malonic acid (Mal), which act as an inhibitor to TCA cycle (Lu et al., 2018), and glucosamine (Glu), which act as an inhibitor to glycolysis (Tesoriere et al. 1975), were added to the priming solution to evaluate the effects of different respiratory pathway on the deterioration of primed rice seeds. The priming treatments in Exp. 2 include NP, HP, HP + Mal and HP + Glu. The primed and non-primed seeds were then aged for 0 and 96 h. The germination traits, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the activities of antioxidant enzymes of rice seeds under different treatments were then determined during germination test. The procedures for seed priming treatment, artificial accelerated aging treatment and standard germination test were described below.

Seed Priming Treatment

The process of seed priming includes soaking the seeds in priming agents and then dried back to 10% seed moisture content (fresh weight basis). The priming agent used for this study were distilled water (HP), 5%, 15% and 25% polyethylene glycol solution (OP, w/v), 0.1 mol L− 1 malonic acid dissolved in distilled water (HP + Mal), and 0.05 mol L− 1 glucosamine dissolved in distilled water (HP + Glu). The seeds were primed in the dark at 25℃ for 24 h with gentle agitation, the ratio of seed weight to solution volume (w/v) was 1:5. The priming solution was changed after every 12 h. After priming, the seeds were washed with distilled water for 2 min, surface dried by blotting paper and transferred to air dry oven at 25–30℃ to reduce the moisture content. Dried primed and non-primed seeds were then subjected to different artificial accelerated aging treatments.

Artificial Accelerated Aging Treatment

The methods for artificial accelerated aging treatment were described by Goel et al. (2003) with slight modification. The primed and non-primed rice seeds were placed in nylon bags in an accelerated aging chamber (LH-150 S, Zhejiang Tuopu Instrument Co., Ltd. China) with 90 ± 3% air relative humidity (RH) at 45 ± 1℃ for 0, 48 h and 96 h. After aging, the seeds were equilibrated in a chamber with 30 ± 3% RH at 25 ± 1℃ for 24 h and then vacuumed and sealed in plastic bags and stored at 4℃ until use.

Germination Test

Fifty healthy seeds from each treatment were evenly placed on two layers of filter paper in petri dishes (14.5 cm diameter). After adding 20 ml water to each treatment, the petri dishes were transferred to growth chamber with 12 h light period, and 30℃ (day): 25℃ (night temperature). Equal volume of distilled water was applied to all petri dishes when their moisture content decreased. All the experiments were laid out in a completely randomized design with twelve biological replications. Four replications were used to determine germination attributes, and the process was monitored until the number of germinated seeds remained constant (8 days in this study). Four replications were sampled at 24 h after sowing to determine physiological parameters, while the other four replications were sampled at 24 h to determine oxygen consumption rate.

Observations

Seed Germination Attributes

The seed germination was measured daily according to the procedure reported by Crosier et al. (1970). Seed was considered to be germinated when radicle length exceeded 2 mm. Based on the number of germinated seeds, germination index (GI=∑(Gt/Tt), where Gt is the number of the germinated seeds on Day t, and Tt is time corresponding to Gt in days) and vigor index (VI = GI×Hs, where Hs is shoot height) were calculated.

Seed Respiration

The oxygen consumption of rice seeds was measured by an optic oxygen microsensor (OXY1-ST, Presens Gmbh, Germany) as described by Ren et al. (2023). twenty rice seeds were put into 2 ml glassy autosampler vials (Agilent, the U.S.). The vial was closed tightly to prevent air diffusion and was then equilibrated in a growth chamber (dark, 30℃) for 15 min. After then, the needle type microsensor (PSt7) was inserted to the vial to monitor oxygen concentration. The respiration rate was calculated as the changes of the oxygen concentration per min. The respiratory control ratio (RCR) is measured by comparing the oxygen consumption rate of the crude mitochondria extract before and after ADP addition. The crude mitochondria was extracted by a commercial plant mitochondria extraction kit (Zci Bio, China), then the RCR was determined by the method described by Yin et al. (2009) with slight modification, 0.1 ml crude extract was added in 1 ml of reaction medium (0.3 M sucrose, 10 mM TESKOH, pH 7.5, 5 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgSO4 and 0.1% (w/v) BSA. 10 mM Succinate, 1 mM NADH) and the primary oxygen consumption rate (rate A) was determined by oxygen microsensor. After then, 0.8 mM ADP was added to the reaction medium to determine the active oxygen consumption rate (rate B). The RCR was calculated as rate B/rate A.

Respiratory Enzyme Activity

0.3 g de-hulled rice seeds were homogenized and rinsed with 10 mL extraction buffer (50 mM Tris, 5 mM DTT and pH 8.0). The crude extract was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant used for enzymatic determinations. The activity of hexokinase (HK), phosphofructokinase (PFK) was determined by measuring the absorbance at 340 nm at room temperature according to the methods described by Doehlert et al. (1988). The activity of pyruvate kinase (PK) and malate dehydrogenase (MDH) was determined according to the methods described by González et al. (1981). The activity of succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) was determined by measuring the absorbance at 600 nm at room temperature according to the methods described by Popov et al. (2010). The activity of α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (KGDH) was determined using a commercial determination Kit (BC0710, Cominbio Co.) by monitoring the attenuation rate of NADH at 340 nm.

Reactive Oxygen Species Content

The content of superoxide anion (O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) in imbibed rice seeds was determined by spectrophotometry using Superoxide Determination Kit (BC1295, Cominbio Co. Ltd., China) and Hydrogen Peroxide Determination Kit (BC3595, Cominbio Co. Ltd., China), respectively. All the assays were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Lipid Peroxidation and Antioxidant Enzyme Activity

To determine the lipid peroxidation and antioxidant enzymes activity, 0.2 g fresh samples were ground in 1.5 ml of 50 mM cool phosphate buffer [pH 7.0, containing 1% (w/v) PVP]. The homogenate was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 20 min at 4 ℃, the supernatant was then used for further assays. The lipid peroxidation level was determined in terms of malondialdehyde (MDA) content by measuring the absorbance at 532 nm and 600 nm according to the method of Dhindsa et al. (1981), the MDA content was calculated as described by Heath and Packer (1968). To evaluate the changes in antioxidant system in primed rice seeds during aging, the activity of three dominant antioxidant enzymes, viz. superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD) and catalase (CAT) was determined by spectrophotometry as described by Wang et al. (2018).

Statistical Analysis

All data were cleared up in Microsoft Excel, analyzed and plotted in R 4.5.0. The difference between treatment was analyzed by ANOVA and LSD using ‘rstatix’ and ‘multcomp’ packages. The significance of regression slopes between treatments was evaluated by ‘simba’ package. The effects of respiratory pathways on post-priming deterioration were analyzed using the partial least squares path model (PLS-PM) by ‘seminr’ package. The test results on reliability, validity, collinearity and bootstrap significance for the PLS-PM model were shown in Table S1 and Table S2.

Results

Seed Deterioration Rate

The changes in germination percentage (GP), germination index (GI), and vigor index (VI) of primed and non-primed rice seeds subjected to artificial accelerated aging (0, 48, and 96 h) were presented in Fig. 1. All priming treatments enhanced seed germination before aging (0 h) as compared to the non-primed control (NP), with increases of 8.6%, 23.9%, and 29.4% in GP, GI, and VI, respectively. To quantify the deterioration rate, linear regression analysis was conducted between aging time and germination traits (Fig. 1A–C). High coefficients of determination (R2 > 0.76) indicated a strong linear decline in seed viability over time. As shown in Fig. 1D, the deterioration rates (represented by the regression slopes) varied significantly among treatments. Hydro-priming (HP) resulted in the most rapid deterioration, with significantly higher slopes for GP and GI compared to NP. However, such negative effects that induced by HP were partially mitigated by osmo-priming, as increasing the PEG concentration of the priming solution from 5% (OP5) to 25% (OP25) significantly reduced the deterioration rates of GP and GI. Notably, for germination percentage, the OP25 exhibited a deterioration rate comparable to, or even lower than NP (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the decrease of vigor index was significantly faster in primed seeds than in NP, but no significant difference was observed among the four priming treatments.

Respiration Rate

Significant differences in the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were observed between priming treatments and the non-primed control (Fig. 2A–D). Under unaged conditions (0 h), the OCR of OP15 and OP25 seeds was increased by 10.3% and 23.1% respectively as compared to NP, whereas other priming treatments showed no significant difference or a slight decrease. After aging, the HP treatment exhibited a significantly lower OCR than NP, with reductions of 42.9% and 56.7% at 48 h and 96 h, respectively. However, increasing the concentration of the priming agent mitigated this decline. For instance, at 48 h of aging, the OCR in OP5, OP15, and OP25 were increased by 8.1%, 35.1%, and 65.0%, respectively, compared to HP. Additionally, the OCR of aged OP15 and OP25 seeds showed no significant difference compared to the NP control.

The respiratory control rate (RCR) was used as an indicator of mitochondria integrity (Fig. 2E). At 0 h, the RCR of HP-treated seeds was 30.9% lower than that of NP seeds. In contrast, the three osmo-priming treatments showed no significant differences from NP, with RCR values rising as the priming concentration increased. When the seeds were aged for 48–96 h, the RCR of HP and OP5 were reduced by 24.7% and 16.2%, respectively, as compared with NP. In contrast, the RCR of OP15 and OP25 were either comparable to or significantly higher than NP. These results indicate that hydropriming exerts a detrimental effect on mitochondrial function and integrity even prior to aging. However, increasing the osmopriming concentration can delay or counteract these negative effects.

Respiratory Enzyme Activity

The activities of key metabolic enzymes involved in glycolysis (HK, PFK, and PK) and the TCA cycle (SDH, MDH, and KGDH) were significantly affected by priming treatments and aging duration (Fig. 3). Under non-aged conditions (0 h), OP25 significantly increased the activities of all respiratory enzymes compared to NP (Fig. 3A). However, this priming-induced enhancement diminished as the priming concentration decreased. As the enzyme activities in HP and OP5 were comparable or significantly lower than those in NP (Fig. 3A). Meanwhile, significant variations in enzyme activities were also observed among NP and priming treatments following accelerated aging (Fig. 3B–C). After 48 h of aging, the activities of HK, PK, PFK, SDH, MDH, and KGDH in the HP treatment were decreased by 8.1%, 11.3%, 18.3%, 12.2%, 32.3%, and 16.9%, respectively as compared to NP. Extending the aging duration to 96 h further accelerated this decline in HP, resulting in an average decrease of 10.9%–176.3% across the six enzymes compared to NP. In contrast, OP treatment mitigated the accelerated aging-induced loss of enzyme activity observed in HP, particularly at higher priming concentrations. For instance, OP25 increased average respiratory enzyme activity by 48.0% and 87.5% as compared to HP after 48 h and 96 h of aging, respectively. Additionally, when the seeds were aged for 96 h, the differences among treatments were more pronounced for KGDH, MDH, SDH, and PFK than for HK and PK.

PLS-PM Analysis

PLS-PM analysis was used to explore the relationships among glycolysis, TCA cycle, aerobic respiration and seed deterioration. As shown in Fig. 4A, all manifest variables loaded strongly and significantly onto their respective latent constructs (p < 0.001), indicating robust reliability and validity. Glycolysis represented a positive effect on the TCA cycle (β = 0.88, P < 0.01) and a negative effect on seed deterioration (β= −1.17, P < 0.01). The TCA cycle showed positive effects on aerobic respiration. While aerobic respiration exhibited a positive effect on seed deterioration (β = 0.37, P < 0.01). The overall effects analysis suggested that aerobic respiration presented the positive total effect on seed deterioration, which was primarily driven by its direct effects (Fig. 4B). In contrast, glycolysis showed a negative total effect, although its beneficial direct impact was partially offset by a positive indirect effect mediated through the TCA cycle.

Effects of Respiration Inhibitor on the Deterioration and Reactive Oxygen Species Content of Primed Rice Seeds

To validate the PLS-PM findings that glycolysis negatively regulates while aerobic respiration positively regulates the deterioration of primed seeds, we evaluated the effects of two respiratory pathway inhibitors added during priming (Mal, a TCA cycle inhibitor; Glu, a glycolysis inhibitor) on the deterioration of primed rice seeds (Fig. 5). The results showed that at un-aged condition, the addition of Mal or Glu increased the GI by 20.3% and 38.9%, and VI by 29.7% and 39.2%, respectively, compared to NP. This indicates that the inclusion of respiratory inhibitors during priming does not diminish the initial priming benefits. When the seeds were subjected to accelerated aging for 96 h, distinct germination patterns were observed among the treatments (Fig. 5A). The HP + Mal resulted in a significantly higher GP, which was increased by 85.0% as compared with HP. Although the GI and VI showed no statistically significance, the increase of 35.6% and 126.0% by HP + Mal was also observed, respectively as compared with HP. These results suggest that inhibiting the TCA cycle effectively retards the deterioration of primed seeds. In contrast, the HP + Glu treatment caused marked reductions in GP, GI, and VI by 50.0%, 57.6%, and 82.9%, respectively as compared with HP, indicating that the inhibition of glycolysis accelerate the deterioration of primed rice seeds. Furthermore, the use of respiratory inhibitor significantly affected the OCR, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and antioxidant enzyme activities of primed rice seeds (Fig. 5B). When compared with HP, HP + Mal treatment exhibited a significantly higher OCR and antioxidant activities, and substantially reduced the levels of MDA, H2O₂, and O₂⁻. HP + Glu treatment displayed effects opposite to those of HP + Mal, as the OCR and antioxidant enzyme activities were significantly reduced, and the ROS accumulation was significantly enhanced in HP + Glu treated seeds, as compared with HP. Collectively, these findings indicate that regulating respiratory metabolism significantly impacts the deterioration of primed rice seeds, and such changes might be attributed to the variation in ROS generation and scavenging.

Discussion

Seed hydration represents a critical stage in seed priming that significantly determines post-priming germination performance. Hydro-priming (HP) often leads to uncontrolled hydration, which may cause imbibition injury (Han et al. 2023). In contrast, osmo-priming (OP) utilizes macromolecules such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) to increase the concentration of the priming solution. This process reduces the rate of water uptake, thereby mitigating imbibition damage. Although the superior effect of OP over HP in enhancing seed vigor is well-documented (Paparella et al. 2015), how these priming methods differentially affect post-priming seed deterioration remains largely unknown. In the present study, OP significantly reduced the deterioration rates of primed rice seeds compared to HP (Fig. 1), which is consistent with our previous findings (Ren et al., 2023). Furthermore, this protective effect became more pronounced as the OP concentration increased (Fig. 1). Previous studies have observed significant difference in ROS generation and membrane integrity between HP and OP seeds during storge, which may contribute to varied deterioration rate (Boucelha et al. 2019; Ren et al., 2023; Wang et al. 2018). Considering that mitochondrial respiration is the primary source of intracellular ROS, the present study focused on characterizing the changes in respiratory metabolism during the aging of primed rice seeds.

Respiration is the fundamental physiological process for seeds during storage (Varier et al. 2010). The present study observed a significant reduction in the OCR of HP seeds compared to NP seeds, whereas OP mitigated this negative effect induced by HP (Fig. 2). Respiratory metabolism generally initiates with cytosolic glycolysis via substrate-level phosphorylation, followed by the mitochondrial TCA cycle, which generates reductants to fuel the electron transport chain (ETC). It has been reported that genes and proteins involved in glycolysis, TCA cycle, the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation were significantly reduced during seed deterioration (Xin et al. 2014), which ultimately lead to the reduction in seed respiration (Ren et al., 2023). Consistent with these findings, the present study detected significant changes in the activities of rate-limiting glycolytic enzymes and key TCA cycle enzymes during deterioration (Fig. 3); however, the magnitude of these declines varied distinctly among priming treatments. Regarding glycolysis, high moisture conditions are known to trigger glycolysis and reserve mobilization (Mira et al. 2010; Umarani et al. 2020), glycolytic ATP has been shown to protect mitochondrial ATP synthase, maintain mitochondrial integrity and suppress intracellular ROS generation, which delays seeds deterioration (Almeida et al. 2001). Additionally, glycolysis may facilitate S-nitrosylation to alleviate cell death during storage (He et al. 2018). The protective effect of glycolysis during seed aging is also supported by the results of present study, as the PLS-PM analysis revealed that glycolysis exerted a negative effect on the deterioration rate of primed rice seeds (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the application of a glycolysis inhibitor during priming significantly accelerated deterioration (Fig. 5). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the positive effects of glycolysis to delay the aging of primed seeds.

Unlike glycolysis, the aerobic respiration showed a positive effect on seed deterioration (Fig. 4). In the PLS-PM model, the latent construct aerobic respiration comprised oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and respiratory control ratio (RCR). OCR is a key indicator of seed deterioration (Bello and Bradford, 2016; Jiang et al. 2018). In the present study, OCR decreased more rapidly in HP-treated seeds than in OP seeds (Fig. 2). This decline may be attributed to several metabolic changes, including degradation of DNA and RNA, impaired synthesis of proteins required for seed germination, reduced capacity for DNA repair, and increased ROS production (Bailly 2004; Rajjou et al. 2012). Moreover, the decreased OCR might also be attributed to mitochondria damage, as the RCR, the proxy indicator for mitochondrial function (Brand and Nicholls 2011), represented great changes between HP and OP treatment when suffering accelerate aging (Fig. 2). In this study, HP seeds exhibited significantly lower RCR than NP seeds even prior to aging (Fig. 2). This reduction is likely attributed to imbibitional injury caused by uncontrolled hydration, which compromises mitochondrial membrane integrity in HP seeds (Wang et al. 2012). Since the ETC relies on the structural integrity of the inner mitochondrial membrane to maintain the proton motive force (Møller 2001), membrane damage directly impairs electron transport capacity. For OP treatments, however, RCR showed an increasing trend with osmotic potential prior to aging. This improvement is likely because OP allows for controlled hydration (Chen and Arora 2013), which benefits the recovery of mitochondrial structure as compared to un-controlled hydration (Wang et al. 2012). Furthermore, OP seeds may retain a “priming memory” of stress-tolerance mechanisms, including the upregulation of antioxidant systems and membrane-stabilizing proteins such as LEAs and dehydrins (Chen et al. 2012), thereby preserving mitochondrial function. Nevertheless, the present study only used RCR as an indirect indicator for mitochondrial function. Further investigation is needed to elucidate the role of mitochondria in primed rice seeds deterioration regarding mitochondrial membrane potential, mitochondria ultrastructure, and individual ETC complex activity.

Inhibiting the TCA cycle, an upstream component of oxidative phosphorylation, mitigated deterioration in HP-primed seeds, and this beneficial effect was associated with reduced ROS accumulation (Fig. 5). Mitochondrial ROS production is a major driver of oxidative stress (Møller 2001; Bailly 2004). When electron flow through energy-conserving complexes is restricted, excess electrons may leak, particularly from complexes I and II, leading to superoxide and hydrogen peroxide formation (Bailly et al. 2008). The protective effect of the TCA-cycle inhibitor may therefore reflect a reduction in TCA-derived reducing power, which in turn decreases electron leakage and ROS accumulation under conditions where mitochondrial function might be reduced in primed rice seeds (Møller and Kristensen 2006). This interpretation, however, remains to be verified experimentally. Additionally, although OP25 and TCA-cycle inhibition both reduced primed seed deterioration, their underlying regulatory mechanisms may differ. The protective effect of OP25 is likely primarily associated with controlled hydration and better preservation of membrane integrity, whereas malonic acid may act more directly by suppressing TCA-cycle activity and limiting ROS generation. It is therefore plausible that combining OP25 with malonic acid could further reduce seed deterioration, although this possibility remains to be tested experimentally.

Several limitations should also be considered when interpreting the present findings. First, seed deterioration was induced using accelerated aging, a widely adopted and standardized method for rapid evaluation of seed longevity. However, metabolic responses under this acute stress may differ from those occurring during natural long-term storage. Therefore, the metabolic changes identified in present study should be further validated under natural aging conditions over extended storage periods. Second, this study was conducted using a single rice cultivar. Although a preliminary experiment involving eight genetically diverse rice cultivars, including both inbred and hybrid varieties (Table S3), showed that HP-treated seeds deteriorated faster than OP25-treated seeds, with no significant interaction among cultivar, aging duration, and treatment, whether the underlying respiratory metabolic changes are consistent across genetic backgrounds remains to be determined.

In summary, the variation in deterioration rates among priming treatments appears to be associated with changes in respiratory metabolism. Specifically, maintaining glycolytic activity may help delay deterioration in primed seeds, inhibiting TCA-cycle activity may contribute to oxidative balance and mitigate primed seeds deterioration. Nevertheless, this study was limited to apparent respiratory characteristics and enzyme activities. Future research should examine mitochondrial ultrastructure, specific ETC functions, and the spatiotemporal dynamics of mitochondrial ROS generation during the aging of primed rice seeds.
