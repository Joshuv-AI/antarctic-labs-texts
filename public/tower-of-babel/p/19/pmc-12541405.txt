Nanomedicines targeting protease-activated receptor 2 in endosomes provide sustained analgesia
Authors: Shavonne L. Teng, Rocco Latorre, Divya Bhansali, Parker K. Lewis, Rachel E. Pollard, Chloe J. Peach, Badr Sokrat, Gokul Sriman Thanigai Arasu, Tracy Chiu, Paz Duran, Nestor N. Jimenez-Vargas, Abby Mocherniak, Matthew Bogyo, Michael M. Gaspari, Stephen J. Vanner, Nathalie M. Pinkerton, Kam W. Leong, Brian L. Schmidt, Dane D. Jensen, Nigel W. Bunnett
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although many internalized G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) continue to signal, the mechanisms and outcomes of intracellular GPCR signaling are uncertain due to the challenges of measuring organelle-specific signals and of selectively antagonizing receptors in intracellular compartments. Herein, genetically encoded biosensors targeted to the plasma membrane and early endosomes were used to analyze compartmentalized signaling of protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2); the propensity of nanoparticles (NPs) to accumulate in endosomes was leveraged to preferentially antagonize intracellular PAR2 signaling of pain. PAR2 agonists evoked sustained activation of PAR2, Gαq, and β-arrestin-1 in early endosomes and activated extracellular signal regulated kinase (ERK) in the cytosol and nucleus, measured with targeted biosensors. Fluorescent dendrimer and core-shell polymeric NPs accumulated in endosomes of HEK293T cells, colonic epithelial cells, and nociceptors, detected by confocal microscopy. NPs efficiently encapsulated and slowly released AZ3451, a negative allosteric PAR2 modulator. NP-encapsulated AZ3451, but not unencapsulated AZ3451, rapidly and completely reversed PAR2, Gαq, and β-arrestin-1 activation in early endosomes and ERK activation in the cytosol and nucleus. When administered into the mouse colon lumen, fluorescent dendrimer NPs accumulated in endosomes of colonocytes and polymeric NPs accumulated in neurons, sites of PAR2 expression. Both NP formulations of AZ3451, but not unencapsulated AZ3451, caused long-lasting analgesia and normalized aberrant behavior in preclinical models of inflammatory bowel disease. These results provide evidence that PAR2 endosomal signaling mediates pain and that nanomedicines that antagonize PAR2 in endosomes effectively relieve pain. NP-mediated delivery may improve the efficacy of other GPCR antagonists for treatment of diverse diseases.

G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) are a large family of seven transmembrane domain proteins that control many physiological and pathological processes (1). GPCRs are the largest drug target, illustrating their importance in disease and medicine (2). GPCRs are conventionally considered as plasma membrane receptors that bind to agonists in the extracellular fluid and couple to intracellular heterotrimeric G proteins. Consequently, drug discovery has focused on targeting GPCRs at the plasma membrane. However, GPCR activity at the plasma membrane is usually transient. GPCRs kinases phosphorylate activated GPCRs, which increases receptor affinity for β-arrestins (βARRs) (3). βARRs uncouple GPCRs from G proteins, desensitizing plasma membrane signaling, and couple GPCRs to clathrin and adaptor protein-2, leading to receptor endocytosis. Although endocytosis was formerly viewed as another mechanism to arrest signaling, many internalized GPCRs continue to signal (4–13). Endosomal signaling of GPCRs underlies the actions of certain hormones (4, 6) and mediators of pain (8, 10, 13, 14). However, our understanding of the mechanisms and functions of intracellular GPCR signaling is hampered by the challenges of measuring GPCR and effector activity in organelles and of selectively antagonizing intracellular rather than cell-surface GPCRs. Moreover, whether antagonists disrupt preexisting GPCR signals in organelles, which requires penetration of plasma and organelle membranes, and engagement with GPCR conformations within multiprotein signaling complexes of acidic organelles, is seldom investigated. The inability of drugs to inhibit GPCR signals in organelles could contribute to their failure in clinical trials for the treatment of chronic diseases, where GPCRs are likely internalized due to overproduction of agonists.

Herein, we investigated signaling of protease-activated receptor 2 (PAR2) in subcellular compartments and devised an approach to antagonize PAR2 signaling in endosomes of key cell types for the treatment of pain. PAR2 is expressed in colonocytes and colonic neurons and is implicated in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) (15). Host and bacterial proteases in the inflamed colon cleave and activate PAR2, inducing receptor endocytosis and intracellular signaling (10, 14, 16). We measured the activity of PAR2, Gα, βARRs, and kinases in subcellular compartments using Bioluminescence Resonance Energy Transfer (BRET) and Förster Resonance Energy Transfer (FRET) biosensors. Since PAR2 signals from endosomes of colonocytes and nociceptors to evoke inflammation and pain (10, 14, 16), drug delivery systems must bypass the plasma membrane and deliver antagonists to endosomes to be effective. We used nanoparticles (NPs) to encapsulate and deliver a negative allosteric modulator of PAR2, AZ3451 (17), to endosomes. While the mechanisms by which NPs traffic to endosomes are not fully understood, endocytosis of small NPs (e.g., <200 nm for clathrin-mediated endocytosis) is ubiquitous in nonphagocytic cells (18). We studied two translatable but fundamentally different NP types: slow-release dendrimers (US NCT05205161) and core-shell polymeric NPs (US NCT03217838). We administered the NPs locally in the colon to mitigate the challenges and side effects of systemic administration and studied whether the NPs disrupt endosomal PAR2 signaling and relieve pain in preclinical models of IBD (19).

Results

Dendrimer and Core-Shell Polymeric NPs Encapsulate and Release a PAR2 Antagonist.

PAR2 recruits βARRs to the plasma membrane and undergoes clathrin-dependent endocytosis (10, 14). PAR2 continues to signal from endosomes by Gαq and βARR-dependent mechanisms that activate Extracellular Signal Regulated Kinase (ERK) (5). ERK causes the redistribution of tight junction proteins leading to increased paracellular permeability of colonocytes (16) and sensitizes ion channels of nociceptors (10), which initiate inflammation and pain. Endocytosis inhibitors suppress PAR2-induced ERK signaling, colonocyte paracellular permeability, and nociceptor sensitization (5, 10, 14, 16). Thus, endosomal PAR2 signaling mediates inflammation and nociception, and PAR2 in endosomes is a therapeutic target for inflammatory pain. We leveraged the prevalent endosomal uptake of NPs to preferentially deliver a PAR2 antagonist to the desired site of action within endosomes. We previously encapsulated neuropeptide receptor antagonists into NPs designed to undergo endocytosis and disassemble and release cargo in acidic endosomes (20–22). After intrathecal or periorbital injection, pH-responsive NPs provide sustained relief from inflammatory, neuropathic, and migraine pain (20–22). However, these routes of administration are challenging in a clinical setting. Moreover, pH-responsive NPs are unsuitable for antagonism of receptors at peripheral sites because they would prematurely disassemble in the acidified extracellular environment of diseased tissues (23). While immediate pH-dependent antagonist release from NPs is ideal for the rapid reversal of acute pain (20–22), sustained release of antagonists is optimal for long-lasting alleviation of chronic pain. To antagonize intracellular PAR2 in inflamed tissues and provide long-lasting analgesia, dendrimer and core-shell polymeric NPs were developed for sustained and pH-independent delivery of AZ3451 (17) to endosomes.

Poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimers self-assemble into NPs with defined size, shape, and a hydrophobic core that can encapsulate hydrophobic drug candidates (24, 25). Cholesterol (Chol) chloroformate was used to modify the surface of amine-terminated generation 3 PAMAM-dendrimers, forming soft, cationic PAMAM-Chol NPs with mucoadhesive properties (26, 27). AZ3451 was entrapped in the core during self-assembly of the cholesterol-functionalized PAMAM-G3 in aqueous solution, forming PAMAM-Chol-AZ (Fig. 1A). AZ3451 was encapsulated with 99.6% efficiency and 20% (w/w) loading. PAMAM-Chol-AZ had a hydrodynamic diameter of 223 ± 3 nm (mean ± SD), a polydispersity index (PDI) <0.2, and a positive zeta potential of +65 ± 1 mV (Fig. 1B). Empty PAMAM-Chol-Ø had a hydrodynamic diameter of 129 ± 5 nm and a zeta potential of +53 ± 1 mV. PAMAM-Chol-AZ NPs were unaggregated and uniformly spherical (Fig. 1C). Passive release of AZ3451 from the dendrimer was observed for at least 48 h (Fig. 1D). AZ3451 was released at a similar rate at pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4, representing endosomal and extracellular environments. Fluorescent cyanine 5 (Cy5) was conjugated to PAMAM-Chol using N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) chemistry. PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 was of similar size and charge to its unlabeled counterpart (Fig. 1B). PAMAM-Chol-Ø (0.3 to 10 μg/mL) did not cause cytotoxicity of HEK293T cells after 4 or 24 h (Fig. 1E). The highest concentration (10 μg/mL) corresponds to 3.3 μM PAMAM-Chol-AZ, which exceeds the maximum concentration tested in signaling assays.

Flash Nanoprecipitation (FNP) permits assembly of core-shell polymeric NPs by diffusion-limited self-assembly driven by hydrophobic interactions (28, 29). FDA-approved poly(lactic acid)-poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG-b-PLA) was selected as the stabilizing block copolymer to create a biocompatible NP with a neutral, nonfouling surface. PEG was chosen to achieve a neutral outer PEG brush layer, known to enable deep tissue penetration (30), whereas PLA was selected as the hydrophobic block for its biodegradability and glassy morphology (31). An organic stream of tetrahydrofuran (THF) containing dissolved AZ3451, PLA, and PEG-PLA was rapidly mixed against ultrapure water streams to form core-shell PEGylated polymeric-AZ3451 (PEG-Poly-AZ) (Fig. 1F). AZ3451 encapsulation efficiency was 99.1%, which resulted in NP with 8% (w/w) AZ3451 loading. PEG-Poly-AZ had a hydrodynamic diameter of 99 ± 1 nm, a PDI of <0.07, and a near neutral zeta potential of −5 ± 1 mV (Fig. 1G). Empty PEG-Poly-Ø had an average hydrodynamic diameter of 98 ± 6 nm and a zeta potential of −5 ± 1 mV. PEG-Poly-AZ NPs were unaggregated and uniformly spherical (Fig. 1H). The in vitro release profiles of AZ3451 at pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4 were similar, demonstrating sustained release for at least 160 h (Fig. 1I). PEG-Poly encapsulating fluorescent quantum dots (QD) had a PDI of <0.2, a hydrodynamic diameter of 99 ± 1 nm, and a zeta potential of −0.7 ± 0.1 mV. PEG-Poly-Ø (0.3 to 10 μg/mL) did not induce toxicity after 4 or 24 h, except for a slight decrease in viability at the highest concentration of PEG-Poly-Ø (Fig. 1J).

Dendrimer and Core-Shell Polymeric NPs Accumulate in Early and Late Endosomes.

Confocal microscopy was used to detect the uptake and subcellular localization of NPs in HEK293T cells. Cells were transduced with Rab5a-RFP and Rab7a-GFP to identify early and late endosomes, respectively. PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 (Fig. 1 K L) and PEG-Poly-QD (Fig. 1 M N) were detected in early and late endosomes after 2 and 4 h. 3D projections of z-stacks of confocal images confirmed the accumulation of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 (Movie S1) and PEG-Poly-QD (Movie S2) in Rab5a+ve early endosomes that were distributed throughout the cytosol. Intensity line scans through the center of individual cells confirmed the coincidence of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD with Rab5a-RFP and Rab7a-GFP (Fig. 1 K M). The extent of colocalization of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD with Rab5a-RFP and Rab7a-GFP was quantified by measurement of the Manders’ overlap coefficient in multiple cells. This analysis revealed that a large proportion of early and late endosomes contained PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD at both 2 and 4 h (Fig. 1 L N). At 2 h, ~84% of early endosomes and ~67% of late endosomes contained PAMAM-Chol-Cy5, and ~91% of early endosomes and ~95% of late endosomes contained PEG-Poly-QD. Thus, both NP formulations can be used to deliver cargo to early and late endosomes.

PAR2 Signals At the Plasma Membrane and in Early Endosomes.

We used enhanced bystander BRET (ebBRET), which exploits the affinity of pairs of Renilla (R)-tagged proteins (32, 33), to assess PAR2-induced trafficking of mini (m) Gαq, mGαi, and βARR1 to the plasma membrane and early endosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). mGα proteins are N-terminally truncated forms of Gα that interact with activated GPCRs (34, 35), allowing detection of activated conformations of PAR2 in subcellular compartments. Although mGα subtypes were generated through mutating key residues of mGαs, they have shown appropriate subtype selectivity across GPCR families (34). Rluc8-mGαq, Rluc8-mGαi, or βARR1-RlucII were expressed in HEK293T cells with the plasma membrane marker CAAX coupled to RGFP or the early endosomal marker Rab5a coupled to tandem (td)RGFP. Rab5a regulates endocytosis and is found in clathrin-coated vesicles and early endosomes (36).

PAR2 agonists 2-furoyl-LIGRLO-NH2 (2F) (10 μM), an analogue of the tethered ligand, and trypsin (100 nM) rapidly increased ebBRET between Rluc8-mGαq, Rluc8-mGαi, or βARR1-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX and tdRGFP-Rab5a (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B–G). mGαq and mGαi were rapidly recruited to the plasma membrane and early endosomes with similar kinetics. mGαq ebBRET signals were maintained during the 20 min post-stimulation period. mGαi ebBRET signals declined at the plasma membrane and were sustained in early endosomes. Although βARR1 was rapidly recruited to the plasma membrane and early endosomes, βARR1 ebBRET signals at the plasma membrane declined after 2 min, whereas signals in early endosomes continued to increase during the 20 min post-stimulation period. The decline in plasma membrane βARR1 and its accumulation in endosomes is consistent with PAR2 and βARR1 endocytosis. These results suggest that PAR2 remains active at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes, where it can couple to Gαq, Gαi, and βARR1.

We used confocal microscopy to confirm PAR2 agonist-stimulated translocation of mGαq to the plasma membrane and early endosomes in HEK293T cells expressing Venus-mGαq and Rab5a-RFP. In unstimulated cells, Venus-mGαq was cytosolic (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). After 5 min with 2F (10 μM), Venus-mGαq was detected at the plasma membrane and in the cytosol. After 15 and 30 min, Venus-mGαq was sequestered from the cytosol and detected at the plasma membrane and in Rab5a-RFP+ve early endosomes. 3D projections of z-stacks of confocal images confirmed the accumulation of Venus-mGαq in Rab5a+ve early endosomes that were distributed throughout the cytosol (Movie S3). Intensity line scans showed that 2F stimulated coincidence of Venus-mGαq and Rab5a-RFP after 15 min (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H).

NanoLuc Binary Technology BRET (NanoBiT-BRET, nbBRET) was used to simultaneously measure the proximity of PAR2, an effector (mGα or βARR), and a plasma membrane or early endosome marker (14, 37) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I). Human PAR2 was C-terminally tagged with Natural Peptide (NatP) NanoLuc fragment (13 residue NanoBiT) and HA-tagged localization markers of the plasma membrane (CAAX) or early endosomes (FYVE) were tagged with large NanoBiT fragment (LgBiT). Luminescence occurs from a complex between PAR2-NatP and LgBiT markers, which serves as an energy donor for Venus-mGα or βARR-YFP. The expected colocalization of LgBiT-FYVE and a CellLight™ marker for early endosomes (Rab5a-RFP) in HEK293T cells was confirmed by confocal microscopy (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). 2F and trypsin rapidly increased nbBRET between PAR2-NatP and Venus-mGαq, Venus-mGαi or βARR1-YFP, and LgBiT-CAAX and LgBiT-FYVE (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 J–O). The PAR2, Gαq, and Gαi nbBRET sig-nals at the plasma membrane and in endosomes were maintained during the 20 min post-stimulation period. The PAR2 and βARR1 nbBRET signals at the plasma membrane declined with time, whereas signals in endosomes were sustained or increased. These results suggest that PAR2, Gαq, Gαi, and βARR1 signaling complexes assemble at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes.

We used an ebBRET-based G Protein Effector Membrane Translocation Assay (GEMTA) to monitor PAR2-induced activation of Gαq in subcellular compartments of HEK293T cells (38). GEMTA measures Gα activity by monitoring the ability of activated Gα proteins to recruit downstream effectors, and does not require modification of the receptor or the Gα protein with BRET tags. To assess compartmentalized Gαq activation, we monitored the trafficking of p63-RhoGEF, a Gαq effector, to the plasma membrane and endosomes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1P). HEK293T cells were transfected with FLAG-PAR2-HA, p63-RhoGEF-RlucII, and plasma membrane marker RGFP-CAAX or early endosome marker tdRGFP-Rab5a. 2F and trypsin rapidly stimulated ebBRET between p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX and tdRGFP-Rab5a with similar kinetics (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 Q and R). ebBRET signals at the plasma membrane declined during the 20 min post-stimulation period, whereas ebBRET signals in endosomes were sustained after an initial minor decline. These results provide evidence that PAR2 activates Gαq at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes.

NP Encapsulation Enhances Antagonism of PAR2 Signaling in Early Endosomes.

Endosomal PAR2 signaling induces disassembly of colonocyte tight junctions (14, 16) and sensitizes nociceptors (10). We hypothesized that NP encapsulation, by delivering a PAR2 antagonist to endosomes, could more effectively abrogate these ongoing pronociceptive signals. We first determined whether NP-encapsulated AZ3451 reverses the activation of PAR2, Gαq, and βARR1 in early endosomes and suppresses ERK activity in the cytosol and nucleus. PAR2 signaling from endosomes by Gαq and βARR-mediated pathways activates ERK, which increases paracellular permeability of colonocytes and sensitizes nociceptors (5, 10, 14, 16).

To determine whether NP-encapsulated and unencapsulated AZ3451 reverse ongoing activation of PAR2 at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes, nbBRET was measured between PAR2-NatP, Venus-mGαq or βARR1-YFP, and LgBiT-CAAX or LgBiT-FYVE in HEK293T cells. Cells were challenged with 2F or trypsin and then exposed to vehicle (control), unencapsulated AZ3451, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, or PEG-Poly-AZ (1 or 3 μM AZ3451) 7 min later when nbBRET signals were stable (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). In vehicle-treated cells, 2F and trypsin stimulated nbBRET between PAR2-NatP and Venus-mGαq or βARR1-YFP and LgBiT-CAAX or LgBiT-FYVE that was sustained for 30 min (Fig. 2 B–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B–E). Unencapsulated AZ3451 caused a delayed, small, and incomplete inhibition. In contrast, PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ caused a faster and larger inhibition that was complete within 30 min. Analysis of the integrated response (area under the curve, AUC, 30 min postantagonist) allowed comparison of the extent of antagonism. Unencapsulated AZ3451 did not significantly inhibit 2F- or trypsin-stimulated nbBRET between PAR2-NatP and Venus-mGαq or βARR1-YFP at the plasma membrane or in early endosomes when compared to vehicle. PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ did not significantly inhibit PAR2-NatP and Venus-mGαq nbBRET at the plasma membrane but did significantly inhibit PAR2-NatP and βARR1-YFP nbBRET at the plasma membrane. PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ significantly inhibited 2F- and trypsin-stimulated nbBRET between PAR2-NatP and both Venus-mGαq and βARR1-YFP in early endosomes.

GEMTA was used to monitor the effects of NPs on PAR2-induced activation of Gαq in subcellular compartments of HEK293T cells coexpressing FLAG-PAR2-HA, p63-RhoGEF-RlucII, and RGFP-CAAX or tdRGFP-Rab5a. Cells challenged with 2F or trypsin were exposed to vehicle, unencapsulated AZ3451, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, or PEG-Poly-AZ (1 or 3 μM AZ3451) 2 min later when ebBRET was stable. In vehicle-treated cells, 2F and trypsin stimulated ebBRET between p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX or tdRGFP-Rab5a that was stable for 22 min (Fig. 2 F G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 F and G). Unencapsulated AZ3451 caused a delayed, small, and incomplete inhibition, whereas PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ completely inhibited ebBRET signals within 10 min. Analysis of the integrated responses (AUC 20 min postantagonist) showed that PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ significantly inhibited 2F- and trypsin-stimulated ebBRET between p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX or tdRGFP-Rab5a compared to vehicle. In contrast, unencapsulated AZ3451 did not significantly inhibit these signals, except for a small inhibition of 2F-stimulated ebBRET between p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and tdRGFP-Rab5a.

To determine whether NP-encapsulated AZ3451 could reverse compartmentalized ERK signaling, FRET biosensors targeted to the cytoplasm (cytoEKAR) or nucleus (nucEKAR) were expressed with FLAG-PAR2-HA in HEK293T cells. Cells were challenged with 2F and then exposed to vehicle, unencapsulated AZ3451, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, or PEG-Poly-AZ (1 or 3 μM AZ3451) 7 min later when responses were stable. 2F-stimulated ERK activity in the cytoplasm and nucleus was sustained for 35 min in vehicle-treated cells (Fig. 2 H I). Unencapsulated AZ3451 did not significantly inhibit ERK activation, whereas PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ completely and significantly inhibited 2F-stimulated ERK activity in the cytosol and nucleus.

AZ3451 interacts with an allosteric hydrophobic pocket formed by transmembrane helices 2, 3, and 4 of PAR2 (17). The inability of low concentrations (1, 3 μM) of unencapsulated AZ3451 to access this site may explain its inability to inhibit PAR2 and Gαq signaling at the plasma membrane. However, high concentrations (10, 30 μM) of unencapsulated AZ3451 gradually and completely reversed 2F-stimulated nbBRET between PAR2-NatP and Venus-mGαq at the plasma membrane (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Empty PAMAM-Chol-Ø and PEG-Poly-Ø (equivalent solids concentrations used to encapsulate 1 or 3 μM AZ3451) did not affect 2F-stimulated nbBRET between PAR2-NatP and Venus-mGαq at the plasma membrane (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), which suggests that possible disruption of the membrane by NP components does not account for the inhibitory effects of NP-encapsulated AZ3451. High concentrations (10, 30 μM) of unencapsulated AZ3451 also inhibited 2F-stimulated recruitment of p63-RhoGEF-RlucII to the plasma membrane (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 D and E), whereas PAMAM-Chol-Ø and PEG-Poly-Ø were inactive (SI Appendix, Fig. S4F).

To ascertain whether NP encapsulation affects the potency of AZ3451, HEK293T cells were preincubated with unencapsulated AZ3451, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, PEG-Poly-AZ, PAMAM-Chol-Ø, or PEG-Poly-Ø for 4 h (to allow cell penetration and antagonist release) and were then challenged with 2F (SI Appendix, Fig. S4G). Unencapsulated AZ3451, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, and PEG-Poly-AZ concentration-dependently inhibited 2F-induced PAR2 and mGαq nbBRET at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes with identical potency and efficacy (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 H–J). Equivalent solids concentrations of PAMAM-Chol-Ø or PEG-Poly-Ø had no effect (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 K and L). Thus, AZ3451 retains full antagonistic activity when encapsulated into NPs.

When encapsulated into two different NPs that accumulate in endosomes, AZ3451 reverses activation of PAR2 and its Gαq and βARR1 effectors in early endosomes. The downstream consequence is inhibition of ERK, which mediates the proinflammatory and pronociceptive actions of PAR2. Unencapsulated AZ3451 does not effectively suppress these ongoing signals in endosomes.

NP-Encapsulated Antagonist Blocks PAR2 Actions On Colonic Epithelial Cells and Nociceptors.

To provide physiological relevance, PAR2 signaling was examined in T84 colon adenocarcinoma cells. PAR2 and βARR signaling in endosomes of T84 cells activates ERK, which controls tight junction disassembly (14, 16). After 1 h incubation with T84 cells, PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD were detected in early endosomes, identified by early endosomal antigen 1 (EEA1) immunostaining (Fig. 3 A B). Intensity line scans confirmed the coincidence of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD with EEA1. Measurement of the Manders’ overlap coefficient in multiple cells revealed that ~91% of early endosomes contained PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and ~95% of early endosomes contained PEG-Poly-QD after 1 h (Fig. 3 C D).

The effect of NPs on PAR2-stimulated Gαq activity at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes was determined by GEMTA measurements of p63-RhoGEF-RlucII proximity to RGFP-CAAX or tdRGFP-Rab5a. Cells were stimulated with 2F (50 μM) and 3 min later treated with vehicle, empty NPs, unencapsulated AZ3451 or NP-encapsulated AZ3451 (1 μM AZ3451) (Fig. 3E). 2F stimulated ebBRET between p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX or tdRGFP-Rab5a, which was sustained for 20 min (Fig. 3 F G). PAMAM-Chol-Ø and PEG-Poly-Ø did not affect these signals. Unencapsulated AZ3451 partially reversed p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX ebBRET but did not affect p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and tdRGFP-Rab5a ebBRET. In contrast, PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ completely reversed p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and RGFP-CAAX ebBRET and significantly inhibited p63-RhoGEF-RlucII and tdRGFP-Rab5a ebBRET. These results show that NP-encapsulated AZ3451 more effectively inhibits PAR2-induced activation of Gαq at the plasma membrane and in early endosomes of colonic adenocarcinoma cells than unencapsulated AZ3451, which is unable to inhibit intracellular Gαq activity. They align with the results from HEK293T cells.

PAR2 is expressed by dorsal root ganglia (DRG) nociceptors (14), where endosomal PAR2 signaling activates ERK, which induces sustained sensitization (10). PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD accumulated in EEA1+ve early endosomes of NeuN+ve neurons in cultures of mouse DRG after 1 h (Fig. 3 H I). Intensity line scans confirmed the coincidence of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 and PEG-Poly-QD with EEA1. Measurement of the Manders’ overlap coefficient in multiple neurons showed that ~90% of early endosomes contained PAMAM-Chol-Cy5, and ~83% of early endosomes contained PEG-Poly-QD after 1 h (Fig. 3 J K).

To determine whether NP-encapsulated and unencapsulated AZ3451 inhibit PAR2-induced sensitization, neurons were untreated or incubated with PAMAM-Chol-Ø, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, PEG-Poly-Ø, PEG-Poly-AZ, or unencapsulated AZ3451 (120 min, 100 nM AZ3451); neurons were washed, recovered in antagonist-free medium for 90 min, challenged with trypsin (50 nM, 10 min), and washed again. Rheobase (minimum input current to fire an action potential) was measured at T = 30 min post-trypsin by perforated-patch clamp recording (Fig. 3 L M). Trypsin decreased the rheobase to 48.33 ± 4.8 pA (34.1 ± 6.5% decrease, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3N) and 45.29 ± 4.6 pA (39.6 ± 6.1% decrease, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3O) compared to vehicle (rheobase 73.33 ± 7.1 pA and 75 ± 5.7 pA). PAMAM-Chol-Ø, PEG-Poly-Ø, and unencapsulated AZ3451 did not affect the response to trypsin, whereas PAMAM-Chol-AZ and PEG-Poly-AZ prevented trypsin-induced hyperexcitability of nociceptors (rheobase 69.16 ± 4.1 pA and 85.18 ± 6.9 pA, respectively). Thus, NP-encapsulated AZ3451 causes a sustained and complete inhibition of PAR2-evoked hyperexcitability of nociceptors that underlies pain.

Luminally Administered NPs aCcumulate in Endosomes of Epithelial Cells and Neurons in the Colon.

Observations in knockin mice expressing PAR2 fused to monomeric ultrastable GFP (muGFP) reveal that PAR2 internalizes in the inflamed colon (14), attributable to protease activation (39). Since PAR2 signals from endosomes of colonocytes and neurons to evoke inflammatory pain, the delivery of NP-encapsulated antagonists to endosomes of both cell types is therapeutically desirable.

To examine cellular and subcellular targeting of NPs in the colon, PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 or PEG-Poly-QD was administered into the colon lumen of Par2-mugfp mice by enema. The colon was excised and sectioned for detection of fluorescent NPs by microscopy. PAR2-muGFP was detected by GFP immunostaining (14). PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 was prominently and uniformly detected in colonocytes expressing PAR2-muGFP within 1 h, with maximal signals at 2 h and retention for 8 h (Fig. 4A and Movie S4). In contrast, PEG-Poly-QD was rarely detected in colonocytes but instead penetrated into the region of nerve plexuses in the submucosa and musculature (Fig. 4A). Quantification of fluorescent signals revealed preferential targeting of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 to the mucosa, consistent with colonocyte uptake, and of PEG-Poly-QD to the muscle layer, consistent with accumulation in the embedded myenteric plexus, and confirmed retention of both NPs in tissues for 8 h (Fig. 4B).

The uptake of PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 NPs into colonocytes expressing PAR2-muGFP was confirmed by PAR2-muGFP immunostaining and microscopy (Fig. 4C). 3D projections of z-stacks of confocal images revealed that PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 NPs were distributed throughout the cytoplasm and that PAR2-muGFP was concentrated at the basolateral plasma membrane of colonocytes (Movie S5). PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 NPs sometimes colocalized with PAR2-muGFP and EEA1 in early endosomes (Fig. 4C). The uptake of PEG-Poly-QD NPs into nerves in the colon was confirmed by immunostaining for the neuronal marker protein gene product 9.5 (PGP9.5) and confocal microscopy (Fig. 4D). PEG-Poly-QD NPs largely bypassed the mucosa but when present were closely associated with PGP9.5+ve nerve fibers. PEG-Poly-QD NPs were internalized in the soma of neurons of the submucosal and myenteric plexuses. 3D projections of z-stacks of confocal images revealed that PEG-Poly-QD NPs were distributed throughout the cytosol of PGP9.5+ve neurons the myenteric plexus (Movie S6).

PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 is positively charged, whereas PEG-Poly-QD is negatively charged. To explore the influence of surface charge on cellular targeting, PAMAM-Chol-Cy5 NPs were synthesized with a negative charge (zeta potential −15 ± 1 mV). Whereas PAMAM-Chol-Cy5(+) was detected mostly in colonocytes and rarely found in proximity to neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B), PAMAM-Chol-Cy5(−) was excluded from colonocytes, retained in the lamina propria, and preferentially homed to neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). Thus, a positive surface charge favors epithelial cell delivery, whereas a negative surface charge favors mucosal penetration and delivery to the nervous system.

NP Encapsulation Enhances Antagonism of PAR2-Induced Colonic Pain.

NPs that preferentially deliver AZ3451 to endosomes of colonic epithelial cells and neurons were used to determine the impact of cell and organelle targeting of PAR2 antagonists on treatment of inflammation and pain. To determine whether NP-encapsulated PAR2 antagonists ameliorate PAR2-induced colonic pain, vehicle, PAMAM-Chol-Ø, PAMAM-Chol-AZ, or unencapsulated AZ3451 was administered into the colonic lumen 30 min before intracolonic administration of 2F (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Abdominal nociception was evaluated by measuring withdrawal responses to stimulation of the abdomen with von Frey filaments (VFF). In vehicle-treated mice, 2F caused a sustained decrease in withdrawal threshold for 6 h, indicating mechanical allodynia (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B–J). PAMAM-Chol-Ø did not affect the response to 2F. PAMAM-Chol-AZ (150 μl, 1, 5, 10 μM AZ3451) caused a dose-dependent and sustained increase in VFF withdrawal threshold, consistent with antinociception (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B–G). Intracolonic PEG-Poly-AZ (150 μl, 5 μM AZ3451) also inhibited 2F-evoked mechanical allodynia for 6 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 H and I). Unencapsulated AZ3451 (150 μl, 1, 5, 10 μM) had a small and transient inhibitory action. Intravenous AZ3451 (5 μM, 150 μl) did not affect 2F-induced mechanical allodynia (SI Appendix, Fig. S6J). The results suggest that NP encapsulation increases the magnitude and duration of the antinociceptive actions of AZ3451 administered to the colon of mice.

NP-Encapsulated PAR2 Antagonist Reverses Pain and Normalizes Aberrant Behavior in Preclinical Models of IBD.

The efficacy of a NP-encapsulated PAR2 antagonist for the treatment of colonic pain was determined in two preclinical mouse models of IBD, induced by administration of trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS) or dextran sodium sulfate (DSS), which cause colonic inflammation, pain, and endocytosis of PAR2-muGFP (14).

At 3 d after intracolonic TNBS, vehicle, PAMAM-Chol-Ø, PAMAM-Chol-AZ or unencapsulated AZ3451 (150 μl, 5 μM AZ3451) was administered into the colon lumen by enema (Fig. 5A). TNBS caused weight loss and colon shortening (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), signs of inflammation. TNBS decreased the VFF threshold for abdominal withdrawal, consistent with mechanical allodynia (Fig. 5 B C). TNBS stimulated expression of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), and chemokine (C-X-C motif) ligand 1 (CXCL1) mRNA in the colon, indicating inflammation (Fig. 5 D–F). Intracolonic administration of vehicle or PAMAM-Chol-Ø had no effect on TNBS-stimulated mechanical allodynia or expression of TNFα, IL-1β, or CXCL1. PAMAM-Chol-AZ significantly inhibited TNBS-induced mechanical allodynia from 3 to 6 h, which was confirmed by analysis of the integrated response. PAMAM-Chol-AZ completely normalized expression of TNFα, IL-1β, and CXCL1, which were restored to basal levels. Unencapsulated AZ3451 had no effect on abdominal VFF withdrawal threshold but partially and significantly reduced colonic expression of TNFα, IL-1β, and CXCL1.

The effect of TNBS colitis on behavior was determined using a spectrometer, which objectively quantifies behaviors affected by pain (21, 40). TNBS colitis markedly altered locomotor, exploratory, and grooming behavior (Fig. 5 G–I and SI Appendix, Fig. S8 and Movie S7). TNBS increased the still time and number of still events and decreased abdominal grooming time and events. TNBS decreased average velocity, track length, activity, and body length (indicative of hunching). At 3 h after intracolonic injection, PAMAM-Chol-AZ normalized TNBS-induced changes in still time and events, abdominal grooming time and events, average velocity, track length, activity, and body length. Vehicle, PAMAM-Chol-Ø, and unencapsulated AZ3451 had no effects on TNBS alterations in locomotor, exploratory, and grooming behavior.

The superior efficacy of PAMAM-Chol-AZ was confirmed in mice with DSS-induced colitis. DSS in drinking water caused weight loss, disease symptoms, and colonic shortening, consistent with inflammation (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 C–E). At 8 d after starting DSS administration, vehicle, PAMAM-Chol-Ø, PAMAM-Chol-AZ or unencapsulated AZ3451 (150 μl, 5 μM AZ3451) was administered into the colon lumen by enema (Fig. 5J). DSS caused abdominal mechanical allodynia (Fig. 5 K L), increased TNFα, IL-1β, and CXCL1 mRNA expression in the colon (Fig. 5 M–O), and increased spleen weight (Fig. 5P). Intracolonic administration of vehicle or PAMAM-Chol-Ø had no effect on DSS-stimulated mechanical allodynia or expression of TNFα, IL-1β or CXCL1. PAMAM-Chol-AZ significantly inhibited allodynia from 3 to 4 h and significantly inhibited spleen weight, but did not significantly inhibit TNFα, IL-1β, or CXCL1 mRNA expression. Unencapsulated AZ3451 had no effect on mechanical allodynia, cytokine and chemokine expression, or spleen weight.

DSS strikingly affected locomotor, exploratory, and grooming behaviors (Fig. 5 Q–S and SI Appendix, Fig. S9 and Movie S8). DSS decreased center arena visit times and events, average velocity, track length, activity, distance from the wall, total, body, and abdominal grooming events and times, and walk, trot, and run events and times. DSS increased still events and time and decreased body length. At 3 h after intracolonic injection, PAMAM-Chol-AZ normalized locomotor, exploratory, and grooming behavior, and restored body length (reversal of hunching). Vehicle, PAMAM-Chol-Ø, and unencapsulated AZ3451 had no effect.

The antinociceptive and anti-inflammatory actions of PEG-Poly-AZ were similarly examined in mice with TNBS- and DSS-evoked colitis. Intracolonic PEG-Poly-AZ (150 μl, 5 μM AZ3451) significantly reversed TNBS-evoked mechanical allodynia at 2 and 6 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–C) and DSS-evoked mechanical allodynia at 4 and 5 h (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 J–L). PEG-Poly-AZ significantly inhibited TNBS-evoked TNFα and CXCL1 expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D–F). PEG-Poly-AZ significantly inhibited DSS-evoked TNFα and IL-1β expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 M,N). PEG-Poly-AZ restored TNBS- and DSS-induced changes in locomotor, exploratory, and grooming behaviors (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 G–I, P–R and S11 and S12 and Movies S9 and S10).

The results show that locally administered NPs that deliver a PAR2 antagonist to endosomes of colonic epithelial cells and neurons normalize nociception and aberrant behaviors in two preclinical models of IBD. Unencapsulated antagonist is largely ineffective. These findings support the hypothesis that endosomal PAR2 is a mediator and therapeutic target for colonic pain.

Discussion

Our results show that NPs that deliver a PAR2 antagonist to endosomes of colonocytes and colonic neurons provide a transformational increase in efficacy for the treatment of pain in preclinical models of IBD. Factors that are responsible for increased efficacy likely include NP-mediated delivery of antagonist to colonocytes and neurons that participate in pain; accumulation and retention of NP-encapsulated antagonist in endosomes that are sites of PAR2 signaling; and sustained slow release of antagonist from NPs. These attributes of NPs would be expected to attain high antagonist concentrations in endosomes, minimizing antagonist dilution and clearance and achieving effective antagonism of preexisting PAR2 signals in endosomes that mediate pain, demonstrated in cell-based assays. It is also possible that NPs facilitate the access of AZ3451 to the known allosteric hydrophobic pocket formed by transmembrane helices 2, 3, and 4 of PAR2 (17). Unencapsulated antagonist was minimally efficacious in IBD models and was unable to fully reverse endosomal PAR2 signals. We have thus identified an effective and long-lasting therapeutic approach based on the local administration of nanomedicines designed to deliver antagonists to intracellular sites of signaling in the key cell types that control pain in the colon.

Antagonism of PAR2 Signaling in Endosomes Provides Efficacious and Long-Lasting Relief From Pain.

Experiments with BRET and FRET biosensors provided evidence that PAR2 agonists stimulate sustained activation of PAR2, Gαq, Gαi, and βARR1 in early endosomes, and persistent ERK signaling in the cytosol and nucleus. These results are in accordance with observations that PAR2 and βARRs traffic to early endosomes, where βARRs serve as scaffolds for compartmentalized ERK signaling (5, 41). Whether Gα isoforms traffic with PAR2 to early endosomes or whether PAR2 couples to preexisting stores of G proteins in endosomes (42) requires further investigation. The detection of endogenous G proteins in early, late, and recycling endosomes supports the notion that endosomes are a legitimate site of GPCR signaling (42). PAR2 could be predisposed to signal in endosomes because, in contrast to soluble ligands that dissociate from GPCRs in acidified endosomes, the trypsin-exposed ligand remains tethered to PAR2.

The overexpression of biosensors, particularly those with inherent affinity, may influence their proximity and subcellular location, providing artifactual results. However, we verified that the LgBiT-FYVE nbBRET biosensor is confined to early endosomes and observed that PAR2 agonists evoke trafficking of Venus-mGαq to early endosomes, determined by microscopy. Differences in the timing of detection of mGαq recruitment to endosomes by BRET and microscopy assays likely reflect differences in sensitivity of these approaches. Since mGα biosensors are unable to hydrolyze GTP or interact with the lipid bilayer, they are limited to determination of the kinetics of GPCR rather than G protein activation. Therefore, we used GEMTA to confirm Gαq activation in endosomes. Studies of signaling of endogenous PAR2 in nociceptors will be required to determine the physiological mechanisms of compartmentalized signaling of pain.

Because GPCRs are cell-surface receptors, antagonists are generally evaluated for their ability to prevent GPCR activation at the plasma membrane. Whether antagonists terminate preexisting GPCR signals in endosomes, which requires penetration of plasma and endosome membranes and engagement of GPCRs within multiprotein signaling complexes of acidified endosomes, is seldom tested. We found that a PAR2 antagonist encapsulated into NPs rapidly and completely terminates the activation of PAR2, Gαq, and βARR1 in early endosomes, and reverses the activation of ERK, a PAR2 effector, in the cytosol and nucleus. Unencapsulated antagonist had minor and delayed inhibitory effects or was inactive, which suggests that it is unable to fully penetrate the plasma and organelle membranes to antagonize preactivated PAR2. We propose that NP trafficking to endosomes, where AZ3451 is released and accumulates at high concentrations, accounts for the effective antagonism of PAR2 signaling in these compartments. Once released from NPs in endosomes, AZ3451 likely diffuses throughout the cell to antagonize PAR2 in other compartments. NP encapsulation may also enhance the delivery of AZ3451, a lipophilic molecule, to the allosteric site outside the transmembrane helical bundle of PAR2 (17).

Our results show that NPs containing a PAR2 antagonist, when administered locally into the colon, inhibit nociception and normalize locomotor, exploratory, and grooming behavior in mouse models of IBD. The normalization of behavior is likely attributable to the antinociceptive effects of the NP-encapsulated antagonist. NP-encapsulated AZ3451 more completely reversed nociception in the TNBS than the DSS IBD model, which suggests a greater role for proteases and PAR2 in TNBS colitis. NPs that deliver the antagonist to endosomes of colonocytes (dendrimers) or colonic neurons (core-shell polymers), prominent sites of PAR2 expression (14), were both efficacious. Unencapsulated PAR2 antagonist did not inhibit nociception or normalize behavior. We hypothesize that NP-mediated delivery of AZ3451 to sites of PAR2 pain signaling in endosomes of relevant cells accounts for the therapeutic benefits of NPs. In support of this hypothesis, inhibitors of endocytosis abrogate PAR2 endosomal signaling and nociception (10, 14). Antagonism of PAR2 signals in endosomes is necessary for optimal therapy because PAR2 redistributes from the plasma membrane to endosomes of colonocytes and nociceptors in the inflamed colon (10, 14) due to protease activation (39). Endosomal signaling of PAR2 in colonic epithelial cells induces the redistribution of tight junctional proteins, leading to increased paracellular permeability and influx of inflammatory factors from the lumen (14, 16). PAR2 signals from endosomes of nociceptors to mediate sustained sensitization and nociception (10). However, deletion of PAR2 from nociceptors abrogates protease-evoked allodynia, suggesting a prominent neuronal mechanism (10). PEG-Poly-QD were internalized in the soma of isolated DRG neurons and neurons in colonic nerve plexuses. Although PEG-Poly-QD largely bypassed the mucosa, when present they were associated with mucosal nerve fibers. Higher-resolution imaging will be required to determine whether NPs internalize in nerve fibers and terminals. Whereas NP-encapsulated AZ3451 was far superior to unencapsulated antagonist for the reversal of nociception, both antagonists similarly inhibited cytokine and chemokine expression. The analysis of PAR2 signaling in immune cells may provide an explanation for this discrepancy. NP-mediated retention of antagonists in tissues, as we have previously reported (22), could further enhance efficacy and duration of action.

The physicochemical properties of dendrimer and polymeric NPs account for their differential cellular targeting. The mucosal retention of dendrimer NPs is likely due to their soft structure and cationic surface charge, rendering them mucoadhesive (27, 43). Accumulation of dendrimers in endosomes of colonocytes is attributable to the positive surface charge that enhances interactions with negative cell surfaces (43, 44). The slightly negative core-shell NPs penetrate deeply into the mucosa, submucosa, and musculature, gaining access to neurons. Mucus and tissue penetration is consistent with previous studies of PEGylated NPs (45). While colonocyte and neuronal endosomal uptake is important for efficacy, further development of NPs by surface conjugation of targeting moieties to enhance delivery to specific cell types, and core optimization to maximize drug loading and sustained endosomal release are required to advance such formulations to the clinic.

AZ3451 release from dendrimer and core-shell NPs was not pH-dependent, suggesting that pH-dependent cargo release in acidified endosomes is unlikely to contribute to persistent efficacy, in contrast to pH-tunable soft polymer NPs (20–22). pH-responsive NPs are not suitable for the treatment of inflammatory pain because they would be expected to prematurely disassemble before cellular uptake due to the acidity of extracellular fluid in inflamed tissues (23). In contrast to the slow kinetics of AZ3451 release from NPs in vitro (i.e., in solution), NP-encapsulated AZ3451 reversed PAR2 signaling in cultured cells within minutes. Although in vitro release experiments are valuable for confirming batch-to-batch consistency and evaluating drug release mechanisms (e.g., pH-triggered release), they do not predict release rates in complex and dynamic biological environments. The rapid uptake of NPs and AZ3451 concentration in endosomes likely account for the rapid reversal of PAR2 signals in cells.

Therapeutic Implications.

NP-encapsulated PAR2 antagonists may provide a much-needed nonopioid therapy for abdominal pain (46). Coupling these nanomedicines with an oral delivery strategy would expand clinical utility. Toxicology and pharmacokinetic analyses are required to advance NP-encapsulated PAR2 antagonists to clinical trials. Further studies are needed to understand the basis of NP tissue penetration, cell-specific targeting, and retention in endosomes, which could further improve therapeutic efficacy.

Our finding that an NP-encapsulated PAR2 antagonist terminates signaling in endosomes and provides sustained analgesia has broad implications. GPCRs are the largest target of approved drugs (2). Although modification of the lipophilicity and acidity of GPCR antagonists can enhance antagonism of intracellular receptors (37), the criteria for development of endosomally-targeted antagonists are uncertain. Encapsulation into NPs could facilitate antagonist delivery to sites of disease-relevant GPCR signals in organelles and thereby improve therapeutic efficacy. The local administration of NPs for targeting receptors in endosomes confers further advantages. For applications that require therapeutics to reach cytoplasmic or nuclear targets, the necessity and obstacles of endosomal escape restrict the usefulness of NP-mediated organelle targeting strategies (47). The use of NPs to deliver therapeutics to endosomal targets avoids this limitation. NPs engineered to target and persist in diseased tissues and to exhibit sustained release of drug cargo offer the prospect of improved therapeutic efficacy while minimizing dosing and side effects from systemic exposure (48). However, systemically administered NPs face delivery hurdles including opsonization, phagocytosis, and sequestration in the liver, NP surface fouling with a protein corona that impedes targeting, and the challenge of selectively targeting NPs to diseased cells. The local administration of NPs encapsulating antagonists of endosomally-enriched GPCR targets surmounts these obstacles and is applicable to treatment of many diseases.

Materials and Methods

See Supporting Information. C57BL/6J and Par2-mugfp (14) mice were used. NPs were prepared as described (24, 49). To assess NP endocytosis, fluorescent NPs were incubated with cells or injected into the mouse colon (enema). PAR2, Gα, and βARR activity was measured using BRET biosensors (14, 32, 33, 37, 38). ERK activity was measured using FRET biosensors (8). Sensitization of nociceptors was measured by patch-clamp (10). To induce colitis in mice, 2F or TNBS was injected into the colon or DSS was administered in drinking water. Reflexive and nonreflexive nociception was measured (21, 40). Inflammatory mediators were measured by qRT-PCR (14).
