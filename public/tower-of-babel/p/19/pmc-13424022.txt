Experiences and Perspectives on Diabetic Retinopathy Screening and Treatment in Mexico: A Subanalysis of the Diabetic Retinopathy Barometer Study
Authors: Ingrid Y Pita-Ortiz, Raul Velez-Montoya, Marlon García Roa, Beatriz J Pérez Aragón, Luis F Rosales Rodríguez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic macular edema (DME) are leading causes of vision loss worldwide and represent a growing public health concern in Mexico. Despite the availability of effective treatments, including anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapies, adherence and persistence remain suboptimal, compromising outcomes. This study, part of the international DR Barometer project, examined the perspectives of patients, healthcare providers, and clinic staff to better understand challenges and opportunities in DR/DME care delivery in Mexico.MethodsA cross-sectional, survey-based study was conducted across five high-volume ophthalmology clinics in Mexico. Standardized, paper-based questionnaires were completed anonymously by patients with DR or DME, healthcare providers, and clinic staff. Data were collected between October and November 2023, scanned, and analyzed descriptively.ResultsA total of 1151 participants were included: 558 patients with DME, 265 patients with DR, 122 providers, and 206 clinic staff. Key barriers to adherence included financial burden of treatment (reported by 67.4% of patients with DME; emphasized by 97.5% of providers), logistical difficulties such as travel and long waiting times, and emotional concerns about being a burden to family (71.5% of patients with DME; 61.1% of patients with DR). Nonadherent patients were more likely to prioritize competing health issues and report limited support from care teams. While most patients stated that eye treatment was a priority (87–91%), only 61% of providers believed this to be true. Opportunities highlighted across stakeholders included proactive provider–patient communication, continuity of care with the same clinical team, appointment reminders, and financial assistance. Clinic staff emphasized the need for improved educational materials and better coordination of diabetes and eye care services.ConclusionsThis study reveals substantial challenges to adherence in DR/DME management in participating Mexican referral centers, rooted in financial, logistical, and psychosocial factors. Strengthening communication, expanding financial support, and integrating coordinated, patient-centered care strategies could improve long-term treatment engagement and outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains Supplementary Material available at 10.1007/s40123-026-01428-8.Keywords: Adherence, Anti-VEGF therapy, Care coordination, Diabetic macular edema, Diabetic retinopathy, Health systems, Mexico, Patient perspectives, Persistence, Vision impairment

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Diabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic macular edema (DME) are common diabetes complications and leading causes of preventable vision loss, with a growing public health burden in Mexico.Despite effective treatments (notably anti-VEGF for DME), adherence and persistence are suboptimal, undermining real-world outcomes and increasing long-term clinical and system burden. What did the study ask?/What was the hypothesis of the study? The study asked what patients, providers, and clinic staff in Mexico perceive as the main barriers and opportunities affecting DR/DME screening and treatment engagement (especially adherence to anti-VEGF regimens). What was learned from the study? The survey in five Mexico clinics (October–November 2023; 1151 people) found that the biggest reasons patients miss or stop DR/DME care are cost, travel/long wait times, and worries about being a burden to family.The results suggest that better communication, seeing the same care team, appointment reminders, more patient education, and financial support—plus closer coordination between diabetes and eye clinics—could improve long-term treatment adherence.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) and diabetic macular edema (DME) are two of the most prevalent ocular complications of diabetes mellitus and leading causes of preventable vision impairment and blindness worldwide [1]. Both conditions reflect the systemic burden of diabetes and present complex management challenges for patients and healthcare systems alike.

Globally, approximately 10.5% of the population lives with diabetes, a figure projected to increase to 12% by 2045 [2]. Among individuals with diabetes, it is estimated that 35.4% exhibit signs of DR, and roughly one-third of these individuals develop DME [3]. As diabetes prevalence rises—driven in large part by aging populations, sedentary lifestyles, and increasing rates of obesity [4]—so too does the burden of DR and DME. The resultant clinical, social, and economic impacts are substantial, with DR and DME placing considerable pressure on healthcare resources.

In Mexico, the burden of DR and DME has been increasing. Early studies conducted in 1994 in low-income districts of Mexico City reported DR in 54.1% of men and 45% of women, and DME in 8.2% and 4.7%, respectively [5]. By 2015, DR prevalence among individuals with type 2 diabetes in Northeastern Mexico had increased to 68% [6]. More recently, data from 2013 to 2022 indicate a DME prevalence of 2.91% among diabetic patients undergoing ophthalmologic evaluation in Mexico City [7]. These findings illustrate a growing public health concern.

Treatment for DR primarily focuses on prevention through glycemic, blood pressure, and lipid control, while DME is typically managed using intravitreal injections of anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents as well as corticosteroid-based therapies for selected patients [8, 9]. In Mexico, anti-VEGF therapy—including ranibizumab, and aflibercept—is integrated into public healthcare practice [10]. Despite their proven efficacy, these therapies impose logistical and economic burdens that affect adherence and persistence. The AQUILA study, conducted across 11 clinics in Mexico, found that most patients received fewer than the label-recommended number of injections, although visual outcomes still improved [11]. Given the high prevalence of DR and DME in Mexico [12], and the challenges associated with sustained treatment, there is a critical need to understand the factors contributing to suboptimal adherence and persistence.

DR Barometer is a project developed by the International Federation on Ageing (IFA), in collaboration with the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) and the International Agency for the Prevention of Blindness (IAPB). Conducted as a multicountry quantitative study, it is based on a survey that examined risk awareness, access to diabetic retinopathy (DR) screening and treatment, the nature of health services and community support available, as well as protocols, guidelines, and the socioeconomic consequences of DR and DME [16]. The first DR Barometer study report was published in 2017 and included information from eight countries [13]. The current version of the DR Barometer, presenting the first international results, includes 28 countries. The aim of this manuscript is to present data from Mexico and provide a comprehensive overview of the perspectives of patients, healthcare providers, and clinic staff involved in the management of DR and DME at urban referral ophthalmology centers throughout the country.

Methods

Design

The clinic primer questionnaires were completed online after each clinic agreed to participate. The paper-based optical mark recognition (OMR) questionnaires were translated into local languages, delivered to clinics by courier, completed by participants, and returned in batches to Exploristics, an independent company responsible for analyzing the results. Data collection at each clinic was performed during 3 months. Each OMR questionnaire included a unique barcode specific to its language and type, linking it to the corresponding clinic to ensure accurate data attribution.

The standard definitions of “adherent patient,” “non-adherence,” “missed visit,” and “non-persistence” established by the nAMD Barometer Leadership Coalition were used (Supplementary Material: Supplementary Table 1) [14].

Participants

Clinics

The five clinics that participated in this project in Mexico were: Asociación para Evitar la Ceguera en México; Dr. Roberto Nettel Flores General Hospital; Fundación Hospital Nuestra Señora de la Luz, I.A.P.; Instituto Mexicano de Oftalmología, I.A.P.; and Centro Oftalmológico Sala Uno. The participating clinics were selected on the basis of their involvement in the international DR Barometer initiative, their high patient volume in DR/DME care, and their geographic distribution across different regions of Mexico. These urban centers are referral ophthalmology institutions with experience in the management of diabetic retinal diseases.

Patients

Patients with a confirmed diagnosis of DR or DME who were attending one of the five clinics participating in this project and who agreed to take part in the study were included. Participation was entirely voluntary. The survey procedure was explained to each patient, and they were asked if they wished to complete the questionnaire anonymously. If the patient was able to respond independently, they did so. If not, a companion was invited to assist. In cases where neither the patient nor the companion could complete the survey, a member of the medical staff supported the patient by reading the questions aloud and recording their responses.

Healthcare Professionals

All healthcare professionals for the five selected clinics were invited to participate in the study. The ones that accepted completed the questionnaire anonymously. Healthcare providers surveyed included residents, fellows, and attending physicians, as well as allied health personnel.

Because participation was voluntary and anonymous, the number of professionals who declined participation was not systematically recorded.

Surveys

To understand perspectives on diabetic retinal conditions and their treatment, five paper-based questionnaires were developed, each tailored to a specific group: (i) DME patient questionnaire—for patients currently receiving or who previously received anti-VEGF injections for DME; (ii) DR patient questionnaire—for patients diagnosed with DR who had not developed DME; (iii) provider questionnaire—for healthcare professionals who prescribe or administer anti-VEGF injections; (iv) clinic staff questionnaire—for non-prescribing staff (e.g., technicians, nurses, and administrative personnel) who regularly interact with patients with DR/DME; and (v) clinic primer questionnaire—completed by each clinic to determine how many questionnaires to distribute to each group and to collect clinic-level data.

These five questionnaires defined the five analysis populations: (i) patients with DME, (ii) patients with DR, (iii) providers, (iV) clinic staff, and (v) clinics.

Across the questionnaires, there were a finite number of multiple-choice responses available per question and no free-text answers collected. The questionnaires are available in the Supplementary Material: Supplementary Fig. 1. The OMR questionnaires were scanned into the survey database so the data collected could be analyzed.

The questionnaires were developed as part of the international DR Barometer initiative through collaboration among clinicians, patient representatives, and survey methodology experts. Standardized instruments were translated and adapted for local use in participating countries. Prior to implementation, questionnaire content and survey constructs underwent internal review and validation procedures coordinated by Exploristics, the independent organization responsible for survey administration and analysis. However, formal psychometric validation specifically in the Mexican population was not performed.

The survey was completed by the participants between October and November 2023.

All procedures adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, the Mexican Federal Law for the Protection of Personal Data in Possession of Individuals (NOM-024-SSA3-2010), and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) regulations.

The survey was designed and developed according to the Declaration of Helsinki and the World Health Organization’s International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research. While the survey is a primary market research survey (which does not require ethics committee approval), individual institutions assessed local requirements.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants. Because the survey was anonymous and did not collect personal identifying information, written consent was not required. However, consent was implied by voluntary completion of the survey, without coercion, financial incentive, or influence on medical care.

Statistics

Qualitative variables were described by count and percentage. Data processing and analysis were performed using Excel software (Microsoft, WA, USA).

Results

Participant Characteristics

There were a total of 1151 participants in this survey recruited from five clinics: 558 were individuals with DME, 265 were individuals with DR, 122 were providers, and 206 were staff. The demographic characteristics of the population are displayed in the Supplementary Material: Supplementary Tables 2–4.

Patients with DME

Among the challenges related to managing DME appointments, the three most frequently reported were (Fig. 1): difficulty traveling to the clinic (57%), having someone available to accompany the patient (56.6%), and long waiting times during visits (55.9%). In terms of reasons behind missed appointments or treatment discontinuation, the leading responses were “I am concerned about being a burden to family/friends” (71.5%) and “Personal costs related to the drug itself makes it difficult” (67.4%).

When considering interactions with their doctor, the only statement falling short of 80% agreement was “I always have enough time to discuss my questions or concerns” (78.3%). Despite these barriers, the vast majority (90.7%) agreed with “Even though my life is busy, my eye treatment is a priority for me.” As for diabetes-related complications affecting appointment attendance, most were seen as only mildly problematic—with the exception of “Vision loss,” which 42.8% rated as somewhat or extremely difficult.

Patients with DR

For patients managing DR appointments, the most commonly cited challenges included traveling to the clinic (45.3%), long waiting periods (43.0%), and expenses related to office or parking fees (39.6%). The top concerns linked to missed appointments or stopping treatment were “I am concerned about being a burden to family/friends” (61.1%) and “Personal costs related to the treatment make it difficult” (27.9%).

In the patient–provider dynamic, the only doctor-related challenge below the 70% mark was “I always have enough time to discuss my questions or concerns” (67.5%). Nevertheless, a large majority (87.2%) affirmed that “Even though my life is busy, my eye treatment is a priority for me.” Regarding complications stemming from diabetes, all were seen as less impactful—rated below 26%—except for “Vision loss,” noted by 44.5% as a considerable challenge.

Adherent and Nonadherent Patients with DME

When comparing adherent and nonadherent patients with DME, certain challenges stood out owing to notable differences. Nonadherent individuals more frequently selected “Appointments interfere with holiday/vacation” (50.8% versus 29.0%), “Other chronic health conditions make it difficult” (63.1% versus 43.4%), and “Difficult to reschedule a new appointment once one is missed” (55.4% versus 38.1%).

As for motivations behind missing appointments or stopping treatment, nonadherent patients were more likely to agree with “I prioritize other health issues over my DME treatment” (60.0%), “I feel treatment was successful and I no longer need it” (55.4%), and “Receiving treatment is just not that important to me” (50.8%).

Differences also emerged in how patients relate to their care team. A greater share of nonadherent patients disagreed with the statements “I am comfortable asking for additional support services to help manage my DME” (23.1% versus 14.4%) and “They understand my challenges in managing my diabetes and/or DME” (21.5% versus 13.3%).

In terms of treatment prioritization, 20.0% of nonadherent patients disagreed that “Even though my life is busy, my eye treatment is a priority for me,” compared with just 4.8% among adherent patients. Similarly, diabetes-related complications such as “Vision loss” (44.6% versus 28.8%) and “Loss of feeling in hands or toes” (35.4% versus 21.8%) were more often perceived as barriers by the nonadherent group.

Providers

From the provider perspective, the most prominent challenges patients face in managing DR/DME treatment were “Personal costs related to the drug makes it difficult” (97.5%), “The frequency of treatment can be too much” (90.2%), and “Patients are not sure if treatment is working as their vision is not getting better or it is getting worse” (86.9%). An equally high proportion (86.9%) agreed that “Patients are not sure the effort associated with the treatment is worth it.”

Other patient-related concerns reported by providers included “Concern about being a burden to their family/friends” (77.0%) and “Patients prioritize other health issues over their DR/DME treatment” (77.0%). Furthermore, a lack of understanding on the part of patients was recognized by 71.3% of providers, who agreed that “Patients do not understand their DR/DME and/or treatment need.”

Time constraints during consultations were also identified as a challenge. Only 41.8% of providers agreed with the following statement: “There is always enough time during an appointment to discuss a patient’s questions or concerns.” In addition, just 60.7% felt that “Patients see their eye treatment as a priority,” and only 54.9% believed patients understood the treatment goals and required frequency.

Clinic Staff

Clinic staff also pointed to several key challenges in managing patients with DR/DME. The most pressing issues identified were “Personal costs related to the drug makes it difficult” (93.7%), “Patients are not sure if treatment is working as their vision is not getting better or it is getting worse” (85.4%), and “The frequency of treatment can be too much” (85.0%). High levels of concern were also noted for “Patients are not sure the effort associated with the treatment is worth it” and “Fear of the procedure itself makes it difficult,” both at 83.5%.

On the topic of prioritization, 82.0% observed that “Patients prioritize other health issues over their DR/DME treatment,” while 82.5% cited “Concern about being a burden to family/friends” as a widespread challenge.

When evaluating patients’ understanding, 79.6% of staff agreed that “Patients do not understand their DR/DME and/or treatment need,” and 78.2% agreed that “Patients feel treatment was successful and no longer need it.”

Forgetfulness also emerged as a barrier, with 77.2% stating that “Patients tend to just forget about appointments.” Other logistical hurdles, such as insurance coverage limitations (74.3%), procedural pain, transportation costs, and difficulties with rescheduling, were acknowledged but ranked somewhat lower than the top challenges.

Patients with DME

When exploring opportunities to enhance support for patients with DME, the highest-rated needs were “My doctor proactively discussing any challenges I may have” (88.4%), “Always having the same clinic staff and doctor treating me” (88.0%), and “Phone consultations to answer any questions/concerns I may have” (82.3%). Additional priorities included “Financial assistance with my DME drug/prescription costs” (80.6%) and “Extra time with my doctor to plan the next 6 months of treatment” (78.7%).

Regarding appointment logistics, “Appointment reminders sent to me by the clinic” was rated as highly important by 78.1% of patients. Opportunities that support patient autonomy and self-management—such as “Ability to monitor my vision accurately with a home monitoring machine” (78.1%) and “Dedicated nurse in the clinic to provide guidance to improve diabetes management and answer questions about my DME” (77.8%)—also stood out.

Though lower on the list, logistical supports such as “Medical services/treatment that travel to or near my home” (70.4%) and “Transportation assistance to attend my treatment/office visits” (68.6%) were still seen as valuable by a meaningful portion of patients.

Patients with DR

In the case of patients with DR, the most strongly supported opportunities were similar. “Always having the same clinic staff and doctor treating me” (81.9%), “Financial assistance with my DR treatment costs” (80.0%), and “My doctor proactively discussing any challenges I may have” (79.2%) emerged as top needs. Educational and relational supports were also emphasized: “Better material available to improve my understanding of DR” (76.6%) and “More involvement of the person who accompanies me in my care” (57.7%).

On the topic of treatment, a notable 87.2% agreed with “I would accept more treatment if it allowed me to keep my vision.” Meanwhile, 59.2% saw value in “Increasing the timeframe between needing treatment.” Importantly, over 6 in 10 (61.9%) felt that “It is difficult to stay on treatment and I would like extra support,” pointing to a clear need for sustained follow-up and guidance.

Adherent and Nonadherent Patients with DME

Comparative insights between adherent and nonadherent patients revealed key divergences. Nonadherent patients were more likely to value “If DME in both eyes, treat both eyes on the same day” (80.0% versus 65.8%), “An information pack for my supervisor/teacher to explain DME and the need for my frequent eye appointments” (76.9% versus 65.8%), and “More involvement of the person who accompanies me in my care” (76.9% versus 70.4%). These differences highlight a greater need for practical and social support within the nonadherent group.

Similarly, logistical enablers such as “Medical services/treatment that travel to or near my home” (75.4%) and “Transportation assistance” (72.3%) were more frequently prioritized by nonadherent patients compared with their adherent counterparts (70.8% and 68.4%, respectively).

While both groups strongly valued “My doctor proactively discussing any challenges I may have” (88.2% versus 87.7%), nonadherent patients were less likely to consider “Financial assistance with my DME drug/prescription costs” a top priority (72.3% versus 82.4%).

Furthermore, gaps in clinic-based coordination emerged. Fewer nonadherent patients considered the following as crucial: “Providing diabetes care services within the eye clinic” (76.9% versus 88.5%), “Coordination of my diabetes appointments by a professional” (69.2% versus 85.6%), and “Better material to improve my understanding of DME” (76.9% versus 85.6%).

Despite these differences, agreement on the need for support was widespread: “It is difficult to stay on treatment and I would like extra support” was acknowledged by 76.9% of nonadherent and 78.2% of adherent patients. A more notable gap appeared in the statement “I would accept more treatment if it allowed me to keep my vision,” with lower agreement among nonadherent patients (87.7% versus 94.6%).

Providers

From the providers’ viewpoint, multiple areas were identified as opportunities to better support patients with DR/DME. A vast majority (91.8%) agreed that patients struggle to manage their diabetes and would benefit from extra support, with 92.6% specifically highlighting challenges in treatment adherence among individuals with DME. Moreover, 91.0% believed that patients would be open to receiving more treatment if it helped preserve their vision, and 82.0% saw value in increasing the interval between treatments.

Among the practical strategies identified, providers prioritized “More time to answer questions during appointments” (87.7%), “Appointment reminders from the clinic” (86.1%), and “Financial assistance with DME-related drug or prescription costs” (82.8%). Additionally, integrated care measures—such as having a “Dedicated nurse to improve diabetes management” (81.1%) and offering “Eye care services within diabetes clinics” (87.7%)—were strongly supported.

When it comes to anti-VEGF therapy, provider consensus was clear: 96.7% agreed that early, intensive treatment leads to meaningful vision gains; 95.1% affirmed that completing the full loading phase improves outcomes. Furthermore, 89.3% supported early intensive therapy as a means to reduce treatment burden in later years, and 88.5% felt that lengthening the interval between injections could boost adherence.

Lastly, gaps in provider training were acknowledged. Nearly 29% reported having little or no training on contraindications of antidiabetic medications, and 18.9% cited a lack of preparation in communication skills—areas that were flagged for improvement to enhance patient care.

Clinic Staff

Clinic staff also recognized a range of opportunities to improve care for patients with DR/DME. A strong majority (83.0%) felt that patients need additional support to manage their diabetes, and 90.3% believed patients would accept more treatment if it preserved their vision. In addition, 84.0% noted that staying on treatment is a common challenge, and 75.2% emphasized the importance of longer intervals between appointments.

From a care delivery standpoint, the most valued strategies were “More time for the doctor to answer questions” (90.8%), “Financial assistance with drug/prescription costs” (85.9%), and “Extra time to plan the next six months of treatment” (85.0%). Communication-focused opportunities such as “Appointment reminders” (84.5%) and “Proactively discussing patient challenges” (81.1%) were also highly rated.

Clinic staff placed considerable emphasis on care coordination and education. “Better material to improve understanding of DR/DME,” “Coordination of diabetes appointments by a professional,” and “Informing patients about treatment schedules” were all rated extremely or very important by 92.7% of respondents. Integrated care across specialties was also favored, with 88.8% supporting diabetes care within the eye clinic, and 88.3% endorsing eye care within diabetes clinics.

Although ranked slightly lower, practical supports such as “Transportation assistance” and “Financial help with office/parking fees” (both 67.0%) were still seen as relevant. Fewer staff members prioritized “Treating both eyes on the same day” (66.0%), indicating a potential area for greater consensus in care logistics.

Providers

Regarding the level of discussion providers have with patients during the first 3 months of diagnosis or treatment, the most consistently discussed topics were “Basic explanation of what DR/DME is” (98.4%) and “Risk that DR/DME could result in vision loss” (94.2%), followed closely by “How improved diabetes control may protect their vision” (92.6%) and “Correlation between diabetes and risk of complications” (93.4%). Providers also frequently addressed “How often they would need treatment” (91.8%) and “How treatment may reverse the progression of DR/DME” (93.4%).

Conversely, topics with lower levels of discussion included “Where to find more information on DR/DME or support services” (63.9%) and “Where to find more information on improving diabetes management” (65.6%), with roughly one-third of providers indicating they rarely or never discuss these topics (33.6% and 31.4%, respectively). Similarly, “How to include their family/friends in their care” was rarely or never discussed by 36.1% of providers.

In terms of treatment expectations and support, most providers (89.3–91.8%) regularly discuss what to expect with vision changes, the importance of continuing treatment, and the duration of treatment needed. However, opportunities remain to enhance the breadth and consistency of conversations—particularly on support resources and the involvement of caregivers—during the early stages of diagnosis and treatment​.

Clinic Staff

In terms of communication with patients with DR/DME, clinic staff reported varying levels of engagement across specific educational and supportive topics. The most frequently discussed theme was the importance of attending appointments, with 84.0% of staff indicating this topic is addressed in most patient interactions. In contrast, more clinical and long-term management subjects were less consistently covered.

Topics related to how DR/DME may affect vision (55.3%), concerns with vision (53.9%), and the correlation between diabetes management and risk of complications (48.5%) were moderately discussed. Meanwhile, practical guidance on personal steps to reduce the risk of disease progression (48.5%) and treatment-related concerns (41.7%) were addressed less frequently.

Patients with DME

In terms of information provided to patients with DME, only a third (33.7%) reported receiving clear guidance on what DME is and what to expect from treatment over time. Notably, 31.9% stated they did not receive any written or digital materials at all. Communication consistency between general practitioners and ophthalmologists also appeared limited—only 47.3% of patients said the information from both sources was the same, while another 32.3% indicated it was only sometimes aligned.

When asked about treatment duration, more than half of patients (52.5%) expected therapy to last 1 year or less, whereas nearly a quarter (24.4%) anticipated continuing treatment for 2 years or longer. Regarding the frequency of injections in the following year, 56.1% anticipated six or fewer, and 35.3% expected three or fewer. Despite these uncertainties, patient outlook was generally positive: 84.4% believed their vision would improve, with only 6.3% expecting it to worsen.

Patients with DR

Similar patterns emerged among patients with DR regarding expectations for anti-VEGF therapy. Just 33.7% reported receiving clear, written or digital information explaining what DR/DME is and what treatment entails over time. Additionally, 31.9% mentioned they had received no educational materials whatsoever. As with patients with DME, inconsistencies in communication were common—only 47.3% affirmed receiving the same information from both their general physician and eye doctor, while 32.3% said the information was only occasionally consistent.

Treatment expectations followed a similar trend: 52.5% believed treatment would last no longer than a year, while 24.4% expected it would extend to 2 years or more. Expectations around injection frequency also reflected this: 56.1% predicted receiving six or fewer injections in the next 12 months, and 35.3% expected three or fewer. In terms of anticipated outcomes, 84.4% hoped for visual improvement, whereas 9.3% expected no change and 6.3% feared deterioration.

Providers

From the clinical standpoint, providers demonstrated strong alignment with key treatment principles regarding anti-VEGF use in DME. Nearly all (96.7%) agreed that intensive treatment during the first year can yield meaningful vision improvements. Likewise, 95.1% believed patients who complete the full loading phase (as indicated on the label) achieve better visual outcomes than those who do not.

Furthermore, 92.6% emphasized that missing injections during the initial year significantly increases the risk of worse long-term outcomes. Providers also recognized the potential benefits of early intensive therapy beyond vision gains: 89.3% noted it may improve DR severity (as measured by the Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale [DRSS]), reduce the number of injections in later years, and help maintain gains for up to 5 years—if follow-up and retreatment protocols are followed.

Clinic Staff Perspective

Clinic staff, in contrast, showed considerable variability in how thoroughly they discuss expectations around anti-VEGF treatment. The most frequently covered subject was the importance of attending appointments, addressed by 84.0% overall, with the highest engagement among retina specialists and resident doctors (94.3%).

However, other critical topics were discussed far less frequently. Only 55.3% of staff routinely explained how DR/DME can affect vision. Less than half provided guidance on how diabetes management influences complications (48.5%) or on actions patients can take to slow disease progression (also 48.5%). Even fewer touched on concerns patients may have about treatment (41.7%) or explained where to find additional support services (38.3%).

When asked how informed they felt about their condition and care, less than half of patients with DME (44.1%) believed they had sufficient knowledge about where to access additional resources or support services. In comparison, individuals with DR reported slightly lower levels of information awareness across most topics during the initial 3 months of care. The least understood areas were: “Where to find more information on DR or support services” (40.5%), “How to include your family/friends in your care” (55.6%), and “What to expect after completing the first year of treatment” (56.5%). These figures highlight limited awareness around both external support options and long-term treatment planning.

Conversely, patients demonstrated better comprehension of foundational concepts. For instance, 75.3% agreed with “Basic explanation of what DR is,” and 76.3% acknowledged “Why DR is a chronic condition requiring long-term regular treatment.” The strongest area of agreement was “Risk that DR could result in vision loss,” with 77.3% of patients feeling well informed.

Discussion

The Mexico Barometer study offers critical insights into the lived experiences of individuals with DR and DME, alongside the perspectives of healthcare providers and clinic staff across the country.

This report provides an overview of the issues faced by patients with DR and by patients with DME receiving or having received anti-VEGF eye injections at five clinics in Mexico. It identifies a number of challenges and opportunities that could impact the ability of the patients to adhere to their treatment. The perspectives of the patients themselves, as well as their providers and clinic staff, were used to compile this report.

Starting with the challenges reported, a key convergence among all stakeholder groups is the recognition of the financial burden associated with treatment. Patients across both DR and DME cohorts cited “personal costs related to the drug” as a primary barrier to adherence—a view echoed almost unanimously by providers (97.5%) and clinic staff (93.7%). Variability in access between institutions and geographic regions may partially explain the high proportion of patients reporting financial and logistical barriers to treatment adherence. Differences in treatment availability, infrastructure, waiting times, and continuity of follow-up likely contribute to heterogeneous patient experiences across Mexico. This aligns with a prior study demonstrating that out-of-pocket expenses, especially for anti-VEGF therapy, significantly reduce the likelihood of long-term treatment continuation [15]. These findings emphasize the urgent need for policies that expand financial assistance programs and explore models of cost-sharing to reduce dropout rates.

Another widely shared challenge is the emotional toll of feeling that one is a burden to family or friends, with high concern reported by patients (DME: 71.5%, DR: 61.1%) and corroborated by providers (77.0%) and clinical staff (82.5%). This emotional dimension is often overlooked in clinical management but has been associated with lower appointment adherence and self-management confidence [16]. Incorporating psychosocial screening and offering caregiver-inclusive interventions may be essential to mitigate this burden.

Additionally, treatment burden, including frequent appointments and injections, emerged as a prominent concern across stakeholders. Providers (90.2%) and staff (85.0%) highlighted the overwhelming frequency of visits, while patients reported logistical barriers such as difficulty traveling to the clinic, long wait times, and scheduling challenges. Prior works have shown that injection fatigue is a leading cause of nonadherence in real-world settings, reinforcing the value of treatment regimens that offer longer durability or fewer visits without compromising efficacy [17, 18].

Despite these shared concerns, notable perception gaps exist, particularly around communication and treatment prioritization. For example, while most patients expressed that their eye treatment is a priority (DME: 90.7%, DR: 87.2%), only 60.7% of providers believed this to be true of their patients. Similarly, only 41.8% of providers felt there was adequate time to address patient concerns during visits, while nearly 70–78% of patients felt otherwise. This mismatch suggests that patients and providers may value different aspects of clinical communication—patients may perceive time spent as sufficient, while providers may feel pressured by time constraints that limit deeper engagement. These findings are consistent with other reports highlighting the importance of aligning provider–patient expectations to improve shared decision-making [19, 20].

Differences were especially pronounced when comparing adherent and nonadherent patients with DME. Nonadherent individuals more frequently cited competing health conditions, difficulty rescheduling, and lack of emotional support as key barriers. They were also less likely to feel understood by their care team and less comfortable asking for help, highlighting a dual burden of logistical and relational challenges. This mirrors findings from patient-centered studies emphasizing that nonadherence often reflects not resistance, but unmet needs around support, trust, and flexibility [17, 20].

Lastly, clinic staff provided a distinct lens, emphasizing practical issues such as forgetfulness (77.2%) and procedural fear (83.5%), often underrepresented in patient self-reporting. These insights suggest that staff, through frequent contact with patients, may observe behavioral and emotional cues that providers might overlook, underscoring the importance of engaging all clinic roles in patient-centered care strategies.

Overall, this multiperspective approach reveals a critical need for more integrated, flexible, and empathic care pathways. Addressing both the shared and divergent challenges across stakeholder groups—through policy, practice redesign, and communication strategies—will be vital to improving adherence and clinical outcomes in DR and DME.

The analysis of the opportunity-related questionnaires also revealed relevant information. Across stakeholder groups, there was broad consensus on several shared opportunities—particularly those aimed at improving communication, reducing logistical burdens, and enhancing continuity of care. For instance, the opportunity for providers to proactively discuss challenges with patients emerged as a top priority for both individuals with DME and DR (88.4% and 79.2%, respectively), and was strongly supported by clinic staff (81.1%). This reinforces prior evidence suggesting that empathetic, patient-centered communication plays a central role in promoting adherence and engagement in chronic disease care [21].

Another highly valued opportunity was continuity of care, specifically “always having the same clinic staff and doctor.” This was ranked highly by both patients with DME (88.0%) and DR (81.9%) and echoes findings from other studies that have linked consistent provider relationships with improved adherence and greater patient satisfaction [22]. Similarly, appointment reminders, extra time during consultations, and financial assistance for treatment costs were consistently ranked among the most important interventions across patients, providers, and clinic staff—underscoring the importance of both structural supports and affordability in chronic disease management [22].

At the same time, key differences between stakeholder groups reveal opportunities for more tailored strategies. One of the clearest divides emerged between adherent and nonadherent patients with DME. Nonadherent individuals placed greater importance on social and logistical enablers—such as being able to treat both eyes on the same day, receiving transportation support, or having information materials to justify time off to supervisors—highlighting that nonadherence is often driven by real-life constraints and social pressures, rather than indifference or lack of knowledge. This supports earlier work suggesting that nonadherent behavior frequently reflects unmet support needs rather than outright treatment refusal [17].

In contrast, providers emphasized clinical efficacy, with 96.7% agreeing that early, intensive anti-VEGF treatment leads to meaningful vision gains. While this reflects alignment with best-practice treatment protocols [23], the difference in focus—efficacy versus experience—may partly explain patient hesitancy in some cases. Notably, while nearly all providers believed patients would accept more treatment if it preserved vision (91.0%), only 87.7% of nonadherent patients agreed, suggesting some disconnect between clinical intent and patient readiness.

Clinic staff offered yet another point of view. While they supported many of the same opportunities as providers and patients, they placed particular emphasis on educational materials, inter-service coordination, and structured patient guidance (all rated ≥ 92.7%). As professionals who interact frequently with patients outside of formal clinical encounters, staff are well-positioned to observe gaps in understanding, appointment adherence, and emotional readiness. Their unique perspective reinforces the need to integrate educational and navigational supports into standard care protocols.

Taken together, these findings argue for a more integrated and stakeholder-responsive approach to DR/DME management. Shared priorities—such as consistent care teams, appointment reminders, and financial relief—can be implemented at scale, while more individualized supports (e.g., transportation, caregiver involvement, and patient education) may need to be adapted to specific patient profiles, particularly for nonadherent groups.

Moreover, this study supports the growing consensus that engaging all levels of the care team, including providers and clinic staff, is essential to closing the gaps in treatment adherence and patient satisfaction. Programs that combine early, intensive treatment with wraparound support services and personalized communication strategies are more likely to yield sustainable improvements in both clinical outcomes and patient-reported experiences.

Important patterns in the level of discussion can also be highlighted from the survey data. Across stakeholder groups, there was general alignment in the emphasis placed on foundational disease education and appointment adherence. Nearly all providers reported regularly discussing a “basic explanation of what DR/DME is” (98.4%) and the “risk that DR/DME could result in vision loss” (94.2%), which were also among the highest-rated topics by patients in previous sections. Clinic staff, while not as deeply involved in clinical counseling, consistently emphasized the “importance of attending appointments” (84.0%), reinforcing a shared understanding of the need for adherence to treatment schedules as a vital component of care continuity.

However, the analysis also revealed clear differences in the depth, content, and consistency of communication. Providers were significantly more likely to discuss clinical details, such as the correlation between diabetes control and disease progression, the potential reversibility of DR/DME with treatment, and treatment frequency or duration.

In contrast, clinic staff reported lower engagement in discussing long-term treatment goals or risk-related education, and rarely addressed more complex clinical topics. Their communication tended to center on logistical matters or appointment coordination, which is expected given their operational roles. Still, this segmentation presents a missed opportunity: With proper training and tools, clinic staff could play a more significant role in reinforcing key messages and helping to bridge gaps in patient understanding.

Notably, both providers and staff under-discussed support-oriented topics. Discussions around “where to find more information or support services” and “how to include family/friends in care” were reported inconsistently across both groups. These omissions are concerning, as psychosocial support and caregiver involvement have been shown to improve treatment adherence and patient satisfaction in different diseases [24–27].

To address these gaps, multidisciplinary communication protocols and structured discussion checklists may help ensure all patients receive consistent, holistic information—spanning from disease mechanisms to support resources—regardless of which team member they engage with. Such integration could improve both short-term engagement and long-term adherence, ultimately enhancing outcomes in DR/DME care.

Clear communication of treatment expectations plays a central role in patient adherence and satisfaction [28], particularly in chronic ocular conditions such as DR and DME, where treatment burden is high and therapeutic timelines are long. Our findings reveal both alignment and significant gaps in how different stakeholders approach communication regarding anti-VEGF treatment expectations.

One of the most consistent themes across stakeholder groups was the importance of maintaining regular appointments. Clinic staff, in particular, emphasized this topic, with 84.0% reporting that they routinely stress the necessity of attendance. Providers reinforced this indirectly, with 92.6% agreeing that missing injections in the first year leads to poorer long-term outcomes. These results echo previous studies by Jackson et al. (2020) and Okada et al. (2020), suggesting that reinforcement of adherence by multiple care team members can significantly influence patient behavior and persistence with intravitreal therapy [15, 29].

However, despite this shared emphasis on adherence, our data point to a clear communication breakdown regarding foundational treatment information. Only one-third of both individuals with DME and DR (33.7%) reported receiving clear written or digital information explaining the nature of their condition and what to expect from treatment. Nearly the same proportion stated they received no materials at all. This is particularly concerning given that providers overwhelmingly affirmed the importance of early, intensive treatment (96.7%) and the benefits of completing the full loading phase (95.1%). The disconnect suggests that while providers hold a strong clinical understanding of optimal treatment pathways, this knowledge is not being translated effectively into patient-centered communication.

The lack of alignment between messages from general practitioners and eye care specialists further compounds this issue. Just 47.3% of patients reported receiving consistent information across their care team, highlighting a fragmented communication model that could contribute to confusion and treatment dropout.

Clinic staff, while well-positioned to reinforce provider messages, reported discussing critical topics—such as disease progression, treatment rationale, or emotional concerns—far less frequently. Less than half consistently addressed how diabetes control affects vision outcomes (48.5%) or what steps patients can take to reduce risk (also 48.5%). These omissions are notable, especially considering the growing body of evidence supporting team-based education models in chronic care, where coordinated messaging can enhance treatment comprehension and shared decision-making [30, 31].

In summary, while stakeholder alignment exists around the value of adherence and the efficacy of anti-VEGF treatment, major differences remain in the depth, consistency, and delivery of information. To bridge these gaps, integrated communication strategies—including standardized educational materials and cross-role training—should be prioritized to ensure all patients receive timely, accurate, and unified guidance across the care continuum.

As in any other disease, information awareness of their condition and treatment can be a critical determinant of adherence, satisfaction, and outcomes in chronic conditions such as DR and DME. The findings from this study reveal both encouraging patterns and notable gaps in information awareness among patients during the early stages of care, consistent with previous research emphasizing the importance of tailored education in retinal disease management [19].

Across both patient populations, there was encouraging alignment in the comprehension of core disease concepts. The majority of patients with DME and DR felt informed about the risk of vision loss (77.4% and 77.3%, respectively), the chronic nature of their condition, and the need for long-term, regular treatment. These results suggest that foundational education provided at the point of diagnosis or treatment initiation is generally effective, likely reflecting standard communication practices by ophthalmologists during initial consultations.

However, the data also underscore important differences in the depth and breadth of patients’ informational awareness. Individuals with DR reported consistently lower levels of understanding across a range of supportive and long-term planning topics. For instance, fewer than half (40.5%) felt adequately informed about where to access further resources or support services, and only 56.5% understood what to expect after completing their first year of treatment. Such gaps are concerning, as they may contribute to reduced engagement with follow-up care and long-term disease management.

Meanwhile, patients with DME, although slightly better informed overall, expressed a significant unmet need for information. Notably, 42.5% explicitly stated they did not feel informed about where to find support services, highlighting a critical opportunity for intervention through structured education and resource navigation tools.

These findings also point to an underutilization of the broader care team in patient education. While physicians may effectively convey clinical information, topics related to psychosocial support, caregiver involvement, and long-term treatment planning often fall outside the scope of brief consultations. Integrating structured educational interventions through clinic staff or patient navigators may help bridge these gaps.

In conclusion, while baseline disease understanding appears robust among patients with DR and DME, consistent with similar studies, critical knowledge gaps remain in areas that influence long-term care engagement. Addressing these through coordinated, accessible, and patient-tailored information delivery should be a priority in DR/DME care models.

Although efforts were made to mitigate both selection and information bias, these sources of bias cannot be entirely ruled out. Consequently, the results of this study should be interpreted with these limitations in mind. As with many survey-based studies, there is a potential for selection bias [32], since participants were not randomly selected and may not fully represent the broader populations of clinics, patients with DME and DR, providers, or staff. Moreover, participating clinics were selected from high-volume referral ophthalmology centers participating in the DR Barometer initiative and therefore may not fully represent all healthcare settings in Mexico, particularly rural or low-resource environments without specialized retinal services. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive of the surveyed population and may not be fully generalizable to the broader Mexican population with DR/DME. Recall bias is another possible concern, particularly for patients diagnosed in the more distant past who may have difficulty accurately recalling their initial experiences. Nonetheless, recall-related questions were limited. Another limitation is that, although corticosteroid-based intravitreal therapies are part of current DME management in Mexico, the survey primarily explored experiences related to anti-VEGF treatment pathways. Consequently, perspectives specifically associated with steroid-based therapies may be underrepresented in the present analysis.

Conclusions

This study highlights the complex and multifaceted challenges faced by individuals living with DR and DME in participating Mexican referral centers, as well as the critical role of healthcare providers and clinic staff in shaping adherence and care experiences. While there is strong alignment across stakeholders on the importance of treatment adherence and communication, gaps remain in emotional support, logistical facilitation, and consistent patient education. Addressing these gaps through integrated care models, enhanced educational tools, and targeted support strategies can improve patient engagement and long-term outcomes. Future efforts should focus on implementing and evaluating stakeholder-informed interventions to ensure care is both clinically effective and truly patient-centered.
