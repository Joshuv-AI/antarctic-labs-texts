Structurally engineered Co-CeO2 dispersed on N-lemon biochar for efficient energy storage and water splitting
Authors: Sohail Ahmad, Mohamed Rahmtalla Elamin, Javeria Zahid, Syed Imran Abbas Shah, Imran Shakir, Sijie Zhang, Jameel Ahmed Baig, Nadeem Raza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Herein, we report Co-doped CeO2 anchored on N-doped lemon-peel biochar (Co-CeO2/N-LB) as a multifunctional platform for simultaneous energy conversion and storage. The synergistic interaction between Ce3+/Ce4+ and Co2+ redox centers, abundant oxygen vacancies, and stable M–O–N covalency significantly enhances charge transfer, hydrophilicity, and structural stability. Consequently, the catalyst exhibits lower overpotentials of 289 mV (OER) and 64.51 mV (HER), smaller Tafel slopes, and high TOF (0.552 and 3.97 s−1). Mechanistically, Ce–O–Co chains stabilize Co-3d electronic states, enabling parallel *H adsorption during HER and spin-assisted oxygen intermediate coupling during OER. Beyond electrocatalysis, Co-CeO2/N-LB delivers rapid energy-storage performance, achieving higher specific capacitance (1664.16 F g−1), energy density (36.98 Wh kg−1) and (0.166 kW kg−1) power density. Charge storage kinetics are governed by a near-equal diffusive (49%) and capacitive (51%) contribution, ensuring fast response and excellent durability over 9500 cycles. Furthermore, when supported on carbon paper, Co-CeO2/N-LB demonstrates lower overpotentials (359.63 and 244.43 mV) and stable performance over 10 000 cycles along-with high specific capacitance (1650 F g−1), while post-stability analyses verify retention of the Co-CeO2 and N-LB planes in Co-CeO2/N-LB, highlighting its promise as a dual-functional electrode for integrated electrocatalysis and rapid energy storage.

Introduction

Surging global energy consumption, propelled by population growth and heavy dependence on depleting fossil fuels, has intensified CO2 emissions, highlighting the imperative demand for renewable energy substitutes.1 Hydrogen (H2) is a propitious clean energy carrier owing to its exorbitant gravimetric energy density (142 MJ kg−1) as well as clean combustion (H2O sole product),2 but it is not freely available and is largely produced from fossil fuels (∼96%), emitting ∼5 tons of CO2 per ton H2 under high-temperature (up to ∼1023 K) and pressure conditions.3,4 Water electrolysis provides a scalable, carbon-neutral route for H2 production via HER/OER,5 with alkaline electrolyzers preferred for their simplicity, low cost, and scalability. Utilizing alkaline saltwater electrolytes offers a cost-effective solution amid freshwater scarcity. However, performance is constrained by stagnant anode kinetics, larger overpotentials, and cathode durability issues, highlighting the need for streamlined and robust electrocatalysts.6–8 While noble-metal oxides (RuO2, IrO2) remain activity benchmarks, their cost and insufficiency obstruct widespread implementation.9–13 Addressing these challenges requires efficient, durable electrocatalysts capable of sustaining both half-reactions under harsh conditions. Simultaneously, rapid energy storage remains a bottleneck, where hybrid devices such as supercapacitors can complement conversion technologies and meet transient power demands.14–16

A practical approach to meet growing global energy demand is the construction of stable, affordable dual-functional materials that simultaneously act as high-performance electrode materials for both energy conversion and energy storage, enabling integrated energy conversion and rapid charge–discharge capabilities. In alkaline media, Co is attractive for its low cost, strong intrinsic activity, and highly reversible redox behavior enabled by N3− coordination, while Ce introduces fast Ce3+/Ce4+ transitions and 4f-state participation, enhancing charge transport, oxygen binding capacity, and active-site density, resulting in improved conductivity and enhanced energy conversion and storage.17,18 In Co-CeO2, mixed spin-up/spin-down Co states provide active *H adsorption sites, while Ce promotes spin-parallel coupling at Co, strengthening M–H bonds and enhancing HER kinetics. During OER, electron donation from M–O bonds, coupled with modulation by non-bonding oxygen (O-NB), optimizes M–O covalency and stabilizes reaction intermediates. Rare-earth (RE) elements in RE–O–TM networks utilize their adjustable 4f electron levels and substantial spin-orbital interaction to precisely fine-tune the material's electronic properties. Electron withdrawal from higher-lying 4f states, together with Ce 4f–O 2p–Co 3d hybridization, stabilizes Co against overoxidation and enables simultaneous spin-parallel (surface) and spin-antiparallel (subsurface) interactions, facilitating both HER and OER. In this context, Ce 4f manifolds act as electronic buffers, dynamically modulating *H adsorption and OER intermediates to enhance bifunctional catalytic performance.18–20 Performance is further amplified by defect engineering, where oxygen vacancies and strong electronic interaction by vacancy–dopant (Co) clusters increase ionic conductivity, Co-induced lattice disorder and tensile strain, enhance oxygen mobility, and lower oxygen migration enthalpy and energy barrier collectively boosting electrochemical activity.21,22 Nitrogen doping in biochar complements these effects by increasing charge density, tuning the electronic structure, and exposing abundant active sites: graphitic N (N-Q) preserves sp2 conduction pathways, while pyridinic (N-6) and pyrrolic (N-5) sites improve surface reactivity, hydrophilicity, and structural stability. These N moieties (N-5, N-6, N-Q, N-oxide) exhibit strong basicity and superior H2 adsorption, accelerating electron transfer and limiting active-species accumulation.23,24 This strategy aligns with literature benchmarks, such as CeO2-NiCoPx on NCF, showing mere overpotential values (39 and 205 mV for HER and 260 and 455 mV for OER) at j10 and j500 along-with reduced Tafel slopes (67 mV dec−1 for HER and 72 for OER).25 Sajjad S. Mofarah et al. fabricated layered ultrathin CeO2−x containing ∼60 at% Ce3+ redox centers, giving pronounced pseudocapacitive response having ultra-high volumetric capacitance (1873 F cm−3), attributed to oxygen vacancies resulting in improved electronic conductivity.26 Yong Zhao et al. emphasized on nitrogen-doped carbon based electrode materials as an efficient OER electrocatalyst giving 10 mA cm−2 at lower overpotential (0.38 V) in alkaline solution, attributed to pyridinic-nitrogen and quaternary-nitrogen containing active regions.27 Kesong Tian et al. reported single pyrrolic N-doped carbon materials (SPNCMs) giving pronounced pseudocapacitive performance, due to enriched pyrrolic nitrogen content, resulting in 618 F g−1 at 1 A g−1 and ultra-durable cyclic performance (467 F g−1 initial Csp at 10 A g−1), retaining 100% capacitance over 30 000 cycles via three-electrode configuration in 1 M H2SO4 (ref. 28). Together, these examples demonstrate that rationally engineered dual-functional catalysts can simultaneously address sluggish anode kinetics, high overpotentials, and durability challenges, highlighting a viable pathway for integrated water splitting and energy storage. Co-CeO2 is uniformly dispersed in the N-doped lemon-peel biochar (N-LB) leading to a dual-component/dual-atom structure, which prevents aggregation of nanoparticles, deactivation of the active sites in alkaline media, and dissolution. The design is rationally engineered to combine the electronically optimized active centers, defect-rich environments and N-doped conductive supports to offer a powerful base for water splitting and high-performance energy storage, while solving the long-standing issues of overpotential, durability and conductivity.

Herein, we report a defect-engineered bifunctional Co-CeO2/N-doped biochar (N-LB) heterostructure, designed as an integrated platform for electrocatalytic water splitting and energy storage. Uniformly dispersed Co-CeO2 nanoparticles anchored on conductive N-LB and supported on Ni foam were synthesized via a facile hydrothermal–pyrolysis route. Strong Ce–O–Co interfacial coupling suppresses Co overoxidation, while M–O–N covalency enhances charge redistribution, oxygen-vacancy formation, interfacial electronic interaction, and overall conductivity. The optimized electrode exhibits low overpotentials for both OER and HER, smaller Tafel slopes, higher TOF values, improved conductivity, and 84.53% stability. As a supercapacitor, it delivers a high specific capacitance, energy density, with balanced capacitive and diffusive contributions (51% and 49%). TEM/HRTEM confirms preservation of the (200), (111), (331) planes of Co-CeO2 and (002), (210) planes of N-LB, indicating structural robustness. When integrated on carbon paper, the catalyst maintains low overpotentials with post-stability analyses confirming retained crystallographic integrity. Overall, this work demonstrates that electronically coupled and vacancy-rich Co-CeO2/N-LB heterostructures offer a stable and multifunctional electrode system for efficient energy conversion and storage.

Experimental section

Chemicals and reagents

Cerium nitrate hexahydrate (Ce (NO3)2·6H2O, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%), cobalt nitrate hexahydrate (Co (NO3)·6H2O, Sigma-Aldrich, 98%), urea (H2NCONH2, Merck, 99%), trisodium phosphate (Na3(PO4), Merck, ≥98%), ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), nitrogen gas, lemon peel waste and deionized water (H2O, AK traders). All chemical reagents except lemon peel biomass are of analytical grade and used in their original condition, without employing further purification.

Fabrication of Co-CeO2

Co-CeO2 nanoparticles were fabricated using a facile hydrothermal method.19 Initially trisodium phosphate solution (0.02 moles) was dropwise added in cerium nitrate hexahydrate solution (0.1 moles in 60 ml) during continual stirring. Afterwards, the above reaction mixture was mixed with 2 moles % of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate solution, resulting in formation of white colored colloidal solution, under same reaction conditions for 30 minutes. Afterwards, the residual solution was hydrothermally treated at 180 °C via Teflon lined stainless-steel autoclave for at-least 15 h. After that the autoclave containing above solution was cooled down to ambient temperature and centrifugation was performed of the resultant colloidal solution using centrifugation and afterwards washed multiple times using the mixture of ultra-pure water and ethyl alcohol and afterwards drying was conducted at 80 °C for at-least 4 h. Then the annealing was conducted of resultant product at 500 °C for 4 h duration, developing Co-CeO2 nanoparticles. Afterwards, resultant Co-CeO2 nanoparticles were crushed via agate pastel and mortar in order to lessen particle size distribution, together with bringing freshly exposed active planes on surface.

Fabrication of N-LB

N-LB was fabricated via a facile pyrolysis method.29,30 Citrus lemon peel biomass was used as a raw material. In order to fabricate lemon peel biochar, citrus lemon peel waste was collected and washed thoroughly using water to remove debris and 80 °C to remove moisture from the biomass. Afterwards, the dried lemon peel biomass was grinded into fine powder using pastel and mortar to escalate the surface area and to facilitated the pyrolysis of biomass. Subsequently, the above biomass was pyrolyzed at 400 °C to 700 °C in the muffle furnace under inert nitrogen environment, resulting in the production of solid residual biochar of lemon peels. Then the lemon peel biochar was cooled at room temperature under inert atmosphere to prevent oxidation of sample. Afterwards, the 1 : 4 wight ratio of dried lemon peel biochar was mixed with H3PO4 (50 wt%) and 30% of urea (dry weight biomass : urea), and then kneaded for homogenous paste formation, and then drying was conducted at 80 °C in the drying oven overnight. Subsequently pyrolysis was conducted of impregnated sample in a specially designed reactor at 800 °C temperature condition under inert nitrogen environment for at least 1 h duration, at 10 °C min−1 ramping rate condition. The pyrolysis temperature of 800 °C was optimized to balance graphitization degree and nitrogen heteroatom retention. Lower temperatures (700 °C) produce insufficiently carbonized scaffolds with poor electrical conductivity, while higher temperatures (900 °C) accelerate N–C bond decomposition and reduce active N-site density, previously documented for urea-derived N-doped biochar systems.31 This selection was further validated by LSV measurements at 700, 800, and 900 °C under identical conditions (Fig. S9), confirming superior HER activity at 800 °C. Then the by-products and residual area removed from pyrolyzed carbon by washing via centrifugation using deionized water until PH become neutral, and then dried using drying oven at 75 °C for 24 h, subsequently grinded to attain N-LB nanoparticles.

Fabrication of Co-CeO2/N-LB nanocomposite

Co-CeO2/N-LB was fabricated by weighing and dissolving (1 : 1) amount of Co-CeO2 nanoparticles and N-LB in 50 ml ultra-pure water (Scheme 1).

Afterwards the above solution mixture was ultrasonically homogenized via sonicator for 1 h duration and afterwards drying was conducted at 80 °C for at-least 24 h. Subsequently, the aforementioned dried Co-CeO2/N-LB nanocomposite was manually ground via agate pastel and mortar in order to reduced nanoparticles size and increase active surface sites by brining freshly exposed active planes on surface.

Working electrode fabrication

Nickel foam substrate (1 × 1 cm) was utilized to create working electrode. Their unique porous architecture enhances electrolyte penetration, accelerates ion and electron transport, and ensures uniform dispersion, all while eliminating the need for binders. Firstly, NF pieces were rinsed with acetone and deionized water in order to eradicate any oxidized particles and then drying was conducted in drying oven at 80 °C respectively. Then 90 µl of deionized water and slurry of 0.005 g fabricated Co- CeO2/N-LB electrocatalyst was prepared. Afterwards, the afore-mentioned slurry was deposited on the NF and drying was conducted in the drying oven at 80 °C. All electrochemical analysis were carried out in a 13.6 pH alkaline (1 M) KOH electrolyte solution, providing an appropriate condition to analyze electrocatalytic activity of Co-CeO2/N-LB nanocomposite, ensuring reliability of electrochemical testing for practical energy conversion and energy storage application.

Physical characterization

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) technique was utilized to examine the surface valence state as well as chemical configuration, using Thermo Fisher, ESCALAB250Xi having Al Ka radiation (1253.6 eV). Deconvolution of XPS spectrum were performed via origin software. The unpaired electrons present in nanomaterial were analyzed using electron paramagnetic resonance analysis (EPR). Xray diffraction (XRD) analysis were performed to analyze phase purity, crystallographic structure, crystalline nature, using advance Bruker D8 X-ray diffractometer, fitted with a Ni-filter containing Cu-Kα radiations (λ = 1.541 Å), having 2θ values ranging from 24–90°. The comprehensive microstructural features of fabricated sample were analyzed utilizing transmission electron microscopy analysis (TEM, JEM-2100F). Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) measurements was utilized for examining the surface and elemental composition of nanomaterials, using Nova Nano SEM, 450 containing EDX detector.

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical measurements were conducted using AUTOLAB potentiostat (PGSTAT-204). Electrochemical evaluation encompasses cyclic voltammetry analysis (CV), linear square voltammetry (LSV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), potentiostatic i–t and electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) evaluation. Before electrochemical analysis, electrolytic cell was thoroughly cleaned with 3 : 1 mixed (3 : 1) sulfuric and nitric acid, and afterwards rinsed using ultra-high purity water. Subsequently, cleaned with ultrapure water and acetone mixture and dried at 80 °C for half an hour to eliminate remining acetone. In electrolytic cell, cleaning of Pt wire (counter electrode) was conducted using ultra-high pure water and then soaked in 20% nitric acid solution. Although graphite rod is recommended as counter electrode to avoid trace Pt dissolution in non-noble metal catalyst evaluation.32,33 Under the alkaline conditions (1 M KOH) employed in this study substantially suppress Pt corrosion, minimizing any potential contamination effect. Electrochemical analysis was carried out using Ag/AgCl as a reference electrode, while Co-CeO2, N-LB, Co-CeO2/N-LB modified nickel foam (NF) was employed as a working electrode. Electrocatalytic overall water splitting activity of Co-CeO2, N-LB and Co-CeO2/N-LB was examined using three-electrode configuration, via cyclic voltammetry analysis (CV) and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), conducted at 10 mV s−1 in 1.0 M alkaline KOH solution. Various parameters were calculated for electrochemical examination of Co-CeO2, LB and Co-CeO2/N-LB were given in SI section S1.

Results and discussion

XPS survey spectrum for Co-CeO2/N-LB reveals peaks corresponding to Ce 3d, Co 2p, O 1s, N 1s, C 1s (Fig. S1A), while XPS analysis of Ce 3d (Fig. 1A) reveals peaks corresponding to Ce3+ (3d5/2 ∼886.4 eV) and Ce4+ (3d3/2 ∼906.6, 900.7 and 916.3 eV), along with characteristic Ce4+ 3d5/2 multiplets (882.3 and 898.0 eV) indicating the coexistence of Ce3+/Ce4+.34,35 The Co 2p spectra (Fig. 1B) display peaks corresponding to Co–N, Co2+, Co–O, and a shake-up satellite (780.35 and 796.2, 781.3, 782.4, 802.4 eV), which is favorable for OER and HER by Co-CeO2/N-LB (Ce3+/Ce4+ redox centers) under alkaline conditions.36–38 High-resolution XPS spectrum of N 1s, C 1s, O 1s (Fig. S1B–D) confirms nitrogen functionalities in N-LB and oxygenated carbon, supporting M–O–N covalency across hybrid interface. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Fig. 1C) further confirms the defect-rich, mixed-valent nature of the catalyst. A pronounced signal at g ≈ 0.782 (865.48 mT) corresponds to oxygen vacancies, while additional signals are consistent with paramagnetic Ce3+ (4f) and Co2+ centers. These observations indicates oxygen vacancy richness and mixed-valence sites, facilitating oxygen-ion transport, electronic conductivity, and enhanced electrocatalytic activity within Co-CeO2/N-LB. Notably, (111) peak at 2θ ∼28.65° exhibits a 0.05° shift to lower angles in Co-CeO2/N-LB (28.60°), signaling lattice expansion driven by mixed cation incorporation and interfacial strain rCe ∼1.82 Å vs. rCo ∼1.26 Å (Fig. 1D). Together with Ce3+/Ce4+ redox buffer, these defects underpin gradient coupling between Ce 4f–O 2p–Co 3d states and stabilize reactive intermediates, with XRD confirming structural integrity through coexistence of cubic Co-CeO2 and hexagonal N-LB phases (Fig. S2A), while Williamson hall plot (Fig. S2B, E and H), size strain plot (Fig. S2C, F and I), Sherrer plot (Fig. S2D, G and J), Rietveld refinement gives detailed XRD analysis of Co-CeO2, N-LB and Co-CeO2/N-LB (Fig. S2K–M). The microstructural basis for these electronic effects, further evident from TEM micrographs, revealing dense, percolated, spherical/spheroidal Co-CeO2 grains embedded within brighter porous N-LB matrix (Fig. 1E). HRTEM at higher magnification (Fig. 1F) confirms uniform dispersion of Co-CeO2 nanoparticles throughout carbon network. Lattice fringes with d ≈ 0.35 nm match (111) plane of Co-CeO2, additional spacing of 0.2 nm, 0.277 nm and 0.398 nm is consistent with (200) and (331) reflections for Co-CeO2 and features from porous N-LB, respectively, fully aligned with XRD assignment of composite. Fast Fourier transform (FFT) and inverse FFT analysis shows that CeO2 host crystallinity is preserved after Co incorporation, while particular contact with N-LB maintains M–O–N covalency across interface, described in the Fig. 1I–IV and I′–IV′. The even spatial distribution of described sites throughout surface analyzed by EDS, ensuring the availability of Co, Ce, C, O and N atoms in the nanocomposite (Fig. 1G–L). This strain and high energy grain boundaries (arising from electronegativity/radii mismatch) shift d-band center, lower adsorption barriers, and create additional active regions for reactant binding and intermediate stabilization, benefitting both OER and HER kinetics and electrochemical double-layer capacitance.

To interrogate how gradient orbital interaction by Ce 4f-O 2p-Co 3d and M-O-N covalency govern bifunctional activity, we evaluated Co-CeO2, N-LB, and Co-CeO2/N-LB in a three-electrode configuration. 1 M KOH was used for examining the OER performance of Co-CeO2/N-LB, via cyclic voltammetry (CV), at scan rate of 10 mV s−1 respectively. Fig. 2A clearly indicates that Co-CeO2/N-LB acquires larger current densities and improved energy conversion in both oxidative and reductive phenomena, as compared to pure Co-CeO2 and N-LB, which may be due to synergetic properties of both Co-CeO2 and N-LB in the Co-CeO2/N-LB nanocomposite. The surface area of Co-CeO2/N-LB was altered by the addition of Co-CeO2 into the N-LB, escalating permeability and enhancing the catalytic active binding sites, due to the combined interaction of Co-CeO2 with N-LB, enabling fast adsorption and desorption of numerous chemical intermediate species. Fig. 2A clearly shows as the potential escalates from 0 V to 2 V, current density enhances from 204.75 mA cm−2 to 401.55 mA cm−2. Fig. 2A represents that Co-CeO2, N-LB and Co-CeO2/N-LB achieved the maximum current density (10 mA cm−2) at the lower overpotential (330 mV, 320 mV and 289 mV) respectively. Fig. 2A clearly indicates that overpotential decrease for Co-CeO2/N-LB nanocomposite as compared to pure Co-CeO2 and N-LB. Fig. 2A illustrates that the CV polarization curves of Co-CeO2/N-LB have sharp intense peaks, validating the OER performance, indicative of escalated electrocatalytic behavior by both cerium and cobalt moieties in Co-CeO2/N-LB nanocomposite, attributed to oxidation of Ce3+ and Co2+ into Ce4+ and Co3+, resulting in oxyhydroxides surface active species generation, a crucial transient in KOH containing electrocatalytic overall water splitting. Then deprotonation and peroxide formation illustrates initiation of rate-determining stage in OER performance, although at sluggish rate. Additionally, the OER activity of Co-CeO2/N-LB was evaluated in 1 M alkaline KOH electrolyte, via linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), at sweep rate of 10 mV s−1. The Co-CeO2/N-LB delivers enhanced LSV-OER response, clearly visible in magnified LSV-OER inset plot, showcasing larger current density (388 mA cm−2) requiring lower onset potential of 1.53 V, in comparison to Co-CeO2 (311.07 mA cm−2, onset 1.54 V) and N-LB (206.81 mA cm−2; onset 1.55 V) in series (Fig. 2B). In LSV HER, described in Fig. 2C, Co-CeO2/N-LB exhibits a markedly lower overpotential of 64.51 mV at 10 mA cm−2 than either Co-CeO2 (282.04 mV) or N-LB (298.99 mV), and maintains maximum cathodic current (−749.45 mA cm−2) than Co-CeO2 (−645.18 mA cm−2) and N-LB (−569.99 mA cm−2), validating escalated hydrogen-evolution kinetics. In LSV HER (Fig. 2C), Co-CeO2/N-LB sustains largest cathodic current (−749.45 mA cm−2) versus Co-CeO2 and N-LB, obvious through magnified LSV-HER inset plot, confirming accelerated hydrogen-evolution kinetics. Co-CeO2/N-LB gives better OER efficiency, represented by sharp, intense peaks, reflecting rapid formation of Ce3+/Co2+ → Ce4+/Co3+ oxyhydroxides, which generate surface-active species crucial for overall water splitting in alkaline media, as well as enhanced HER activity, requiring lowest overpotential (64.51 mV at 10 mA cm−2) and highest TOF (3.97 s−1). The initial hump corresponding to metals transitions as Ce 3+/4+ and Co2+/3+, via high resolution XPS Ce 3d and Co 2p analysis. The OER on Co-CeO2/N-LB in alkaline medium proceeds via sequential OH− adsorption and surface oxidation at catalytically active Co and Ce sites, forming Co–OH/Ce–OH and Co–O/Ce–O intermediates. The subsequent conversion of M–O to M–OOH constitutes the rate-determining step, involving O–O bond formation and governed by surface OH− adsorption, indicative of an adsorption-controlled OER mechanism. The Co2+ component (781.3 eV) reflects the formation of in situ Co–Ce oxyhydroxide (CoCeOOH) under anodic polarization, a known OER-active phase emerging at the Co–Ce interface. Instrumental Tafel polarization analysis (Fig. 2D) confirms both the kinetic and durability advantages of the Co-CeO2/N-LB hybrid. The composite exhibits significantly reduced cathodic (31.19 mV dec−1), and anodic Tafel slopes (53.14 mV dec−1), compared with Co-CeO2 and N-LB, indicating faster electron-transfer kinetics with controlled oxidation behavior. In parallel, Co-CeO2/N-LB shows a markedly lower corrosion current density (9.1724 × 10−5 µA cm−2), reduced corrosion rate (1.076 mm a−1), and a more stable corrosion potential (−0.279), outperforming Co-CeO2 (Fig. S3A) and N-LB (Fig. S3B), demonstrating superior corrosion resistance and open-circuit stability. These results highlight the role of the N-doped carbon matrix and Co–Ce interfacial coupling in suppressing degradation under electrochemical bias. The modest slope indicates a rate-determining M–O bond formation step at Ce–O–Co interfacial sites, enabled by Ce3+/Ce4+ redox buffering, Ce 4f–O 2p–Co 3d gradient coupling, and M–O–N covalency, indicating faster surface kinetics with more favorable adsorption–desorption equilibria.20,39 All of these kinetic and corrosion tests confirm Co-CeO2/N-LB as a defect-tolerant, electronically percolated and durable dual function catalyst for high rate energy conversion (OER metrics summarized in Table 1). Co-CeO2/N-LB has a smaller Tafel slope value when considering the HER, suggesting that the overall kinetics shift to a more favorable Volmer–Heyrovsky mechanism featuring rapid proton adsorption and effective electrochemical desorption (Fig. 2D). The enhanced kinetics is confirmed with both the OER overpotential-TOF map (Fig. 2E, 289 mV, 0.55 s−1) and the HER overpotential-TOF map (Fig. 2F, 64.51 mV, 3.97 s−1), the latter demonstrates that the performance boost is not merely due to higher conductivity or electrochemical surface area but more to energetics favoring HER at the active Co–Ce sites. To compare the catalytic activities of Co-CeO2/N-LB with commercial RuO2 and Pt/C under alkaline conditions, CV and LSV curves were plotted (Fig. S8), and a comparison with recently reported bifunctional electrocatalysts is summarized in Table 2, which demonstrates competitive activity of Co-CeO2/N-LB with state of the art. EIS measurements, where OER-EIS (Fig. 2G and H) shows reduced Rct (1753.9 kΩ, σ = 0.89 S m−1) and cathodic EIS (Fig. 2H) reveals dramatically lower Rct (9.97 Ω, σ = 0.15 S m−1), reflecting accelerated interfacial electron transfer via percolated N-doped carbon pathways, M–O–N covalency, and defect-assisted hopping from mixed-valent Co/Ce centers.35 (HER parameters summarized in Table 1). Mechanistically, Ce 4f-mediated electronic buffering within the Co–O–Ce framework optimizes H adsorption (ΔGH ≈ 0) and aligns bonding/antibonding states*, while M–O–N covalency ensures rapid electron delivery and interface stability.37 These synergistic effects enable high intrinsic activity (TOF = 3.97 s−1, mass activity = 2000 A g−1) and durable HER performance.

Comparative electrocatalytic water splitting activity investigation with various electrocatalysts

The electrochemical behavior of Co-CeO2/N-LB electrode was investigated in a three-electrode configuration. Cyclic voltammetry (−0.10–0.60 V vs. Ag/AgCl, 5–60 mV s−1) analysis of Co-CeO2, N-LB (Fig. S3A and B) and Co-CeO2/N-LB (Fig. 3A), reveals quasi-reversible redox features, characteristic of pseudocapacitive charge storage dominated by surface and near-surface faradaic processes. Conclusion

In summary, a Co-CeO2/N-LB composite electrode integrated on porous Ni foam was successfully fabricated via a combined hydrothermal–pyrolysis strategy, demonstrating its effectiveness as a robust bifunctional platform for energy conversion and rapid energy storage. The Ce3+/Ce4+–Co2+ regulated electronic structure enables spin-assisted *H adsorption during HER, while Ce-induced modulation of Co–O covalency and in situ CeCoOOH formation significantly accelerate OER kinetics. Furthermore, strong M–O–N electronic interactions, abundant oxygen vacancies, and a conductive N-doped biochar framework improve charge transport, surface hydrophilicity, and long-lasting structural durability. Thus, the electrode delivers reduced overpotentials (289 mV for OER activity and 64.51 mV for HER), a reduced onset potential (1.537 V), smaller Tafel slopes, high TOF (0.552 and 3.97 s−1), large mass activity (2000 A g−1), and only 4.53% current decay, confirming excellent electrochemical durability. Co-CeO2/N-LB exhibits significantly higher specific capacitance (1664.16 F g−1), energy density (36.98 Wh kg−1), power density (0.166 kW kg−1) and areal capacitance (16.64), and stable operation for 9500 cycles, with diffusion- (49%) and surface-controlled (51%) charge storage, which are well balanced. The overall results presented here indicate that the Ce–Co redox coupled biochar electrode can not only facilitate efficient water splitting but also possess a high-rate energy storage capability, which provides an inspiring plan for the design of a sustainable, low-cost, multifunctional electrode material for future energy technologies.
