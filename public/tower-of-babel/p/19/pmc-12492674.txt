Neovascular glaucoma: comprehensive evaluation of etiology, treatment modalities, and visual prognosis
Authors: Sıdıka Gerçeker Demircan, Aysun Şanal Doğan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to determine the demographic characteristics of patients with neovascular glaucoma (NVG), identify the factors involved in its etiology, and evaluate the effects of various treatment methods on intraocular pressure (IOP).MethodsThis study included 29 eyes of 24 patients diagnosed with neovascular glaucoma who were treated and followed at a single tertiary eye care center. Patient demographics, medical history, and treatment protocols were recorded, including anti-VEGF injections, panretinal photocoagulation (PRP), and surgical interventions. IOP measurements and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) were monitored throughout the follow-up period.ResultsOf the patients included in the study, 6 were women and 18 were men, with an average follow-up period of 44.2 ± 34.8 months. The mean age of patients was 67.9 ± 11.4 years, with NVG being caused by diabetic retinopathy (11 eyes), central retinal vein occlusion (6 eyes), ocular ischemic syndrome (6 eyes), and other factors. Pre-treatment IOP was 36.7 ± 7.9 mmHg, which decreased to 18.03 ± 3.19 mmHg post-treatment. Surgical interventions were required in 9 eyes, and significant postoperative IOP reduction was observed. (p˂0.05) Bevacizumab was the most commonly used anti-VEGF agent, with visual acuity outcomes varying among patients, though no statistically significant improvement in BCVA was noted.ConclusionNVG is a severe condition that requires prompt treatment to prevent permanent vision loss. Introduction

Neovascular glaucoma (NVG) is a severe form of secondary glaucoma that arises due to neovascularization in the anterior segment, resulting in synechial angle closure and increased intraocular pressure (IOP). Iris neovascularization was initially documented by Bader in 1868 [1]; however, it was not until 1963 that Weiss et al. [2] provided a detailed description of NVG. Subsequent studies revealed that NVG results from retinal ischemia [3]. NVG is most frequently a secondary complication of proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) or ocular ischemic syndrome (OIS) [3]. These conditions cause severe retinal hypoxia and non-perfusion.

There is an increase in IOP due to leakage from newly formed immature vessels and angle closure created by peripheral anterior synechiae and intertrabecular neovascular tissue [4, 5]. In NVG, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) released from the ischemic retina has been shown to be 100 times higher in the aqueous humor [6]. Therefore, current research focuses on anti-VEGF agents and their effects. Although intravitreal anti-VEGF is known to cause regression of iris neovascularization, its role in penetrating glaucoma filtration surgery has not yet been fully elucidated [7, 8].

Neovascular glaucoma has poor visual outcomes despite medical and surgical treatment and may sometimes require evisceration or enucleation to alleviate pain in a blind eye. If inadequately managed, up to 50% of NVG patients may completely lose light perception [9]. NVG accounts for approximately 3.9–4.3% of all glaucoma cases [10, 11] and 14% of secondary glaucoma cases [10]. The treatment of NVG is based on two primary principles: suppressing neovascularization and regulating IOP [12].

The aim of this study is to determine the demographic characteristics of patients with an NVG diagnosis who are being followed in our clinic, identify the factors involved in its etiology, and investigate the effects of the treatment methods applied in our clinic on IOP.

Methods

In this study, 29 eyes of 24 patients followed in our clinic’s glaucoma unit between 2013 and 2023 were retrospectively evaluated. Cases with a follow-up period of less than six months and those with irregular follow-up visits were excluded from the study. Local ethics committee approval was obtained for the study.

Patients’ demographic characteristics, mean follow-up durations, biomicroscopic and gonioscopic examination findings at initial presentation and during follow-ups, angiographic examination findings, IOP measurements obtained with applanation tonometry, and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) values were recorded from patient files. BCVA data were retrospectively collected and converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) values for statistical analysis. Eyes with BCVA recorded in numerical form both at presentation and after treatment were included in the analysis. Cases with only qualitative visual acuity such as positive or negative light perception (P+/P−) were excluded from logMAR analysis, in accordance with previous recommendations [13].

Additionally, the etiology of the disease, preoperative anti-VEGF injections, panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) treatments, the number of IOP lowering medications used, and the surgical procedures performed were recorded. A standardized protocol was followed for the administration of anti-VEGF and PRP treatments. Anti-VEGF therapy consisted of intravitreal injections of either bevacizumab (1.25 mg/0.05 mL) or ranibizumab (0.5 mg/0.05 mL), administered in the operating room under topical anesthesia. The choice of agent was based on availability and clinical assessment. Injections were typically performed at the time of NVG diagnosis or when active iris or angle neovascularization was detected on slit-lamp or gonioscopic examination. The median number of injections was 3, ranging from 1 to 5.

PRP treatment was administered to all patients with clear ocular media who were managed with topical antiglaucoma, anti-inflammatory and/or steroid, and cycloplegic therapy. PRP was typically performed within 1–2 weeks following diagnosis, using a 532-nm wavelength laser in 2–3 sessions, delivering 1200–1600 laser burns across the peripheral retina. Treatment protocols were applied consistently across patients, with minor adjustments based on clinical condition.

During follow-up, eyes that developed angle and/or iris neovascularization and had an IOP exceeding 21 mmHg were classified as NVG.

Cyclodestructive treatment was performed on cases in which IOP could not be regulated with medical therapy and those with no light perception. Mitomycin-C-assisted trabeculectomy or shunt surgery was performed on cases in which IOP could not be regulated despite dual, triple, or quadruple antiglaucoma therapy or anti-VEGF treatment. For patients who underwent surgery, BCVA and IOP measurements were recorded preoperatively and postoperatively. Throughout the follow-up period, the development of complications, the recurrence rate of iris neovascularization, and the need for additional surgical or medical treatment were determined.

Statistical analysis

A multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted to investigate potential predictors of change in BCVA. The dependent variable was defined as the change in BCVA, calculated by subtracting the pre-treatment logMAR value from the post-treatment logMAR value. Independent variables included age, gender (male/female), NVG etiology (categorical), baseline IOP, number of antiglaucoma medications at presentation, and whether the patient underwent glaucoma surgery (yes/no).

Numerical variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as count and percentage. The normality of numerical variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The IOP of the two procedures was compared using an independent samples t-test. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, Fisher’s Exact Test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test where appropriate. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 29 eyes of 24 patients were included in the study. Of the 24 patients, 6 (25%) were female and 18 (75%) were male. The patients, with an average age of 67.9 ± 11.4 years (range 43–92), were followed up for an average of 44.2 ± 34.8 months (range 8–120). Ten patients are under 65 years old, while fourteen patients are over 65 years old. NVG was observed to be caused by diabetic retinopathy in 11 eyes, central retinal vein occlusion (CRVO) in 6 eyes, ocular ischemic syndrome in 6 eyes, trauma in 3 eyes, branch retinal vein occlusion (BRVO) in 2 eyes, and chronic retinal detachment in 1 eye (Table 1). The reduction in mean IOP across all procedures was statistically significant (95% CI: 15.46 to 21.88; p = 0.0001). Two antiglaucoma agents were used in 7 eyes (24.1%), three agents in 10 eyes (34.4%), and four agents in 11 eyes (37.9%). A case with NVG following trauma, who had a baseline IOP of 54 mmHg, underwent cyclocryotherapy without prior medical treatment. Intraocular Pressure (IOP) Reduction by Type of Surgical Intervention

Of the 12 eyes that underwent intravitreal bevacizumab injection, a decrease in final BCVA was observed in 2 eyes, an increase in 1 eye, and no change in 9 eyes. Of the 5 eyes that underwent intravitreal Ranibizumab injection, a decrease in final BCVA was observed in 1 eye, an increase in 1 eye, and no change in 3 eyes. Fourteen eyes did not receive any intravitreal injection; among them, final BCVA decreased in 4 eyes, increased in 1 eye, and remained unchanged in 8 eyes. One eye was excluded from the evaluation due to evisceration. Among the four eyes that underwent MMC trab, visual acuity improved in one eye, while it remained unchanged in another. After medical or surgical treatment, there was no change in BCVA in 18 eyes, while BCVA decreased in 8 eyes. Of the 3 eyes with an increase in visual acuity, 2 had diabetic retinopathy and 1 had NVG due to CRVO. Among the 29 eyes included in the study, 15 eyes had both pre- and post-treatment BCVA data available in numerical logMAR format. The mean pre-treatment BCVA was 1.64 ± 1.03 logMAR, while the mean post-treatment BCVA was 1.57 ± 0.97 logMAR. The BCVA values were not normally distributed (pre-treatment p = 0.0028; post-treatment p = 0.0067), prompting the use of the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. The change in BCVA was not statistically significant (p = 0.357).

In the multivariate model evaluating potential predictors of BCVA change, none of the variables were found to be statistically significant. Age (95% CI: − 0.031 to 0.021, p = 0.666), gender (95% CI: − 1.448 to 1.107, p = 0.762), etiology (95% CI: − 0.207 to 0.293, p = 0.697), baseline IOP (95% CI: − 0.050 to 0.052, p = 0.967), number of medications (95% CI: − 2.095 to 2.928, p = 0.706), and surgical intervention (95% CI: − 0.787 to 0.791, p = 0.995) were not significantly associated with changes in visual acuity. The overall model had an R² of 0.054, indicating poor explanatory power.

Discussion

Neovascular glaucoma is a severe sight-threatening condition that has a very poor long-term visual prognosis. The primary diseases causing neovascular glaucoma include retinal vascular diseases, with advanced diabetic retinopathy being the most common. CRVO and OIS follow diabetic retinopathy in this regard [11]. Although not all ischemic CRVO cases develop NVG, the highest risk period for NVG development is within the first 7–8 months, and the overall incidence over the years reaches a maximum of 45%. Approximately 20% of all CRVO cases are ischemic. Therefore, the likelihood of developing NVG in a patient with CRVO is at most 9–10%. In cases of hemicentral retinal vein occlusion, the probability of NVG occurrence is even lower, around 3% [14]. The etiology of NVG among this study is consistent with the three most common causes of NVG [11, 15]. This highlights that NVG may be preventable if underlying risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension are appropriately managed. Lin et al. [16] identified diabetic retinopathy (DR), retinal vein occlusion (RVO), and retinal detachment (RD) as the three most common causes of NVG. They attributed the identification of retinal detachment as the third most common cause of NVG instead of OIS to geographic and ethnic differences. They noted that the prevalence of underlying conditions associated with OIS, such as atherosclerosis, is significantly higher in Caucasian populations compared to cohorts in China and other Asian countries. In the study by Lin et al. [16], which had the largest NVG sample size, the male-to-female ratio was found to be 2.01:1 (66.8%/33.2%). In our study, the male-to-female ratio was found to be 3:1 (75%/25%). We believe that this situation may be related to the higher predisposition to the primary causes of NVG in men compared to women. In our study, consistent with the study by Lin et al. [16], the most common cause of NVG in patients over 65 years old is CRVO rather than DR.

The traditional treatment of neovascular glaucoma is two-step: first, treating the underlying disease, and second, lowering the IOP [12]. Timely administration of intravitreal VEGF inhibitors and application of PRP may improve outcomes for patients with NVG [17–25]. In our study, the preferred VEGF inhibitor for NVG treatment was bevacizumab in 12 cases and Ranibizumab in 5 cases, which aligns with the VEGF inhibitor usage reported by other centers [17–22]. In the review investigating the effectiveness of anti-VEGF in NVG, it was shown that anti-VEGF medications, as an adjunct to conventional treatment, may help reduce IOP in the short term (4–6 weeks) in NVG, but there is no evidence to suggest this is likely in the long term. The available evidence regarding the short- and long-term effectiveness and safety of anti-VEGF medications in achieving control of IOP, visual acuity, and complete regression of new iris vessels in NVG is insufficient [26]. In our study, intravitreal injection was observed not to improve the final BCVA but to prevent a decrease in visual acuity and preserve existing vision. When compared to eyes that did not receive intravitreal injection, this change was found to be not statistically significant. A recent Bayesian network meta-analysis by Wang et al. [27] compared the efficacy and safety of various anti-VEGF agents combined with different delivery routes in NVG. Their findings indicated that intravitreal conbercept, particularly when administered simultaneously via intravitreal and intracameral injection, achieved the greatest IOP reduction and ranked highest in safety compared with intravitreal ranibizumab and bevacizumab. In contrast, our cohort predominantly received intravitreal bevacizumab due to its cost-effectiveness and availability in our setting. While bevacizumab demonstrated short-term IOP reduction in our patients, consistent with previous reports, no statistically significant advantage in final visual outcomes was observed. Additionally, the unavailability of conbercept in our region and economic constraints likely influenced treatment selection, underscoring the need for future prospective studies to evaluate its potential benefits in diverse healthcare settings.

The management of intraocular pressure (IOP) often requires both topical and systemic treatment; however, in most cases, surgical intervention is inevitable. Main aqueous suppressants, such as topical beta-blockers, alpha-agonists, and carbonic anhydrase inhibitors, may be beneficial in reducing IOP [11]. Systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitors can also be effective for short-term temporary IOP reduction but should be used with caution in patients with impaired renal function [28]. Although the primary treatment for NVG patients is the reduction of retinal ischemia through PRP, surgical intervention is often required to control elevated IOP and prevent irreversible complications such as optic nerve damage. In our study, medical treatment was administered with 2 antiglaucoma agents in 7 eyes, 3 antiglaucoma agents in 10 eyes, and 4 antiglaucoma agents in 11 eyes. None of the eyes treated with 2 antiglaucoma agents required surgical intervention. Among the 10 eyes treated with 3 antiglaucoma agents, trabeculectomy with mitomycin-C was performed in 2 eyes, and cyclocryotherapy was applied in 1 eye. Among the 11 eyes treated with 4 antiglaucoma agents, trabeculectomy with mitomycin-C was performed in 2 eyes, cyclocryotherapy in 1 eye, Ahmed valve implantation in 1 eye, and evisceration in 1 eye. Surgical intervention was required in 9 out of a total of 29 eyes. In this study, the choice of surgical intervention was made by glaucoma surgeon and was based on factors such as visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and angle status. A significant reduction in IOP was observed postoperatively in all eyes that underwent surgical intervention. In the multicenter study conducted by Pegu et al. [30], similar to our findings, a statistically significant reduction in IOP was observed across all surgical groups in patients with NVG. In that study, BCVA improved significantly in the trabeculectomy and glaucoma drainage device implantation groups, whereas a significant decrease in BCVA was reported in the trans-scleral cyclophotocoagulation group. In contrast, our study did not demonstrate any statistically significant change in BCVA across all surgical groups. This discrepancy may be attributable to several factors. First, in our cohort, surgical indications were predominantly assigned to advanced-stage cases with limited visual potential, thereby reducing the likelihood of meaningful postoperative visual improvement. Second, while the multicenter study by Pegu et al. [30] included a larger sample size, our study was retrospective and involved a relatively small cohort, which may have limited statistical power and prevented small visual changes from reaching significance. Furthermore, the high proportion of missing BCVA data in logMAR format, along with the presence of eyes with only light perception or no light perception, restricted our ability to quantitatively assess visual change.

In this study, we conducted a multivariate regression analysis to evaluate factors that may influence changes in BCVA among patients with NVG. Although various clinical predictors, such as age, gender, NVG etiology, baseline IOP, number of medications, and surgical intervention, were included in the model, none were found to be significantly associated with changes in visual acuity. The low R² value and wide confidence intervals indicate limited explanatory power of the model, likely due to the small sample size. These findings highlight the need for larger scale studies to identify robust predictors of visual outcomes in NVG.

Our study provides highly detailed data on patient demographics, etiological distribution, treatment methods used, and visual outcomes. Various treatment options, including medical therapy, intravitreal injections, and surgical interventions, have been evaluated. Although the sample size in our study was limited, the effect size for IOP reduction after treatment was calculated as large (Cohen’s d = 2.63), indicating a substantial treatment effect. Nevertheless, the small cohort may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings, which should be considered when interpreting the results. However, to draw stronger conclusions about NVG management, a study with a larger patient cohort could be conducted, or the current data could be compared with larger studies in the literature. Future studies could investigate predictive factors to improve visual acuity outcomes.

Recent studies [31], including lipidomic analyses, have identified novel metabolic pathways potentially involved in NVG pathogenesis, suggesting therapeutic targets beyond the VEGF pathway. These advances broaden the understanding of NVG’s molecular mechanisms and introduce innovative treatment strategies that may complement or even surpass current anti-VEGF approaches. Furthermore, the refinement of surgical techniques such as micropulse and endoscopic cyclophotocoagulation supports the development of a more personalized, biomarker-driven approach to NVG management [31]. In contrast, our study was conducted in a resource-limited, single-center setting where such molecular profiling and advanced surgical modalities were not available, and management was largely based on conventional anti-VEGF therapy (predominantly bevacizumab) combined with PRP and standard surgical options. This difference in therapeutic landscape may explain the limited long term visual gains observed in our cohort. Future studies incorporating molecular diagnostics, broader anti-VEGF agent availability, and newer surgical technologies may allow earlier intervention, improved stratification of high risk patients, and tailored treatment regimens, potentially leading to better anatomical and functional outcomes than those achieved in our current series.

In conclusion, this study highlights the complex clinical profile and management challenges of NVG in a resource limited, single center setting. Our findings confirm the significant short term IOP lowering effect of combined medical, anti-VEGF, and surgical therapies, but also underscore the limited long term visual improvement achievable with current treatment modalities. The results emphasize the need for timely detection, individualized management strategies, and the integration of newer pharmacologic agents and surgical techniques into routine care. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and molecular profiling could help refine prognostic models and guide more targeted interventions.
