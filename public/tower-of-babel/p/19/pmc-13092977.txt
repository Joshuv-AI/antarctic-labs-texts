MicroRNA-Mediated Obstruction of Stem–loop Alternative Splicing (MIMOSAS) regulates long-range alternative splicing in Drosophila
Authors: Kai Ruan, Jiaqi Liu, Melanie Xia, Federica Bertolotti, Ivo Hofacker, Stefan Wuchty, Rong Grace Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA secondary structures are key regulators of long-range pre-messenger RNA (mRNA) alternative splicing, yet the rules governing these structures and their effects on splice site selection are not well understood. We uncover a direct role for microRNAs in controlling alternative splicing by remodeling splice-relevant stem–loop structures in pre-mRNAs, a mechanism we term MicroRNA-mediated Obstruction of Stem–loop Alternative Splicing (MIMOSAS). Using a curated set of Drosophila genes containing conserved complementary regions (“boxes”), we developed a bioinformatic pipeline that integrates genome-wide structural prediction with energetic modeling to identify microRNAs that disrupt long-range stem–loop structures associated with alternative donor/acceptor usage or putative polyadenylation sites. We experimentally validated these predictions in vivo in Drosophila and in mammalian cell lines incorporating several split fluorescent protein-based splicing reporters. Our data show that microRNAs bidirectionally modulate splice isoform ratios by engaging structured pre-mRNA regions in an AGO1-dependent process. MIMOSAS-active microRNAs often use noncanonical pairing and optimize local folding energies rather than strict seed matches, underscoring the importance of RNA secondary structure in functional specificity. These findings broaden microRNA function beyond canonical silencing or translation repression and reveal a structurally grounded, potentially widespread layer of alternative splicing regulation with implications for RNA-based therapies.

Introduction

Alternative splicing plays a fundamental role in cellular differentiation and stress responses under both physiological and pathological conditions. The vast majority of human genes undergo alternative splicing [1–3], and disruptions in this process are tightly linked to disease. Notably, 22% of disease-causing mutations are splicing sensitive [4], and ~25% of disease-causing exonic mutations induce exon skipping [5], underscoring alternative splicing as a major regulatory and pathogenic node. Beyond protein-coding sequences and core spliceosomal components, noncoding RNAs have emerged as important regulators of splicing. For example, a long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) promotes inclusion of a specific exon in FGFR2 [6], double-stranded RNAs can modulate splicing of genes implicated in muscular dystrophy, and a small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) regulates alternative splicing of the serotonin receptor 2C, associated with Prader–Willi syndrome [7]. Together, these findings highlight diverse RNA-based mechanisms that influence splice site choice and isoform expression.

MicroRNAs are short noncoding RNAs (~22 nt) best known for their roles in post-transcriptional gene silencing. They typically bind complementary sequences in the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of target messenger RNAs (mRNAs) via the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC) [8–10]. However, microRNA targeting rules are complex and flexible: microRNAs can also bind UTRs, coding sequences, and noncoding RNAs via both canonical and noncanonical base pairing [11]. A mapping study of Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus microRNA binding sites in B cell lymphoma-derived and epithelial cells revealed that most microRNA target sites reside within coding sequences rather than 3′ UTRs [12]. The prevalence of microRNA binding within exonic and intronic regions suggests that microRNAs may influence pre-mRNA processing, including splice-site selection and alternative splicing outcomes, in addition to their established roles in controlling mRNA stability and translation.

Although most microRNA-target interactions lead to decreased translation of the target [13], microRNAs have also been reported to upregulate translation [14–21], often through binding the 5′ UTR [16, 19, 20]. Given the ubiquity of microRNA–RNA interactions, we hypothesized that microRNA binding could disrupt splice-relevant RNA secondary structures, thereby revealing an additional mechanism of splicing and RNA structural regulation [22, 23]. We refer to this mechanism as MicroRNA-Mediated Obstruction of Stem–loop Alternative Splicing (MIMOSAS). Previously, by combining sequence composition and computational RNA structural modeling of Nmnat pre-mRNA [24], we scanned splice junctions for complementary sequences (“boxes”) and identified a stem–loop structure that sequesters intermediate introns [25]. Assuming that (i) the microRNA–mRNA duplex forms at the stem of a hairpin loop and (ii) this duplex is energetically more favorable than the stem–loop itself, we predicted that miR-1002 interferes with a splice-relevant stem–loop on Nmnat mRNA regulating the alternative splice variants RA and RB. We then experimentally confirmed that miR-1002 shifts splicing toward RB and away from RA [26].

While our initial work focused on a single microRNA–mRNA pair, our current results suggest that MIMOSAS may operate more broadly as a mechanism of splicing regulation. To explore this possibility, we developed a computational pipeline to predict microRNAs that disrupt splice-relevant secondary structures across the Drosophila genome and validated a subset of these predictions both in vivo and in cultured mammalian cell models, providing initial experimental support that this regulatory principle extends beyond the Nmnat locus.

Materials and methods

RNA secondary structure prediction and selection

Minimum free energy (MFE) secondary structures and base-pairing probabilities were calculated with the RNAfold program from the ViennaRNA package [24] for pre-mRNA regions spanning both boxes and nearby exon–intron boundaries to confirm that the boxes form a stem with high probability. In some instances, this analysis revealed additional box pairs. Secondary structures were visualized using the FORNA web server (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/forna/) [27]. The initial candidate set consisted of evolutionarily conserved complementary regions (“boxes”) identified in [28]. For each box pair within a gene of interest, we assessed whether the secondary structure formed by their pairing could affect isoform selection. To compute the hybridization energy and base-pairing pattern of interacting RNA molecules, we used the duplexfold function from RNAstructure [29] and the RNAcofold web server from the ViennaRNA package [30].

Identification of MIMOSAS microRNAs with RNAup

For each microRNA with a potential binding site overlapping our boxes, the secondary structure that would be formed after miR binding was predicted using RNAfold with constraints that forbid the target site from forming intramolecular base pairs. The predicted structures were inspected to check whether the box interaction was indeed destroyed, as well as to compute the opening energy for miR binding as the difference between the folding energies with and without constraints. Moreover, we used RNAup [31] to compute the strength of interaction between the microRNA and mRNA. The free energy of binding computed by RNAup consists of the duplex energy, i.e., the free energy of the intermolecular duplex formed between miR and mRNA, as well as the opening energy necessary to make the binding sites accessible for interaction, ΔGMIMOSAS = Gduplex– ΔGopen. In principle, the binding free energy allows to compute the fraction of bound mRNAs if both miR and mRNA concentrations were known. In addition, RNAup computes the conditional probability that the interaction covers a given position. A value close to 1 ensures that no other favorable binding sites exist in the region considered. Since most splicing events occur co-transcriptionally, we mimicked the effects of co-transcriptional folding by performing RNAup computations for three different windows along the mRNA, roughly representing three different time points during transcription.

Nucleotide synthesis and plasmid construction

The primer sequences used for detection and cloning were synthesized by Millipore Sigma and Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) and are listed in Supplementary Table S6. The Drosophila expression plasmid pBID-UASC was a gift from Brian McCabe (Addgene plasmid #35200) [32]. The mammalian expression plasmids pSF-CAG-Amp was purchased from Oxford Genetics, and pcDNA3.1_sfCherry2(1-10) was a gift from Bo Huang (Addgene plasmid # 82602; http://n2t.net/addgene:82602; RRID:Addgene_82 602) [33]. Alternative splicing reporters (ASRs) for Drosophila Nmnat and RpL3 were synthesized and obtained from VectorBuilder. In total, 18 recombinant plasmids were generated for this study, including pBID-UASC-Nmnat_AltReport v2, pSF-CAG-Nmnat_ASR, pLV[Exp]-puro-EF1A-RpL3_AltReport, and pcDNA3.1_sfCherry2(1-10)-miR-9c, pcDNA3.1_sfCherry2(1-10)-miR-304, as well as wild-type and mutant constructs for miR-210, miR-988, and miR-992.

Fly stocks and culture

Flies were reared on a cornmeal-molasses-yeast medium at room temperature (22°C) and 60%–65% humidity. All Drosophila lines, including elav-GAL4, nysb-GAL4, and vGlutOK371-GAL4, as well as microRNA overexpression and knockdown lines (microRNAs: UAS-LUC-miRs and UAS-mCherry.miRs.sponge.V2) and microRNA loss-of-function mutants, were obtained from the Bloomington Stock Center.

RNA extraction

RNA samples were isolated from freshly dissected or ultrarapidly frozen fly heads and from human HEK293T cells. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) from at least 40 fly heads per group or from one well of a 6-well plate per transfection group, following the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 260 nm, and 2 μg of total RNA from each sample was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems).

Fluorescence in situ hybridization of miR-210-3p

The fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) probe for miR-210-3p was an antisense oligonucleotide designed to be specifically complementary to the mature miR-210-3p sequence, while the corresponding sense sequence was used as a negative control (Supplementary Table S6). Dissected salivary glands from wandering third instar larvae were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in Schneider’s Drosophila Medium at room temperature for 10 min, washed one time with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 5 min at room temperature. Followed with removing the PBS and washed the tissue for at least 10 min with 100 µl of in situ mix: (combine 12.5 ml of 100% formamide, 12.5 ml of 20× SSC, 0.9 ml of 0.5 M citric acid, pH 6, 0.75 ml of 20% Triton™ X-100, and 23.35 ml nuclease-free H2O to make a final volume of 50 ml) at room temperature. The probe mix was prepared by diluting the probe to 3 µg/sample and DAPI (to a final concentration of 1 µg/ml) in the in situ mix. The specimen was incubated with the probe overnight at 38°C. After the incubation, the probe was removed, and the specimen was washed for at least 10 min with 100 µl of in situ mix with 1 µg/ml DAPI at room temperature [34].

Drosophila Nmnat and RpL3 variant detection

Standard polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using the FailSafe PCR System (EpiCentre, Chicago, IL, USA) under the following conditions: 25 cycles of 50 s at 95°C, 50 s at 60°C, and 1 min at 72°C. Primers (Supplementary Table S6) used for Nmnat variant detection consisted of a common forward primer targeting exon 1 and two distinct reverse primers targeting exon 5 or exon 7, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S3). For RpL3 variant detection, a common forward primer spanning the exon 2–exon 3 junction was used together with three distinct reverse primers recognizing unique coding sequences specific to each splice variant, located in exon 2 or exon 3 (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Real-time PCR

mRNA levels were quantified using a CFX Connect real-time detection system (Bio-Rad) and TaqMan probe-based gene expression assays (Applied Biosystems). Each 20-µl reaction contained 100 ng of single-stranded cDNA reverse transcribed from total RNA and 1 µl of a gene-specific TaqMan probe–primer set. Amplification was performed using a two-color multiplex real-time PCR program consisting of 40 cycles of 10 s at 95°C, 15 s at 55°C, and 1 min at 72°C. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2[-Delta Delta C(T)] method [35]. Eight TaqMan probes were used in this study: FAM-Dm02150883_g1 (MIMOSAS) and VIC-Dm02144515_g1 (STEM) for Nmnat mRNA variant detection; FAM-Dm01810909_g1 (STEM) and VIC-Dm01810910_m1 (MIMOSAS) for Fas3 mRNA variant detection; FAM-Dm02135667_g1 (Total), FAM-Dm02135669_g1 (RA + RH), and VIC-Dm02148683_g1 (RG) for RpL3 mRNA variant detection; and VIC-Dm02151827_g1 for rp49 detection as an internal control.

In-cell reporter assay

HEK293T or COS-7 cells were co-transfected with Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies). One to two micrograms of cDNA was diluted into 100 µl of Opti-MEM I Medium (Invitrogen) and mixed gently. Lipofectamine 2000 mixture was prepared by diluting 2–4 µl of Lipofectamine 2000 in 100 µl of Opti-MEM I Medium. The ratio of DNA to Lipofectamine 2000 used for transfection was 1∶2 as indicated in the manual. The DNA–Lipofectamine 2000 mixture was mixed gently and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were directly added to the 200 µl of DNA–Lipofectamine 2000 mixture. After 48-h transfection, cells were fixed and stained with 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and Lamin A/C conjugated Alexa Fluor® 647 (1:500; Cell Signaling Technology, #41357). Samples were visualized with an Olympus IX81 confocal microscope under ×60 magnification.

In-fly reporter assay

Drosophila pupal brains were fixed in PBS with 3.7% formaldehyde for 20 min and washed in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.4% Triton X-100 (PBTX). DAPI (1:1000; Invitrogen) staining was performed after being washed three times. Before imaging, tissues were mounted on microscope slides in Vectashield Mounting Medium for Fluorescence (Vector Laboratories).

Immunofluorescence analysis of salivary gland cells

Salivary glands dissected from wandering third instar larvae were fixed in 3.7% formaldehyde at room temperature for 20 min and washed three times with 0.1% PBTX. Tissues were then incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti-AGO1 primary antibody (1:200; Abcam) diluted in a blocking solution containing 5% normal goat serum (Sigma) in 0.1% PBTX. After three 10-min washes in 0.1% PBTX, tissues were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody (1:250; Invitrogen) diluted in the same blocking solution. Following three additional washes in 0.1% PBTX, tissues were stained with DAPI (1:1000; Invitrogen) and mounted on microscope slides in Vectashield Fluorescence Mounting Medium (Vector Laboratories) prior to imaging.

Confocal image acquisition and processing

Confocal microscopy was performed with an Olympus IX81 FV4000 confocal imaging system and processed using FluoView 10-ASW (Olympus) or ImageJ (NIH), and Adobe Photoshop 2026 (Adobe, USA).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were represented as mean ± s.d. or median ± quartiles. Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism using unpaired Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with post hoc tests (Tukey or Bonferroni). Values of P ≤ .05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Genome-wide computational analyses identify microRNA-sensitive stem–loop structures that regulate long-range alternative splicing in Drosophila

Splicing of long introns is thought to be facilitated by RNA secondary structures that form within intronic regions of pre-mRNAs. These long-range interactions can bring distal 5′ and 3′ splice sites into close proximity and are often evolutionarily conserved and tightly linked to alternative splicing [22, 28, 36]. A key molecular mechanism underlying this process involves the formation of stem–loop RNA structures between complementary intronic or exonic sequences, termed “boxes” (Fig. 1A). When box sequences are located near splice sites, stem–loop formation can juxtapose distant splice sites and thereby facilitate long-range splicing (Fig. 1A).

These steps are based on the assumption that the energy gained by microRNA–mRNA binding (Gduplex) compensates for the energy required to open splice-relevant secondary structures (ΔGopen= G0 – G1). If Gduplex> ΔGopen, microRNA binding that disrupts the splice-relevant secondary structure is energetically favored. Assuming that nascent mRNAs rapidly form secondary structures co-transcriptionally, predictions are refined by analyzing a sequence window upstream of each box and calculating the MIMOSAS energy ΔGMIMOSAS = Gduplex − ΔGopen as well as the probability that a given microRNA binds within the box region.

In the first step of our bioinformatic pipeline, we sought to identify regions within pre-mRNA sequences that are likely to form secondary structures at splice-relevant positions (Fig. 1B). Using RNAplex algorithm [37], we assessed base-pair complementarity and the energetic stability of these putative structures, by calculating hybridization free energy (ΔG) for each box pair, including flanking sequences—20 nucleotides downstream of boxes I, half of the intervening distance to box II, the remaining distance to box II, and 20 nucleotides upstream of box II [38] (Fig. 1B). Most box pairs formed stable Stems with ΔG < −15 kcal/mol (Supplementary Table S1).

Using a dataset of highly conserved complementary box sequences located near splice sites in Drosophila [28, 36], we found that stem–loop structures typically adopt in one of three configurations: intron–intron (ii), intron–exon (ie)/exon–intron (ei), or exon–exon (ee) (Fig. 1B). To capture the full ensemble of possible structures, we computed stem-formation probabilities based on their energetics and identified 49 genes harboring splice-relevant stem–loop regions with the potential to modulate alternative splicing, including alternative donor/acceptor usage and putative polyadenylation events. Among these 49 genes, 31 harbor intron–intron (ii), 16 intron–exon (ie)/exon–intron (ei), and 4 exon–exon (ee) structures. Notably, these genes are distributed across all four chromosomes, indicating a widespread and abundant presence of these potentially splice-relevant RNA secondary structures in the Drosophila genome (Fig. 1C).

In the second step of our pipeline, we examined whether these splice-relevant secondary structures overlap with potential microRNA binding sites. We hypothesized that microRNA binding could energetically compensate for the cost of unfolding these secondary structures. To evaluate this, we calculated the energy difference between the mRNA structure with an intact stem (G0) and the structure lacking the stem (ΔGopen = G0 – G1). A candidate microRNA was considered functionally relevant if the free energy of the microRNA–mRNA duplex (Gduplex) was more negative than ΔGopen, indicating that microRNA binding—and consequent disruption of the splice-relevant stem—would be energetically favorable [24, 39] (Fig. 1D). As summarized in Supplementary Tables S2–S5, we found that in most cases, candidate microRNAs were predicted to target the complementary box sequences, thereby potentially destabilizing stem formation.

In the third step, we refined our predictions by incorporating co-transcriptional folding of nascent mRNAs, which can rapidly form secondary structures as they are synthesized by RNA polymerase. To account for this, we analyzed a sequence window upstream of the 5′ end of each box and calculated the difference in free energy between the microRNA-mRNA duplex (Gduplex) and the energy required to open the stem (ΔGopen), We defined this quantity as the MIMOSAS energy: ΔGMIMOSAS = Gduplex − ΔGopen. In addition, we estimated the probability of specific microRNAs binding to each box [24, 31] (Fig. 1D), enabling a more comprehensive and probabilistic assessment of potential regulatory interactions.

microRNA-mediated disruption of stem–loop structures regulates Fas3 alternative splicing in Drosophila

To experimentally validate our computational predictions, we selected Fas3 as an initial model gene due to its complex, multiexonic structure and extensive repertoire of splice variants. A previous study identified complementary sequences around the splice donor and acceptor sites flanking exon 5 [22], but these sequences did not form a stem structure in our RNAplex-based analysis. Because sequence complementarity alone does not guarantee stem formation, we refined our approach by calculating the ensemble energy of secondary structures formed by the region encompassing exon 5 and its 5′ and 3′ flanking introns. By plotting nucleotide interaction probabilities within this mRNA subsequence (Supplementary Fig. S1), we identified alternative box regions, one of which overlapped with a previously predicted complementary region. Further structural analysis revealed a stable Stem encompassing these boxes in the MFE conformation (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Using the box sequences depicted in Fig. 2A, we next identified microRNAs predicted to bind box I and box II (Fig. 2B) and ranked these candidates by their ΔGMIMOSAS values. From this list, we selected two classes of microRNAs with distinct binding energetics: (i) strong candidates predicted to disrupt the stem and promote the RC (MIMOSAS) splice variant, and (ii) weak candidates unlikely to affect stem stability. We hypothesized that a strong candidate microRNA would disrupt the stem and shift splicing toward the RC splice variant (MIMOSAS), whereas in the absence of effective MIMOSAS activity, alternative splice variants (RA/B/D/E/F/G) would be favored (Fig. 2C).

To further refine these predictions, we incorporated co-transcriptional folding by analyzing a 200-nucleotide nascent pre-mRNA segment upstream of the 5′ end of each box, as it would be synthesized by RNA polymerase. We incrementally shifted this window by the length of the box and recalculated microRNA binding energetics within these changing structural contexts. Three transcriptional stages, represented by different colors, illustrate the dynamic nature of microRNA binding: green indicates the binding probability when RNA polymerase has just reached the 5′ end of the box; orange indicates the probability when the 3′ end of the box has just been transcribed; and dark red indicates the probability after RNA polymerase has transcribed an additional downstream segment equal in length to the box. This framework highlights that microRNA binding is highly dynamic. Notably, miR-973 showed both a high ΔGMIMOSAS and a high binding probability in the box I region, making it a prime candidate to promote the RC splice variant, whereas miR-976 showed negligible binding within the box region (Fig. 2D). Similarly, miR-1000 emerged as a strong MIMOSAS candidate at box II, whereas miR-999 had minimal binding potential (Fig. 2E). The prominent microRNA binding peak in the green window outside the box suggests that the microRNA may initially bind an upstream region before the box is fully transcribed. In MIMOSAS, regulation depends not only on complementary base pairing but also on the secondary structure and energetic favorability of the target RNA and likely occurs co-transcriptionally.

To test these predictions in vivo, we overexpressed the respective microRNAs in Drosophila brains and quantified splice variant abundance using real-time PCR. Our previously established microRNA overexpression system enables pre-microRNA expression that closely mimics endogenous conditions [40, 41]. Expression of miR-973 or miR-1000 significantly increased the relative abundance of the RC (MIMOSAS) splice variant compared with the nontargeting control miR-9c (Fig. 2F). To further confirm the specificity of this MIMOSAS-mediated regulation and exclude experimental artifacts, we used microRNA sponge constructs to knock down endogenous microRNAs. Both overexpression and knockdown are well-established approaches for probing microRNA function in Drosophila [40, 41]. Consistent with our model, real-time PCR revealed effects opposite to overexpression for miR-973 and miR-1000 when compared with a scramble sponge control (Fig. 2G), whereas manipulation of miR-976 or miR-999 had no detectable impact on splicing (Fig. 2F and G). Furthermore, a miR-999 loss-of-function mutant exhibited Fas3 splicing patterns indistinguishable from the wild-type (w1118) control. Collectively, these results validate our computationally predicted microRNA candidates and support MIMOSAS-mediated regulation of alternative splicing as a bona fide endogenous mechanism in Drosophila.

In vivo analysis reveals microRNA-mediated MIMOSAS regulation of Nmnat splicing

Previously, we identified miR-1002 as a candidate microRNA capable of disrupting a splice-relevant stem–loop in Nmnat pre-mRNA, thereby modulating splicing between the RA and RB variants. Mutagenesis of the box sequences in the pre-mRNA significantly altered the splicing outcome, confirming their functional importance [26]. Consistent with our predictions, miR-1002 overexpression shifted splicing toward the neuroprotective RB variant at the expense of RA. Our initial identification of splice-relevant stems in Nmnat relied solely on complementary subsequences at the exon 5 splice sites and adjacent upstream intronic regions. To refine this analysis, we examined the full ensemble of predicted secondary structures spanning exon 5 and its flanking introns (Supplementary Fig. S2). This approach revealed substantial overlap between predicted stem–loops and the boxes defined by sequence complementarity. Notably, stem formation is associated with the RA variant, in which exon 5 is excluded, whereas stem disruption favors exon 5 inclusion and production of the RB variant (Fig. 3A). To determine the endogenous Nmnat variants, we performed RT-PCR exclusively in wild-type backgrounds (Supplementary Fig. S3). We used variant-specific amplification primers in exon 5 (E5R) and exon 7 (E7R) to identify the mRNA transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Notably, when using the same primer pair to detect the RB splice variant, we also observed additional mRNA species alongside the MIMOSAS-dependent RB variant (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B) [42]. Sequencing confirmed that the upper band of 1.1 kb corresponds to an unspliced transcript with intron retention (Supplementary Fig. S3C), suggesting that MIMOSAS-mediated alternative splicing may interfere with canonical splicing by extending the spatial distance between splice sites and promoting intron retention.

The boxes within exon 5 were predicted targets not only of miR-1002 but also of numerous other microRNAs (Fig. 3B). By analyzing 200-nucleotide windows upstream of the 5′ end of each box and calculating microRNA binding energies, we identified a spectrum of microRNAs with varying affinities for box I and box II (Supplementary Fig. S4). For example, miR-137 showed strong binding probability to box I, whereas miR-307a showed no detectable binding. Similarly, miR-278 and miR-983 exhibited robust binding probabilities at box II (Supplementary Fig. S4). To experimentally validate these predictions, we performed real-time PCR after expressing selected microRNAs in Drosophila. microRNAs with strong predicted binding—such as miR-210 and miR-137 (box I) and miR-983, miR-9b, miR-278, and miR-1002 (box II)—significantly increased the abundance of the MIMOSAS-specific RB variant. By contrast, microRNAs with minimal predicted binding, including miR-307a and miR-193, had no detectable effect on RB levels and served as negative controls, similar to the nontargeting miR-9c (Fig. 3C). To further confirm specificity and exclude experimental artifacts, we used microRNA sponges to knock down endogenous microRNAs (Fig. 3D). Consistent with their roles in promoting RB production, sponge-mediated depletion of miR-137, miR-210, miR-983, miR-9b, miR-278, and miR-1002 reversed the splicing changes observed upon overexpression (Fig. 3D). We also analyzed loss-of-function mutants (Fig. 3E): compared with wild-type (w1118), miR-137KO and miR-210KO mutants recapitulated the effects of their corresponding sponges on Nmnat splicing. In contrast, miR-1002 sponge expression and miR-1002 knockout did not elicit a robust MIMOSAS effect relative to knockdown of more highly expressed endogenous microRNAs, likely because miR-1002 is expressed at very low levels in the adult head [43]; further reduction of such low endogenous levels produces only modest effects.

To directly visualize MIMOSAS in vivo, we engineered fluorescence-based genetic reporters that monitor alternative splicing in a cell-type-specific manner. Building on our previous Nmnat splicing reporter [42], we redesigned the construct by relocating the N-terminal sequence encoding the shared first seven amino acids, including the start codon for EGFP and mCherry, to the 5′ side of the endogenous intron containing box I. We then fused the C-terminal unique sequence of EGFP to the endogenous 3′ UTR of the Nmnat-RB variant in exon 5 (containing box II), followed by endogenous intron 5 and the C-terminal unique sequence of mCherry (Fig. 3F). In this configuration, EGFP fluorescence serves as a specific readout of MIMOSAS events, whereas mCherry reports alternative splicing driven by stem formation between the two boxes (STEM) (Fig. 3F). Only correctly processed splice variants yield functional fluorescence, while mis-spliced or incompletely processed transcripts remain nonfluorescent, allowing accurate quantification of splice variant production.

For in vivo visualization, we generated a transgenic line carrying the UAS-AltSplicing Reporter and co-expressed the reporter and candidate microRNAs in neurons using nysb-GAL4. Expression of miR-137, miR-210, miR-983, miR-9b, or miR-278 led to a marked reduction in mCherry (stem) intensity and a concomitant increase in EGFP (MIMOSAS) levels in miR-9b- and miR-278-expressing brains (Fig. 3G and H). These changes resulted in a significant decrease in the STEM-to-MIMOSAS ratio, represented by the mCherry/EGFP signal, compared with brains expressing a nontargeting microRNA (miR-9c) or the negative control miR-307a (Fig. 3I). Together, these in vivo imaging data corroborate our computational predictions and highlight the robust capacity of microRNAs to regulate alternative splicing via the MIMOSAS mechanism.

In vitro analysis reveals sequence-specific miR-210-mediated MIMOSAS of Nmnat via an intronic box

Our previous work identified miR-1002 as a candidate microRNA that binds box II within exon 5 of Nmnat pre-mRNA and disrupts a splice-relevant stem–loop, thereby shifting splicing between RA and RB [26]. Mutagenesis of the box sequences confirmed their functional importance by altering splicing outcomes [26]. To further explore microRNA-mediated MIMOSAS regulation through sequence-specific interactions with intronic box elements, we performed in silico predictions of RNA secondary structures for Nmnat ASRs and miR-210 duplexes.

We first determined the native stem–loop structure of the Nmnat ASR using RNAfold from the Vienna RNA package [24] with default parameters (Fig. 4A). MFE structures were visualized with the FORNA web server [27], allowing us to model the structural effects of miR-210 binding to intronic box I, as well as the consequences of miR-210 mutations. Consistent with earlier studies [22, 26, 42], the Nmnat ASR forms a stem–loop through base pairing between box I (cyan) and box II (pink), a configuration we confirmed using wild-type Nmnat pre-mRNA structure predictions (Supplementary Fig. S2). We then used RNAstructure 6.1 [29] to calculate binding energies for wild-type miR-210-3p and three miR-210-3p mutants carrying scrambled stretches of 3, 4, or 6 consecutive nucleotides. All mutants displayed higher (less negative) MFE values for the duplex (Gduplex) compared with wild-type miR-210-3p, indicating reduced thermodynamic stability. Correspondingly, their ΔGMIMOSAS values decreased, predicting a lower probability of mediating MIMOSAS events.

To experimentally test these predictions, we established a cell-based reporter assay to measure miR-210-mediated MIMOSAS via intronic box I (Fig. 4B). We generated two plasmids: one expressing a pre-microRNA fused to sfCherry (superfolder Cherry) [33], and another encoding a split-GFP Nmnat ASR that preserves the native stem–loop structure of Nmnat pre-mRNA. Alternative splicing outcomes were monitored using a self-complementing split GFP system [33]. NLS or NESs were engineered into the reporter so that splice variant identity could be inferred from the subcellular distribution of GFP fluorescence. Human HEK293T cells were co-transfected with reporter and pre-microRNA plasmids, and only cells displaying both red (sfCherry) and green (sfGFP) fluorescence—indicating successful co-transfection—were analyzed (Fig. 4C).

Because the ASR retains all essential sequences required for stem formation, we were able to use previously validated TaqMan probes that detect endogenous Drosophila Nmnat splice variants [42]. To quantitatively assess the impact of miR-210 variants on splicing via intronic box I, we performed two-color multiplex real-time PCR in co-transfected HEK293T cells. The TaqMan probes specifically recognize Nmnat splice variants, thereby directly reporting ASR mRNA splicing as a measure of miR-210-mediated MIMOSAS activity. Wild-type miR-210 significantly decreased the STEM-to-MIMOSAS mRNA ratio relative to the nontargeting control miR-9c (Fig. 4D), consistent with the in vivo data in Fig. 3. This suggests that the MIMOSAS mechanism can operate using endogenous human splicing machinery. In contrast, progressive scrambling of miR-210 reduced MIMOSAS activity in parallel with increases in duplex MFE, and the mutant with six substituted nucleotides (MT3) was indistinguishable from the negative control, indicating loss of functional interaction with box I (Fig. 4D).

Confocal quantification of fluorescence provided a parallel readout of MIMOSAS for each miR-210 variant. Wild-type miR-210 caused a robust increase in cytoplasmic GFP (MIMOSAS) signal compared with miR-9c (Fig. 4C and E), in agreement with the PCR results (Fig. 4D). None of the three mutant miR-210 constructs produced a significant increase in cytoplasmic GFP relative to miR-9c (Fig. 4E). Moreover, the magnitude of miR-210-mediated MIMOSAS activity correlated with miR-210 expression level, as inferred from sfCherry intensity, following a logarithmic relationship (Fig. 4F). Minor discrepancies between fluorescence- and PCR-based measurements likely reflect differences in assay sensitivity and/or in the stability of nuclear versus cytoplasmic GFP isoforms. Together, these data demonstrate that the split-GFP splicing reporter enables both molecular and cellular quantification of MIMOSAS and validate the predicted sequence-specific effects of miR-210 variants on intronic box I. Collectively, our findings support a model in which miR-210 drives MIMOSAS by directly engaging intronic box I in a sequence-dependent manner.

In vivo analyses identify microRNAs driving RpL3 alternative splicing

Both Fas3 and Nmnat exemplify alternative splicing regulated by relatively simple, single stem–loop secondary structures. To investigate how more complex RNA architectures affect splicing, we turned to RpL3 pre-mRNA. In addition to the previously described complementary boxes I and II near the exon 3 splice sites [28, 36], we identified a nested stem–loop architecture in RpL3 pre-mRNA. Analysis of the lowest free-energy secondary structure of exon 3 confirmed stem formation between these complementary sequences (Supplementary Fig. S5). When we extended the prediction to include exons 4, box II instead paired with a newly identified complementary element in exon 4, designated box III (Supplementary Fig. S5). Thus, RpL3 pre-mRNA can adopt alternative conformations in which stems form either between boxes I/II or boxes II/III, enabling production of distinct splice variants (Fig. 5A). Specifically, stem formation between boxes II/III favors variants RA/RH, stem formation between boxes I/II generates RG, and an open conformation without stems gives rise to RD (Fig. 5A). Strikingly, RA/RH (stem II/III) dominates the splicing profile, accounting for ~94% of total RpL3 transcripts, while RG (stem I/II) and RD (open conformation) represent ~4% and ~2%, respectively (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S6).

The nested stem–loop configuration in RpL3 pre-mRNA implies a highly dynamic landscape in which box sequences compete for pairing, thereby generating diverse splicing outcomes. microRNAs binding to specific box regions could therefore bias this competition and reshape the splicing pattern. Using energy- and structure-based computational criteria, we identified several microRNAs with strong predicted affinity for RpL3 box regions that can disrupt stem formation (Fig. 5A). miR-210-3p is predicted to target both box I and box II, whereas the 5′ and 3′ arms of miR-988 are predicted to bind box I and box II, respectively. In addition, miR-992-3p is predicted to interact strongly with box II (Supplementary Fig. S7).

To test the regulatory impact of these microRNAs on RpL3 splicing, we performed two-color multiplex real-time PCR using variant-specific TaqMan probes. We designed probes to quantify: (i) total RpL3 mRNA, (ii) RA/RH plus RG together, and (iii) RG alone (Fig. 5A). Overexpression of miR-210, miR-988, or miR-992 significantly increased total RpL3 mRNA relative to the nontargeting control miR-9c and the negative control miR-304 (Fig. 5C), suggesting that these microRNAs may indirectly upregulate RpL3 expression. However, miR-210 and miR-988 overexpression significantly reduced the proportion of both RA/RH and RG among all spliced RpL3 variants compared with miR-9c and miR-304 (Fig. 5D and E). miR-988 caused only a modest reduction in RG relative to miR-210 (Fig. 5E). Consistent with a shift away from stem-based variants, both miR-210 and miR-988 increased the inferred proportion of the RD variant (Fig. 5F). By contrast, miR-992 overexpression selectively increased the fraction of RG without altering RA/RH or RD proportions (Fig. 5D–F).

To further assess specificity and rule out any potential experimental artifacts, we quantified endogenous RpL3 splice variants in microRNA loss-of-function mutants. Unexpectedly, total RpL3 mRNA was also significantly elevated in miR-210 knockout (miR-210KO) flies compared with wild-type (w1118) controls (Fig. 5G), again pointing to an indirect effect of miR-210 on RpL3 expression level. Interestingly, miR-210KO specifically altered the fraction of RG, reversing the effect seen upon miR-210 overexpression, while leaving RA/RH and RD proportions unchanged (Fig. 5H–J). In miR-988KO mutants, we detected no significant changes in RA/RH or RG levels (Fig. 5H and J). Although RG became undetectable in miR-988KO (Fig. 5I), total RpL3 levels showed only a small, nonsignificant decrease (Fig. 5G), consistent with RG representing only ~4% of total RpL3 in wild-type flies.

Taken together, these data support a model in which miR-210, by targeting both box I and box II, disrupts both stems and favors the open conformation, thereby promoting RD production (Fig. 5D–F). miR-988 similarly enhances RD splicing but with a weaker effect on RG, suggesting that it primarily destabilizes the II/III stem (Fig. 5D–F and H–J). In contrast, miR-992 shifts splicing toward RG (Fig. 5E), likely by destabilizing the II/III stem and/or stabilizing the I/II stem. The agreement between variant profiles measured by real-time PCR and the structural/energetic predictions reinforces the role of microRNAs as regulators of RpL3 alternative splicing. Nonetheless, direct quantification of RD is currently limited by the absence of RD-specific probes, so its abundance must be inferred as the difference between total RpL3 and the sum of other measured variants.

To directly assess the effect of MIMOSAS on RpL3 pre-mRNA splicing, we designed a dual-color split-fluorescent protein (FP) reporter that mimics the complex secondary structure of the pre-mRNA. All sequences essential for stem formation were preserved to reflect physiological conditions. To visualize spliced mRNA variants, we utilized self-complementing split FPs [33]. Our reporter system employed two distinct FPs, sfGFP and sfCherry, each tagged with either nuclear (NLS) or cytoplasmic (NES) localization signals, enabling discrimination of splicing outcomes based on both fluorescence color and subcellular compartment (Fig. 6A). Specifically, the stem (II/III) structure, which promotes the RA/RH variant, was detected by cytoplasmic NES-tagged sfGFP. This variant comprised split GFP1-10 and GFP11 subunits linked by a 96 bp linker and a cytoplasmic NES tag, facilitating functional GFP reconstitution and green fluorescence in the cytoplasm. Splicing that favored the RG variant through the stem (I/II) resulted in nuclear green fluorescence. In contrast, the absence of stem formation produced the RD variant—a chimera of the sfGFP1-10 subunit fused to the sfCherry11 subunit. Co-expression with a pre-microRNA-linked sfCherry1-10 subunit allowed reconstitution of functional sfCherry, resulting in red fluorescence.

We analyzed the entire splicing reporter and determined its lowest free-energy secondary structure, revealing a nested stem–loop configuration mirroring that of the RpL3 pre-mRNA (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. S5). Notably, a miR-210 mutant carrying two nucleotide substitutions (MT5), as well as a miR-988 mutant, exhibited lower MFE values for binding box II compared to their respective wild-type forms (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S8).

As predicted, expression of miR-304 as a negative control did not alter the production of any spliced variants relative to the nontarget control miR-9c (Fig. 7A and A’’). In contrast, miR-210 expression resulted in marked reductions in both cytoplasmic GFP (RA/H variant) and nuclear GFP (RG variant), accompanied by increased mCherry fluorescence (Fig. 7B and B’’, and Supplementary Fig. S9), consistent with real-time PCR results for endogenous RpL3 splice variants (Fig. 5D–F). Progressive scrambling of miR-210 diminished MIMOSAS activity as duplex MFE increased (Fig. 7B and B’’, and Supplementary Fig. S9). Notably, a miR-210 mutant with eight seed sequence substitutions (MT4) retained partial regulatory capacity similar to wild-type, whereas the mutant with two substitutions (MT5), despite a slightly lower MFE for box II, exhibited effects similar to the negative control (Fig. 7B and B’’). These findings suggest that MIMOSAS-active microRNAs generally do not require extensive complementary base pairing or canonical seed sequence matching to the box sequence.

Expression of wild-type and mutant miR-988 also exhibited opposing effects on RA/RH and RD variants; notably, mutant miR-988 increased RA/RH variants and reduced RG, implying enhanced box I interaction, which may hinder box I/II stem formation. Despite computational prediction of lower MFE for box II interaction, this suggests that MIMOSAS outcomes are influenced by both energetic favorability and RNA secondary structure, and strong binding does not always correlate with pronounced regulation. These effects may be explained by mutations shifting miR-988-3p binding from the 5′ end to internal regions of box I (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S8).

Similar patterns were observed with miR-992. Compared to nontarget control, none of the miR-992 variants significantly affected RD; however, wild-type miR-992 elevated nuclear GFP (RG) and reduced cytoplasmic GFP (RA/RH) (Fig. 7D and D’’). Mutation with seven seed sequence substitutions (MT1) reversed this pattern, while a mutation outside the seed sequence restored wild-type activity (Fig. 7D and D’’). This indicates that miR-992 primarily interacts with box II, impacting box II/III formation. Furthermore, the MT1 mutation’s upregulation of RA/RH may reflect a shift in miR-992-3p binding from the 5′ end to internal regions of box I (Fig. 6D and Supplementary Fig. S8), thus increasing box I interaction and preventing box I/II stem formation.

Overall, the split-FP dual-color splicing reporter provided direct in vivo visualization of MIMOSAS, showing that miRs-210, 992, and 988 disrupt box II/III pairing, reduce RA/H variant levels, and promote RD variant production. Sequence window analysis upstream of box I and II further revealed that miR-992′s regulatory activity may be mediated by stabilization of box I/II interaction, as indicated by increased RG variant abundance. These results demonstrate the reporter’s effectiveness in dissecting individual microRNA contributions to pre-mRNA structural dynamics and alternative splicing. While energetic favorability is an important determinant of MIMOSAS regulation, RNA secondary structure ultimately governs the outcome.

AGO1-dependent nuclear localization of microRNAs is required for MIMOSAS-mediated alternative splicing

During RISC assembly, mature Drosophila microRNAs are predominantly loaded onto Argonaute 1 (AGO1) before engaging their target transcripts [44, 45]. AGO1, the Drosophila homolog of mammalian Argonaute 2, is the principal RISC component for microRNA loading [46]. Growing evidence, including our own studies, indicates that AGO1 is also present in the nucleus [26, 47, 48], implicating it in nuclear processes such as transcriptional regulation. We previously showed that AGO1 is required for MIMOSAS-mediated splicing of Nmnat [26], prompting us to investigate AGO1’s broader role.

To this end, we used AGO1 heterozygous (AGO1+/−) mutant flies, which exhibit significantly reduced AGO1 levels [49]. We expressed miR-210—a microRNA targeting both Nmnat and RpL3—in Drosophila salivary gland cells using the vGlutOK371-GAL4 driver in either AGO1 wild-type (AGO1+/+) or heterozygous (AGO1+/−) backgrounds. To further validate our previous findings, we examined the subcellular localization of endogenous AGO1 in salivary gland cells from both genetic backgrounds, with or without wild-type miR-210 expression. Confocal imaging showed that AGO1 was predominantly cytoplasmic in both wild-type and heterozygous tissues, consistent with its canonical role as a cytoplasmic RISC component mediating post-transcriptional gene silencing (Fig. 8A and B). However, overexpression of miR-210 in salivary gland cells not only enhanced nuclear accumulation of AGO1 but also increased overall endogenous AGO1 levels (Fig. 8C). Notably, robust nuclear enrichment was observed only in the wild-type background (Fig. 8A and B). Although AGO1+/− tissues displayed a slight, nonsignificant increase in nuclear AGO1, this effect was amplified upon miR-210 expression (Fig. 8B), indicating that miR-210 overexpression promotes nuclear localization of endogenous AGO1.

To determine whether overexpressed microRNA also redistributes to the nucleus in parallel with AGO1, we performed fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) to visualize and quantify the subcellular localization of miR-210-3p. Strikingly, miR-210-3p was highly enriched in nuclei in wild-type tissue but was significantly reduced in AGO1+/− mutant tissue (Fig. 8D and E, and Supplementary Fig. S10). Importantly, loss of AGO1 impaired the nuclear enrichment of miR-210 (Fig. 8E) without altering total miR-210 levels (Fig. 8F), indicating a specific role for AGO1 in directing microRNAs to the nucleus.

To assess the functional consequences of reduced AGO1, we performed real-time PCR analysis of Nmnat and RpL3 splice variants in brains expressing either miR-9c (control) or miR-210. Under normal conditions, miR-210 disrupts stem formation in both Nmnat (Fig. 3) and RpL3 (Fig. 5). In AGO1+/− brains, however, the MIMOSAS effects of miR-210 were markedly attenuated for both Nmnat (Fig. 8G) and RpL3 (Fig. 8H), whereas miR-9c did not affect alternative splicing of either gene in either genetic background (Fig. 8G and H). Together, these data indicate that AGO1 is required for efficient nuclear localization of microRNAs and for MIMOSAS-mediated alternative splicing. We therefore propose that MIMOSAS events occur primarily in the nucleus, where AGO1–microRNA complexes engage structured pre-mRNAs to remodel stem–loop architectures and bias splice site selection.

Discussion

Alternative splicing is a key mechanism for gene expression diversity, with its regulation relying on the recognition of specific pre-mRNA elements by trans-acting factors. RNA secondary structures are known to regulate alternative splicing of long-range pre-mRNAs, yet what actively modulates these structures to influence splice site selection remains poorly understood. Here, we identify a previously unrecognized function of microRNAs: direct regulation of alternative splicing via modulation of pre-mRNA stem–loop structures. To uncover this mechanism, we developed a computational pipeline that integrates sequence complementarity with the energetics of RNA secondary structures to systematically screen the genome for microRNA–pre-mRNA interaction sites in Drosophila. Focusing on evolutionarily conserved complementary “boxes” associated with alternative donor or acceptor sites or putative polyadenylation sites, we identified numerous microRNAs predicted to engage splice-relevant stem–loop regions. Using three distinct long-range pre-mRNA substrates—Fas3, Nmnat, and RpL3—we experimentally validated a subset of these predictions in both mammalian cell culture and in vivo Drosophila models. Our data show that microRNAs can bind directly to intronic or exonic regions of pre-mRNAs, thereby destabilizing or stabilizing stem–loop structures and consequently altering splicing outcomes.

Importantly, we demonstrate that endogenous microRNAs can drive these splicing changes under conventional overexpression conditions [40, 41], and at physiological expression levels, as loss-of-function mutations in the corresponding microRNAs also affected splicing outcomes. Mutagenesis of microRNA binding sites and of the structured pre-mRNA regions further shows that these sequences are necessary, but not alone sufficient, for MIMOSAS regulation. MIMOSAS-active microRNAs typically do not require extensive complementarity or canonical seed sequence pairing to the target box sequence. While favorable binding energetics contribute to MIMOSAS regulation, the decisive determinant is the local RNA secondary structure; mutations within these regions significantly altered MIMOSAS outcomes [26]. Although the scale of our MIMOSAS analysis is smaller than that of traditional genome- or transcriptome-wide studies, our work intentionally focused on evolutionarily conserved sequences forming stem–loop structures associated with alternative splicing events that generate alternative donor or acceptor sites, or putative polyadenylation sites, thereby producing distinct mature mRNA isoforms. Within this defined framework, our findings therefore extend the functional repertoire of microRNAs—classically linked to mRNA degradation and translational repression—by revealing a mechanistically distinct, potentially genome-wide layer of alternative splicing control.

Alternative splicing depends on the ability of trans-acting factors to recognize key elements within a single-stranded pre-mRNA, including splice sites, branch points, and cis-acting regulatory sequences. RNA secondary structures can profoundly influence spliceosome assembly by suppressing or exposing splice sites, masking or revealing cis-acting elements, mediating “looping-out” mechanisms [50], and engaging in competitive structural arrangements [51]. Regulatory noncoding RNAs have been implicated in post-transcriptional control of splicing by modifying chromatin states, hybridizing with genomic loci or nascent/pre-mRNA transcripts to form RNA–DNA or RNA–RNA duplexes, or modulating the expression and activity of splicing factors [52]. MicroRNAs, another major class of regulatory noncoding RNAs, are classically associated with mRNA degradation and translational repression, and their effects on alternative splicing are generally viewed as indirect, occurring through post-transcriptional regulation of splicing factors [53]. Nonetheless, microRNAs can, in principle, regulate splicing post-transcriptionally via toehold-mediated gene silencing [54, 55], and can behave in a manner analogous to toehold-mediated invasion when engaging structured RNA regions or duplexes [56, 57]. We propose that MIMOSAS regulation involves a mechanism reminiscent of microRNA-driven invasion of RNA duplexes through an initial “toehold” of complementary single-stranded sequence. However, in the context of MIMOSAS, microRNA binding is not confined to a discrete toehold; rather, the full microRNA sequence can participate in the regulatory interaction. Importantly, our model extends beyond earlier frameworks such as the “growing intron loop” model [50], which focused exclusively on protein–RNA interactions and did not account for the dynamic RNA–RNA secondary structures formed by emerging intronic sequences. By explicitly incorporating these structural features, our findings provide strong evidence that direct microRNA–pre-mRNA interactions constitute a critical determinant of splice site choice and alternative splicing outcomes.

Given that most splicing occurs co-transcriptionally [58], the nuclear localization of microRNAs and Argonaute proteins observed in our work and in recent reports strongly supports a nuclear setting for MIMOSAS events. Advanced imaging approaches have now visualized the movement of endogenous mature microRNAs from the cytoplasm into the nucleus, using super-quencher molecular probes delivered by optoporation to selectively permeabilize single cells [59]. In parallel, several studies indicate that karyopherin XPO1 (Exportin-1) and IPO8 (Importin-8) mediate the nuclear import of microRNA–AGO complexes in human cells [60–62]. Although the functions of nuclear microRNAs are not yet fully defined, accumulating evidence suggests that nuclear RISC complexes can modulate chromatin compaction to achieve post-transcriptional gene silencing and can influence RNA polymerase II elongation rates, thereby impacting alternative splicing outcomes [63, 64]. A recent study further showed that AGO proteins associate with core RISC components and bind within coding regions and possibly introns in the nucleus of mammalian embryonic stem cells, contributing to post-transcriptional gene silencing and revealing that nuclear Ago localization in specific cell types expands the microRNA-regulated transcriptome beyond previous expectations [65]. Consistent with this, altering nuclear levels of AGO1, AGO2, or DICER1 has been shown to affect splicing choices at particular alternatively spliced exons [26, 47, 48, 63]. Together, these findings underscore both the plausibility and biological relevance of nuclear MIMOSAS events.

In both Drosophila and primates, microRNAs are produced by a conserved biogenesis pathway and act through seed-based recognition to repress target mRNAs, primarily via 3′ UTR binding. However, their genomic repertoires and regulatory landscapes differ substantially. Drosophila encodes a few hundred miRNAs, many of which are deeply conserved and act on relatively short 3′ UTRs, resulting in more compact regulatory networks. In contrast, primates possess a much larger and more rapidly evolving set of microRNAs, including many lineage-specific species, which act on long, complex 3′ UTRs enriched for multiple combinatorial binding sites. As a consequence, primate microRNA networks are particularly elaborate in tissues such as the brain and immune system and are extensively implicated in human disease. These differences extend to noncanonical and nuclear functions. While the canonical cytoplasmic role of microRNAs in post-transcriptional gene silencing is shared between Drosophila and primates, noncanonical nuclear activities—such as modulation of transcription, chromatin state, and RNA processing—have been more widely documented and functionally diversified in mammalian and primate systems. Our findings on nuclear microRNA-mediated MIMOSAS-like regulation in Drosophila therefore highlight a conserved but underexplored aspect of microRNA biology, bridging classical cytoplasmic functions and emerging nuclear roles that are particularly prominent in primates.

Given the dynamic expression patterns and pronounced cell type and tissue-specificity of microRNAs, the MIMOSAS mechanism provides a means for highly contextual control of splicing variants, adding an additional regulatory layer to the already complex network of post-transcriptional gene regulation. Importantly, MIMOSAS raises the prospect of therapeutically targeting microRNAs to fine-tune alternative splicing outcomes, a strategy with considerable relevance for diseases driven by splicing defects—which account for up to 60% of disease-associated point mutations [66]. Recent work has demonstrated that duplex RNAs can be used to modulate alternative splicing in mammalian cells [48], and that single-stranded siRNAs can be engineered to correct the splicing of Dystrophin RNA associated with Duchenne muscular dystrophy [67]. These advances highlight the feasibility of using small RNAs, including microRNAs, as precise tools to therapeutically manipulate alternative splicing.

In summary, our identification of MIMOSAS reveals a previously unrecognized, direct role for microRNAs in the regulation of alternative splicing, and emphasizes the need to consider microRNA-driven modulation of RNA secondary structure in both fundamental biological studies and the design of RNA-based therapeutics.
