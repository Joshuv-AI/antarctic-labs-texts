Effect of classical music on light-plane anaesthesia and analgesia in dogs subjected to surgical nociceptive stimuli
Authors: S. G. Georgiou, T. L. Anagnostou, A. I. Sideri, P. G. Gouletsou, L. V. Athanasiou, G. Kazakos, V. Tsioli, E. Dermisiadou, A. D. Galatos
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The objectives of this prospective, randomized, blinded, crossover, experimental study were to detect the potential anaesthetic- and analgesic-sparing effects of classical music provided to dogs undergoing skin surgery, and to investigate the role of substance P as an intraoperative pain indicator. Twenty dogs were included, each subjected to three different treatments: Chopin music, Mozart music and no music. They were premedicated with acepromazine, butorphanol and meloxicam and anaesthetized with propofol and isoflurane. Fentanyl was used as rescue analgesia. The anaesthetic depth was monitored by using the bispectral index along with standard anaesthetic monitoring, and autonomic nervous system responses were used to monitor the adequacy of analgesia. Furthermore, measurements of substance P serum concentration were carried out. Dogs exposed to music required less isoflurane and fentanyl. Furthermore, a statistically significant effect of time on substance P concentration was observed regardless of exposure to music, and there was a significant interaction effect between different timepoints and the type of acoustic stimulus. Classical music seems to have an isoflurane and fentanyl sparing effect on dogs undergoing minor surgery. Following surgical stimulation, the serum substance P concentration increases rapidly, and thus appears to be a potentially useful pain indicator.

Introduction

In humans, music exposure during the perioperative period seems to be beneficial1–7. The first report of music as a means of patient calming during surgery occurred more than a century ago8. In domestic animals, the effect of music on behavior and stress response, or as an environmental enrichment method, has been studied9,10; however, its impact during the perioperative period has barely been evaluated. The only relevant studies were reported in cats under anaesthesia11,12 and dogs under sedation13,14 and anaesthesia15. Based on the aforementioned studies in humans and domestic animals, it seems that the appropriate music genre, rhythm, sound volume level and perhaps particular frequency bands should be investigated and standardized for music to exert its positive effect when applied.

Concerning the music genre, classical music is supposed to have beneficial effects on a variety of different animal species9,16, including cats under anaesthesia11,12 and dogs9,14,16–20; dogs, in particular, seem to prefer slow rhythm music14,17,19–21. With regard to the frequency range of an auditory stimulus, a recent study reported that exposure to low-pitch music resulted in arousal-related behaviour in dogs. When the dogs were exposed to low-pitch tracks, they appeared more alert and unsettled, not only compared to the rest music groups but also to the control group where dogs were exposed to ambient noise10. Finally, acoustic stimuli < 80 dB seem to be appropriate both for unsedated10,17–21 and sedated dogs14 and for cats under anaesthesia11,12. In a recent study, dogs exposed to Mozart or Chopin music achieved deeper sedation levels and required lower propofol doses for tracheal intubation than controls, when premedicated with a mild premedication protocol14.

Mira et al.11 attributed the beneficial effects of music to the potential preservation of auditory sensory stimuli processing under anaesthesia. While this is an indirect assumption, studies both in humans and animals seem to agree that the auditory processing is a likely phenomenon, at least under light anaesthesia, if not under deeper levels of anaesthesia, as well. It has been suggested that a dose-related impairment in the central processing of auditory information is caused by propofol22,23 and sevoflurane24 in humans, while similar results have been reported in various animal species under various injectable anaesthetic combinations25,26 and isoflurane27–29. However, injectable and inhalant anaesthetics do not completely blunt cortical responses to auditory stimuli, indicating that the process of auditory information, although suppressed, may persist under anaesthesia.

Substance P (SP) is a neurotransmitter released by sensory neurons located in the dorsal horn of the spinal cord and is known to act as a modulator or mediator of nociception. The potential role of the serum SP concentration as a pain indicator is supported by the identification of neurokinin 1 (NK1) receptors, which have the greatest affinity for SP, in neurons throughout the neuroaxis that are involved in the integration of pain, stress, and anxiety30,31. It has also been observed that dental procedures and experimentally induced tooth pain result in a direct increase in the SP concentration32,33, while the SP concentration also increases in arthritic human patients34,35. Furthermore, in human patients undergoing orthopaedic surgery, increases in the SP are correlated with acute postoperative pain35. Similar observations have been recorded in different animal species under a variety of painful conditions30,36, and in dogs with central sensitization related to osteoarthritic pain37, neuropathic pain38 and fractures or patellar luxation39. Furthermore, the administration of different analgesic combinations appears to attenuate its release during surgical procedures in cows30 and rats40. Takasusuki et al.40 reported that propofol, pentobarbital, isoflurane and nitrous oxide alone did not inhibit NK1 receptor internalization, while fentanyl had a presynaptic effect by blocking dorsal horn SP release.

The main objective of this study was to investigate the potential anaesthetic and/or analgesic sparing effect of music on dogs undergoing minor (i.e. skin) surgery. A secondary objective was to investigate the release of SP following acute surgical nociceptive stimulation. We hypothesized that classical music would have an anaesthetic and/or analgesic sparing effect on dogs undergoing skin surgery and that SP release would reflect the level of nociception associated with surgical stimulation.

Results

There was no difference in the duration of anaesthesia or surgery between treatments, and no dog was excluded from the study due to signs of emergence from anaesthesia (voluntary movements, swallowing, or coughing). Duration of anaesthesia was 80.05 ± 9.02 min for CM, 83 ± 7.6 min for MM and 90 ± 17.7 min for NM (p = 0.133 for CM-NM, p = 0.212 for MM-NM, p = 0.346 for CM-MM). Duration of surgery was 62.45 ± 4.85 min, 63 ± 7.6 min and 67 ± 11.23 min, for CM, MM and NM, respectively (p = 0.127 for CM-NM, p = 0.229 for MM-NM, p = 0.771 for CM-MM). No correlations were observed between anaesthesia and surgery duration, in all treatments. Two dogs in the NM treatment exhibited signs of dysphoria upon recovery and one of them vomited 12 h later, with no other adverse effects being recorded.

Therefore, for SP, we focused on and decided to evaluate only the potential differences between the music and the no music treatments.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that exposure to classical music has a sparing effect on anaesthetic and analgesic agents, at least under a light plane of anaesthesia for minor skin surgery. Furthermore, the serum SP concentration increases within 10 min following skin incision.

The choice of the appropriate musical stimuli for this study was a challenge, because the conclusions drawn by studies in humans cannot be applied to dogs due to many limitations. Therefore, two classical music pieces were chosen with particular characteristics of rhythm (andante, lento sostenuto), one of which (Mozart Κ.448) has already displayed positive effects in humans4,42–44, rats45,46, and only recently in dogs under sedation14. A “Mozart effect” theory related to the particular musical piece has been described (K.448). This musical piece has displayed some beneficial effects both on humans and domestic animals. In addition to that, it seems that the appropriate music genre, rhythm, sound volume level and perhaps particular frequency bands should be investigated and standardized for music to exert its positive effect when applied. Therefore, we decided to investigate the potential effect of a musical piece with characteristics similar to those of Mozart K.448, to determine whether the so-called "Mozart effect" has to do with Mozart music exclusively or with particular characteristics instead, which could also be found in many other composers' musical pieces. Before the experiments, we conducted a signal processing (frequency analysis) of the Mozart K.448 and compared the results to a variety of classical musical pieces with the same characteristics (frequency range and rhythm) and found that the Chopin piece was similar to the aforementioned parameters.

The objective of our study was to investigate the effect of classical music on parameters related to anaesthesia and analgesia during the intraoperative period in dogs. Assessment of such parameters may be feasible during the pre- and postoperative periods by means of observer-dog interaction, but is much more challenging during the intraoperative period. Autonomic nervous system responses, jaw tone, eye position and various reflexes to nociceptive stimuli during surgery are directly influenced by anaesthetic or analgesic drugs, making the decision to administer more of them challenging. Furthermore, minor, if any, changes in physiologic variables have been observed in humans reporting awareness under anaesthesia47, while utilization of MAC as an indicator of hypnosis or unconsciousness has some limitations48. MAC may not be informative regarding the state of consciousness but, as loss of motor responsiveness occurs at a higher dose than that of “consciousness”, MAC is a very good practical endpoint because it ensures that the patient is likely to be unconscious48. As a result, more sophisticated techniques should also be used in the evaluation of anaesthetic depth and the adequacy of analgesia during the intraoperative period.

In the present study, a precise correlation between concentrations of isoflurane and anaesthetic depth was pursued, so that safe conclusions could be drawn about the potential effects of music. To achieve this, BIS was used, aiming for a more objective approach to the evaluation of anaesthetic depth, along with standard anaesthetic monitoring of the reflexes (palpebral, corneal), jaw tone and eye position. The use of BIS in humans seems to be an integral part of perianaesthetic management for the anaesthetist, having high accuracy at classifying the state of consciousness49 and thus reducing the administration of anaesthetics, recovery times and adverse effects50. Apart from humans, BIS values, as an indicator of anaesthetic depth, seem to correlate with the amount of volatile agents administered in dogs under anaesthesia41,51. Furthermore, studies in the veterinary literature have proposed that the BIS can discriminate between different levels of anaesthesia in dogs; however, the sensitivity and specificity of BIS values were low, and variability among patients must be taken into account when monitoring the depth of anaesthesia via the use of the BIS51,52. Therefore, its use as the only means of estimating anaesthetic depth may not always be reliable. Therefore, in the present study, it was used along with standard methods for monitoring anaesthetic depth, to more accurately predict anaesthetic depth in every dog.

Furthermore, in the current study, given that assessing nociception can always be challenging under anaesthesia, measurements of SP concentrations in the dogs’ serum were carried out to objectively evaluate nociception during the intraoperative period. SP seems to be released under painful conditions both in humans32–35 and domestic animal species30,36–39, and besides, administration of analgesics appears to attenuate its release in domestic animal species30,40.

In our study, dogs in the CM and MM treatments required significantly less isoflurane than did dogs in the NM setting for the completion of the surgical procedure, indicating an isoflurane-sparing effect of music. This has not yet been confirmed in humans under anaesthesia, with the differences between groups of music and controls being non-significant53,54. In contrast, a study in cats under anaesthesia, concluded that classical music applied intraoperatively contributes to a reduction in anaesthetic requirements11. However, no mention was made of the isoflurane concentrations used, or of the exact anaesthetic depth assessment method. In the aforementioned studies in humans53,54, to whom music had no significant effect on inhalant requirements, assessment of anaesthetic depth was made by the use of BIS, targeting BIS values of 50–60 as indicative of deep (surgical) anaesthesia. On the other hand, in studies in which the desired anaesthetic depth was lighter (BIS ≈ 70), as in the present study, the results were different, as the total consumption of anaesthetics was lower in subjects exposed to musical stimuli than in controls3–5. This may be due to dose-dependent suppression of auditory function by anaesthetics, as has already been proposed for humans22,24,55 and domestic animal species25–29. In humans anaesthetized with propofol and with BIS ≈ 70, the auditory function was still active but strongly reduced compared to that of fully conscious subjects23. Patients under the same anaesthetic conditions were able to distinguish louder acoustic stimuli of 80, 110 or 120 dB compared to 50 dB stimuli which were not perceptible55. Furthermore, mice under different isoflurane concentrations (1–2.5%) distinguished only high frequency tones, with strong suppression of low and middle frequencies28,29. This is the reason why in the present study, it was decided to perform the procedures under a light anaesthetic depth (BIS ≈ 70), so that the perception of auditory stimuli would be more likely, according to the literature. However, in the present study, the MAC was not predetermined. The MAC has been proposed as a method of titrating volatile anaesthetics and was defined as the minimum alveolar concentration of a volatile anaesthetic, preventing purposeful movement in response to a supramaximal noxious stimulus in 50% of individuals56. First, considering that the nociceptive stimulus in the present study had been preset (three consecutive identical interventions consisting of three skin biopsies and three incisions), we would probably need several skin incisions for MAC determination, which would raise ethical issues and would be much beyond the methodology of the other study protocol on wound healing. Furthermore, skin incision as a nociceptive stimulus has been proposed not to be a supramaximal stimulus57. Second, our target was a light anaesthetic depth (BIS ≈ 70, along with clinical signs of light plane anaesthesia), while MAC determination would probably lead to a surgical depth of anaesthesia which was not our aim, due to literature evidence for a dose-dependent suppression of auditory function by anaesthetics and considering the potential mild effect of intraoperative music exposure as a nonpharmacologic, complementary approach. On the other hand, MAC determination is a standard method for comparing the potency of inhalation anaesthetics and enables researchers to study the effect of an intervention on inhalation anaesthetic requirements. If the objective was the achievement of surgical anaesthetic depth, then we should firstly determine the baseline MAC, which is the isoflurane MAC value before the nociceptive stimulus. In that case the intraoperative period should be separated into two phases: before and after surgical stimulation. In our study, we targeted light plane anaesthesia under preset conditions (BIS ≈ 70 and respective clinical assessment) and we designed a specific treatment for every component of anaesthesia (nociception vs decreased anaesthetic depth), in the case of any alteration. Any deviation, compared to baseline values on the aforementioned anaesthesia components, was a marker for the immediate cessation of the surgical stimulus and application of the respective intervention. Anyway, the surgical stimulus was not re-initiated unless the altered parameters returned to baseline values. Τhe results of the present study showed that music applied intraoperatively lowers isoflurane requirements and indirectly showed that the perception of a musical stimulus may be feasible, at least for the particular end-tidal isoflurane concentrations used [1.0 (0.9–1.3)% and 1.1 (0.9–1.5)%]. However, further studies are needed to determine whether music has an anaesthetic-sparing effect with deeper levels of anaesthesia.

During the intraoperative period, fentanyl was used as rescue analgesia, whenever alterations > 20% in HR, BP and RR were observed, since these autonomic nervous system responses were interpreted as insufficient analgesia. Dogs in the music settings required significantly less fentanyl than did controls during the intraoperative period; however, the prevalence of rescue analgesia was not significantly different between the different treatments. In relation to fentanyl requirements, such an effect of music on intraoperative analgesic requirements has been observed only in humans. Human patients exposed to music while undergoing urologic operations without anaesthesia require less analgesics (alfentanil, fentanyl)1,2, while under anaesthesia with sevoflurane, children undergoing tonsillectomy require less additional intraoperative fentanyl6. On the other hand, this event was not as obvious in studies under inhalational anaesthesia, in which music groups required less fentanyl intraoperatively; however, these differences were not statistically significant54,58. The effect of music appeared less profound in studies under general anaesthesia, probably because of excessive anaesthetic depth, e.g. target BIS values of 50–6054.

SP serum concentrations were significantly elevated at time point T1 compared to those at T0 but appeared to be significantly reduced at time point T2 compared to those at T1, regardless of the type of acoustic stimulus applied. Therefore, approximately 10 min after skin incision (T1), SP concentrations were significantly elevated compared to baseline values (T0), which apparently confirms our initial hypothesis that the SP concentration increases after the application of a nociceptive stimulus and can be associated with acute pain. It has been reported that SP release occurs within 10–60 min after the application of a nociceptive stimulus in humans32,33,59 and within 8–30 min in rats36,60,61. However, in the majority of these studies, no analgesics were used33,36,60,61 with the exceptions of Buvanendran et al.59, Caviedes-Bucheli et al.32 and the present study in which butorphanol and meloxicam were used pre-emptively. To the authors’ knowledge, no data on the potential influence of κ-agonists or NSAIDs (meloxicam) on acute SP release are available, while μ-agonists and fentanyl in particular block its release in the dorsal horn of rats40. This fentanyl block on SP release may have been observed to some extent in the present study, as the concentrations of SP at time point T2 (the end of the surgical procedure) were reduced compared to those at T1. Another reason for that reduction could be the potential normalization of SP at that timepoint. Dogs exposed to music exhibited less profound elevations in SP concentration than dogs not exposed to music intraoperatively. However, there are no studies that directly correlate music listening with variations in SP concentration in a surgical setting.

The indicators of insufficient analgesia during the intraoperative period in the present study were autonomic nervous system responses that were strictly, directly and identically treated, as has already been mentioned. No statistically significant differences in HR, RR or BP during the intraoperative period were detected between the treatments. Similar results were reported by Bilginer et al.15 who noted no statistically significant changes in the same physiologic parameters in ovariohysterectomized dogs under xylazine–ketamine anaesthesia. However, these results may not be directly comparable to the results of the present study due to the totally different anaesthetic and analgesic approaches. In contrast, a study in cats subjected to ovariohysterectomy under anaesthesia detected statistically significant differences in all physiologic parameters recorded, with lower values observed when cats were exposed to classical music than when they were exposed to other types of music or no music11,12. Fluctuations in the values of physiologic parameters were not as remarkable in the present study, probably because every autonomic nervous system response indicative of pain was strongly suppressed by fentanyl, in contrast to the study in cats11 in which no analgesics were administered intraoperatively. However, analgesics, as well as anesthetic agents contributing to analgesia, were used. This hypothesis is supported by human studies in which rescue analgesia was applied intraoperatively if required, with no remarkable fluctuations in physiologic parameters being detected7,58 as in the present study. If the assumption that music may suppress autonomic system responses after nociceptive stimuli, as proposed by Mira et al.11 was to be investigated, no analgesics should have been administered during the intraoperative period, a measure that was considered unethical.

A few potential limitations of our study concern SP. Although we detected a significant increase in SP concentrations with every treatment, there was no treatment group without a nociceptive stimulus (sham anaesthesia). Therefore, we cannot come to a definite conclusion about the exact aetiology of the SP increase, i.e., whether it was due to the existence or the degree of nociception or was increased by surgery. Furthermore, the preoperative administration of meloxicam may have attenuated SP release to some extent; however, considering the short duration effect of butorphanol, the dogs at the time of the application of the nociceptive stimulus would have no or only minimal background analgesia, which is considered to be unethical.

In conclusion, exposure to music has a sparing effect on anaesthetic and analgesic agents, at least as utilized in the present experimental study (light plane of anaesthesia for minor surgery). This effect of music, in combination with its low cost, ease and safety, indicates that music may be an attractive nonpharmacologic complementary tool for use during the perianaesthetic period. In addition, the serum concentration of SP increases rapidly within 10 min following skin incision, indicating its potential relation to acute pain. Finally, this study provides indirect evidence that auditory sensory stimuli processing may persist under light plane anaesthesia, at least with the end-tidal concentrations of inhalant anaesthetic utilized. However, it would be of interest to investigate to what extent anaesthetics may suppress auditory function, and what impact such a nonpharmacological therapy may have in clinical settings where the painful stimulus is moderate to severe, or deeper levels of anaesthesia may be required.

Materials and methods

Animals

This was a prospective, blinded, crossover, randomized experimental study. This study was approved by and followed the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Greece (license number 1117/45296 21.3.2017) which confirmed that it complied with the standards of the national and EU legislation (Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments) regarding animal experimentation. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and the authors complied with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Twenty healthy, adult dogs (seven females and 13 males), scheduled to undergo skin surgery (for another study protocol), were used in the study. The dogs were purpose-bred laboratory Beagles owned by the Clinic of Surgery of the Faculty of Veterinary Science of University of Thessaly. They were individually kenneled and had free access to water, and food was provided twice daily. Their mean ± SD age and weight were 3.6 ± 1.3 years and 11.9 ± 2.2 kg, respectively. Physical examination, auditory function, complete blood count and serum biochemical analyses were within normal limits before the commencement of the experimental study. Auditory function was assessed by otoscopic examination of the external ear and tympanum radiography of the tympanic bullae, neurologic examination and clinical observation of the response to a sound stimulus outside the animal’s visual field. Food was withheld for 8 h and water for 2 h before each experiment.

Study design

We generated 20 sets of three numbers ranging from 1 to 3, where each number corresponded to one of the different treatments (1-Chopin music, 2-Mozart music, 3-no music), using an online randomization tool (research randomizer, http://www.randomizer.org). Depending on the number (1, 2 or 3), we employed the respective treatment to randomize the order in which the same 20 dogs received the three treatments. Special effort was made to minimize dogs’ stress before and during the experimental procedure (standardized daily routine, housing conditions and handling) because acute and chronic stress factors seem to be able to modify animals’ response to pain31. All dogs were unfamiliar with music listening as part of their daily routine and were free of acute or chronic inflammatory conditions that could potentially affect SP serum concentrations37,38. Furthermore, none of the dogs included in the study received any sedatives or analgesics during the previous 2 months. The experiments were strictly set with a 3 month gap to ensure both uniformity and bias control, to achieve anaesthetic washout from the previous procedures and return of SP values to baseline, i.e., those of a healthy dog.

In this study, three different types of acoustic stimuli were applied to the same 20 dogs, and a total of 60 experiments were performed. In each of the three treatments, a different type of musical stimulus was applied preoperatively (via loudspeakers) and intraoperatively (via headphones). Music always stopped just before the induction of anaesthesia by an individual not related to the study, to ensure the anaesthetist’s blinding to the treatment and was re-initiated after the application of the headphones. In the first treatment, the dogs listened to Chopin music (Nocturne in D-flat major, Op. 27, No 2-lento sostenuto) (CM), in the second to Mozart music (Sonata for two pianos in D-major, K.448, andante) (MM), and the third was the no-music control treatment (NM), in which the whole process was performed without music. Intraoperative music was provided by headphones covering the whole ear, whether the dogs were exposed to music or not, and both the anaesthetist and the surgeon were unaware of the type of acoustic stimulus, to ensure blinding. The volume of the stimulus was set at 65 dB, as confirmed by the use of a decibel meter, and remained stable during the whole process, while playback of the musical stimulus was continuous.

Procedure (anesthesia and nociceptive stimuli)

Dogs were premedicated with 0.03 mg/kg acepromazine (Acepromazine, Alfasan, Netherlands), 0.1 mg/kg butorphanol (Dolorex, MSD, Netherlands), intramuscularly (IM), and 0.1 mg/kg meloxicam (Metacam, Boehringer Ingelheim, Germany) subcutaneously (SC). Forty-five minutes later, the cephalic vein was catheterized and lactated Ringer’s solution was administered at a rate of 5 ml/kg/h. Oxygen at 4 L/min was supplied via a face mask before and during induction of anaesthesia with intravenous (IV) propofol (Propofol, Fresenius Kabi, Greece) increments until tracheal intubation, and the endotracheal tube was connected to a circle anaesthetic circuit for maintenance of anaesthesia with a mixture of isoflurane (Isoflo, Abbott, UK) in oxygen. Afterwards, 20 mg/kg cefuroxime (Zinacef, GlaxoSmithKline, Greece) was administered IV, the bispectral index (BIS) was adjusted and headphones were placed to supply the music during the intraoperative period. SpO2, heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), noninvasive arterial (systolic, mean and diastolic) blood pressure (BP), BIS, partial pressure of expired carbon dioxide (PECO2), inspired and expired isoflurane concentrations, and rectal temperature were recorded intraoperatively every 5 min until the end of anaesthesia using a multiparametric monitor (Datex Ohmeda S/5, Datex-Ohmeda Division, Instrumentarium Corp, Finland). The temperature was strictly maintained between 37 °C and 38.2 °C by means of a warm water circulating mattress pad, whenever needed. Hypothermia may have a direct effect on EEG cerebral function, so it seems that it can directly influence the BIS value62,63.

BIS assessment and standard anaesthetic monitoring (neurological assessment) were both used for the assessment of anaesthetic depth. Acute autonomic nervous system responses (acute or abrupt increases in HR, BP, RR) associated with surgical stimulation were used to monitor the adequacy of analgesia. Nociceptive stimuli included three consecutive identical interventions consisting of three skin biopsies and three incisions, each 3.5 cm long, and closure of the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the lateral thoracic area. This specific type of nociceptive stimulation was implemented because it was part of another study protocol on wound healing, as already mentioned. The initial vaporizer setting was 1.2% isoflurane in oxygen. The time at which the desired anaesthetic depth to begin the surgical procedure had been reached, was assessed by BIS along with neurological assessment (evaluation of the palpebral and corneal reflexes, relaxation of the jaw tone and eye position). The proper inspired isoflurane concentration for the initiation of the surgical procedure was adjusted by increasing or decreasing the vaporizer isoflurane setting, targeting the desired anaesthetic depth, i.e. the depth denoted by BIS values of ≈ 70, which corresponds to a light plane of anaesthesia, as has been proposed for dogs and cats, and not BIS values of 40–60 which correspond to a deep surgical plane of anaesthesia52. After achievement of the desired anaesthetic depth, steady BIS values (≈ 70) for 10 min, along with clinical signs of light-plane anaesthesia (present but reduced palpebral reflex, mild jaw tone and presence of corneal reflex), as has been proposed52, ensured that the anaesthetic depth was almost the same for all dogs and that the experimental conditions were appropriate for the application of the nociceptive stimulus. In our setting, the end-tidal isoflurane values corresponding to the desired anaesthetic depth for the initiation of the surgical procedure (BIS values ≈ 70) varied from 0.8 to 1%. Just before that, all the aforementioned parameters were recorded as baseline values, and by that time every alteration was strictly treated in exactly the same way. Specifically, whenever BIS values exceeded 75 or were lower than 40, or when the neurological assessment indicated alterations in the anaesthetic depth, the inspired isoflurane concentration increased or decreased by 0.2%, respectively. Any increase in physiologic parameters (HR, BP, RR) > 20% from baseline was considered indicative of inadequate analgesia, and fentanyl (fentanyl, Janssen–Cilag, Greece) 1 μg/kg IV was provided as rescue analgesia. In either case, application of the nociceptive stimulus ceased and was not re-applied until the altered parameter returned to baseline values. After a delay of 10 min, all parameters were reassessed and if all values were at baseline levels again, the type of intervention was recorded and the procedure was continued. If not, fentanyl was re-administered or a further increase in isoflurane was carried out (according to the respective assessments) and another delay of at least 10 min was allowed. If signs of emergence from anaesthesia (voluntary movements, swallowing, coughing) were noted, the experiment was terminated due to an unacceptable anaesthetic outcome.

At the end of anaesthesia, the total dose of fentanyl required was recorded, the BIS and headphones were removed, and the dog was allowed to recover while monitored. Buprenorphine (Bupaq, Neocell, Greece) 10 μg/kg IM was administered during recovery, and every 8 h for 2 days, while meloxicam 0.1 mg/kg SC was continued once a day for 5 days along with consecutive postoperative pain assessments.

Monitoring of the anaesthetic depth—BIS

The anaesthetic depth was assessed using the BIS, apart from the neurological assessment. Just before sensor attachment, each dog’s skull was shaved and the skin was assiduously cleaned with alcohol swabs for sebum removal, in order to ensure maximal impedance between the skin surface and the BIS sensor. The sensor used (Pediatric Four BIS Sensor, Aspect Medical Systems, USA) was disposable, attached to an appropriate monitor (Bispectral monitor A-2000 XP; Aspect Medical Systems Inc, USA) and placed in a frontal–temporal position. This type of positioning is an adaptation of what is recommended by the manufacturer for use in humans, as has already been proposed for dogs41.

Substance P

Three venous blood samples were collected from the jugular vein for SP measurement, during the perioperative period. The 1st blood sampling (T0) was performed before the initiation of each experiment (before exposure to music and administration of the premedication) and the result was considered the baseline value for each dog. The 2nd (T1) blood sample was collected approximately 10 min after the application of the nociceptive stimulus, while the 3rd (T2) blood sample was collected after the end of the surgical procedure (approximately 60 min after skin incision). The quantification of SP was performed using a 96-well strip plate ELISA kit (Parameter™ Substance P Assay KGE007 R&D Systems, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and results are expressed in pg/ml. The individual running the ELISA measurement procedure was blinded to each treatment and unaware of the study design.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of the study was based on another study protocol on wound healing that was performed simultaneously, as previously mentioned. Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD, while ordinal data are presented as median and range. To examine the fit to the normal distribution, we used the Shapiro–Wilk test, due to the small sample size (< 30 participants). To evaluate differences of surgery duration, anaesthesia duration and physiologic variables (HR, RR, BP) among treatments, we employed paired t-test. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the relationship between two continuous variables. The Kruskal Wallis test was used to compare the median values of isoflurane and fentanyl among the three treatments, followed by pairwise post-hoc comparisons using Wilcoxon tests with Bonferroni correction. To compare the SP values at three time points between music and no-music treatments we used two-way repeated-measures ANOVA. All tests were performed at the 5% level of significance.
