Biomechanical comparison of Mitchell versus Chevron osteotomy with the third generation minimally invasive screw fixation technique
Authors: Fa-Chuan Kuan, Chang-Hao Lin, Chi-Ming Huang, Wei-Ren Su, Chen-Hao Chiang, Sheng-Pin Lo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Mitchell and Chevron osteotomies are two widely performed surgical techniques for the correction of hallux valgus deformities. While both approaches are commonly used, there remains limited biomechanical evidence comparing their structural stability, particularly when paired with advanced fixation techniques. This study aims to evaluate and compare the biomechanical properties of these procedures when utilizing the third-generation minimally invasive (MIS) screw fixation technique.MethodsSixteen synthetic first metatarsal (Sawbones Pacific Research Laboratories, Vashon, WA) were divided into two groups. The Mitchell osteotomy (n = 8) and the Chevron osteotomy (n = 8) were performed to simulate hallux valgus correction surgery. Both osteotomy groups were fixed using an identical technique. Two parallel, fully threaded, headless screws were positioned separately: one in a three-points fixation and the other in an intramedullary pattern. Each specimen was tested using a cantilever bending model. Following a 20-N preload, 500 cycles loading test was applied with the force ranging from 20 to 200 N (valley to peak). Interfragmentary displacement was recorded after the cyclic loading test. Finally, all specimens were subjected to destructive loading until catastrophic failure occurred.ResultsIn terms of construct stiffness, there were no significant differences (p = 0.12). The value was 321.8 ± 91.4 N/mm in the Mitchell osteotomy group and 224.3 ± 95.2 N/mm in the Chevron osteotomy group. All specimens completed the whole cyclic loading test without catastrophic failure, and the fragment displacement after 500 cycles was 1.2 ± 0.7 mm for the Mitchell osteotomy and 1.9 ± 1.1 mm for the Chevron osteotomy group (p = 0.20). No significant differences were observed between the groups regarding the ultimate failure load and failure mode.ConclusionsThe third generation MIS screw fixation technique provides comparable construct stability in both Mitchell and Chevron osteotomies, particularly in conditions requiring substantial correction. The current study offers biomechanical evidence supporting its clinical application.

Background

Hallux valgus, commonly known as a bunion, is a prevalent deformity of the first metatarsophalangeal joint, characterized by lateral deviation of the hallux and medial deviation of the first metatarsal [1, 2]. The pathology often results in pain, functional impairment, and cosmetic concerns [3]. Surgical correction is frequently indicated for symptomatic cases, with osteotomies being a common intervention. In order to translate metatarsal head laterally to achieve adequate correction, a number of techniques including the Mitchell, Chevron or transverse osteotomy could be implemented depending on the surgeon preference [4–6]. Ensuring stable and reliable fixation after an osteotomy is essential for accelerating postoperative rehabilitation protocol and promoting bony union.

A large degree of deformity is typically observed in cases of severe hallux valgus. Limited translation of the metatarsal head can lead to an inadequate correction of intermetatarsal angle (IMA) and sesamoid position, resulting in poor clinical outcomes and a high rate of recurrence [7]. Therefore, more stable fixation at the osteotomy site is necessary when an excessive translation ratio is employed. Commonly used device including isolated and multiple Kirschner-wires, screws, and plates, have been utilized in previous literature [8, 9]. A biomechanical study comparing single screw fixation with two parallel K-wire fixation and found no significant difference in the stability of distal fragment following the Chevron osteotomy [10]. Additionally, the method of screw placement significantly influences the mechanical strength of the construct. The screw fixation technique, commonly performed with Chevron osteotomy as the third generation MIS fixation technique, has demonstrated satisfactory clinical outcomes in various studies [5, 11].

The Mitchell and Chevron osteotomies have been regarded as the most commonly used procedures over the past decades, each with its own proponents [12–15]. The plantar limb of the Chevron osteotomy offers greater inherent stability compared to the Mitchell osteotomy; however, it also limits the potential for pronation correction [16]. In contrast, Mitchell osteotomy allows greater lateral translation and correction of pronounced pronation, but is inherently less stable [12, 17]. To our knowledge, few studies have investigated the biomechanical characteristics of these two osteotomy techniques. Recent systematic reviews have summarized the outcomes and methodologies of various osteotomies for hallux valgus, highlighting the diversity of techniques and the ongoing need for biomechanical evaluation of different constructs [17]. Previous biomechanical studies have compared different osteotomy constructs in cadaveric models or via finite element analysis, but none specifically focused on third generation MIS screw fixation technique [18–21]. The purpose of this biomechanical study was to evaluate biomechanical properties of two commonly used procedure: The Mitchell and Chevron osteotomies with third generation MIS screw fixation technique.

Methods

A total of sixteen synthetic composite first metatarsal (fourth-generation, Sawbones, Pacific Research Laboratories, Vashon, WA, USA) were used in the biomechanical test. Previous studies have shown that composite bone is a viable option, as it reduces the variability between specimens that is often observed in cadaveric tissue [22]. The specimens were randomly assigned to Mitchell or Chevron distal metatarsal osteotomy cohorts. All the procedures were performed by a single foot and ankle fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon. To simulate the hallux valgus corrective procedure, a 7-mm micro-oscillating saw blade was utilized to osteotomize the bone at the metadiaphyseal junction. The Chevron osteotomy was performed with a 60°–70° apex angle, with the apex located at the metatarsal neck approximately 1 cm proximal to the center of the metatarsal head. The Mitchell osteotomy was likewise positioned at the metatarsal neck (1 cm proximal to the head center) using a step-cut geometry with a 2 mm thickness and a depth of 1/2 the metatarsal neck width. Following the cuts, the distal fragment was displaced laterally by one-half the width of the bone. After that, the distal fragment was displaced laterally until one-half the width of bone. The K-wire was applied to stabilize the correction and maintain the metatarsal head in the sagittal plane, the first guide pin was introduced from the base of medial cortex proximally to penetrate the lateral cortex of the metatarsal. Then the pin was advanced into the subchondral area of the metatarsal head. The second guide pin, starting from the medial cortex distal to the first pin, was introduced intramedullary into the metatarsal head. The correction and guide pins position were confirmed under fluoroscopy to be in thesatisfactory position. Subsequently, by using a 3.2-mm drill bit, the standard surgical procedure was conducted to prepare the screw hole, the length of which should be enough to capture the subchondral bone. Finally, two parallel 4.1-mm headless screws (Aplus, Taiwan) were placed alongside the guide pin to complete the third-generation MIS fixation construct (Fig. 1).

Biomechanical testing

The mechanical experiments were conducted in a materials-testing machine (AG-X; Shimadzu Corp., Tokyo, Japan). A magnetic tracking system (Polhemus, Colchester, VT, USA) capable of collecting the displacement at 240 Hz during the static and cyclic loading was utilized by using three 1.8 mm microsensors that were attached to the metatarsal head, and the diaphysis of the specimen respectively. To simulate the ground reaction and muscular force during the standing condition, the cantilever loading protocol described by Favre et al. was performed in present study [23]. Each specimen was rigidly mounted into a fabricated metal clamp to ensure the angle of 15°between the axis of the first metatarsal and a horizontal line (Fig. 2). Initially, a 20-N plantar-to dorsal preload was applied for 2 min. Second, the cyclic loading test was performed over 500 cycles at 1 Hz with a force between 20 and 200 N (valley/peak). The interfragmental displacement between the metatarsal head and shaft was recorded periodically at intervals of 100 cycles. Finally, while maintaining the same setup, each specimen was loaded with destructive force at the rate of 5 mm/min until the catastrophic failure occurred, which means a sudden discontinuity or decline in the load-displacement curve. The maximum load detected during this period was referred to as the ultimate failure load. The mode of failure was also recorded for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Following the pilot study, in which three specimens in each group was conducted, a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power. The effect size calculated from the preliminary results were 0.72 for construct stiffness and 0.59 for the ultimate failure load. To ensure a study power of 0.8 and an α value of 0.05, the sample size was set at 8 for each group.

The final data were analyzed using SPSS software (Version 25, IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Given the small sample size, we utilized the Mann-Whitney U test to assess the statistical significance of biomechanical parameters between the two experimental groups. As for failure mode, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare the distribution between the two groups.

Construct stiffness

The linear section of load-displacement curve at the first 20 to 200 N cycle was used to calculate the construct stiffness, which was 321.8 ± 91.4 N/mm in the Mitchell osteotomy group and 224.3 ± 95.2 N/mm in the Chevron osteotomy group (p = 0.12).

Cyclic displacement

The interfragmental displacement after 500 cycles loading test was no significantly difference between the Mitchell and Chevron osteotomy group (1.2 ± 0.7 mm vs. 1.9 ± 1.1 mm, p = 0.20).

Ultimate failure load

All the specimens completed the entire cyclic loading test without structural failure. Destructive loading was then applied to each specimen until catastrophic failure occurred, defined as a sudden discontinuity in the load-displacement curve. The maximum load detected on the load-displacement curve was defined as the ultimate failure load, which was 470.4 ± 98.7 N in the Mitchell osteotomy group and 433.9 ± 134.6 N in the Chevron osteotomy group (p = 0.35).

There were no significant differences in the failure modes between the two groups (p = 1.00). In the Mitchell osteotomy group, six specimens failed due to proximal cortex breakage, while two failed due to distal segment migration. Similarly, in the Chevron osteotomy group, five specimens exhibited failure due to proximal cortex breakage, and three failed due to distal segment migration.

Discussion

In this biomechanical study comparing two procedures for hallux valgus using the third generation MIS screw fixation technique, the results demonstrated that the Mitchell osteotomy is comparable to the Chevron osteotomy in terms of construct stiffness, cyclic displacement, and ultimate failure load. Additionally, all specimens withstood the entire 500-cycle test. Two failure modes including distal segment migration and cortical breakage were observed during the load-to-failure test. There were no significant differences in failure modes between the two experimental groups. Our results demonstrate that, under biomechanical testing conditions on synthetic bone, the Mitchell osteotomy fixed with third generation (MIS) screw fixation provides comparable construct stability to the Chevron osteotomy. These findings indicate that, based on biomechanical stability, either procedure can be selected; however, further clinical research is needed to provide clearer recommendations.

Previous studies have extensively investigated various fixation techniques for the Mitchell osteotomy, demonstrating favorable clinical outcomes. Ayoubi et al. evaluated 67 feet treated using the modified Mitchell technique with K-wire fixation. The study reported a significant improvement in AOFAS scores, along with a reduction in the hallux valgus angle (HVA) from 37.0° to 10.2° (p < 0.01) and the intermetatarsal angle (IMA) from 12.1° to 5.6° (p < 0.01) [24]. Another study using of multi-use compression (MUC) screws in the Mitchell osteotomy fixation also reported statistically significant improvements in the HVA, IMA, and AOFAS score when comparing to preoperative status [25]. However, all these studies have emphasized the necessity of postoperative protection lasting from 4 to 6 weeks, which indicates that there are still concerns about the stability of Mitchell osteotomy in the early postoperative stage. The present study provides evidence that utilizing two parallel fully threaded, headless compression screws—comprising one intramedullary and one three-points fixation screw —achieves biomechanical stability comparable to that of the Chevron osteotomy using the same fixation construct. This fixation technique achieved biomechanical stability comparable to that of the Chevron osteotomy with same fixation construct. As a result, this innovative approach has the potential to reduce the required postoperative protection period for patients undergoing Mitchell osteotomy for hallux valgus, thereby facilitating an earlier return to daily activities.

The recurrence of hallux valgus following surgery is closely associated with the postoperative position of the sesamoids. Okuda et al. found that incomplete reduction of the sesamoids position could be a significant risk factor for hallux valgus recurrence [26]. However, in cases of severe hallux valgus, achieving optimal sesamoid reduction typically requires significant lateral translation of the metatarsal (MT) head, which inevitably reduces bone contact at the osteotomy site. As a result, the stability of the fixation construct may be compromised, increasing the risk of complications such as nonunion or malunion. Finite element and biomechanical investigations have quantified how different osteotomy configurations and fixation strategies affect metatarsal stress distribution and fragment displacement, which are recognized contributors to hallux valgus recurrence, supporting the rationale for stable fixation observed in our study [21, 27]. A recent study comparing a novel h‑shaped bone plate with commercial endosteal plating systems demonstrated lower displacement and reduced screw pull‑out risk under multi-directional loading, highlighting the importance of construct stability in maintaining postoperative alignment [28]. In the present study, the amount of lateral translation was standardized to 50% to simulate a severe clinical scenario. Moreover, postoperative persistent metatarsal pronation is a recognized independent deformity recurrence factor [29]. Chen et al. published that first ray persistent pronation was associated with inferior outcomes and greater dissatisfaction [30]. However, the plantar limb of the Chevron osteotomy inherently limits its ability to correct pronation deformities. In contrast, Mitchell osteotomy offers the advantage of simultaneously correcting both valgus and pronation deformities. The present study confirms that the third generation MIS screw fixation construct provides similar biomechanical stability in both Mitchell and Chevron osteotomies in 50% lateral translation.

The cantilever bending model was selected in the present study to simulate the ground reaction force at the first metatarsal during the early postoperative phase. This protocol has been extensively applied in recent biomechanical research. Favre et al. compared three different osteotomy with single screw fixation. The biomechanical results demonstrated a failure strength of approximately 150 N following the Chevron osteotomy [23]. Another biomechanical study compared two fixation methods in Chevron osteotomy, reporting a failure strength of 187.2 N for the single-screw fixation group. Although failure strength is influenced by various factors, including bone density and loading protocols [31]. Our results demonstrated that the double-screw construct achieved significantly greater failure strength (433.9 ± 134.6 N) compared to values reported in previous studies on Chevron osteotomy. The findings suggest that the addition of a three-point screw effectively increases the fixation strength, facilitating an early weight-bearing strategy. The concept is also supported by a biomechanical study that demonstrated tricortical k-wire fixation is stronger than traditional unicortical K-wire fixation [32]. The failure mode is also a critical parameter in investigating the stability of the construct. In the present study, all specimens failed due to proximal cortex breakage or distal segment displacement. The cortex breakage originated at the screw insertion site and extended dorsally (Fig. 3). There are no significant differences in the failure mode between the two experimental groups (p = 1.00).

There are several limitations of the current study. First, composite bone was used as the testing material in the experimental setup instead of cadaveric bone, which would more accurately simulate an in vivo scenario. However, the inherent osteoporotic nature of cadaveric bones and the variability in bone density could potentially confound the data analysis. Second, this is an in vitro biomechanical study; therefore, only time-zero mechanical properties were evaluated. Postoperative factors, such as the immobilization period and the healing process, were not considered. Third, this study was conducted on synthetic bone models, which do not include soft tissues, ligaments, or muscle tension, and therefore cannot fully replicate in vivo conditions. Not all force components were included in the study. For instance, axial loading and torsion tests were not conducted. Last, the other fixation methods such as plates or k-wires were not included in the current study. Additionally, the sample size was relatively small. Although the sample size was determined via a priori power analysis and effect sizes were calculated to provide insight into potential trends, the small number of specimens may limit the ability to detect subtle differences and correlations. Larger studies would be required to confirm these findings. Given that the goal of this study is to compare three-point screw construct, including too many groups in one study would lead to obscure and confusing results.

Conclusions

These findings indicate that Mitchell osteotomy is not inherently inferior when modern MIS screw fixation is applied, and that the fixation construct, rather than osteotomy geometry alone, determines stability in cases requiring substantial correction. These results challenge the traditional assumption that Chevron osteotomy is biomechanically superior solely due to its plantar limb. In summary, the third generation MIS screw fixation technique provides comparable construct stability in both Mitchell and Chevron osteotomies, particularly in conditions requiring substantial correction. The current study offers biomechanical evidence supporting its clinical application.
