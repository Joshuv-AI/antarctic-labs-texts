Inhibition of NCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferritinophagy attenuates ferroptosis in PDLCs and alleviates orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption
Authors: Liyan Liu, Dongyang Li, Zhe Zhou, Hong Ding, Runze Zhang, Xueyuan Han, Kexin Huang, Chunmiao Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDisruption of iron homeostasis is closely associated with ferroptosis and inflammation-related diseases. Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4)-mediated ferritinophagy plays a central role in regulating intracellular iron levels and inducing ferroptosis. However, its involvement in orthodontically induced inflammatory root resorption (OIIRR) remains largely unexplored.Methods In vitro, a compressive force (CF) loading model was established using human periodontal ligament cells (hPDLCs). Western blotting and immunofluorescence staining were performed to detect the expression of ferroptosis-related proteins, including NCOA4, FTH1, and GPX4. Intracellular levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), Fe²⁺, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) were measured using a ferroptosis assay kit. JC-1 staining was used to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. NCOA4 was silenced via lentiviral transfection, and the small-molecule inhibitor 9a was used to further assess its functional role in ferroptosis. In vivo, an OIIRR mouse model was established. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining, and micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) were used to assess root resorption. The expression of NCOA4, FTH1, and GPX4 in periodontal tissues was evaluated via immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence. Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a ferroptosis inhibitor, was administered intraperitoneally to investigate its therapeutic effect on OIIRR.ResultsCF and IL-1β stimulation induced ferroptosis in hPDLCs, as indicated by increased intracellular Fe²⁺, ROS, and MDA levels, decreased GPX4 expression, and mitochondrial damage. NCOA4 expression was markedly upregulated in both in vitro and in vivo models, leading to ferritin degradation and iron overload. Fer-1 treatment effectively reduced oxidative stress and iron accumulation in vitro and alleviated OIIRR in vivo. Compound 9a suppressed NCOA4 expression and mitigated ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis.ConclusionsNCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferritinophagy promotes ferroptosis under compressive and inflammatory conditions, contributing to OIIRR pathogenesis. Targeting the NCOA4/FTH1 axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for preventing orthodontically induced root resorption.Graphical abstract Keywords: Ferritinophagy, NCOA4, Ferroptosis, Root resorption, Compressive force, IL-1β

Introduction

Orthodontically induced root resorption (OIIRR) is a common complication induced by excessive mechanical stress during orthodontic treatment and has a high incidence ranging from 5% to 100% [1]. Studies suggest that mild root resorption occurs in most orthodontic patients, while severe cases affecting more than 4 mm of root length are observed in 1−5% of patients [2, 3]. The etiology of OIIRR includes the magnitude of the forces, treatment duration, and individual susceptibility. Severe OIIRR may lead to tooth mobility, reduced masticatory function, and even loss of tooth, affecting the patient’s quality of life. Several studies have reported the underlying mechanisms of inflammatory factors and immune cells interacting with cementoblasts, periodontal ligament cells (PDLCs), osteoblasts, and osteoclasts, leading to root and alveolar bone resorption [4–6]. However, the pathogenesis of OIIRR remains unclear, particularly regarding the detailed mechanisms of how compressive force (CF) affects PDLCs, which are the first cell line to suffer mechanical stimulation. Moreover, the periodontal tissues in OIIRR are characterized by a persistent inflammatory environment, which further contributes to the complexity of OIIRR pathogenesis.

Excessive orthodontic force and inflammatory stimulation create a unique microenvironment in the periodontal tissue in OIIRR, in which PDLCs and cementoblasts are subjected to stress-induced damage. Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, leading to oxidative stress-induced membrane damage [7]. Irons are essential for ferroptosis as they participate in the Fenton reaction, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that drive lipid peroxidation. Ferritinophagy regulates intracellular iron homeostasis by degrading ferritin and releasing free iron into the labile iron pool [8]. This process increases iron availability for the Fenton reaction, promoting oxidative stress and contributing to ferroptosis. It has been reported that iron is essential for the differentiation of human PDLCs (hPDLCs), and iron overload has a direct effect on osteoblast activity, leading to osteoporosis and osteopenia [9]. Whether ferritinophagy-induced ferroptosis is observed in hPDLCs under CF and inflammatory stimulation, there is still no publication.

Nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4) functions as a selective cargo receptor in ferritinophagy by binding to ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) and directly to lysosomes for degradation, leading to the release of free iron [10]. This process plays a critical role in maintaining cellular iron homeostasis. However, excessive ferritinophagy leads to the release of large amounts of Fe2+ and the generation of ROS, which in turn triggers lipid peroxidation, causing oxidative damage and ferroptosis [11]. In Parkinson’s neurodegenerative diseases, excessive NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy contributes to iron accumulation and oxidative stress, exacerbating neuronal damage [12]. Additionally, studies have shown that ferritinophagy has been reported to be involved in the pathogenesis of some inflammatory diseases in the oral-facial area, such as temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis and periodontitis [13, 14]. However, the role of NCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in the progression of OIIRR has not been reported.

Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) is a potent lipophilic antioxidant that specifically inhibits ferroptosis by preventing lipid peroxidation. It scavenges ROS and blocks the oxidative degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids in cell membranes. By reducing oxidative stress and preserving membrane integrity, Fer-1 protects cells from ferroptosis in various diseases [15, 16]. It has been reported that Fer-1 inhibited ferroptosis in human gingival fibroblasts exposed to Porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide, thereby reducing inflammation and oxidative stress associated with periodontitis [17]. Additionally, studies indicate that Fer-1 mitigates chondrocyte ferroptosis in rat temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis models, suggesting its potential therapeutic role in inflammation [18]. However, its effects on OIIRR, particularly in the context of NCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis remain unexplored.

Methods

Cell culture

Human PDLCs were isolated from the extracted healthy premolars of volunteers (12–18 years old) receiving orthodontic treatment with informed consent. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (Approval No. QYFYWALL29622). Periodontal tissues were collected from the middle third of the root surface and transferred to a culture dish containing 2 mg/mL collagen type I for digestion for 30 min. Subsequently, the tissues were cultured in α-MEM culture medium (PM150421, Procell, China) supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (164210-50, Procell, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PB180120, Procell, China). Cells from passages 3 to 6 were used for the subsequent experiments.

Mice OIIRR model establishment

Fifteen male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old, specific pathogen-free) were purchased from Jinan Pengyue Animal Center (Jinan, China) and randomly assigned to three groups: control group, compressive force group, and compressive force + Fer-1 group, with 5 mice per group. All mice were sedated via intraperitoneal injection of 10% chloral hydrate (4 mL/kg) and positioned in a supine orientation on the operating table. For the mice OIIRR model, one end of a nickel-titanium spring (wire size, 0.15 mm; diameter, 1.4 mm; Chengdu Mingxing Spring Co., Ltd) was attached to the cervical area of the right upper first molar using a 0.1 mm ligature wire, and the other end was fixed to the cervical region of the central incisor. Fluid resin was used to secure the ligature wires. The spring applied approximately 50 g of force. The mice in the control group were not subjected to mechanical force. Mice in the compressive force + Fer-1 group received intraperitoneal injections of 5 mg/kg Fer-1 (HY-100579, MedChemExpress, USA) every 2 days. After 7 days, the mice were euthanized for subsequent analysis. All procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao University (Approval No. 20240401C573320240430181).

Micro-CT scanning

To observe the changes in root resorption, the samples were scanned by micro-CT (Quantum GX2, PerkinElmer, Japan) at 90 kV and 80 mA with a voxel resolution of 10 μm. Micro-CT scanning data were transferred to Mimics 21.0 (Materialize, Leuven, Belgium) for 3D image reconstruction. Based on the grayscale differences among various tissues, threshold segmentation was performed on the maxillary first molar and the segmentation results were saved as masks. After manual segmentation, modification, and editing, an accurate segmentation mask of the first molar was obtained. The root was segmented at the enamel-cementum junction, and the root volume was measured using 3D Properties. The volume of root resorption is defined as the difference between the volume of the mesial root of the right maxillary first molar in the control group and that in the OIIRR group.

Histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The mice maxillae were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, followed by decalcification in 10% EDTA (pH 7.4) at room temperature for 28 days. After decalcification, the specimens were embedded in paraffin and sectioned into serial sagittal slices at a thickness of 5 μm for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the maxillary first molars. For IHC staining, the paraffin sections were dewaxed in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treatment with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 25 min, followed by blocking with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at room temperature. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GPX4 (1:200, ab125066, Abcam, USA), NCOA4 (1:800, A5695, ABclonal, China), FTH1 (1:800, A1144, ABclonal, China), and LC3 (1:1000, A17424, ABclonal, China). After rinsing, the sections were incubated with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG for 1 h, followed by color development using diaminobenzidine (DAB) for 2–3 min. Hematoxylin was used for nuclear counterstaining. Images were captured using a Panoramic Tissue Cell Scanning Analyzer (Model: 2022044000, 3DHISTECH, Hungary).

Tartrate-resistant acid phosphate (TRAP) staining

TRAP staining was performed on tissue sections from different experimental groups in vivo using a commercial TRAP staining kit (G1050-50T, Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Stained sections were imaged with a Panoramic Tissue Cell Scanning Analyzer. Osteoclasts were identified as TRAP-positive multinucleated cells containing three or more nuclei. For quantitative analysis, three random fields were selected from each group for observation and cell counting.

In vitro compressive force loading model

Human PDLCs were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured until reaching 80% confluence. To apply the compressive force to the hPDLCs, a glass layer was placed on the cells, and a container with weighted metal balls was used to adjust the magnitude of the compressive force by varying the number of metal balls. Additionally, 20 ng/mL of IL-1β (HY-P7028, MedChemExpress, USA) was administered to the hPDLCs under a 2 g/cm² compressive force for 24 h. Ferroptosis and ferritinophagy were inhibited in vitro using Fer-1 (1µM; HY-100579, MedChemExpress, USA) and 9a (0.5µM; HY-149457, MedChemExpress, USA), respectively.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from the cell samples after compressive force and IL-1β stimulation. A BCA protein assay kit was used to determine the protein concentration and equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% DAS-PAGE and transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies NCOA4 (1:800; A5695, ABclonal, China), FTH1 (1:800; A1144, ABclonal, China), LC3 (1:1000; A17424, ABclonal, China), GPX4 (1:5000; ab125066, Abcam, USA), and GAPDH (1:30,000; AN004430L, Elabscience, China). After washing with TBST three times, the membrane was then incubated with a secondary antibody (1:3000; E-AB-1003; Elabscience, China) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein detection was carried out using a highly sensitive ECL kit (E-IR-R307, Elabscience, China), and semi-quantitative analysis of protein levels was performed using ImageJ software.

Detection of intracellular ROS

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (G1706, Servicebio, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, hPDLCs were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After removing the culture medium, the cells were washed with PBS three times and incubated with DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Then cells were washed with PBS and visualized under fluorescence microscope (G1706, Servicebio, China).

Mitochondrial membrane potential measurement

The mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using JC-1 staining probe (E-CK-A30, Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. hPDLCs were incubated with the JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 20 min. The cells were then washed three times with buffer solution. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope. In this analysis, green fluorescence indicates a reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, while red fluorescence indicates a normal mitochondrial membrane potential. The fluorescence density was quantified as the red/green ratio and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Determination of iron content

The treated hPDLCs were homogenized and centrifuged at 4 °C, 10,000 g, 15 min. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation and iron levels were measured using an iron assay kit (G4301, Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) determination

MDA concentration was assessed using an MDA assay kit (E-BC-K028-M, Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MDA in the sample reacts with thiobarbituric acid (TBA) to form MDA-TBA adducts. The fluorescence of the MDA-TBA adducts was quantified by measuring excitation at 532 nm and emission at 533 nm.

Immunofluorescence staining

After stimulation, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (T8787, Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin dissolved in TBST for 1 h. Primary antibodies were used as follows: FTH1 (1:100, A1144, rabbit pAb, ABclonal, China), LC3 (1:300, A17424, mouse mAb, ABclonal, China), and NCOA4 (1:100, A5695, rabbit pAb, ABclonal, China). The cells were then incubated with secondary antibodies: goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (1:800, ab150077, Abcam, USA), goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 647) (1:800, ab150079, Abcam, USA) and goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor® 488) (1:800, ab150113, Abcam, USA). After incubation, the cells were washed and stained with DAPI (P0131, Beyotime) for 5 min. Finally, slides were examined using a confocal microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

SiRNA transfection

The lentiviral vector (GV112) was purchased from Jikai Biotechnology CO., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Transfection with the viral vector was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with the lentiviral blank vector transfection group (LV-miR-NC) serving as the control. The target sequence for LV-shNCOA4 was siRNA: GGTATTGTAGCTGTCCCTTTC. The sequence for the siRNA targeting negative control was TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACGT. Knockdown efficiency was assessed by qRT-PCR and western blot analysis 48 h after transfection.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA-easy Isolation Reagent (R701, Vazyme, China), and mRNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT kit (AG11701, Accurate Biology, China). RT-PCR amplification was carried out using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS (AG11701, Accurate Biology, China). The PCR primers were purchased from Shenggong, China. The primer sequences used were as follows: human NCOA primers, forward 5′-TCAGCGAATGGCTCCTTGGAA-3′ and reverse 5′- GCAGGCTCTGGAAGAAGTCTGA-3′; human GAPDH primers, forward 5′-TCAGCAATGCCTCCTGCAC-3′ and reverse 5′-CCAGGAAATGAGCTTGACAAA-3′.

Cell viability analysis

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay kit (CX001M, Epizyme Biotech, China). Briefly, after stimulation, hPDLCs were seeded in 96-well plates with 100 µL of medium per well, and four replicates were used for each condition. Then, the cells were incubated with 10% CCK-8 reagent for 2 h in a dark room. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax Plus 384, Molecular Devices, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.2. The results are presented as the mean ± SD from multiple independent experiments, each performed in triplicate. A two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between two groups, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Fer-1 prevents OIIRR in the mice

Fer-1 is a common inhibitor of ferroptosis. To investigate its role in OIIRR, we established a mice model of OIIRR (Fig. 1A) and administered Fer-1 via intraperitoneal injection to observe its effects on OIIRR (Fig. 1B). Micro-CT analysis showed that compared to the control group (Con), the compressive force group (CF) exhibited obvious resorption pits on the root surface, with a significant increase in root resorption volume (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Intraperitoneal injection of Fer-1 (CF + Fer-1) reduced both the number and volume of these resorption pits(P < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). H&E staining showed that the cementum on the compressive side of the root in the CF group exhibited prominent resorption pits compared to the Con group (Fig. 1D). After intraperitoneal injection of Fer-1, the irregularities in the cementum on the root surface were reduced, and OIIRR was alleviated (Fig. 1D). This suggested that Fer-1 prevents OIIRR in the mice.

To assess whether Fer-1 influences osteoclast formation during the alleviation of OIIRR, we performed TRAP staining on tissue sections from different groups. The results showed that the number of osteoclasts near the tooth root resorption sites was significantly increased in the CF group compared to the Con group. Moreover, after Fer-1 treatment, the number of osteoclasts in the compressed cementum region decreased significantly, with most osteoclasts being localized to the compressed alveolar bone region (Fig. 1E). This suggests that Fer-1 inhibits osteoclast production during OIIRR in mice. Additionally, we also assessed the expression level of inflammatory cytokine IL-1β in the periodontal tissue on the compressive side of the mice OIIRR model. The results demonstrated that IL-1β was significantly higher in the CF group compared to that in the Con group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1F), and Fer-1 could significantly reduce IL-1β expression in the CF + Fer-1 group compared to the CF group (P < 0.01). This indicated that IL-1β plays a crucial role in the inflammatory microenvironment on the compressive side of OIIRR.

CF and IL-1β induce ferroptosis in hPDLCs

Western blot analysis showed a time-dependent decrease in GPX4 protein levels, a well-established marker of ferroptosis, at 0, 6, 12 and 24 h following 2 g/cm2 compressive force stimulation (Fig. 2A). The expression of GPX4 was significantly decreased in 24 h group compared to 0 h group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 2A). Given the significant upregulation of IL-1β expression in the periodontal tissue on the compressive side in mice OIIRR model, we employed CF and IL-1β as in vitro stimulatory conditions in experiments in vitro. Western blot analysis revealed a remarkable reduction in GPX4 protein levels in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β co-stimulation, compared to the control group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Additionally, the reduction in GPX4 expression was more pronounced under co-stimulation with CF and IL-1β than under either stimulus alone (Fig. 2B).

To further confirm the induction of ferroptosis, we assessed the expression levels of intracellular ROS, mitochondrial membrane potential, Fe²+, and MDA in hPDLCs under various treatment conditions. The results demonstrated that CF and IL-1β co-stimulation significantly promoted the production of intracellular ROS (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C), the accumulation of Fe²⁺ (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2E), as well as an increase in lipid peroxidation, as indicated by elevated MDA levels (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2F) compared to the control group. Notably, these changes were more pronounced compared to either CF or IL-1β treatment alone. Additionally, the reduction of mitochondrial membrane potential in the CF + IL-1β group was more significant than that in the Con group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2D). Especially, JC-1 fluorescence shifted from red, indicative of high membrane potential (JC-1 aggregates), to green, indicative of low membrane potential (JC-1 monomers) (Fig. 2D). In summary, these findings suggest that CF and IL-1β stimulation induced ferroptosis in hPDLCs.

Fer-1 inhibits ferroptosis of hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation

To further identify the occurrence of ferroptosis in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation, the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 was incorporated into our in vitro study. Western blot analysis showed a significant increase in GPX4 expression following Fer-1 treatment compared to the untreated group (Fig. 3A), indicating that Fer-1 exerts a protective effect against CF and IL-1β-induced ferroptosis. Moreover, the levels of Fe2+ and MDA in the Fer-1-treated group were notably lower than those in the untreated group (Fig. 3B, C). Compared to the CF + IL-1β group, Fer-1 significantly reduced Fe2+ and MDA levels in the CF + IL-1β + Fer-1 group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3B, C). Similarly, the intracellular ROS levels in the Fer-1-treated group were markedly lower than those in the untreated group, and the fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these findings suggest that ferroptosis is induced under CF and IL-1β stimulation in hPDLCs.

CF and IL-1β induce ferritinophagy in hPDLCs

It has been reported that enhanced ferritinophagy facilitates ferritin degradation, thereby increasing intracellular iron levels, which promotes the Fenton reaction and exacerbates ferroptosis. In our study, we observed that NCOA4 protein expression increased progressively with the duration of CF stimulation, peaking at 6 h before slightly decreasing. After applying CF for 24 h, NCOA4 expression was significantly higher than that in the 0 h control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4A). In contrast, FTH1 protein levels decreased gradually with prolonged CF stimulation, reaching their lowest point at 12 h time point, followed by a slight increase. Notably, FTH1 expression level in 24 h group was significantly lower than that in the 0 h control group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4A). Additionally, the temporal expression patterns of NCOA4 and FTH1 in response to CF stimulation are shown in Fig. 4B. Moreover, intracellular Fe²⁺ progressively increased with the duration of CF stimulation (Fig. 4D), suggesting the accumulation of Fe²⁺ in hPDLCs. We also evaluated the expression of autophagy-related proteins LC3 and P62 using Western blot analysis and found that LC3 expression increased gradually over time with extended CF stimulation, while P62 expression decreased (Fig. 4C). Compared to the 0 h group, LC3 and P62 expression levels were significantly changed in 24 h group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4C).

Subsequently, immunofluorescence staining revealed that NCOA4 was predominantly localized in the nucleus of hPDLCs, and its expression was markedly upregulated under combined CF and IL-1β stimulation compared to untreated group (Con) (Fig. 4E). Western blot analysis further confirmed a significant increase in NCOA4 protein levels in CF + IL-1β group relative to the Con group (P < 0.001), while the expression of FTH1 protein was significantly decreased under the same conditions (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4F). Additionally, dual immunofluorescence staining of LC3 and FTH1 demonstrated a marked increase in LC3 expression in nucleus and cytoplasm of hPDLCs and a significant reduction of FTH1 levels under CF and IL-1β co-stimulation compared to non-treated group (Con) (Fig. 4G).

NCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferritinophagy promotes ferroptosis in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation

In our study, we have revealed that CF and IL-1β can induce ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in hPDLCs. To further investigate the effect and underlying mechanism of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation, we employed LV-shNCOA4 to knockdown NCOA4 expression in hPDLCs. After NCOA4-knockdown, both mRNA and protein levels of NCOA4 in hPDLCs were significantly reduced by qRT-PCR and Western blot analysis (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5A, B). Western blot analysis showed that NCOA4 knockdown attenuated CF and IL-1β-induced degradation of FTH1 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5B). Additionally, NCOA4 knockdown significantly increased the levels of the ferroptosis-negative regulatory protein GPX4 (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5B). These results indicate that NCOA4 is involved in ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation.

To further verify this hypothesis, we assessed the changes in ROS, Fe²⁺, and MDA in hPDLCs in different groups, and found that NCOA4 knockdown attenuated the CF and IL-1β-induced upregulation of these ferroptosis-associated indicators (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5C, D, F). Furthermore, mitochondrial membrane potential was also tested in different groups. The reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential caused by CF and IL-1β was partially restored following NCOA4 knockdown (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5E). From the results above, NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy plays a pivotal role in ferroptosis in CF and IL-1β-stimulated hPDLCs.

9a inhibits ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation

9a is a novel inhibitor of ferritinophagy by disrupting the binding of NCOA4 to FTH1 [19]. CCK-8 assays showed that the cell viability of hPDLCs was markedly decreased under CF and IL-1β stimulation (P < 0.001), and 9a treatment rescued the proliferative capacity of hPDLCs (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6A). We hypothesize that 9a mitigates ferroptosis by modulating ferritinophagy. To test this hypothesis, we assessed the protein levels of NCOA4, FTH1 and GPX4, and found that 9a significantly reversed the upregulation of NCOA4 and the downregulation of FTH1 under CF and IL-1β stimulation (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6B). However, the changes of GPX4 were not statistically significant upon treatment with 9a (P >0.05). Additionally, we evaluated ROS, Fe²⁺, and MDA levels in hPDLCs, and found that 9a significantly reduced the upregulation of these ferroptosis indicators upon CF and IL-1β stimulation (Fig. 6C, D, F). Moreover, mitochondrial membrane potential was significantly higher in the 9a treatment group compared to that in the CF and IL-1β stimulation group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these findings suggest that 9a inhibits ferritinophagy by blocking NCOA4/FTH1 interactions, thereby alleviating CF and IL-1β-induced ferroptosis in hPDLCs.

Fer-1 inhibits ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis in mice OIIRR

To further identify the therapeutic effect and underlying mechanism of Fer-1 on OIIRR induced by excessive compressive force in vivo, we tested the expression levels of NCOA4, FTH1, LC3 and GPX4 by immunohistochemical staining. The results showed that GPX4 and FTH1 levels were significantly downregulated in the periodontal tissues in the CF group compared to the control group (Con) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7A, B, D), while NCOA4 and LC3 were markedly upregulated in the CF group (P < 0.001) (Fig. 7A, C, E). Following treatment with Fer-1, a significant increase in the expression of GPX4 and FTH1 was observed (Fig. 7A, B, D), and a marked decrease in the expression of NCOA4 and LC3 (P < 0.05) (Fig. 7A, C, E). Additionally, immunofluorescence double staining for the ferritinophagy marker NCOA4 and the ferroptosis marker GPX4 revealed that NCOA4 and GPX4 mainly localized in the cytoplasm of hPDLCs, and NCOA4 expression was upregulated, while GPX4 expression was downregulated in the periodontal tissues in the CF group compared to the Con group (Fig. 7F). Moreover, these changes were reversed following Fer-1 treatment. These results suggest that Fer-1 partially inhibits ferritinophagy-mediated ferroptosis in the compressive side of the periodontal ligament in the mice OIIRR model. Consequently, the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 exerts a therapeutic effect on CF-induced OIIRR.

Discussion

During orthodontic tooth movement (OTM), the application of mechanical force induces PDLCs to produce inflammatory mediators within the periodontal ligament (PDL) and regulate the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts, leading to bone remodeling. In a rat OIIRR model, the inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-1α, IL-1β, and TNF-α, were significantly upregulated around resorptive areas of the root, contributing to the occurrence of OIIRR [20]. Our previous study demonstrated that during the early stages of OIIRR, the secretion of IL-1β is significantly increased in PDLCs under excessive compressive force in rats, contributing to intensified sterile inflammation in periodontal tissues and consequently the recruitment of osteoclasts [4]. In order to investigate the detailed mechanism and therapeutic strategy of OIIRR, an in vitro compressive force loading model was established, in which CF and IL-1β stimulation were exerted on the hPDLCs. We found that NCOA4/FTH1 signaling pathway mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β co-stimulation in vitro, and 9a and Fer-1 could significantly inhibit the activated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. Additionally, Fer-1 markedly attenuates root resorption in the mice OIIRR model. We first identified the new mechanism that NCOA4/FTH1 mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in OIIRR and the therapeutic effects of Fer-1.

Autophagy is a cellular process in which damaged organelles and proteins are degraded via lysosomes under conditions of stress and energy deprivation, thereby contributing to the maintenance of cellular homeostasis. Ferritinophagy, a specialized form of autophagy, is primarily mediated by the selective autophagy receptor NCOA4, which delivers ferritin complexes to the lysosome for degradation and releases free iron to regulate intracellular iron homeostasis [21, 22]. Our results demonstrated that NCOA4 expression in hPDLCs gradually increased with prolonged compressive force loading time, reached a peak at 6 h, and then declined, reflecting the dynamic activation of ferritinophagy. However, the overall level of autophagy, as indicated by LC3 and p62, increased progressively in a time-dependent manner (0, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h). Gao et al. reported that autophagy contributes to ferroptosis, and although the degree of autophagy activation appeared relatively modest, ferritinophagy was likely preferentially activated [23]. These evidences suggest that the dynamics of the overall autophagy and selective ferritinophagy are not fully synchronized in their activation. When ferritinophagy is excessively activated, elevated levels of free iron promote ROS production through the Fenton reaction. If the antioxidant defense system fails to counteract the oxidative stress, ferroptosis is triggered [24, 25]. Therefore, autophagy, ferritinophagy, and ferroptosis are closely interconnected: autophagy provides the mechanistic framework, ferritinophagy acts as a bridge linking iron metabolism, and ferroptosis represents the pathological outcome of uncontrolled iron homeostasis and lipid peroxidation. In our study, we observed that ferroptosis was more pronounced in hPDLCs at the 24 h time point following CF and IL-1β stimulation (Fig. 2A), while ferritinophagy was more significant at 6 h time point under the stimulation (Fig. 4A). Notably, ferroptosis was less evident at 6 h, leading us to select the 24 h time point as the optimal time frame for assessing ferroptosis and ferritinophagy in this model. Previous studies have shown that knockdown of NCOA4 suppresses ferritinophagy, reduces intracellular Fe²⁺ levels, and thereby inhibits lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis; conversely, NCOA4 overexpression enhances cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis [26, 27]. NCOA4 overexpression can enhance the sensitivity of cells to ferroptosis, which is mainly reflected in two aspects: on the one hand, the large amount of Fe2+ produced by ferritinophagy increases oxidative stress; on the other hand, the degradation of antioxidants such as GPX4 greatly weakens cellular antioxidant capacity. Therefore, NCOA4 is considered a key regulator in maintaining the balance of intracellular and systemic iron levels [28, 29]. Ferritinophagy has also been shown to be strongly associated with inflammatory diseases such as periodontitis [14, 30], cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury [31], neurodegenerative diseases [32], and osteoarthritis [13]. However, there is no literature on the relationship between ferritinophagy and OIIRR progression. Our study first reported the involvement of NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy in OIIRR in vivo.

Ferritin is a spherical protein made up of 24 subunits which are made up of heavy chains (FTH) and light chains (FTL) and are the main way iron is stored in cells. FTH1 is an important subunit of ferritin [33]. Studies have shown that NCOA4 and FTH1 interact with each other, with NCOA4 directly recognizing and binding FTH1 and transporting ferritin to autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation and iron release [19]. The release of iron leads to an increase in intracellular free iron levels, which induces ferritinophagy and ferroptosis. We also found that the protein level of FTH1 decreased with time at 3, 6, and 12 h of stimulation, while increasing slightly at 24 h, possibly because elevated intracellular iron levels induced an increase in FTH1 [23]. NCOA4 controls the transfer of ferritin to lysosomes via autophagosomes and leads to ferritinophagy and ferroptosis, and NCOA4 depletion can block the colocalization of ferritin with lysosomes and leads to a diffusion localization pattern [22]. Thus, disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction through reducing NCOA4 expression or increasing intracellular FTH1 levels can inhibit ferritinophagy.

Benzimidazole derivatives have been extensively studied and are known to exhibit a wide range of biological activities, including antibacterial, antiviral, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, anti-tuberculosis, and other pharmacological effects [34, 35]. Notably, previous studies have demonstrated that certain benzimidazole derivatives can inhibit glutamate-induced ferroptosis in HT22 mouse hippocampal cells by targeting the system xc⁻ antiporter [36]. 9a is a novel ferroptosis-inhibiting small molecule designed through structural optimization based on aromatic heterocycles, belonging to the benzimidazole derivative. The mechanism of ferroptosis-inhibitory effects of 9a primarily involves two pathways: on one hand, 9a blocks NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy, thereby inhibiting intracellular ferritin degradation, reducing the release of free Fe²⁺, and decreasing ROS production via the Fenton reaction. On the other hand, 9a enhances the cellular capacity to eliminate lipid peroxides and thereby disrupts the ferroptosis signaling cascade [19, 37]. In our study, a concentration of 0.5 µM 9a was used for in vitro experiments, and it could effectively inhibit ferritinophagy and ferroptosis of hPDLCs under CF and IL-1β stimulation. Previous report indicates that 10 mg/kg 9a significantly ameliorates ischemic-refusion injury in rats through intraperitoneal injection [19]. However, the specific in vivo concentration of 9a required for therapeutic efficacy remains to be further elucidated, and its application in the treatment of OIIRR warrants additional in vitro and in vivo investigation.

Fer-1 is a classical synthetic radical-trapping antioxidant (RTA) that primarily blocks ferroptosis by suppressing lipid peroxidation [38]. Its main mechanisms include scavenging lipid peroxyl radicals, inhibiting iron-induced ROS accumulation, and preserving mitochondrial structure and function to maintain intracellular homeostasis. Fer-1 has been widely applied in the treatment of various inflammatory diseases [18, 39]. In this study, Fer-1 significantly inhibited the excessive intracellular accumulation of ROS induced by CF and IL-1β co-stimulation, and reduced the levels of Fe2+ and MDA in hPDLCs in vitro. Furthermore, in vivo experiments demonstrated that Fer-1 significantly attenuated the progression of OIIRR in mice. This protective effect is likely attributable to the ability of Fer-1 to suppress inflammatory responses within periodontal tissues and inhibit subsequent osteoclastic activity, both of which are thought to be initiated by PDLCs. Our findings also demonstrated that Fer-1 reduced the expression of NCOA4 and LC3 in periodontal tissues on the pressure side. Although the direct inhibitory mechanism of Fer-1 on ferritinophagy remains unclear, we speculate that its effects may be attributed to its ability to scavenge ROS and lipid peroxides, interrupt lipid peroxidation chain reactions, reduce intracellular Fe2+ accumulation, and alleviate iron-dependent oxidative stress. These actions may indirectly downregulate NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy. Therefore, the precise role and underlying mechanisms of Fer-1 in modulating NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy warrant further investigation.

Regarding the mechanism by which NCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferroptosis in PDLCs directly regulates osteoclast activity, we propose that ferroptotic PDLCs may release a spectrum of inflammatory mediators that reshape the periodontal microenvironment in a paracrine manner, thereby promoting osteoclast recruitment and differentiation. Recent studies have demonstrated that osteocyte ferroptosis upregulates its RANKL expression and facilitates osteoclastogenesis through the secretion of cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α [40]. Similarly, ferroptosis of alveolar osteocytes has been shown to exacerbate periodontitis progression by releasing inflammatory cytokines that stimulate osteoclast formation and impair osteoblast function [41, 42]. In addition to these paracrine effects, iron overload itself may directly enhance osteoclast activity. Excessive iron has been reported to increase the RANKL/OPG ratio and augment osteoclast responsiveness to RANKL, thereby promoting their differentiation and bone-resorptive activity [43]. Furthermore, transferrin receptor-1 (TFR1)-mediated iron uptake has also been shown to facilitate osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption [44]. These findings suggest that NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy liberates substantial amounts of free Fe2+, which not only induces ferroptosis in PDLCs but also creates a high-iron microenvironment conductive to osteoclast overactivation, ultimately aggravating root resorption.

This study has several limitations. First, the in vitro compressive force loading model we established does not fully replicate the biomechanical environment in vivo. Second, we did not investigate the relationship between ferritinophagy, ferroptosis, osteogenesis, and osteoclastogenesis, which will be the focus of our future research. Third, the interplay among ferroptosis, autophagy, and ferritinophagy is complex, and the precise mechanisms and regulatory networks require further investigation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study elucidates the underlying mechanism of NCOA4/FTH1-mediated ferritinophagy and ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of OIIRR. NCOA4-mediated ferritinophagy promotes ferroptosis in hPDLCs in vitro, thereby aggravating OIIRR. The ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 effectively attenuates ferroptosis of hPDLCs in vitro and significantly alleviates OIIRR in vivo. Moreover, compound 9a directly targets NCOA4, disrupting the NCOA4/FTH1 interaction, thereby inhibiting ferritinophagy and reducing ferroptosis in hPDLCs. These findings suggest that further development of therapeutic agents specifically targeting the NCOA4/FTH1 axis may offer a promising and more precise strategy for the treatment of OIIRR.
