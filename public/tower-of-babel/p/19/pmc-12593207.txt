Spontaneous intraperitoneal bladder rupture in a pediatric patient due to pan-urethral stricture
Authors: Zeeshan Zubair, Ambuj Bhalla, Kristina Gam, Margaret K Gannon, Mohan S Gundeti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpontaneous bladder rupture (SBR) in children is rare and typically linked to posterior urethral valves in neonates. We report the first known case of intraperitoneal bladder rupture secondary to urethral stricture in a 9-year-old male with Balanitis Xerotica Obliterans and prior genitourinary surgeries. He presented with abdominal pain, nausea, and urinary retention. Despite partial urine output, he was found to have bladder perforation managed with laparotomy and suprapubic diversion. This case emphasizes the need for high clinical suspicion, highlights limitations of standard CT, and supports dedicated cystography and early surgical intervention in children with obstructive uropathy.

Introduction

Spontaneous bladder rupture (SBR) in the pediatric population is exceptionally uncommon. Most reported cases occur in neonates and are attributed to congenital anomalies, most notably posterior urethral valves (PUV). PUV is the leading cause of urinary ascites in neonates, responsible for approximately 70 % of cases, and can result in bladder rupture due to severe outflow obstruction and increased intravesical pressure.1,2 These ruptures are typically diagnosed in the perinatal period, often presenting with abdominal distention, urinary ascites, and renal dysfunction. Classic imaging findings include a thick-walled, trabeculated bladder and evidence of urinary extravasation on voiding cystourethrogram.1

In children beyond the neonatal period, SBR is exceedingly rare and often idiopathic, with no clear precipitating factor identified. Al Abadla et al. described a two-year-old who developed spontaneous bladder perforation presenting as bowel obstruction and renal failure, with no underlying anatomic or infectious cause identified.3 Similarly, Alshareef et al. reported idiopathic bladder perforation in early childhood, emphasizing the diagnostic challenges and the need for surgical exploration in unclear cases.4

Other etiologies for pediatric bladder rupture include trauma, neurogenic bladder, and iatrogenic injury, but these are much less common than congenital causes.5,6 Notably, while urethral strictures are well recognized in children—arising from trauma, prior surgery, or inflammatory conditions such as BXO—they have not previously been implicated as a direct cause of bladder rupture in the pediatric literature. In adults, however, bladder rupture secondary to chronic urinary retention from urethral stricture is a recognized phenomenon.7 The absence of pediatric cases is striking, given that strictures can induce acute urinary retention, a known precursor to elevated intravesical pressure.

Diagnosis of bladder rupture in children is challenging, as initial symptoms may mimic more common conditions such as urinary tract infection or gastrointestinal pathology. Imaging, particularly cystography, is essential for confirming the diagnosis. Prompt urinary diversion and surgical repair are critical, as delayed intervention is associated with significant morbidity and mortality.3

The present case is the first known to implicate urethral stricture as the primary etiology of SBR in children. This case expands the etiological spectrum of pediatric bladder rupture and underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion in children with known outflow obstruction, even outside the neonatal period. Early recognition and intervention are vital for optimizing outcomes.

Case presentation

A 9-year-old male with a complex medical and surgical history presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain, flank pain, bloating, nausea, and vomiting. His past medical history was significant for partial androgen insensitivity syndrome with bilateral undescended testes status post orchiopexy and proximal hypospadias repaired in two stages, complicated by BXO, recurrent epididymo-orchitis secondary to a persistent prostatic utricle status post robotic-assisted laparoscopic excision, and left-sided grade III vesicoureteral reflux status post-robotic ureteral reimplantation. Additional comorbidities included a history of premature birth, congenital heart defects (status post VSD and ASD repair), and chronic constipation.

The patient had been undergoing daily self-calibration (performed by mother) of the meatus with 8f Catheter over the last few years to stabilize the meatal stenosis and distal urethral recurrent stricture. On the morning of presentation, his mother was unable to pass the catheter. Later that day, he developed right-sided abdominal pain exacerbated by bowel movements, along with multiple episodes of non-bloody, non-bilious emesis. These symptoms prompted his mother to take him to the emergency department.

On arrival, he was febrile to 37.9 °C, with a heart rate of 102 bpm and a respiratory rate of 36 breaths per minute. Abdominal exam revealed diffuse tenderness with suprapubic and right-sided predominance, without overt distention or palpable bladder. The remainder of the exam was notable for a patent, but poorly visualized urethral meatus consistent with prior hypospadias repair. Initial workup for appendicitis was negative; however, CT imaging showed bladder wall thickening and peri-vesical fat stranding, raising concern for cystitis. Labs revealed Leukocytosis (WBC 20.5 x 109/L) and acute kidney injury with a creatinine of 0.99 mg/dL (baseline 0.3–0.5 mg/dL). Urinalysis revealed positive leukocyte esterase, 2+ blood, and 10–20 RBCs and WBC per high-power field; however, nitrites and bacteria were absent, and urine culture remained negative.

Urology was consulted later that evening for ongoing urinary retention. Multiple catheterization attempts under ketamine sedation were unsuccessful due to significant distal and proximal urethral resistance. Ultrasound revealed an overdistended bladder. On the third attempt, an 8Fr catheter was successfully placed with difficulty, yielding approximately 100 mL of clear yellow urine. Despite this, the patient developed worsening abdominal distention and signs of peritonitis in the following days, prompting transfer to the PICU. Repeat imaging demonstrated increasing ascites and a left lower quadrant abscess, for which interventional radiology performed paracentesis and placed a drain (Fig. 1).

Axial contrast-enhanced CT of the pelvis demonstrating a left lower quadrant fluid collection and low-attenuation free fluid in the peritoneal cavity.

Due to ongoing pain and abdominal distension, a cystogram was requested, confirming a posterior intraperitoneal bladder perforation (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). Given the extent of injury and signs of peritonitis, the patient was taken emergently to the operating room and underwent exploratory laparotomy with abdominal washout and suprapubic tube and JP drain (SPT) placement. Post-operatively, he improved clinically, and the JP drain was removed shortly thereafter. At follow-up, he was stable, tolerating oral intake, and progressing well. The SPT remained functional, and definitive urethral reconstruction was scheduled for the following month.

Discussion

SBR is a rare and potentially life-threatening event in pediatric patients, typically associated with neonatal PUV, idiopathic causes, trauma, or neurogenic bladder. While it can occur secondary to urethral stricture in adults, it remains exceedingly uncommon even in that population.8 To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a pediatric bladder rupture secondary to progressive urethral stricture disease in the absence of trauma or recent instrumentation.

Our patient presented with a complex medical history, including partial androgen insensitivity, recurrent proximal hypospadias, BXO, and multiple prior surgical interventions. Pathologically, chronic tissue inflammation and scarring from these conditions contributed to a progressive pan-urethral stricture, leading to obstructive uropathy, in turn increasing intravesical pressure and resulting in a spontaneous bladder rupture. Notably, this mechanism is distinct from the chronic, high-pressure voiding seen in PUV during infancy; instead, our case illustrates an acute, post-neonatal bladder rupture due to stricture-related retention.

Of note, the patient (mother) had been performing daily urethral calibrations at home. However, after a missed session, he developed urinary retention, abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting. Multiple catheter attempts were unsuccessful due to resistance in the urethra. Eventually, a small volume of urine (∼100 mL) was drained via an 8Fr catheter, but his abdominal distention continued to worsen, accompanied by rising creatinine and signs of peritonitis. These findings prompted PICU admission, imaging which confirmed ascites, and ultimately the diagnosis of spontaneous bladder rupture.

The presence of urine output via catheter initially gave a false sense of reassurance. However, in cases of intraperitoneal bladder rupture, partial drainage may still occur, particularly if the rupture is incomplete or located away from the dome. Moreover, the CT scan performed on admission demonstrated peri-vesical fat stranding and bladder wall thickening but did not identify a bladder rupture. This is consistent with existing literature, which notes that standard contrast-enhanced CT is often insufficient and inconclusive for bladder rupture.9 Dedicated CT cystography, involving retrograde instillation of contrast into the bladder, is considered the gold standard for diagnosis, reportedly detecting bladder rupture with an overall sensitivity and specificity of 95 % and 100 %, respectively.10 In our case, bladder perforation was only confirmed after worsening ascites and clinical deterioration prompted a cystogram on hospital day 10.

This case emphasizes three critical points. First, a high index of clinical suspicion must be maintained for SBR for any child with obstructive uropathy who presents with abdominal pain, distention, and acute kidney injury, even if some urine is drained. Second, standard contrast-enhanced CT is often inconclusive for the diagnosis of SBR, and a dedicated cystography is warranted for any patient with suspected bladder injury, particularly when conservative management options do not yield improvement. Finally, prompt surgical intervention is vital in signs of peritonitis or sepsis to prevent life-threatening complications and reduce the risk of mortality.

Conclusion

This present case establishes urethral stricture as a novel and clinically significant risk factor for spontaneous bladder rupture in children, particularly in those with complex urological histories and prior interventions. It demonstrates that, even in the presence of some urinary output, clinicians must maintain vigilance for this rare but life-threatening complication in pediatric patients presenting with urinary retention, abdominal pain, and signs of peritonitis.

Timely recognition, appropriate diagnostic cystography, and urgent surgical intervention are critical to optimizing outcomes and reducing morbidity. This report expands the differential diagnosis for SBR in children and emphasizes the importance of considering outflow obstruction as a potential precipitant, especially when standard imaging modalities are inconclusive. Children with a history of urethral stricture, BXO, or multiple urological surgeries warrant close monitoring for urinary retention and abdominal complications.

Moreover, this case highlights the need to update current diagnostic and management algorithms for pediatric bladder rupture to include urethral stricture as a recognized risk factor. Future research should aim to identify early warning signs, refine imaging strategies, and develop preventive protocols for at-risk populations. By broadening our understanding of the etiological spectrum of SBR, we hope this case will contribute to improved recognition, timely diagnosis, and better outcomes for pediatric patients facing similar presentations.
