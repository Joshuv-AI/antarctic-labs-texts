Fuzapladib reduces postsurgical inflammation in the intestinal muscularis externa
Authors: Noriyuki KAJI, Kosuzu IWAOKA, Shinichiro NAKAMURA, Atsushi TSUKAMOTO
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Postoperative ileus (POI) is a surgical complication that induces emesis and anorexia. Fuzapladib (FUZ), an inhibitor of leukocyte-function-associated antigen type 1 (LFA-1) activation, a leukocyte adhesion molecule, exerts anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting leukocyte migration into the inflammatory site. In this study, we examined the prophylactic impact of FUZ on POI in a mouse model. POI model mice were generated by intestinal manipulation, and the effect of FUZ on intestinal transit and the infiltration of inflammatory cells into the ileal muscularis externa was assessed. The increased number of macrophages was significantly suppressed by FUZ, whereas the infiltration of neutrophils into the ileal muscularis externa was not sufficiently inhibited in the POI model mice. Additionally, FUZ did not ameliorate delayed gastrointestinal transit in POI model mice. In conclusion, our results suggest that FUZ does not improve delayed gastrointestinal transit but partially inhibits inflammation in the ileal muscularis externa in POI model mice. FUZ may be a potential anti-inflammatory agent for the management of post-surgical inflammation.

Postoperative ileus (POI) is a gastrointestinal (GI) motility disorder that arises following laparotomy, leading to symptoms such as abdominal pain, vomiting, loss of defecation, and abdominal distention, all of which diminish quality of life [16]. Most patients recover spontaneously, but sometimes delayed recovery can lead to abdominal adhesions or sepsis [17]. The early phase of POI is attributed to the activation of the sympathetic nervous system due to anesthesia and surgical invasion. In contrast, the primary cause of POI in the middle and later stages is localized inflammation in the muscularis externa of the GI tract due to intestinal manipulation (IM) performed during surgery [42]. Drugs that enhance GI motility, such as 5-HT4 receptor agonists and dopamine D2 receptor antagonist, have been employed to address POI in both human and dogs [37, 39]. However, there is a lack of an ideal medication to resolve acute postoperative inflammation, and new therapeutic agents are needed.

Leukocyte-function-associated antigen type 1 (LFA-1) is an adhesion molecule expressed on leukocytes, playing an important role in their migration and infiltration into sites of inflammatory [20]. When chemokines and cytokines stimulate leukocytes, the cell surface protein LFA-1 undergoes activation via inside-out signaling. This process facilitates the adherence of LFA-1 on leukocytes to intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), a protein present on vascular endothelial cells. Consequently, the attached leukocytes can migrate from the blood vessel into the inflamed tissues. Fuzapladib (FUZ, development code: IKV-741) is a compound that inhibits the interaction between phospholipase C-β2 and RAS-related C3 botulinus toxin substrate 1 (RAC1), both essential for LFA-1 activation [28]. Preclinical studies in rodents have reported the effectiveness of FUZ in models of chronic pancreatitis, colitis, and acute lung injury by inhibiting inflammatory cell infiltration into inflamed tissues [15, 29, 40, 41]. FUZ is currently used in veterinary medicine to treat acute pancreatitis in dogs [4].

IM during laparotomy stimulates the resident macrophages located in the muscularis externa of the GI tract. Activated macrophages release cytokines and chemokines, leading to an initial inflammatory response. In response to this inflammation, neutrophils and monocyte-derived macrophages migrate from the vessel and spleen, producing prostaglandins and nitric oxide (NO). These mediators induce the inhibition of smooth muscle contraction and dysfunction of the pacemaker cells of GI motility, resulting in GI dysmotility [9, 12, 27]. Given that inflammation in the muscularis externa is a critical step in the development of POI, FUZ is anticipated to offer a protective effect against POI. Therefore, this study aims to explore the potential of FUZ in preventing POI using a mouse model.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All animal experiments and husbandry procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Azabu University. Seven-week-old male BALB/c mice (BALB/cAnNCrlCrlj, Jackson Laboratory Japan Inc., Kanagawa, Japan, n=27) were used in this study. These mice were housed in standard polycarbonate cages with ad libitum access to food and water. The mice were maintained at a constant temperature of 24 ± 2°C, under 50 ± 10% humidity, and a 12-hr light-dark cycle (light; 6:00–18:00, dark; 18:00–6:00).

Preparation of POI model mice

The mice were divided into three groups: a healthy control group, an IM group, and an IM + FUZ group. POI was induced by performing IM following previous reports [9, 35]. Briefly, laparotomy was performed under isoflurane anesthesia, the small intestine was exposed outside the abdominal cavity, and the middle of the small intestine to the ileocecum was massaged using a sterile cotton swab wetted with saline. Mice were awakened after abdominal closure, and GI transit and inflammation were assessed 24 hr after IM. The IM+FUZ group received FUZ dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) subcutaneously at a dose of 20 mg/kg, both 1 hr prior and 4 hr post-IM. The healthy control and IM groups received PBS. FUZ was supplied by Ishihara Sangyo Kaisha, Ltd. (Osaka, Japan).

Intestinal transit

GI transit was evaluated as previously reported [42]. After 24 hr of IM, mice were given 100 μL of 0.5% fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled dextran solution (FITC-dex, molecular weight 60,000–67,000, Sigma‒Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) via the intragastric route. An hour later, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia, and the entire GI tract was extracted. The GI tract was divided into the following segments: stomach (Sto), 10 segments of the small intestine, cecum (Cec), and 3 segments of colon. Each segment was flushed with 1 mL of PBS, and the luminal contents were collected. The supernatant obtained by centrifugation of the collected contents was measured by a microplate reader (POWERCAN MX, DS Pharma Biomedical, Osaka, Japan) to determine the absorbance (excitation at 488 nm; absorption at 520 nm). The geometric center (GC) was calculated using the following formula: GC=Σ (% of fluorescence intensity in each segment) × (segment number)/100.

Fluorescence immunostaining

The quantification of macrophages in the myenteric plexus of ileum was performed through whole-mount immunofluorescence staining for CD68 [26]. Ileal muscularis specimens were obtained by carefully removing the mucosa from the tissue using tweezers (one specimen per mouse). These specimens were then fixed with ice-cold acetone, followed by PBS washing. To minimize non-specific antibody binding, a 2% bovine serum albumin solution was applied for 1 hr. The specimens were then incubated overnight at 4°C with a rat anti-mouse CD68 antibody (1:500, BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA). After PBS washing, the specimens were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG antibody (1:500, Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 1 hr at room temperature. After another round of washing, the specimens were examined using a confocal microscope (TCS SP5 II, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). The number of CD68-positive cells was counted in four randomly selected fields of view on each specimen, and the values were averaged and converted to the number per mm2 area.

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) staining

The quantification of neutrophils in the ileal muscularis externa was performed using whole-mount MPO staining [6, 19]. Ileal muscularis specimens (one specimen per mouse) were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin at 4°C for 24 hr. After PBS washing, the specimens were incubated in PBS containing 0.1% Hanker-Yates reagent (Polyscience, Warrington, PA, USA) and 0.03% (v/v) hydrogen peroxide (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals, Osaka, Japan) for 10 min. After another PBS wash, the specimens were visualized using an ACT-1C optical microscope for DXM1200C (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The number of MPO-positive cells was counted in four randomly selected fields of view on each specimen, and the values were averaged and converted to the number per mm2 area.

Statistical analysis

All results obtained in this study are expressed as the mean ± SD. Tukey’s test was used to analyze statistical significance, with P values of <0.05 considered to indicate statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using Stat Mate V (ATMS, Tokyo, Japan).

DISCUSSION

The development of POI is closely associated with inflammation triggered by IM. In our research, we aimed to assess the potential of FUZ, known for its inhibitory effect on leukocyte infiltration into inflammatory tissues, in preventing POI using an animal model. Our findings demonstrated that FUZ effectively suppressed the increase in macrophage numbers in the ileal myenteric plexus layer in the mouse model of POI. However, it did not adequately inhibit neutrophil infiltration. Furthermore, FUZ was unable to alleviate the IM-induced delay in intestinal transit.

In the present study, we observed no suppression of MPO-positive neutrophil infiltration into the muscularis externa following FUZ treatment after IM. This observation contrasts with prior research on animal models of pancreatitis and colitis, where FUZ treatment led to marked reductions in neutrophil infiltration into inflamed tissues [15, 30, 40, 41]. Such a discrepancy implies that the LFA-1 activation might play a less pivotal role in neutrophil infiltration during POI. Neutrophil infiltration is influenced by both LFA-1 and macrophage-1 antigen (MAC-1 or integrin αMβ2) [8, 25]. One study has demonstrated that MAC-1 blockade exhibits greater efficacy in suppressing neutrophil infiltration induced by TNF-α than LFA-1 blockade [32]. Moreover, the reliance of neutrophils on either LFA-1 or MAC-1 for infiltrating inflamed tissues might differ across various diseases. For instance, in a model of sepsis-induced liver injury, the LFA-1 knockout mouse failed to suppress neutrophil infiltration into the liver, whereas the MAC-1 knockout mouse successfully inhibited neutrophil infiltration [14]. Previous reports have also indicated that inhibiting ICAM-1, capable of interacting with both LFA-1 and MAC-1, led to a decrease in neutrophil infiltration in a POI model [12, 34]. Thus, it is likely that MAC-1 plays a more significant role than LFA-1 in neutrophil infiltration in POI.

Like neutrophils, monocytes also express LFA-1 [32]. This suggest that FUZ could potentially inhibit the monocyte infiltration. In line with this, FUZ was found to suppress the increase in ED1-positive macrophages in an animal model of chronic pancreatitis [10]. Notably, Patients with POI exhibited an increase in LFA-1-positive muscularis macrophages [11]. In our study, FUZ successfully inhibited the accumulation of CD68-positive macrophages in the myenteric plexus of ileum induced by IM. This implies that FUZ might have suppressed the infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages. The reason FUZ could restrain macrophage infiltration but not neutrophil infiltration in POI remains clear in this study’s context. While neutrophils rely on LFA-1 for adhesion to vascular endothelium, they dependent on MAC-1 for intraluminal crawling [7, 25]. Conversely, monocytes have been reported to utilize LFA-1 for both adhesion and crawling [2]. This varying reliance on adhesion molecules during the extravascular migration phase between neutrophils and monocytes might explain the distinct efficiency of FUZ observed our study.

Muscularis-resident macrophages have been reported to show an upregulation of LFA-1 following abdominal surgery in both humans and rats [12, 13]. Thus, FUZ might have suppressed the activation of LFA-1 on resident macrophages. However, the effects of FUZ on resident macrophages remain ambiguous in the context of our study. LFA-1 also acts as a receptor for interferon stimulated gene 15 (ISG15) [33]. It has been reported that when the LFA-1 on tumor-associated macrophages is stimulated by ISG15, it induced a polarization to M2-like phenotype [3]. Therefore, the LFA-1 inactivation on muscularis-resident macrophages by FUZ might modify their inflammatory response to IM.

While FUZ was able to weakly reduce the macrophages infiltration in the myenteric plexus induced by IM, it did not improve GI transit. Macrophages are known to play critical roles in the development of POI, given that the deficiency of macrophages has been shown to suppresses POI [38]. M1 type macrophages play a crucial role in the initial pro-inflammatory response, while there are macrophages promote tissue repair by releasing anti-inflammatory mediators [1, 23]. Interestingly, a recent report has indicated that genetically suppressing the infiltration of monocyte-derived macrophages did not inhibit delayed GI transit; rather it delayed the recovery of GI transit [5, 31]. These findings suggest that while activated resident macrophages induce GI dysmotility via the production of inflammatory mediators, monocyte-derived macrophages possess anti-inflammatory properties in POI. Our current findings partially support this concept. Thus, the prevention of monocyte recruitment may not be an appropriate therapeutic strategy for POI. Further investigations are needed to determine whether the administration of FUZ might delay the recovery from POI and to examine the changes in populations of muscularis macrophage caused by FUZ.

Effective management of post-surgical inflammation is important for achieving optimal peri-operative care, since post-surgical inflammation is closely associated with complications such as pain and anorexia [18]. Conventional anti-inflammatory agents exhibit certain undesirable adverse reactions during the peri-operative period. For instance, corticosteroids might cause delayed wound healing and immunosuppression, while the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs may result in gastrointestinal toxicity [22, 36]. Although FUZ did not improve GI transit in our study, it was evident that FUZ inhibits macrophage infiltration in the target organ. Macrophage produces pro-inflammatory cytokines and being recognized as key cells in the process of post-surgical inflammation [21]. Therefore, our finding suggests that FUZ can be a novel therapeutic option for the management of post-operative inflammation. Notably, FUZ exerts its unique mechanism of action, specifically targeting inflammatory cell infiltration, while displaying fewer adverse reactions, compared to existing drugs [4]. Based on this study, further investigations are warranted to comprehensively assess the effects of FUZ on post-surgical inflammation and related complications.

The primary limitation of our study is the undetermined blood concentrations of FUZ in mice throughout the experiment. In this study, we administered FUZ 1 hr before and 4 hr after IM based on a previous report using a mouse model of lung injury [30]. Yet, the pharmacokinetics of FUZ in mice have not been reported, and our study did not confirm that FUZ consistently maintained an effective blood concentration throughout the experimental duration. According to a previous report [24], the total clearance of FUZ was 18 mL/hr.kg in dogs, 93 mL/hr.kg in cats, and 660 mL/hr.kg in rats. These results suggest that FUZ might be eliminated from the body more rapidly in rodents. If there were intervals in our study when FUZ level descended below the effective blood concentration, it is possible that we undervalued the anti-inflammatory effect of FUZ on POI. Techniques to ensure a steady FUZ concentration in the blood (e.g., using osmotic pumps) may the concern. The pharmacokinetics of FUZ in mice should be clarified for preclinical studies examining the effects of FUZ on mouse models of disease.

In conclusion, while FUZ inhibited macrophage infiltration into the muscularis externa, it did not influence the delayed GI motility post-IM. Although FUZ did not ameliorate POI pathology, it could serve as a valuable pharmacological tool to elucidate the pathophysiology of leucocyte infiltration in POI. While FUZ may not ameliorate POI, it holds promise as a potential anti-inflammatory agent for managing post-surgical inflammation.
