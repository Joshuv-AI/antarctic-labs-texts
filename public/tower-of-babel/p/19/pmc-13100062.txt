Stakeholder-based assessment of historical urban landscapes as cultural heritage within the context of sustainable development: a case study of İznik (Nikaia)
Authors: Umut Kapuci, Canan Cengiz, Richard C Smardon, Bülent Cengiz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents an innovative and integrated approach for integrating stakeholder-based assessment results applied to the city of Iznik. This city is located on the shores of Lake Iznik in the Marmara Region of Türkiye and holds a significant place in the archaeology of the Bithynia Region. The main objectives of the article are: (i) to identify the components of the historical urban landscape (ii) to analyze stakeholders’ evaluations of Iznik’s historical urban landscape components based on sustainability indicators, and (iii) to develop proposed urban design strategies consistent with sustainable development goals for the holistic preservation and development of the historical urban landscape of Iznik based on stakeholder assessment. In this regard, survey was conducted to assess and understand the perceptions of stakeholders (n = 120). The results emphasize the critical role that cultural landscapes play in achieving Sustainable Development Goals, and that this role is further strengthened through stakeholder participation and collaborative decision-making processes. This article contains conclusions that may help decision-makers better understand the factors that support sustainable development in similar urban archaeological heritage areas and guide them for using holistic approaches in making cultural heritage management decisions in the future.Keywords: Landscape archaeology, Historical urban landscapes, Stakeholder survey, Urban design, Cultural heritage, Sustainable environments, İznik, Nikaia, BithyniaSubject terms: Development studies, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography, History, History

Introduction

Sustainability was initially based on social, environmental, and economic concepts, and it was later enriched by adding culture to these concepts, making it more comprehensive1. The most common definition of sustainable development was provided in 1987 by the World Commission on Environment and Development2. To achieve success in sustainable development, it is important to develop and revitalize historical cities, considering socio-cultural, environmental, and economic indicators in the formulation of planning, design, and management strategies3. This approach aims to provide a holistic view of the multi-layered structure of the city and at the same time understand the elements that shape the identity of the city4.

Cultural heritage includes tangible components such as architectural, sculptural, painted, archaeological monuments as part of human-made landscapes5,6. It plays a marginal role in the 2030 Sustainable Development Agenda. The New Urban Agenda recognizes cultural heritage as an important factor in urban sustainable development5. Cultural heritage is considered an effective approach in terms of protection. In this way, the identity of the city can be better preserved, and its morphological, cultural, social, ecological and economic characteristics can be included within the planning process4. There are many researchers who emphasize the role of cultural heritage in urban sustainable development5.

According to UNESCO7, cultural landscapes fall into three main types:

“Landscape designed and created intentionally by people” (gardens and parks).

Today, more than 70% of cultural World Heritage sites are located in urban areas, and so are vulnerable to pressures of urbanization. UNESCO Member States adopted the Historic Urban Landscape (HUL) Recommendation in 2011 to provide support in the conservation and management of urban heritage. The HUL Recommendation, adopted by the UNESCO General Conference is a vital tool for managing heritage in the wider environment as well8. The HUL approach provides a flexible methodology to tackle various challenges by establishing links between urban areas of different scales and their surrounding urban and rural environments9.

The HUL Recommendation advocates the following approaches8:

Research scope (backround) and aims

In the 2014 Florance Declaration on “Heritage and Landscape as Human Values,” published by ICOMOS under the theme of “Heritage and Landscape as Human Values,” landscapes are acknowledged as an integral part of cultural heritage that can provide tangible and intangible connections to past civilizations, serving as a living memory for future generations. It emphasizes that cultural heritage and landscape are indispensable elements of social identity, expressing cultural diversity through heritage and landscape values and underscoring the importance of sustainable, harmonious, and cross-cultural development achieved through the integration of cultural heritage and landscape values10.

With the evolution of sustainability definitions from the scale of buildings to the scale of cities, there have been significant developments in historical processes needed for social, cultural, environmental, and economic sustainability.

The study area has been on the UNESCO World Heritage Tentative List since 2014, fulfilling Criteria (ii), (iii), (v), and (vi)11. Within the sustainable cultural heritage development of İznik, archaeological and agricultural landscapes within and surrounding the city have been evaluated in a broad perspective for their contribution to sustainability goals. In the study, where results based on stakeholder assessment were obtained, the focus was on general urban design strategies that will contribute to sustainable development goals.

The article contains guiding conclusions for the holistic planning, design, and management of cultural heritage within the Historic Urban Landscape of İznik for future studies. The results obtained from this article are indicative for the preservation of İznik walled town, by establishing a record and inventory. The findings of this study will offer a new interdisciplinary perspective for understanding sustainability of walled settlements as well as the sustainable development of cultural heritage in Türkiye.

Conceptual framework and research questions

Setting priorities in historic cities based on sustainability indicators is crucial for achieving a balance between cultural heritage preservation and urban development. This approach allows for the systematic evaluation of the unique identities, spatial integrity, and socio-cultural values of historic cities.

Due to its integrative character, the concept of the HUL is gaining increasing attention in the context of sustainability. However, due to its rather broad and comprehensive nature, all its components have not yet been comprehensively analyzed within the context of sustainability12. This new approach has been developed to allow relatively more sustainable and integrated ways of preserving heritage within the urban context. Additionally, it serves as a management tool to support comprehensive Area Management Plans for historical and cultural heritage areas listed in the World Heritage List4. It is even argued that the concept of HUL has created a new global standard for urban conservation12.

Among the various types of integrative research, interdisciplinary approaches are believed to have the greatest potential in responding to the demands and necessities of the 21st century13,14. The concept of urban heritage in HULs has global connections with numerous definitions and contexts that it engages. Urban heritage is not limited to designated protected areas or heritage monuments and sites; rather, it describes the interaction among the components of a system where the urban landscape is defined as heritage15,16. Archaeological sites, monuments, public spaces, agricultural landscapes, and residences collectively form a texture with unique and local characteristics within historical cities17.

In this context the archaeological heritage values of a city, which bridge the past and present, are of particular importance. Urban archaeology is defined as the presentation of archaeological heritage values ​​obtained through archaeological excavations and research. It has become an interdisciplinary field due to the need to integrate it with future planning efforts18. Urban archaeology aims to understand past lifestyles and urban organization by examining the cultural layers, archaeological remains, and architectural fabrics formed throughout the historical development of cities. Evaluating archaeological data as sustainability indicators can allow us to understand the past, assess the present, and develop strategies for the future. This can create a scientific framework for balancing the preservation of archaeological heritage with urban development.

A landscape component that has played an important role in the production of life-sustaining resources throughout historical processes are agricultural areas. The importance of agricultural areas in HUL can be evaluated with a multifaceted cultural heritage approach including; cultural heritage, ecological balance, economic sustainability, and the preservation of social lifestyles. According to Xunling et al.17 the type of local agricultural production and the distribution of agricultural areas are among the most influential factors in the formation of landscape patterns within the historical landscape fabric. Agricultural areas, which contribute to the development of historical cities, are also important identity elements for HUL. They have interacted with other urban uses and so contribute to the formation of the macro form of urban areas. Agricultural landscapes are a vital resource not only for the conservation of biodiversity but also for human well-being. Furthermore, they can contribute to solving urban problems and supporting sustainable urban development.

Špulerová and Petrovič19 focused on historically valuable agricultural landscapes in Slovakia, defining them as significant cultural landscapes. Jiang et al.20 conducted a scientific study on the spatial distribution of urban heritage using the example of Suzhou (China). Based on the HUL approach, the study recommended integrating traditional agricultural areas into urban landscapes with a comprehensive approach to the sustainability of urban identity. Fleury21 and Pearson et al.22 defined agricultural landscapes as a significant component influencing urban plans in terms of buffer zones, green belts, heritage values, social identity, and economic infrastructure. This study includes evaluations that are parallel to the planning and design strategies we also proposed in our article.

Davoodi23, in his research evaluating social sustainability and livability in walled cities, using the example of the walled Famagusta city, states that social interaction and a sense of place can measure social sustainability within a community. Karaderi and Turkan24, in their article focusing on the Chrysaliniotissa Church in Nicosia, a city surrounded by walls with five gates, employed qualitative research methods based on theoretical knowledge, on-site field studies, and literature review. The research highlights the significance of this cultural heritage site in contributing to the sustainability of Nicosia’s historic urban fabric. El Faouri and Sibley25 drew attention to the relationship between heritage-focused urban studies and sustainable development initiatives. By identifying culture and heritage as integral parts of the Sustainable Development Goals, it emphasizes the importance of social and cultural sustainability. Trusiani and D’Onofrio26 define strategies and policies for the conservation and enhancement of cultural heritage in Sharjah, UAE as well as development of a strategic plan. They emphasized the importance of cultural components by recognizing them as the fourth dimension of sustainability. Similar to our article, Selman27 emphasized that stakeholder participation is widely recognized as a fundamental component for landscape planning and management. The European Landscape Convention stipulates stakeholder participation as one of four general measures in ensuring procedures for the definition and implementation of landscape policies28. Freeman29 defines stakeholders as any group or individual who can influence or be influenced by achieving goals, while Eden and Ackermann30 define them as “individuals or small groups who have the power to respond to, negotiate and change the strategic future”31.

Stakeholder participation is important to improve the quality of planning and design, reduce the possibility of conflict, and ensure the sustainability of strategies. In addition, stakeholder participation should be included in heritage planning to strengthen community ownership of heritage through education and increase trust in heritage management31–33. In this respect, stakeholder participation was also the basis of our study.

The relationship between HUL and sustainable development

Protecting cultural heritage as a central element of a city’s sustainable development can improve its liveability, promote economic development and social cohesion, and help achieve a balance between urban development and quality of life34. In summary, the HUL approach aims to develop a synergy between urban conservation and development and different and interrelated elements within communities. The HUL approach guides the international community in achieving the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)35. This is reflected in the UN 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and the Sustainable Development Goals.

UNESCO has introduced the 2011 Recommendation on the Historic Urban Landscape and the 2015 Policy Document for the Integration of a Sustainable Development Perspective into the Processes of the World Heritage Convention to expand heritage conservation within the sustainable development paradigm25. The HUL Proposal enhances synergies with the 1972 World Heritage Convention, the New Urban Agenda and the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development36. The HUL approach argues that focusing on local community values is crucial for activating cyclical processes and creating synergies between different perspectives. Thus, it promotes resilience, cultural heritage, and sustainable development37–40. Stakeholder engagement is essential for a deeper understanding and increased awareness of local characteristics and potential, particularly in HUL. Thus, it can contribute to decision-makers developing collaborative and original strategies for conservation and development41.

The main objective of this research is to evaluate the contributions of archaeological heritage and agricultural landscape values within the city of İznik to social, cultural, environmental, and economic sustainability within the framework of a participatory approach. Consequently, general urban design proposals for the HUL of İznik, aligned with sustainable development goals, are presented from a stakeholder perspective. This article poses two main research questions:

RQ1) What is the role of HUL components of İznik in determining priorities based on sustainability indicators through stakeholder participation in demonstrating the relationship between cultural heritage and sustainable development?

RQ2) Based on a stakeholder-based assessment, what general urban design strategies can be developed, aligned with sustainable development goals, for the preservation and development of İznik’s HUL?

Site description

İznik is a district within the province of Bursa, situated in the southeastern part of the Marmara Region (Fig. 1), adjacent to the eastern shore of Lake İznik, with a total population of 43,531. Lake İznik, the most significant hydrographic feature of the city, is the fifth-largest freshwater lake in Türkiye.

Location of İznik.

The city’s terrain is flat and surrounded by steep mountains, influencing the formation and development of the grid plan transportation system in İznik. Consequently, this grid plan has led to the city’s division into four directions connected with the land and Lake İznik, forming expansive, level agricultural areas. The city’s location within a basin surrounded by parallel mountains along the coastline has been a decisive factor in shaping its climate features. Reflecting the transitional characteristics of the Mediterranean and Black Sea climates, the Marmara Climate influences the region. Temperature, one of the climate elements, is a crucial factor that significantly impacts human and economic activities. It particularly determines the start and end periods of agricultural activities, the variety of cultivated crops, and the spatial distribution of these crops throughout the year.

İznik is a distinctive city that exemplifies the interaction between its natural and cultural landscapes, hosting archaeological heritage sites of national and international importance. Surrounded by city walls dating back to the Hellenistic Age, İznik has been profoundly influenced by the civilizations it has hosted throughout its long history, including the Hellenistic Age, Roman Period, Byzantine Period, Seljuk Period, and Ottoman Period.

The historical urban structure of the İznik can still be seen, with its grid settlement plan from the Hellenistic Period and monumental structures from the Roman, Byzantine and Ottoman periods. The 4970 m long walls, built during the late Roman period and expanded and strengthened during the Byzantine and Ottoman periods, are one of the distinguishing features of the city. İznik, which was the capital of the Seljuk, Byzantine and Ottoman states respectively, bears extraordinary witness to the early examples of the cultural, architectural and artistic achievements of these cultures. It has hosted the most important examples of the early church development within Christian history. The earliest examples of mosques, madrasahs and soup kitchens from the Ottoman Period were built in İznik. It is also a place of coexistence that showcases the unique synthesis of cultural, architectural and artistic creations of Byzantine and Ottoman cultures. As a matter of fact, İznik still maintains its spiritual value for Christians. At the same time, İznik became world famous for the second time as the production center of tiles, one of the most beautiful and enduring types of Turkish-Islamic art during the Ottoman Empire. Ceramic art, which has its origins in Neolithic Çatalhöyük, reached its perfection in İznik in the 16th century11.

The city played a pivotal role in hosting two crucial councils for Christianity—the 1st Ecumenical Council in 325 A.D. and the 2nd İznik Council between September 24 and October 23, 787, at the Hagia Sophia Church. This elevated the city’s importance throughout the Christian world. Throughout its history, İznik, interacting continuously with natural and cultural, historical, and socio-economic elements, has cultivated a unique living culture and distinctive landscape characteristics. The interplay between the urban texture, encompassing physical, architectural, cultural, and aesthetic elements, and the dynamic social structure has been instrumental in the city’s urban development. Historical urban landscape components of İznik

The components of HUL, which constitute the entirety of tangible and intangible values shaping humanity’s memory—including monuments, examples of civil architecture, archaeological assets, traditional production gardens, and natural features—were evaluated holistically. İznik, having witnessed intense construction activity throughout its urbanization process, stands out as a settlement reflecting the military, political, religious, social, and cultural lifestyles of four different periods. This historical and archaeological structure plays a role in shaping İznik’s current settlement structure, developing its tourism infrastructure, and ensuring the sustainability of its urban cultural heritage. Therefore, the systematic analysis and evaluation of historical urban landscape components is of particular importance in defining a conceptual framework for conservation, sustainable use, preservation, and development for the city of İznik. Thus, it can contribute to the creation of spatial decision support systems and serve as a guide for implementation. This study focuses on the archaeological values (city plan, walls and gates, monuments), agricultural landscape features, Lake İznik, and natural landscape features as components of the historical urban landscape of İznik.

İznik’s urban archaeological landscape values

The historical urban landscape of İznik has been summarized through archaeological and epigraphic data. The landscape of İznik has undergone changes throughout history due to both human activities and natural events. Cultural landscapes have been shaped based by the utilization of the area considering climate and soil characteristics. The city has been home to various civilizations throughout different periods. In the Ancient Age, traces of early settlements in İznik, located within the borders of Bithynia, extend back to the Prehistoric periods50.

During the Hellenistic Period, the city was founded by Antigonos Monophtalmos near Lake İznik (Askania Limne) was initially named Antigoneia, and later renamed as Nicaea. The city plan described by Strabo, “The circumference of the city is one hundred and sixty-five stadia, and it is quadrangular in shape, founded on a plain, and has four gates. It’s streets intersect one another at right angles; so that the four gates, set in a stone in the middle of the gymnasium, can be seen.” aligns with Strabo’s descriptions and exhibits the characteristics of the Hippodamian plan dating back to the Hellenistic Period45, which has been preserved until today (Fig. 2).

İznik Ancient City Plan44.

Additionally, agricultural landscapes in İznik have been represented as epigraphic and mythological values throughout history. Neophytos of Nicaea, who lived during the Diocletian Period, mentions the characteristics of Nicaea’s agricultural landscape as follows51,52:

“The beauties of Nicaea in Bithynia are not only numerous but also delightful. What are these? The suitability of its location, a mild climate, a surrounding fertile land that abundantly yields, clusters of vineyards, olive-laden trees, flowing waters from all directions, rows of mills, vineyards, gardens, and everywhere, baths, the impregnability of the walls, the grandeur of the temples, the excellence of its people, the abundance of products coming from elsewhere and emerging from the nearby lake, and the benefits derived from the sea less than a day away.”

Neophytos’s emphasis on olives and olive cultivation in Nicaea, as expressed in his words ‘The olive trees of Nicaea,’ can be supported by archaeological findings. In this context, within the scope of the cultural inventory project conducted in Bursa and its districts in 2017, a marble artifact identified within the boundaries of İznik and dated to the 5th-6th centuries AD described as an ‘olive oil press screw weight stone,’ is crucial for demonstrating not only the long-standing knowledge of olives and olive oil in İznik, but also proving industrial-scale production53. Considering olive oil as a secondary product, it is possible to discuss a cultural model involving the use and production of different and functionally new products existing in the city’s current situation54.

In addition to olive cultivation, in the Ancient Age, the shores of Askania Limne (Lake İznik) and especially the Nicaea (İznik) plain were suitable for all types of agriculture, particularly renowned for vineyards and wines. Even before the name Nicaea, the city was known as Helikore, meaning ‘abundant in ivy, abundant in vineyards’51,55. This is indicative of the city’s enduring relationship with vineyards and vine gardens, which have been significant for it’s sources of income since the Ancient Age. Moreover, the city’s founding story in ancient Greek mythology is associated with the wine god Dionysos. According to the myth, Dionysos established a city surrounded by vine branches and grape clusters in honor of his lover nymph Nikaia, giving the city the name Nikaia in dedication to his beloved52. Both mythological narratives supported by archaeological evidence and epigraphic and numismatic data indicate the continuity of the Dionysian cult, synonymous with grapes, wine, and abundance, concerning agriculture, from ancient times to the present day in İznik.

Agricultural landscapes, integral to the city’s natural and archaeological heritage, possess aesthetic and functional characteristics. This holistic urban concept not only provides a healthy urban living environment but also contributes to the local economy by establishing a transition zone and correlation between urban and rural landscapes, thereby contributing to the historical urban landscape element.

There are two main axes in the city that intersect perpendicularly. The location where the two main streets intersect is today, as was the case in the past, used as the city center. Walls

The walls surrounding the city and forming the boundary of the Archaeological Site are 4,970 meters in length58. The walls, which bestowed the status of a ‘Walled City’ upon İznik, were initially constructed during the Hellenistic Period and had a length of 16 Stadia (approximately 2,960 m)52. In front of the İznik walls, constituting Türkiye’s second most significant fortification after Istanbul, there is a defensive trench along with a secondary wall.

Lefke Gate

The gate located to the east of the city, made as a triumphal arch during the Vespasianus period with İstanbul Gate, was repaired during the Hadrianus period, following the earthquake in AD 120, which affected many structures in the region52.

Göl Gate

Located to the west of the city, this gate is aligned with the Lefke Gate and opens towards the lake. According to Schneider & Karnapp58, it was constructed during the II. Claudius Gothicus period (AD 268–270). Today, only its remnants can be observed.

İstanbul Gate

The city’s most significant and largest gate, located in the north, is İstanbul Gate. Along with the Lefke Gate, this structure connects Nicaea to important centers along the military route extending from Rome to Jerusalem. The said gate was initially built as a triumphal arch during the Vespasian period and later restored and renewed during the Hadrianus period52.

Yenişehir Gate

The structure situated in the southern direction of the city is positioned along the same axis as the İstanbul Gate. According to Şahin52, it was constructed during the Vespasianus period, while according to Schneider & Karnapp [58], it was built during the II. Claudius Gothicus period (AD 268–270), along with Göl Gate52.

Obelisk (Tomb of Gaius Cassius Philiscus)

The obelisk consists of five blocks on top of each other located in the northeast direction of the city. The 12 m. high obelisk dates back to the 2nd century AD and its inscription states that it is a tomb belonging to Gaius Cassius Philiscus59.

Roman Theater

In the 2nd century AD (98–117) during the Trajanic Period, a theater covering an area of 102 × 79 m, constructed on vaults in a flat space, and believed to have a seating capacity of approximately 10,000 spectators, was built. In the post-4th century AD, it underwent religious use, and during the Byzantine and Ottoman periods, it was utilized as a ceramic workshop60.

Hagia Sophia

One of the most significant works of Christian architecture, the Hagia Sophia Church, was built in the 4th century AD. It is believed to have been constructed at the intersection of roads leading to the city’s four gates, in the east-west direction, and on the gymnasium described by Strabo, either as a temple or in its location61–63. The First Ecumenical Council in AD 325 and the Seventh Ecumenical Council in AD 787, both important for the Christian world, convened here64.

İznik Underwater Basilica (St. Neophytos Church)

In early 2014, during the Bursa Metropolitan Municipality’s ‘Identification and Aerial Photography Studies of Historical-Cultural Heritage,’ remains of a basilica were discovered underwater in Lake İznik, approximately 50 m offshore and at a depth of 2 m. Surface surveys and excavations have been conducted since 201565,66. As a result of these studies, the basilica, measuring approximately 41 × 18 m with an east-west orientation and dating back to the late 4th century AD, is considered one of the top ten archaeological discoveries in the world.

Baptistery

According to Gönenç67, the circular planned domed structure features an entrance gate to the west, reached by 11 steps arranged in an ‘L’ shape. In the center of the underground space, there is a baptismal pool surrounded by square marble stones with a depth of 1 m, and the structure, is constructed as a baptistery with four asymmetrically arranged niches on its walls.

Civil architectural features

The city of İznik has suffered significant damage throughout its history due to earthquakes, numerous sieges, and fires. As a result, aside from monumental structures, no residential examples specific to the settlement have survived to the present day. Only a structure that was used as the Tekel Building until recently has remained standing.

İznik’s agricultural landscape values

In İznik, various types of agricultural landscapes exist, including olive and vineyard landscapes, as well as urban farming areas situated between settlements. Throughout history, the city of İznik has benefited from the favorable climate conditions in a fertile plain on the shores of a lake, providing its inhabitants with an agricultural source of livelihood. Olive groves and vineyards, integral to İznik’s agricultural landscape, contribute significantly to the city’s identity and socioeconomic structure.

Today, agriculture remains a primary source of income for the community, with olive groves and vineyards shaping and complementing the urban fabric. These agricultural areas not only reinforce the city’s identity but also transform it into an agricultural hub. Both within and outside the city walls, expansive agricultural landscapes hold special significance as identity-defining elements, making substantial contributions and highlighting the archaeological heritage’s value. In the context of ensuring the sustainability of İznik’s identity, shaped by the synergy of its archaeological heritage and agricultural landscape, holistic cultural heritage management is vital for the city (Fig. 5).

Lake İznik and natural landscape features

İznik is situated on the eastern shore of Lake İznik, the largest freshwater source in Bithynia. Lake İznik is the fifth-largest natural lake in Türkiye, with an east-west length of approximately 32 km and a maximum width of 11.5 km. The majority of the lake is generally around 30 m deep (up to a maximum of 80 m), making it one of Türkiye’s deepest lakes. The lake has a tectonic structure and is located on the central branch of the North Anatolian Fault75.

The sustainability of the Lake İznik ecosystem is crucial for the preservation and continuity of archaeological heritage and agricultural production. Lake İznik serves as one of the city’s significant natural landscape features as an ecosystem. The most significant utilization of Lake İznik is the irrigation of the surrounding fertile agricultural areas. In addition, Lake İznik holds importance for urban cultural heritage in terms of underwater archaeology, olive orchards, and its contribution to traditional agricultural production. At the same time, it offers significant opportunities for fishing activities, tourism-recreation areas, and underwater archaeology, contributing to the city’s image and overall quality of life potential (Fig. 6).

Materials and methods

The data were collected using a survey measurement and evaluation method. A survey study was conducted to determine the evaluations of different stakeholders regarding the contribution of the components of İznik’s HUL to the city’s cultural, social, economic, and environmental sustainability.

Survey

The research methodological processes were conducted in accordance with the Bartın University Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee Directive, which was prepared based on the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Directive of Higher Education Institutions and national/international principles related to scientific ethics. Ethics committee approval for the research was unanimously approved by the Bartın University Rectorate Social and Human Sciences Ethics Committee, under protocol number 2025-SBB-0511, at its 9th meeting on May 7, 2025. Individuals who participated in the stakeholder assessment described in the method participated in the research on a voluntary basis, and their informed consent was obtained. Participants were informed about the content of the research, and no personal or identifying information was requested. All participants were informed that the data obtained would be used solely for scientific purposes.

The stakeholder-based assessment conducted for this article enables a holistic sustainability assessment by combining academic knowledge, traditional knowledge, and local experience. As highlighted by Freeman29, stakeholders were selected from groups that could influence or be influenced by the success of sustainability goals. In this context participants of the survey consist of scientific-technical professionals from different professional disciplines in environmental, social and economic dimensions, as well as local people (individuals who are users of the city and have social knowledge). Accordingly, academic, public, and private sector professional groups that can represent the institutional, managerial, and application dimensions of the field have been preferred. The primary criterion for selecting stakeholders was that they possess professional experience and local knowledge related to the field of study. By including academic, public, and private sector stakeholders, the aim was to represent different types of knowledge and perspectives. In this context, stakeholders were selected from among the following professions: landscape architect, architect, archaeologist, restoration specialist, urban planner, agricultural engineer, geomatics engineer, civil engineer, tourism sector professionals (business manager, tour operator, tour guide), İznik tile designer, and landscape technician.

The survey sample included 120 professionals (31 academics, 35 public, and 54 private sector employees) during a field visit and was completed both in person and online. The survey was conducted in May 2025. The sample size to be used in the study was determined through power analysis conducted with the G-Power 3.1 software. In the analysis, based on a statistical power of 80% (1-β) and an effect size of 0.40 (medium level according to Cohen78, it was determined that at least 90 participants were required to achieve statistically significant results. However, to reduce potential error margins and data loss that might occur during the sampling process, the study was conducted with 120 participants. This approach was preferred to enhance the reliability of the analysis results and strengthen the generalizability of the findings.

Data analysis

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA)

The quantitative data for the study were collected through a survey administered to a sample group of 120 participants. The obtained data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22 software. In the initial phase, frequency analyses and descriptive statistics were used to provide an overall profile of the participants. Since the scale was developed in this study, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to identify its factor structure and assess construct validity; Varimax rotation was applied to obtain a clearer factor solution. To test the validity of the scale, Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted, and the Varimax rotation method was chosen to clarify the factor structure. To assess the applicability of EFA, the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were performed. No statistical weighting was applied; all responses were treated with equal weight. The study is exploratory and findings should be interpreted as sample-based results rather than confirmatory, population-level claims.

To evaluate the reliability of the obtained subdimensions, Cronbach’s Alpha coefficients for each factor were calculated and reported. Additionally, for each subdimension, Composite Reliability (CR) and Average Variance Extracted (AVE) values were computed to support the structural validity. All these analyses were conducted to comprehensively evaluate the scale’s validity and reliability and to ensure the statistical significance of the measurement model.

Integrating archaeological sites with agricultural areas that are ecologically, economically, socially, and spatially significant and possess cultural heritage value can create a driving force for sustainable development within the city. Comprehensive consideration of these values at the urban planning and design scales can be considered part of İznik’s development goals. In fact, this research aims to document the necessity of balancing development and conservation, which can be achieved in İznik by promoting the sustainability of archaeological and agricultural landscapes.

In summary, participatory-based general urban design proposals are presented for the historical urban landscape of İznik, considering the balance between cultural heritage preservation and sustainable development. In this context, the necessity of holistic approaches that strengthen the integration of urban cultural landscapes with the Iznik Lake landscape, contribute to community welfare, aim to increase tourism diversity, and emphasize pedestrian-prioritized designs with public outdoor spaces focusing on monumental structures has been highlighted in the research area.

Discussion

Theoretical implications

Cultural heritage, which includes historical layers, experiences, beliefs, and lifestyles, is crucial for creating a sustainable city79. In the Florance Declaration published by ICOMOS10with the theme “Heritage and Landscape as Human Values,” landscapes are recognized as an integral part of cultural heritage that can provide tangible and intangible connections to past civilizations as living memories for future generations. The declaration emphasizes the importance of integrating values between cultural heritage and landscape for promoting sustainable, harmonious, and intercultural development It also highlights the importance of developing tools to assess the role of archaeological and agricultural landscapes in the relationship between cultural heritage and social well-being for future research in the context of cultural heritage sustainability10.

Our research highlights the importance of considering applications planned for cultural heritage within the scope of sustainable development. In this respect, our article is parallel with the study of Wang et al.80. Among the scientific studies conducted on the city of İznik, Bodur81provided general evaluations by providing information on the ancient structures in the city, Yalduz70made determinations on the historical and religious importance of the city, and Şen62examined the historical geography of the city and provided information on cultural lifestyles, population movements, and forms of government within the historical process. These scientific studies on the city of Iznik have primarily focused on its historical features. In our study, the historical urban landscape of Iznik was evaluated from a multidimensional and interdisciplinary perspective using a participatory approach, in line with sustainable development goals.

Managerial implications

The concept of Historic Urban Landscape, initiated by UNESCO82, has brought a new comprehensive perspective with parameters to assist with more sustainable preservation of heritage areas. Within this article we examine the cultural heritage values of Iznik and the landscape features of Lake Iznik together within the framework of historical urban landscape characteristics. In parallel, Wu83emphasized that nature and culture are a holistic system in which they evolve together and stated the necessity of considering all assessments related to culture, nature, people and place within a unifying framework.

Trusiani and D’Onofrio26address the sensitivity to cultural heritage at the urban scale in their study, while focusing on conservation and development strategies at the levels of urban fabric, buildings, archaeological sites, and landscapes. This research shares similarities with our work, underscores the significance of a comprehensive approach to cultural heritage. Aureli and Del Baldo40, drawing attention to participatory governance, emphasized the critical role of stakeholder perspectives in the formation of cultural heritage policies. In parallel, our study aimed to highlight the importance of stakeholder participation in cultural heritage areas.

In our article, general urban design strategies consistent with SDG-8, SDG-11 and SDG-17 goals are proposed, taking into account elements such as monumental structures, walled settlements, public open spaces, Lake Iznik and its surroundings, cultural heritage tourism and agricultural tourism. Simultaneously, based on stakeholder-based evaluation results, general design proposals were developed to strengthen the green infrastructure and the landscape of Lake Iznik, addressing the low satisfaction level in environmental sustainability. For cultural, social, and economic sustainability areas with high satisfaction levels, general urban design proposals were included to ensure the continuity of practices supporting these areas (re-functionalizations in line with the city’s identity, archaeopark designs, diversification of tourism in line with the identity, proposals for connectivity between cultural routes and natural-cultural landscapes, etc.).

Another issue to consider within the scope of environmental sustainability is the potential for negative impact on water quality from the interaction between agricultural activities in the research area and Lake Iznik. Elements such as nitrogen and phosphorus, which mix with the soil during agricultural activities in the research area, can easily dissolve in the lake in the form of nitrate when rainwater washes the soil. If these elements reach high levels in the lake’s water, it could pose a risk to the ecological health of the lake. For agricultural practices, consideration should be given not only to the agricultural production needs but also to the degree of impact on lake water quality. The focus should be to develop regionally appropriate organic farming practices.

At the same time, the negative impacts of climate change are also significant for olive groves, one of the components of İznik’s historical urban landscape that are important for the economic and socio-cultural sustainability of the city of İznik. This is an important area that requires special attention within the scope of heritage protection. Although the olive tree is a species resistant to drought conditions, it is one of the agricultural crops most affected by global climate change. Even the most optimistic future scenarios indicate a decline in fruit production in most olive-growing areas84. Extreme drought stress, especially during the growing season, negatively impacts yields and development. Water stress in olive trees causes significant changes in fruit density, ripening, and oil content85. In this context, within the scope of İznik’s sustainable cultural heritage strategies, there is an urgent need for forward-looking plans, policies, and actions to address agricultural production areas in response to climate change. Regarding climate change, changes in olive behavior should be focused on and evaluated within a holistic approach to the city.

According to Wardekker et al.86, cultural heritage is vulnerable not only to biophysical and environmental threats such as earthquakes, acid rain, or climate change but also to social threats such as war. In addition to examining such vulnerabilities, literature proposing solutions that enhance heritage resilience in the face of these threats is crucial. Due to its proximity to the active branch of the North Anatolian Fault System, which passes 3 km south of İznik, destructive earthquakes have occurred in İznik’s history87. In parallel with the solution proposals mentioned in Wardekker et al.86’s study, the studies conducted in our article on the analysis and documentation of cultural heritage in Iznik can form a basis for risk management plans that will be vital in the event of potential threats.

In coastal regions, climate change, earthquakes, and other natural disasters and their effects pose a threat to life in coastal cities and the cultural heritage they contain88. Policies and plans for the protection of archaeological and architectural sites are primarily aimed at mitigating the impact of urbanization, tourism pressure, or human-induced degradation. The necessary measures to protect cultural heritage against natural disasters have not been mentioned. In this context, “in-situ conservation” stands out. Therefore, identifying disaster-related risks and developing risk mitigation measures should be among the most important goals of cultural heritage conservation89. In this context, İznik is one of the areas facing multiple risks stemming from earthquakes, archaeological fragility, and climate change. However, considering the threats that these risks and related disasters, with their different dynamics, could pose to heritage sites in İznik, the lack of a disaster management model that addresses the relationship between cultural heritage, landscape, and disaster management within a holistic framework is noteworthy. Increased humidity, temperature rise, potential changes in lake water levels, and flood risks can negatively impact material preservation in historical structures and archaeological sites. Damage to agricultural areas is also a concern. Therefore, guidelines incorporating climate-adaptive conservation practices at both building and landscape scales are needed. In this context, the lake shore, agricultural areas, and open spaces have high potential for nature-based solutions within the framework of green infrastructure and ecosystem-based adaptation strategies. Utilizing this potential also offers solutions to the views expressed in stakeholder-based analyses regarding the need to support the environmental sustainability of the city of İznik.

One of the significant problems in historical urban landscapes is the damage caused by intensive visitor use. Tourism pressure poses a threat to the preservation of heritage values. These negative impacts can be felt physically or socially. In historical urban landscapes under tourism pressure, it is important to analyze the relationships between tourism impact, carrying capacity, and visitor management90. In this context, the city of İznik is an important cultural tourism attraction with its heritage values. Considering potential intensive tourism activities in the future, the preparation of medium and long-term tourism management plans focused on carrying capacity for the city is among the needs for the sustainability of the historical urban landscape. Based on stakeholder evaluations indicating high levels of satisfaction, these initiatives aimed at supporting cultural sustainability are expected to also generate positive impacts in the field of social sustainability, where moderate levels of satisfaction have been identified.

Future research directions and limitations

Adopting a comprehensive planning and design approach is crucial for the transmission of cultural heritage values to future generations. This article discusses the contribution of archaeological heritage values and agricultural landscape features to the sustainable development of the city and urban design proposals that will strengthen sustainable development within a unifying framework. The study’s findings can provide basic data for more detailed and comprehensive future studies. Additionally, the diversity of İznik’s landscape and its multi-layered urban structure dating back to various periods require an interdisciplinary perspective for managing, governing, and monitoring cultural heritage.

Future research can integrate intangible cultural heritage components allowing for a broader examination of the sustainability of cultural heritage in İznik, considering both tangible and intangible cultural heritage components together.

Since this study is specific to a geographic context, making comparative assessments has not been possible. This article includes the results obtained specifically for the walled settlement of İznik. Therefore, the study has some limitations. Expanding the study to different geographical areas or making comparisons with other cities nationally or internationally with similar characteristics would allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of the subject. Comparative analyses could also help obtain findings for developing universal strategies. Furthermore, including other social actors in the evaluation to obtain their perceptions would be beneficial in future studies.

Conclusions

Sustainability indicators enable holistic analysis of multidimensional data in areas characterized by diverse dynamics, such as historic urban landscapes. This enables decision-making processes to be based on a scientific basis. Priority areas for historical urban landscapes can be determined. In addition to preserving the integrity of cultural landscapes, it can contribute to the development of strategies that support the well-being of local communities, economic vitality and ecological sustainability.

The results of the study indicate that the components of İznik’s historical urban landscape (HUL) make a significant contribution to cultural and economic sustainability. When evaluating the city in terms of social sustainability, stakeholders expressed a positive but more cautious level of satisfaction. In the area of environmental sustainability, the data gathered highlights the need to support this aspect of the city with relevant strategies.

The synergy of İznik historical urban landscape components is important for sustainable urban development. It provides a comprehensive framework addressing sustainable development and cultural heritage protection and emphasizes the interdependence of these. This approach, by considering the interaction between natural and cultural landscapes, can enable innovative tools and strategies for sustainable land-use planning and heritage protection, going beyond traditional methodologies. As a result, it can support the compatibility of “heritage protection-preservation-development” efforts with sustainability goals and enhance the potential of historical urban landscape to achieve sustainable development. The interaction of agricultural landscape, archaeological heritage and İznik Lake landscape in the city of İznik may provide opportunities that can support İznik’s potential to move from the UNESCO tentative list to the World Heritage List.
