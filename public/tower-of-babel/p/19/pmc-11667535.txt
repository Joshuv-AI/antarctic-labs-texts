Artificial intelligence–assisted oculo‐gait measurements for cognitive impairment in cerebral small vessel disease
Authors: Huimin Chen, Hao Du, Fang Yi, Tingting Wang, Shuo Yang, Yuesong Pan, Hongyi Yan, Dandan Liu, Mengyuan Zhou, Yiyi Chen, Mengxi Zhao, Jingtao Pi, Yingying Yang, Xiangmin Fan, Xueli Cai, Ziyu Qiu, Jipeng Zhang, Yawei Liu, Wenping Gu, Yilong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONOculomotor and gait dysfunctions are closely associated with cognition. However, oculo‐gait patterns and their correlation with cognition in cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) remain unclear.METHODSPatients with CSVD from a hospital‐based cohort (n = 194) and individuals with presumed early CSVD from a community‐based cohort (n = 319) were included. Oculo‐gait patterns were measured using the artificial intelligence (AI) –assisted ‘EyeKnow’ eye‐tracking and ‘ReadyGo’ motor evaluation systems. Multivariable linear and logistic regression models were employed to investigate the association between the oculo‐gait parameters and cognition.RESULTSAnti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity were significantly associated with cognition in both patients and community dwellers with CSVD, and could identify cognitive impairment in CSVD with moderate accuracy (area under the curve [AUC]: hospital cohort, 0.787; community cohort, 0.810) after adjusting for age and education.DISCUSSIONThe evaluation of oculo‐gait features (anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity) may help screen cognitive impairment in CSVD.Highlights Oculo‐gait features (lower anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity) were associated with cognitive impairment in cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD).Logistic model integrating the oculo‐gait features, age, and education level moderately distinguished cognitive status in CSVD.Artificial intelligence–assisted oculomotor and gait measurements provide quick and accurate evaluation in hospital and community settings. Keywords: artificial intelligence, cerebral small vessel disease, cognition, gait, eye tracking

BACKGROUND

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a disorder of the cerebral arterioles, small arteries, capillaries, small veins, or venules. CSVD manifests with clinical symptoms such as cognitive impairment, gait disorder, and autonomic dysfunction, and lesions such as white matter hyperintensity (WMH), lacunes, perivascular space (PVS), and microbleeds on neuroimaging. 1 CSVD, in its current conceptualization, is not a distinct but heterogeneous entity; it is a syndrome comprising diverse cognitive and gait problems. Thus, it is important to understand the comprehensive patterns of the clinical manifestations of CSVD. 2

Gait dysfunction is the cardinal symptom of CSVD. However, impaired gait pattern in CSVD is largely heterogeneous, which may reflect cognitive or locomotive dysfunction, or a combination thereof. Cognition and gait function are strongly linked in older adults, 3 but the gait signature of cognitive impairment in CSVD remains unclear.

Oculomotor features are promising behavioral biomarkers of brain function but have rarely been investigated in CSVD. Eye movement demands intact coordination between motor and cognitive resources, whereas oculomotor features, such as fixation, smooth pursuit, and saccades, require intact functioning of the brainstem, cerebellum, cortex, and white matter fibers. Emerging evidence suggests that several oculomotor signs can reflect cognition 4 and help evaluate disease severity in neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease. 5 Two previous studies that investigated oculomotor function in CSVD reported longer reaction time in nine patients with subcortical vascular dementia and impaired fixation, smooth pursuit, and saccades in 25 patients with clinically diagnosed CSVD in a laboratory environment, respectively. 6 , 7 However, the sample sizes of the previous studies were quite small, and the clinical picture of oculomotor impairment and its correlation with cognition in CSVD warrants further investigated in large prospective studies.

Two populations were recruited prospectively in the present study, viz., a hospital‐based population recruited from five medical centers in China and another community‐based population from Lishui City, Zhejiang Province, China. The two cohorts were examined using video‐based artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted eye‐tracking and gait analysis systems, which provide quick, movable, and easy‐to‐use evaluation paradigms in clinical settings. By studying the hospital‐based population, we explored the pathologic gait and oculomotor signs related to cognition in CSVD and developed a model to distinguish cognitive statues based on the oculo‐gait features. By studying the community‐based population, we validated the cognition‐related oculo‐gait patterns and the oculo‐gait model in the presumed early CSVD population. This study aimed to (1) explore the association between oculo‐gait features and cognitive impairment in patients with clinically diagnosed CSVD using a hospital‐based cohort, (2) validate the oculo‐gait correlates of cognitive impairment in presumed early CSVD using a community‐based population, and (3) establish an oculo‐gait model for screening cognitive impairment in CSVD. The oculo‐gait model can overcome the language barrier of most available cognitive rating scales, and may help screen cognitive impaired individuals in clinical settings. This study meets the need for examining the interrelationship between gait performance and cognitive status in CSVD. 2 We hypothesized that AI‐assisted eye tracking and gait measurements could serve as valuable behavioral biomarkers of cognitive impairment in CSVD.

METHODS

Study population

This study included two cohorts. The hospital‐based prospective CSVD registry study recruited patients with predefined CSVD from five medical centers in China (Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Xiangya Hospital, Chongqing Tungwah Hospital, Xiuwu County People's Hospital, and Tianjin Institute of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated Hospital) between January 2020 and July 2023. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the supplementary material.

The community‐dwelling cohort was prospectively recruited from six villages and four communities in Lishui City, Zhejiang Province, Southeast China. A total of 1157 adults 50–75 years of age who underwent CSVD imaging evaluation and gait and oculomotor assessments were recruited between May 2017 and September 2019. 8 The exclusion criteria were individuals with contraindications for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and a life expectancy of ≤4 years due to advanced cancer, other diseases, and mental diseases. The presence of CSVD was defined as a CSVD burden score of ≥1, 9 and 319 individuals (27.6% of the 1157 assessed community dwellers) fulfilled the criteria and were presumed to have early CSVD.

The two cohort studies were approved by the ethics committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital and performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their closest relatives.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) acquisition and CSVD imaging assessment

The magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences included axial T1‐weighted, axial T2‐weighted, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), and axial susceptibility‐weighted imaging. WMH was defined as increased brightness on T2 imaging in the brain white matter. Periventricular and deep WMHs were evaluated using the Fazekas rating scale. 10 Lacunes were defined as rounded or ovoid lesions measuring 3–20 mm in diameter observed in the cerebrospinal fluid signals. Cerebral microbleeds were rounded, hypodense lesions measuring 2–10 mm in diameter on susceptibility‐weighted imaging. The total number of lacunes and cerebral microbleeds was also recorded. A PVS was defined as a small (<3 mm) punctate or linear hyperintensity on T2 imaging, and PVS in the basal ganglia was graded using a semi‐quantitative rating scale developed by the Edinburgh group. 11 The imaging assessment of each CSVD marker was rated by four well‐trained raters (M. Zhou, Y. Chen, J. Pi, and M. Zhao; one rater was responsible for two markers) who were blinded to the participants’ clinical data, according to the STandards for ReportIng Vascular changes on nEuroimaging. 12 Images with inconsistent results were assessed by another senior neurologist (Y. Yang) who was blinded to the initial results. The kappa coefficients for inter‐rater reliability of the CSVD markers on brain MRI were as follows: 0.82 for the WMH Fazekas scale, 0.80 for the presence of lacune, 0.80 for the presence of microbleeds, and 0.90 for the severity of PVS in the basal ganglia. CSVD burden scores were calculated according to Wardlaw criteria 9 : one point was allocated for WMH burden (periventricular WMH Fazekas 3 or deep WMH Fazekas 2–3), presence of lacunes, presence of cerebral microbleed, and moderate‐to‐severe basal ganglia PVS (N > 10), yielding a total score of 4 points.

Artificial intelligence–assisted gait analysis

The National Medical Products Administration–approved ‘ReadyGo’ motor evaluation system was used for quantitative gait analysis. The device integrates a high‐resolution RGB (red, green, blue) camera and time‐of‐flight sensor to record the testing process and capture information about the joints in the human body. Human body‐tracking algorithms allow the device to accurately identify and track postures and movements in a three‐dimensional space. The sensor is designed to recognize and track the skeleton, including 32 joints. These points include key areas such as the head, neck, shoulders (left and right), clavicles (left and right), elbows (left and right), wrists (left and right), hands (left and right), finger tips (left and right), thumbs (left and right), spine (thoracic and lumbar), pelvis, hips (left and right), knees (left and right), ankles (left and right), feet (left and right), nose, and the right and left eyes and ears.

The system extracts spatiotemporal features based on the motion information in each frame of the video, and subsequently calculates gait parameters using a constructed algorithm, a bidirectional long short‐term memory deep neural network. A 3‐m round‐trip walk task was performed using this system. Before the formal test, a guidance video with instructions was shown to the participants, and a pre‐experimental task was performed to ensure that the task was well understood and performed properly by each participant. The participants were then instructed to walk at their regular speed to a marker placed 3 m away, turn around, and walk back. After the task, the video was reviewed to ensure that the software recognized the key actions and body markers properly. The stance phase (left and right), swing phase (left and right), double‐stance phase (left and right), stride length (left and right), speed, step width, step height (left and right), stride velocity (left and right), and swing velocity (left and right) were also recorded.

Artificial intelligence–assisted eye tracking

The National Medical Products Administration–approved ‘EyeKnow’ eye‐tracking assessment system was used to measure oculomotor function. This system measures eye movements at a 120‐Hz sampling rate. Stimuli were presented on the integrated monitor of the eye‐tracking system, and embedded data processing modules analyzed and calculated the eye movement parameters. Four tasks were performed, viz., pro‐saccade, smooth pursuit, fixation, and anti‐saccade (the procedures are described in the supplementary material). Calibration was completed using a nine‐point procedure to achieve a maximum calibration error ≤2° in radius before the formal test. Subsequently, a pre‐experimental task with guidance was performed to ensure that each participant understood and performed the task properly.

ReadyGo and EyeKnow systems have been validated rigorously throughout the registration and approval process to ensure their feasibility, reliability, stability, precision, and safety. The utility of the two systems have also been validated in a previous publication. 13

Cognition and mood assessment

Cognition was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Beijing version, as it is more sensitive for screening mild cognitive impairment (MCI) than the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE). 14 , 15 The education‐corrected definition of cognitive impairment was used, based on a previous study 15 : one point was added for participants with education of 12 years or less to correct for the effect of education, and total MoCA scores <26 were considered to indicate cognitive impairment. Patients with CSVD also underwent assessment for anxiety (Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale) and depression (Hamilton Depression Rating Scale).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were presented as percentages, and continuous variables as means with the SDs or medians with the interquartile ranges (IQRs), according to the pattern of distribution. The demographic profiles, gait, and oculomotor parameters of the groups were compared using the χ2 test for categorical variables and the two‐sample t‐test or Mann–Whitney test for continuous variables.

Multivariable linear and logistic regression analyses were conducted to further screen for risk factors and oculo‐gait correlates of cognitive impairment in patients with CSVD in the hospital‐based cohort. Specific oculo‐gait parameters showing significant group difference were selected to enter the multivariable linear and logistic regression analyses. Gait and oculomotor parameters were used as independent variables and the MoCA score was employed as the dependent variable, with adjustment for variables that showed significant differences between groups (p < 0.05). For external validation, the association between the oculo‐gait features and cognition was validated in community dwellers with presumed early CSVD using a multivariable linear regression model. The unstandardized coefficients (B) or odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

After determining the cognitive related oculo‐motor feature in CSVD, logistic regression and receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted in patients with CSVD, followed by validation in the community dwellers with presumed early CSVD. The ORs, 95% CIs, and areas under the curve (AUCs) were calculated. Records with missing values of interest were omitted from the analysis. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05 (two‐sided). All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

Oculo‐gait model for cognitive impairment in CSVD

Based on the above analysis, anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity (bilaterally averaged), and swing velocity (bilaterally averaged) were selected as factors to identify cognitive impairment in CSVD. DISCUSSION

In the present study, we investigated the association between oculo‐gait parameters and cognitive impairment in patients with CSVD in a hospital‐based cohort. Thereafter, we validated the cognition‐related oculo‐gait features in community dwellers with presumed early CSVD. Finally, an oculo‐gait model for the identification of cognitive impairment in CSVD was constructed and tested using the two cohorts. The results showed that lower anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity were significantly associated with poor cognition in both patients with CSVD and community dwellers with CSVD. Moreover, oculo‐gait features (lower anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity) were suggestive of cognitive impairment in the two cohorts with moderate accuracy after adjusting for age and education level (AUC = 0.787 in hospital‐based CSVD patients; AUC = 0.810 in the community population). We concluded that oculomotor and gait features (lower anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity) assessed using AI‐assisted techniques may serve as valuable behavioral biomarkers of cognitive impairment in CSVD.

Oculomotor behavior is fundamental to sensing the environment and searching for visual information. In humans, oculomotor behavior can generally be classified into two categories: gaze stabilization during motion (e.g., vestibulo‐ocular reflex and optokinetic nystagmus) and gaze orientation (e.g., foveation, saccade, and smooth pursuit). Compared with the former, neural control of the gaze system is more complex and demands a higher level of cortical resources. For instance, the dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex is responsible for prosaccade inhibition and attention, the supplementary eye field for preparing and planning eye movements, and the parietal eye field for spatial representation and directing spatial attention. The frontal eye field is a critical region for the preparation and initiation of saccades and pursuit, and for the maintenance of fixation. 16 Because eye movements share a behavioral and neural basis with high‐level cognition, it is understandable that oculomotor features are strongly associated with cognition. 17 , 18

Our results showed that low anti‐saccade accuracy was suggestive of cognitive impairment from among numerous oculomotor features in patients with CSVD. Anti‐saccade deficit occurs early in dementia due to Alzheimer's disease, 4 , 19 and the association between anti‐saccade deficiency and cognitive impairment has been reported consistently in multiple neurodegenerative diseases. 18 In the anti‐saccade task, the participant is asked to look in the direction opposite to the abrupt‐onset peripheral target, which requires the inhibition of the initial saccade impulse. Compared with prosaccades, anti‐saccades require higher levels of attention and executive function. In this condition, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, supplementary eye field, and parietal eye field are recruited, and higher frontal activation is associated with better anti‐saccade performance. 20 Anti‐saccade deficit may reflect impaired attention and executive function in patients with cognitive impairment associated with CSVD. Because we did not assess the association between anti‐saccade performance and the exact cognitive domains, this hypothesis needs to be tested in future studies. Rösler et al. evaluated oculomotor function in CSVD and reported longer reaction time in the prosaccade task in nine patients with subcortical vascular dementia; however, the anti‐saccade task was not investigated. 6 Pinkhardt et al. found multiple oculomotor abnormalities, including larger anti‐saccade errors in 25 patients with clinically diagnosed CSVD, in a laboratory environment; however, they did not investigate the correlation of cognition with anti‐saccade deficiency in these patients. 7 A strength of our study is that it provides evidence from two relatively large prospective cohorts. We postulate that lower anti‐saccade accuracy is associated with impaired cognition in patients with CSVD.

The gait patterns of CSVD can be diverse. Historically, gait in CSVD was described as short‐stepped gait, parkinsonian ataxia with a component of gait apraxia, or “higher‐level gait disorder,” 21 , 22 , 23 emphasizing the contribution of impaired cognition to gait disturbance in CSVD. In addition, abnormal gait patterns are more predictive of vascular dementia than Alzheimer's disease, 2 , 24 supporting a strong link between gait and cognition in CSVD. The close link between cognition and gait is well established during aging, as several cohort studies demonstrated that gait disturbance is an early and reliable predictor of cognitive decline in the elderly population. 25 , 26 , 27 Gait aging was shown to be associated with hyperactivity in the prefrontal cortex and hypoactivity in the primary sensorimotor areas, which was similar to the pattern of cognitive aging. 28 , 29 , 30 The hyperactivity in the prefrontal cortex was associated with better cognitive and gait performance and was postulated to be compensated by cognitive resources for impaired gait during aging. 31 A Similar pattern of hyperactivity in the prefrontal cortex was found in CSVD patients during gait tasks, suggesting this compensatory cognitive recruitment in gait‐impaired CSVD patients. 32 For instance, a functional MRI study demonstrated excessive recruitment of the pre‐supplementary motor area and the frontal and occipital areas during lower limb movement in patients with severe WMH. 33 A brain perfusion study also revealed relative under‐activation of motor‐related regions and overactivation of the premotor regions in mobility‐impaired individuals with CSVD. 34 As for CSVD gait pattern, previous studies found that CSVD was associated with lower step speed, shorter step length, longer stance time, longer swing time, larger step width, and higher gait asymmetry. 2 , 35 , 36 In our study, patients with CSVD with cognitive impairment exhibited slower bilateral stride and swing velocity, longer bilateral double‐stance phase, longer right stance phase, shorter swing phase, shorter bilateral stride length, and slower speed compared to patients with intact cognition, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies and the clinical picture of CSVD. Therefore, slower bilateral stride and left swing velocities were associated with worse cognition and may represent the gait signature of cognitive impairment in CSVD.

The close association among gait, oculomotor, and cognition implies that there might be a shared neural mechanism in CSVD. In patients with CSVD, Radboud University Nijmegen Diffusion tensor and Magnetic resonance imaging Cohort (RUN DMC) study found a significant association between microstructure change in mesocortical tracts and an overall clinical decline including cognition, gait, and apathy. 37 Mesocortical tracts contain dopaminergic projection from the ventral tegmental area to the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), and play crucial roles in attention and reward. 38 , 39 , 40 Oculomotor tasks such as saccade and anti‐saccade are also highly attention dependent; whether the impaired meso‐cortical pathway is the common basis of gait, oculomotor, and cognitive dysfunction warrants further investigation. Cross‐sectional and longitudinal analysis of RUN DMC cohort also reported that deficiency in global network efficiency may be crossly associated with cognition and gait dysfunction in CSVD patients. 41 , 42 As the current study did not investigate these neuroimaging features, the exact alteration in brain structure and function underlying the comorbid gait, oculomotor, and cognitive impairment needs to be examined in the future.

Oculo‐gait model for cognitive assessment has several translational and clinical values. First, cognition assessment scales such as MMSE and MoCA are difficult or inaccurate to apply to individuals unable to talk or those with language barriers (e.g., dialects, foreign languages). AI‐assisted oculo‐gait analysis can overcome these language barriers. As the camera technique further advances, a single camera may quickly assess cognitive status by analyzing how the person walks and moves their eyes. Application of such techniques will increase the diagnostic rate of cognitively impaired individuals, especially in rural or developing areas. Second, the AI‐based evaluation of gait and oculomotor function can detect subtle behavior changes quantitatively and may be able to screen pre‐dementia individuals. However, this postulation needs to be tested in future studies. Third, the cognition‐related oculo‐gait features have been rarely investigated in CSVD patients. This oculo‐gait feature selection is the first step to revealing the interaction between cognitive, oculomotor, and motor brain networks and exploring the behavior projection of cognitive aging in CSVD. The two cohorts studied contained multimodal imaging data including functional MRI and diffusion tensor imaging, and were followed up longitudinally (hospital cohort: every year; community cohort: every 2 years). Future studies will investigate the brain structural and functional alteration underlying the decline of these oculo‐gait functions in CSVD. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to construct an oculo‐gait model for screening cognitive impairment in CSVD. Although oculo‐gait features alone were less accurate in identifying the cognitive status in community dwellers with CSVD, they were still significantly associated with cognitive impairment in the logistic regression model. The addition of age and education level to the logistic model yielded moderate accuracy in both the patient and community populations. Hence, oculo‐gait features may help screen cognitive impairment in patients with CSVD, and the present results warrant future follow‐up studies to validate the value and reliability of the oculo‐gait model.

This study has several strengths. First, it included two relatively large prospective cohorts, which reduced bias and increased the reliability of the results. Second, both the hospital‐ and community‐based cohorts provided consistent results, yielding good external consistency and generalizability. Third, the AI‐assisted oculomotor and gait analysis techniques used in our study were able to provide more homogeneous results than manual measurement. This study also had several limitations. First, this was a cross‐sectional analysis of two prospective cohorts. Although the present results indicated the promising value of the oculo‐gait model for screening cognitive impairment in CSVD, future follow‐up studies are needed to validate the results and evaluate the value of oculo‐gait features in monitoring cognitive progression. Second, internal validation was not conducted due to insufficient sample size of the discovery data (the hospital cohort). Third, cognition was measured using the MoCA scale, and domain‐specific cognitive impairment was not assessed discretely. Domain specific oculo‐gait change needs to be tested in CSVD.

In conclusion, oculomotor and gait features (anti‐saccade accuracy, stride velocity, and swing velocity) measured by AI‐assisted techniques may serve as valuable behavioral biomarkers of cognitive impairment in CSVD.
