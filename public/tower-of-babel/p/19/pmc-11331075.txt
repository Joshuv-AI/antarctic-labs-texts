Taking a closer look at the synapse
Authors: Jeremy S. Dittman
Published: 2024

Communication between neurons relies on highly specialized structures (synapses) where an activated presynaptic neuron releases packets of neurotransmitter molecules that diffuse across a narrow gap (synaptic cleft) and bind to ligand-gated ion channels (and other types of receptors) on the postsynaptic neuron. Postsynaptic receptor activation delivers the synaptic message in the form of electrical currents and intracellular signaling cascades. The extraordinary speed and functional diversity of this elaborate process have fascinated and baffled researchers for decades, while the small size of synapses (significantly less than a micron in many cases) has posed a formidable challenge for visualizing and studying the underlying synaptic machinery. In PNAS, Held et al. provide an unprecedented picture of the synapse using cryo-electron tomography on synapses between cultured hippocampal neurons (1). This study sheds light on some long-standing questions and opens avenues for asking questions that have previously not been tractable (Fig. 1).

Many of the big questions currently being explored by synaptic biologists are centered on the mechanistic details of the exocytic process and how the two sides of the synapse coordinate signal transmission with reception, briefly summarized as follows: On the presynaptic side, small neurotransmitter-filled synaptic vesicles (SVs) traffic to the cleft-facing electron-dense region of the plasma membrane termed an “active zone” (AZ) where they become tightly attached to the membrane by a core set of proteins (a process termed vesicle docking and priming). Upon arrival of an action potential and subsequent opening of presynaptic voltage-gated Ca2+ channels (VGCCs), elevated Ca2+ triggers some of these prepared SVs to fuse with the plasma membrane within a fraction of a millisecond, rapidly transmitting a chemical signal across the cleft (2). A local reserve of SVs replenishes the “release-ready” vesicles on a subsecond time scale to sustain ongoing synaptic activity (3). Just after fusion, the bolus of neurotransmitter rapidly dissipates within the cleft via diffusion, binding to transporters, and in some synapses, by enzymatic degradation (4). In the case of low-affinity receptors such as AMPA-type glutamate receptors found in glutamatergic synapses, only postsynaptic receptors that are closely apposed to the site of fusion will be maximally activated by the neurotransmitter during a brief window of time before the concentration has fallen too low for efficient binding (5–7). Most of these postsynaptic receptors are arranged within a postsynaptic electron-dense structure termed the postsynaptic density (PSD). The exquisite temporal performance and precise spatial constraints of all the synaptic components provokes several mechanistic questions. How are the AZ protein complexes arranged to couple Ca2+ elevation to exocytosis on such a rapid time scale? And related to this, are there special sites in the AZ where SVs dock, prime, and fuse or can SVs fuse anywhere on the AZ membrane? Are SV fusion sites precisely aligned with postsynaptic receptor clusters to ensure maximal receptor activation? Are there different types of synaptic transmission events that convey distinct information between the synaptic partners?

The detailed process described above has emerged from decades of research generally via three distinct thrusts of investigation. Dynamic techniques such as electrophysiology and calcium imaging have provided a functional assessment of synaptic performance while our picture of synaptic organization arose through a variety of imaging approaches in the realms of light and electron microscopy (8). And third, genetic approaches have assigned rich molecular detail to the pre- and postsynaptic machinery required for synaptic transmission (9–11). Recent efforts using high-resolution approaches such as cryo-electron tomography (12), freeze-fracture replica labeling electron microscopy (EM) (13, 14), and three dimensional (3D) stochastic optical reconstruction microscopy (STORM) imaging (15) have revealed some molecular organizational features such as clusters of VGCCs and AZ proteins such as Munc13-1 and RIM1 with high spatial resolution, but the relationships of these clusters to docked and primed SVs remains unclear. Superresolution approaches have also uncovered spatial alignment of molecular clusters within both the AZ and the PSD [termed nanocolumns (NCs)], suggesting a transsynaptic organization that may optimize information transfer by positioning exocytosis directly across from clusters of postsynaptic receptors (16, 17). Some researchers have proposed that distinct types of SV fusion events (e.g., spontaneous fusion in the absence of calcium influx, or synchronous versus asynchronous release) relay different types of information across the synapse or signal to different receptor subtypes and therefore play specialized roles in neuronal communication (18–20).

Held et al. investigated subsynaptic structure using a cryo-electron tomography approach on cultured hippocampal synapses under near native conditions. To minimize image degradation due to electron scattering in thick samples, the authors utilized focused ion beam milling and shaved the synaptic tissue down to 150 to 200 nm in thickness (a length scale that would correspond to a stack of only 3 or 4 SVs). These exceptionally thin tissue samples produced a set of remarkably high-resolution 3D tomograms encompassing regions of the AZ, membrane-proximal SVs (MPSVs), and the associated PSD across the synaptic cleft. At nanometer resolution, clusters of high electron density (around 80 nm in size) on both sides of the synapse were quantified and compared with each other and with MPSVs for spatial correlations. Moreover, in some cases, the protein tethers associated with MPSVs could be resolved at near atomic resolution, although not quite sufficient for definitive molecular identification. Interestingly, there was a broad heterogeneity in the proteinaceous densities associated with MPSVs, and perhaps unexpectedly, no stereotyped nanoarchitecture was observed. In some cases, elongated protein tethers could be nicely correlated with Munc13-1 and globular densities at the point of contact between vesicle and plasma membrane could accommodate models of synaptotagmin-1 C2B domain bound to an assembled trans-Soluble N-ethylmaleimide-Sensitive Factor Attachment Protein Receptor (SNARE) complex and complexin (21–24). The AZ and PSD nanoclusters coaligned better than chance, providing further independent evidence for the existence of transsynaptic NCs. But perhaps most surprisingly, MPSVs did not appear to align with either the AZ or PSD nanoclusters. Instead, MPSVs followed an apparently random spatial distribution, implying that if MPSVs were to fuse, their contents would not necessarily be aligned with postsynaptic receptor clusters based on the corresponding PSD nanoclusters. The authors performed Monte Carlo simulations of glutamate diffusing from a MPSV-guided fusion event and liganding AMPA receptors for each MPSV tomogram. Whether AMPA receptors were corralled within PSD nanoclusters or randomly distributed across the postsynaptic membrane, the average response to the fusion of a MPSV (mean number of open AMPA receptors) was similar. By contrast, variability between each distinct MPSV simulation was increased when AMPA receptors were clustered instead of uniformly distributed. One implication of this simulation is that, assuming AMPA receptors adopt a clustered arrangement within the PSD, then some SVs have a larger postsynaptic impact than others. Given previous observations that NCs at these synapses may be altered by synaptic plasticity (16), perhaps the lack of correlation between MPSVs and PSD nanoclusters observed in this study provides a substrate for boosting synaptic strength via tightening the alignment between MPSVs and PSD receptor clusters. Combined with previous proposals for enhanced AMPA receptor clustering in response to plasticity induction, these effects could harness the variability observed on the simulations of Held et al to produce a large enhancement in synaptic strength (16, 25).

While this study establishes an approach to explore the native synaptic topography at nanometer resolution, many questions and challenges remain. What are the molecular identities and copy numbers of the proteins in the AZ and PSD nanoclusters and the MPSV tethers? Do the SVs that are closest to the plasma membrane in this study actually fuse at those locations or is there some degree of mobility with fusion probability being a function of position (these tomograms did not catch any MPSVs in the act of fusing)? Do the different MPSV tether architectures correspond to different classes of fusion events (e.g., spontaneous, synchronous, delayed)? And are these distinct MPSVs correlated with AMPA vs. N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors? Finally, because synaptic functional and morphological diversity is a hallmark of the mammalian brain, to what extent do the findings described here carry over to other types of synapses? Given the accelerated pace of breakthroughs in cryo-electron tomographic approaches applied to native tissues, we may soon have answers to many of these questions.
