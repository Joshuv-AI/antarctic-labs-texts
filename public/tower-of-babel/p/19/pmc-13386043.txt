Detecting infection after trauma: why clinical context still matters
Authors: Mariaelena A Boyle, Pauline K Park
Published: 2026

In critically ill trauma patients, distinguishing infectious complications from the sterile inflammatory response to injury remains an important diagnostic challenge.1 Structured adjudication tools may help clarify infection status when infection and sepsis definitions are conflicting.2 3

In this issue of TSACO, Junak et al reported an important pilot study evaluating a structured adjudication instrument for determining infection status in adult trauma patients admitted to the intensive care unit.4 The authors developed standardized electronic case report forms (CRFs) to abstract pre-specified patient data from the electronic health record (EHR), facilitating subsequent rapid electronic review. For each case, two primary adjudicators retrospectively determined whether infection was present, its anatomical source, infection type, the hospital day that infection was clinically suspected, and the day antimicrobial therapy was indicated.

The study cohort included primarily blunt injury, with a mean Injury Severity Score (ISS) of 23.5+12.1. Concordance between the two primary adjudicators was 82.5% (33 of 40 cases) in the first round of CRF reviews. Infection occurring within the first 10 days following admission was identified in 18.2% (6 of 33) of patients. In the seven non-concordant cases, a third adjudicator identified infection in an additional six of the cases. The total prevalence of infection was 30% (12 of 40), with additional review required in one-half of the infection determinations. Notably, disagreements in assigning presence of infection were attributed to lack of prehospital data, patient symptom reporting, and rationale for ordering tests.

This study underscores the enduring importance of the clinical context derived from fundamental patient-physician interactions and bedside assessments. As the authors note, structured clinical data may not include key contextual and symptom-related details that inform clinical decision-making. While retrospective EHR review cannot support real-time care, integration with artificial intelligence tools promises to make real-time infection and sepsis detection commonplace. This study highlights that future performance will need to address the quality and completeness of data inputs, including quantitative data lacking clinical context and free-text data that may be inaccurate, incomplete, or difficult to interpret.5 6

Despite progress in reducing early and overall trauma mortality, infectious complications in critically ill trauma patients remain difficult to identify, often delaying timely treatment. Accurate identification of infection and sepsis is essential for the development of reliable clinical detection and diagnostic tools. This study represents an important step toward establishing rigorous, standardized definitions to support future clinical trials and improve the early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of infection and sepsis in high-risk trauma patients.
