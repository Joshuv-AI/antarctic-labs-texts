Gastric Cancer Organoids: Mechanistic Insights, Drug Discovery, and Translational Advances in Precision Medicine
Authors: Na Liu, Yan Zhang, Yao Liu, Xiao Dong, Yu Li, Jinrui Geng, Yonghong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Significant advances in the treatment of localized gastric cancer have been achieved through early screening, improved diagnostic techniques, surgeries, and adjuvant chemotherapy; however, the need for personalized treatment remains under debate. This review highlights the use of gastric cancer organoid (GCO) models to study cancer mechanisms, drug discovery, and precision medicine, focusing on their ability to simulate the tumor microenvironment, reveal cell heterogeneity, and assess drug responses. In particular, the integration of CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing with GCOs provides a powerful platforms for identifying new drug targets and supporting clinical applications in precision medicine. This review also explores the potential of these organoids in future research, particularly in optimizing treatment strategies and developing personalized therapies, thus offering new directions for precision treatment of gastric cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Gastric cancer remains a major global health challenge due to its high incidence, mortality, and complex clinical management. According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer GLOBOCAN 2024 report, approximately 1.24 million new cases and 790,000 deaths occur annually, with Asia accounting for 56% of cases, largely owing to Helicobacter pylori infection, dietary factors, and genetic predisposition [1]. Despite advances in multimodal treatments, including surgery, chemotherapy (S-1 plus oxaliplatin/capecitabine plus oxaliplatin), targeted therapy (Claudin18.2 and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 [HER2] agents), and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [2345], the 5-year survival rate remains below 10%. This poor outcome is largely attributed to the pronounced heterogeneity of gastric cancer at the molecular, histological, and cellular levels. Overcoming this heterogeneity through precise molecular stratification and personalized therapeutic strategies is key to improving prognosis [67891011]. However, the lack of early symptoms and the complexity of underlying molecular mechanisms continue to hinder early detection and effective, precise treatment [7].

Traditional tumor models have major limitations in studying gastric cancer mechanisms and clinical translation, as they fail to reproduce the in situ biological features of patient tumors and thus cannot meet the requirements of precision medicine. Conventional 2-dimensional (2D) cell cultures, lacking 3D structures and essential elements of the tumor microenvironment (TME), such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and endothelial cells, cannot accurately mimic cell proliferation, differentiation, or drug resistance in vivo. They only reflect drug responses at the single-cell level, which differs markedly from real physiological conditions [121314]. Patient-derived organoid (PDO) technology offers a transformative platform that integrates disease modeling, drug screening, and precision oncology. PDOs faithfully preserve the genetic, histological, and functional characteristics of primary tumors, thus providing a more physiologically relevant model for mechanistic studies and personalized therapy. Standard PDOs are generated by enzymatically digesting gastric cancer epithelial cells and culturing them in 3D Matrigel to preserve the genetic, histological, and functional traits of the tumor. These models are suitable for mechanistic studies and personalized treatment. However, they lack essential TME components, such as immune cells and fibroblasts, limiting their ability to mimic tumor-TME interactions. To address this, the organoid co-culture system incorporates TME-related components (such as immune cells, fibroblasts, and vascular endothelial cells), creating a more accurate model of TME interactions. Taken together, these 2 models complement each other and advance the development of precision medicine for gastric cancer.

In recent years, gastric cancer organoids (GCOs) have achieved remarkable progress in mechanistic studies, drug screening, and precision medicine. As an in vivo-like research platform, GCOs enable a deeper investigation of gastric cancer initiation, progression, invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance mechanisms [151617]. In drug development, high-throughput screening based on GCOs has become a powerful approach to identify novel anticancer agents and optimize combination therapies [1819]. In the context of precision medicine, GCO-guided individualized treatment strategies have demonstrated promising clinical potential, paving the way for personalized therapies. Overall, organoid technology has significant theoretical and translational value in gastric cancer research and is increasingly serving as a bridge between basic scientific and clinical applications. This review summarizes the current status of gastric cancer research and recent advances in organoid technology, focusing on the construction methods, research advantages, and applications of GCOs in disease modeling, drug discovery, and precision therapy. Finally, we discuss the existing challenges and propose future directions for enhancing clinical translation.

AN OVERVIEW OF ORGANOID TECHNOLOGY

Definition and development history of organoids

Organoids are 3D culture systems derived from stem cells or tissue-specific progenitor cells that can self-organize under defined culture conditions to form miniature structures exhibiting cellular architecture, composition, and partial functional characteristics similar to those of corresponding organs in vivo [2021]. Compared with conventional 2D cultures, organoids more closely mimic in vivo cellular organization, polarity, and organ-specific physiology, making them valuable tools in developmental biology, disease modeling, drug discovery, and regenerative medicine. Organoids exhibit the key properties of self-renewal, self-organization, and organ specificity [2223]. Their generation relies on an appropriate 3D scaffold, such as Matrigel, and essential growth factors, such as Wnt, epidermal growth factor (EGF), Noggin, and R-spondin [2024], which together maintain stem cell stemness and differentiation potential, enabling long-term and stable in vitro culture.

Organoid technology has evolved from an early conceptual discovery to rapid expansion across multiple organ systems (Table 1). Organoid technology began with the discovery of cell self-organization by Wilson [25] in 1907. With advancements in stem cell biology and key technological developments, they have expanded to multiple organ systems [262728]. The successful establishment of mouse intestinal and gastric organoids from Lgr5+ adult stem cells (ASCs) by Sato et al. [24] in 2009 marked the emergence of modern organoid technology. Since then, organoid models have been developed for multiple organs, including the stomach [15], lungs [293031], pancreas [32], colorectum [333435], and bladder [36].

The emergence of PDOs has further extended their application in oncology. PDOs faithfully retain the genetic, histological, and drug-responsive characteristics of primary tumors, providing a robust experimental platform for individualized therapy. A major leap occurred in 2018 when Nanki et al. [15] optimized the culture medium by removing certain components, increasing the establishment efficiency from 54.7% to 74.6%. Concurrently, Vlachogiannis et al. [37] built PDOs from metastatic and biopsy tissues of 71 patients with gastric and esophageal cancer, achieving markedly improved clinical relevance. Recent years have witnessed significant technological innovations. In 2021, Lo et al. [17] used CRISPR/Cas9 to construct an AT-rich interaction domain 1A (ARID1A)-deficient GCO model. In 2022, Lee and Sung [38] developed a microfluidic stomach chip that incorporated organoids to model gastric peristalsis. In 2023, Mircetic et al. [39] identified the critical role of the KDM1A-NDRG1 axis in gastric cancer through genome-wide CRISPR screening. In 2024, Li et al. [18] established a biological library of 57 gastric cancer PDOs, highlighting variations in chemotherapy responses. In 2025, Chan et al. [40] created an enteric neuron-GCO fusion model to study neuro-tumor interactions in metabolism, drug resistance, and progression. Collectively, these advances have driven cancer organoids from laboratory research to clinical translation, offering powerful tools for mechanistic studies, therapeutic target discovery, and personalized treatment.

Organoid technology represents a continuous advancement in its ability to recapitulate organ physiology in vivo. The emergence of GCOs has three key advantages: high genetic fidelity (retaining primary tumor mutational profiles), strong clinical relevance (high concordance between drug sensitivity and therapeutic response), and a controllable microenvironment (enabling the simulation of components such as CAFs and neural cells). GCOs are poised to become a routine tool for optimizing salvage therapies in advanced drug-resistant cases and to propel precision oncology for gastric cancer into a new era.

Comparison of organoids with other in vitro models

Traditional in vitro tumor models, including 2D cell lines, early 3D spheroid cultures, and patient-derived xenografts (PDX), have shown increasing limitations as the demand for models with greater physiological relevance and clinical translatability has increased. In recent years, organoids, as an advanced 3D culture system, have emerged as a pivotal model bridging basic research and clinical application. Their distinct advantages in preserving tissue heterogeneity, recapitulating native architecture, and supporting individualized therapy make them particularly valuable. 2D cell lines

2D cell lines were among the earliest and most widely used in vitro tumor models because of their low cost, experimental simplicity, and rapid proliferation. Classic gastric cancer cell lines, such as MKN-45, SGC-7901, and AGS, remain widely used [4142]. Studies using MKN-45 cells revealed that phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway activation promotes epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [43], whereas research on SGC-7901 cells demonstrated that ARID1A mutations contribute to chemotherapy resistance [44]. Prolonged in vitro culture and selection pressure readily lead to monoclonalization, resulting in genetically and phenotypically homogeneous cell populations [4546] that fail to capture the heterogeneity of primary tumors; moreover, the lack of 3D architecture and TME limits the accurate modeling of in vivo cell proliferation, differentiation, and drug resistance [121314]. Long-term passaging also causes genetic drift, leading to the loss of key molecular traits such as HER2 amplification, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) latency, and CDH1 mutations [47], and preventing faithful recapitulation of CAF-mediated transforming growth factor (TGF)-β signaling [48]. Consequently, drug response profiles derived from 2D gastric cancer models often show poor correlation with actual clinical outcomes [18], limiting their translational relevance in precision oncology.

Primary 3D culture model

To overcome the limitations of 2D cultures, spheroid models generate 3D aggregates that partially recapitulate nutrient-oxygen gradients and cell–cell interactions, thereby improving their physiological relevance [49]. After cisplatin treatment, MKN-45 spheroids show significantly higher survival rates than 2D cells, reflecting increased in vivo-like drug resistance [50]. Similarly, a nanomedicine targeting circ_0008315 markedly inhibits spheroid growth, supporting the utility of this model for evaluating drug efficacy [51]. However, gastric cancer spheroids remain overly simplistic, comprising only tumor cells and lacking key TME components, including CAFs, immune cells, endothelial cells, and the extracellular matrix (ECM) [5253]. Consequently, spheroids cannot reproduce CAF-induced chemoresistance [54] or maintain balanced stem cell subpopulations, often leading to bias [55]. In contrast, organoids derived from stem or tumor stem cells exhibit long-term self-renewal, multicellular complexity, and greater stability, making them superior models for gastric cancer research [56].

PDX

PDX are established by transplanting fresh tumor tissues into immunodeficient mice (such as non-obese diabetic (NOD)/severe combined immunodeficiency and NOD scid gamma strains) [57]. This model preserves the histological architecture, intratumoral heterogeneity, and molecular subtype features of primary gastric cancer, including EBV-positive and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) phenotypes, while partially retaining stromal components such as CAFs and the ECM [58]. In gastric cancer research, PDX models reliably recapitulate subtype-specific characteristics and are widely used for the preclinical validation of therapeutic strategies. Notably, EBV-positive gastric cancer PDX models maintain long-term latent membrane protein 1 and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression [59], providing a valuable platform for investigating EBV-driven tumorigenesis and immunotherapy responses. Similarly, in HER2-positive gastric cancer, combined treatment with trastuzumab and DS-8201 showed marked synergistic efficacy in PDX models, supporting further exploration of resistance mechanisms [60]. However, the application of PDX models is limited by several factors. First, the modeling process is time-consuming and inefficient, with success rates below 25% for diffuse gastric cancer [61]. Second, high cost and low throughput hinder large-scale drug screening [62]. Third, the transplanted tissues may contain EBV-infected B cells, which can develop into lymphoma in immunodeficient mice, leading to model failure or biased results [6364]. Therefore, rigorous histopathological and genomic validation, including hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, CK and CD20 immunohistochemistry [65], EBV DNA copy number analysis, and tumor-specific mutation detection, are essential to ensure model authenticity [63666768]. Although the PDX model remains the gold standard for in vivo validation, addressing its limitations in modeling efficiency, cost, immune reconstitution, and the risk of EBV-related lymphomas is crucial to fully harness its potential for precision gastric cancer treatment and immunotherapy research.

PDOs and the trend of multi-model fusion

PDOs derived from primary gastric cancer tissues are advanced patient-specific tumor models that offer high genetic fidelity, scalability, and clinical relevance [69]. They preserve tumor heterogeneity, key mutations (such as TP53 and ARID1A), and histological features [69]. However, traditional PDO models lack crucial TME components, such as stromal, immune, and endothelial cells, limiting their ability to fully mimic tumor-stromal and tumor–immune interactions. By incorporating non-tumor cells such as CAFs and peripheral blood lymphocytes, PDOs can partially reconstruct TME interactions, facilitating studies on drug resistance and immune regulation [6870]. PDOs are effective in high-throughput drug screening and serve as vital platforms for bridging basic research with clinical precision treatment [71]. However, PDOs may acquire mutations different from those in the primary tumor due to clonal drift or selection pressure during culture, potentially compromising their representativeness [727374]. Therefore, ensuring the genetic stability of PDOs across generations is crucial. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) and gene expression profiling can be used to verify the consistency between PDOs and primary tumors and to improve the accuracy of drug screening and treatment response studies [7576]. Despite their significant potential in tumor modeling, TME research, and immune regulation, challenges such as clonal drift and genetic stability remain. With technological advances and multi-model integration, PDO-based systems are poised to play a key role in precision drug discovery and personalized treatment for gastric cancer.

The basic principles and optimized conditions of organoid culture

Organoid construction replicates the stem cell microenvironment under 3D culture conditions, allowing cells from normal or tumor tissues to self-renew and self-organize. Based on their cellular origin, organoids are classified as either adult stem cell-derived or tumor-derived. Normal organoids originate from ASCs that reside in specific tissue niches and exhibit self-renewal and multilineage differentiation abilities [77]. In the stomach, ASCs are mainly located at the base of the gastric glands and express stemness markers such as Lgr5 and CD44. Under suitable 3D culture conditions, isolated ASCs can self-organize into gastric organoids with glandular-like structures that replicate the cell composition and functions of the native gastric mucosa, including acid secretion and mucus production [78]. In contrast, tumor organoids are derived from cancer stem cells (CSCs) or tumor progenitor cells that drive tumorigenesis, progression, and drug resistance [79]. GCOs are typically established from fresh surgical or biopsy tissues, enriching CSC subpopulations such as CD44+ and ALDH1+ cells [80]. These cells proliferate through the aberrant activation of pathways such as Wnt and PI3K/AKT, preserving the heterogeneity of the original tumor [69].

Organoid construction replicates the in vivo growth microenvironment, including ECM, signaling pathways, and nutrient supply. The core components include a 3D matrix scaffold, growth factors, pathway modulators, and optimized culture conditions with specific adaptations for GCOs. The 3D scaffold provides structural and biochemical cues for cell anchorage and self-organization. Matrigel is commonly used, whereas gastric-derived decellularized ECM hydrogels can further improve morphological fidelity and culture stability [81]. Essential growth factors, including EGF, Noggin, and R-spondin1, maintain stemness and proliferation, and EGF activates the PI3K/AKT and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways via epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling [37]. CHIR99021 (a GSK3β inhibitor) enhances Wnt signaling, improving organoid formation efficiency in low-stemness samples [82]. Organoids are typically cultured in Advanced Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium/F12 medium with B27, N-acetylcysteine, and antibiotics at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity. Some protocols introduce 5% hypoxia to mimic the TME and maintain malignant traits [37]. These optimized systems have broad applications in cancer modeling, high-throughput drug screening, and precision medicine, establishing organoids as a core technology in modern biomedical research.

CONSTRUCTION AND CHARACTERIZATION OF GCOs

The clinical relevance of GCOs depends largely on their tissue source. Primary patient tissues remain the main source, whereas derivative samples are used for specific research purposes. Therefore, ensuring sample viability and purity during processing is essential. Major sources include surgical specimens, endoscopic biopsies, and metastatic tissues obtained via fine-needle aspiration. Surgical specimens provide abundant cells, enabling repeated digestion, stable organoid line establishment, and biobank construction [83]. Endoscopic biopsies are minimally invasive and obtainable from various gastric regions (cardia, fundus, body, and antrum), and are suitable for non-surgical or longitudinal sampling, although small tissue volumes require optimized culture protocols (Fig. 1A). Metastatic samples reproduce invasive and drug-resistant phenotypes of advanced gastric cancer [1684], offering valuable tools for dynamic efficacy evaluation and personalized therapy.

The establishment of GCOs involves several key steps, including tissue processing, enzymatic dissociation, matrix embedding, culture, and preservation (Fig. 1A). Strict aseptic techniques and controlled conditions are essential to ensure reproducibility. Fresh gastric cancer tissues are rapidly processed using mechanical fragmentation combined with enzymatic digestion (using collagenase II or dispase) to obtain suspensions enriched in epithelial or tumor stem cells [85]. The cells were embedded in Matrigel or similar 3D scaffolds to form organoid structures. The culture medium was supplemented with Wnt3A, R-spondin1, EGF, Noggin, and gastrin to support stem cell proliferation and morphogenesis [49]. The addition of Rho-kinase (ROCK) inhibitors or TGF-β modulators can further improve viability and modeling efficiency according to sample characteristics. Established GCOs typically display vesicular or multilumen cystic and dense cluster-like morphologies that mirror their parental tumor histology [86]. H&E staining and immunohistochemistry are used to assess the structural and molecular markers [49]. Validation of molecular fidelity is essential for translational applications and requires evaluation across the genetic, epigenetic, transcriptomic, and functional levels. Studies have shown that gastric cancer PDOs closely reproduce the mutation profiles and drug response patterns of primary tumors [848788]. Moreover, PDO drug sensitivity correlates strongly with clinical outcomes, confirming its value as a predictive tool for precision oncology. The different pathological subtypes of gastric cancer exhibit distinct organoid-forming characteristics. Intestinal-type tumors, with well-differentiated glands, readily form lumen-containing and morphologically regular organoids that exhibit strong expandability and passage potential [89]. In contrast, diffuse-type gastric cancers, characterized by weak cell adhesion and abundant cytoplasm, form solid clusters with limited growth, though modeling efficiency can be improved by adjusting matrix stiffness or adding ROCK inhibitors [9091]. The absence of E-cadherin disrupts epithelial integrity and directional cell division, promoting tumor cell spread, a hallmark of diffuse-type gastric cancer. This adhesion-deficient growth leads to disordered structures in 3D cultures, limiting the formation of well-polarized glandular organoids [92]. During diffuse-type gastric cancer progression, tumor cell signaling and stromal matrix remodeling (such as collagen I deposition) synergistically sustain Wnt pathway activity, allowing tumor cells to inhibit differentiation and maintain proliferation even without classical Wnt niche factors, highlighting their niche independence [93]. Studies have shown that diffuse-type gastric cancer cells have unique niche requirements and high adaptability to the microenvironment, relying on specific mechanical signals, paracrine factors, and the matrix composition for survival and proliferation. This dependence complicates in vitro organoid culture and standardized amplification. Research has also highlighted that CAFs regulate the EMT phenotype and invasion of tumor cells by secreting matrix-related factors, such as fibulin-5, emphasizing the importance of tumor-stroma interactions. This reliance on microenvironmental signals poses challenges for in vitro models that lack stromal support to reconstruct and amplify the diffuse-type gastric cancer phenotype [94]. Therefore, large-scale organoid studies have shown that diffuse-type gastric cancer models exhibit greater variability in growth and expansion than intestinal-type cancers, complicating standardized cultivation and functional research [89]. Mucinous organoids are enriched in mucus-secreting cells, and maintaining their secretory phenotype requires a reduction in differentiation factors during culture [37]. The establishment rate of low-differentiation organoid subtypes (64.6%) may be higher than that of intermediate-differentiation subtypes [95], indicating a potential link between tumor differentiation and the success rate of organoid cultivation. Subtype-specific organoids have advanced our understanding of gastric cancer heterogeneity, offering a valuable platform for targeted therapy, drug screening, and the study of drug resistance mechanisms (Fig. 1B).

The in vitro construction and standardized amplification of GCOs are more challenging and less stable than those of colorectal cancer organoids because of the pathological subtype heterogeneity, strong dependence on microenvironmental signals, and unique molecular pathway regulation. In contrast, colorectal cancer organoids rely on the intrinsic Wnt pathway activation (often involving APC mutations), and as additional mutations accumulate, their dependence on exogenous stem cell niche factors decreases, allowing growth under simplified culture conditions [9697]. GCOs show dynamic changes in cell-cell and cell-matrix adhesions during tumor progression. Unlike normal gastric organoids, those associated with diffuse-type gastric cancer and driver mutations, such as CDH1, RHOA, or ARHGAP fusions, can escape reliance on adhesion and matrix support, gain resistance to ROCK inhibitor removal or matrix absence, and exhibit stronger antiapoptotic resistance and growth autonomy [98]. Colorectal cancer is primarily characterized by conventional adenocarcinoma with gland formation as a key grading criterion [99]. Studies have shown that patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids have a success rate of approximately 80% or higher and can be cultured long-term while retaining tumor characteristics [100101]. In contrast, gastric cancer has various subtypes, with the diffuse type maintaining Wnt activity autonomously [93]. The establishment rate of the low-differentiation organoid subtypes (64.6%) may be higher than that of the intermediate-differentiation subtypes [95]. There are variations in the methods used for culturing GCOs and determining drug sensitivity, with no standardized methodology. These differences limit the comparability of results across studies and hinder their clinical translation.

GCOs accurately reproduce the genomic features and drug responses of primary tumors, thus providing a robust basis for precision therapy and personalized treatment. Through their standardized construction and validation, they have become essential tools for mechanistic studies, drug screening, and treatment optimization. With continued technological progress, organoid models will play an increasingly central role in gastric cancer research by offering reliable experimental platforms and clinical guidance for advancing precision oncology.

PROGRESS IN THE APPLICATION OF GCOs

Research on disease mechanisms

PDOs serve as high-fidelity models for exploring tumorigenesis and molecular mechanisms in gastric cancer. By preserving the structural and genetic characteristics of primary tumors, PDOs provide an ideal platform for dissecting tumor initiation, evolution, and key signaling pathways.

Using samples from different pathological stages, ranging from intestinal metaplasia and dysplasia to carcinoma in situ, PDOs have been used to reconstruct the stepwise process of gastric carcinogenesis [102]. The integration of single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptomics has revealed that organoids retain clonal heterogeneity and reflect the evolutionary trajectories of tumor progression [10]. Recent studies have mapped the interaction networks among tumor cells, immune cells, and fibroblasts, highlighting the dual driving mechanisms of intrinsic mutations and microenvironmental regulation in gastric cancer development [93].

The CRISPR-Cas9 system, derived from bacterial immune defense against foreign DNA, has become a powerful tool for precise gene editing and functional genomic studies [103]. Its application in GCOs enables the modeling of tumorigenesis and the discovery of therapeutic targets. By knocking out key driver genes, such as TP53, CDH1, and APC, in normal gastric organoids, researchers have simulated stepwise gastric cancer development by observing changes in cell polarity, proliferation, and genomic stability. Nanki et al. [15] created a multi-gene mutation model using 37 PDOs and showed that combined mutations allowed tumors to escape Wnt/R-spondin regulation, thus supporting a genotype-phenotype screening approach for tumor classification. Lo et al. [17] demonstrated that ARID1A deletion via CRISPR-Cas9 induced morphological alterations and tumorigenicity; although Wnt/β-catenin activation enhanced mucus differentiation, it failed to restore proliferation. Moreover, large-scale CRISPR screens have identified novel therapeutic targets and clarified the molecular mechanisms underlying gastric carcinogenesis [104]. Taken together, these studies underscore the synergy between CRISPR technology and organoid models in advancing precision oncology.

PDOs are involved in several key signaling pathways that drive tumor initiation and progression (Fig. 2). The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is essential for the maintenance of cancer stemness. The CRISPR knockout of APC or RNF43 confers Wnt-independent growth [105], while EGFR/ERBB amplification or KRAS activation induces EGF-independent proliferation via the autocrine epiregulin-MAPK loop [106]. Similarly, abnormal activation of the PI3K/AKT/mammalian target of rapamycin pathway is frequently observed in PDOs, offering a potential target for precision therapy [107108]. Xu et al. [16] demonstrated that Wnt3A or Wnt5A knockdown via CRISPR/Cas9 reduced β-catenin nuclear localization and inhibited organoid invasion, confirming the role of Wnt signaling in peritoneal metastasis. Further research revealed that after activation of the Wnt pathway, the expression of a series of downstream genes related to cell proliferation and migration (such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1) was upregulated, and this effect could be blocked by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout [109]. During metabolic reprogramming, ARRB1 promotes aerobic glycolysis by blocking PKM2 tetramerisation [110], whereas PKM2 activation (DASA-58) or ARRB1 deletion restores oxidative metabolism and suppresses proliferation [111]. In addition, the RNA-editing enzyme adenosine deaminases acting on RNA 1 (ADAR1) enhances lipid metabolism and drug resistance by editing the stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1) mRNA. CRISPR-mediated ADAR1 knockdown or SCD1 inhibition (SIP-4) reduced lipid synthesis and restored chemosensitivity [112]. Together, these findings underscore the value of gastric cancer PDOs as models for uncovering molecular mechanisms and therapeutic targets, thereby bridging basic research and translational precision medicine.

The integration of CRISPR gene editing with single-cell sequencing (such as perturb-seq) or spatial transcriptomics (such as DBiT-seq) enables the high-resolution mapping of signaling networks in GCOs [113]. Simultaneous knockout of YAP and Wnt pathway components within the Hippo axis leads to the loss of stemness and induces differentiation, revealing synergistic regulation between the 2 pathways [114]. Moreover, integrating organoid-microenvironment co-culture with CRISPR/Cas9 has uncovered mechanisms ranging from tumor initiation to metabolic and RNA regulatory pathways, providing a strong mechanistic basis for therapeutic innovation. In the future, coupling PDO co-culture systems with AI-driven multiomics will help build a comprehensive tumor ecosystem model, offering solid support for precision medicine in gastric cancer.

DRUG SCREENING AND SENSITIVITY TESTING

PDOs have been integrated into high-throughput drug-screening platforms to systematically assess the responses to chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. Multiple research groups have established PDO biobanks for dose-response testing of drugs such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), oxaliplatin, trastuzumab, and novel compounds such as SIGX1094 using 384-well or higher-density systems [18115]. Li et al. [18] constructed a biobank of 57 gastric cancer PDOs and identified gene expression profiles linked to drug sensitivity and resistance. The PDO results showed 91.7% consistency with patients’ clinical outcomes, confirming their predictive value. Large-scale CRISPR screening revealed that ERCC6 and ERCC4 in the nucleotide excision repair pathway are key modulators of cisplatin resistance, advancing our understanding of chemoresistance mechanisms [104]. The CureGA high-throughput platform applies PDOs for drug combination testing, facilitating an optimized therapy design [116]. In 2024, Yang et al. [117] discovered subtype-specific differences in kinase inhibitor sensitivity, verifying that aurora kinase inhibitors selectively target diffuse-type gastric cancer. These studies provided robust evidence for stratified treatment and personalized drug optimization for gastric cancer.

The predictive accuracy of gastric cancer PDOs for clinical drug response has been extensively validated. Comparative studies have demonstrated that PDO sensitivities to standard chemotherapy regimens (such as fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin and docetaxel [FLOT] and trastuzumab) and targeted agents show high concordance with patients’ actual clinical outcomes, supporting their role as reliable predictors of therapeutic efficacy [18115]. Building on this foundation, the functional drug sensitivity testing platform, an approach grounded in functional precision medicine, has entered multicenter clinical trials in China, enabling personalized screening of treatment regimens for patients with gastric cancer [22]. Moreover, a vascularized organoid model developed by Choi et al. [19] using 3D bioprinting technology successfully recapitulated tumor vascular networks, allowing the precise assessment of drug delivery dynamics. This model significantly improves the predictive accuracy of anti-angiogenic agents (such as vascular endothelial growth factor receptor inhibitors), highlighting its potential for simulating individualized therapeutic responses.

Microenvironment-enhanced PDOs and co-culture models have been employed to explore immune escape mechanisms and evaluate programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) checkpoint inhibitor therapies [118]. These systems faithfully reproduce immune evasion pathways in vitro, enabling screening of novel immunomodulatory drugs [119120]. Compared with PDX models, which require 2–6 months to establish and are costly, PDOs can be generated and screened within 2–3 weeks, supporting high-throughput and reproducible testing [121122]. Their predictive accuracy for clinical response reaches 80%–90%, highlighting their potential in precision immunotherapy for gastric cancer [123124].

The combination of the organoid technology with emerging approaches has expanded the scope of gastric cancer research. Chan et al. [40] developed an intestinal neuron-GCO co-culture model, enabling the CRISPR-Cas9 screening of 20,000 genes. Two lipid metabolism regulators were identified, and neuronal interactions were found to enhance lipid dependence, reprogram tumor metabolism, and increase sensitivity to lanosterol synthase inhibitors, revealing a novel neurometabolic therapeutic pathway. In immunotherapy, organoid-based systems are used to recreate the TME [125126127]. Co-culturing tumor organoids with autologous immune cells or tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) enhances T cell cytotoxicity and induces tumor-specific immune responses [53], while elucidating the roles of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) in immune suppression [128]. These advances demonstrate that GCOs have evolved from drug prediction tools to mechanistic TME platforms capable of reproducing neural, stromal, and immune interactions and supporting high-throughput combination therapy testing. Such systems bridge the shift in gastric cancer immunotherapy from generalized approaches to personalized precision medicine.

Studies on tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance

Tumor heterogeneity is a key driver of treatment failure and drug resistance at genetic, phenotypic, and microenvironmental levels. PDOs that retain the subclonal diversity of primary tumors provide an ideal platform for investigating tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance mechanisms, thereby supporting precision therapy.

Unlike 2D cell lines that lose low-abundance resistant clones during culture [129130131132133134], gastric cancer PDOs show a high success rate of up to 78% [18], maintaining histological integrity, genomic variation, and stable gene expression for over 50 passages [84]. Their gene expression profiles show far greater similarity to patient tumors than those of conventional models. More importantly, PDOs can accurately preserve and characterize low-frequency resistant subclones. For instance, PDOs derived from primary lesions and metastatic lymph nodes of gastric adenosquamous carcinoma replicated both adenocarcinoma and squamous carcinoma phenotypes, while retaining highly drug-resistant subpopulations [135]. Through integration of single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics, researchers identified seven tumor meta-programs (MP1-MP7) with distinct spatial distributions in gastric cancer. Among them, MP7 displayed the highest malignancy and was tightly linked to metastatic potential and therapy resistance [136]. T Such spatially resolved heterogeneity mapping provides a powerful framework for precisely targeting resistant subclones, paving the way for next-generation precision therapeutics in gastric cancer.

The drug-resistant phenotype of gastric cancer subclones results from the interplay between genetic alterations and the TME. For chemotherapy resistance, multi-omics analysis identified the matrix-enriched (CS2) subtype as highly drug-tolerant. Loss of KLF9 increases IL-6 and IL-1β expression, strengthening resistance and confirming the key role of inflammatory signaling [137]. In targeted therapy (Fig. 2), trastuzumab resistance in HER2-positive gastric cancer involves HER2 mutations and the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway via PIK3CA mutations or PTEN loss, enabling escape from HER2 inhibition [138139]. Additionally, MP7 subclones recruit fibroblasts through the GDF15/TGFBR2 axis, inducing myofibroblastic CAFs to secrete R-spondin3, promoting MP7-type resistance and suppressing CD8+ T cell infiltration, leading to immune evasion [136]. Targeting the GDF15–R-spondin3 loop may offer new therapeutic strategies.

PDOs function not only as faithful models of tumor subclonal heterogeneity but also as effective platforms for validating drug resistance mechanisms and guiding precision therapy. PDO-based drug sensitivity tests showed a 91.7% match with clinical outcomes; for instance, PDO responses to 5-FU in diffuse-type gastric cancer have directly informed treatment selection [18]. Targeted interventions demonstrate strong potential; the PI3K inhibitor taselisib selectively eliminates MP7 subclones, and its combination with lapatinib blocks their chemotactic activity [136]. In HER2-resistant models, combining antibody-drug conjugates with PI3K inhibitors restores therapeutic sensitivity by co-suppressing receptors and downstream signaling [140141]. Integrating spatial transcriptomics, single-cell omics, and gene editing will further enhance the role of PDOs as core preclinical platforms for unraveling gastric cancer heterogeneity and overcoming drug resistance.

Organoids combined with immune microenvironment studies

The heterogeneity of gastric cancer and its complex TME hinder the precision of immunotherapy. Co-culture systems that combine PDOs with immune cells offer an effective approach to overcome this limitation. These models preserve tumor genetic fidelity and phenotypic diversity while reproducing immune-tumor interactions, making them key platforms for studying immune escape and predicting therapeutic responses.

Advances in organoid-immune cell co-culture technologies have enabled precise reconstruction of the gastric cancer TME (Fig. 3). Three major approaches are commonly used. Matrix-gel models introduce exogenous immune cells such as peripheral blood lymphocytes or cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) to study tumor–immune interactions [53142]. Co-culturing CTLs with dendritic cells can replicate anti-PD-L1-mediated cytotoxicity [143144]. The air-liquid interface method preserves endogenous immune and stromal components, maintaining a high-fidelity T-cell receptor repertoire and supporting studies on immune checkpoint blockade [120126]. Microfluidic chip systems recreate oxygen gradients and integrate CAFs and MDSCs [53145], allowing observation of enhanced tumor proliferation and immunosuppression in gastric cancer PDOs [146147148].

However, reconstructing a complete TME in vitro, particularly by incorporating CAFs and autologous immune cells into a co-culture system, remains a significant technical challenge [127]. The gastric cancer TME is dynamic and complex, with interactions between immune cells, matrix components, and tumor organoids that change both spatially and temporally [149]. The interaction between stromal cells (such as CAFs) and immune cells is crucial, as these stromal cells influence tumor growth and metastasis and regulate tumor cell gene expression and drug response by remodeling the microenvironment [150]. The GCO and immune cell co-culture system has been used to model immunosuppressive mechanisms in the gastric cancer immune microenvironment, including PD-1/PD-L1 signaling inhibition of CD8+ T cells and the role of MDSCs. This system helps to predict immunotherapy efficacy [53]. However, the precise reconstruction of the dynamic interactions between GCOs and the immune microenvironment remains a challenge. Recent advances in microfluidic chip technology and 3D cell culture platforms have improved the simulation of immune escape mechanisms but still face challenges in maintaining precise interactions between cells and matrix components in vitro [68]. Metabolic regulation, such as the inhibition of nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase to modulate nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide+ metabolism and enhance CD8+ T cell function, has also emerged as a promising strategy for improving immune checkpoint inhibition therapy, as demonstrated in organoid and immune cell co-culture models [151].

Maintaining the functionality and activity of autologous immune cells, particularly the balance between TILs and peripheral blood mononuclear cells, remains a significant technical challenge. PDO immune co-culture systems are powerful models for studying PD-1/PD-L1-mediated immunotherapy responses. Spatial interactions between CD8+ T cells and PD-L1+ tumor cells can be visualized by immunofluorescence, while cytokine assays (interferon-γ, tumor necrosis factor-α) quantify ICI-induced immune activation [53152]. The tumor suppressor hypermethylated in cancer 1 promotes gasdermin D-dependent pyroptosis, enhances CD8+ T cell infiltration, and raises tumor inhibition rates to more than 80% when combined with PD-L1 blockade, suggesting a new target for combination therapy [53152]. The natural compound trefoil factor 2-mouse serum albumin restores anti-PD-1 sensitivity by modulating C-X-C chemokine receptor 4 signaling and reducing immunosuppressive neutrophils, thus offering new immunotherapy strategies [153]. PDO-based studies have also revealed mechanisms underlying resistance to immunotherapy. In diffuse gastric cancer PDO-macrophage co-cultures, aurora kinase inhibitor-induced senescence drives monocyte chemoattractant protein-1/chemokine (C-C motif) ligand 2 secretion, promoting M2 macrophage polarization and immune suppression, thus providing a rationale for senolytic and ICI combination therapies [117154155]. Moreover, H. pylori infection recruits dendritic cells via epithelial chemokine secretion, which alters T cell activation and reshapes the gastric immune microenvironment [156157].

With the integration of AI technology into organoid platforms, immunotherapy predictions have become more precise. Machine learning models can analyze cell morphology, cytokine profiles, and other multidimensional data from co-culture systems to predict treatment responses and automate culture optimization [53157158]. The Food and Drug Administration’s acknowledgment of organoid-based assays underscores the transition from laboratory research to clinical applications [158]. In the future, the incorporation of vascularized structures and gut microbiota will further enhance organoid-immune models for mechanistic studies and personalized immunotherapy in gastric cancer. Although progress has been made, the co-culture system for simulating GCOs and the immune microenvironment requires further optimization. With advancements in multiomics, spatial transcriptomics, and metabolic reprogramming, more accurate models are expected to be developed, providing a reliable platform for immunotherapy and supporting personalized treatment plans.

Organoid biobank construction and precision medicine

Precision medicine, which tailors therapy to a patient’s genomic and molecular profile, is crucial for treating heterogeneous tumors such as gastric cancer. PDOs offer a powerful platform for this approach by enabling biobank construction, biomarker screening, and multi-omics integration, thereby advancing personalized treatments for gastric cancer.

The construction of a large-scale organoid library

The establishment of GCO biobanks enables the systematic collection of samples across different patients, subtypes, and stages, providing essential resources for drug screening, resistance studies, and treatment evaluation. Yan et al. [89] built a biobank of organoids from the normal, dysplastic, tumor, and metastatic tissues of 34 patients, offering integrated whole-exome and transcriptomic data for mechanistic studies. A 2025 multicenter study achieved a 78% success rate in constructing 57 PDOs from 73 patients with gastric adenocarcinoma using Matrigel embedding and customized growth factors (Wnt3a and EGF), confirming the high genetic and phenotypic fidelity [18]. Ruijin Hospital established paired organoids from primary and metastatic lesions (SPDO1P/SPDO1LM) that maintained karyotype stability and were enriched in EMT-related genes [84]. Meanwhile, Shenzhen introduced droplet-engineered organoids and microsphere printing, significantly reducing the modeling time and enabling high-throughput standardized preparation [159]. These biobanks, integrated with clinical data, enable the correlation of organoid drug responses with patient outcomes and provide a robust foundation for drug development and personalized gastric cancer therapy.

Biomarker screening and prognostic prediction tools

PDOs that retain the molecular characteristics of gastric cancer provide an effective platform for biomarker discovery and prognostic evaluation. Analyses of immune infiltration, PD-L1 expression, and key mutations, such as PIK3CA and TP53, enable the identification of drug resistance markers and the prediction of immunotherapy responses, supporting precision treatment across chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy.

Li et al. [18] established 57 gastric cancer PDO lines from 73 patients (78% success rate), covering the intestinal, diffuse, and EBV-related subtypes. WES confirmed strong concordance with primary tumors, including TP53, CDH1, and ARID1A mutations, HER2 amplification, and MSI-H status. High-throughput screening of 5-FU, oxaliplatin, and other drugs showed that 5-FU-sensitive PDOs were enriched in p53 pathway genes (TP53INP1, CDKN1A), while resistant ones highly expressed FKBP10 and LDHA; the predictive AUC of the resistance gene panel exceeded 0.8, matching 91.7% of clinical responses [18]. Moreover, the multi-omics integration of transcriptome, DNA methylation, and mutation data has enabled the development of a gastric cancer multi-omics prognostic score (GMPS) based on programmed cell death genes [89162]. PDO-based prognostic prediction tools have evolved from static gene panels to dynamic assessment systems that integrate drug sensitivity phenotypes and metabolomic-derived prognostic models, thereby significantly improving their clinical applicability. A European multicenter study on FLOT neoadjuvant chemotherapy for locally advanced esophageal and gastric adenocarcinoma established PDOs from endoscopic biopsy samples of 120 patients and defined a PDO drug sensitivity threshold that effectively differentiated FLOT responders from non-responders in an independent cohort (n=85), achieving 90% sensitivity, 100% specificity, and 92% accuracy [115]. This approach helps avoid unnecessary toxicity caused by ineffective chemotherapy. Integrating metabolomics with PDO further enhances prognostic modeling. Chen et al. [163] developed a machine learning-based prognostic model (28-PM) using organoid metabolomic data (28 key metabolites, such as glutathione and S-adenosylmethionine). The model achieved robust predictive performance during validation (C-index=0.83, AUC=0.832, sensitivity=90%, specificity=70%). In drug resistance and prognosis research, key advances have emerged: PDO models have identified KHDRBS3 as a mediator of 5-FU resistance, and gene knockout has reversed the resistant phenotype [80]. Sun et al. [54] showed that CAF-derived exosomal CCT6A enhances PDO chemoresistance via β-catenin activation, correlating with poor patient prognosis. Furthermore, in a cohort of 24 patients, PDO sensitivity was strongly correlated with the percentage of tumor necrosis in surgical specimens (r=0.87, P=0.003), underscoring the reliability of PDOs in predicting clinical outcomes.

The combination of omics data and precision medicine

The integration of omics technologies has significantly advanced the application of precision medicine in gastric cancer. By combining PDOs with genomic, transcriptomic, and epigenetic data, researchers can comprehensively characterize the molecular landscape of gastric cancer and elucidate the mechanisms underlying drug response and resistance. Through WES and RNA sequencing, significant differences in gene expression, mutation patterns, and epigenetic profiles have been identified among gastric cancer subtypes, providing a robust theoretical basis for individualized therapies [164]. Single-cell sequencing enables PDOs to reveal intra-tumoral cellular heterogeneity, offering new insights into tumor evolution and therapeutic stratification [165].

The integration of organoid biobanks with multiomics technologies marks a transition from phenotypic association to mechanistic exploration. The introduction of spatiotemporal omics in 2024 will open new research avenues in spatial biology. The laminated organoid spatial-resolution transcriptomics technology, developed by Tsinghua University and collaborators, overcomes the challenges of small organoid size and sparse cellularity through mechanical lamination and stereo-seq-based single-cell spatial transcriptomics. This approach successfully mapped the cellular architecture of mouse lung and liver organoids, revealing neighboring interactions between alveolar type II cells and macrophages [159]. The same strategy can be applied to tumor organoids to quantitatively analyze tumor–immune spatial communication networks and enable precise immunotherapy target discovery. In gastric cancer research, single-cell transcriptomics play a pivotal role in multiomics integration. Identification of the CCKBR+ stem-like cancer cell subpopulation provided insights into tumor plasticity and progression [166]. Further genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic analyses of paired gastric cancer PDOs at Ruijin Hospital revealed upregulated angiogenesis pathways in metastatic organoids, correlating drug sensitivity with expression patterns; primary PDOs were more responsive to oxaliplatin, while metastatic PDOs showed resistance [84]. These findings provide molecular evidence for personalized chemotherapy. Moreover, integrating microfluidic platforms with omics enables the real-time analysis of cell-microenvironment interactions. Optimized organoid-on-chip systems can capture both signaling dynamics and gene expression changes. For instance, H. pylori VacA was found to induce immune evasion in gastric cancer PDOs [167].

Construction of GCO biobanks provides high-fidelity models and robust experimental evidence for precision medicine. CURRENT CHALLENGES AND FUTURE DEVELOPMENT DIRECTIONS

PDOs, advanced 3D culture models that closely mimic the molecular and structural features of primary tumors, hold great promise in tumor research, drug screening, and precision medicine. However, challenges remain in terms of standardization, microenvironmental reconstruction, cost, and clinical translation. Future efforts should focus on improving culture protocols, reducing costs, and integrating multimodal technologies to enhance the translational and clinical value of GCOs for personalized therapy.

Despite recent progress, there is no standardized protocol for cultivating GCOs (PDOs). Variations in cultivation conditions, such as matrix gel type, culture medium composition, and growth factors, affect organoid morphology, gene expression, and proliferation. For example, Matrigel derived from mouse sarcomas shows batch-to-batch variability, which impacts consistency [168]. Additionally, microenvironmental factors, such as oxygen concentration, pH, and ECM components, significantly influence organoid growth and adaptability. As Vlachogiannis et al. [37] emphasized, standardized culture conditions and analytical protocols are essential for the reliable use of PDOs as in vitro diagnostic tools. Although PDOs effectively simulate the cellular heterogeneity of gastric tumors, their ability to reproduce the TME remains limited. Traditional GCO models primarily reflect epithelial components and lack essential elements of the in vivo tumor ecosystem, such as immune cells, CAFs, vascular endothelial cells, and neurons. Consequently, these simplified systems fail to replicate complex tumor–microenvironment interactions, including immune evasion, angiogenesis, fibrosis, and treatment resistance. This limitation makes them inadequate for evaluating increasingly important immunotherapies, such as PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors and antiangiogenic drugs [169]. Cattaneo et al. [170] systematically expounded the challenges and strategies for constructing organoid models containing immune cells, identifying this as a major technical bottleneck in the field. The success rate of establishing GCO biobanks is not 100% and is significantly influenced by the tumor subtype, sample quality, and history of neoadjuvant therapy. The large-scale biobank constructed by Yan et al. [89] reported an overall success rate of approximately 70%–80%, although certain subtypes remain challenging to culture and maintain. Furthermore, the process from sample acquisition to organoid expansion and drug screening often takes weeks to months, posing a serious time window challenge for patients with advanced gastric cancer who require urgent therapeutic guidance. However, the high cost of culture media and the dependence on skilled technicians limit the widespread clinical adoption of PDO technology. Currently, PDO models focus primarily on cancer cell cultures; however, the true TME consists of multiple interacting cell types.

Co-culture technology effectively addresses these challenges by combining GCOs with autologous immune cells, CAFs, or endothelial progenitor cells to create models that better mimic the tumor immune microenvironment. Ota et al. [149] demonstrated the potential of co-culturing gastric cancer PDOs with autologous immune cells to evaluate personalized immunotherapy regimens, whereas Neal et al. [120] showed that such co-culture systems could effectively simulate the efficacy of ICIs. In conclusion, these models are valuable tools for studying tumor-immune system interactions and screening combination therapies, enabling more personalized gastric cancer research. However, the gastric cancer co-culture system faces several challenges, including differences in the components of gastric cancer and immune cell culture media, which may affect cell health and function in co-culture [171]; difficulty obtaining sufficient, high-quality autologous immune cells, especially in patients with advanced gastric cancer, where the immune system may be impaired; and the complexity of immune cell collection within a short time frame, hindering subsequent cultivation. Finally, although microfluidic systems offer dynamic TMEs for studying metastasis and immune escape, high design and production demands can cause batch-to-batch variability, affecting reproducibility and scalability [172].

A unified and standardized cultivation protocol is crucial for the reliable use of GCOs. Future efforts should focus on developing animal-free matrices, optimizing standardized culture media, and establishing consistent drug sensitivity testing procedures to ensure reproducibility across laboratories and institutions. Combining organoid technology with the cutting-edge organ-on-a-chip and bioengineering approaches represents a promising direction for the future. Organ-on-chip systems can apply controlled fluid shear stress, mechanical tension, and spatial architecture to replicate key physical cues within tumors, such as vascular perfusion and gastrointestinal peristalsis, thus providing a dynamic platform to investigate cancer cell invasion and metastasis [173174175]. Kim et al. [81] developed gastrointestinal ECM hydrogels that enabled long-term passaging and transplantation of organoids by recreating a physiologically relevant gastrointestinal microenvironment. Proteomic profiling further delineated the tissue-specific and age-dependent ECM signatures that influence organoid development. Integrating organoids with single-cell multi-omics sequencing, CRISPR-based functional screening, and other molecular technologies allows for an in-depth dissection of the molecular origins of tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance mechanisms [176177178]. As Huang et al. [179] envisioned, the integration of organoids with bioengineering platforms is pivotal for achieving functional maturation and improving translational fidelity. A fundamental solution to the issue of standardization is the development of chemically defined, animal-free matrices and optimized, uniform culture media. Advancement of automated high-throughput systems for organoid culture, processing, imaging, and data analysis is equally important. Cao et al. [180] highlighted the significant potential of automation in reducing human error, increasing reproducibility, and enhancing throughput, thereby paving the way for large-scale drug screening and clinical translation. Emerging technologies, such as AI, 3D bioprinting, and regenerative medicine, are expected to transform the organoid landscape. AI-driven algorithms can analyze organoid morphology, correlate gene expression profiles with drug sensitivity, and construct rapid predictive models, such as predicting drug responses based on image-recognized apoptosis rates, to markedly shorten testing cycles [181]. Zhou et al. [182] introduced PharmaFormer, a model that enhances drug response prediction by integrating organoid-specific pharmacogenomic datasets with large-scale cell line pharmacological data. Furthermore, 3D bioprinting technology enables the scalable and standardized production of organoids, minimizes batch-to-batch variability, and meets the growing demands for high-throughput drug screening [183184185]. The ability of 3D-printed models—such as those described by Ju et al. [186]—to closely mimic in vivo physiological conditions underscores their value in identifying therapeutic targets and predicting patient-specific responses. Finally, combining organoid technology with regenerative medicine allows the generation of normal gastric mucosal organoids that can be co-assessed with tumor organoids to simultaneously evaluate drug efficacy and toxicity, thereby providing a dual assessment platform that bridges preclinical research and clinical applications.

GCO technology holds great promise for tumor research and personalized treatment, but it faces challenges in standardization, co-culture techniques, and microenvironment reconstruction. Future developments will focus on optimizing culture conditions; integrating platforms such as microfluidic systems, AI, and 3D bioprinting; and enhancing clinical applications. With technological advances, GCOs are evolving into key platforms for personalized treatment and drug screening, with the potential to drive breakthroughs in precision oncology and provide more effective and personalized cancer solutions.

CONCLUSIONS

Organoids have emerged as powerful and versatile in vitro models for studying gastric cancer. Owing to their high biological fidelity and ability to recapitulate the TME, GCOs have become invaluable tools for basic research, drug screening, and personalized therapy. Compared to traditional 2D cell lines, organoids more accurately reproduce the complex heterogeneity and microenvironmental architecture of tumors, offering a superior experimental platform for investigating tumorigenesis, progression, and drug resistance mechanisms. In the fields of drug discovery and precision oncology, GCOs can simulate patient-specific tumor characteristics, enabling reliable drug response predictions and guiding the development of personalized treatment regimens. Nevertheless, several challenges remain, including standardization of culture protocols, culture efficiency, cost control, and faithful reconstruction of the TME. Future research should focus on optimizing culture conditions, enhancing the scalability and stability of organoid models, and incorporating key microenvironmental components such as immune, vascular, and stromal cells to better mimic in vivo tumor biology. The integration of AI, big data analytics, and multi-omics technologies is expected to further increase the predictive and translational power of organoid systems, facilitating precise drug screening and treatment response assessment. In summary, the rapid advancement of GCO technology provides a robust foundation for precision medicine and individualized therapies. In the future, these models will play an increasingly pivotal role in accelerating new drug development, refining therapeutic strategies, and improving patient outcomes.
