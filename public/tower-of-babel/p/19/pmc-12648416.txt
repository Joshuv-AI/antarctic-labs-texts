Highly Emissive BODIPY-Grafted Polymer Dots for Cellular and Tissular STED Imaging
Authors: Xingxing Yao, Jiantao Ping, Long Chen, Liqing Qi, Kehua Tang, Ren Cai, Li Li, Yating Xiao, Lubin Qi, Qiongzheng Hu, Xiaohong Fang, Yifei Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy requires fluorescent probes to exhibit high brightness, good photostability, a sensitive optical depletion response, and narrow spectral features. There are great interests in using polymer dots (Pdots) for STED imaging due to their exceptional brightness and photobleaching resistance. However, the conventional Pdots either suffer from broad spectra or an unsatisfactory STED response. Herein, we developed a general method for obtaining Pdots with desirable optical properties for STED microscopy. Specifically, boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY) chromophores were grafted on to a polystyrene backbone to obtain polymers with narrow spectral profiles. The grafting ratio was precisely controlled to minimize aggregation-induced quenching. Conjugating BODIPYs to side chains reduced interactions between the chromophores, resulting in a long excited state lifetime, which is critical for obtaining complete fluorescence depletion. Using this strategy, we synthesized three-color Pdots with narrow spectra features. Compared to directly encapsulating BODIPYs into nanoparticles, our strategy achieved 2–10 times higher single-particle brightness. We used Pdots for single-particle, cellular, and tissular STED imaging. The Pdots showed high spatial resolutions and could clearly resolve subdiffraction-limit structures in cells and tissue sections, indicating great application potential in in vitro diagnostics and biomedical imaging applications.

Introduction

In recent years, stimulated emission depletion (STED) microscopy has become a powerful tool in biomedical research, enabling detailed imaging of subcellular structures and molecular interactions at nanoscale. , Compared to other super-resolution techniques, STED imaging combines high spatial resolution with decent imaging speed and does not rely on postacquisition data processing for resolution enhancement. , However, STED microscopy utilizes a stimulated emission process to reduce the area of excitation to the nanometer scale, which places high demands on the photophysical properties of the fluorescent probes. An ideal STED probe should exhibit high brightness, good photostability, and sensitive optical depletion response. , Narrow spectral features are also desirable when performing multicolor STED imaging. , Conventional STED probes, including small molecule dyes and fluorescent proteins, exhibit relatively low brightness and are prone to photobleaching in STED imaging. Fluorescent nanoparticles, in contrast, typically show high brightness and good photostability, thus having great application potential in STED microscopy.

Among different types of fluorescent nanoparticles, polymer dots (Pdots) stand out due to their excellent photophysical properties, relatively small size, and facile bioconjugation. − Previous researches have shown that Pdots are particularly suited for long-term STED imaging due to their exceptional brightness and photostability. , However, their optical depletion responses are not as sensitive as those of organic dyes. This is due to the fact that Pdots are comprised of high density of chromophores, which results in strong interchromophore interactions and reduced fluorescence lifetime upon formation of nanoparticles. − The fast spontaneous emission competes with the stimulated emission process, which makes it difficult to achieve complete optical depletion and obtain a high spatial resolution in STED microscopy. In addition, Pdots previously used in STED imaging typically exhibit broad absorption and excitation spectra, which were not suitable for multiplexed STED imaging. ,

To overcome these limitations, we developed a general method for obtaining Pdots with desirable optical properties for STED microscopy. Boron-dipyrromethene (BODIPY) derivatives were chosen as the fluorophores due to their high fluorescence quantum yields (QYs), large molar extinction coefficients, long excited state lifetime, and narrow spectral profiles. − As shown in Scheme , BODIPY chromophores of different colors were synthesized and grafted onto a polystyrene backbone to obtain multicolor polymers. The grafting ratio was precisely controlled to minimize aggregation-induced quenching. Using the BODIPY polymers, we synthesized three-color Pdots with narrow spectral profiles. The full width at half-maximum (fwhm) of the absorption/emission spectra was as low as 25 nm, about half of those of conventional Pdots. Compared to directly encapsulating BODIPYs into nanoparticles, our strategy achieved 2–10 times higher single-particle brightness. Conjugating BODIPYs to side chains of the polymers reduced interactions between the chromophores in Pdots, resulting in long excited state lifetimes (3–5 ns), which are critical for obtaining complete fluorescence depletion. We used the Pdots for single-particle, cellular, and tissular STED imaging. The Pdots showed high spatial resolutions (40–85 nm) and could clearly resolve subdiffraction-limit structures, including microtubule networks and the distribution of CK19, MUC1, and EpCAM proteins, in cells and tissue sections. Overall, we developed a general method to synthesize highly emissive Pdots with a long fluorescent lifetime and narrow spectral profiles for STED imaging, which has great application potential in in vitro diagnostics and biomedical imaging applications.

Results and Discussion

Preparation of Multicolor BODIPY Pdots

The synthesis routes and steps for small molecules and polymers are presented in the Supporting Information (Figures S1–S3). Polystyrene polymers with different ratios of conjugation functional groups were synthesized. BODIPY of three absorption/emission colors (denoted as BD488, BD561, and BD640) were synthesized and grafted onto a polystyrene backbone to obtain multicolor polymers (denoted as P1, P2, and P3, respectively). The Pdots were then synthesized from the BODIPY polymers using the nanoprecipitation method reported previously. − As shown in Figure S4, the highest fluorescence intensity of the Pdots was achieved when the grafting ratio was 10%. Upon further increasing the grafting ratio to 20%, aggregation-induced quenching occurred. Therefore, a 10% grafting ratio was used for the following characterizations. For comparison, BODIPY small molecule dots were also prepared, which involved encapsulating the three BODIPY derivatives with polystyrene to form nanoparticles (denoted as BD488 dots, BD561 dots, and BD640 dots). Under the same encapsulated chromophore density, the fluorescence quenching of the small molecule dots was evident as compared to the Pdots. As shown in Figure A, under the excitation of a UV lamp, the BODIPY Pdots exhibited a significantly stronger fluorescence intensity than the BODIPY small molecule dots of the same concentration. The Pdots and the small molecule dots exhibit similar spectral features (Figure B,C and Figure S5). All the Pdots exhibit relatively narrow full bandwidth in both absorption (ranging from 24 to 31 nm.) and emission spectra (ranging from 22 to 51 nm). Specifically, P1 Pdots showed a maximum absorption peak at 495 nm and exhibited an fwhm of 52 nm in the emission spectrum. P2 Pdots displayed a maximum absorption at 571 nm and a narrower emission fwhm of 25 nm. P3 Pdots exhibited peak absorption at 650 nm and an emission fwhm of 26 nm. These results demonstrate that the absorption spectra of the three Pdots are well aligned with standard laser excitation wavelengths, and their relatively narrow absorption and emission bandwidths highlight their potential for applications in multicolor STED microscopy.

We then characterized the size of the Pdots by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Figure D–F, all Pdots exhibited uniform spherical morphologies, with hydrodynamic diameters centered at approximately 24 nm. TEM imaging confirmed monodisperse distributions without detectable aggregation. The zeta potentials of the Pdots were −20 mV. These results demonstrate the successful preparation of colloidally stable, homogeneous Pdots with well-defined sizes, which are suitable for biolabeling and imaging.

For quantitative comparison of the nanoparticle brightness, we systematically measured the QYs of the BODIPY Pdots and BODIPY small molecule dots. As summarized in Table , the P1 Pdots exhibited a QY of 20.67%, significantly surpassing the 11.20% QY of BD488 dots (2-fold enhancement). Similarly, P2 Pdots achieved a QY of 59.71% versus 8.15% for BD561 dots (6-fold improvement), while P3 Pdots demonstrated a QY of 35.25% compared to 2.70% for BD640 dots (10-fold improvement). This systematic comparison demonstrates that the covalent grafting of modified BODIPY derivatives onto polymer side chains substantially amplifies fluorescence QY compared to direct encapsulation. This is due to the fact that the nonfluorescent polymer spacers sterically isolate BODIPY molecules, effectively suppressing the π nointeractions between the chromophores and, as a result, minimizing quenching. We also calculated the molar extinction coefficients of single fluorophores in the Pdots and the BODIPY small molecule dots. P1 and P3 Pdots exhibited molar extinction coefficients that are several times higher than those of the corresponding small molecule dots. Though, for P2 Pdots, the molar extinction coefficient of the small molecule dots was higher. Combining the molar extinction coefficients and the QYs, we concluded that the BODIPY Pdots exhibited much higher brightness than the corresponding small molecule dots, which is consistent with the observation that the Pdots showed much stronger emission than the BODIPY small molecule dots under the same concentration. To investigate the pH sensitivity of the Pdots, the emission spectra of P1–P3 Pdots were systematically tested across pH 5–9. The results confirmed that the fluorescence intensity was greatest at physiological pH (7.0) and decreased under acidic/alkaline conditions (see Figure S6 in the Supporting Information).

We then characterized the fluorescence lifetimes of the BODIPY Pdots. In general, fluorophores with fluorescence lifetimes in the range of a few nanoseconds are most suitable for STED. If the fluorescence lifetime is too short (e.g., below 1 ns), then spontaneous decay can compete with the stimulated emission, leading to incomplete optical depletion and unsatisfactory STED imaging results. On the other hand, if the fluorescence lifetime is too long (e.g., tens of nanoseconds or more), then it is difficult to accumulate fluorescence photons and could affect the imaging efficiency. According to our test, the fluorescence lifetimes of the Pdots are between 3 and 5 ns (Figure S7), which are well suited for STED imaging.

Specific Cellular Labeling and Imaging of the Pdots

For specific cell labeling, bioconjugation was performed by utilizing the EDC-catalyzed reaction between carboxyl groups on the Pdots surface and amine groups on the secondary antibody or streptavidin. − Then, these functionalized Pdots were coincubated with fixed cells prelabeled with primary antibodies. In Figure A, we demonstrated the targeting of three different proteins in cells (lamin A, tubulin, and clathrin) using the three-color Pdots. Due to their narrow excitation/emission spectral profiles, the images showed accurate protein localization with minimal interchannel interferences.

(B) Plots of the fluorescence intensity of P1 Pdots (Ex: 488 nm), P2 Pdots (Ex: 561 nm), and P3 Pdots (Ex: 640 nm) against photoirradiation time. To investigate the photostability of the Pdots, time-dependent images were recorded using a confocal fluorescence microscopy, acquired at 2 min intervals for a 20 min duration, and analyzed using ImageJ (Figure B,C). As shown in Figure C, the top panel depicts the imaging of cell microtubules (MTs) by P1 Pdots under excitation of a 488 nm laser, the middle panel shows the imaging of cell MTs by P2 Pdots under excitation of a 561 nm laser, and the bottom panel displays the imaging of cell MTs by P3 Pdots under excitation of a 640 nm laser. We also performed cell imaging with the BODIPY small molecule dots. However, severe leakage of fluorophores during the cell labeling process was observed, resulting in weak fluorescence signals. Therefore, for comparison of the photostability, we performed labeling and long-term imaging using three commercially available secondary antibody-conjugated fluorescent probes (AF488, AF594, and CL647) under the same conditions, and the results are shown in Figure S8. As presented in Figure B and Figure S8, the Pdots exhibited better photobleaching resistance and showed minimal fluorescence intensity attenuation under prolonged laser irradiation, demonstrating superior photostability suitable for long-term bioimaging applications.

Single-Particle and Cellular STED Imaging of BODIPY Pdots

Then, we further investigated the performance of P1 Pdots, P2 Pdots, and P3 Pdots in STED microscopy (Figure A–C). For STED imaging of the Pdots, the excitation wavelengths were 488, 561, and 640 nm, and the depletion wavelengths were 594, 660, and 775 nm, respectively. Figure A–C shows confocal and the corresponding STED images of the Pdots dispersed on a coverslip. Pronounced improvement in the lateral resolution can be observed in the images. The STED resolution was obtained from the line profile analysis of the selected particle (Figure D–F). Among the three Pdots, P1 Pdots exhibited the highest spatial resolution (40 nm, Figure D), which is several times better than the cross-sectional size obtained in the corresponding confocal image. For the other two Pdots, STED imaging achieved spatial resolutions of 70 nm (P2 Pdots, Figure E) and 85 nm (P3 Pdots, Figure F), further validating their suitability for super-resolution applications. In the cellular STED imaging experiments, the three-color Pdots were used to label microtubules (Figure A–C). The microtubules show continuous line structures in all of the imaging channels. Line profile analysis (Figure D–F) of microtubules demonstrated an fwhm ranging from 80 to 200 nm, consistent with the single-particle resolution of the 3 Pdots, the size of the antibodies used for labeling, and the diameter of the microtubules.

Multiplexed STED Imaging of Pathological Markers in Cells and Tissue Sections

To demonstrate the clinical application potential of these Pdots, we selected three pathological markers for endometrial cancer (CK19, MUC1, and EpCAM) and imaged them in cells and tissues. For labeling, we used rabbit, mouse, and rat primary antibodies to target CK19, MUC1, and EpCAM in cells and tissues, respectively. Then, the samples were incubated with the P1, P2, and P3 Pdots conjugated with different species secondary antibodies. The validation and multiplexing results of the target protein in the cells are shown in Figure A and the Supporting Information's Figure S9. The precise subcellular localization of these three target proteins, CK19 at cytoskeletons, MUC1 in cytoplasmic domains, and EpCAM at membrane-associated regions, can be clearly observed, providing critical spatial information for pathological investigations.

(A) Cell labeling of target proteins in HeLa cells measured by confocal microscopy. P1 Pdots labeled CK19, P2 Pdots labeled MUC1, and P3 Pdots labeled EpCAM. Scale bars = 10 μm. (B) Labeling and imaging of the Pdots on tissue sections performed across DAPI, FITC, CY3, and CY5 channels, respectively. Scale bars = 50 μm. (C) Comparative resolution analysis of P1–P3 Pdots under confocal and STED imaging modes. The cross-sectional sizes of 0.30, 0.36, and 0.71 μm were obtained from STED images, respectively, which are much smaller than the cross-sectional sizes obtained from the confocal images, which are 0.56, 0.61, and 1.47 μm, respectively.

We then investigated the performance of these Pdots in the labeling and imaging of endometrial cancer tissues. Imaging was performed across four spectral channels (DAPI, FITC, CY3, and CY5), and the results were summarized in Figure B. The P1 Pdots exhibited exclusive fluorescence emission in the FITC channel, indicating the presence of CK19. Similarly, P2 Pdots demonstrated channel-specific signals in CY3 with accurate MUC1 localization, while P3 Pdots predominantly emitted in the CY5 channel for EpCAM detection. Under STED imaging mode, the spatial resolution of the target proteins in tissues can be further improved, evidenced by the clearer visualization of the protein clusters and the narrower line profiles in STED images compared to confocal images (Figure C). Collectively, these findings confirm the distinct emission signatures, target-specific localization, and subdiffraction-limit resolution of these Pdots, highlighting their potential as robust candidates for multiplexed, super-resolution cellular and tissular pathology.

Conclusions

In summary, we designed three narrow-band-emissive BODIPY derivatives and covalently conjugated them onto polymer side chains, yielding Pdots (P1 Pdots, P2 Pdots, and P3 Pdots) with tailored photophysical properties. These Pdots exhibit narrow emission bandwidths, high fluorescence QYs, and exceptional antiphotobleaching performance, attributable to the spatial isolation of BODIPY fluorophores by nonfluorescent polymer spacers. Compared to directly encapsulating BODIPYs into nanoparticles, our strategy achieved 2–10 times higher single-particle brightness. Systematic validation through microtubule labeling, tumor-specific protein targeting, and tissue imaging demonstrated robust biotargeting specificity and exceptional photostability under prolonged laser irradiation. Super-resolution STED imaging showed that these Pdots exhibited exceptional spatial resolutions (40–85 nm) and could clearly resolve subdiffraction-limit structures. The integration of narrow spectral bandwidths, high brightness, and sensitive STED response positions these Pdots as versatile tools for multiplexed super-resolution imaging and diagnostic applications.
