Modeling the atrioventricular conduction axis using human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiac assembloids
Authors: Jiuru Li, Alexandra Wiesinger, Lianne Fokkert, Priscilla Bakker, Dylan K. de Vries, Anke J. Tijsen, Yigal M. Pinto, Arie O. Verkerk, Vincent M. Christoffels, Gerard J.J. Boink, Harsha D. Devalla
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe atrioventricular (AV) conduction axis provides electrical continuity between the atrial and ventricular chambers. The “nodal” cardiomyocytes populating this region (AV canal in the embryo, AV node from fetal stages onward) propagate impulses slowly, ensuring sequential contraction of the chambers. Dysfunction of AV nodal tissue causes severe disturbances in rhythm and contraction, and human models that capture its salient features are limited. Here, we report an approach for the reproducible generation of AV canal cardiomyocytes (AVCMs) with in vivo-like gene expression and electrophysiological profiles. We created the so-called “assembloids” composed of atrial, AVCM, and ventricular spheroids, which effectively recapitulated unidirectional conduction and the “fast-slow-fast” activation pattern typical for the vertebrate heart. We utilized these systems to reveal intracellular calcium mishandling as the basis of LMNA-associated AV conduction block. In sum, our study introduces novel cell differentiation and tissue construction strategies to facilitate the study of complex disorders affecting heart rhythm.

Introduction

Sequential contraction of atrial and ventricular chambers of the heart ensures coordinated contractions and blood flow. This critical feature is already present in embryonic development and is facilitated by a specialized region of myocardial cells called the atrioventricular (AV) canal, which connects the atrial and ventricular muscle.1 Slower conduction in the AV canal introduces a delay in propagation of electrical impulses, allowing sufficient time for the ventricular chamber to fill with blood. Studies, primarily in mouse and zebrafish models, have shown a role for wingless/integrated (WNT), bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), and Notch signaling in AV canal development.2 These signaling pathways activate a transcriptional program involving TBX2, TBX3, and MSX2, critical for maintenance of AV canal identity and suppression of chamber myocardial genes.2,3 In addition, GATA4/6, NKX2-5, and TBX5 have been implicated in the proper patterning and maintenance of homeostasis of the AV conduction system.2,3 As development proceeds, the myocardium of the AV canal forms the AV node and continues to maintain impulse delay. The distinct electrophysiological properties of AV nodal cardiomyocytes have been attributed to the presence of ion currents, which include the predominant expression of funny current (If), acetylcholine-activated potassium current, (IKACh), L-type calcium current ICa,L, and an absence of fast sodium current (INa).4 Dysfunction of the AV node, either due to genetic or acquired causes, results in a partial or complete block of impulse transmission, clinically termed AV block.5,6 In addition, the AV conduction axis is also the source of a number of arrhythmias, including Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome and AV nodal reentry tachycardia.7,8 Current treatments for AV conduction disorders involve medication, ablation, and/or implantation of an electronic pacemaker.9,10 Although certainly lifesaving, the implantation of electronic pacemakers is mainly aimed at symptom relief and does not address the root cause of the problem itself.

Tools to better understand how AV nodal cells function in health and disease would offer insights into biological processes that can be harnessed to develop novel therapies. Recently, efforts have been made to generate AV canal cardiomyocytes (AVCMs) from human pluripotent stem cells.11,12,13 Schmidt et al. introduced a two-stage approach, where mesoderm induction (by modifying concentrations of activin A and BMP4) from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), which most optimally promoted AV canal fate, was first determined, followed by application of BMP4 at the mesoderm stage to generate three-dimensional (3D) cardioids. While this manuscript was under review, Ye et al. reported the generation of AVCMs by exposing human embryonic stem cell (hESC)-derived differentiated cardiomyocytes at day 11 to GSK3ß inhibitor CHIR99021, which activates WNT signaling. Despite this progress, much remains to be learned about the molecular and electrophysiological properties of AVCMs, including their behavior in the context of bridging impulse propagation between atria and ventricles as well as their suitability for modeling complex conduction disorders.

Here, we developed a monolayer-based directed differentiation approach to generate AVCMs from hiPSCs using the application of WNT2 and retinoic acid (RA) at the cardiac mesoderm stage. The resulting cells demonstrated molecular and electrophysiological properties in line with in vivo AVCMs. In order to simulate the AV conduction axis, we created a multi-component tissue system composed of atrial, AVCM, and ventricular spheroids. These so-called cardiac assembloids were applied to uncover the molecular basis of AV block in patients with pathogenic variants in the LMNA gene.

Results

WNT2 and RA steer mesoderm to AV cardiomyocyte fate

To direct differentiation toward AVCMs, we followed the general principles of steering subtype specification in differentiating hiPSCs, which relies on modulating specific signaling pathways at the cardiac mesoderm stage (Figure 1A). Based on knowledge from embryonic heart development in animal models, we hypothesized that activation of WNT and RA signaling pathways would drive AVCM fate.14,15,16,17 To this end, we treated cardiac mesodermal cells at day 4 with WNT ligand WNT2 and RA (W+R). For comparison, we generated other cardiomyocyte subtypes, i.e., sinoatrial nodal (SANCMs), atrial (ACMs), and ventricular cardiomyocytes (VCMs) from hiPSCs using previously described protocols.15,16 Contractile cardiomyocytes were typically observed in all groups around day 10. We performed reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) to assess mRNA expression of classic cardiomyocyte lineage marker genes and of genes associated with specific subtypes (Figure S1). Pan muscle marker ACTN2 was expressed in all subtypes, albeit lower in SANCMs and W+R cardiomyocytes. NKX2-5 was similarly expressed in ACM, VCM, W+R groups, and was expectedly lower in SANCMs, in which only a subset of cells express this transcription factor.2 Importantly, the expression of BMP2, MSX2, TBX2, and TBX3, genes implicated in AVCM development were predominantly expressed in W+R cardiomyocytes.2 Immunostaining confirmed that the expression of TBX2 and MSX2 at the protein level was also predominant in W+R cardiomyocytes compared with other subtypes (Figures S2A and S2B). Expression of pacemaker genes TBX18 and SHOX2 found in the SANCM group was significantly lower in W+R cardiomyocytes. Furthermore, analysis of NPPA and NR2F2 (atrial lineage) and MYL2 and MYH7 (ventricular lineage) suggests that the W+R cultures contain both cells of AVCM identity and, potentially, a fraction of cells with chamber identity (Figure S1).

To determine the transcriptome and composition of W+R cultures, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of differentiated cells at day 19 (Figures 1C–1H). For the analysis, transcriptome data from W+R cells were combined with our previously published datasets constituting hiPSC-derived SANCMs, ACMs, and VCMs, resulting in a total of 2,412 cells.18,19 Unsupervised clustering identified 12 clusters, excluding 2 clusters (clusters 8 and 13) with dead cells/ambient RNA (Figure 1C). Based on the expression of TNNT2 and ACTN2 (Figure 1D), we determined that clusters 0–4, 6, and 11 represented cardiomyocytes. Cardiomyocytes in cluster 11 expressed several cyclin-dependent kinase genes (Table S1), indicating that they are a subpopulation at a different cell-cycle stage and were therefore excluded from further analysis. Clusters 5, 7, 9, 10, and 12 were composed of non-cardiomyocytes (COL1A1+, COL1A2+, COL6A3+, and VIM+ mesenchymal cells/fibroblasts in cluster 5; TBX18+, IGFBP5+, RDH10+, and KRT18+ proepicardial cells in cluster 7; and three small clusters [9, 10, and 12] of unknown identity) (Table S1).

The six cardiomyocyte clusters were extracted (Figures S3A and S3B) and annotated according to their gene expression patterns. Cardiomyocytes resulting from W+R treatment predominantly contributed to a distinct cluster (cluster 2) (Figure S3C), characterized by the expression of AVCM-associated genes TBX2, TBX3, and MSX2 as well as RSPO3 (Figure S3D). Cluster 2 also differentially expressed GATA6, NKX2-5, and TBX5 (Table S1), transcription factors crucial for heart development, with particular significance to AVCM development and function.2 The gene expression signature of cluster 2 was unique compared with the other cardiomyocyte subtypes, such as SANCMs (cluster 1; SHOX2+/ISL1+), sinus venosus (SV) (cluster 4; SHOX2+/TCF21+/COL3A1+), ACMs (cluster 0; NPPA+/NR2F2+), VCMs (cluster 3; MYL2+/HOPX+), and outflow tract (OFT) (cluster 6; ISL1+/HAPLN1+/PITX2+) (Figures S3E–S3I; Table S1).

To determine the composition of cells obtained from W+R treatment in more detail, we performed secondary clustering of this group, including all of the cells obtained from this differentiation protocol, which identified four clusters (Figure 1E; Table S2). The majority of cells obtained from W+R treatment are composed of cardiomyocytes expressing TNNT2 and ACTN2, distributed in clusters 0 (45%) and cluster 1 (24%), respectively (Figures 1E and 1F). Furthermore, both these clusters expressed AVCM-associated genes TBX2, TBX3, and BMP2 (Figure 1G). However, we noticed a key difference between the two clusters. In addition to AVCM-associated genes, cluster 1 differentially expressed atrial genes NPPA, HEY1, MYL7, and MYL4 (Figures 1G and 1H; Table S2), which is a feature of cells in the atrial transition zone of the AV conduction axis.20 Lastly, clusters 2 (20% of W+R population) and 3 (11% of W+R population) were identified as non-cardiomyocytes as they lacked expression of cardiomyocyte genes (Figures 1F and 1H). Although cluster 2 could be identified as proepicardial-like cell population based on the expression of WT1, TBX18, and BNC1 (Figures 1G and 1H; Table S2), we were not able to determine the identity of cluster 3, which was the smallest cluster. In summary, transcriptional characterization of W+R cells suggests that they are distinct from other hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte subtypes and their gene expression is suggestive of AVCM-like identity.

Cells generated from WNT2- and RA-treated cultures recapitulate molecular signature of in vivo AVCMs

To test the correlation between W+R cardiomyocytes and in vivo cell types, we utilized available scRNA-seq datasets.21,22,23,24 As hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes are expected to best correlate with fetal hearts, we first compared the W+R population with datasets from mouse hearts of embryos ranging from embryonic day (E) E9.25 to E13.5.21,22 For the integration of human and mouse data, we converted human gene names to their mouse orthologs using the R package “babelgene.” After integration and clustering, five cardiomyocyte clusters were identified in the murine heart datasets (Figure 2A). We ascertained the identity of these clusters as AVCMs (Figures 2B and S4A; Table S3), ACMs, and combined SV and SANCMs (SV_SANCM) populations that share developmental origin and phenotypic properties, VCMs, and OFT cardiomyocytes (Figures S4A, S4B, and S3), respectively, based on marker gene expression. This approach revealed that around 64% of W+R cardiomyocytes clustered together with murine AVCMs (Figures 2C and 2D). About 13% and 20% of the cells clustered with murine ACMs or VCMs, respectively (Figure 2D). To further confirm this finding, the top 50 differentially expressed genes of mouse AVCs (compared with mouse ACMs, SV_SANCMs, OFT cardiomyocytes, and VCMs) were extracted and utilized to assess gene module scoring. Among the different hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte subtypes (uniform manifold approximation and projection [UMAP] from Figure S3A), the highest correlation of the top 50 mouse AVCM genes was observed with the W+R population, reiterating their AVC-like identity (Figure 2E). Similarly, we also assessed the correlation of transcript signatures of W+R cardiomyocytes to the recently reported human fetal23 and adult24 AV node. The top 50 differentially expressed genes extracted from these human datasets and subsequent gene module scoring confirmed that W+R cardiomyocytes correlate closely with the human fetal and adult AV node (Figure 2F). Next, we assessed the comparability between AVCMs generated in this study and two other recently published studies that reported the derivation of AVCMs, including their expression profiles, from hiPSCs12 or hESCs,13 respectively. We first performed gene module scoring of their datasets using the top 50 genes from the mouse, human fetal, and human adult AV canal/node to ascertain identity (Figures S4C and S4D). The AVCM population from Schmidt et al.12 associated with the top 50 genes from the in vivo datasets (Figure S4C). However, the cell type reported in Ye et al.13 did not show meaningful correlation (Figure S4D). To gain further insight into the comparability of AVCMs from Schmidt et al.12 and AVCMs from this study, we performed gene module scoring using their top 50 genes (Figure S4E). This analysis shows that their top genes best correlate with a subset of our AVCMs (Figures S4E and S4F). Differences in signaling molecules/concentrations used to direct mesoderm generation, as well as AV canal fate or culture conditions (two-dimensional [2D] vs. 3D; media components), might underlie these differences. Overall, the comparison of W+R-treated cardiomyocytes with in vivo AVCMs confirms their lineage identity. W+R cardiomyocytes are referred to as AVCMs hereafter.

Next, we evaluated the action potential properties (Figure 3B) of AVCMs by single-cell patch-clamp and compared them with SANCMs. Representative action potential traces are shown in Figure 3C. To gain insights into the extent to which they recapitulate the salient differences seen in in vivo nodal cells, we included patch-clamp data of E17.5 murine nodal cells, considering the challenges in obtaining cells from the human heart. Representative action potential traces of murine nodal cells are shown in Figure 3D. Although cycle lengths in hiPSC-derived nodal cells were comparable, murine SANCMs had shorter cycle lengths compared with murine AVCMs (Figures 3E and 3F). Both hiPSC-AVCMs and murine AV nodal cells exhibited a more negative maximal diastolic potential (MDP) (−68.3 ± 1.1 mV in hiPSC-AVCMs; −66.1 ± 1.8 mV in murine AVCMs) compared with their respective SANCM counterparts (−63.3 ± 1.9 mV in hiPSC-SANCMs; −59.2 ± 2.0 mV in murine SANCMs). Upstroke velocities in murine nodal cells were comparable. Small but significantly faster upstroke velocities were noted in hiPSC-AVCMs compared with hiPSC-SANCMs. In addition, we observed that action potential amplitude (APA) was significantly higher in both human and murine AV groups (86.9 ± 3.2 mV in hiPSC-AVCMs; 94.9 ± 3.9 mV in murine AVCMs) compared with SANCMs (73.0 ± 2.4 mV in hiPSC-SANCMs; 76.8 ± 6.4 mV in murine SANCMs). Lastly, action potential duration (APD) at 20% (APD20), 50% (APD50), and 90% (APD90) repolarization was comparable between the nodal cell types in both mouse and hiPSC groups (Figures 3E and 3F). Our results demonstrate that hiPSC-derived AVCMs recapitulate salient electrophysiological properties of in vivo AVCMs.

In addition, we compared the action potential properties of hiPSC-derived AVCMs with hiPSC-derived ACMs and VCMs (Figure S5). Representative traces are shown in Figure S5A. Cycle lengths in AVCMs were shorter compared with VCMs, but not significantly different from ACMs (Figure S5B). Average MDP in ACMs was more negative than in AVCMs and VCMs, albeit not significant. Upstroke velocities in AVCMs were slower than in VCMs and comparable with those in ACMs. In addition, APAs in AVCMs and ACMs were similar and were significantly lower than those in VCMs. Lastly, APD20 and APD50 in AVCMs were significantly different from both ACMs and VCMs, while APD90 only differed from VCMs (Figure S5B). In conclusion, differences in action potential properties between AVCMs and chamber subtypes, particularly VCMs, were apparent and confirm their distinctive identity.

In addition, preferential expression of MSX2 and TBX2, along with lower expression of chamber genes NPPA and MYL2, corroborated the AVCM identity of the resulting cells (Figure 4A), further supported by their ion channel gene profile (Figure 4B). Electrophysiological properties measured using single-cell patch-clamp (Figure 4C) revealed the nodal nature of these cells, as evidenced by the prominent diastolic depolarization (phase 4) as well as slower upstroke velocities (Figures 4D and 4E), and were largely in agreement with the data shown in Figures 3 and S5. In an effort to further characterize some of the ion currents contributing to the action potential characteristics of AVCMs, we performed voltage clamp experiments. INa, which distinguishes chamber cells from nodal cells, was significantly lower in AVCMs compared with ACMs and VCMs (Figure 4F), in agreement with the lower mRNA expression of SCN5A.

Furthermore, voltage clamp measurements of If (Figure 4G) as well as the response to 3 μM ivabradine, which prolonged cycle length in AVCMs (Figure 4H), demonstrated that If contributes to automaticity in AVCMs.27 In addition to If, IKAch has been shown to be important for modulation of pacemaking activity.28 10 μM carbachol, which activates IKAch, prolonged the cycle length in AVCMs and ACMs, where this current is expected to be present (Figure 4I).29 In summary, molecular, and functional characterization demonstrated the translatability of WNT2- and RA-driven AVCM differentiation to an independent hiPSC line.

Cardiac assembloids simulate conduction patterns observed in vivo

Next, we aimed to assess the behavior of AVCMs in 3D tissues, particularly in a setting where we could study their role in the conduction of electrical activity from the atria to the ventricles. To this end, we prepared ACM, AVCM, and VCM spheroids from the respective subtype cultures as schematically represented in Figure 5A. We tested 100,000, 250,000, and 500,000 cells for spheroid aggregation. Spheroids made of higher cell numbers formed empty cores and did not aggregate properly. Optical mapping of individual subtype spheroids revealed slower propagation in AVCMs (1.84 ± 0.25 cm/s) compared with ACMs (5.27 ± 0.70 cm/s) and VCMs (8.30 ± 0.68 cm/s) (Figure S6A). Immunostaining further confirmed subtype-specific expression patterns in the spheroids (Figures S6B–S6D). Expression of NR2F2 was predominant in the ACM spheroid (Figure S6B). Expression of MYL2 was only observed in the VCM spheroid (Figure S6C). Importantly, MSX2 was preferentially expressed in the AVCM spheroid (Figure S6D).

To create a three-component tissue model, we implemented the assembloid approach, previously described for neuronal organoids.30,31 48 h after fabrication, individual spheroids were allowed to fuse together in microwell plates to enable the formation of so-called “cardiac assembloids” composed of ACMs, AVCMs, and VCMs in physiological order (Figure 5C). Impulse propagation in these tissue constructs was assessed 5 days later using optical mapping. When assembled in the morphological order of the vertebrate heart tube, i.e., AVCMs sandwiched between ACMs and VCMs (Figure 5C), impulse initiation was observed from the ACM end in at least 60% of the assembloids (Figure 5D). The propagation was unidirectional and followed a fast-slow-fast activation pattern (ACMs 4.78 ± 0.61 cm/s, AVCMs 1.41 ± 0.22 cm/s, and VCMs 5.42 ± 0.73 cm/s), reminiscent of the heart tube in vertebrate embryos (Figure 5D).32 To further confirm that slower conduction is an intrinsic property of AVCMs independent of their position within the assembloids, we fabricated constructs with ACMs and AVCMs toward the periphery and VCM spheroids in the center (Figure 5E). In these tissues, impulses were once again initiated primarily from the ACM end. The propagation in these assembloids followed a fast-fast-slow pattern (ACMs 3.70 ± 0.60 cm/s, VCMs 3.74 ± 0.51 cm/s, and AVCMs 1.45 ± 0.19 cm/s) (Figure 5F). To further strengthen the observations, we also generated “quad assembloids” containing all four cardiomyocyte subtypes in the physiological order, i.e., SANCMs, followed by ACMs, AVCMs, and VCMs. In 90% of the constructs, pacing began consistently at the SANCM end (Figure S6E). The propagation followed a “slow-fast-slow-fast” pattern, as expected from nodal and chamber-like tissues. Lastly, immunofluorescence analysis of subtype regions in the assembloids (Figures 5G and 5H) confirmed respective identities, demonstrating congruence with observations from single spheroids prior to fabrication. Overall, these findings demonstrate that assembloids display appropriate gene expression and conduction patterns and are, therefore, a valuable tool to study the AV conduction axis in vitro.

Following up on these findings, we performed single-cell patch-clamp as well as assays to determine [Ca2+]i homeostasis. Representative action potential traces of LMNAc.1477C>T and isogenic AVCMs are shown in Figure S7A. Analysis of action potential properties revealed prolonged cycle length and longer APD90 in LMNAc.1477C>T AVCMs compared with the isogenic control. All other parameters were comparable (Figure S7B). We next tested the susceptibility of LMNAc.1477C>T to triggered activity. We used a fast-pacing protocol followed by an 8-s pause and dynamic clamp methodology, as described recently.34 The number of delayed afterdepolarizations (DADs) was significantly higher in cardiomyocytes generated from LMNAc.1477C>T hiPSCs (Figures S7C and S7D). Noticeably, the propensity for DADs was greater in AVCMs compared with the other subtypes (Figure S7D), suggesting an [Ca2+]i overload in this cell type.

To determine whether alterations in [Ca2+]i homeostasis are indeed present in AVCMs, we performed [Ca2+]i transient measurements of LMNA and isogenic AVCMs. Figure 7B shows typical [Ca2+]i transient recordings of LMNA and isogenic AVCMs following 0.7 Hz electrical stimulation. A summary of average [Ca2+]i characteristics is presented in Figure 7C. Diastolic [Ca2+]i was increased in LMNA AVCMs as compared with the isogenic control, while there were no significant differences in systolic [Ca2+]i or amplitude of the transients. Mean rise time of calcium [Ca2+]i was not significantly different between LMNA AVCMs and isogenic controls. Notably, the decay of the [Ca2+]i transient was significantly slower in LMNA AVCMs, as indicated by a significant increase in time constant (tau) of decay (Figure 7C).

To better understand the cause of the slower decay time in LMNA AVCMs, we measured the activity of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), Ca2+ ATPase (SERCA), and Na+-Ca2+ exchanger (NCX) and the slow mechanisms (mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter and sarcolemmal Ca2+ ATPase) in ([Ca2+]i) sequestering by the application of caffeine and nickel chloride (NiCl2) pulses.35 Representative transients of isogenic and LMNA AVCMs are shown in Figure 7D. The amplitude of caffeine-evoked transients in the presence of NiCl2 was reduced in LMNA AVCMs (Figure 7E), indicating a reduction in SR calcium. Neither fractional SR Ca2+ release nor the relative contributions of SERCA, NCX, and slow mechanisms differed significantly between groups. However, the rate constant of SERCA2A (KSERCA) and the rate constant of NCX (KNCX), denoting their activity, were significantly slower in LMNA AVCMs, indicating that both mechanisms contribute to the slower calcium transient decay (Figure 7E). Key electrophysiological findings, including impulse block in assembloids as well as alterations in action potential properties and increased propensity for DADs, were also observed in AVCMs derived from an additional LMNAc.1477C>T clone (Figure S8). Taken together, LMNAc.1477C>T AVCMs exhibit impaired calcium handling, which can contribute to reduced excitability and compromise conduction relay.

Finally, we tested a pharmacological strategy to rescue propagation block in LMNAc.1477C>T AVCMs. Previous findings that reduced Casq2 is associated with increased leakiness of Ryr channels,36 as well our observations pointing to elevated diastolic calcium, evidenced by DADs and calcium transient measurements, prompted us to test S107. This compound stabilizes the binding affinity of RYR channels with Calstabin-1 (FKBP12) and has been reported to diminish its leakiness.37 Application of 10 μM S107 to LMNAc.1477C>T AVCMs resulted in a reduction in the number of DADs by more than 2-fold (Figure 7F). Moreover, in assembloids paced at 3 Hz, S107 resolved propagation block in 4 out of 5 assembloids (Figure 7G). In conclusion, these results demonstrate the power of hiPSC-derived AVCMs and assembloids for revealing molecular, cellular, and functional causes underlying complex conduction disorders.

Discussion

Despite the breadth of advances in generating human cellular and tissue models of the heart,38 constructs that recapitulate the AV conduction axis have been lacking. In this study, we report the generation of hiPSC-derived AVCMs and their integration in cardiac assembloids that display key properties in common with the AV conduction axis. Our data show that activation of WNT and RA signaling pathways at the cardiac mesoderm stage steers differentiation toward an AVCM lineage. Previous studies in both zebrafish and mouse models have shown that WNT signaling is necessary for AV canal development.16,17 In addition, loss of Wnt2, which is specifically expressed in the inflow tract of the developing heart, results in AV canal defects.39 To test the effect of WNT signaling, we used both the GSK3β inhibitor, CHIR99021, and the WNT ligand WNT2. Whereas we found that WNT2 promoted AV fate, even lower concentrations of CHIR inhibited cardiomyocyte specification (data not shown). We further identified that the effects of WNT2 were the strongest when applied together with RA. The role of RA in inflow tract development has been extensively studied and also applied for differentiation of cardiac lineages in vitro.40 Nevertheless, its role in AV canal development is poorly defined. We hypothesized the involvement of RA in AVCM differentiation, based on the observations that the expression domain of chicken GATA6 enhancer, which marks the AV canal region, is expanded upon treatment with RA.14 In addition, embryos lacking RA synthesizing enzyme Raldh2 appear to have AV canal defects.15 Molecular characterization of WNT2- and RA-treated cardiomyocytes revealed that the majority of cells from this group identified with AVCMs of the mouse heart. Smaller fractions of VCM- and ACM-like populations were also present in these cultures. Although we did not observe noticeable differences in heterogeneity of resulting cell populations between consecutive differentiations, changes in growth factor batches, passage number influencing cell state, and differences in individual cell lines may influence cell identity and must be taken into consideration when implementing directed differentiation strategies. Ion channel expression, action potential properties, and If and INa measurements of AVCMs generated from two independent wild-type lines were in line with expected behavior of AV nodal cardiomyocytes.4,41 However, as reported for other cardiomyocyte subtypes derived from hiPSCs, these cells are likely immature. The conduction velocity of AVCMs was around 1.4–1.8 cm/s, comparable with human fetal AVN, which is reported to be around 1 cm/s.42

A wide repertoire of cardiac cell types, including cardiomyocyte subtypes such as sinoatrial, atrial, and ventricular, as well as non-cardiomyocyte cell types such as epicardial cells, endocardial cells, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts, can now be generated from hiPSCs.38 The obvious next step for the field in recent years has been the creation of tissue systems that incorporate these various cell types. Approaches to achieve this have included both self-organizing systems and the defined assembly of multi-cell-type tissues.38 Replicating the complexity of the heart through self-organization is challenging, given the myriad signaling cues and tight control of concentration gradients that are required in a temporal manner to generate tissues that are molecularly and functionally representative of the human heart. An alternative approach is to generate desired individual cell types using developmentally guided differentiation processes and subsequently assemble them into physiologically relevant tissues. To develop such constructs to simulate the AV conduction axis, we were inspired by the assembloid approach reported to study neuronal interactions. Spheroids positioned in proximity, confined in either a 96-well plate or Eppendorf tube, have been shown to fuse together in a selected order to generate three-part or four-part assembloids.30,31 In this study, we show that cardiac assembloids made by the fusion of atrial, AVCM, and ventricular spheroids reproduce electrical propagation patterns seen in the heart.43 This tissue model is reminiscent of the embryonic heart, where the AV canal provides electrical continuity between a singular atrial and singular ventricular chamber.1 Albeit being a simple approach, we demonstrate that it works reliably in four different cell lines. Considerations to improve success rate include the use of custom-made plates or inserts to restrict the movement of spheroids. Alternatively, advances in 3D bioprinting, which allow control over size, shape, structure, and sequence of printing, would allow the creation of more robust and scalable assembloid systems.

Two recent studies reported the generation and characterization, including single-cell gene expression profiles, of AVCMs from hiPSCs12 or hESCs.13 Schmidt et al.12 optimized mesoderm induction, followed by exposure to BMP4, to generate AVCMs. The advantage of our approach over this method is that it is a simpler, monolayer-based protocol with fewer factors to titrate. In a similar study, Ye et al.13 differentiated AVCMs from hESCs by exposing differentiated cardiomyocytes at day 11 to WNT signaling activator CHIR99021. Activation of WNT signaling at this late stage has been shown to induce expansion of hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes by halting their progression toward maturation.44 We performed gene module scoring of AVCMs from the present study as well as the aforementioned studies using the top 50 genes from the mouse and human AV canal/node. Of all the cell types reported in this study and in Schmidt et al.,12 AVCM populations showed the most correlation with top genes expressed in the in vivo AV node. In contrast, AVCMs reported in Ye et al.13 did not show a correlation with human AV nodal gene expression; additional analyses are therefore necessary to establish the identity of cells generated in their study. In addition to molecular features, AVCMs described in this study clearly displayed slower impulse propagation compared with atrial and ventricular spheroids, accurately recapitulating patterns reported in the vertebrate heart.

To evaluate the potential of AVCMs for uncovering the basis of conduction dysfunction observed in humans, we used hiPSCs carrying a heterozygous mutation in LMNA. Our results illustrate that key aspects of patient phenotypes such as impulse block can be modeled in vitro.

Furthermore, using assembloid constructs where LMNA AVCMs were sandwiched by chamber cell types from isogenic controls, we identified that the conduction block phenotype is driven by intrinsic defects in AVCMs. RT-qPCR was performed to understand the underlying cause revealed significantly lower expression of [Ca2+]i handling genes in LMNA AVCMs. The role of spontaneous calcium releases from the SR in regulating the automaticity of human cardiac pacemaker cells has recently been demonstrated.45 Indeed, we observed that LMNA AVCMs exhibit longer cycle lengths at the cellular level. Further analysis of [Ca2+]i dynamics revealed elevated diastolic calcium as well as slowed calcium transient decay. We speculate that reduced expression of SERCA2A and NCX1, as well as reduced CASQ25, contribute to higher diastolic calcium. Prior work has shown that reduction of Casq2 is associated with increased SR reticulum calcium leak.36 Reduced re-uptake by SERCA2A and NCX1 also results in higher diastolic calcium.46 Experimental evaluations in LMNA AVCMs indeed confirmed lower SERCA2A and NCX1 activity. Finally, we tested whether an attempt to counter increased diastolic calcium would mitigate the impulse propagation issues. We used S107, which targets leakiness of RYR channels, and found that it attenuated impulse block in assembloids as well as diminished DADs.

All in all, our findings demonstrate a link between [Ca2+]i and conduction. In agreement is the observation that increased cytosolic calcium (stemming from an increase in the late sodium current) precipitates AV block in a mouse model of Scn5a1798insD/+.47 Nevertheless, AV block is a complex phenotype, likely emerging from a multitude of factors that involve a combination of cardiomyocyte and non-cardiomyocyte effects, such as cell-intrinsic electrical changes, altered gap junction coupling, and increased fibrosis. Future work, both in the context of LMNA and other conduction disorders, is expected to clarify the relationship between these factors and eventual disease phenotype. In essence, our work provides new avenues to study complex disorders of the heart in a human tissue system that incorporates multiple cardiomyocyte subtypes. As a proof of principle, we have shown the utility of this approach to uncover molecular mechanisms of LMNA-associated conduction dysfunction and even identified a plausible therapeutic strategy. This platform can be applied to study disease affecting any or all of these subtypes, including assessment of compartment-specific pharmacological and toxicological effects.

Limitations of the study

This study only focuses on the cardiomyocyte aspect of the AV conduction axis. During fetal stages, the AV canal gives rise to parts of the AV node. The AV node itself is a complex structure encompassed in extensive connective tissue. In addition, there is remarkable heterogeneity in the composition of AV nodal cardiomyocytes, which include transitional cells extending both in the direction toward the atria and toward the ventricles. These aspects are not yet represented in our system. Cardiac assembloids were made by fusion of individual cell-type-specific spheroids, which were manually aggregated by bringing them in proximity. Custom plates/inserts or 3D bioprinting could be considered for future studies to further improve success rate. Furthermore, we recognize the challenge that hiPSC-derived cardiomyocytes are inherently immature. Nevertheless, we show that aspects of human heart physiology can be recapitulated in these systems and provide opportunities for continued advancements.

Experimental model and study participant details

hiPSC lines

hiPSC lines, LUMC0099iCTRL#04 (Female) and LUMC0047iCTRL#1A (Male) used in this study were generated by the iPSC core facility of the Leiden University Medical Center.

hiPSC line from a 48-year-old female patient carrying LMNAc.1477C>T variant was generated after obtaining informed consent and approval of the medical ethics committee of the Amsterdam University Medical Center. Skin biopsy material was cultured on fibronectin (5 μg/ml; Corning, #356008) coated flask in BIO-AMF2 medium (Biological Industries, #01-194-1). Isolated primary fibroblasts were reprogrammed to hiPSCs using the CytoTune 2.0 Sendai Reprogramming Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific, #A16517) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. LMNAc.1477C>T, clone D44 (Figures 6, 7, 8, and S7) and LMNAc.1477C>T, clone A18 (Figure S8), were used in this study. A corresponding isogenic control line was generated by correcting the LMNAc.1477C>T mutation in clone D44 using the PE3 prime editing approach.48 In short, LMNAc.1477C>T hiPSCs were nucleofected with three plasmids, i.e. plasmid encoding the pCMV-PE2-P2A-GFP prime editor (Addgene, #132776),48 a plasmid encoding the pegRNA (Addgene, #132777)48 containing the template to correct the LMNAc.1477C>T variant and silence the PAM sequence, and a plasmid encoding the sgRNA (Addgene, #65777)49 for additional second nick. For nucleofection the Human Stem Cell Nucleofector Kit (Lonza, #VPH-5012) was used. Nucleofected iPSCs were plated as single cells on a 10 cm dish coated with growth factor reduced Matrigel (Corning, #356231). Colonies were manually picked for further expansion. Colonies were genotyped by Sanger Sequencing and stable karyotype confirmed by G-banding.

hiPSCs from all lines were maintained on growth factor reduced Matrigel-coated plates (Corning, #354230) in mTESR1 medium (Stem Cell Technologies, #85850) at 37°C / 5% CO2 and passaged once a week using 0.5 mM EDTA (Invitrogen, #15575-038).

Method details

Differentiation of hiPSC lines to cardiomyocytes

For cardiomyocyte differentiation, hiPSCs were dissociated using 1x TryPLE Select (ThermoFisher, #12563011) and seeded at a density of 1.8-3.2 x104cells/cm2 in mTESR1 medium (Stem Cell Technologies, #85850). Differentiation was induced when cells reached 80 – 90% confluency. Mesoderm induction medium was composed of BPEL54 supplemented with 20 ng/mL Activin-A (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-115-012), 20 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D systems, #314-BP-101/CF) and 1.5 μM CHIR99021 (Axon Medchem, #1386). After 72 hours (day 3 of differentiation), medium was replaced with BPEL containing 5 μM XAV939 (TOCRIS, #3748/10). For VCM differentiation, XAV939 was left for 96 hrs. For SANCM, ACM, and AVCM differentiation, cells were treated with XAV939 for 24 hours followed by addition of subtype-specific factors (day 4 of differentiation). For SANCM, this included BPEL medium supplemented with 5 μM XAV939, 2.5 ng/mL BMP4, 5 μM SB431542 (TOCRIS, #1614), 250 nM RA (Sigma, #R2625-50MG), 250 nM PD173074 (Selleck Chemicals, #1264) and incubated for 48 hrs. ACM cells were treated with BPEL containing 1 μM BMS753 (Tocris, #3505) and 5 μM XAV939 for 72 hrs. For W+R (AVCM condition), cells were incubated with BPEL containing 5 ng/mL WNT2 (Sigma Aldrich, #SRP6560-10UG) and 250 nM RA (Sigma, #R2625-50MG) for 72 hrs. Media containing subtype specification factors were removed on day 6 or day 7 of differentiation (See Figure 1A). Henceforth, all cultures were refreshed every 3 – 4 days with BPEL media. Cells were used for downstream experiments from day 16 onwards.

Flow Cytometry

Differentiated cardiomyocytes were dissociated using 1x TrypLE Select (ThermoFisher, #12563011). Cells were fixed using fixation buffer (Biolegend #420801) for 20 minutes at room temperature. Staining was performed with Cardiac Troponin T antibody (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-120-403, 1:50) or isotype control (Miltenyi Biotec #130-118-446, 1:50) diluted in 1x intracellular staining buffer (Biolegend #421002) and incubated on ice in the dark for 30 minutes. Acquisition was performed using FACS Symphony A1 (BD Biosciences). Data was analyzed using FLowJo v10.

RNA isolation and reverse transcription quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated using Nucleospin RNA kit (Machery Nagel, # MN740955.50). cDNA was synthesized using Superscript II (ThermoFisher Scientific, #18064071) with oligo (dT) primers (125 μmol/L, Sigma) and dNTP set (100nM, Solis Biodyne, #02-21-00100) according to manufacturer's protocol. Each qPCR reaction contained 1x LightCycler 480 SYBR Green I Master (Roche, #4887352001), 1 μM primers (Forward and Reverse) and cDNA (equivalent to 10 ng RNA). RT-qPCR Primer pairs were designed to span an exon-exon junction and/or at least one intron (Table S5). Target sequences were amplified using the LightCycler 2.0 Real-Time PCR system (Roche Life Science) with the following amplification protocol: 5 min at 95 °C followed by 45 cycles of 10 sec at 95 °C, 20 sec at 60 °C, and 20 sec at 72 °C. Data analysis was performed using LinRegPCR program.50 Relative expression values were obtained by normalizing N0 values to the Geomean of two experimentally assessed reference genes, RPLP0 and GUSB.

Cell sorting for scRNA-seq

Single cell sequencing was performed using SORT-seq approach.55 Cells from two independent differentiations were collected on day 19. Single cells were sorted into 384-well plates, each well containing an oil droplet with barcoded primers, spike-ins and dNTPs. Preparation of single-cell libraries was performed using the CEL-Seq2 protocol.55,56 Paired-end sequencing was performed on the NextSeq500 platform using 1x75 bp read length kit.

Bioinformatic Analysis

Reference genome annotation

Mapping was performed using BWA-MEM against the (human) genome assembly GRCh38 (hg38). Count matrices were generated using MapAndGo, filtering reads with a minimum quality score of 60 and no alternative hits.

scRNA-seq data preprocessing, normalization and batch-correction, clustering, differential gene expression, cell-type identification, and visualization

Data analysis was performed using the R toolkit Seurat version 4.51 Quality control and preprocessing, dimensional reduction, clustering, and differential gene expression were performed according to the standard workflow (https://satijalab.org/seurat/). Briefly, high quality single cells collected on D19 were selected according to the following parameters: gene count > 1,000 and < 8,000 and mitochondrial gene count < 50%. Normalization, scaling, and identification of variable features (nfeatures=3000) based on variance stabilizing transformation ("vst") was performed using the SCTransform command.57 Since technical plate-to-plate variations were observed, SCTransform data integration was performed by normalizing each dataset individually, identifying integration anchors within the datasets collected on the same timepoint and integrating the datasets. The same approach was used for integrating our in vitro dataset with in vivo datasets after converting human genes to mouse orthologues using the R package “babelgene”. Mouse datasets published in De Soysa et al.21 and Hill et al.22 were retrieved and cardiomyocyte clusters were extracted using TNNT2 and ACTN2 expression. Mouse cardiomyocyte populations and hiPSC-AVCM (genes converted to mouse orthologues) were merged and analyzed together. Dimensionality reduction was performed using principal component (PC) analysis and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) with the top 25 PCs (hiPSC-derived cardiomyocyte subtype datasets, Figures 1 and S3), top 20 PCs (clustering of W+R-hiPSC-CM, Figure 1 and integration of in vivo and in vitro datasets, Figures 2 and S4) and seed set to 2020. For cell clustering, a KNN (K-nearest neighbor) graph was constructed based on euclidean distance in PCA space and clusters were identified using the Louvain algorithm, as implemented in the FindNeighbors and FindClusters command. Identified clusters were then visualized in a UMAP using the DimPlot command. For differential expression testing and visualization, LogNormalization was performed according to the standard workflow on the uncorrected dataset and differential gene expression was determined using Wilcoxon rank sum test. Differentially expressed gene lists show genes, which are expressed in at least 25% in either of the two fractions of cells and limited to genes, which are differentially expressed (on average) by at least 0.25-fold (log-scale) between the two compared cell fractions. Cell type specific marker genes were used to annotate cell clusters. VlnPlot, FeaturePlot and DoHeatMap commands were used to visualize gene expression. For gene module score calculation, the Top 50 differentially expressed genes of several datasets (Schmidt et al., 2023,12, Farah et al.,23 Ye et al.,13 and Kanemaru et al., 202324) were used as input features for the AddModuleScore command. A complete list of the Top 50 genes used can be found in Table S4.

Immunocytochemistry of 2D cardiomyocyte monolayers

Differentiated cardiomyocytes were dissociated using 1x TrypLE Select (ThermoFisher Scientific #12563011) and seeded at a density of 4.0 x 105 per coverslips (12 mm, Epredia, #CB00120RA020MNZ0). Cells were fixed four days later with 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR, #28794.295). Cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton-X (Sigma, T8787). A blocking step was performed with 4% normal swine serum (Jackson Immunoresearch, #014-000-121) for one hour at room temperature. Primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in 4% normal swine serum and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (Sigma Aldrich #D9542). Image acquisition was performed using the LEICA confocal SP8-X DLS lightsheet microscope. Image visualization and processing was performed with LAS-X (Leica) software. Details of antibodies used are provided in STAR Methods.

Single Cell Patch-Clamp

Differentiated cardiomyocytes at day 16 were dissociated using 1x TryPLE Select (ThermoFisher Scientific #12563011) and seeded at a density of 7.0x103 per coverslip (Epredia, #CB00120RA020MNZ0). Single cell patch-camp was performed seven days later. Cells with a smooth surface and intact membrane, which displayed spontaneous activity were chosen for measurements. Action potentials were recorded at 37°C with the amphotericin-B-perforated patch-clamp technique using a Axopatch 200B Clamp amplifier (Molecular Devices Corporation). Measurements were carried out in Tyrode’s solution containing 140 mM NaCl, 5.4 mM KCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1.0 mM MgCl2, 5.5 mM glucose and 5.0 mM HEPES. pH was adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH. Pipettes (borosilicate glass; resistance 1.5-2.5 MΩ) were filled with a solution containing 125 mM K-gluconate, 20 mM KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 0.4 mM amphotericin-B and 10 mM HEPES, pH was adjusted to 7.2 with KOH. Signals were low-pass-filtered (cutoff frequency 10 kHz) and digitized at 40 kHz. Potentials were corrected for the estimated change in liquid junction potential.58 Data acquisition and analysis were performed using custom software, Scope4PC. DAD susceptibility was tested with procedures as described34,35 and DADs were defined as 1 mV or larger depolarization deflections.

INa was activated by 50 ms depolarizing pulses from -120 mV using modified solutions at 37°C. Extracellular solution contained 130 mM NaCl, 10 mM CsCl, 1.8 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgCl2, 11.0 mM glucose, 5.0 mM HEPES. pH was adjusted to 7.4 with CsOH. 5 μM nifedipine was added to block ICa,L. Pipettes were filed with 133 mM CsCl, 3.0 mM NaCl, 2.0 mM MgCl2, 2.0 mM Na2ATP, 2.0 mM TEACl, 10 mM EGTA, 5.0 mM HEPES. pH was adjusted to 7.2 with CsOH. If was measured as ivabradine-sensitive current (3 μM) using the same solutions as used for the action potential measurements and 2 sec hyper- and depolarizing voltage clamp steps from a holding potential of -40 mV. Currents were normalized to cell capacitance as described previously.34.

Preparation of cardiac spheroids and their fabrication into assembloids

ACM, AVCM, or VCM differentiations containing >70% cardiomyocytes on day 16 were dissociated using 1x TryPLE Select and plated at 10 x 104/well in low adherence, V-bottom 96-well plates (Griener, #651161). Cells typically compacted within 24 hours, forming contractile spheroids. Spheroids with suboptimal compaction (most often observed when differentiation efficiency of the starting cultures were <70%) were not used. After another 24 hours, individual spheroids were combined to prepare assembloids in 24-well plates containing silicon inserts (ibidi #80209) or flat bottom 96-well plates (Griener, #655185). The plate was positioned at a 45-degree angle and spheroids were serially pipetted in close proximity. They were arranged such that they were in contact with one another, using a pipette tip to guide them. The plate was cultured in the incubator for 24-hours at a 45-degree angle using a prop. Fusion of spheroids, resulting in assembloids occurred within the first 24 hours. We assessed the expected order of the spheroids using fluorescent trackers (dyes or lentiviral reporters) to track individual subtypes, which confirmed a 100% success rate in the desired order of individual spheroids. Only one assembloid was cultured per well and media changes were performed every other day. Successful assembly was evaluated 72 hours after fabrication, by bright field microscopy to observe fusion between individual spheroids and presence of coordinated contractions as well as by optical mapping to test spontaneous impulse propagation from one end to the other. Based on these criteria, success rate of assembly was 70 – 75%.

Optical Mapping

Spheroids or Assembloids, four-five days after preparation, were incubated in media containing 20 μM Di-4 ANEPPS (Anaspec Inc, #AS-84723) and 1 μM Blebbistatin (Cayman Chemical, #13013) at 37°C for 25 minutes. For mapping, spheroids/assembloids were transferred to a water bath containing modified Tyrode’s solution prepared as described above with pH maintained at 7.4 by equilibration with a mixture of 95% O2 and 5% CO2. Excitation light was provided by a 5 W power LED (filtered 510 ± 20 nm). Fluorescence (filtered>610 nm) was transmitted through a tandem lens system on CMOS sensor (100x100 elements, sampling rate 5 kHz, MICAM Ultima). Optical action potentials were visualized using Brainvision Inc software and analyzed using Rhythm and Orca softwares.52,53 Pacing of assembloids was provided by a twin tipped micro electrode (900 μA, 2 ms pulse) and custom-made OT & Sien Heart Stimulator software.

Immunocytochemistry of spheroids and assembloids

Single spheroids or assembloids were fixed seven days after fabrication with 4% paraformaldehyde (VWR, #28794.295) were imbedded in 1% agarose (Sphaero Q, #S103B) followed by paraffin imbedding. The paraffin-imbedded single spheroids or assembloids were sectioned at 5 μm. Sections were mounted onto silane-coated slides, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated in graded ethanol series and washed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed using unmasking solution (Vector Labs #H-3300–250). A blocking step was performed with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich #A7906) for one hour at room temperature. Afterwards, sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich #A7906) overnight at 4°C. After washing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), sections were incubated with fluorochrome-conjugated secondary antibodies and DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich #D9542) in the dark at room temperature for 2 hr. Image acquisition was performed using the LEICA Thunder Wide Field Fluorescence Microscope. Image visualization and processing was performed with LAS-X (Leica) software. Details of antibodies used are provided below.

[Ca2+]I Measurements

Day 16 cardiomyocytes were dissociated, and measurements performed 5 – 7 days later. Cells were loaded with 1 μM Indo-1 AM (Cayman Biotech) for 20 minutes at 37°C in Tyrode’s Solution. Cells were subsequently washed with the same Tyrode’s solution to remove excess Indo-1. Dual wavelength emission (405 nM/505 nM) of Indo-1 AM after excitation at 340 nm was recorded at 37°C in Tyrode’s solution. [Ca2+]I transients were elicited at 0.7 Hz using field stimulation. Signals were digitized at 1 kHz, filtered at 100 Hz, and corrected for background fluorescence.

[Ca2+]i and SERCA, NCX, and slow extrusion exchange measurements and calculations were performed as we described previously in detail.35 Data acquisition and analysis were accomplished as described previously.59
