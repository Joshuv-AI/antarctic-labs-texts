Amazon deforestation weakens Atlantic Niño variability
Authors: Shengbiao Wei, Chunzai Wang, Wenju Cai, Xin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Amazon Basin has undergone rapid deforestation since the 1970s, causing biodiversity loss, regional climate shifts, and increased greenhouse gas emissions. Yet its impact on tropical ocean–atmosphere coupled variability remains unknown. Using observational datasets and targeted coupled general circulation model experiments, here we find that Amazon deforestation has contributed substantially (~23%) to the observed weakening of Atlantic Niño variability since 1970. Observational analyses and model experiments reveal that local warming and drying associated with deforestation enhance the interhemispheric thermal contrast over the tropical Atlantic. The enhanced thermal contrast strengthens surface cross-equatorial southerly winds, which in turn reduce the sensitivity of zonal wind stress to the zonal sea surface temperature gradient, weakening the Bjerknes feedback and Atlantic Niño variability. The combined observational and modeling results highlight a critical influence of land surface changes on the dynamics of the tropical coupled ocean–atmosphere system.

Introduction

Deforestation is one of the human activities with profound impacts on biodiversity and climate. In the tropics, the most extensive deforestation occurs in the Amazon Basin. Brazil’s Amazon forest has undergone continuous decline since the 1970s due to persistent deforestation, with deforestation rates trending upward after 19911,2. According to the United Nations Food and Agricultural Organization’s (UNFAO) Forest Resource Assessments (UNFAO-FRA)3,4, the loss of forest area in Brazil’s Amazon totaled about 126.1964 Million hectares (Mha) during 1980–2020, corresponding to a reduction of about 20% relative to 1980 (Fig. 1a). Tropical deforestation results in substantial changes to land surface properties, including increased surface albedo, reduced evapotranspiration, and decreased surface roughness height5,6. These changes in land surface properties alter the local energy balance and atmospheric circulations, leading to an increase in surface temperature7,8 and a reduction in precipitation9–13. Local climate changes can further induce large-scale variations in atmospheric and oceanic circulations14, triggering climatic teleconnections to remote regions15,16. Several studies suggested that tropical deforestation in the Maritime Continent can even alter the properties of interannual variability in the tropical Pacific Ocean17,18. However, the influence of deforestation on the tropical Atlantic Ocean remains unknown. Over the equatorial Atlantic Ocean, the dominant mode of interannual climate variability is the Atlantic Niño, which features sea surface temperature (SST) warming in the central-eastern equatorial Atlantic and usually peaks in boreal summer19–21. The development of Atlantic Niño is facilitated by the Bjerknes feedback and can be triggered by variability within the Atlantic Ocean22,23 or from the Indian and Pacific Oceans24–26. The occurrence of Atlantic Niño results in anomalous climate phenomena in peripheral region, causing droughts in the Sahel and increased precipitation in northeast South America27–29, and influencing the occurrence of tropical cyclones over tropical north Atlantic Ocean30,31. It also has teleconnections to remote regions, leading to changes in temperature and precipitation in Europe and India32–34, and sea ice variations in west Antarctic35. Furthermore, Atlantic Niño is a key component in the interactions between tropical oceans36,37, with effects on development of El Niño-Southern Oscillation38–42. Therefore, understanding how it changes in response to anthropological climate change is of great importance to society and remains an ongoing interest for the research community.

Over the past decades, variability of Atlantic Niño has been observed to have weakened43–45. Observations show that the variability of the equatorial zonal SST gradient experienced a decline since 195044. In response to future greenhouse warming, Atlantic Niño variability is also projected to weaken46,47. Internal climate variability, such as a phase reversal of Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation48, and anthropogenic forcing, such as inter-hemispheric differences in aerosol emissions44, have been proposed to explain the weakening of Atlantic Niño variability. However, other important factors may still be at play. In particular, the role of Amazon deforestation remains unknown. Given the extensive deforestation in the Amazon Basin, its likely persistence, and its substantial climatic impacts, it is crucial to investigate the potential impact. Below, we find that Amazon deforestation likely contributes substantially to the observed weakening of Atlantic Niño variability.

Results

Changes in Amazon deforestation and Atlantic Niño variability

Deforestation in the Amazon Basin can at least be traced back to the early 1970s1,2. According to the forest data from UNFAO-FRA (see “Data” section in Methods), the loss in Brazil’s Amazon totaled about 126.1964 Mha during 1980–2020, reaching ~20% of the original forest area in 1980 (Fig. 1a). Studies have documented that large-scale deforestation in the Amazon Basin leads to increased surface temperatures and reduced precipitation11. Under the deforestation since the 1970s, Amazon (15°S–0°, 40°W–80°W) surface air temperature has exhibited an increasing trend (0.26 °C per decade, p < 0.01), while precipitation has shown a decreasing trend (−0.15 mm day−1 per decade, p < 0.01, Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 1). The time series of air temperature and precipitation are significantly correlated with each other (r = −0.72 with p < 0.01, see “Statistical tests” section in Methods). Even with trends removed, the correlation is statistically significant (r = −0.52 with p < 0.01). These changes align with the conventional understandings that large-scale Amazon deforestation results in warmer and drier conditions5–10. Reduced precipitation is usually associated with long-term, large-scale deforestation, which is the focus of this study. Small-scale deforestation, such as patchy and fishbone patterns, may produce disproportionate or locally distinct impacts (see “A note on the impacts of different scales of Amazon deforestation” in Supplementary notes).

In the meantime, Atlantic Niño variability has weakened, as indicated by a reduction in the variances of Atlantic Niño SST and zonal wind stress. Specifically, the variability of SST in the Atlantic 3 (Atl3) region (3°S–3°N, 0°–20°W, see “Region and index definitions” section in Methods) has declined markedly since 1990 (Fig. 1c, d). The variability of zonal wind stress in the western equatorial Atlantic (3°S–3°N, 20°W–40°W) has trended downward since the 1970s (Fig. 1c, d). By dividing the analyzed period into a high variability period (1970–1997) and a low variability period (1998–2024), we find that the JJA-averaged Atl3 SST variability has declined 45.1% in the low variability period compared to the high variability period (Fig. 1d). The change in variability of JJA-averaged western equatorial Atlantic zonal wind stress is more pronounced, with a decline reaching 57.6% (Fig. 1d). Decreases are also observed in the variability of the equatorial zonal SST gradient and the depth of the Atl3 20 °C isotherm (D20, Supplementary Fig. 3). According to the Bjerknes feedback, these changes reinforce one another, leading to the pronounced weakening in Atlantic Niño variability45–47. To examine connections between Amazon deforestation and the weakening of Atlantic Niño variability, below we examine the causes of the latter.

Weakened Atlantic Niño variability linked to strengthened mean southerly winds

Because the Atlantic Niño originates from the coupling between the tropical ocean and atmosphere, inevitably, the weakening in its variability results from a less effective Bjerknes feedback22,45–47,49. Through diagnosing the anomalous atmospheric zonal momentum budgets, we find that the response of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient is linked to the climatological surface meridional winds over the western equatorial Atlantic (see “Weakened response of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient linked to strengthened western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds” section in Supplementary notes). During boreal summer, the intertropical convergence zone (ITCZ) shifts northward, and mean southerly winds blow over the western equatorial Atlantic. By inducing northward momentum advection, these southerly winds convey the zonal wind stress initially generated by the zonal pressure gradient associated with the zonal SST gradient to the north, thereby exerting a negative contribution to zonal wind stress formation (Supplementary Fig. 4). As a result, the southerly winds in the western equatorial Atlantic act to dampen the response of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient50. As the southerly winds in the western equatorial Atlantic strengthen (Fig. 2b), the damping effect of the momentum advection has also strengthened (Supplementary Fig. 5), which reduces the sensitivity of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient and results in a less effective Bjerknes feedback.

To further demonstrate the influence of changes in western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds on Atlantic Niño variability, we examine their relationships in simulations from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6, see “CMIP6 data” section in Methods). We first evaluate the performance of a total of 34 CMIP6 models (Supplementary Table 1) in simulating the Atlantic Niño and then examine intermodel relationships among the models with good performance. Here, a model’s ability to simulate the Atlantic Niño is assessed based on the spatial pattern of SST anomalies associated with the phenomenon. We regress JJA SSTs onto the Atl3 index in each model and compare the resulting spatial patterns with that in reanalysis data (Supplementary Fig. 6). Models with a spatial correlation above 0.7 over the tropical Atlantic region (15°S–15°N, 50°W–20°E) are classified as good-performing. The good-performing models are able to simulate SST warming confined to a narrow band of the central-eastern equatorial Atlantic, similar to observations. Whereas the poor-performing models simulate SST warming spanning a broader latitudinal extent, with a peak locating in the central equatorial Atlantic, which resembles Central Atlantic Niño45. This process results in a selection of 30 good-performing models (Supplementary Fig. 6). These good-performing models simulate an Atlantic Niño with a reasonable magnitude compared to observations. The simulated Atl3 SST standard deviation ranges from 0.23 °C to 0.66 °C (53% to 151% of the observed amplitude), with an ensemble mean value of 0.46 °C (103.9% of the observed amplitude) (Supplementary Fig. 6).

The intermodel relationship between JJA climatological western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds at 1000 hPa and Atl3 SST variance among the selected models, along with the reanalysis, shows a statistically significant negative correlation (r = −0.41 with p < 0.03, Fig. 2c). Thus, models simulating stronger southerly winds tend to produce weaker Atlantic Niño variability. Moreover, the sensitivity of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient is also negatively correlated with western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds (r = −0.41 with p < 0.03, Fig. 2d), suggesting that weaker Atlantic Niño variability is linked to a reduced sensitivity of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient. The correlations are only moderately above the confidence level, suggesting the explained variance is not very large. The low correlation implies that other factors, such as the depth and east-west tilt of mean equatorial thermocline44,46,47, the mean zonal current49 and the atmospheric stability46 may also operate in CMIP6 models. Nevertheless, the statistically significant correlation results support our findings from observations that an increase in southerly winds results in a decline in the sensitivity of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient, thereby a weakening in Atlantic Niño variability. Next, we examine the connection with Amazon mean state changes.

Reduced Amazon precipitation strengthens cross-equatorial southerly winds

As shown earlier, surface temperature is highly correlated with precipitation in the Amazon, we use precipitation variation as a representative of deforestation-induced local mean state changes in the followings. Results are similar if surface temperature is used. A strengthening of southerly winds is linked to changes in the mean state of the Amazon Basin. Examination of the time series indicates that western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds in JJA are highly correlated with reduced Amazon precipitation (r = 0.73 with p < 0.01, Fig. 3a). Even after removing linear trends, the correlation coefficients remain statistically significant above the 99% confidence level (r = 0.62 with p < 0.01). To further elucidate the underlying dynamics, grid-point anomalies of SST, surface winds, and precipitation are regressed onto negative Amazon precipitation anomalies (precipitation time series are multiplied by −1 so the results correspond to reduced Amazon precipitation, Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 7). Associated with reduced Amazon precipitation, the trade winds strengthen over the southern Atlantic and weaken over the northern Atlantic, leading to more oceanic latent heat release in the south but less release in the north (Supplementary Fig. 8). As a result, SSTs are warmer in the northern Atlantic than in the southern Atlantic, enhancing the inter-hemispheric thermal contrast and strengthening western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds. These changes may be further amplified through local wind-evaporation-SST feedback51. As Amazon deforestation reduces local precipitation and increases surface temperatures5–10, it contributes to the strengthening of western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds.

Land surface warming induced by Amazon deforestation enhances the land-sea thermal contrast between South America and the tropical southern Atlantic Ocean, as indicated by the regression of 2 m air temperature averaged over 15°S–5°S onto negative JJA Amazon precipitation (Fig. 3b), leading to stronger trade winds over the tropical southern Atlantic. Meanwhile, reduced precipitation due to Amazon deforestation intensifies local atmospheric subsidence, strengthening the northeastward atmospheric circulation between South America and the tropical northern Atlantic (northeastward circulation with atmospheric subsidence in South America and ascending motion in the tropical northern Atlantic Ocean, see definition in the “Region and index definitions” section and Supplementary Fig. 1) (Fig. 3d). The anomalies result in a northward shift of the ITCZ, enhanced precipitation and weaker trade winds in the tropical northern Atlantic (Fig. 3b).

Further examination of the long-term trends shows that their spatial patterns closely resemble those of the regressions (Fig. 3b–e, Supplementary Fig. 7), with an enhanced land-sea thermal contrast between South America and the tropical southern Atlantic Ocean (Fig. 3c), warmer SST in the northern Atlantic than in the southern Atlantic (Fig. 3c), strengthened northeastward atmospheric circulations between South America and the tropical northern Atlantic (Fig. 3e), enhanced precipitation in the tropical northern Atlantic and cross-equatorial southerly wind anomalies in the western equatorial Atlantic (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Amazon deforestation-induced weakening in Atlantic Niño variability

The above results qualitatively show that the weakening of Atlantic Niño variability is linked to the Amazon mean state changes associated with deforestation. However, the quantitative contribution of Amazon deforestation remains unclear. To this end, we conduct idealized numerical experiments using a coupled general circulation model (CGCM, see “The CGCM” section in Methods) to gauge the influence of Amazon deforestation on Atlantic Niño variability. Three experiments with different Amazon forest cover configurations are performed: Control, Deforest-Partial, and Deforest-Total experiments (Supplementary Fig. 9). In the Control experiment, the forest cover in the tropical South America (20°S–5°N, 80°W–35°W, a region slightly broader than that used to calculate Amazon precipitation and surface temperature), is maintained at a high level, which is the default setting. The Deforest-Partial experiment simulates a moderate deforestation scenario, resembling realistic conditions with the loss of forest occurring mainly in the outer ring of the Amazon Basin. Since the exact forest area loss during 1970–1980 is unclear, as an estimate of the observed forest area loss during 1970–2024, we reduce the mean Amazon forest cover by 30% in the Deforest-Partial. The Deforest-Total experiment represents an extreme scenario with complete deforestation of the Amazon Basin. Numerical results are calculated from simulations of 30 years in each experiment. To increase the reliability of the results, both the Deforest-Partial and Deforest-Total experiments consist of 10 ensemble members. The CGCM experiment reasonably simulates both the Atlantic climatological mean state in boreal summer and the Atlantic Niño (see “Performance of CGCM in simulating Atlantic Niño” section in Supplementary notes).

Compared to the Control experiment, Atlantic Niño variability weakens in both the Deforest-Partial and Deforest-Total experiments, with the weakening being more pronounced in the Deforest-Total experiment (Fig. 4a). The ensemble mean variance of Atl3 SST decreases by 10.5% (±3.6%) in the Deforest-Partial experiment (parentheses indicate ±1 standard error). This decrease accounts for 23.2% (±7.9%) of the observed decrease in Atl3 SST variance between the high variability period (1970–1997) and the low variability period (1998–2024). In the extreme scenario with complete deforestation, the decrease in Atl3 SST variance reaches 16.6% (±4.3%). The reduction in Atlantic Niño variability is associated with strengthened surface southerly winds in the western equatorial Atlantic and reduced zonal wind stress response to the zonal SST gradient (Fig. 4b, c, Supplementary Fig. 11). Both the Deforest-Partial and Deforest-Total experiments feature strengthened western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds, implying that the driving mechanisms in the numerical experiments are consistent with those identified earlier. The local warming (Supplementary Fig. 12) and drying (Supplementary Fig. 11) effects of Amazon deforestation are reproduced in the numerical experiments. Accompanying these changes, trade winds are weakened and precipitation is enhanced in the tropical northern Atlantic (Fig. 4b, c). These wind changes lead to an enhanced inter-hemispheric thermal contrast through modulating oceanic latent heat release (Supplementary Fig. 12). Because the strengthening of trade winds in the tropical southern Atlantic is less obvious than in observations (Fig. 4b, Fig. 3c), the change in inter-hemispheric thermal contrast is smaller in CGCM experiments than in observations (Supplementary Fig. 12). In spite of the differences in magnitude, the influencing mechanism responsible for strengthened western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds is consistent with that identified from observations. It is also noted that all these changes are more pronounced in the Deforest-Total experiment than in the Deforest-Partial experiment, suggesting that future intensified deforestation would cause more pronounced Atlantic Niño variability change. Therefore, the CGCM numerical experiments reaffirm that, by strengthening western equatorial Atlantic southerly winds and weakening the effectiveness of the Bjerknes feedback, Amazon deforestation could result in a weakening of Atlantic Niño variability.

Summary

We find that Amazon deforestation could contribute substantially to weakening of Atlantic Niño variability. Local warming and reduced precipitation induced by Amazon deforestation lead to strengthened trade winds in the southern Atlantic and weakened trade winds in the northern Atlantic. These changes enhance the inter-hemispheric oceanic thermal contrast and strengthen the surface southerly winds in the western equatorial Atlantic. The strengthened surface southerly winds in turn reduce the sensitivity of zonal wind stress to the zonal SST gradient by enhancing northward momentum advection. The interhemispheric oceanic thermal contrast due to trade wind changes strengthens the surface southerly winds (black arrows) in the western equatorial Atlantic (⑤), weakening the response of zonal wind stress (wide yellow arrow) to SST gradient (⑥) and the effectiveness of the Bjerknes feedback, and resulting in a weakening of Atlantic Niño variability.

Different from previous studies that addressed the influence of internal climate variability48, or anthropogenic greenhouse gases and aerosol emissions44,46,47, our study underscores the impacts of tropical land surface changes resulting from human activity. A comparison of Amazon deforestation with other influencing factors shows that its impacts could be comparable to those of other natural and anthropogenic forcing (see “Comparison of the impacts of Amazon deforestation and those of other influencing factors” section in Supplementary notes), showing that tropical surface land changes could play a role as important as other previously identified factors. The findings of our study highlights that Amazon deforestation has a far greater impact that transcends the tropical lands, as it affects tropical variability whereby influencing droughts and floods in the Sahel and northeast South America27–29, tropical cyclones in tropical north Atlantic Ocean30,31, climate in Europe and India32–34, and sea ice in west Antarctic35.

Methods

Data

The monthly mean SST data are from the Hadley Centre Sea Ice and Sea Surface Temperature (HadISST) dataset52. The atmospheric reanalysis data, including mean sea level pressure (SLP), precipitation, surface temperature, surface winds, and winds on pressure level, are from the fifth generation European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) reanalysis for the global climate and weather (ERA-5)53. The ERA-5 precipitation and surface temperature are validated against observational datasets (see section “Comparisons of Amazon precipitation and surface temperature obtained from ERA-5 with observational datasets” in the Supplementary notes). The oceanic latent heat flux data are from the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institute’s (WHOI) Objectively Analyzed air-sea Fluxes (OAFlux) for the Global Oceans54. As nonnegligible uncertainties exist in the current latent heat flux reanalysis products55,56, the mean of ERA-5 and OAFlux is used to represent observational latent heat flux when comparing model simulated results with observations. The depth of oceanic 20oC isotherm data is from the ECMWF Ocean Reanalysis Systems 557. Country-based forest extent assessment data are from the United Nations Food and Agricultural Organization’s Forest Resource Assessment (UNFAO-FRA) in 1990 and 20203,4. The Brazil forest extent in 1980 is inferred from the annual deforestation and plantation rates during 1981–1990 from UNFAO-FRA 1990, and those in 1990, 2000, 2010 and 2015 are from UNFAO-FRA 2020. The period 1970–2024 is analyzed in this study, partly because the Amazon deforestation was detected since the 1970s, and partly because the observations are sparse in earlier period, which may lead to large uncertainties. All anomalies are calculated relative to the 1991–2020 climatology. Using other climatological baselines gives similar results.

Statistical tests

The statistical significance of linear trends is determined based on the Mann–Kendall test. Other statistical tests in reanalysis are determined based on the two-tailed Student’s t-test. Bootstrap tests are used to examine the significance of an ensemble mean change in CGCM simulations. We resample 10 time from the ensemble members with replacement, obtaining a new sample, and the mean of this new sample is calculated. This process is repeated 10,000 times to calculate the standard deviation and the 2.5% lower and 97.5% higher confidence bounds. If the lower and higher confidence bounds are of the same sign, then the change is statistically significant.

Region and index definitions

Atlantic 3 (Atl3) region refers to the eastern equatorial Atlantic region (3°S–3°N, 0°–20°W). Western equatorial Atlantic refers to the region (3°S–3°N, 20°W–40°W). Atl3 index is defined as the anomalous SST averaged in the Atl3 region. Equatorial Atlantic east-minus-west SST gradient is defined as the difference of SST between the east (3°S–3°N, 10°W–10°E) and the west (3°S–3°N, 25°–45°W) equatorial Atlantic. The northeastward atmospheric circulation between South America and the tropical northern Atlantic Ocean is characterized by atmospheric subsidence in South America and ascending motion in the tropical northern Atlantic Ocean. The lower-level horizontal wind of this circulation is pointing northeastward, which is calculated by rotating u, v winds counterclockwise by 45° (the northeastward direction and the longitude limits are indicated by the green diamond in Supplementary Fig. 1a). Anomalous precipitation and 2 m air temperature in Amazon basin are averaged over the region (15°S–0°, 40°W–80°W). These regions are schematically shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a.

CMIP6 data

Monthly outputs from 34 Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) models (Supplementary Table 1) are examined in this study, from which we only use SST, surface stress and meridional winds at 1000 hPa. The analyzed period is 1950–2014 from the historical simulations with linear trends removed.

The CGCM

The coupled general circulation model (CGCM) used in this study is the Community Earth System Model 1.2.2 (CESM1.2.2). The fully coupled version of the model is used, in which the Community Atmosphere Model version 5, the Community Land Model version 4.0 with satellite phenology, the Parallel Ocean Program version 2.1, the Community Ice CodE, and the River Transport Model are coupled through the CESM coupler version 7. Deforestation in the model is implemented by replacing broadleaf evergreen trees and broadleaf deciduous trees with C4 grasses. The CGCM experiments are run with the B_2000_CAM5 component set, which features the present-day greenhouse gases emission and represents the present-day climate state. Experiments are initialized in the startup way and integrated for 45 model years. The first 15 years are used as spin-up, and the outputs from the last 30 years are used for analyses. Ensemble members are realized by perturbing the initial air temperature by an order of machine round-off errors, which is a standard method in CAM5.
