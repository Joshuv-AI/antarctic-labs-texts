Mitochondrial DNA copy number and risk of papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Materah Salem Alwehaidah, Rana Al-Awadhi, Moody Al Roomy, Tahani Al Baqer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number is associated with tumor activity and carcinogenesis. This study was undertaken to investigate mtDNA copy number in papillary thyroid cancer (PTC) tissues and to evaluate the risk of PTC development. The clinicopathological features of patients and mtDNA copy number were correlated. The value of mtDNA copy number was evaluated as a biomarker for PTC.MethodDNA was extracted from 105 PTC tissues and 67 control thyroid tissues, and mtDNA copy number mtDNA oxidative damage were determined using qPCR techniques.ResultsOverall, the relative mtDNA copy number was significantly higher in PTC patients (p < 0.001). The risk of developing PTC increased significantly across the tertiles of mtDNA copy number (p trend < 0.001). The higher the mtDNA copy number tertile, the greater the risk of developing PTC. Patients with follicular variants had an odds ratio of 2.09 (95% CI: 1.78–2.44) compared to those with classical variants (p < 0.001). The level of mtDNA oxidative damage in PTC was significantly elevated compared to controls (p < 0.001). The ROC analysis of mtDNA copy number indicated an area under the curve (AUC) of 77.7% (95% CI: 0.71 to 0.85, p < 0.001) for the ability of mtDNA copy number z-scores in differentiate between PTC and controls.ConclusionOur results indicated that the augmentation of mtDNA content plays a significant role during the initiation of thyroid cancer, and it might represent a potential biomarker for predicting the risk of PTC.Keywords: Papillary thyroid carcinoma, mtDNA copy number, Oxidative damage, Formalin fixed paraffin embedded, Thyroid tissue

Background

Thyroid cancer (TC) is one of the most common endocrine tumors, with a noticeable increase in incidence in the past few decades worldwide [1]. In Kuwait, thyroid cancer is the third most common type of cancer and the second most common among females (11.2/100,000) after breast cancer [2]. According to the Kuwait cancer registry, approximately 6.9% of cancer cases diagnosed in 2020 were thyroid cancer cases and 44% of cases being papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) predominantly in the age group of 40–49 years old [2]. Radiation exposure, family history of thyroid cancer, and nodular thyroid disease are well-known risk factors for PTC [3].

Accumulated evidence has demonstrated that chromosomal and genetic alterations play a crucial role in the tumorigenesis of PTC [4, 5]. Moreover, alteration in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) have been identified in various human cancers, including PTC [6, 7]. However, the relationship between mtDNA and thyroid cancer remains ambiguous.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is a 16.5 Kb double-stranded circular molecule containing 37 genes encoding 22 transfer RNAs (tRNA), two ribosomal RNAs (rRNA), and 13 polypeptides for the electron transport chain. The mtDNA copy number varies extensively among cell types, ranging from 100 to 10,000 copies per cell, and is strictly regulated by tissue type. However, the mechanism of mtDNA copy number regulation is still unknown, but it is correlated with mitochondrial function and energy requirements.

Mitochondria are the main source of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and are a major site of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation. Imbalance in the oxidant–antioxidant mitochondrial system causes oxidative stress, leading to overproduction of endogenous ROS, contributing to anti-apoptosis, proliferation, and initiation of tumorigenesis [6, 8].

Thyroid hormone synthesis takes place in follicular cells via a complex mechanism involving the H2O2 enzyme. Early detection of this catalytic enzyme in transformed thyroid cells signifies the presence of oxidative stress and DNA damage, which are the key triggers of neoplasia [9]. Moreover, oxidative DNA damage has been detected in advanced stages of thyroid cancer, contributing to continuous genomic instability during tumorigenesis phases [9]. Alteration in mitochondrial copy number, a common biomarker for aberrant mitochondrial function, is prevalent in human cancers, including TC [10]. To date, few studies have reported mtDNA copy number in thyroid cancer. Jian et al. [10] reported high mtDNA- copy number in peripheral blood leukocytes in TC, positively correlated with oxidative damage. Additionally, the average mtDNA copy number in PTC tumors was four times higher than in adjacent normal tissues [7, 11].

Several epidemiologic studies have investigated mtDNA copy numbers in leukocytes in breast cancer, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and thyroid cancer, showing associations with increased disease risk and worse prognosis [10, 12–15]. However, few studies have assessed tissue- specific associations between mtDNA copy number and cancer risk. Here, we determined mtDNA copy numbers in papillary thyroid cancer tissues and evaluated the risk of PTC development. Additionally, we correlated clinicopathological features of patients with mtDNA copy numbers and assessed mtDNA oxidative damage in thyroid tissue. Moreover, we evaluated mtDNA copy number as a biomarker for thyroid cancer.

Method

Study subject

Archived Formalin Fixed Paraffin Embedded (FFPE) thyroid tissue samples were obtained from 2019 to 2021 from the histopathology laboratory archives. Samples were classified according to the fourth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumors of Endocrine Organs, 2017 classification [16]. These 105 patients were diagnosed with PTC based on cytological or histological findings. Histological classification of papillary carcinoma cases was subdivided as follows: 75 cases of the classic variant and 30 cases of the follicular variant. All selected cases were in stage I; tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging was performed according to the AJCC/TNM eighth edition staging system [17]. Additionally, a total of 67 age-matched control samples were included, with histological reports confirming the absence of cancer. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Ministry of Health and the Health Science Center (HSC) Ethics Committees (No: VDR/EC/3730).

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was isolated from FFPE samples using the MagNA Pure LC DNA Isolation Kit II and MagNA Pure LC 2.0 instrument (Roche Molecular Systems), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Prior to DNA extraction, the deparaffinization step was performed as follows: 800 µl of xylol was added to the tissue section and inverted several times; followed by adding 400 µl absolute ethanol and inverting several times. The mixture was then centrifuged for 2 min at 14,000 rpm, and the supernatant was discarded. The tissue pellet was dried for 10 min at 55 ºC. Subsequently, 80 µl of tissue lysis buffer and 20 µl of proteinase K solution were added, vortexed several times and left overnight at 55ºC. The mixture was then centrifuged for 1 min at 8,000 rpm. Once tissue hemolysis was complete, the tissue lysate was transferred into a sample cartridge and placed on the reagent/sample stage. The DNAII Tissue External proteinase K protocol of the MagNA Pure LC 2.0 instrument was employed as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Roche Molecular Systems). DNA samples were quantified and qualified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE).

Determination of mitochondrial DNA copy number

The relative mtDNA copy number was measured by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) as previously described [18]. Briefly, the NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 gene (mt-ND2) was used as the mitochondrial target gene, and beta-2-macroglobulin (ß2 M) was used as an internal nuclear reference gene. The relative mtDNA copy number was determined as the ratio of mtDNA gene (mt-ND2) to nuclear gene (ß2 M) gene expression. The primer sequences for mt-ND2 amplification were as follows: forward primer (mt-ND2-F), 5’-CAC AGA AGC TGC CAT CAA GTA-3’ reverse primer; (mt-ND2-R), 5’ CCG GAG AGT ATA TTG TTG AAG AG- 3’. The second primer sequences for ß2 M amplification were as follows: 5’- CCA GCA AGA AAT GGA AAG TCA A -3’ and reverse primer 5’- TCT CTC TCC ATT CTT CAG TAA GTC AAC T-3’. The qPCR reaction mixture consisted of 1X SYBR Green Master mix, 50 nmol/l mt-ND2-F (or ß2 M-F) primer; 50 nmol/l mt-ND1-R (or ß2 M-R) primer, 10 ng of genomic DNA, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 10 µl. The qPCR for mt-ND2 and ß2 M copy number detection was performed in triplicate in the same run using a 7900HT real-time PCR system (Applied Biosystems; Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Thermal cycling conditions included denaturation at 50 °C and 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. The relative mtDNA copy number was quantified using the Eq. 2− ∆∆Ct (∆Ct = Ct mt−ND1 -Ct ß2M). The intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation for Ct values of mt-ND2 gene were less than 3%. A negative control without DNA was included in each run.

Determination of mtDNA oxidative damage

mtDNA oxidative damage was detected with 8-hydroxyl 2’- deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), the most common marker of oxidative DNA lesions. 8-OHdG can be digested by formamidopyrimidine [fapy]-DNA glycosy-lase (Fpg) which breaks DNA fragments at the lesion site. One hundred nanograms of DNA was digested for one hour at 37ºC in a total volume of reaction mixture containing 1 unit of FPG enzyme (New England Biolabs, UK), 1xNEB buffer and 0.1 mg/mL bovine serum albumin. After that, the digested DNA was amplified by qPCR under the same cycling protocol and conditions as mentioned above. The value of oxidative damage was determined as ΔCt (ΔCT = CT treated - CT untreated).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics in the study are reported as the mean ± standard deviation. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables. We employed the z-transformation technique to standardize the distribution of the data in two groups, ensuring comparability and removing any potential bias stemming from differences in their original scales. The associations between the z-scores of mtDNA copy number data and the risk of PTC were evaluated using logistic regression models. Two models were employed: one without adjustment for age and gender, and another with adjustment for age and gender to account for their potential confounding effects. In stratified analyses, the chi-square-based Q test was used to evaluate the effect of modification by confounding factors of TC. SPSS software (v25.0; SPSS Inc., IL, USA) was used to perform the statistical analyses.

Results

In this study, clinical and histopathological data from 105 cases of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) were analyzed and compared with 67 cases of total thyroidectomy with benign histology reports. The age and gender were matched between both groups (p = 0.2 and p = 0.19, respectively). Among the PTC cohort, 42% had the classical variant while 31% had the follicular variant. Additionally, 37.5% of PTC patients had multifocal tumors, and 33.3% had bilateral tumors. Approximately 46% of PTC patients had metastasis to adjacent tissues (Table 1).

Using quantitative PCR techniques, we measured the relative mtDNA copy number with respect to the ß2 M gene in 105 PTC patients and 67 controls free of thyroid cancer. Overall, the relative mtDNA copy number was significantly higher in PTC patients (2.1 ± 0.8) than in controls (0.9 ± 0.4) (p < 0.001; Table 1; Fig. 1).

Next, participants were categorized into three groups based on the tertile distribution of mtDNA copy number z-scores in controls, and the relationship between mtDNA copy number z-scores and thyroid cancer risk was evaluated (Table 2). After adjusting for covariates, the odds ratio for the association of continuous mtDNA copy number z-scores with PTC risk was 2.11 (95% CI, 1.73–2.68; P < 0.001). Furthermore, compared with the subjects in the first tertile group, the risk of developing PTC increased significantly across the tertiles of mtDNA copy number (P trend < 0.001; Table 2). The higher the mtDNA copy number tertile, the greater the risk of developing PTC. Specifically, the risk estimates for T3 were twofold greater than those for T1. Adjusted for age and sex, the odds ratio was 2.40 (95% CI, 1.48–3.25), which was statistically significant at p < 0.001 (Table 2).

The association between mtDNA copy number z-scores and the clinicopathological characteristics of the PTC group is depicted in Fig. 2. Using logistic regression, we evaluated the associations between mtDNA copy number z-scores and clinicopathological parameters of PTC. Patients with a follicular variant had an odds ratio of 2.09 (95% CI: 1.78–2.44) compared to those with a classical variant (p < 0.001). Other clinicopathological parameters such as multifocality, invasion, location, and tumor size did not show significant associations with mtDNA copy number z-scores (Fig. 2).

Figure 3 presents the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve evaluating the ability of mtDNA copy number z-scores to distinguish between PTC and control groups. The area under curve (AUC) was 77.7% with a 95% CI: 0.71 to 0.85 (Fig. 3). The sensitivity was 97.8% and the specificity was 70.8% (p < 0.001). The AUC value falling within the range of 0.70 to 0.90, indicates moderate accuracy in distinguishing between the two groups.

Discussion

mtDNA copy number alterations contribute to the development of different types of carcinomas, such as lung cancer [19], breast cancer [20], hepatic cell carcinomas [21], and gastric cancers [22]. However, elevated mitochondrial DNA copy number has been demonstrated as a marker for oxidative stress and impaired aerobic pathways involved in the molecular mechanisms of carcinogenesis [23, 24]. An increased mtDNA copy number may enhance cancer progression by increasing mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), as has been detected in colorectal cancer [25]. Thyroid tumor cells also present an increase in the mtDNA copy number [11]. However, the relationship of mtDNA copy number with the risk of cancer is still inconclusive.

In previous studies, increased mtDNA copy number was associated with the risk of thyroid cancer development [7]. This study confirms what was previously published [7, 26] and adds by showing the risk of developing papillary thyroid carcinoma could increase by twofold as the mtDNA copy number increases. This finding agrees with that of Su et al. [7], who showed that the average mtDNA content in tumors was nearly four times higher than that in adjacent normal tissues. Several studies have investigated the correlation between the clinical and pathological characteristics of tumors and the mtDNA copy number [7, 10, 26]. In this study, we found that the mtDNA copy number of follicular variants of papillary carcinoma was significantly elevated compared to the classical variant. To our knowledge, this study is the first to find such a correlation. Zheng et al. [10] found a significant difference between papillary thyroid carcinoma and follicular thyroid carcinoma. In our series, we analyzed papillary thyroid carcinoma as the only type and compared its histological variations.

The level of mtDNA oxidative damage in the PTC was significantly higher than that in the enzyme-treated mtDNA of the control. We could not find any correlation between mtDNA copy number and mtDNA oxidative damage in the diseased and control groups. This implies that changes in mtDNA copy number were not strongly associated with oxidative markers in the context of thyroid cancer and healthy individuals. Zheng et al. [10] evaluated DNA oxidative damage biomarkers in patients with thyroid cancer and found a positive correlation between mtDNA content and DNA oxidative damage biomarkers. This difference between the two studies could be explained by the difference in the type of samples used. Zheng et al. used plasma samples and white blood cells, while we used archived FFPE thyroid tissue.

In this study, mtDNA copy number was evaluated as a biomarker for thyroid cancer. The sensitivity of the test was 97.8%, and the specificity was 70.8%, indicating moderate accuracy. This study is the first to document such results for the use of mtDNA copy number testing.

Various methods can be used to measure the mitochondrial activity. They are qPCR and 8-hydroxyl 2’- deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a mtDNA oxidative damage marker to evaluate the levels of mitochondrial DNA copy number and mtDNA oxidative damage. This method is the most widely used method for measuring mtDNA copy numbers, partly due to its low cost and quick turnaround time. Another method used is Next-Generation Sequencing, which can identify copy number variant and cellular function comprehensively, such as WGS (Whole Genome Sequencing) [27]. Although there have been many advances using this method, one limitation is that this technique is very expensive and requires very lengthy steps of preparation. Another method is the absolute quantification of mtDNA copy numbers by using standard curves, this method may improve upon the performance of qPCR. Other methods such as studying the expression of mitophagic proteins FAM162A, NIX, and BNIP3 could contribute to a better understanding of the biology and development of thyroid cancer and support current cancer diagnosis techniques [28].

In conclusion, mtDNA copy number is a strong biomarker for detecting early-stage papillary thyroid carcinoma and may be implicated in thyroid carcinogenesis. The authors of the study suggest implementing the following measures to improve the accuracy of future study, including the following: to correlate the outcome with clinicopathological characteristics of patients on a larger-scale study, to include papillary thyroid carcinoma samples with different histological variants and different TNM stages and to replicate results with independent patient cohorts, for example, using publicly available datasets from the TCGA consortium and GWAS catalog.
