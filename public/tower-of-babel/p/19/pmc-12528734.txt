Demographic correlates of multicultural counseling competencies and the role of multicultural training
Authors: Cafer Kılıç, David Vaněček
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explored how demographic characteristics relate to the cultural competencies of counselor candidates and assessed whether multicultural training moderated these relationships. Earlier research primarily focused on a limited set of variables, such as gender, race/ethnicity, and involvement in multicultural education and training. On the other hand, other demographic features have received little attention in multicultural counseling research. Various factors linked to cultural competence in the general population, such as bilingualism, having a diverse family background, and international experience, may also enhance counselors’ cultural competence. This study broadens the scope of prior work by including both examined variables, investigating new demographic features, and examining the moderation effect of multicultural training. The research was conducted as a descriptive cross-sectional study based on a quantitative research approach. Three hundred and forty-one counseling students participated in the present study (Mage = 22.46, SD = 2.51, 80.6% female). The Demographic Information Form and the Miville–Guzman Universality–Diversity Scale–Short Form served as data collection tools. Data analysis included multiple regression and moderation techniques using SPSS and the R package. Among the demographic factors of counselor candidates, gender, academic level, peer diversity, and political views were consistently linked to aspects of their universality-diversity orientation. Additionally, multicultural training moderated some of the relationships between these demographic variables and universality-diversity orientation. These findings provide insight into which demographic characteristics are linked to counselor candidates’ cultural competencies, how these competencies evolve through training programs, and their implications for counseling practice.Keywords: Multicultural counseling, Competence, Demographics, Training, ModerationSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

The Multicultural and Social Justice Counseling (MCSJ) approach has been the complementary element of psychological services with the inclusion of the cultural diversity of clients and counselors in recent decades1,2. Initially, the concept of multicultural counseling and related competencies were constructed and recommended by preliminary research to respond to the various needs of cultural groups and individuals, addressing counselors’ cultural backgrounds, clients’ cultural contexts, and the application of culturally appropriate interventions2,3. Nonetheless, recent research extended the scope of the multicultural approach and competencies both in terms of counselors and clients1. The MSJC approach formulated four main constructs: a) addressing diversity and multiculturalism in the therapeutic setting; b) highlighting how oppression and mental health are interrelated; c) understanding the cultural context of the individuals; and d) promoting social justice competencies in various settings of counseling. Although multicultural/social justice counseling has been evolving since its establishment, there has been reasonable criticism against it, such as the ambiguity related to the definition of cultural competencies4 and how this approach can be used for the decolonization of the profession5. Considering these well-established criticisms against the MSJC and competencies, another criticism can be raised for inconsistent and inconclusive results regarding counselors’ competencies and a lack of practical implications. In other words, which personal, professional, personal, and cultural were associated with these competencies require further investigation. Thus, the main purpose of this study was to investigate the associations between multicultural competencies and demographic and professional features of counselor candidates, which are crucial for counselor education.

The emergence of intersectional6 and social class theories7 and the re-positioning of research within this framework can be pillars of the reconsideration of culturally sensitive counseling research that generated inconclusive and inconsistent findings. First, previous studies examining the associations between multicultural counseling competencies and demographic characteristics of counselors, which are also a part of their professional identity, in different samples have yielded inconsistent results. Counselors’ gender, age, and race/ethnicity have been the focus of the literature, which can be identified as a notable limitation. Counselors’ gender has been one of the main variables investigated in previous research, but results have been inconclusive8,9. While evaluating the overall findings regarding the relationship between gender and multicultural counseling competencies, some studies validated that being female and awareness of gender roles contributed to multicultural counseling competencies8,10–12; other studies found no significant relationship or difference between these variables13–15. It is not possible, therefore, to synthesize and generalize the findings of these studies because of the variation in their samples—college counselors, school counselors, and graduate counseling students— and the measurement of these competencies, leading to further investigation of these variables with various samples and assessment methods.

In contrast to findings related to gender and age, research on race and ethnicity has produced more consistent results. Racial and ethnic minority counselors and therapists have generally demonstrated higher levels of multicultural competence and associated behaviors than their white counterparts16,17. These group differences were particularly evident in the domains of multicultural awareness8,18 and knowledge19. In addition, compared to white therapists, racial and ethnic minority counselors reported being more engaged in their communities, incorporating cultural frameworks into their clinical work, and perceiving their practice environments as more culturally responsive18. While white therapists tended to develop greater awareness through formal education and training20, no meaningful differences emerged between racial/ethnic groups in terms of multicultural skills. Moreover, age and years of professional experience were not significantly associated with variations in multicultural competence11,19.

Unlike demographic characteristics, educational and training experiences—such as participation in multicultural courses, supervision, and formal instruction—have consistently been linked to increased multicultural counseling competencies. Factors like the length and intensity of graduate-level education13,19,21, engagement in multicultural coursework22, direct counseling practice23,24, and multicultural supervision13,25 have all been shown to significantly support the development of these competencies. Beyond curriculum-based learning, experiential activities—such as immersion activities26,27, participation in supplementary training programs28,29, service-learning-infused courses30, international travels31, and historical site visits32—also contribute meaningfully to counselors’ cultural competence. While most of this research has focused on graduate students in North America, comparable outcomes have been observed in Türkiye, though with fewer studies33–35. Collectively, these findings underscore the value of diverse educational and experiential opportunities in fostering multicultural competence among counseling trainees.

This study sought to explore additional demographic factors that may influence multicultural counseling competencies, extending beyond those commonly examined features, such as age, gender, and education. Investigating a wider range of demographic and educational characteristics offers a more comprehensive understanding of multicultural competencies among counselors. One such factor is religiosity/spirituality (R/S), which plays a central role in identity development. Although the counseling profession has traditionally emphasized secular and humanistic principles aligned with Western cultural values36,37, recent research has emphasized the importance of integrating R/S within the MCSJ framework38,39. Consistent with the field’s foundational values, counselors have generally been less religious and conservative than the broader population40,41 and are often reluctant to incorporate spiritual and religious concerns into therapeutic practice41. However, emerging evidence suggests that counselors characterized by strong multicultural orientations—either exclusively or in combination with religious commitment—tend to engage more frequently in multicultural training compared to those with predominantly religious or uncommitted orientations42. Similarly, students who actively seek personal meaning report higher levels of multicultural awareness and knowledge compared to peers with passive religious orientations shaped by external influences43. Another relevant dimension is political ideology. Research has consistently shown that mental health professionals tend to endorse liberal viewpoints40,44, and over half acknowledge that their political beliefs influence their clinical work to some degree40. Furthermore, clinical preferences appear to align with ideological leanings, with conservative practitioners more likely to favor cognitive-behavioral approaches and liberals tending toward humanistic, existential, or constructivist models44. Among counseling students, only engagement in advocacy activities—out of several advocacy behaviors—was found to significantly predict universal-diverse orientation45. The relative scarcity of research examining belief systems and political attitudes, particularly in diverse cultural contexts, underscores the relevance of including these factors in the present study.

Although direct research on the relationship between international experience and multicultural competence in counseling is limited, findings from studies conducted with broader populations offer valuable insights. For example, in a counseling-specific context, Fawcett et al.31 found that counselor trainees reported enhanced multicultural competencies following a ten-day study program in Guatemala. Similarly, studies with general populations have demonstrated that international exposure and intercultural engagement are linked to culturally responsive professional behaviors46, shifts in multicultural personality traits during international courses47, improved multicultural effectiveness following participation in international mobility programs48,49, and greater intercultural competence through collaborative online learning experiences50. Language, as a cultural construct that embodies thought, emotion, and perspective, also plays a critical role in multicultural competence. It is therefore plausible that multilingual counselors demonstrate higher levels of multicultural competence than their monolingual peers. Supporting this notion, it was reported that bilingual counseling students reported significantly greater multicultural awareness and knowledge than their non-bilingual counterparts51. Additionally, it was highlighted that bilingual counselors found multicultural training beneficial for practicing in other languages, although language itself has often been overlooked in multicultural counseling research52. Broader studies also support the association between bilingualism and intercultural competence, with bilingual individuals exhibiting higher levels of intercultural competence than monolinguals53,54. Taken together, these findings suggest that examining multicultural exposure and bilingualism as factors influencing multicultural competence can meaningfully build upon existing literature and offer practical implications for counselor training.

Research on family and social networks within the general population offers valuable insights that can inform the exploration and advancement of counselors’ cultural competence. Studies have shown that culturally diverse networks foster the emergence of culturally relevant and innovative ideas55, parents play a role in fostering their children’s bicultural competence56, and interracial relationships are associated with positive outcomes such as reduced minority rejection, more diverse social networks, and enhanced sociability and leadership57,58. These findings suggest that exposure to diverse social networks may enhance individuals’ intercultural engagement. In the counseling field specifically, higher multicultural competence among racially, ethnically, or culturally diverse counselors has been associated with the breadth and quality of their social connections18,19. For example, it was found that the number of LGBTQ friends was positively correlated with three dimensions of multicultural counseling competence, and that community counseling students demonstrated greater competence related to sexual orientation than school counseling students59. These findings underscore the importance of examining how family and social networks influence counselors’ multicultural understanding and practices—a relatively underexplored yet promising area for future research.

Current research

Building on the findings outlined in the literature, this study is grounded in several foundational considerations. Previous research has shown that counselor candidates’ basic demographic characteristics11,18,19, as well as their educational and training experiences24,28, are related to their multicultural competencies. However, several variables linked to cultural competence in the general population—such as bilingualism, international exposure, and family diversity—remain largely unexplored within the multicultural counseling literature. To address this gap, the current study broadens the scope of prior work by incorporating both traditionally examined and novel demographic factors. While earlier research tended to focus narrowly on variables such as gender, race/ethnicity, or multicultural course participation—often producing inconsistent results—this study utilizes a large sample of counselor candidates to revisit and extend prior findings. Moreover, rather than treating these variables merely as control factors or using them for basic group comparisons, this research investigates both direct and moderating (interaction) effects. A further distinction lies in the participant pool: unlike prior studies that predominantly involved graduate-level trainees or practicing professionals in North America, this research draws from undergraduate counseling candidates in a different cultural context. These cultural distinctions may reveal meaningful variations in multicultural understanding and practice. Thus, the present study aimed to examine the relationships between various demographic characteristics of counselor candidates and their multicultural competence in a large sample, using a correlational research design within a quantitative framework. Additionally, this study aimed to investigate whether multicultural educational experiences during their training—such as participation in multicultural courses, multicultural training sessions, and multicultural supervision—moderated the associations between demographic characteristics and multicultural competence. In this research, demographic characteristics were treated as independent variables predicting multicultural competence, in contrast to previous studies where they were commonly used as control variables. In light of this, the present study sought to address the following research questions: (1) Do counselor candidates’ demographic and educational characteristics predict their multicultural counseling competencies? (2) Does multicultural training moderate the relationship between these demographic variables and multicultural competence?

Method

Participants and procedures

Three hundred and forty-one counseling students at the undergraduate level in various universities participated in the present study (Mage = 22.46, SD = 2.51, 80.6% female). Participants were drawn from the third (46.6%) and fourth (53.4%) years of their undergraduate programs, selected due to their relevant coursework, field experience, and counseling practice, which aligned with the objectives of this study. Most participants identified as Turkish (93.3%) and reported coming from low- to middle-income families (99.4%). Approximately 11% were raised in multilingual households, and 7% had lived abroad at some point in their lives. Regarding parental education, 86.5% of mothers and 72.7% of fathers had a high school diploma or lower, while 13.5% of mothers and 27.3% of fathers held college or graduate degrees. In terms of perceived diversity, 11% of students described their families as “diverse” or “very diverse,” and about one-third characterized their peer networks similarly. Religiosity played a significant role in the daily lives of many participants, with around 40% indicating it was “very important” or “important.” Politically, nearly half of the sample self-identified as liberal. A total of 66% of participants reported having had some form of multicultural experience, including enrollment in multicultural coursework (62.5%), participation in additional training programs (12.6%), or multicultural supervision (10.9%).

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Uşak University. Data were collected via an online survey that included demographic information items and a validated measure of multicultural attitudes. The survey was distributed to counseling departments through convenience sampling. Prior to participation, students provided electronic informed consent and were assured that their participation was voluntary. Completing the web-based survey required approximately five minutes. No items in the study required participants to disclose personal information, and all responses were obtained anonymously. The data were stored securely and utilized solely for research purposes to maintain confidentiality.

Instruments

Demographic information form

The demographic form included items assessing participants’ age, gender, ethnicity, parental education levels, socioeconomic background, and various multicultural exposure variables. These included participation in multicultural education, engagement in additional multicultural training beyond coursework, multicultural supervision, and completion of an individual practicum. The form also inquired about bilingualism, perceived family and peer network diversity, religiosity, and political orientation. A composite variable representing multicultural education exposure was created by categorizing participants based on their responses (“yes” or “no”) to the three relevant items: participation in multicultural courses, engagement in additional multicultural learning experiences beyond coursework, and multicultural supervision.

Miville–Guzman universality–diversity scale-short form (M-GUDS-SF)

The short version of the M-GUDS-SF was used to measure counselor candidates’ acceptance of similarities and differences, defined as universality–diversity orientation60,61. The instrument consisted of three dimensions measuring behavioral, cognitive, and affective elements of universality–diversity orientation. The instrument is rated on a six-point Likert-type scale with responses ranging from 1 = “strongly disagree” to 6 = “strongly agree.” The short version of the instrument had satisfactory reliability coefficients, 0.82 for DC, 0.74 for RA, and 0.75 for CD. The psychometric properties of the Turkish version indicated excellent fit62. The internal consistency of the total score was 0.86 in the present study.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS-23 and RStudio. The skewness and kurtosis values of the variables under study suggested a normal distribution; specifically, the skewness values for DC, RA, and CD were − 0.43, − 0.58, and − 0.66, respectively, while kurtosis values were 0.01, 1.06, and 0.17. These values fell within the acceptable range of − 2 to + 263, confirming the assumption of normality. There were no missing data in categorical variables, and continuous variables had less than 1% missing values. The missing data were determined to be completely random [χ2(61,409) = 56, p = 0.288] and were handled using mean substitution, a conservative method that maintains the data distribution without estimating missing values64. Assumptions for linear regression—including linearity, absence of autocorrelation, and no multicollinearity—were checked through correlation coefficients, tolerance, variance inflation factor, Durbin–Watson statistics, and residual plots. To evaluate whether multicultural training moderated the relationship between demographic variables and universality-difference orientation, Hayes’ PROCESS Macro model 4 was utilized65. Bootstrap resampling with 10,000 iterations provided bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals for both the interaction effect and simple slopes.

Moderation analyses were performed to examine the effect of multicultural training on the relationships between demographic features and the dimensions of universality-diversity. Multicultural training moderated the relationship between gender and DC (B = − 3.29, SE = 1.59, t = − 2.07, p = 0.039, 95% CI [ − 6.42, − 0.17]), suggesting that the relationship between gender and DC depends on the levels of multicultural training. Simple slope analyses indicated that while the relationship between gender and DC was insignificant at low levels of multicultural training (B = − 0.29, p = 0.732), significant at high levels of multicultural training (B = − 3.59, p = 0.008); male counselor candidates reported lower scores of DC. The moderation model was also significant for the relationship between peer diversity and DC (B = − 1.03, SE = 0.49, t = − 2.10, p = 0.036, 95% CI [− 1.99, − 0.07]). Simple slope analyses showed that the relationship between peer diversity and DC was significant at low levels of multicultural training (B = 1.80, p < 0.001), while it had a weaker but significant effect at the higher levels of multicultural exposure (B = 0.77, p = 0.042), indicating that counselors benefit more from peer diversity when they lower levels of multicultural training compared to the other group. The moderation effect of multicultural training on the relationship between grade level and RA was significant (B = 1.86, SE = 0.96, t = 1.93, p = 0.055, 95% CI [− 0.04,3.75]. Simple slope analyses revealed that while the relationship between grade level and RA was not significant at lower levels of multicultural training (B = 0.13, p = 0.82), the relationship was significant at higher levels of multicultural training (B = 1.99, p = 0.012). The relationship between grade level and RA was stronger only when counselor candidates reported higher levels of multicultural training. Finally, the relationship between family diversity and CD was also moderated by multicultural training (B = 1.17, SE = 0.51, t = 2.28, p = 0.023, 95% CI [0.16, 2.17]). Simple slope analyses revealed that while the relationship between family diversity and CD was not significant at lower levels of multicultural training (B = 0.00, p = 0.99), the relationship was significant at higher levels of multicultural training (B = 1.17, p = 0.007). Suggesting that when counselor candidates had higher multicultural training, the relationship between family diversity and CD was endorsed.

Discussion

This study explored the relationships between counselor candidates’ demographic characteristics and their cultural competencies, while also assessing whether multicultural training moderated these associations. Among the demographic factors, gender, grade level, peer diversity, and political orientation showed consistent links with the universality-diversity orientation dimensions. Other demographic variables did not significantly relate to cultural competence. Additionally, multicultural training was found to moderate some of the relationships between demographic features and universality-diversity orientation. The findings both extended prior research and offered valuable implications for counselor education. The study not only examined how demographic characteristics relate to cultural competence but also revealed how multicultural training can influence these relationships, emphasizing the importance of integrating demographic factors into counselor training and practice.

The findings of this study indicated that gender significantly predicted all three dimensions of multicultural competence. While previous research has yielded mixed results regarding the relationship between gender and multicultural competencies8, several studies have reported that women tend to demonstrate higher levels of multicultural competence compared to men10–12,66, whereas other studies found no significant gender differences among counselors13,15. Consistent with these findings, the present study revealed that female counselor candidates exhibited greater multicultural competence across the emotional, cognitive, and behavioral dimensions. These results align with prior research employing the same cultural competence measure67, as well as studies demonstrating that women generally possess higher intercultural competencies than men in broader populations68,69, further supporting the current study’s outcomes.

The present study also found that grade level was significantly related to two dimensions of multicultural competence—contact with diversity and comfort with differences—but not to relativistic appreciation. Previous research has consistently identified multicultural training as a key factor in enhancing counselor candidates’ multicultural competencies. Specifically, time spent in postgraduate education13,19, enrollment in multicultural courses33,35, individual counseling practice23,24, multicultural supervision13,25, and participation in extracurricular activities such as cultural immersion26,27 and service-learning-infused courses30 have all been shown to promote the development of cultural competencies. In the context of counselor education in Türkiye, candidates typically engage in practical experiences during their final year, including counseling practice, supervision, relevant coursework (e.g., counseling skills, cultural counseling), and internships in schools. For example, Kağnıcı33 reported increases in multicultural awareness and knowledge among counselor candidates following a culturally sensitive course, with these gains also indirectly influencing their families through genogram activities. Such educational practices may explain the observed association between grade level and multicultural competence among counselor candidates.

Relatedly, political orientation emerged as a significant predictor across all three dimensions of cultural competence, with counselor candidates holding liberal views demonstrating higher multicultural competence. This aligns with the foundational values of the counseling profession, which are rooted in liberal ideology44,70. These values emphasize individuality, freedom, self-exploration, and autonomy—concepts broadly encompassed by clinical pragmatism and humanism36. Consequently, it is unsurprising that counselor candidates tend to endorse liberal ideologies. However, this finding warrants cautious interpretation, as such tendencies may partly result from exposure to specific counseling approaches during training, particularly humanistic models. Additionally, previous research has indicated that mental health professionals generally lean toward liberal political orientations30,44, which can influence their clinical practice to some degree40. Supporting this, a recent study identified fairness and care moral foundations—reflective of liberal values—as the only variables associated with universality-diversity orientation among counselor candidates34. Thus, the present findings are consistent with both the core values of the counseling profession and existing research on counselor ideologies.

Another key finding of this study was the positive association between peer diversity and multicultural competence; counselor candidates with more diverse peer networks demonstrated higher levels of multicultural competence. Although direct research on the influence of peer diversity on multicultural competence is limited, this finding aligns with broader literature on cultural competence and diverse social environments. Prior studies have shown that counselors with racially, ethnically, or culturally diverse backgrounds tend to exhibit greater multicultural competence than those from more homogeneous identities18,19. One plausible explanation is that individuals with multiple or intersecting identities often engage in broader and more diverse social networks, fostering cultural awareness and communication skills. Supporting this, Bidell59 found a positive relationship between the number of LGBTQ + friends and multicultural counseling competencies. Diverse peer groups may serve as informal yet powerful contexts for developing cultural competence by exposing individuals to varied perspectives and experiences. For example, culturally diverse networks have been shown to foster novel, culturally relevant ideas55, and interracial relationships have been linked to positive psychosocial outcomes57,58. Collectively, these findings suggest that peer diversity may serve as a meaningful avenue for cultivating multicultural competencies among counselor candidates.

The results indicated that age, ethnicity, international experience, bilingualism, and family diversity did not significantly contribute to any of the three regression models predicting multicultural competence. This finding aligns with expectations in certain cases. First, the majority of participants were in their early twenties with limited professional experience, reducing the likelihood of age serving as a meaningful predictor, consistent with previous research11,19. Second, although ethnicity has frequently been associated with multicultural competence17, where counselors from ethnically diverse backgrounds report higher awareness and knowledge compared to their White counterparts18,19, no such association was found in this study. This null result may be attributed to the limited representation of ethnic diversity in the sample, which could have hindered the detection of group differences. International experience, another theoretically relevant variable, was also not associated with multicultural competence in this study. This finding contrasts with earlier research demonstrating that even brief international travel experiences can enhance multicultural awareness and self-reported competence among counselor candidates31, as well as studies showing increased multicultural effectiveness following participation in international student mobility programs48,49. The lack of significance may reflect the small number of participants with such experiences or the overlap between international exposure and peer diversity in this sample. Similarly, family diversity did not contribute significantly to the models, likely due to the limited variability in this variable—most participants reported minimal or no family diversity. Finally, although bilingualism has previously been linked to enhanced multicultural awareness and understanding among counselors51 and greater intercultural competence in the general population53,54, no such association emerged in this study. Again, this may be due to the limited number of bilingual participants or the complex interaction between language use and contextual factors that were not captured in the present design.

Moderation analyses revealed several significant interactions between demographic characteristics and multicultural competence, contingent upon the level of multicultural training. First, gender differences in the diversity of contact dimension were observed only among those who reported high levels of multicultural training. At low levels of multicultural training, no significant gender difference was found. This suggests that multicultural training may amplify gender-related differences in multicultural awareness and engagement, possibly by encouraging deeper reflection on diversity-related issues during counselor education. Although previous findings regarding the link between gender and multicultural competence have been mixed12,15,66, the present study demonstrates that gender-based differences may become more salient when considered alongside multicultural training exposure. Another significant moderation effect was found between peer diversity and the diversity of contact dimension. At higher levels of multicultural training, this relationship was statistically significant but weak; however, at lower levels of training, peer diversity played a more substantial role in predicting diversity of contact. This suggests that in the absence of structured multicultural training, diverse peer networks may serve as a compensatory mechanism for fostering contact with cultural differences. Prior research supports the idea that diverse social networks, including friendships with marginalized groups, can facilitate the development of multicultural competence55,56,59. Multicultural training also moderated the relationship between grade level and relativistic appreciation. Specifically, this relationship was stronger among counselor candidates who reported higher levels of multicultural training. This finding aligns with previous literature showing that educational experiences—such as multicultural coursework, supervision, and immersion activities—are positively linked to multicultural competence24,27,30,35. It appears that multicultural training enables students to more effectively recognize and appreciate both the commonalities and differences between their own and others’ worldviews, including those of their clients. A final moderation was observed between family diversity and comfort with differences. While this relationship was not significant at low levels of multicultural training, it became significant at higher levels. Although a diverse family environment may offer initial exposure to cultural differences, it may not be sufficient on its own to promote multicultural competence. However, when such familial experiences are integrated with formal multicultural training, counselor candidates appear more capable of developing emotional readiness and openness toward cultural differences. These findings suggest that structured multicultural education can strengthen the foundation laid by personal and familial experiences, enhancing counselor candidates’ comfort with diversity in clinical contexts55,56,58.

Limitations and future directions

Although this study offered a comprehensive examination of counselor candidates’ demographic characteristics and multicultural competencies, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. First, while the sample size was sufficient for the analyses conducted, it may not be representative of all counseling students nationwide. A larger, more inclusive sample encompassing counseling students from across the country could yield more generalizable results. Second, the relatively small subgroup sizes for ethnicity, international experience, and bilingualism limited the ability to detect potential associations, particularly in moderation analyses. Future research should aim to increase these subgroup sizes to better explore such relationships. Third, although the Miville–Guzman Universality–Diversity Scale–Short Form is a practical instrument for assessing multicultural attitudes, it may have limited applicability in both counseling and Turkish contexts. For example, items referencing racial diversity (e.g., “I am only at ease with people of my own race”) may be less relevant in Turkey, where ethnicity and religiosity play a more prominent role in national identity. Fourth, participants were undergraduate students with limited exposure to counseling practice, multicultural coursework, and supervision. Subsequent studies could focus on graduate students or practicing school counselors, allowing for more nuanced moderation analyses differentiating multicultural education, training, practice, and supervision. Fifth, the reliance on self-reported multicultural competencies raises concerns about social desirability bias. Future studies could incorporate evaluations from peers or supervisors during practicum experiences to enhance measurement accuracy. Finally, the cross-sectional design of this study precludes conclusions about causal or longitudinal effects; for example, it remains unclear whether counselor candidates held liberal values prior to their education or developed these ideologies during their training.

Conclusion

In summary, this study underscored the important role of demographic factors in influencing counselor candidates’ cultural competencies, as well as the moderating impact of multicultural training. While certain demographic characteristics—namely gender, grade level, peer diversity, and political orientation—were significantly related to cultural competence, other demographic variables showed no meaningful association. Furthermore, multicultural training functioned as a significant moderator, strengthening the relationships between demographic factors and cultural competencies. Overall, the findings offered two key contributions: they expanded upon previous research and provided meaningful insights for counselor education. First, this study broadened the existing literature by examining the relationships between counselors’ demographic characteristics and cultural competencies in a large sample, while also exploring the moderating effect of multicultural training. Additionally, the results highlighted how counselor candidates’ demographic features can be integrated into training and practice to support the development of cultural competence.
