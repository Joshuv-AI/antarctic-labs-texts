Neuroergonomic evaluation of risk-warning eHMI penetration rates in vehicle platoons: effects on pedestrians’ mental workload, situation awareness, and gap acceptance
Authors: Fang Yang, Xu Sun, Jing Ma, Siyuan Zhou, Bingjian Liu, Puhong Li, Wei Lyu, Shijiang Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe deployment of Level 4–5 autonomous vehicles (AVs) in real traffic environments presents significant challenges for pedestrian interaction, primarily due to the absence of human driver cues. To address this limitation, risk-warning external human–machine interfaces (eHMIs), which communicate dynamic crossing risks based on the AV’s time-to-arrival (TTA), have received growing attention. However, given the visual complexity of real-world traffic and the potential transition from partial to full eHMI adoption within vehicle platoons, the cognitive impacts of such systems on pedestrians, particularly in terms of mental workload (MW) and situation awareness (SA), require further investigation. This study investigates the impact of three eHMI penetration rates—zero (all vehicles without eHMI), partial (half with and half without), and full (all vehicles with eHMI)—within vehicle platoons on pedestrians’ MW, SA, and gap acceptance. To simulate realistic traffic conditions, vehicle speeds (30 km/h and 36 km/h) and temporal gap sizes (ranging from 2 to 6 s in 0.5-s increments) were randomized. A video-based experiment was conducted with 24 participants who simulated street-crossing decisions by selecting appropriate temporal gaps when encountering vehicle platoons. SA was assessed using the Situation Awareness Rating Technique (SART), and MW was evaluated through a concurrent oddball task, with P300 event-related potentials (ERPs) serving as indicators of residual attentional capacity. Results showed that full eHMI penetration resulted in significantly higher SA and lower MW compared to partial penetration condition. When compared to zero penetration, full eHMI penetration enhanced pedestrians’ sensitivity to gap size, indicating that as the gap size increased, the probability of pedestrians crossing increased more sharply. Notably, this effect occurred without significantly altering MW or SA. In contrast, partial penetration condition led to pedestrians crossing less frequently when encountering vehicles equipped with eHMI and was associated with significantly higher MW and lower SA. These findings suggest that risk-warning eHMIs enhance pedestrian–AV interactions effectively under full penetration conditions. However, partial penetration may impair pedestrians’ decision-making and cognitive performance, highlighting the importance of strategic planning during the transitional deployment phase of eHMIs.Keywords: Pedestrian, Autonomous vehicle, eHMI penetration rates, Mental workload, Situation awarenessSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

The emergence of Level 4–5 autonomous vehicles (AVs), as defined by Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE), enables vehicles to independently perform all driving tasks under complex conditions, thereby allowing human drivers to engage in non-driving activities such as resting or working1. However, the integration of AVs into mixed-traffic environments—where they must coexist with traditional vehicles (TVs) and vulnerable road users like pedestrians—presents significant human factors challenges, e.g.,2.

One of the most pressing concerns is the interaction between AVs and pedestrians in uncontrolled environments, such as areas lacking traffic signals or clear road markings3. In conventional settings, pedestrians often rely on social cues—such as eye contact, gestures, or body language—from human drivers to negotiate crossing priority4. In the context of AVs, the absence of social cues—due to the absence of human drivers or their engagement in non-driving activities—has been found to contribute to pedestrian confusion, hesitation, and an increased perception of crossing risk3.

To bridge this communication gap, researchers have proposed the use of external human–machine interfaces (eHMIs) on AVs5. These interfaces—commonly displaying abstract lights, symbols, or textual messages on vehicle surfaces (e.g., windshield or grille)—aim to convey vehicle status, intentions, or risk warnings to pedestrians. While eHMIs have been shown to enhance perceived safety, crossing decisions, and behaviors, some studies have warned that such systems may come at a cognitive cost—specifically, increased mental workload (MW) for pedestrians, e.g.,5–7. Notably, empirical evidence has demonstrated that eHMIs can elevate MW8, and some participants have even reported experiencing mental overload9,10.

MW is defined as the degree of activation of a finite pool of cognitive resources, limited in capacity, while processing a primary task over time11. Elevated MW can impair situation awareness (SA), an individual’s understanding of what is happening in the current situation, which is critical for performance across a range of domains12. As mental resource is limited in capacity, elevated MW for task performance could reduce the mental resources available for maintaining SA13. It has been found that people may pay less attention to peripheral information, become more disorganized in scanning information, and are more likely to succumb to attentional tunneling when affected by elevated MW12. People are also more likely to arrive at a decision without taking into account all available information12. For pedestrians, effective SA is essential for making safe road-crossing decisions14, as shown in Fig. 1. Therefore, assessing the effects of eHMIs on MW and SA, beyond merely evaluating crossing decisions or behaviors, is critical for understanding their overall cognitive impact and practical effectiveness.

Recent studies emphasize the importance of ensuring that eHMIs do not inadvertently increase MW beyond baseline levels observed in scenarios without eHMIs6,7. While various eHMI types have been explored—such as displays for vehicle intent15–18, automation status19,20—risk-warning eHMIs remain underexamined in terms of their cognitive implications.

Risk warning eHMI penetration rates within vehicle platoon

Risk warning eHMIs dynamically communicate risk levels to pedestrians based on the time-to-arrival (TTA) of the oncoming AV21–23. For example, a vehicle might display a "low-risk" signal when far away (TTA ≥ 5.1 s), switch to "medium-risk" as it approaches (3.7 s ≤ TTA < 5.1 s), and present a "high-risk" warning when close (TTA < 3.7 s), helping pedestrians decide whether to proceed or wait23. When pedestrians decide to cross based on a “low-risk” signal, AVs can then adapt their speed or yield accordingly. Conversely, upon perceiving a “high-risk” signal, pedestrians are more likely to refrain from crossing, thereby reducing the likelihood of emergency braking by AVs. Such eHMI designs avoid the overly conservative strategy of requiring AVs to always yield to pedestrians under all conditions. Instead, they hold promise for enhancing pedestrian safety while simultaneously improving overall traffic efficiency23. As emphasized by24, the design of AV-pedestrian interactions should strive to achieve an optimal balance between operational efficiency and road safety.

The evaluation of risk-warning eHMI effects on MW and SA is particularly relevant in vehicle platoon scenarios, where multiple vehicles pass in succession with varying speeds and TTAs21,23. Existing studies have raised concerns that in high-complexity environments, equipping all AVs with eHMIs may cause visual clutter, potentially resulting in mental overload25,26. Although previous research has demonstrated the potential of risk-warning eHMIs to enhance crossing safety during interactions with vehicle platoons21,23, understanding their cognitive implications in terms of MW and SA is essential to ensure their effectiveness in supporting both cognitive processing and behavioral decision-making.

Furthermore, studies have predicted that AVs will maintain relatively partial penetration rates within transportation systems for an extended period, resulting in the long-term coexistence of AVs and TVs16,27. Therefore, vehicle platoons are anticipated to transition from partial to full AV deployment, with the penetration of eHMIs gradually expanding from use in some vehicles to all vehicles. In other words, the penetration of eHMIs will undergo a progression from zero, to partial, and eventually to full deployment.

Although existing studies suggest that pedestrians tend to make less safe crossing decisions when encountering AVs without eHMIs under partial penetration conditions23, the underlying cognitive mechanisms have not been systematically examined. Prior research has shown that even in scenarios where a pedestrian faces two AVs, MW is significantly higher when only one AV is equipped with an eHMI compared to when both AVs display consistent signals16,27. Therefore, it is necessary to investigate how varying rates of eHMI penetration in platoons affect pedestrian MW and SA. A deeper understanding of this issue may help explain unsafe crossing behavior under partial penetration and inform strategies for a smoother transition toward fully equipped eHMI-AV environments.

MW measurements

MW can be measured through both subjective methods (e.g., NASA-TLX) and physiological measures (e.g., cardiovascular, ocular, and brain activity)13. Previous research on AV–pedestrian interactions has predominantly assessed the effect of eHMIs on MW using NASA-TLX, e.g.,19,20,22,28–30 or eye-tracking techniques, e.g.,16–18. Eye-tracking commonly evaluates fixation counts, fixation durations, saccades, and pupil diameter, based on the assumption that higher MW is associated with more fixations, longer durations, more saccades31, and larger pupil size32.

However, eye-tracking presents several limitations in assessing the cognitive impact of visual eHMIs. First, gaze-based metrics such as fixation duration and saccades may be influenced by the novelty, duration, and complexity of the visual stimulus itself33. Second, variations in luminance within the visual field can affect pupil diameter independently of MW18. Third, eye-tracking primarily captures visual attention and is vulnerable to the “look-but-do-not-see” phenomenon, where individuals may look at but not cognitively process the information presented34. Given these limitations, neurophysiological measures such as event-related potentials (ERPs) have gained attention as more direct and objective indicators of MW13. One such ERP component is the P300, which is typically elicited using an oddball task that runs concurrently with a primary task35,36. In this paradigm, participants are asked to identify infrequent target stimuli (e.g., 20%) among frequent standard stimuli (e.g., 80%). As mental resources are limited, the amplitude of the P300 evoked by the concurrent task could serve as an indicator of the mental resources allocated to the primary task35,36. Furthermore, since elevated MW may impair SA by reducing awareness of other road elements, task performance in the oddball task (i.e., the correct detection of target stimuli) can also serve as a behavioral indicator of pedestrian SA37.

Research aim

The present study aims to investigate how risk-warning eHMIs and their penetration rates within AV platoons influence pedestrian MW, SA, and gap acceptance, using ERP, subjective, and behavioral measures.

Research Questions (RQs):

RQ1: How does the presence of a risk-warning eHMI affect pedestrian MW, SA, and gap acceptance?

RQ2: How do different rates of eHMI penetration in AV platoons influence pedestrian MW, SA, and gap acceptance?

By examining the effects of risk-warning eHMIs and their penetration rates in platoon scenarios on pedestrian MW, SA, and gap acceptance, this study seeks to inform the design of AV–pedestrian interaction strategies that are both safer and cognitively efficient.

Methods

Participants

Prior to gathering data, we conducted a priori power analysis using G*Power38, which showed that a minimum of 18 participants was required to achieve a statistical power of 0.95. This calculation was established on a high effect size of f = 0.4 and an alpha probability value of p = 0.05. A total of 24 participants (10 females, 14 males, M = 22.71 years, SD = 1.76) were recruited for this study through an online advertisement. All participants were staff and students from Qilu University of Technology. Regarding familiarity with AVs, 8.33% (2/24) of participants had driven a L3 or higher AV, 29.17% (7/24) had seen such AVs on the road, such as delivery vehicles, and 33.33% (8/24) of participants were familiar with the latest developments in autonomous driving technology. In terms of trust in AVs, 16.67% (4/24) of participants did not trust them, 33.33% (8/24) were uncertain, and 50% (12/24) trusted them. On average, participants spent 92.92 min (SD = 61.95) walking on roads daily. All participants were right-handed and reported having normal or corrected vision and hearing. None had a history of neurological or psychological disorders. This study received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Nottingham Ningbo China, and it was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to the commencement of the experiment, each participant consented in writing and was informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without facing any consequences.

Apparatus and materials

Street-crossing task

In this study, a video-based method was employed to simulate street-crossing tasks. This safe and controllable method has been widely used to investigate the impact of eHMIs on pedestrian-AV interactions. It is particularly useful for examining how pedestrians subjectively and visually perceive the external features of AVs and the influence of eHMI design on their decision-making when crossing the street39,40.

The video presented a traffic scene from a pedestrian’s perspective on a 3440 × 1440 resolution screen (see Fig. 2), as if the viewer was standing on the curb, perpendicular to the road, and waiting to cross. The viewpoint was fixed, preventing pedestrians from adjusting the scene, thereby maintaining consistency across participants.

Video-based experimental setup with EEG recording.

The street-crossing scenario, developed using Unity (version 2023.2.3), depicted a two-lane open road without traffic lights or zebra crossings. Each lane was 3.5 m wide, and the visible road section stretched for 100 m. A stream of traffic traveled from left to the right. All vehicles kept driving with a constant speed without acceleration or deceleration. The other lane was empty.

To realistically simulate platoon complexity, variations in vehicle speeds and temporal gap sizes were introduced. Each eHMI penetration block consisted of 72 vehicles, with two variables, speed and gap size, randomized. The initial vehicle appeared from the far left of the screen, starting 100 m away from the pedestrian, and traveled at a constant speed of 36 km/h. Subsequent vehicles had randomized speeds of either 30 km/h or 36 km/h. Speeds below 40 km/h were chosen because pedestrians tend to underestimate vehicle speeds above this threshold41. Additionally, the two relatively close speeds were selected to reflect realistic variations in vehicle speeds under similar road conditions. The program ensured that after the rear of the preceding vehicle passed the participant, the gap size of the approaching vehicle, i.e., TTA, was randomized between 2 and 6 s in 0.5 s intervals (i.e., 2 s, 2.5 s, 3 s, 3.5 s, 4 s, 4.5 s, 5 s, 5.5 s, 6 s).

The eHMI used in this study was similar to23, which categorizes the real-time risk level based on the TTA of oncoming vehicles into high risk (TTA < 3.7 s), medium risk (3.7 s ≤ TTA < 5.1 s), and low risk (5.1 s ≤ TTA). Since the risk warning eHMI has not yet been standardized, the eHMI used in this study employs a triadic color scheme (red, yellow, and green, as shown in Fig. 3) to represent different crossing risk levels: red indicates high risk, yellow indicates medium risk, and green indicates low risk.

TVs without eHMI and AVs with eHMI.

Additionally, a cyan light (CIE chromaticity values: x = 0.15, y = 0.38) atop the AV indicated its autonomous driving mode42, as shown in Fig. 3.

A within-subjects design was utilized, comprising three blocks (as seen in Table 1): zero, full and partial penetration rate. As shown in Fig. 4, in the zero penetration condition, all vehicles were TVs without an eHMI (highlighted with black borders in the figure). In the full penetration condition, all vehicles were AVs with an eHMI (highlighted with cyan borders in the figure). In the partial penetration condition, half of the vehicles were AVs with an eHMI (denoted as Partial penetration_AVs, highlighted with cyan borders in the figure) and the other half were TVs without an eHMI (denoted as Partial penetration_TVs, highlighted with black borders in the figure). AVs with eHMI and TVs without an eHMI appeared on the road in a random order in equal proportion under partial penetration condition.

Concurrent oddball task

An auditory oddball task was required to perform concurrently with the primary street-crossing task. Participants were presented target stimulus (a high-pitch tone at 2000Hz, 52.5 dB, lasting 500ms, with a probability of 0.2) amid a series of commonly occurring distractor stimuli (low-pitch tones at 1000 Hz, 52.5 dB, also 500ms long, with a probability of 0.8). The chosen frequencies were based on the study by43, which do not reflect the auditory signals in pedestrian street-crossings scenarios. During the auditory oddball task, a total of 60 high-pitch tones (target stimuli) and 240 low-pitch tones (distractor stimuli) were randomly played. The proportion of target stimuli was consistent with the oddball task paradigm35,36. The interval between stimuli was set to 500 ms.

The presentation of audio stimuli and the time stamping of events were managed by E-Prime (Version 2.0, Psychology Software Tools, Inc.) on a laptop. EEG recordings were conducted using the Neuroscan system and Curry 8.0 software. A set of 64 Ag/AgCl active electrodes was employed, following the extended international 10–20 EEG system placement (illustrated in Fig. 2). Although this study only focused on EEG data from the Cz electrode, the full array of 64 electrodes was applied and recorded to collect comprehensive EEG data for potential use in subsequent analyses. All channels were digitized at a rate of 1000 Hz.

Procedure

After providing written, informed consent, participants received briefings on both the primary street-crossing task and the concurrent oddball task. Regarding the primary task, participants were informed that there were no crosswalks or traffic lights, and that they need to decide whether to cross the street based on their observation of the oncoming vehicles. They were also informed that, in all three penetration conditions, vehicles would not slow down or yield, requiring them to choose a sufficiently large time gap for a safe street crossing. This clarification helps eliminate confusion and ensures that pedestrians base their decisions solely on the vehicle speed, gap size and eHMI, without mistakenly considering whether the vehicle will stop.

Next, participants were introduced to the meaning of the eHMI signal due to that once participants internalized the meaning of the eHMI, the influence of different eHMI modalities on pedestrians’ street-crossing decisions diminished or disappeared44,45.

After, participants were instructed that, if they decided to cross, they should record their decision by pressing the '1' button on the keyboard. An observation was recorded for each vehicle, including eHMI penetration condition, vehicle type, vehicle speed, gap size, and pedestrians’ gap acceptance (cross or wait). The flow of vehicle sequences proceeded regardless of whether participants pressed the key or not.

As for the concurrent task, participants were instructed to count occurrences of infrequent high-pitch tones. Counting was opted instead of providing a physical response to the target stimuli due to that prior research found that motor-evoked potentials could potentially interfere with the P300 effects36. After, participants were repeatedly presented with two auditory tones until they could reliably distinguish between the target high-pitch and distractor low-pitch stimuli.

It was emphasized to the participants that the primary task was the selection of gaps for street crossings, and that their attention should be primarily focused on this task.

Afterward, the participant was comfortably seated 60cm away from a screen, as shown in Fig. 2. They then completed a practice phase, which included the primary street-crossing task under the zero penetration condition and the concurrent oddball task, to familiarize themselves with both tasks.

Following that, participants were fitted with EEG helmets, as shown in Fig. 2. Electrode gel (Easy Gel) was applied to reduce impedance across all channels using a blunt-tip syringe. After application, electrode impedance was monitored for one minute. If any channels still exhibited impedance above 20 kΩ, the experimenter gently stirred the gel within those channels with the syringe. If impedance persisted above 20 kΩ, additional gel was applied to those channels as needed. Once the impedance of each electrode was reduced below 20 kΩ, the formal experiment began.

Participants completed three eHMI penetration blocks: zero, partial, and full eHMI penetration street-crossing tasks, each accompanied by the same concurrent oddball task. The order of these three blocks was counterbalanced among participants using a Latin Square design. The Balanced Latin Square Generator (https://hci-studies.org/balanced-latin-square) was used, and the tool automatically generated sequences for 6 participants, as shown in Table 2. To accommodate the total number of participants, these sequences were repeated 4 times.

After each block, participants assessed their SA for the street-crossing task using the 3D-SART and reported their counting of target audio stimuli.

The practice phase lasted 5 min, followed by each of the subsequent experimental blocks, which also lasted 5 min, with a 3-min rest interval between blocks. The entire experiment took approximately 1 h to complete. A remuneration of 50 RMB was provided to each participant.

Dependent variables

Gap acceptance

Gap acceptance was whether a participant crossed in front of a vehicle or not. For each approaching vehicle with a predetermined gap (i.e., the time interval between the approaching vehicle and the pedestrian when the former vehicle just passed the pedestrian), pedestrians’ gap acceptance was recorded as “wait” or “crossed”. When the gap size is larger, it is safer for pedestrians to cross. Conversely, when the gap size is smaller, it is unsafe for pedestrians to cross.

P300 effect

The P300 effect, identified as the third positive deflection occurring between 250 and 450 ms post-stimulus, was primarily observed in the centro-parietal scalp regions46. In our analysis, the P300 effect was measured by the peak amplitude between 250 and 450 ms at the Cz electrode.

Oddball counting accuracy

Oddball counting accuracy refers to the precision with which participants count infrequent deviant auditory stimuli presented within a sequence of standard stimuli in the concurrent oddball task.

Situation awareness

SART was used to assess pedestrians’ SA, as it is widely recognized for providing valuable insights into the comparative effectiveness of different interface designs47. The 3D-SART assessed the three constructs of SA: the demand on attentional resources, the supply of attentional resources, and the understanding of the situation48. Demand represented how much demand was placed on pedestrians’ attention during the street-crossing gap selection task; Supply represented how much spare attention and mental ability was available to the pedestrians during the street-crossing gap selection task; and Understanding represented how well the pedestrians understood the street-crossing situation. Responses for each construct, Understanding, Demand, and Supply, were rated on a scale from 1 (low) to 7 (high). The overall SA score was calculated as follows: SA = Understanding—(Demand—Supply).

Data analysis

Gap acceptance

Vehicle speed and gap size were initially introduced as randomized traffic parameters to simulate naturalistic traffic dynamics. As both factors have been shown to influence pedestrian decision-making21,23, their effects on gap acceptance were also analyzed.

To evaluate pedestrians’ gap acceptance, a Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) with a logit link function was fitted using R (version 4.3.2). Gap acceptance was modeled as a binary outcome (cross vs. wait) for each vehicle–pedestrian encounter, with gap size, vehicle speed, and traffic condition as fixed effects and participant as a random effect by the maximum likelihood method. Gap size (2.0–6.0 s, in 0.5-s intervals) was standardized (M = 0, SD = 1) prior to analysis. We began with a full model including all main effects, two-way interactions, and the three-way interaction. Then, model selection employed stepwise reduction based on Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), which balances model fit and complexity. AIC was chosen over the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) due to its suitability for exploratory analyses, as it imposes a less stringent penalty on model complexity, enabling better representation of intricate pedestrian-AV interactions in our dataset.

P300 effect

The EEG data was processed in MATLAB R2020a (MathWorks, Natick, USA) using EEGLAB v2022.1 and ERPLAB v10.02 toolboxes. After an initial review of the continuous EEG data, three participants were excluded due to poor data quality, which was caused by issues such as electrode contact problems. These problems likely resulted from factors such as thick hair or a loose-fitting EEG cap, compromising the quality of the EEG data. Additionally, three more participants were excluded because the EEG data for different experimental conditions were not properly separated during the experiment. Specifically, although the experimenter pressed the stop button at the end of the first condition, the marker from the first condition persisted into the data for the second condition, leading to repeated labeling within a single condition. As a result, the integrity of the EEG data was compromised, necessitating the exclusion of these participants.

For the EEG data of the remaining 18 participants, segments with significant noise were initially discarded. Next, a Finite Impulse Response (FIR) band-pass filter was applied (‘locutoff’, 1Hz; ‘hicutoff’, 30Hz). Artifacts in the EEG data were identified through extended infomax Independent Component Analysis (ICA). Independent Components (ICs) with a probability exceeding 90% of belonging to muscle, eye, or heart-related artifacts were then marked and removed. Afterward, the EEG data was re-referenced, assigned to bins, and segmented into epochs ranging from 200 ms before to 800 ms after the onset of the target stimuli. Baseline correction for those epochs was then conducted by subtracting the mean voltage of the 200 ms pre-stimulus interval. Subsequently, abnormal epochs were identified and removed. The peak values of each epoch within the time window of 250ms to 450ms were extracted, and the mean and standard deviation of these peak values were calculated. Epochs with peak values exceeding two standard deviations from the mean were considered anomalous and were subsequently removed. The average ERPs for target stimuli were calculated for each experimental condition (zero, partial and full penetration rate).

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.3.2 to compare pedestrians’ P300 across the three penetration conditions (zero, partial and full), which focused on the averaged P300 across the entire block of the street-crossing task under each eHMI penetration condition, rather than on individual pedestrian-vehicle encounter. The Shapiro–Wilk test (from the stats package in R) was used to assess the normality of the data. If the data were normally distributed, analyses were conducted using ANOVA (from the afex package in R) to determine whether significant differences existed between the conditions. If significant, Tukey’s HSD post-hoc tests (from the emmeans package in R) were performed. If the data were not normally distributed, the Friedman test (from the rstatix package in R) was applied to assess differences between the conditions. If significant, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (from the rstatix package in R). To control for Type I error due to multiple comparisons, p-values were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction.

Oddball counting accuracy

The participants’ count of infrequent deviant auditory stimuli was subtracted by 60, and the absolute value of the result was used as their accuracy. The analysis focused on the total error rate across the entire block of each eHMI penetration condition, rather than on individual pedestrian-vehicle encounter. Statistical analyses to compare pedestrians’ oddball counting accuracy across the three penetration conditions were conducted in the same manner as for P300.

Situation awareness

Statistical analyses to compare pedestrians’ overall SA, and Understanding, Demand, Supply across the three penetration conditions were conducted in the same manner as for P300. The analysis focused on the SA scores assessed after the entire block of each eHMI penetration condition, rather than for individual pedestrian-vehicle encounter.

Effects of speeds and gap sizes on gap acceptance

As shown in Table 3 and Fig. 5, both gap size (p < 0.001) and vehicle speed (p < 0.001) significantly influenced gap acceptance, with larger gaps and higher speeds increasing the likelihood of crossing. Moreover, a significant interaction between gap size and speed was observed (p = 0.012); at higher speeds, the probability of crossing rose more sharply as gap size increased.

The predicted outcome of gap acceptance in probability and logit space.

Effects of eHMI penetration rates on gap acceptance

Participants’ gap acceptance in the full penetration condition did not significantly differ from that in the zero penetration condition (p = 0.238; see Table 3). However, the interaction effect between the full penetration condition and gap size was significant (p = 0.032), indicating that, under full penetration, participants’ probability of crossing increased more rapidly with gap size for AVs equipped with eHMI (as illustrated in Fig. 5). Furthermore, participants’ gap acceptance when encountering AVs with eHMI under the partial penetration condition was significantly different from the zero penetration condition (p = 0.008), with participants crossing less frequently in front of AVs with eHMI under partial penetration conditions.

P300 effects

Figure 6 presents the averaged ERPs recorded from the Cz electrode. Target stimuli induced a pronounced positive deflection within the 250–450 ms time window. The Shapiro–Wilk test indicated that the P300 amplitude for Zero and Full penetration followed a normal distribution, whereas Partial penetration did not (p = 0.003). Friedman test revealed a significant effect of eHMI penetration rate on P300 amplitude (χ2 (2) = 8.78, p = 0.012). Specifically, the amplitude in the full penetration condition (M = 1.62 μV, SD = 1.05 μV) was significantly higher (p = 0.012) than that in the partial penetration condition (M = 0.68 μV, SD = 1.30 μV). The partial penetration condition also exhibited a significantly lower P300 amplitude (p = 0.042) compared to the zero penetration condition. No significant difference (p = 1.000) was observed between the full and zero penetration conditions (M = 1.44 μV, SD = 0.78 μV).

This study investigated the impact of eHMI penetration rates in vehicle platoon on pedestrians’ MW, SA, and gap acceptance. It employed physiological methods (P300) to measure pedestrians’ MW, utilizes subjective assessments (SART) to measure SA, and records pedestrians’ gap acceptance across different temporal gap sizes and vehicle speeds. Effects of eHMI penetration on MW and SA

The findings of MW indicated that, compared to full penetration conditions, partial penetration results in higher MW. This finding is consistent with previous research examining pedestrian interactions with two AVs simultaneously—specifically, comparing scenarios in which only one AV of the two is equipped with an eHMI versus both AVs using eHMIs16,27. These studies found that pedestrians experienced increased MW when only one AV employed an eHMI. When both AVs were equipped with eHMIs, the consistent communication of intent enhanced pedestrians’ sense of certainty and reduced MW27.

Additionally, an important finding is that under partial penetration conditions, pedestrians’ MW is significantly higher and SA is significantly lower compared to the zero penetration condition. To the best of our knowledge, this has not been empirically evaluated in the existing literature. This finding provides solid physiological evidence that supports the argument put forward by23, which suggests that the random distribution of vehicles with and without eHMI within the platoon may necessitate pedestrians to exert additional effort in monitoring and switching between the two strategies for handling different vehicles. An increase in pedestrian MW may cause pedestrians to pay less attention to peripheral information, become more disorganized in scanning information, and be more susceptible to attentional tunneling12. A decrease in pedestrian SA may lead to heightened feelings of danger, a diminished sense of relief, and a reduced ability to make decisions safely and efficiently49,50.

Furthermore, this MW result is consistent with the findings related to the “supply” construct in SA, which refers to the remaining attention and cognitive capacity available during the task in this experiment48. Under partial penetration conditions, the “supply” construct in SA is significantly reduced, suggesting that pedestrians have fewer cognitive resources available while performing the street-crossing task. This consistency suggests that P300 amplitude can serve as a valid measure of the impact of eHMI penetration rate on pedestrian MW. Specifically, as mental resources are limited, a lower P300 amplitude in the oddball task (indicating fewer available mental resources for the secondary task) correlates with higher MW in the primary street-crossing task (indicating more mental resources taken up by the primary task). However, while significant differences were observed in the P300 results, no such differences were found in oddball counting accuracy. This discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that participants occasionally missed some deviant stimuli or miscounted regular stimuli as deviants, leading to an overall count that does not accurately reflect whether each deviant stimulus was correctly identified.

Another noteworthy finding is that full eHMI penetration did not result in a higher MW compared to the zero penetration condition. This suggests that under full penetration conditions, although the amount of visual information pedestrians need to process increases, these additional details do not further burden the pedestrians. A possible reason was that the information provided by eHMI reduces the uncertainty pedestrians face when crossing the street, thereby enhancing perceived clarity. As Sheridan suggests, reducing uncertainty would not lead to an increase in MW6. Specifically, the experimenter familiarized participants with the meaning of eHMI before the experiment began. By ensuring that pedestrians understand the meaning of eHMI, confusion is less likely to arise44,45.

Effects of eHMI penetration, vehicle speed and gap size on gap acceptance

The findings reveal typical gap acceptance patterns51: pedestrians are more likely to accept larger temporal gaps. Furthermore, the results show that higher vehicle speeds significantly increase the likelihood of crossing. This may be due to the fact that, under higher speed conditions, the spatial distance between the vehicle and the pedestrian is greater for the same temporal gap size. Pedestrians tend to perceive this larger spatial distance as a safer situation. This observation is consistent with the previously identified "farther and safer" perceptual bias52. This perceptual bias is further reflected in the interaction effect between vehicle speed and gap size, where pedestrians accepted more large gaps at higher speeds, resulting in a steeper curve of crossing probability as temporal gaps increased.

Under full eHMI penetration conditions, pedestrians’ gap acceptance was more responsive to the changes in gap size. They rejected more small gaps and accepted more large gaps, resulting in a steeper curve of crossing probability as temporal gaps increased. It is likely that pedestrians relied on the eHMI in addition to their own perception, consistent with the findings of23. These findings suggest that, under full penetration conditions, risk-warning eHMIs help pedestrians reduce the collision risks and near-misses that are more likely to occur in smaller gaps, thereby enhancing pedestrian safety. Moreover, eHMIs contribute to an increase in pedestrian crossing probability in larger gaps, thus improving crossing efficiency.

Under partial penetration conditions, our study found that pedestrians were less likely to cross the street when encountering AVs equipped with eHMI. This finding may be attributed to concerns raised by53, which suggested that pedestrians’ unfamiliarity with the meaning of such signals could turn crossing decisions into an analytical process rather than an intuitive one. In the partial penetration condition, where AVs with eHMI and TVs without eHMI appear randomly, pedestrians have to constantly switch between different strategies for AVs and TVs. For TVs without eHMI, pedestrians might follow their traditional crossing manner and easily make crossing decisions. However, for AVs with eHMI, pedestrians may need to further analyze the additional eHMI, which adds complexity to their decision-making process. Due to partial penetration condition potentially impairing pedestrians’ decision-making, this highlights the importance of strategic planning during the transitional deployment phase of eHMIs. For example, public education campaigns on the meanings of eHMIs54, the development of standardized eHMI39, and the consideration of whether AVs should be restricted to segregated lanes to mitigate the potential adverse effects of partial penetration condition23.

Conclusion

In conclusion, full eHMI penetration resulted in significantly higher SA and lower MW compared to partial penetration condition. When compared to zero penetration, full eHMI penetration enhanced pedestrians’ sensitivity to gap size, indicating that as the gap size increased, the probability of pedestrians crossing increased more sharply. Notably, this effect occurred without significantly altering MW or SA. In contrast, partial penetration condition led to pedestrians crossing less frequently when encountering vehicles equipped with eHMI and was associated with significantly higher MW and lower SA. These findings from both behavioral and cognitive perspectives suggest that risk-warning eHMIs enhance pedestrian–AV interactions effectively under full penetration conditions. However, partial penetration may impair pedestrians’ decision-making and cognitive performance, highlighting the importance of strategic planning during the transitional deployment phase of eHMIs. For example, public education campaigns on the meanings of eHMI, the development of regulatory frameworks for eHMI standards, and the consideration of whether AVs should be restricted to segregated lanes to mitigate the potential adverse effects of partial penetration condition are all crucial. Future research could explore these aspects to investigate how human road users adapt to eHMI partial penetration conditions.

Limitations

Although this paper presents some intriguing findings, there are also some limitations that need to be acknowledged. (1) The limitation in the participant sample may impact the generalizability of the findings across different traffic cultures and demographic groups. Future research should recruit a more diverse participant pool to enhance the robustness of the results, for example, by including both younger and older pedestrians55. (2) Due to the limited availability of real AVs, the risks associated with pedestrian-AV interactions, and the practical challenges of manipulating AV platoons in real-world settings, this experiment employed a video-based simulation paradigm. Although meaningful conclusions and insights were drawn, this experimental paradigm may lower pedestrians’ risk perception, thus affecting their information perception and decision-making process. Therefore, the results of this study need to be validated in future real-world studies. (3) This study selected a relatively abstract eHMI modality in the form of light. Although abstract eHMI (light-based eHMI that employs traffic metaphors or signals to communicate intuitively through a non-concrete, open-to-interpretation interface5) has been shown to enable pedestrians to comprehend its meaning, particularly after multiple encounters56. However, whether other forms of eHMI, such as text-based, anthropomorphized, or symbolic eHMI, can improve the suboptimal performance of abstract eHMI at partial penetration condition remains to be further investigated. (4) Another limitation is that participants may occasionally miss some deviant stimuli or miscount regular stimuli as deviants in the oddball counting task, which results in an overall count that does not accurately reflect whether the participant correctly captured each deviant stimulus. Future experiments could address this by requiring participants to press a button in response to each deviant stimulus to obtain more precise reaction data.
