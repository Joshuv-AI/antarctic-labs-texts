Endoscopic contouring of posterior nasal aperture in children clinical efficacy of multiplanar stenotic OSA: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Haiyan Deng, Wanting Li, Yanjiao Xu, Yufeng Guo, Xiaohui Wu, Xingqiang Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyze clinical data from 112 pediatric patients with adenoid hypertrophy complicated by posterior septal hypertrophy, inferior turbinate hypertrophy, and eustachian tube hypertrophy, and to evaluate the efficacy and safety of endoscopic posterior nasal aperture contouring surgery for Obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS). MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 120 children (ages 4–12 years) diagnosed with multiplanar stenotic OSAHS who underwent endoscopic posterior nasal aperture contouring using radiofrequency ablation between January 2020 and June 2024. Postoperative outcomes were assessed over a 12-month follow-up period using portable sleep monitoring (OAHI index), electronic nasopharyngoscopy, the OSA-18 quality of life questionnaire, and complication rates.ResultsThe mean OAHI index significantly decreased from 12.7 ± 6.2 events/hour preoperatively to 2.1 ± 1.5 events/hour within 1–3 months post-surgery (P < 0.001). At 12 months, all 112 patients maintained stable OAHI levels averaging 1.5 ± 1.2 events/hour. The residual OSAHS rate was 24.1%, and the overall treatment efficacy rate reached 100%.Endoscopic examination revealed a significant enlargement of the posterior nasal aperture, with 88.4% of patients improving from preoperative grade III–IV to grade I (88.4%) or grade II (11.6%) openness. No severe complications, such as posterior nasal adhesion, eustachian tube dysfunction, or postoperative hemorrhage, were observed during follow-up.ConclusionEndoscopic contouring of the posterior nasal aperture, combined with simultaneous intervention of multiplanar anatomical structures, significantly and durably improves airway obstruction in children with multiplanar stenotic OSAHS. This procedure demonstrates a strong safety profile with a low risk of residual OSAHS, represents a promising therapeutic option for this OSAHS subtype.Keywords: Posterior nasal aperture complex, Obstructive sleep apnea in children, Multilevel stenosis, Contouring

What is known

The limitations of standard adenoidectomy/adenotonsillectomy and the existence of multi-level obstruction in some pediatric OSA cases.

What is new

The proposal of the “posterior choanal complex” concept and the preliminary efficacy and safety of the contouring surgery.

What this study adds

An alternative treatment strategy for pediatric OSA with multi-level choanal stenosis, with preliminary comparison to existing literature.

Obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome(OSAHS) in children is a common otolaryngological disorder, with an incidence rate ranging from 1.2% to 5.7% [1–4]. It is primarily caused by upper airway obstruction due to adenoid and tonsil hypertrophy. Currently, low-temperature plasma radiofrequency ablation has become the mainstream surgical treatment for pediatric OSA, owing to its minimally invasive nature and reduced intraoperative bleeding. However, clinical studies indicate that approximately 20%–72% of children continue to experience persistent snoring and hypopnea symptoms after adenoidectomy alone or in combination with tonsillectomy, suggesting that pediatric OSAHS may involve multi-level upper airway stenosis beyond adenoid and tonsil obstruction [5, 6]. In recent years, Drug-Induced Sleep Endoscopy (DISE) has been increasingly utilized to dynamically assess sites of upper airway obstruction and to guide surgical interventions [7–9]. After excluding stenosis in areas such as the lingual root and epiglottis, researchers identified the root cause as insufficient treatment of multi-level obstruction within the retronasal foramen region [10, 11]. Besides adenoids, thickened mucosa at the posterior end of the nasal septum, hypertrophy of the inferior turbinate, and enlargement of the Eustachian tube cricoid collectively contribute to a “nasopharyngeal isthmus stenosis,” which may limit the efficacy of traditional single-target surgical approaches.

Current research worldwide primarily focuses on treating isolated structures such as the adenoids or combined tonsillar hypertrophy, while systematic investigations into multi-structural coordinated interventions within the retronasal foramen region remain limited. Based on clinical observations and anatomical considerations, this study introduces the concept of the “retronasal foramen complex” (Fig. 1), defined as a functional unit comprising the adenoids, tympanic pedicle, posterior nasal septum, and posterior inferior turbinate. We performed contouring surgery targeting the retronasal foramen region in 112 children diagnosed with multiplanar OSAHS. The objective of this study is to preliminarily evaluate the efficacy and safety of this surgical approach, compare its outcomes with existing standard treatment protocols, and contribute further evidence for the surgical management of complex pediatric OSAHS.

Materials and methods

Clinical data

This retrospective study analyzed 112 pediatric patients with posterior nasal aperture multilevel stenotic OSAHS treated at our hospital between January 2020 and June 2024. The cohort consisted of 68 males (61.7%) and 44 females (39.3%), with a median age of 6.2 years (range: 4–12 years) and a disease duration of 18 months (range: 8–36 months). Inclusion criteria were as follows: ① Confirmed moderate-to-severe OSAHS (obstructive apnea-hypopnea index [OAHI] > 5 episodes/hour) via portable sleep monitoring (Philips Alice Night One); ② Intraoperative confirmation of two or more lesions in the posterior nasal aperture region through electronic nasopharyngoscopy, including adenoid hypertrophy (≥ 70% posterior nasal obstruction), posterior septal mucosal hypertrophy (bilateral hyperplasia obstructing posterior nasal passages), posterior inferior turbinate hypertrophy (≥ 50% obstruction), and eustachian tube cartilage hypertrophy; ③ Failure of conservative treatment (nasal corticosteroids combined with antihistamines) for at least 3 months. Exclusion criteria included: ① Craniofacial deformities (e.g., Pierre-Robin syndrome); ② Neuromuscular disorders or coagulation dysfunction; ③ History of previous nasopharyngeal surgery. Clinical manifestations comprised: ① Nighttime symptoms such as persistent snoring (100%), apnea (85%), and disturbed sleep (92%);② Daytime symptoms including attention deficits (76%), hypersomnia (68%), and learning disabilities (45%). Physical examination findings included adenoid facies (65%), mouth breathing (88%), and a high-arched hard palate (53%). Ancillary tests revealed: Sleep monitoring results showed a mean OAHI of 12.7 ± 6.2 episodes/hour, with a minimum blood oxygen saturation (SpO₂) of 82.3 ± 5.6%; Electronic nasopharyngoscopy confirmed grade III–IV posterior nasal obstruction in all patients; Oral and pharyngeal examination indicated that 75.0% of patients had grade II–IV tonsillar hypertrophy; Otoscopic examination detected unilateral or bilateral secretory otitis media in 25.9% of cases.

Surgical approach

All pediatric patients underwent endoscopic posterior nasal aperture contouring using plasma radiofrequency ablation under general anesthesia. The procedure utilized the Jiangsu Bangshi Plasma Surgery System (model ASR600) with an AC401 probe. Surgical targets included the adenoids (100% of cases), eustachian tube crista (90.2%), posterior nasal septum (67.9%), and inferior turbinate (36.6%). Concurrent tonsillectomy was performed in 75% of patients presenting with tonsillar hypertrophy, while 25.9% underwent unilateral or bilateral tympanotomy and tube insertion for secretory otitis media. The primary surgical goal was to maximize the cross-sectional area of the posterior nasal aperture while preserving anatomical integrity. The surgical techniques applied were as follows: ① Adenoid cryoablation: Performed under endoscopy at approximately 70 °C, with a probe depth of 1–2 mm, applying layered ablation to reduce tissue volume. ② Eustachian tube crista shaping: Longitudinal ablation was carried out until the crista was realigned with the nasopharyngeal sidewall (Figs. 2 and 3), maintaining a minimum 2 mm safety margin around the pharyngeal orifice. ③ Nasal septum resection: Using 70° endoscopy, hypertrophic mucosa at the posterior septum was identified (Fig. 4), and precise submucosal ablation was performed to preserve the perichondrium and maintain alignment with the sphenoid body (Fig. 5). ④ Inferior turbinate resection: Targeted ablation focused on the most hypertrophic regions (Fig. 6), with central localized ablation that preserved the integrity of the posterior nasal aperture mucosa (Fig. 7).To minimize bleeding risks, the ablation power was carefully adjusted to level 5 throughout the procedure.

Efficacy and safety assessment

Sleep parameters

OAHI index and LSpO₂ (preoperative, 1–3 months postoperative, 6–12 months postoperative).

Endoscopic evaluation

The degree of posterior nasal aperture obstruction was graded on a scale from I to IV based on the percentage of nasal obstruction observed: Grade I: 0%–25% obstruction Grade II: 26%–50% obstruction Grade III: 51%–75% obstruction Grade IV: 76%–100% obstruction.

Quality of life

The OSA-18 questionnaire, administered to primary caregivers preoperatively and postoperatively, evaluates five domains: sleep disturbances, physical symptoms, emotional distress, daytime functioning, and caregiver impact. The scale consists of 18 items, each rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = never, 7 = always), with total scores ranging from 18 to 126. Higher scores indicate greater impairment in quality of life.

Complications

Postoperative complications, including bleeding, nasal adhesions, eustachian tube dysfunction, and recurrence, were systematically recorded. Patients were monitored in the intensive care unit for a minimum of two hours immediately following surgery. Throughout hospitalization and subsequent follow-up visits, the attending physician conducted regular clinical assessments and physical examinations to detect and manage any complications.

Definition of “efficiency”

Clinical efficacy is determined based on monitoring results within 6–12 months after surgery. OAHI < 1 episode/h indicates recovery; 1 ≤ OAHI < 5 episodes/h with a reduction of ≥ 50% compared to pre-surgery is considered effective. If 1 ≤ OAHI < 5 episodes/h but the reduction is < 50%, or if OAHI ≥ 5 episodes/h, it is considered ineffective. In this study, “efficacy” is defined as the proportion of children who meet the criteria for “recovery” or “effective” [1].

Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 software was used for data analysis. Measurement data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. For comparisons between preoperative and postoperative values at 1–3 months, paired-sample t-tests were conducted, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. For comparisons at multiple postoperative time points, repeated-measures analysis of variance was used; if significance was detected, the Bonferroni method was applied for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Improvement in sleep monitoring parameters

During the first 3 months postoperatively, the OAHI decreased significantly from a preoperative mean of 12.7 ± 6.2 episodes/hour to 2.1 ± 1.5 episodes/hour (mean difference: −10.6; 95% CI: −11.8 to − 9.4; P < 0.001). Concurrently, the minimum blood oxygen saturation (LSpO₂) improved from 82.3 ± 5.6% preoperatively to 94.5 ± 3.2% (mean difference: 12.2; 95% CI: 10.9 to 13.5; P < 0.001). All 112 pediatric patients completed at least 12 months of follow-up. Repeated measures ANOVA conducted on OAHI and LSpO₂ values at two postoperative time points (1–3 months, 6–12 months) revealed significant time effects (OAHI: F(2, 222) = 25.83, P < 0.001; LSpO₂: F(2, 222) = 18.95, P < 0.001). Post-hoc analysis with Bonferroni correction showed no statistically significant differences between the 1–3 month and 6–12 month follow-ups for both OAHI (P = 0.104) and LSpO₂ (P = 0.255), indicating stable and sustained therapeutic outcomes (see Table 1). According to the study’s efficacy criteria, defined as achieving an OAHI < 5 episodes/hour combined with a ≥ 50% reduction from preoperative levels, 6–12 months after the surgery, 85 cases (75.9%) achieved “cure”, and 27 cases (24.1%) showed “effective” results. The therapeutic effect remained stable, with a total effectiveness rate of 100% (112/112).

Posterior nasal aperture

Preoperative electronic nasopharyngoscopy revealed that all pediatric patients exhibited grade III–IV posterior nasal obstruction. Follow-up examinations conducted 1–3 months postoperatively demonstrated complete detachment of the nasopharyngeal mucous membrane, with posterior nasal aperture improvement to grade I in 88.4% of patients and grade II in 11.6% (Figs. 8). Long-term follow-up confirmed the absence of postoperative adhesions or stenosis in the posterior nasal passages.

As shown in Table 2, the total OSA-18 score and all its subdomains demonstrated significant postoperative improvement. Safety analysis

No serious complications, including postoperative bleeding, symptomatic nasal adhesions, Eustachian tube dysfunction, or disease recurrence requiring surgical intervention, were observed during the follow-up period. Mild postoperative pain and temporary increased nasal discharge will subside on their own within 1–2 weeks after the surgery.

Discussion

Pediatric OSAHS is a significant condition in otolaryngology, with an incidence rate ranging from 1.2% to 5.7% in children. Nasal posterior orifice multilayer stenosis, a distinct subtype of OSAHS, presents substantial treatment challenges due to its frequent association with multilayer obstructions, including adenoid hypertrophy, posterior septal hypertrophy, posterior inferior turbinate hypertrophy, and hypertrophy of the eustachian tube crista. While traditional surgical interventions such as simple adenoidectomy or combined tonsillectomy may alleviate some symptoms, approximately 20% to 72% of pediatric patients continue to experience persistent snoring and hypopnea postoperatively, underscoring the limitations of single-target surgeries for multilayer stenosis. The advent of DISE has enabled researchers to identify persistent nasal and nasopharyngeal stenosis or collapse as common obstructive factors in children with ongoing symptoms after adenoidectomy and tonsillectomy [12]. Failure to adequately address these structural abnormalities can compromise the effectiveness of conventional surgeries. Existing studies demonstrate that simultaneous bilateral radiofrequency ablation targeting hypertrophic inferior turbinates and adenoids yields excellent outcomes [13]. Furthermore, the “Technical Standards for Standardized Treatment of Pediatric Tonsil and Adenoid Hypothermia Radiofrequency Ablation” (2025) recommend secondary surgical intervention for pediatric patients with persistent symptoms caused by eustachian tube hypertrophy leading to posterior nasal obstruction after adenoidectomy [10]. These clinical guidelines and empirical findings underscore the critical importance of comprehensive management for multilayer stenosis in the nasal posterior orifice region.

The present findings indicate that endoscopic choanal contouring surgery significantly improves sleep-related respiratory parameters in pediatric patients. Compared with conventional adenotonsillectomy alone, this technique demonstrates superior efficacy in reducing postoperative residual obstructive sleep apnea-hypopnea syndrome (OSAHS). In our cohort, the mean obstructive apnea-hypopnea index (OAHI) at 1–3 months postoperatively decreased from 12.7 ± 6.2 events·h-1 preoperatively to 2.1 ± 1.5 events·h-1 (P < 0.001). All children met the predefined criteria for clinical response, defined as an OAHI < 5 events· h-1 and a ≥ 50% reduction from baseline. When postoperative residual OSAHS was defined as an OAHI ≥ 1 event·h-1, a proportion of patients in this cohort exhibited mild residual disease; however, the incidence (27/112) was substantially lower than that reported for conventional surgical approaches in the literature. For instance, a review by Xu Zhifei et al. reported residual OSAHS rates ranging from 21.6% to 49.0% following traditional adenotonsillectomy [14], while a retrospective study by Zhang Xiaolong et al. documented a postoperative symptom persistence rate of 34.87% [15]. Furthermore, a European multicenter study demonstrated that up to 62.8% of children continued to experience snoring and abnormal sleep monitoring findings after surgery [16]. In the present study, although mild residual disease was observed during the early postoperative period, the OAHI remained stable at 1.5 ± 1.2 events· h-1 at 6–12 months postoperatively. Repeated-measures analysis of variance revealed a significant main effect of time (OAHI: F(2, 222) = 25.83, P < 0.001), whereas post hoc comparisons showed no significant differences among postoperative time points (P > 0.05), indicating sustained therapeutic efficacy over the follow-up period.

Conventional adenotonsillectomy primarily targets obstruction at two anatomical levels, the adenoids and the palatine tonsils. However, in children presenting with concomitant multilevel narrowing, such as posterior septal thickening, posterior inferior turbinate hypertrophy, or enlargement of the torus tubarius, the likelihood of residual OSAHS following surgery is significantly increased. Residual disease may result from several factors [14]: (1) untreated stenotic segments within the nasal cavity and nasopharynx; (2) comorbid conditions, including obesity and asthma, that adversely Influence airway mechanics; and (3) postoperative regrowth of lymphoid tissue or scar formation. In the present study, the contouring procedure incorporated simultaneous management of the adenoids, posterior nasal septum, posterior inferior turbinates, and torus tubarius, thereby facilitating comprehensive remodeling and volumetric reduction of the multilevel anatomical framework of the “choanal complex.” This integrated strategy may provide more complete resolution of functional stenosis within this region. Furthermore, intraoperative submucosal ablation assisted by low-temperature plasma technology enabled precise tissue reduction while preserving mucosal integrity and Eustachian tube function to the greatest extent possible. This approach may decrease the risk of postoperative adhesions and restenosis. Collectively, these characteristics represent distinct advantages of this technique over conventional surgical approaches in terms of both anatomical reconstruction and functional preservation.

Notably, the concept of the “choanal complex” and the corresponding contouring surgical strategy proposed in this study are consistent with the principles outlined in the most recent international expert consensus. The consensus recommends that, in children with persistent obstructive sleep apnea following adenotonsillectomy, surgical management should not be limited to the adenoids and tonsils but should instead adopt a comprehensive approach that systematically evaluates and addresses additional sites of airway obstruction, including the nasal cavity. In our cohort, the contouring strategy involved simultaneous, multilevel interventions (targeting two or more anatomical sites), including the adenoids, posterior nasal septum, posterior inferior turbinates, and torus tubarius. This integrated approach was designed to maximize enlargement of the posterior choanal cross-sectional area while maintaining anatomical integrity and preserving physiological function.

However, this study has several limitations. First, its single-center retrospective design and lack of a control group limit the ability to definitively attribute therapeutic effects to the contouring surgery and may introduce selection bias. Prospective randomized controlled trials comparing this approach with standard adenoidectomy are needed to clarify its additional benefits. Second, obstruction evaluation was based on endoscopic examination in awake patients rather than the gold standard of DISE, potentially leading to underassessment of dynamic airway collapse at certain levels [12]. Moreover, objective measurements of nasal resistance were not obtained, and the follow-up duration was insufficient to evaluate long-term impacts on maxillofacial development [17, 18]. Despite these limitations, the findings preliminarily suggest that posterior nasal aperture contouring surgery is a feasible and effective treatment for carefully selected pediatric patients with multiplanar stenosis of the postnasal foramen complex and OSA. The precise therapeutic advantages and long-term outcomes warrant validation through future high-quality, large-scale studies.

Conclusion

Endoscopic contouring surgery of the posterior choanal region represents an effective therapeutic approach for OSAHS associated with multilevel stenosis of the choanal complex. In this retrospective study, we provide preliminary evidence demonstrating that this procedure can achieve a significant and sustained reduction in the obstructive apnea-hypopnea index (OAHI). Furthermore, the postoperative incidence of residual OSAHS was substantially lower than that reported for conventional surgical techniques, and these improvements were accompanied by enhanced quality of life and a favorable short-term safety profile. For pediatric patients with well-defined anatomical evidence of multilevel airway narrowing, this comprehensive surgical strategy may yield superior clinical outcomes compared with traditional single-site interventions. However, its potential advantages over standard surgical protocols warrant further validation through well-designed prospective controlled studies.
