HIF‐1 Targeting Intervention Renders Protection From Alzheimer's‐Like Pathology in a Humanized Mice Model of HIV Infection
Authors: Sudipta Ray, Mohit Kumar, Divya T. Chemparathy, Prasanta K. Dash, Susmita Sil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHIV‐associated neurocognitive disorders (HAND) affect 30%–50% of individuals living with HIV on combination antiretroviral therapy, with Alzheimer ’s‐like pathology as a potent comorbidity of HAND. Our previous studies have implicated hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 alpha (HIF‐1α) as a central regulator of HIV‐1 Tat‐mediated amyloid production in astrocytes, which are further released via astrocyte‐derived extracellular vesicles (ADEVs), inducing synaptodendritic injury and Alzheimer's‐like pathology in naive mice. Based on this premise, we hypothesized that ADEVs carrying HIF‐1α‐targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA) would alleviate HIV‐1‐induced Alzheimer's‐like pathology and neurodegeneration in CD34+ NSG HIV‐infected humanized mice. Intranasally administered mCherry‐TSG101‐tagged ADEVs in mice demonstrated efficacy of brain delivery, especially to the hippocampus and cortex. In CD34+ NSG mice infected with HIV‐1, intranasal delivery of HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEVs suppressed HIF‐1α, reduced amyloid precursor protein (APP), AβmoC64, Aβ fibrils, and hyperphosphorylated tau (pTau), dampened glial activation as indicated by reduced GFAP and IBA1 expression, and partially restored synaptic proteins, which were dysregulated due to HIV‐1 infection. Trends of improvement were also observed in behavioural deficits in spatial memory, anxiety‐like behaviour, and sensorimotor gating induced by HIV‐1. These findings position HIF‐1α as a pivotal mediator of HIV‐associated Alzheimer's‐like pathology and neurodegeneration in the CD34+ NSG mice and underscore the promising role of ADEV‐mediated HIF‐1α siRNA delivery as a non‐invasive therapeutic strategy for HAND.

Introduction

Although the widespread use of cART has reduced the HIV‐1 viremia (Jean et al. 2019) and incidence of severe forms such as HIV‐associated dementia (HAD), milder forms of HAND—a spectrum of cognitive impairments—continue to persist in individuals living with HIV (Gannon et al. 2011; Elendu et al. 2023; Bandera et al. 2019; Nightingale et al. 2023; Saylor et al. 2016). Recent studies indicate that the global prevalence of HAND is approximately 50.41%, with regional variations and population demographics (Zenebe et al. 2022). In the United States, the prevalence of HAND among people living with HIV is estimated to be between 19% and 52% (Zenebe et al. 2022). A 2020 meta‐analysis found that the overall prevalence of HAND was 42.6%. This included 23.5% asymptomatic neurocognitive impairment, 13.3% mild neurocognitive disorder, and 5.0% HIV‐associated dementia (Wang et al. 2020). A 2023 meta‐analysis of 49 studies found a pooled prevalence of HIV/AIDS‐associated neurocognitive impairment to be 46.34% (Mekuriaw et al. 2023).

Several studies indicate that individuals living with HIV are at heightened risk for neurocognitive disorders, particularly HAND, which share key pathological features with Alzheimer's disease (AD), notably amyloid‐beta (Aβ) accumulation (Marino et al. 2020; Aksenov et al. 2010; Cohen et al. 2017; Turner et al. 2016), although the precise relationship remains complex and, at times, contradictory. Both conditions exhibit hallmark features such as amyloid‐beta (Aβ) deposition, hyperphosphorylated tau protein (pTau), synaptic loss, and neuroinflammation (Casagrande et al. 2022; Hellmuth et al. 2018; Stern et al. 2018). Recent epidemiological studies have reported that PLWH aged ≥70 have a 26%–44% prevalence of AD‐like cognitive deficits (Yu et al. 2023) and up to a 58% increased risk of dementia by age 50+ (Yu et al. 2023; Lam et al. 2021). Additionally, PLWH also show a 1.5–2.5‐fold higher AD incidence compared to age‐matched HIV‐negative populations (Yu et al. 2023; Sundermann et al. 2023; Umlauf et al. 2019; Lam et al. 2022), suggesting a potentially heightened vulnerability to neurodegeneration, particularly in the context of HAND. Abundant intracellular Aβ accumulation has been observed in HIV patients aged 38–60 years compared to age‐matched controls (Achim et al. 2009). In a middle‐aged cohort with a mean age of 51.4 ± 0.9 years, 58% exhibited neuronal ptau and 29% showed Aβ plaque accumulation (Gonzalez et al. 2023). Additionally, in individuals aged 80 years and older living with HIV, the prevalence of AD and related dementias (ADRD) increased from 26.2% to 33.3% among males and from 31.4% to 44.1% among females (Yu et al. 2023). This persistence may be attributed to chronic inflammation (McArthur and Johnson 2020), ongoing immune activation (Siddiqui et al. 2022) and potential neurotoxic effects of both HIV (Siddiqui et al. 2022) and certain antiretroviral drugs (Elendu et al. 2023; Ambrosius et al. 2019). Aβ accumulation, which naturally increases with ageing, is accelerated in the brain by HIV‐1 infection, correlating with viral loads and the onset of HAND (Chai et al. 2017). Supporting the role for HIV in neurodegeneration, Australian Imaging, Biomarkers and Lifestyle (AIBL) cohort data showed comparable Aβ levels in HAND and AD patients (Howdle et al. 2020). In deceased HIV‐1 seropositive individuals with HAND, Aβ accumulation was observed within neurones in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and hippocampal dentate gyrus, but no amyloid plaques were found in these regions (Li et al. 2022). Extracellular amyloid deposition was first observed in HIV‐positive individuals prior to the advent of cART (Esiri et al. 1998; Green et al. 2005). While ART reduces severe neurocognitive disorders, it does not fully mitigate cognitive risk. Neuropathological studies on brains from PLWH revealed Aβ and pTau accumulation, especially among those on protease inhibitor‐based ART (Howdle et al. 2020; Fields et al. 2020). These features, for example, diffuse Aβ deposits and Alzheimer's‐like changes in the brains of PLWH, persist in the post‐cART era (Stern et al. 2018; Achim et al. 2009; Gonzalez et al. 2023; Chai et al. 2017; Kim et al. 2013; Meehan et al. 2023; Xu and Ikezu 2009). Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of Aβ1‐42 and Aβ1‐40 were found to be elevated in HIV patients exposed to ART, with particularly higher levels observed in those receiving protease inhibitors (PIs) (Trunfio et al. 2022). Although cART is effective in suppressing systemic viral replication, it does not adequately address HIV‐associated neurodegeneration due to limited CNS penetrance, persistent neuroinflammation, and viral reservoirs within the brain. Therefore, adjunctive therapies are necessary to mitigate ongoing neurodegenerative processes in individuals receiving cART. In contrast, some studies have reported conflicting results, indicating a lack of correlation between CSF Aβ42 levels and fibrillary amyloid deposition measured by Pittsburgh Compound B PET imaging in HIV‐positive individuals with HAND (Trunfio et al. 2022; Clifford et al. 2009; Ances et al. 2012; Ances et al. 2010). 11C‐PiB PET exhibits high affinity and selectivity for β‐sheet‐rich fibrillar Aβ in compact plaques, and to a lesser extent, for diffused plaques (Umlauf et al. 2019; Howdle et al. 2020; Green et al. 2005; Neale et al. 2018; Cohen et al. 2012; Levine et al. 2017; Lockhart et al. 2007; Soontornniyomkij et al. 2019; Rempel and Pulliam 2005), however, HAND patients have been shown to demonstrate the presence of diffused non‐fibrillar Aß plaques or intracellular deposition (Umlauf et al. 2019; Howdle et al. 2020; Green et al. 2005; Soontornniyomkij et al. 2019; Rempel and Pulliam 2005), therefore, binding of PiB may be reduced in HAND patients.

Despite significant advances in cART, the accumulation of viral proteins like Tat, gp120, Nef, Vpr and persistence of viral reservoirs in the brain remains a significant challenge. In APP/PS1 mice, Tat was found to increase hippocampal Aβ42 and plaques via APP relocalization (Kim et al. 2013), while astrocytic Tat in PSAPP mice enhanced Aβ, tau phosphorylation, and neuronal loss (Giunta et al. 2009), especially with morphine co‐exposure (Ohene‐Nyako et al. 2021). Intracerebroventricular infusion of gp120 in mice induced amyloidogenesis, inflammation, and cognitive deficits (Lee et al. 2021); while transgenic‐gp120 mice showed CCR5‐linked neuroinflammation and synaptic damage (Yang et al. 2025). Furthermore, in EcoHIV‐infected mouse model, it has been reported that HIV‐1 infection can impact triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (TREM2) levels in the brain, which plays a crucial role in the activation of microglial cells, which are important for clearing debris, dead cells, and Aβ in the brain (Avalos et al. 2024). In aged HIV‐1 transgenic rats, abnormal intraneuronal β‐amyloid accumulation was observed in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus compared to control F344/N rats (Li et al. 2022). In hu‐PBL‐NSG mice, HIV elevated Aβ, tau phosphorylation, neurodegeneration, BBB disruption, and impaired Aβ transport (Bhargavan et al. 2021). The interactions of HIV with neuronal and glial cells, leading to increased Aβ production and impaired clearance, illuminate the complex inter‐relationship between neuropathogenesis of HAND & AD.

In vitro studies have also demonstrated that HIV‐1 viral proteins like Tat, gp120, and Nef disrupt Aβ and tau homeostasis mainly in neurones and endothelial cells, as well as in microglia and astrocytes. In neuroblastoma and glioma cell lines, recombinant HIV‐1 Tat directly interacts with APP, promoting its redistribution to lipid raft microdomains enriched in β‐ and γ‐secretases. This relocalization enhances β‐secretase cleavage of APP, significantly increasing Aβ42 production and β‐C‐terminal fragments, while reducing α‐C‐terminal fragments—indicating a shift towards the amyloidogenic pathway (Kim et al. 2013). HIV‐1 Tat has been reported to bind Aβ fibrils, promoting their aggregation into rigid, disorganized multifibrillar structures with enhanced β‐sheet content and mechanical strength. These Tat–Aβ complexes showed synergistic neurotoxicity in rat hippocampal neurones, thus offering a mechanistic link between HAND and AD pathology (Hategan et al. 2017). Also, Tat and gp120 significantly increased Aβ1‐42 secretion in primary rat hippocampal neurones (Aksenov et al. 2010). Tat exposure in primary rat hippocampal neurones was also found to cause endolysosomal enlargement and an elevated pH, indicating lysosomal dysfunction that precedes increased Aβ levels. Tat also promoted APP and Aβ accumulation within endolysosomes and elevated BACE1 levels and activity, implicating endolysosomal dysfunction as a key mechanism linking Tat to Aβ production (Chen et al. 2013). Additionally, in cultured neurones, gp120 also elevated transcription of beta‐site amyloid precursor protein cleaving enzyme 1 (BACE1) by inhibiting peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), resulting in enhanced APP expression and increased Aβ formation (Bae et al. 2014). Other than Aβ accumulation, in cultured mouse cortical neurones, the presence of HIV gp120 has been reported to induce tau hyperphosphorylation and promote tau release into the extracellular space, another key feature of AD‐related neurodegeneration (Sathler et al. 2022). Additionally, HIV‐1 Tat significantly impaired Aβ1‐42 phagocytosis by cultured murine microglia (Giunta et al. 2008), as well as disrupted ApoE3‐mediated Aβ uptake, by interfering with LRP signalling (Giunta et al. 2008). Besides Tat and gp120, exosomes containing HIV‐1 Nef protein and mRNA from individuals with HIV‐associated dementia (HAD) were shown to induce Aβ1‐42 production and secretion in SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma cells. The uptake of these exosomes led to intracellular Nef expression, suggesting an active mechanism of intercellular communication that contributes to amyloid pathology (Khan et al. 2016).

In addition to HIV‐viral proteins, HIV‐1 infection was reported to directly increase endothelial Aβ production and accumulation (András et al. 2010). Other than endothelial cells, primary human astrocytes in culture, infected with HIV‐1, also displayed elevated extracellular Aβ levels compared to uninfected controls (Martínez‐Bonet et al. 2019). HIV‐1 has also been shown to induce Aβ fibril accumulation, which subsequently enhances HIV‐1 entry and replication in T cells, macrophages and microglia; with Aβ additionally facilitating virion attachment and membrane fusion, pointing to a potential role of amyloid‐mediated viral amplification in driving HIV‐associated neurodegenerative pathology (Olari et al. 2025). Additional reports suggest that HIV‐1 infection may lead to early accumulation of Aβ due to both enhanced production and reduced degradation of Aβ, as a result of the dysfunction of mononuclear phagocytes (macrophages and microglia), key players in Aβ clearance, that are actively infected by HIV‐1 (Lan et al. 2011).

The collective findings from diverse human, animal models, and in vitro studies provide compelling evidence that HIV‐1 and HIV‐1 proteins drive Alzheimer’s‐like pathology via several intersecting biological pathways, including Aβ accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, dysregulated Aβ clearance, synaptic injury, neuroinflammation, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction. These effects are mediated not only by direct neuronal impact but also through astrocytes, microglia, and extracellular vesicles. The observed neuropathological changes, which closely resemble those seen in AD, occur even in the absence of active viral replication. Although cART is effective in suppressing systemic viral replication, it does not adequately address HIV‐associated neurodegeneration due to limited CNS penetrance, persistent neuroinflammation, and viral reservoirs within the brain. Even with effective viral suppression with ART, Tat protein promotes amyloid generation by increasing BACE1 activity and inhibiting Aβ degradation, while ART, particularly protease inhibitors (PIs), increases intracellular Aβ and tau accumulation (Howdle et al. 2020; Fields et al. 2020), impairs microglial Aβ clearance promoting amyloidosis (Giunta et al. 2011), upregulates BACE1‐mediated APP processing, disrupts autophagy and exacerbates neuroinflammation (Giunta et al. 2011; Chemparathy et al. 2024; Daily et al. 2006; Kaul and Lipton 2006; Gannon et al. 2017), thus contributing to neurodegeneration (Zhang et al. 2021; Nathan et al. 2005). Therefore, adjunctive therapies are necessary to mitigate ongoing neurodegenerative processes in individuals receiving cART and exhibiting Alzheimer'slike pathology.

HIV protein Tat also exacerbates hypoxia‐inducible factor 1‐alpha (HIF‐1α) dysregulation in HAND, promoting oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and Aβ aggregation, with astrocytes playing a central role (Hategan et al. 2017; Daily et al. 2006; Chen et al. 2012; Cho et al. 2017). Another HIV protein, gp120, increases oxidative stress and calcium influx in human primary astrocytes, which in turn activates an Nrf2‐dependent antioxidant defence (Reddy et al. 2012) and upregulates pro‐inflammatory chemokine (IL‐8) in astrocytes mediated by the NF‐κB pathway (Shah and Kumar 2010), which contributes to neuroinflammation (Chang et al. 2025). Our previous studies have demonstrated that exposure of human primary astrocytes (HPAs) to HIV‐1 Tat significantly upregulated the expression of amyloid precursor protein (APP), Aβ40, Aβ42 and the key amyloidogenic enzyme BACE1. Tat exposure induced accumulation and nuclear translocation of hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1α (HIF‐1α), which bound to the promoter region of the long noncoding RNA BACE1‐AS, enhancing its transcription. This activation of the HIF‐1α–BACE1‐AS–BACE1 mRNA axis facilitated the formation of BACE1‐AS/BACE1 mRNA duplexes, stabilizing BACE1 mRNA, increasing BACE1 protein levels, and consequently elevating Aβ42 production. Silencing either HIF‐1α or lncBACE1‐AS using siRNA significantly reduced BACE1 expression and Aβ generation. In the brains of SIV+ macaques, astrocytes in cortical and hippocampal regions exhibited elevated expression of APP and Aβ peptides, mirroring the changes seen in vitro. Additionally, increased expression of the toxic Aβ mOC64 conformer (Aβ42), along with APP, HIF‐1α, BACE1 and phosphorylated tau (p‐Tau), was observed in the frontal cortex and hippocampus of HIV‐infected individuals—including those who were asymptomatic, or with minor cognitive impairment (MCI), Alzheimer's disease (AD) or cerebral ischaemia—compared with uninfected controls. Notably, significant upregulation of Aβ42, reflected by a reduced Aβ40/42 ratio, was observed explicitly in HIV‐positive individuals with cognitive impairment, but not in asymptomatic cases. Furthermore, Aβ1–42 accumulation was predominantly localized to astrocytes in the frontal cortex and hippocampus, rather than forming the β‐sheet‐rich plaques characteristic of AD, supporting a model of astrocyte‐driven amyloidosis in HIV that is molecularly distinct yet pathologically overlapping with AD (Sil et al. 2020). In another study from our group, it was demonstrated that human primary astrocytes treated with HIV‐1 Tat triggered the release of ADEVs enriched in amyloidogenic proteins, including Aβ peptides. Rat hippocampal neurones exposed to Tat‐ADEVs showed amyloid accumulation within neurones, synaptodendritic injury, and electrophysiological deficits (including altered miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents reflecting disrupted synaptic transmission) (Chemparathy et al. 2024). This study also demonstrated that Tat‐ADEVs could induce Aß accumulation and neuroinflammatory milieu after intrahippocampal injection of these EVs in naïve mice brains, while HIF‐1α‐silenced Tat‐ADEVs demonstrated protective effects.

Based on this premise, our current study was undertaken to assess the therapeutic efficacy of HIF‐1α small‐interfering RNA (siRNA) in mitigating HAND‐related neurodegeneration using HIV+ CD34+ NSG humanized mice, as they replicate key features of human HIV infection, including immune dysfunction, establishment of viral reservoirs, and neuroinflammation, making them invaluable for examining HAND (Su et al. 2021; Dash et al. 2021; Gorantla et al. 2010; Gorantla et al. 2010). In this study, ADEVs loaded with HIF‐1α siRNA were used to downregulate HIF‐1α in the brains of HIV+ CD34+ NSG humanized mice. We followed the intranasal route for delivery, as it is non‐invasive and facilitates direct CNS targeting, bypassing systemic metabolism and reducing off‐target effects. Our findings underscore the central role of HIF‐1α in mediating amyloidogenic processing, glial activation, and synaptodendritic injury, key features underlying HAND. The targeted silencing of HIF‐1α via intranasal delivery of ADEVs loaded with HIF‐1α siRNA effectively mitigates HIV‐induced Alzheimer's‐like pathology. By targeting a specific molecular pathway—HIF‐1α signalling—with a highly specific delivery system, we aim to provide proof of concept for a targeted therapeutic strategy with minimal systemic toxicity. This EV‐based delivery strategy presents a non‐invasive, targeted and efficient approach to address HAND and holds translational potential for other neurodegenerative disorders characterized by Alzheimer's‐like pathology by optimizing EV‐based delivery systems. This will, in turn, pave the way for the development of innovative, targeted therapies to alleviate HAND‐related cognitive impairments and potentially for other neurodegenerative conditions.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

The following reagents and antibodies were used in this study: HIF‐1α siRNA (sc‐35561, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA), anti‐human HIV‐1 p24 (clone Kal‐1, M0857, Dako, 1:10), HIF‐1α (NB100‐449, Novus Biological Company, CO, USA), Aβ mOC64 (ab201060, Abcam, MA, USA), APP (ab15272, Abcam, MA, USA), TSG101 (ab125011, Abcam, MA, USA), Ago‐2 (ab186733, Abcam, MA, USA), Alix (ab275377, Abcam, MA, USA), Calnexin (ab133615, Abcam, MA, USA), GAD65 (ab239372, Abcam, MA, USA), Drebrin (ab178408, Abcam, MA, USA), Flotillin (ab133497, Abcam, MA, USA), CD63 (ab216130, Abcam, MA, USA), GFAP (G3893, Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA), Iba1 (ab17884, Abcam, MA, USA), Iba1 (ab283342, Abcam, MA, USA), Iba1 (019‐19741, FUJIFILM Wako, Osaka, Japan), p‐Tau 622 (ab76128, Abcam, MA, USA), MAP2 (ab5392, Abcam, MA, USA), Anti‐APP (6E10; NBP2‐62566, Novus Biologicals Co, USA), Anti‐Amyloid fibrils (OC) (57005, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) β‐Actin (A5316, Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA), GAPDH (ab8245, Abcam, MA, USA), Goat anti‐mouse (sc‐2005, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA), Goat anti‐rabbit (sc‐2004, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, TX, USA). For flow cytometric analysis, the antibody panel included FITC‐conjugated mouse anti‐human CD45 (555482, BD Biosciences, CA, USA), Alexa Fluor 700‐conjugated mouse anti‐human CD3 (557943, BD Biosciences, CA, USA), APC‐conjugated mouse anti‐human CD4 (555349, BD Biosciences, CA, USA), BV421‐conjugated mouse anti‐human CD8 (562428, BD Biosciences, CA, USA), PE‐Cy7‐conjugated mouse anti‐human CD14 (555398, BD Biosciences, CA, USA) and PE‐Cy5‐conjugated mouse anti‐human CD19 (555414, BD Biosciences, CA, USA).

Animal Study

CD34+ NOD. Cg‐Prkdcscid Il2rgtmLWjl/SzJ (NSG) mice were bought from Dr. Santhi Gorantla at the University of Nebraska Medical Centre (UNMC) for these studies (Gorantla et al. 2010; Gorantla et al. 2010; Dash et al. 2019). Following successful humanization by intrahepatic engraftment of human CD34+ haematopoietic stem cells (hu‐HSC, ∼ 20 weeks), the mice were subsequently infected with HIV‐1. The details of this procedure are discussed later. All mice were maintained with ad libitum access to food and water in a temperature‐ and humidity‐controlled environment under a standard 12‐h light/dark cycle. Two weeks after the HIV‐1‐infection, HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEVs were administered intranasally daily (10⁶–10⁸ EVs/100 µL or ∼3 µg EV protein per mouse) from Weeks 22 to 29 (∼2 months), followed by behavioural study and sacrifice for further experimentation.

Study Approvals

All experimental procedures involving laboratory animals were conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at the UNMC under protocol number 18‐030‐04‐FC. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use.

Flow Cytometry

Blood samples were collected into EDTA‐coated tubes via submandibular venipuncture at baseline (before infection), 2 and 8 weeks post‐HIV‐infection, which hereafter will be referred to as 0.5 and 2 months, respectively. Around 30–50 µL of whole blood was incubated with fluorescently conjugated antibodies specific to human CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8 and CD14 in the dark at room temperature (RT) for 30 min in accordance with established protocols (Dash et al. 2019). Furthermore, RBCs were lysed by treatment with 1X BD FACS lysing solution for 5 mins (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). To preserve cellular integrity, the samples were subsequently fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using the Attune NxT Acoustic Focusing Cytometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following a standardized workflow. Data acquisition and analysis of human‐cell‐specific populations were carried out using FlowJo v10.5 software (BD Pharmingen, San Diego, CA, USA).

HIV‐1 Infections and Viral Load

At 18–20 weeks of age, CD34+ NSG humanized mice were screened based on total human CD45⁺ cell engraftment, with inclusion criteria defined as >20% human CD45⁺ cells in peripheral blood. Eligible mice were randomly assigned to two experimental groups: uninfected controls and HIV‐1‐infected mice. Mice in the infected group received intraperitoneal (IP) inoculation with HIV‐1ADA at a dose of 1.5 × 10⁴ TCID50/mL. 0.5 months post‐infection, blood samples were collected from all mice into EDTA‐coated tubes. Samples were centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 8 min at 4 degrees to separate plasma from cellular components. A 35 µL aliquot of the plasma was diluted 1:10 by mixing with 315 µL of plasma diluent (AcroMetrix EDTA Plasma Dilution Matrix, Ref: S2284) to ensure accurate downstream viral load quantification. Viral RNA was quantified using the automated COBAS Ampliprep/TaqMan‐48 System V2.0 (Roche Molecular Diagnostics), following the manufacturer's protocol and as previously described (Dash et al. 2023). The assay was calibrated to detect viral RNA across a dynamic range of 200 to 10⁸ copies/mL, ensuring reliable quantification of plasma HIV‐1 RNA levels in all samples (Dash et al. 2019).

Cell Culture

The human astrocytoma cell line A172 (no. CRL‐1620; American Type Culture Collection [ATCC]) was cultured as previously described (Sil et al. 2018) in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium (DMEM; 10–013‐CV, Corning Cellgro, VA, USA) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated foetal bovine serum (FBS; S11550H, Bio‐Techne R&D Systems, MN, USA) and penicillin‐streptomycin (100 units/mL) (15140–122, Life Technologies, CA, USA) in a 5% CO2‐humidified incubator at 37°C. These cells were used for experiments for up to 25 passages. For experiments, the cells were plated at a density of 2 × 10⁶ cells in 150 mL culture flasks. Cultures were maintained until reaching approximately 70% confluency and then serum‐starved to synchronize the cell cycle, ensuring consistent and reproducible conditions for subsequent experiments, followed by the collection of the conditioned media & EV isolation.

mCherry Tagging of EVs

A172 cells were transfected with the mCherry‐TSG101 plasmid (Addgene plasmid #38318, Watertown, MA) to enable the production of mCherry‐tagged ADEVs. Following transfection, mCherry‐labelled ADEVs were isolated from the culture supernatant using previously validated and standardized protocols (Chemparathy et al. 2024). The purified EVs were subsequently administered intranasally to C57BL/6 mice as described later, and the brain was isolated after 3 days for subsequent analysis.

EV Isolation

EVs were isolated from the supernatant of A172 cell cultures using a differential centrifugation protocol, as detailed in prior studies (Chemparathy et al. 2024; Sil et al. 2021). Briefly, conditioned media from A172 cell cultures were collected and sequentially centrifuged to eliminate cellular debris. Initial centrifugation was performed at 300 × g for 10 min, followed by 2000 × g for 10 min at 4°C. Next, the supernatant underwent centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C, followed by filtration through a 0.22‐µm syringe filter to eliminate larger vesicles and particulates. ADEVs, including exosomes, were subsequently concentrated via ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 70 min at 4°C using a Beckman Ti32 rotor (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). At each centrifugation step, the supernatant was carefully collected for further processing, while the pellet was discarded, except at the final ultracentrifugation step, where the EV‐enriched pellet was retained. This pellet was subsequently resuspended in 1× filtered PBS and subjected to characterization to ensure purity (Figure S1).

Transfection of EVs with siRNA

EVs in the range of 10⁶ to 10⁸ particles were suspended in 300 µL of PBS. A total of 20 picomoles of HIF‐1α siRNA at an initial concentration was loaded using the Exo‐Fect siRNA Transfection Kit (EXFT200A‐1, System Biosciences, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, the siRNA was incubated with Exo‐Fect siRNA/miRNA Transfection Reagent at room temperature for 15 min to allow complex formation. Subsequently, the isolated ADEVs were added to this mixture and incubated at 37°C for 1 h to facilitate the incorporation of the siRNA into the ADEVs. Following incubation, the transfection mixture was transferred to a pre‐washed spin column included in the Exo‐Fect kit and gently rotated at room temperature for 10 min to bind and remove unincorporated siRNA and excess reagent. The column was then centrifuged at 1000 × g for 30 s, and the flow‐through containing the purified EVs (10⁶–10⁸ EVs/100 µL or ∼3 µg EV‐protein) loaded with siRNA cargo was collected and used for all downstream experiments.

Intranasal Delivery of EVs

Following confirmation of a viral load exceeding 10⁴ copies/mL, HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEVs in PBS were administered intranasally daily (10⁶–10⁸ EVs/100 µL or ∼3 µg EV protein per mouse, 20 picomoles) from Weeks 22 to 29. Briefly, mice were lightly anaesthetized in an anaesthesia chamber and positioned supine for each treatment. Intranasal delivery was performed using a small pipette, with alternating drops administered into each nasal cavity every 2 min over a total duration of 10 min per session.

Ex Vivo Brain Distribution of EVs

To assess the ex vivo brain distribution and delivery efficiency of the EVs, the brain was harvested, submerged in 70% alcohol overnight at 4°C and then in a 30% sucrose solution. After cryoprotection, each brain tissue was wrapped in aluminium foil and frozen by immersing in pre‐cooled isopentane (chilled in liquid nitrogen) until solid and stored at −80°C until sectioning. Cryosectioning was performed using a cryostat (Leica CM3050S) maintained at −20°C to −25°C. Tissue blocks were equilibrated in the cryostat chamber for 10–15 min prior to sectioning, embedded in Optimal Cutting Temperature (OCT) compound, and sections were cut at a thickness of 30 µm. These sections were collected in PBS. After washing three times in PBS, the sections from each brain were mounted on a Superfrost Plus microscope slide (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The slides were then air‐dried at room temperature and mounted with Prolong Gold antifade reagent containing DAPI (P36935, Invitrogen, CA, USA) (Gasperoni and Dworkin 2024). For the biodistribution analysis of mCherry‐TSG101‐labelled EVs, whole slide scanning was performed using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 Whole Slide Imaging System Z1 (Carl Zeiss, Thornwood, NY, USA) and visualized with ZEN Blue 3.5 Slidescan software AxioVs 40 software (v4.8.0.0; Carl Zeiss MicroImaging GmbH). However, the ZEN Blue software was used solely for visualization and anatomical orientation, not for quantification. To measure fluorescence intensity, we used ImageJ Launcher software (version 1.4.3.67, NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). From each animal (N = 3/group), 10 brain sections were selected for analysis. For each section, regional images were captured as screenshots from ZEN Blue 3.5 slide‐scanning software and imported into ImageJ. Using the “Measure” function under the “Analyze” tab in ImageJ, mean fluorescence intensity and area were quantified. Fluorescence values were normalized for area across sections to account for variability in tissue size, and the average of these values was reported as the mean fluorescence intensity per animal. This workflow ensured standardized and reproducible quantification across samples and regions.

Behavioral Analysis

Y‐Maze Test

The Y‐maze test was performed to assess spatial memory and exploratory behaviour using a modified protocol described earlier (Chemparathy et al. 2024). Each animal underwent one trial session followed by two test sessions in the Y‐maze, following the protocol described in our previous publication (Chemparathy et al. 2024) to assess spatial memory and exploratory behaviour. During the trial phase, each mouse was individually placed in the maze with one of the three arms (Arm C) closed and allowed to explore the remaining two arms (Arms A and B) freely for 15 min. At 1 h and again at 48 h post‐trial, the mice were reintroduced to the maze with all three arms open and allowed to explore freely. The previously closed arm (Arm C), now accessible during testing, was designated as the ‘novel’ arm. Animal behaviour was video recorded and analyzed using AnyMaze software (Version 7, Stoelting Co., IL, USA). The maze was cleaned thoroughly with 70% ethanol between sessions to eliminate residual olfactory cues.

Open Field Test

The open field test was conducted to evaluate locomotor activity and exploratory behaviour, following the protocol described earlier (Chemparathy et al. 2024). The mice were placed in a square open‐field chamber (50 × 50 × 38 cm) for a 45‐min session. Their movement trajectories were continuously recorded and analyzed using the AnyMaze video tracking system (Version 7, Stoelting Co., IL, USA). The arena was cleaned with 70% ethanol between sessions to remove residual olfactory cues and ensure consistency.

Elevated Plus Maze

EPM is designed to assess the anxiety‐like behaviour of rodents. Animals were placed in the central zone of the EPM apparatus (67 cm × 7 cm × 30 cm). EPM has two open arms and two arms enclosed by a wall. Mice prefer to stay in the closed arms, feeling safer there, but animals are driven by natural curiosity to explore unknown territory, therefore, a healthy animal often ventures out into the open arms. EPM test has been adapted from Chaves et al. 2022 with modifications. Mice could freely explore the apparatus for 15 min. The test was conducted in bright light. Different parameters of anxiety have been analyzed by AnyMaze software (Version 7, Stoelting Co., IL, USA).

Prepulse Inhibition (PPI)

PPI assessment was conducted for 2 months following HIV‐1 infection using a sound‐attenuated startle chamber (Kinder Scientific) controlled by StartleMonitor software as per Nielsen et al. 2017. Mice were acclimated to the testing room in their home cages for 1 h prior to the experiment. During testing, animals were restrained and allowed a 5‐min acclimation period in the chamber with continuous 70 dB background white noise. The session comprised 62 trials, beginning with six pulse‐alone trials (40 ms duration, 2 ms rise/fall time, 120 dB intensity), followed by pseudorandomly ordered trial types (pulse‐alone, prepulse–pulse and no‐stimulus) with inter‐trial intervals randomized between 11 and 30 s, and concluded with six additional pulse‐alone trials. Prepulse stimuli (4, 8 or 16 dB above background) were presented 30 ms before the pulse. Startle amplitude was derived from the initial six pulse‐alone trials. Short‐term habituation was quantified by normalizing the average of trials 5 and 6 to the initial response. Mean startle latency was calculated by taking the average of the latencies from all first six pulse‐alone trials. Sensorimotor gating, reflecting prepulse inhibition, was calculated as % PPI = (pulse‐alone response – prepulse + pulse response/pulse‐alone response) × 100%, using maximum startle amplitudes to determine attenuation of the startle reflex when preceded by a prepulse. Background noise remained constant at 70 dB throughout testing.

Western Blot

Western blot analysis was performed as reported earlier (Sil et al. 2021). In brief, protein was extracted from tissues of different brain regions (frontal cortex [FC], parietal cortex [PC], occipital cortex [OC], hippocampus [Hp], striatum [Str] and cerebellum [Cer]) using RIPA lysis buffer, followed by sonication and centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove debris. Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein were mixed with 5X bromophenol blue loading dye and boiled at 99°C for 15 min prior to electrophoresis. Proteins were resolved on 10%–15% SDS‐PAGE gels under reducing conditions and transferred onto pre‐activated polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore Sigma, MO, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat dry milk in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‐20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature to prevent nonspecific binding. Subsequently, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with goat anti‐mouse or goat anti‐rabbit IgG primary antibodies, specific to the target proteins, at optimized dilutions. After washing, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies diluted 1:5000 in TBST for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using SuperSignal chemiluminescent substrates (Pico, Dura or Femto; #34579, #37071, #34094, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) mixed in a 1:1 ratio, and signals were captured using a FluorChem‐R Imaging System (ProteinSimple, Bio‐Techne). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (v1.4.3.67, NIH, MD, USA) and normalized to β‐actin as an internal loading control.

Real‐Time qPCR

Total RNA was isolated (Kumar et al. 2024) from FC, PC, Hp and Str brain regions of humanized mice from the three experimental groups (HIV‐, HIV+ and HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA) using the Quick‐RNA MicroPrep Kit (R1055, Zymo Research Corporation, Irvine, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentrations were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA was reverse transcribed using the Verso cDNA Synthesis Kit (AB1453/B, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) as described earlier (Sil et al. 2020). qPCR was performed using TaqMan probes and primers purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA. Primers included HIF‐1α (Mm00468869_m1) and GAPDH (Mm99999915_g1). The qPCR reaction mix contained 5 µL of cDNA, 10 µL of TaqMan 2× Universal PCR Master Mix, 4 µL of nuclease‐free water, and 1 µL of the respective 20× TaqMan primer. mRNA expression levels were quantified by normalizing the mean CT values to the internal control, GAPDH, and relative fold changes were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Non‐template controls were included to confirm the specificity of amplification.

Immunofluorescence and Immunohistochemical Staining

Immunofluorescence was performed on paraformaldehyde‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded brain tissue sections (5 µm) baked on slides to investigate protein localization as reported earlier (Sil et al. 2020). Slides were deparaffinized in xylene (5–60 min), rehydrated through graded ethanol solutions (100%, 70% and 50%), and rinsed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS). Antigen retrieval was carried out by incubating sections in heated 0.01 M Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for 20 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 10 min. Sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X‐100 in 1X PBS for 10 min at room temperature. To block nonspecific antibody binding, tissues were incubated with 10% normal goat serum (NGS, S‐1000‐20, Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4°C using antibodies against AβmoC64, Aβ6E10, amyloid fibril, p‐Tau, GAD65, drebin, GFAP, Iba1, HIF‐1α and MAP2, diluted in 10% NGS in PBS. Slides were subsequently incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 488, 594 and 647; Invitrogen, CA, USA) diluted in PBS. After washing for 3 times in PBS, sections were mounted with Prolong Gold antifade reagent containing DAPI (P36935, Invitrogen, CA, USA) to counterstain nuclei.

For histochemical staining, formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded brain sections (5 µm) were deparaffinized in xylene (2 × 15 min) and rehydrated through graded ethanol (100%, 95%, 80%) to distilled water. Heat‐induced antigen retrieval was performed in Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for 15 min, followed by cooling to room temperature. Sections were rinsed in TTBS (Tris‐buffered saline with 0.05% Tween‐20) and blocked with 10% normal goat serum (NGS; S‐1000‐20, Vector Laboratories, CA, USA) for 1–2 h at room temperature. Primary antibody against Aβ6E10 diluted 1:100 in 10% NGS was applied overnight at 4°C. After TTBS washes (3 × 10 min), sections were incubated with an HRP‐conjugated polymer secondary antibody (cat. GTX83398, GeneTex) for 1 h at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was visualized using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB, cat. D4293, Millipore Sigma), counterstained with Mayer's haematoxylin (cat. 10015‐074, VWR) and coverslipped with Cytoseal 60 mounting medium (Epredia, cat. 8310‐4). Transmitted brightfield and fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss Axioscan 7 Whole Slide Imaging System (Carl Zeiss, Thornwood, NY, USA) and analyzed with ZEN Blue 3.4 Slidescan software (Carl Zeiss MicroImaging GmbH). Two different methods were applied for intensity and colocalization analyses. For intensity, from each animal, ten different fields were selected from the cortex and hippocampal region of the whole brain from the fluorescence images. Using ImageJ software (v1.4.3.67; NIH, MD, USA), the mean fluorescence intensity and area were quantified, and values were normalized to tissue area across sections to account for variability in section size. The average of these values was reported as the mean fluorescence intensity per animal. Next, colocalization studies and area quantification of both brightfield and fluorescence images were performed using HALO AI software (version 3; Indica Labs). The Area Quantification v2.4.3 module was used to measure the percentage area of AβmOC64‐, Aβ6E10‐ and Aβ fibril‐positive staining, while the Area Quantification FL v2.3.4 module was applied for quantification and colocalization analysis of AβmOC64, GFAP, IBA1 and MAP‐2 immunostaining.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance between two groups was performed using Student's t‐test, and among multiple experimental groups was assessed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Bonferroni's post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Correlational analysis was performed to determine the association between the measured parameters. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 10; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Distribution of mCherry‐Tagged ADEVs in the Brain

ADEVs were isolated from A172 culture media by differential ultracentrifugation and ZetaView Nanoparticle Tracking Analyzer was used to analyze the concentration and size distribution of the ADEVs. The ADEVs isolated at 100,000 × g had diameter in the range of 90–110 nm and the concentration was approximately 3.63 × 1010 EVs/mL. Additionally, these EVs were visualized using transmission electron microscopy, revealing their distinct morphology and subjected to western blot to confirm their purity and isolation efficacy through the presence of specific exosomal markers (Figure S1). These ADEVs were then used for subsequent experiments.

Several studies have indicated that HIV‐1 proteins, such as Tat and gp120, may contribute to amyloid formation and that HIV‐1 itself can enhance amyloid deposition in the brain which contributes to the Alzheimer's‐like pathology in the context of HAND (Sil et al. 2020; Jana et al. 2024; Tan et al. 2014). In our previous studies, it has been reported that HIF‐1α plays an important role in HIV‐1 Tat‐mediated astrocytic amyloid generation & these amyloids are secreted through ADEVs, leading to synaptodendritic impairment and the manifestation of Alzheimer's‐like pathology in naive mice (Sil et al. 2020). Hence, we wanted to assess the therapeutic efficacy of HIF‐1α siRNA loaded ADEVs in HIV‐1 mediated neurocognitive disorders. A172 cells were first transfected with a plasmid encoding the exosome marker TSG101 fused with mCherry (mCherry‐TSG101), followed by isolation of ADEVs from the conditioned media of transfected cells as discussed in our previous study (Chemparathy et al. 2024). Next, these ADEVs were delivered intranasally to wild‐type mice, and after 3 days of intranasal delivery, mice were sacrificed and cryosections were made from the frozen brains of each animal and mounted on slides for whole slide scanning (Figure 1A). Figure 1B demonstrated that intranasally delivered ADEVs were distributed in various brain regions, with prominent accumulation in the cortical & hippocampal region. There was a trend of increase in fluorescence intensity in the whole brain in mice administered with mCherry‐TSG101 tagged EVs compared to those administered untagged EVs. Interestingly, there was a significant increase in the fluorescence intensity (from mCherry‐TSG101 tagged EVs) compared to untagged EVs, in the cortex (p < 0.0001) and hippocampus (p < 0.0022) which indicated localization of mCherry‐TSG101 tagged ADEVs in these brain regions (Figure 1C–E). After confirming the distribution of ADEVs, HIF‐1α siRNA (20 picomole) was loaded into the ADEVs. The optimal concentration of HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEVs required to suppress HIF‐1α expression was first determined in cultured human astrocytes. Western blot analysis revealed that ADEVs loaded with 20 picomoles of HIF‐1α siRNA significantly reduced HIF‐1α protein levels in HPAs (Figure S2).

Effect of HIF‐1α siRNA Treatment on Viral Load and Immune Cell Counts in HIV‐1‐Infected Animals

Once we determined that intranasal administration is an effective route for brain delivery of ADEVs, these ADEVs were intranasally delivered in an experimental model of HIV‐1‐infected humanized mice [CD34+ NSG hu mice infected as detailed in the method section]. The mice were intranasally administered with 10⁶–10⁸ ADEVs/100 µL or ∼3 µg EV‐protein per mice/day loaded with 20 picomoles HIF‐1 siRNA daily, starting 2 weeks post‐infection and continued until the end of the study, as shown in Figure 2A. The human astrocytoma cell line A172 was selected for EV production based on prior studies demonstrating the functional utility of its EVs (Pan et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2018; Lang et al. 2017), particularly siRNA delivery via the intranasal route (Hu et al. 2018). Additionally, the high availability, scalability and experimental robustness of A172 cells make them a practical and reliable source for EV generation in translational research. Furthermore, the protein and RNA cargo composition of A172‐derived EVs has not yet been fully characterized, and EV cargo is known to influence the biological responses of recipient cells (Ma et al. 2019; You et al. 2019). To minimize potential variability arising from the intrinsic content of the EVs (which may occur across different batches of producing cells), all animals in the experimental group (HIV+ and HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group) received EVs derived from the same A172 batch, ensuring a consistent EV background across all conditions, and allowing the observed effects to be attributed predominantly to the delivered siRNA. We next sought to determine the effects of HIV‐1 infection and HIF‐1α siRNA treatment on different parameters‐ body weights, human immune markers (hCD45+, hCD4+ and hCD8+) and plasma viral load in the humanized mice. As demonstrated in Figure 2B, there was no change in body weights among the groups. Since HIV‐1 infection is known to decrease CD4+ T‐cells and increase CD8+ T‐cells (Assogba et al. 2024; Gubser et al. 2019), the levels of CD4+ & CD8+ cells in the blood were assessed. As demonstrated in Figure 2C and D, there was a decrease in CD4+ & increase in CD8+ cells in the HIV+ group, while HIV++HIF‐1siRNA group showed partial protection from these changes (CD45+ CD3+ cells represent T lymphocytes, including both CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytotoxic T cells). Next, we assessed the viral loads in these animals. As demonstrated in Figure 2E, the HIV+ group showed high plasma viral load, which increased over time; however, the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group did not show any further changes in the plasma viral load levels with time compared to the HIV+ group. Next, as shown in Figure 2F and G, HIV‐1 p24 (24 kDa) showed high expression in the Cortex, Hp, Str and cerebellum (Cer) of the HIV+ group; however, there was a significant downregulation (p < 0.05) in the HIV‐1 p24 levels in cortex, and a trend of downregulation was observed in the hippocampus, striatum and cerebellum brain regions of HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group compared to HIV+ group.

Cellular Localization of HIF‐1α in the Brain

After establishing that HIF‐1α siRNA treatment can reduce HIV‐1 p24 expression in the cortex, we next assessed the regional as well as cellular localization of HIF‐1α expression in the brain. The results demonstrated that HIV‐1‐infected mice (HIV+) exhibited elevated HIF‐1α expression in all four brain regions (Figure 3A), that is, FC, PC, Hp and Str, with the most significant change in mRNA levels being observed in the PC (p = 0.0308) and Hp (p = 0.0030). However, HIF‐1α siRNA treatment reduced HIF‐1α mRNA levels (Figure 3A) in all the four brain regions, with the most significant downregulation observed in the PC (p = 0.0224) & Hp (p = 0.0053). These results were further confirmed by immunofluorescence staining, which demonstrated significantly elevated levels of HIF‐1α expression in the hippocampus region of HIV+ mice (p < 0.0001) compared to the uninfected group (HIV−). The HIF‐1α siRNA treatment (HIV++HIF‐1siRNA) significantly downregulated HIF‐1α expression in the hippocampus compared to the HIV+ group (p = 0.0001) (Figure 3B,C). We next sought to determine the effect of HIV infection and HIF‐1α siRNA therapy on the cellular expression and localization of HIF‐1α in the hippocampus. As shown in Figure 3D–G, HIV‐1 infection significantly up‐regulated the percentage of colocalization of HIF‐1α within astrocytes (p = 0.0036) and neurones (p = 0.0007), compared to the HIV− control, indicating high expression of HIF‐1α in these CNS cells in the presence of HIV‐1. A trend of increased colocalization of HIF‐1α was also observed with microglia (Figure 3F). Significant downregulation was observed in the percentage of colocalization of HIF‐1α with astrocytes (p = 0.0027) and neurones (p = 0.0051) in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group, and a similar trend was also observed in the microglia. The single channels for HIF‐1α expression for all three groups (HIV‐, HIV+ and HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA) are represented in Figure S3.

HIF‐1α siRNA Treatment Modulated Cognitive Impairments in HIV‐1‐Infected Animals

Next, we sought to assess the HIV‐1‐associated cognitive deficits in HIV‐1‐infected humanized mice. Y‐maze was conducted at 0.5‐ and 2‐month post‐infection to evaluate short‐term (48 h after training) and long‐term (2 months after training) memory (Figure 4A–I). At 0.5 months post‐infection, HIV‐1‐infected mice exhibited significant cognitive and behavioural alterations compared to HIV− mice, including increased entries, reduced time spent (p = 0.0357), greater distance travelled and significantly prolonged latency to first entry (p = 0.0085) in familiar arms (A+B). In the novel arm (C), HIV+ mice showed increased entries, significantly more time spent (p = 0.0298), greater distance travelled and significantly reduced latency to first entry (p = 0.0267). Treatment with HIF‐1α siRNA in HIV+ mice reversed these impairments, showing fewer entries, reduced distance travelled and marginally increased time spent in arms A+B, alongside marginally decreased time spent in arm C. Additionally, the HIV+ group displayed an elevated novelty score compared to HIV‐mice, which was reduced in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group. By 2 months post‐infection, HIV+ mice exhibited directional trends indicative of memory disruption, with fewer entries, reduced time spent and decreased distance travelled in arms A+B, while arm C showed marginally increased entries and time spent, alongside elevated latency to first entry in arms A+B. In contrast, the HIV++HIF‐1siRNA group demonstrated further reductions in entries and distance travelled in both arms A+B and C, increased time spent in these arms and a lower trend in latency to first entry in arms A+B but a marginally higher trend in arm C. The increased novelty score in the HIV+ group at 2 months post‐infection was reversed in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group. These findings suggest that HIV‐1 infection disrupts short‐ and long‐term memory in these mice.

In parallel, an open field study was performed to assess the locomotor activity of these animals. As demonstrated in Figure 4J–Q, at 0.5 months post‐HIV‐1 infection, HIV+ mice exhibited pronounced locomotor deficits, including reduced mean speed, decreased mobility time, fewer mobile episodes and diminished rotational activity, marked by significantly fewer clockwise rotations (p = 0.0252) and a preference for anticlockwise rotations compared to HIV− mice. HIF‐1α siRNA treatment partially reversed these deficits, and significant restoration in mobility time (p = 0.0187) and anticlockwise rotations (p = 0.0278) was observed, alongside marginal improvements in mobile episodes and total rotations. By 2 months post‐HIV‐1 infection, HIV+ mice displayed persistent dysfunction, with declining trends in time mobile/immobile, elevated mobile/immobile episodes and sustained anticlockwise rotation preference, though mean speed, total rotations and clockwise rotations remained unchanged. In contrast, the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group demonstrated robust recovery, with elevated trends in mean speed, time mobile, total rotations (both clockwise and anticlockwise) and reduced immobility time and mobile/immobile episodes, suggesting restoration of exploratory behaviour and coordination.

Elevated plus maze test at 2 months post‐infection revealed anxiety‐like behaviour in HIV+ mice. As demonstrated in Figure 5A–L, HIV+ mice compared with HIV− mice exhibited marginally increased trend of entries and latency to first entry into open arms, alongside reduced average speed, though time spent, distance covered and mean visits in open arms remained unchanged. In closed arms, HIV+ mice showed a decreased trend of entries, time spent and average speed, but elevated latency to first entry and mean visits, though distance covered remained unchanged. In contrast, the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group demonstrated a partial reversal of these behaviours, with increased entries into open and closed arms (p = 0.0016), alongside decreased time spent in open arms and a significant reduction in closed‐arm time (p = 0.0241). In the same group (HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA), there was an increase in distance covered in both open and closed arms, while latency to first entry decreased in open arms and significantly declined in closed arms (p = 0.0266). Notably, mean visits to open arms did not show any change in HIV+ group when compared to the HIV− group but increased significantly (p = 0.0212) in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group. However, the number of mean visits in closed arms increased in the HIV+ as compared to the HIV− group, whereas there was a trend of reduction in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group. Additionally, as shown in Figure S4, time spent, distance covered and average speed in the centre are reduced, while time spent in the periphery was more in the HIV+ group in the open‐field, indicating anxiety‐like behaviour, while time spent in the centre and periphery was restored in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group. These results suggest that HIF‐1α siRNA treatment modulates HIV‐1‐induced anxiety‐like behaviour, promoting exploratory activity in open arms while normalizing closed‐arm avoidance, indicative of restored emotional regulation and reduced anxiety.

Furthermore, we examined the effects of HIV‐1 infection and HIF‐1α siRNA treatment on acoustic reactivity in 2 months post HIV infection using prepulse inhibition (PPI) behaviour test. As demonstrated in Figure 5M–P, at 2 months post‐HIV infection, acoustic startle reactivity to a 120 dB pulse remained unaltered in HIV+ compared to HIV− mice. However, HIF‐1α siRNA treatment significantly amplified startle magnitude in HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA mice (p = 0.0233), suggesting heightened acoustic sensitivity or neuromuscular hyperexcitability. Short‐term habituation to repetitive acoustic stimuli, quantified as the normalized ratio of late‐to‐initial startle responses, revealed an elevated habituation score in HIV+ mice compared to HIV− mice, indicative of accelerated desensitization. Notably, this trend persisted in HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA mice without further modulation. Mean startle latency progressively decreased in HIV+ mice compared to HIV− mice, with HIF‐1α siRNA exacerbating this decline, implying a hypersensitivity to abrupt auditory stimuli. Sensorimotor gating was assessed via prepulse inhibition (PPI) of the acoustic startle response. While HIV+ mice exhibited a trend towards increased PPI at 16 dB prepulse intensity (16 dB) compared to HIV− control, HIF‐1α siRNA treatment demonstrated a trend of amplified effect at 8 and 16 dB, suggesting improved sensory filtering and gating efficiency. These paradoxical increases in PPI, contrary to typical deficits in neuropsychiatric disorders, may reflect compensatory neural adaptations to HIV‐induced neuropathology and HIF‐1α siRNA‐mediated stabilization of prefrontal‐limbic circuitry.

HIF‐1α siRNA Treatment Protects From Alzheimer's‐Like Pathology and Synaptodegeneration in HIV‐1‐Infected Animals

Since HIV‐1 facilitates amyloid accumulation in the brain and plays a key role in driving Alzheimer's‐like pathology in the context of HAND, we next sought to validate the pathogenesis from the above‐mentioned groups of mice to look at the AD markers by western blotting. We assessed the expression of APP (105 kDa), AβmOC64 (∼35 kDa) and phosphorylated tau (pTau622) (56 kDa) in different brain regions (FC, PC, OC, Hp Str and Cer). The expression of APP (Figure 6A) was found to be significantly upregulated (p < 0.05) in the PC (p = 0.0304) and Hp (p < 0.0001) and showed a similar trend in the FC, OC, Str and Cer in the HIV+ group compared to the HIV−. There was significant downregulation in expression of APP in PC (p = 0.0081), OC (p = 0.0102), Hp (p < 0.0001) and Str (p = 0.0336) in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group compared to HIV+ group at 2 months post‐infection. Next, we assessed the expression of AβmOC64 (a subtype of neurotoxic Aβ 42) (Figure 6B), which was found to be significantly upregulated in the FC (p < 0.0001), Hp (p = 0.0286) and Str (p = 0.0044) and showed a similar trend in the PC and Cer in the HIV+ group compared to HIV− group. There was significant downregulation in expression of AβmOC64 in the FC (p < 0.0001), Hp (p = 0.0072) and Str (p = 0.0014) in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group in comparison to HIV+ group, while PC and Cer regions demonstrated a trend of downregulation. Subsequently, we assessed another hallmark of AD: pTau (p622) levels. As demonstrated in Figure 6C, pTau expression was found to be significantly upregulated (p < 0.0001) in the Hp in HIV+ group compared to the uninfected control group. A similar trend of upregulated pTau expression was seen in the FC, PC and Str in the HIV+ group. We also observed significant downregulation (p < 0.0001) in the expression of hippocampal pTau, and a similar trend was followed in the FC, PC, OC and Str in the HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA group compared to the HIV+ group.

Discussion

Our earlier work (Sil et al. 2020) demonstrated that HIV‐1 Tat promotes astrocytic amyloidogenesis via the HIF‐1α pathway, with SIV+ macaques and HIV+ post‐mortem brain samples exhibiting region‐specific upregulation of amyloidogenic components co‐localized with GFAP‐positive astrocytes. Expanding on these observations, we also reported that ADEVs from HIV‐1 Tat‐exposed astrocytes carry amyloids that, upon neuronal uptake, induce synaptodendritic injury—manifested by reduced dendritic arborization, altered spine morphology, decreased synaptic protein levels, and diminished excitatory post‐synaptic currents (Chemparathy et al. 2024). These effects mirror mechanisms observed in AD models, where amyloid‐laden exosomes contribute to trans‐synaptic spread of pathology and synaptic dysfunction (Sardar Sinha et al. 2018; Rajendran et al. 2006). Importantly, silencing HIF‐1α in astrocytes reduced both the release of ADEVs and their amyloid cargo, significantly attenuating neuronal damage (Chemparathy et al. 2024). This supports studies implicating HIF‐1α as a mediator of neuroinflammation and cellular stress in both HIV and neurodegenerative settings (Choi 2024; Duette et al. 2018). We further validated these findings, as hippocampal injection of HIV‐1 Tat‐ADEVs into naïve mice induced cognitive deficits, amyloid accumulation in neurones and astrocytes, and synaptodegeneration—paralleling early pathological features seen in HAND and AD. Notably, these effects were absent when using ADEVs from HIF‐1α‐silenced astrocytes, reinforcing the centrality of the HIF‐1α–ADEV axis in mediating this neurotoxicity (Chemparathy et al. 2024). Based on the therapeutic potential of HIF‐1α silencing from our previous studies in HIV‐1‐induced neurodegeneration, our present study was undertaken to assess the therapeutic potential of ADEVs loaded with HIF‐1α siRNA for mitigating HIV‐1‐induced Alzheimer's‐like pathology and associated neurodegeneration. The findings demonstrate that HIV‐1 infection induces upregulation of HIF‐1α, Aβ 42, and pTau, along with synaptic alterations and glial activation—hallmarks shared with AD. The study introduces a novel therapeutic approach involving intranasal delivery of ADEVs loaded with HIF‐1α siRNA. This intervention significantly reduced glial activation, amyloid burden, and synaptic damage and demonstrated a trend of improvement in cognitive impairments, highlighting the therapeutic potential of EV‐based gene silencing strategies in HAND and related neurodegenerative conditions. The limitations of the current study include that we have not included a group administered with scrambled/control siRNA‐loaded EVs, and we have not explicitly identified the cargo of the isolated EVs prior to HIF‐1α siRNA loading. Our previous studies established that extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from astrocytes transfected with scrambled siRNA did not elicit any adverse neuropathological effects when stereotactically injected into the hippocampus of naïve mice. Specifically, these EVs did not induce amyloid‐β (Aβ) accumulation, glial activation, or synaptodegeneration. In contrast, EVs derived from astrocytes transfected with scrambled siRNA and subsequently exposed to HIV‐1 Tat triggered all these pathological outcomes, indicating that Tat exposure, rather than the scrambled siRNA itself, drives the observed neurotoxicity (Sil et al. 2020). In our current study, we aimed to reduce the number of NSG humanized mice used, given the high cost and the lengthy 6–8 months timeline required for their development and immune reconstitution prior to experimentation. Considering the resource‐intensive nature of this model and our prior evidence showing no pathological or molecular effects of control/scrambled siRNA in either in vitro or in vivo settings, we chose not to include an EV‐loaded control siRNA group in these experiments. Although this is considered a minor limitation of the study, however, non‐inclusion of the scrambled/control siRNA group does not change the interpretation or conclusion of this study. Our findings reinforce the growing body of evidence positioning EVs as promising carriers for the CNS‐targeted therapies (Zhou et al. 2022; Zhuang et al. 2011; Banks et al. 2020; Alvarez‐Erviti et al. 2011). In our study, intranasal delivery of mCherry‐tagged ADEVs revealed robust biodistribution to brain regions critical for cognition, including the hippocampus, frontal and parietal cortices. These results are consistent with prior reports emphasizing the capacity of EVs to bypass systemic metabolism and reach deep brain regions via intranasal administration (Zhuang et al. 2011; Guo et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2024). After intranasal administration of ADEV‐encapsulated siRNA in mice, it bypasses the blood–brain barrier via the olfactory and trigeminal nerves, enabling direct access to the brain. The olfactory bulb is the primary site of uptake due to direct anatomical proximity to the nasal cavity (Thorne et al. 2008). Moreover, the olfactory bulb has a more permeable BBB compared to deeper brain structures, allowing easier access for siRNA (Mistry et al. 2015). From the olfactory bulb, siRNA spreads to the frontal cortex and hippocampus due to their proximity to the olfactory structures, thus lending credence to the intranasal delivery method to relevant brain regions associated with neurocognitive disorders (Dhuria et al. 2010; Kumar et al. 2007; Chen and Liu 2012). The deeper structures of the brain, like the amygdala, striatum, thalamus, hypothalamus, brainstem and cerebellum, show lesser but sometimes detectable levels of siRNA, likely due to both anatomical distance from the nasal epithelium and diffusion limitations (Malhotra et al. 2013; Hanson and Frey 2008). These findings collectively support the rationale for our observation, where the HIV‐1 p24 level significantly decreases in the cortex of HIV+HIF‐1α siRNA‐administered mice after intranasal siRNA administration, as a viable and regionally impactful method for CNS delivery. These findings collectively support the rationale for our current observations. The therapeutic delivery of HIF‐1α siRNA via ADEVs led to robust downregulation of HIF‐1α across key brain regions, particularly in the parietal cortex and hippocampus. We observed high viral load in the plasma as well as in the cortex, Hp, Str and cerebellum (Cer) of the HIV+ group. Interestingly, after HIF‐1α siRNA treatment, while plasma viral loads remained unchanged, we noted a localized reduction of HIV‐1 p24 levels in the cortex region, while the other regions, although they showed a trend of decrease, were not significant. A significant reduction in HIV‐1 p24 protein levels in the cortex and HIF‐1α mRNA in both the cortex and hippocampus, as well as HIF‐1α protein expression in the hippocampus, was also observed in mice administered with ADEV‐encapsulated HIF‐1α siRNA compared to the HIV+ group. Notably, in the cortex, the reduction in p24 levels correlated with decreased HIF‐1α expression, suggesting a potential mechanistic link. In contrast, no such correlation was observed in the hippocampus. Future studies will aim to explore the mechanistic relationship between plasma viral load and tissue‐specific p24 levels following intranasal delivery of ADEV‐encapsulated HIF‐1α siRNA.

We used the NSG (NOD.Cg‐Prkdc^scid Il2rg^tm1Wjl/SzJ) humanized mice for this study. Previous studies by others (Duette et al. 2018; Deshmane et al. 2011; Deshmane et al. 2009) and us (Chemparathy et al. 2024; Sil et al. 2020) have implicated HIF‐1α dysregulation in HIV‐mediated neuroinflammation and amyloidogenesis, driven by viral proteins such as HIV‐1 Tat. Our results corroborate these findings by demonstrating elevated HIF‐1α expression in HIV‐1‐infected humanized mice, specifically in the hippocampus, accompanied by increased APP, Aβ, pTau, and markers of glial activation. These observations align with reports linking chronic inflammation, oxidative stress and HIF‐1α upregulation to HAND and Alzheimer's‐like pathology (Achim et al. 2009; Chemparathy et al. 2024; Cho et al. 2017; Sil et al. 2020; Gelman 2015); however, this is the first report of the development of Alzheimer's like pathology in HIV‐1‐infected NSG humanized mice. HIV‐1 infection resulted in a significant upregulation of APP and Aβ in the hippocampus and cortex. This amyloidogenic processing is a hallmark shared between HAND and AD, with Aβ aggregates contributing to synaptic toxicity and neuronal dysfunction. Similarly, we observed increased expression of pTau, most significantly in the hippocampus, indicative of tau hyperphosphorylation, another pathological feature associated with neurodegenerative processes. Beyond amyloid and tau pathology, our data revealed extensive synaptic alterations. Our western blot analyses revealed significant alterations in the expression levels of key synaptic markers—gephyrin, GAD65, PSD95, and synaptophysin—in different brain regions, significantly in the hippocampus, of HIV‐1‐infected mice. Synaptic integrity has been shown to be critically compromised in both HAND and Alzheimer's‐like pathology, and loss of synaptic proteins is strongly correlated with cognitive decline (Gelman 2015; Ru and Tang 2017). Specifically, gephyrin, a postsynaptic scaffolding protein essential for GABA_A receptor clustering (Tyagarajan and Fritschy 2014), and GAD65, the enzyme catalyzing GABA synthesis, which plays a crucial role in regulating neuronal excitability, were found to be upregulated in HIV‐1‐infected hu‐mice. This indicates compromised inhibitory synaptic architecture, which contributes to excitatory‐inhibitory imbalance, a key driver of network dysfunction and excitotoxicity. Two other markers usually studied to assess synaptic integrity, structure, and function are PSD95 and synaptophysin (Liu et al. 2025). PSD95, a scaffold protein, resides in the postsynaptic density of excitatory synapses and is vital for positioning glutamate receptors and coordinating signalling pathways (Marshall et al. 2018; Cao et al. 2013). On the other hand, synaptophysin is a membrane‐associated glycoprotein localized to presynaptic vesicles, commonly utilized as a marker to assess synaptic density and overall presynaptic function (Wang et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2024). In our study, HIV‐1 infection was associated with a significant increase in PSD95 levels in the hippocampus, as well as an increase in expression of synaptophysin in the different brain regions. HIV‐1 infection triggers immune activation and persistent inflammation (Hoenigl et al. 2019), which could indirectly have a detrimental effect on neuronal synapses. Increase in PSD95 and synaptophysin levels could potentially be linked to compensatory mechanisms in neurons attempting to maintain synaptic function and connectivity in response to damage or stress. To explore possible upstream mechanisms contributing to these synaptic disruptions, we conducted a bioinformatic analysis using the JASPAR database and motif scanning tool, focusing on the potential transcriptional regulation of PSD95 and gephyrin by HIF‐1α. Our analysis identified several canonical hypoxia response elements (HREs) with the consensus motif 5′‐RCGTG‐3′ (R = A or G), which are recognized by HIF‐1α (Wu et al. 2015; Ilaria M.C. Orlando et al. 2020). Although no prior experimental studies have directly linked HIF‐1α to the regulation of gephyrin or PSD95, our in silico findings suggest a potential direct regulatory relationship. However, it is important to acknowledge that our study did not experimentally validate this interaction. Thus, it remains unclear whether the observed changes in synaptic markers are mediated by direct transcriptional regulation or indirectly through immune activation and neuroinflammatory signalling cascades. Future mechanistic studies will be necessary to resolve this distinction. Specifically, experimental approaches such as electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA), chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), and HIF‐1α modulation via genetic or pharmacological tools will be employed to determine whether HIF‐1α directly binds to and regulates the promoters of Gephyrin and PSD95. Nevertheless, our immunofluorescence analyses provide further validation of the molecular and synaptic alterations observed biochemically, offering critical insight into the spatial distribution and extent of HIV‐1‐induced neuropathology within the hippocampus, specifically in subregions such as CA1, CA2, CA3, and dentate gyrus (DG). Further confirming the western blot results, the immunofluorescence results also demonstrated upregulation in the intensity of expression of AD markers Aβ, pTau, and synaptic protein GAD65 due to HIV‐1 infection. On administration of HIF‐1α siRNA treatment, significant restoration of the upregulated levels of pTau and GAD65 was observed, whereas a trend of reduction was found in expression levels of Aβ in the brain hemispheres. The marked escalation in Aβ 42 and pTau expressions in the hippocampal CA1 and CA2 regions of HIV‐1‐infected mice, accompanied by pronounced GFAP upregulation, is indicative of astrocyte activation due to HIV‐1 infection. The increased amyloid and tau burden highlights the convergence of HAND and Alzheimer's‐like pathology. We observed a significant reduction of Aβ levels due to HIF‐1α siRNA administration in the hippocampus, but only a trend of decrease in the other brain regions, which can be attributed to the biodistribution of ADEVs in the hippocampal and cortical regions after intranasal delivery. Additionally, a noticeable reduction in neuronal MAP2 immunoreactivity was observed in the HIV‐1‐infected group, reflecting neuronal damage, particularly within hippocampal pyramidal neurones. Elevated expression of IBA‐1, a marker of microglial activation, is evidently upregulated in the hippocampal region of HIV‐1‐infected animals. This aligns with chronic microglial activation seen in HAND, which perpetuates neuroinflammatory signalling and synaptic pruning. Accompanying this glial activation (both astrocyte and microglia) and substantial loss of MAP2 immunoreactivity, this is the first report that demonstrates an increase in percentage colocalization of Aβ with these three cell types in the hippocampus of HIV‐1‐infected animals, which underscores a pivotal pathological mechanism. Our data also identifies both the hippocampus and cortex as major sites of HIV‐associated amyloid pathology, with significant AβmOC64 accumulation in the frontal (FC) and parietal (PC) lobes, accompanied by increased colocalization with astrocytes, neurons and microglia. Such widespread cellular association suggests impaired amyloid clearance and heightened neuroinflammatory engagement (Kamila et al. 2025). Similar to the results obtained for the hippocampus, elevated GFAP and reduced MAP2 expression further indicate concurrent astrogliosis and neuronal loss in the cortical brain region, consistent with neuropathological findings in HAND (Lawrence et al. 2023; Barsoum et al. 2025). These findings extend prior evidence of cortical vulnerability in HIV (Mcmurtray et al. 2008; Guha et al. 2016), emphasizing the cortex as a critical site where amyloid accumulation, glial activation, and neuronal injury converge to drive cognitive impairment.

The results of the immunofluorescence studies reflect not only impaired Aβ clearance by glial cells but also suggest intracellular amyloid accumulation within astrocytes and neurones, exacerbating inflammation and subsequent synaptic dysfunction. Chronic HIV‐1‐induced neuroinflammation compromises the phagocytic efficiency of astrocytes and microglia, while viruses and viral proteins drive APP processing within neurones (Chemparthy et al. 2021). Together, this contributes to sustained amyloidogenic burden, glial activation, and neurotoxicity, central to the progression of Alzheimer's‐like pathology leading to HAND. Notably, HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEV treatment resulted in a reduction in colocalization of Aβ 42 within astrocytes, microglia, and neurons, which signifies a multifaceted therapeutic benefit at the cellular level. A similar observation was made in case of Aβ fibril, where significant reduction in accumulation of Aβ fibril was observed specifically in the neurons on HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEV treatment in HIV‐1‐infected animals. These results indicate enhanced glial clearance capacity, reduced astrocytic and microglial dysfunction, and a marked decline in amyloidogenic processing. This shift reflects a normalization of cellular homeostasis and highlights the disruption of the pathological feedback loop between neuroinflammation, amyloid burden, and synaptic injury. In HIV‐1‐infected humanized mice, significant reduction in drebrin, an actin‐binding protein critical for dendritic spine morphology, synaptogenesis, and synaptic plasticity (Shirao and Sekino 2017), was observed, reflecting synaptic destabilization and loss of structural integrity. On administration of HIF‐1α siRNA, drebrin expression was markedly restored, particularly in the CA1 subregion, indicating that the intervention preserved dendritic spine architecture and synaptic stability. These findings further highlight HIF‐1α’s role as an upstream driver of neuronal damage and synaptodendritic injury, underscoring its potential as a therapeutic target. One particularly noteworthy observation was the dual role of ADEVs. On one hand, unmodified ADEVs from HIV‐1 Tat‐exposed astrocytes facilitate the spread of neurotoxic proteins, promoting amyloid propagation and neuronal injury, as evidenced in our previous studies (Chemparathy et al. 2024; Tang et al. 2024). On the other hand, by modulating ADEV cargo through HIF‐1α siRNA loading, we transformed these vesicles from pathogenic mediators into therapeutic tools capable of attenuating disease progression. This capacity to regulate EV cargo composition adds a significant layer of versatility to EV‐based therapies, as it allows for precise manipulation of intercellular signalling pathways implicated in neurodegeneration.

The impact of HIF‐1α‐targeted ADEV therapy extended beyond molecular and histopathological outcomes to functional outcomes involving behavioural assessments. Our Y‐maze findings underscore significant HIV‐1‐induced cognitive impairments, affecting both short‐term and long‐term memory functions. Y‐maze testing at 0.5 months post‐infection revealed that HIV‐1‐infected mice displayed notable alterations in spatial habituation and recognition memory compared to uninfected controls. Specifically, infected mice spent less time in the familiar arms and more time in the novel arm, along with changes in latency to arm entry, which strongly suggests impaired spatial habituation and recognition memory. Healthy mice typically spend less time exploring familiar environments (habituation) and show a preference for novelty (recognition) (Leussis and Bolivar 2006; Kim et al. 2023). The reversal of this pattern in HIV+ mice indicates they failed to recognise the familiar arms as such or were unable to suppress exploration of the now‐familiar space, reflecting a core deficit in short‐term spatial memory. The prolonged latency to first entry into familiar arms and, conversely, reduced latency to enter the novel arm further highlight altered decision‐making, potentially indicating increased impulsivity towards novelty or anxiety/avoidance towards the familiar context. The elevated novelty score quantitatively confirms this abnormal preference for novelty over familiarity, a hallmark of recognition memory impairment (Broadbelt et al. 2022). Importantly, treatment with HIF‐1α siRNA reversed many of these parameters at 0.5 months, providing mechanistic insight that implicates the HIF‐1α pathway in HIV‐associated cognitive dysfunction and supports its potential as a therapeutic target. At 2 months post‐infection, although these differences did not reach statistical significance, we observed consistent directional trends indicative of continued impairment in novelty recognition and reduced exploratory drive, possibly reflecting a transition from acute to chronic neurocognitive effects. The Open Field Test is widely used to assess general locomotor activity (Zheng et al. 2024; Pacheco et al. 2019). In our study, at 0.5 months post‐HIV‐1 infection, HIV+ mice demonstrated significant locomotor and behavioural impairments. Specifically, these mice exhibited a reduction in total mobility time, indicating general hypoactivity and reduced exploratory drive (Keledjian et al. 2023; Fitting et al. 2013; Fitting et al. 2016; Canaguier et al. 2025; Kraeuter et al. 2019; Seibenhener and Wooten 2015). Furthermore, HIV+ mice displayed significantly fewer clockwise rotations, reflecting reduced motor coordination (Shabbott and Sainburg 2008; Peng and Charron 2013; Han et al. 2024). In contrast, they exhibited an increasing trend in anticlockwise rotations, suggesting a behavioural asymmetry that may be linked to compensatory movement patterns or potential hemispheric imbalances in basal ganglia or cortical control circuits, often associated with neurodegenerative conditions (Sullivan et al. 1994). Notably, treatment with HIF‐1α siRNA partially rescued these behavioural abnormalities. HIV+HIF‐1α siRNA‐treated mice showed significant restoration in mobility time, suggesting improved overall locomotor function. While the number of clockwise rotations showed an upwards trend, the further increase in anticlockwise rotations may indicate partial recovery of motor engagement, albeit with persistent lateralization bias. At the 2‐month post‐infection time point, HIV+ mice continued to exhibit directional trends consistent with impaired motor function, such as reduced mobility and altered rotational behaviour, though these differences did not reach statistical significance. These results suggest that the acute motor deficits evident at 0.5 months likely correspond with early‐stage cognitive impairments observed in the Y‐maze study. The Elevated Plus Maze is a classic test used to evaluate anxiety‐like behaviour and exploratory activity in rodents (Naeimi Ghahroodi et al. 2025; Yuan et al. 2025). At 2 months post‐infection, HIV+ mice exhibited no changes in the mean number of visits to the open arms compared to HIV− controls, suggesting that anxiety‐like behaviour in terms of exploratory drive towards open spaces remained unaltered by HIV alone. However, in the closed arms, HIV+ mice demonstrated a trend towards reduced entries and decreased time spent, accompanied by an increased latency to first entry. This pattern may reflect altered risk assessment or diminished locomotor activity. These behavioural impairments align with concurrent findings from the Open Field test, where HIV+ mice exhibited reduced total mobility time and decreased clockwise rotations, indicative of general hypoactivity, motor coordination deficits, and potentially diminished exploratory motivation. Similar results were observed in the case of the Y‐maze study, where the increase in the number of entries at 0.5 months post‐infection demonstrates hyperactivity, but a decrease in the same at 2 months post‐infection reflects reduced locomotor activity. Notably, the HIV+ group treated with HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA displayed partial protection from behavioural deficits. Specifically, HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA mice exhibited a significant increase in the mean number of visits to the open arms, indicating reduced anxiety‐like behaviour. Furthermore, these mice demonstrated a significant increase in closed‐arm entries, along with a significant decrease in the time spent in closed arms, suggesting normalization of movement patterns and possibly reduced risk of aversion. Importantly, in HIV++HIF‐1 siRNA mice, latency to first entry into the closed arms was significantly reduced, further indicating restoration of goal‐directed exploration.

PPI is a neurophysiological mechanism in which a weak preparatory stimulus reduces the magnitude of the startle response to the following strong stimulus. Contrary to the typical PPI deficits seen in neuropsychiatric disorders (e.g., schizophrenia), HIV+ mice demonstrated a slight increase in the startle amplitude and habituation score. While classical neuropsychiatric models often associate impaired PPI with cognitive deficits, the increased PPI observed in our study likely reflects impaired startle response, observed in HIV+ individuals (Jha et al. 2025; Minassian et al. 2013; Walter et al. 2021). The behavioural studies collectively demonstrate that HIV‐1 infection induces subtle impairments in cognitive flexibility, locomotor coordination, anxiety‐like behaviour, and startle response, which are partially ameliorated by HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded EV treatment.

Further, our correlation data revealed a complex interplay between neurodegenerative markers, behavioural outcomes, and viral load in HIV‐infected mice (Figure S5). In the HIV+ group, APP and pTau levels showed a trend of positive correlation, while PSD95 significantly correlated positively with viral load, suggesting an association between neurodegeneration and viral load. In case of HIF‐1α siRNA treatment group, even though we observed improvement in pathological parameters, we did not observe any significant correlation between APP, Aβ, and pTau with the viral load, which suggests that the improvements in the pathological parameters are not associated with viral load post‐treatment.

In the context of HAND, HIF‐1α plays a pivotal role in regulating key neuroinflammatory mediators that exacerbate neuronal injury and cognitive impairment (Sharma et al. 2022). HIF‐1α upregulates vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP‐9), which disrupts the blood–brain barrier, sustains chronic neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration in the HIV‐infected brain (Sharma et al. 2022). HIF‐1α upregulation also drives astrocytic production of interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) (Shan et al. 2017) and induces monocyte chemoattractant proteins MCP‐1 (CCL2) and MCP‐5 (CCL12) (Mojsilovic‐Petrovic et al. 2007), driving immune cell recruitment and chronic neuroinflammation in autoimmune encephalomyelitis (Shan et al. 2017), AD (Lee et al. 2018; Conductier et al. 2010; Zhang and Luo 2019), multiple sclerosis (Mahad and Ransohoff 2003), and stroke (Georgakis et al. 2019). Collectively, these findings highlight HIF‐1α as a critical transcriptional regulator of neuroinflammatory cascades in CNS disorders. In our study, the HIF‐1α siRNA treatment does not affect the viral load in HIV+ mice, which indicates HIF‐1 siRNA treatment protects from Alzheimer's‐like pathology and related neurodegeneration, not via containment of the viral load, but other related pathways, probably involving neuroinflammation, which will be addressed in our future studies.

In summary, our study for the first time demonstrated Alzheimer's‐like pathology and associated synaptodegeneration in the NSG‐humanized HIV‐1‐infected mice. The HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEV treatment restored Alzheimer's‐like pathology, glial activation, and synaptic damage, including excitatory‐inhibitory synaptic balance induced by HIV‐1 infection. Our study provides a proof‐of‐concept for a targeted, non‐invasive therapeutic approach that addresses the unique intersection of HAND and Alzheimer's‐like pathology through precision modulation of ADEV cargo, which is translationally relevant. Given the shared molecular hallmarks between HAND and classical AD—such as Aβ accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, and synaptic degeneration—our strategy holds potential applicability beyond the HIV‐1 context to other neurodegenerative diseases. This strategy paves the way for the development of innovative, targeted therapies for HAND and potentially other neurodegenerative disorders. However, this study exhibits several limitations. First, HIF‐1α siRNA‐loaded ADEV‐targeting HIV‐1 viral load exhibited limited efficacy, which could be addressed in future studies by combining this approach with antiretroviral therapy (cART). Second, the small sample size at the 2‐month time point may have contributed to data variability and reduced statistical power in behavioural assessments. Third, the requirement of daily anaesthesia during intranasal delivery from Weeks 22 to 29, mandated by IACUC guidelines due to biosafety concerns in handling immunocompromised, HIV‐infected animals, may limit the translational applicability of this delivery method. Nevertheless, this protocol was essential to ensure both animal welfare and personnel safety during repeated administrations. This study served as a proof‐of‐concept to assess the feasibility of intranasal siRNA‐loaded EV delivery. To enhance clinical relevance, we are now exploring additional routes and conducting follow‐up studies with larger cohorts and optimized protocols. Additionally, our future studies would focus on optimizing EV delivery, assessing long‐term safety of repeated dosing, and evaluating cell‐specific targeting strategies to enhance therapeutic efficacy, as well as investigating the specific downstream molecular pathways modulated by HIF‐1α siRNA treatment that mediate neuroprotection and reversal of Alzheimer's‐like pathology in HAND.
