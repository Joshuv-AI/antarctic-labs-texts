Monoamine Oxidase and Cholinesterase Inhibition Profiles of Semicarbazone and Thiosemicarbazone Derivatives
Authors: Sandeep Bindra, Won Kyeong Jeong, Muzammil Kabier, Sunil Kumar, Sachithra Thazhathuveedu Sudevan, Harish Chandra vishwakarma, Della Grace Thomas Parambi, Naseer Maliyakkal, Sandeep Kumar, Leena K. Pappachen, Hoon Kim, Bijo Mathew
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTwenty semicarbazone and thiosemicarbazone derivatives (T1–T20) were synthesized and evaluated for their inhibition of monoamine oxidases and cholinesterases. Most compounds showed stronger inhibition of MAO‐B than MAO‐A. Among the synthesized compounds, T6 most strongly inhibited MAO‐B (IC50 = 6.45 µM), followed by T17 (IC50 = 9.46 µM). T6 and T17 exhibited the highest selectivity index values (3.60 and >4.23, respectively) for MAO‐B as compared with MAO‐A. T6 was a competitive MAO‐B inhibitor. Regarding structure–activity relationship, the thiosemicarbazone T1 (IC50 = 20.74 µM) showed >1.93 times better MAO‐B inhibition than the semicarbazone T5 (IC50 >40 µM); a similar pattern was observed for T6 vs. T10, T11 vs. T15, and T16 vs. T20, indicating that the thiosemicarbazone derivatives exhibited better MAO‐B inhibition than the semicarbazone derivatives when the terminal –NH2 group was free. All the compounds showed less than 50% inhibition of acetylcholinesterase at 10 µM. Comparatively, compounds T3 and T12 exhibited 70.65 and 61.94% inhibition of butyrylcholinesterase at 10 µM, respectively. Docking studies of the MAO‐B inhibition of the most promising molecules were performed using MzDOCK. Collectively, thiosemicarbazone derivatives, specially T6 and T17, warrant further investigation as the promising candidates for the development of novel therapeutics targeting MAO‐B‐related disorders.

Introduction

Butyrylcholinesterase (BChE; EC 3.1.1.8) is a serine hydrolase that belongs to the cholinesterase family, along with acetylcholinesterase (AChE) [1]. Whereas AChE is primarily responsible for the hydrolysis of the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh) in synaptic clefts, BChE exhibits broader substrate specificity and is predominantly found in the blood plasma, liver, and glial cells of the central nervous system (CNS) [2]. Historically considered a peripheral backup for AChE, BChE has emerged as a significant therapeutic target, especially for Alzheimer's disease (AD) [3]. In the healthy brain, AChE dominates cholinergic neurotransmission regulation. However, during the progression of AD, AChE activity declines, whereas BChE activity is upregulated, particularly in the cortex and hippocampus [4]. This shift renders BChE a more prominent contributor to ACh hydrolysis in late‐stage AD, making its inhibition a promising therapeutic strategy for enhancing cholinergic signaling and mitigating cognitive deficits. BChE inhibitors (BChEIs) are increasingly being studied as standalone treatments or in combination with AChE inhibitors [5]. Although current FDA‐approved cholinesterase inhibitors, such as donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine, primarily target AChE, some inhibitors, such as rivastigmine, also exhibit dual inhibition of both enzymes. Selective BChEIs, such as cymserine derivatives and bisnorcymserine, have shown potential in preclinical studies, offering enhanced cognitive effects with reduced peripheral side effects compared with selective AChE inhibitors [6].

The therapeutic potential of BChE extends beyond its cholinergic functions. BChE has been implicated in the detoxification of neurotoxic compounds, including organophosphates and certain β‐amyloid precursors. It also plays a role in inflammation modulation, lipid metabolism, and noncholinergic neurotransmission, thereby affording additional neuroprotective mechanisms for the treatment of dementia and other neurodegenerative disorders [7]. BChE has also gained attention in the field of multitarget‐directed ligand design. Because AD involves a complex interplay of cholinergic dysfunction, oxidative stress, metal dyshomeostasis, β‐amyloid aggregation, and inflammation, BChE is frequently targeted alongside other enzymes such as monoamine oxidase (MAO)‐B and AChE. For example, hybrid therapies combining BChE inhibition with antioxidant, metal‐chelating, or amyloid‐disrupting functionalities have shown promising multitarget profiles in both in silico and in vitro models. BChE shows significant degree of homology to AChE but possesses a wider and more flexible active site gorge, allowing for the accommodation of bulkier ligands [8, 9, 10].

MAO‐B is a mitochondrial flavin‐containing enzyme that catalyzes the oxidative deamination of monoamines such as dopamine and phenylethylamine [11]. It is predominantly expressed in astrocytes and serotonergic neurons in the brain and plays a key role in neurotransmitter level regulation, particularly within the basal ganglia. Between the two isoforms MAO‐A and ‐B, the latter is of significant interest in the context of neurodegenerative diseases because of its involvement in the metabolism of dopamine and the production of reactive oxygen species [12]. In Parkinson's disease (PD), the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra leads to dopamine deficiency. MAO‐B contributes to this process by degrading dopamine and generating hydrogen peroxide as a byproduct, thus exacerbating oxidative stress and neuronal damage [13]. Elevated MAO‐B levels have also been observed in the aging brain and is associated with an increased susceptibility to neurodegeneration. Consequently, MAO‐B has been validated as a drug target for the symptomatic treatment of PD [14]. Pharmacological inhibition of MAO‐B leads to increased synaptic dopamine levels and reduced oxidative stress, thereby providing symptomatic relief and potentially affording neuroprotective benefits. Several MAO‐B inhibitors have been approved for clinical use. Particularly, selegiline and rasagiline are irreversible inhibitors that bind covalently to the flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor within the MAO‐B active site. In contrast, safinamide is a reversible inhibitor with additional glutamate‐modulating properties, offering both dopaminergic and non‐dopaminergic benefits. Beyond PD, MAO‐B has been implicated in AD, where it may contribute to neuroinflammation and β‐amyloid pathology [15]. Elevated MAO‐B activity has been associated with increased monoamine turnover, oxidative damage, and glial activation. This has prompted interest in the development of dual‐acting compounds that inhibit MAO‐B alongside other relevant targets, such as AChE and N‐methyl‐d‐aspartate receptors [16].

Multitargeted drug design (MTDD) represents a paradigm shift in the field of drug discovery, moving away from the conventional “one drug–one target” philosophy toward a “one drug–multiple targets” strategy [10, 16]. This approach is particularly relevant to the treatment of complex and multifactorial diseases such as cancer, AD, diabetes, and infectious diseases, where multiple signaling pathways and molecular interactions contribute to disease progression [17]. The core objective of MTDD is to develop single therapeutic agents capable of simultaneously modulating multiple disease‐related targets. This polypharmacological approach has several advantages over monotherapy including improved therapeutic efficacy, reduced drug resistance, enhanced patient compliance, and potential cost reduction through simplified drug regimens [18]. In contrast with combination therapy, which uses multiple agents to affect different targets, multitarget drugs reduce the risk of drug interactions and may offer better pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic profiles. Despite its potential, MTDD presents with several challenges, including achieving optimal binding affinity and selectivity across targets; minimizing off‐target toxicity; and ensuring favorable absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity characteristics (collectively referred to as ADME‐Tox characteristics). Additionally, the identification of synergistic target pairs requires a comprehensive biological understanding and often relies on systems biology and network‐based approaches [19].

Research has demonstrated that the imine units of semicarbazone and thiosemicarbazone possess anti‐protozoal, ‐convulsant, ‐cancer, as well as urease and MAO inhibitory properties [20]. Thiosemicarbazides are capable of crossing the blood–brain barrier, rendering them promising candidates for the development of drugs that target neurological disorders [21]. The unique terminal hydrazine unit of semicarbazide and thiosemicarbazide groups enables them to react with carbonyl compounds. Recent pharmacophore‐based research has demonstrated that MAO‐A and ‐B inhibitors possess a hydrogen bond donor (HBD), hydrogen bond acceptor (HBA), and one or more hydrophobic rings. Furthermore, the presence of an HBD and HBA in the thiosemicarbazone units with distinct aryl or heteroaryl units result in significant MAO inhibition. According to a recent study, thiosemicarbazones may represent a novel family of reversible MAO‐B inhibitors for the treatment of neurodegenerative illnesses [22]. These new adducts may be employed as MAO‐B inhibitors to treat neurological illnesses if it is found possible to add a methyl group to the terminal amino acid of 5‐phenyl modified thiosemicarbazones to increase their lipophilicity [23]. Research on aryl thiosemicarbazones under various electron donation and withdrawal conditions has shown that these inhibit multiple MAOs [24]. The present study focused on the inhibition profiles of MAOs and cholinesterases by semicarbazone and thiosemicarbazone derivatives.

Materials and methods

Synthesis

Twenty derivatives of semicarbazone and thiosemicarbazone (T1–T20) were synthesized by reacting semicarbazide or thiosemicarbazide (1eq) with acetophenone derivatives (1eq) in the presence of two added drops of glacial acetic acid as catalyst—using methanol as solvent—under reflux conditions. The reaction was monitored using thin layer chromatography employing 30% hexane and 70% ethyl acetate as solvents. After reaction completion, the mixture was poured into ice‐cooled water to obtain a precipitate. The precipitate was filtered and purified by recrystallization, followed by flash chromatography. All synthesized derivatives were characterized using 1H NMR and 13C NMR and mass spectroscopy in Supporting information.

Enzyme Inhibition Assay

Measured enzyme activities of human MAO‐A and ‐B (recombinant; Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were as follows. Absorbance was measured at 250 (MAO‐A) and 316 (MAO‐B) nm, using 0.06 mM kynuramine and 0.3 mM benzylamine, respectively, for 45 min as previously described [25]. AChE type VI‐S from Electrophorus electricus and BChE from equine serum were used with 0.5 mM acetylthiocholine iodide and 0.5 mM S‐butyrylthiocholine iodide as substrates, respectively, and 0.5 mM 5,5′‐dithiobis (2‐nitrobenzoic acid) was used as development agent [26].

Inhibition assay was done by adding the substrate at the last step to the reaction mixtures containing respective enzyme and inhibitor. Kinetics was carried out for a leading compound T6 for MAO‐B as described previously [25]. Substrate benzylamine concentrations used were 0.0375, 0.075, 0.15, 0.3, and 0.6 mM, and inhibitor concentrations were approximately 1/2x, 1x, 2x IC50, i.e., 3.0, 6.0, and 12.0 µM. Data were analyzed with Lineweaver‐Burk (LB) plots and its secondary plot for Km and Ki values, respectively.

Parallel Artificial Membrane Permeability Assay

The parallel artificial membrane permeability assay assesses the passive transcellular permeability of drugs across the blood‐brain barrier. The experimental setup utilized a microtiter plate and a Millipore filter plate, each with 96 wells and equipped with 0.45 µm pores and a 125 µm thick filter. These components were arranged in a “sandwich” configuration, where 0.1 mL of n‐dodecane was applied to the filter membrane to form a synthetic lipid barrier. Drug stock solutions (10 mM)—prepared in DMSO and stored at 0°C prior to the experiment—were diluted in a pH 7.4 buffer to obtain two separate final solutions with concentrations of 0.01–0.1 and 0.5–1 mM. Subsequently, solution DMSO content was maintained strictly below 1% (v/v) before transferring to a filter plate.

For the experimental setup, acceptor wells were filled with 200 µL of pH 7.4 buffer, and donor wells with 270 µL of the prepared drug solutions. The donor plate was carefully positioned over the acceptor filter plate in a “sandwich” configuration. This arrangement comprised an aqueous donor solution containing the analyte, intervening synthetic lipid barrier, and aqueous recipient solution, together facilitating the permeation with the test substance from the donor wells through to the acceptor wells. The integrity of this setup was verified by inspecting the leaks. Drug penetration was quantitatively assessed by measuring the concentrations in the donor, recipient, and reference wells using UV spectroscopy with subsequent calculations [27] using the following equation: LogPe=−ln[1−CA/Equilibrium]/A×1/VD+1/VA×t, where Pe is permeability (cm/s), CA is receptor concentration, A is area of effective filtration (4.8 cm2), VD is donor volume (mL), VA represents acceptor volume (mL), t represents time of incubation(s), and Equilibrium=CD×VD+CA×VA/VD+VA

Molecular Docking

To study the binding affinities and interaction profiles of T6 and T17, suitable targets were identified and selected from the RCSB PDB database [28]. Human MAO‐A and ‐B were confirmed as the target for the molecular docking study [29, 30]. The MzDOCK tool was used for docking simulation [31]. The compound of interest was initially prepared by force field optimization using MMFF94 at a protonation state of pH 7.4 [32]. The backbone of the protein structure was studied with conformational validation aid by means of a Ramachandran plot [33]. Each protein was prepared by adding hydrogen and Gasteiger charges, removing the water moiety, and retaining cofactor FAD [34]. The binding site was configured at the centroid of the co‐crystal ligand present in both proteins (MAO‐B (resname: SAG) and MAO‐A (resname: HRM)) with a buffer space of 4 Ångström from the co‐crystal ligand. The Vina scoring function was used to run the simulation, with the number of modes and exhaustiveness set to nine and eight, respectively [35]. The binding affinity and interactions between the co‐crystal ligand and the compound of interest were generated [36]. A valid explanation was ultimately devised based on the experimental results.

Molecular Dynamics

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried employed to investigate the stability and structural changes of the protein‐ligand complexes over time. The docked complexes of compound T6 and the reference compound safinamide with the protein (PDB:2V5Z) were simulated for the 200 ns using the Desmond VI (Schrödinger 2025‐4). The SPC water solvent model was implemented within an orthorhombic box, and the system model was neutralized by adding Cl− and Na+ ions at a salt concentration of 0.15 M. The force field OPLS4 was applied under an NPT ensemble with a constant temperature of 300 K and a pressure of 1.01325 bar. To assess the MD simulation, the root mean square deviation (RMSD), interaction fractions root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), protein‐ligand contacts and ligand properties were evaluated to analyze binding stability and fluctuations throughout the trajectories.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

We synthesized twenty derivatives of thio/semicarbazide‐acetophenone as follows. Compounds T1–T5 were synthesized by reacting 3,4‐(methylenedioxy)acetophenone with thio/semicarbazide in methanol in the presence of catalytic amounts of glacial acetic acid under reflux. Compounds T5–T10 were synthesized by reacting 1‐(2,3‐dihydro‐1,4‐benzodioxin‐6‐yl)ethenone with thio/semicarbazide in methanol in the presence of catalytic amounts of glacial acetic acid under reflux. Compounds T11–T15 were synthesized by reacting 4‐ethoxyacetophenone with thio/semicarbazide in methanol in the presence of catalytic amounts of glacial acetic acid under reflux. Compounds T16–T20 were synthesized by reacting 4‐(methylthio)acetophenone in methanol with thio/semicarbazide in the presence of glacial acetic acid in catalytic amounts under reflux. The synthesis is illustrated in Scheme 1. The presence of a –CH3 group in the thio/semicarbazide‐acetophenone complex was confirmed. The appearance of a singlet at 2.22, triplet between 4.24–4.23, singlet at 10.07, and singlet at 9.16 confirmed the presence of –CH3, –OCH2, –NH–N and –NH2 functional groups, respectively, in T6. The final 1H NMR spectrum of T6 contained six deshielded protons, including one –NH proton, two –NH2 protons, and three aromatic protons. Mass spectra confirmed that the calculated molecular weight was consistent with what was observed.

MAO‐A and MAO‐B Inhibition Study of the Compounds

The twenty synthesized compounds were evaluated for MAO‐A and ‐B inhibitory activity. Overall, most compounds showed higher inhibitory activity against MAO‐B. Among these, T6 most strongly inhibited MAO‐B (IC50 = 6.45 µM), followed by T17 (IC50 = 9.46 µM). However, all the compounds demonstrated weak MAO‐A inhibitory activity. Compounds T6 and T17 showed the highest selectivity index values (3.60 and >4.23, respectively) for MAO‐B as compared with MAO‐A; however, these values were relatively low compared with those of other selective inhibitors. The observed selectivity indices (SI) indicate limited discrimination between MAO‐B and MAO‐A isoforms. Compared to clinically used MAO‐B inhibitors such as selegiline and rasagiline, which exhibit high selectivity, these values suggest a potential risk of off‐target MAO‐A inhibition. This may lead to safety concerns, including hypertensive responses and drug–drug interactions. However, given their moderate potency, these compounds may serve as initial lead structures for further optimization to enhance MAO‐B selectivity. The inhibitory profiles are shown in Table 1.

Structure–activity Relationships for MAO‐B Inhibition

In the subset T1–T5 containing 3,4‐methylenedioxy moiety, the thiosemicarbazone T1 (IC50 = 20.74 µM) showed >1.93 times stronger MAO‐B inhibition than the semicarbazone T5 (IC50>40 µM), whereas the semicarbazone derivative T4 (IC50 = 17.81 µM) showed >2.25 times stronger inhibition than the thiosemicarbazone T2 (IC50>40 µM) in the cases where a phenyl group was substituted with a –NH2 group (Table 1). In the subset T6–T10 containing a 2,3‐dihydro‐1,4‐benzodioxin moiety, the thiosemicarbazone T6 (6.45 µM) showed the strongest MAO‐B inhibition, which was 4.36 times stronger that of the semicarbazone T10 (28.09 µM). In the subset T11–T15 containing a 4‐ethoxy moiety, the thiosemicarbazone T11 (12.30 µM) showed >3.25 times stronger inhibition than semicarbazone T15 (>40 µM). In the subset T16–T20 containing a 4‐methylsulfur moiety, the thiosemicarbazone T16 (12.30 µM) exhibited >1.92 times stronger inhibition than the semicarbazone T20 (>40 µM). In contrast, phenyl substitution of the –NH2 group either increased or decreased the degree of MAO‐B inhibition of the four subsets, depending on the substituted moieties. From these results, it can be concluded that the thiosemicarbazone derivatives demonstrate better MAO‐B inhibition than the semicarbazone derivatives when the terminal –NH2 group is free. A graphic representation of the structure–activity relationships are shown in Figure 2. These results suggest that T6 and T17 are effective MAO‐B inhibitors and can be further explored for the study as an agent used in treatment of PD.

Study Employing Parallel Artificial Membrane Permeability Assay for Blood‐brain Barrier Permeation

Effective brain penetration is crucial for successful CNS drug delivery [37]. This study employed a parallel artificial membrane permeability assay for the blood–brain barrier to evaluate the brain permeability of various derivative molecules. The brain penetration potential of each compound was quantified by calculating its effective permeability (Pe) using established formulas. Compounds with Pe values exceeding 4.0 × 10− 6 cm/s were classified as potentially brain‐permeable (CNS+), whereas Pe values below 2.0 × 10− 6 cm/s were considered to indicate a probable inability to cross the blood‐brain barrier (CNS‐).

In this assay, T6 demonstrated excellent penetration capacity, with a permeability (Pe = 8.55 ± 0.13 × 10− 6 cm/s). This value, the highest observed in the study, significantly surpassed that of T17 (Pe = 6.88 ± 0.09 × 10− 6 cm/s) (Table 3), strongly suggesting the promising CNS bioavailability of T6.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulation Study

The MD simulation results for compound T6 and safinamide in complex with MAO‐B enzyme target revealed about its flexibility, stability and interaction as shown in Figure 12. To demonstrate the conformational evaluation between the initial and final frames of the protein‐ligand complexes of the 200 ns trajectories were superimposed. The final conformation can be observed in the red color ligand and blue color in protein. Initial and final simulate poses were superimposed and aligned to understand the dynamics of the complex. Overall, the RMSD results of the protein (MAO‐B) and ligand complex showed minor fluctuation during the simulations (Figure 12). Initially, the protein was achieved equilibrium phase at 0 to 25 ns, after that a steady state was observed throughout simulation, showing a negligible fluctuation between 50 to 200 ns. The RMSD of Cα was observed to be 2.0 to 2.8 Å throughout the simulation. A minor of the fluctuation was evaluated in T6 at 2.5 to 4.0 Å between 100 to 125 ns. In contrast, the safinamide complex, despite initial alignment at 0 to 25 ns, a major of the fluctuation was observed at 28 ns with 3.2 Å during the simulation. This fluctuation indicates the moderate degree of flexibility with safinamide complex. Consequently, the RMSD of the complex presented by MAO‐B and its complex with T6 underwent no notable conformational changes during the simulation, suggesting that the complex has stability compared to the safinamide complex.

Conclusion

A series of twenty semicarbazone and thiosemicarbazone derivatives (T1–T20) was synthesized and evaluated for inhibitory activities against MAOs and ChEs. Most of the compounds displayed higher selectivity toward MAO‐B, with T6 (IC50 = 6.45 µM) and T17 (IC50 = 9.46 µM) emerging as the most potent inhibitors, exhibiting favorable selectivity indices compared with MAO‐A. Structure–activity relationship analysis revealed that thiosemicarbazones generally possessed higher MAO‐B inhibitory potential than their corresponding semicarbazones, particularly when the terminal –NH2 group was free. The synthesized compounds weakly inhibited AChE, whereas T3 and T12 showed moderate inhibition of BChE. Docking studies further supported the binding interactions of the most promising compounds with MAO‐B, highlighting their potential as scaffolds for the design of selective MAO‐B inhibitors. Overall, thiosemicarbazone derivatives especially T6 and T17, emerge as promising lead candidates and merit further investigation for the development of novel therapeutics targeting MAO‐B–associated disorders.
