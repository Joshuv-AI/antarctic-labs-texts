Probiotic potential of Companilactobacillus alimentarius Y4 in alleviating ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella-induced intestinal inflammation through microbiota–metabolite modulation
Authors: Tajin Wang, Shuo Wang, Tao Wan, Qi Cui, Lili Guan, Chengguang He, Jingrui Chen, Lingcong Kong, Haipeng Zhang, Hongxia Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The emergence of ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella poses a significant challenge to antimicrobial therapy. Probiotics that inhibit ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella offer a safer alternative with fewer side effects than conventional antibiotics. In this study, a strain of Companilactobacillus alimentarius Y4, isolated from kimchi, exhibited strong antibacterial activity against Salmonella C1, Escherichia coli, and other intestinal pathogens. Biological characterization showed that C. alimentarius Y4 maintained a relative growth rate of 80% at pH 3.0 and 87.13% in the presence of 0.3% bile salts. It retained over 98.8% antibacterial activity even after exposure to pepsin, trypsin, protease K, and catalase. Additionally, C. alimentarius Y4 downregulated the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, which may contribute to alleviating intestinal injury induced by ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella C1. Gut microbiota analysis revealed that C. alimentarius Y4 colonized the intestine and increased the abundance of beneficial gut microbes, including Lachnospiraceae bacterium COE1 and Muribaculaceae bacterium CAG-485. Metabolomic profiling further indicated that C. alimentarius Y4 upregulated metabolites such as L-leucine, L-lysine, and tryptophan, contributing to the restoration of intestinal metabolic balance. Collectively, C. alimentarius Y4 may mitigate intestinal inflammation by regulating cytokine expression, repairing mucosal damage, rebalancing gut microbiota composition, and restoring metabolic homeostasis. These findings indicate that C. alimentarius Y4 may contribute to alleviating intestinal inflammatory damage caused by multidrug-resistant Salmonella infection, suggesting its potential as a promising probiotic candidate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04920-0.

Introduction

Salmonella enterica of non-typhoid serotypes is one of the most important zoonotic pathogens, causing bacterial gastroenteritis in humans primarily through the consumption of contaminated food. It is also the leading cause of foodborne illness worldwide [1]. The treatment of salmonellosis in both humans and animals often relies on the use of antibiotics, with broad-spectrum antibiotics typically used for highly susceptible individuals or those with clinical complications [2, 3]. In recent years, fluoroquinolones, particularly ciprofloxacin, and third-generation cephalosporins have become the drugs of choice for treating Salmonella infections. However, non-typhoidal Salmonella enterica strains have increasingly developed resistance to these commonly used antibiotics. Alarmingly, fluoroquinolone-resistant Salmonella strains have been listed among the World Health Organization’s 12 “priority pathogens” that pose the greatest threat to human health. This underscores the urgent need for novel, effective alternatives to antibiotics for controlling Salmonella infections in both humans and animals [4, 5].

The human gut, a central component of the digestive system, hosts the body’s largest and most complex microbial ecosystem. Probiotics are widely recognized for their ability to alleviate and prevent gastrointestinal infections and for their essential role in maintaining gut health [6]. These beneficial microorganisms can colonize the intestine and interact with the native microbiota, thereby modulating its composition and promoting a host-friendly intestinal environment in both humans and animals [7]. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) are among the most common probiotics. They are known for their numerous health benefits, including the regulation of intestinal function, prevention of gastrointestinal infections, modulation of immune responses, and reduction of the incidence of respiratory infections [8]. In recent years, numerous studies have explored how LAB improve gut microbiota composition. For instance, Xu Feng et al.demonstrated that Lactobacillus plantarum effectively inhibits alcohol-induced intestinal damage and endotoxemia [9]. Similarly, Bian et al.found that supplementation with Pediococcus pentosaceus LI05 altered the gut microbiota of mice [10]. Collectively, growing evidence from both murine and human studies indicates that probiotic strains can effectively regulate intestinal function and mitigate intestinal disorders [11].

In this study, a strain of Companilactobacillus alimentarius Y4 (C. alimentarius Y4) exhibiting strong antibacterial activity against Ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella C1 was isolated and identified from kimchi. To date, the biological characteristics of C. alimentarius have not been thoroughly investigated in vitro, and its probiotic potential remains largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the probiotic properties of C. alimentarius Y4 against Ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella C1 infection through both in vitro and in vivo experiments. Specifically, we examined its antibacterial spectrum, antibiotic susceptibility, gastrointestinal tolerance, and intestinal adhesion capacity. A mouse model of Salmonella C1 intestinal infection was established to assess the effects of C. alimentarius Y4 at varying doses on serum inflammatory response, gut microbiota composition, and metabolite profiles. This study provides a theoretical basis for the clinical development of C. alimentarius Y4 as a functional probiotic for managing intestinal inflammation associated with antibiotic-resistant Salmonella infections.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of LAB strain

C. alimentarius Y4 was isolated from pickle samples using the following procedure. Pickle samples were thoroughly homogenized and serially diluted in 10-fold increments using sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The dilutions were spread onto de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe(MRS) agar plates (Qingdao Rishui Bio Technologies Co., Ltd) and incubated anaerobically at 28 °C for 48 h [12]. A single white colony was selected and inoculated into 5 mL of MRS broth, followed by incubation at 28 °C for 48 h. The culture was then mixed with 80% glycerol and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent use. Then, genomic DNA was extracted using a commercial Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). The 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using universal bacterial primers 27 F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5′-TACGGCTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) [13]. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) products were sequenced by Sangon Biotech (Changchun, China), and the resulting sequences were compared with reference sequences in the NCBI database using the nucleotide BLAST tool (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi). A phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA version 10.1.8 using the neighbor-joining algorithm and visualized using iTOL (Letunic and Bork, 2021).

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

The C. alimentarius Y4 strain stored at − 80 °C was revived by streaking on MRS agar and incubating anaerobically at 28 °C for 48 h. A single colony was then inoculated into 5 mL of MRS broth and incubated under the same conditions for 48 h. Twelve indicator bacterial strains (Table 1), including Escherichia coli B2 (resistant to polymyxin and carbapenems), E. coli K88, Salmonella H9812, and Salmonella C1(resistant to ciprofloxacin), were used in this study. These strains were cultured on Luria-Bertani (LB) agar at 37 °C for 24 h. Individual colonies were subsequently inoculated into 5 mL of fresh LB broth and incubated with shaking at 37 °C for 24 h. For subsequent experiments, actively growing bacterial suspensions were prepared by transferring 1% (v/v) of the overnight culture indicator bacteria into 5 mL of fresh LB broth and incubating with continuous shaking at 37 °C until the logarithmic growth phase was reached.

In vitro studies on probiotic characteristics

Antimicrobial activity and growth kinetic curve assay

The antibacterial activity of C. alimentarius Y4 against indicator strains was assessed using the agar diffusion method described by Shao et al. [14], with some modifications. Briefly, C. alimentarius Y4 was cultured at 28℃ for 48 h, after which the bacterial suspension was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The cell-free supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone PES membrane filter (Jinteng Experimental Equipment, Tianjin, China). Indicator bacterial suspensions (10⁶ CFU/mL; Table 1) were spread evenly onto LB agar plates. Wells were then filled with aliquots of the C. alimentarius Y4 cell-free supernatant, and the plates were incubated at 37℃ for 12 h. The inhibitory effect was determined by measuring the diameter of the clear inhibition zones surrounding the wells. For growth kinetics analysis, a single colony of C. alimentarius Y4 was inoculated into MRS broth and cultured anaerobically at 28 °C until the OD600 reached 0.5. The culture was then transferred (1%, v/v) into 300 mL of fresh MRS broth and incubated anaerobically at 28 °C for 48 h. During incubation, samples were withdrawn at 2-hour intervals, and the OD600 was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) to construct the growth curve. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Relative growth under acid stress

The relative growth of C. alimentarius Y4 at pH = 3.0 and pH = 6.5 (control) was assessed according to the method of Hossain et al. [15], with slight modifications. Briefly, overnight cultures of C. alimentarius Y4 were centrifuged at 8,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C and washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.0). Equal amounts of bacterial cells were resuspended in MRS broth, and the pH was adjusted to 3.0 and 6.5, respectively. The suspensions were incubated at 28 °C, and optical density at 600 nm was measured after 2, 3, and 4 h of incubation to assess acid tolerance. MRS broth at pH 6.5 containing bacterial cells served as the positive control (N), while cell-free MRS broth was used as the negative control. Bile salt resistance was evaluated according to the method described by Eman et al. [16]., with slight modifications. The cultures were then incubated at 28℃, and the OD600 was measured after 2, 3, and 4 h of incubation to assess bacterial survival under bile salt stress. Antibiotic susceptibility

The antibiotic susceptibility test was performed according to the method described by Maquiné et al. [17]. The disc diffusion method was used to assess susceptibility to 14 antibiotics: amoxicillin (10 µg/disc), gentamicin (10 µg/disc), ampicillin (10 µg/disc), vancomycin (30 µg/disc), doxycycline (30 µg/disc), ciprofloxacin (5 µg/disc), oxacillin (1 µg/disc), neomycin (30 µg/disc), florfenicol (30 µg/disc), streptomycin (10 µg/disc), rifampicin (5 µg/disc), lincomycin (2 µg/disc), ceftazidime clavulanic acid (30/10 µg/disc), and cefoperazone (75 µg/disc). Antibiotic tablets were placed on MRS agar containing C. alimentarius Y4 (106 CFU/mL) and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. The diameters of the inhibition zones around the antibiotic discs were measured, and the susceptibility of the C. alimentarius Y4 strain was classified as resistant (R), intermediate (I), or susceptible (S) based on the standard breakpoints established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). All experiments were repeated at least three times.

Safety assessment

The hemolytic activity of C. alimentarius Y4 was evaluated following the method described by Lin et al. [18], with slight modifications. Briefly, C. alimentarius Y4 was inoculated onto the surface of sheep blood agar plates (Beijing Land Bridge Technology Co., Ltd) using a sterile inoculation loop and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Hemolytic activity was determined by examining the appearance of halos around the colonies: colonies surrounded by a greenish halo were classified as exhibiting α-hemolysis, those with a clear halo as β-hemolysis, and those without visible changes in the medium as γ-hemolysis. Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 was used as a positive control strain.

Proteolytic enzyme stability

The stability of the antimicrobial substances produced by C. alimentarius Y4 was evaluated following the method described by Zhang et al. [19], with slight modifications. Briefly, aliquots of the C. alimentarius Y4 cell-free supernatant were treated with proteinase K, catalase, trypsin, or pepsin (each at a final concentration of 1 mg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min. In addition, a heat treatment was applied by exposing the supernatant to 100℃ for 10 min [17]. The untreated cell-free supernatant served as the control. After cooling to room temperature, the residual antimicrobial activity was assessed using the agar diffusion assay with Salmonella C1 as the indicator strain. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Cell surface hydrophobicity

Hydrophobicity was assessed using the method described by Gracy et al. [20], with slight modifications. Briefly, C. alimentarius Y4 was inoculated into MRS broth and incubated overnight at 28 °C. The culture was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the cell-free supernatant was discarded. The bacterial pellet was washed three times with PBS (pH 7.2) and resuspended in PBS to an OD600 of 0.5 (A0). Xylene was added to the bacterial suspension at a 1:3 (v/v) ratio, followed by vortexing for 2 min. The mixture was then incubated at 28 ± 1 °C for 40 min under aerobic conditions. After phase separation, the aqueous layer was carefully collected, and its absorbance at OD600 was measured (A₁). The percentage of cell surface hydrophobicity was calculated using the following equation:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Hydrophobicity(\%)=\left(1-\frac{A_1}{A_0}\right)\%$$\end{document}

Autoagglutination assay

The autoagglutination ability of C. alimentarius Y4 was assessed using the method described by Abbasiliasi et al. [21], with slight modifications. The initial absorbance OD600 of the suspension was recorded as B₀. The suspension was then vortexed for 10 s and allowed to stand undisturbed at room temperature for 4 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of the upper cell-free supernatant was measured at OD600 and recorded as B₁. The experiment was conducted in triplicate. Animal experiments

Experimental animals and ethical statement

Six-week-old Kunming mice (KM mice), weighing 18–22 g, were purchased from Changchun Changsheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were maintained under controlled environmental conditions at 23 ± 3 °C, humidity of 50% ± 10%, and a 12-h light/dark cycle [22]. All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Jilin Agricultural University (protocol code: 20230317002; approval date: March 17, 2023). All animal experiments, including euthanasia procedures, were conducted per the regulations and guidelines of the Institute of Animal Management, Jilin Agricultural University, Changchun, China. Prior to euthanasia, mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) until complete loss of consciousness and absence of response to toe pinch were confirmed. Euthanasia was then performed by cervical dislocation to ensure rapid death and minimize pain or distress.

Experimental scheme

Sixty mice were randomly allocated to six groups (n = 10 per group; five males and five females): the negative control (NC), positive control (PC), modeling group (STM), low-dose C. alimentarius Y4 (LY4), medium-dose C. alimentarius Y4 (MY4), and high-dose C. alimentarius Y4 (HY4) groups. Following a one-week acclimatization period, all groups except the NC group were orally administered Salmonella C1 (1 × 10⁹ CFU in 200 µL) once daily for seven consecutive days to induce intestinal infection. Mice in the NC group received an equivalent volume of PBS by gavage. On day 7, successful infection in the STM group was confirmed. After a 12 h fast, all mice in the STM group were euthanized, and blood and cecal contents were aseptically collected and stored at − 80 °C. The remaining groups then entered the experimental phase. From day 8 to day 28, C. alimentarius Y4 suspensions were administered via gastric gavage at 200 µL/day. The specific treatment plan was as follows: The NC group received PBS, and the PC group was given 1 × 108 CFU of Live BIFICO (a mixture of Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Enterococcus Capsules, Shangyao Xinyi Pharmaceutical, Shanghai, China) [23]. The experimental groups received C. alimentarius Y4 suspension at low (1 × 107 CFU), medium (1 × 108 CFU), and high (1 × 109 CFU) doses by intragastric administration. Cecal contents were collected on days 14 and 28. Throughout the experiment, diet composition, housing conditions, and feeding schedules remained constant. Before euthanasia, blood and fecal samples from the mice in each group were aseptically collected. Feces were suspended in sterile saline for the quantification of viable lactic acid bacteria. The activity, behavior, and overall health of mice were monitored daily, while body weight, water intake, and food consumption were recorded every other day (Fig. 1) [24].

Histopathological analysis

Histopathological evaluation of cecal tissues was performed following the method of Shi et al. [25, 26]. Briefly, samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), mounted, and examined under a light microscope. The thickness of the mucosal layer and the depth of intestinal glands were quantified using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Measurements were taken five times in different visual fields at magnifications of 100× and 200×, and the results were expressed in millimeters.

Cytokine assays

On days 21 and 28, mice were fasted for 12 h, and blood samples were collected from the orbital sinus. The samples were centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and the resulting serum was stored at − 80 °C. Serum levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-10 (IL-10) were determined using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Jiangsu Kete Biological Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Gene sequencing analysis of 16 S rRNA

On day 28, cecal contents were collected from three mice per group for gut microbiota analysis using 16 S rRNA sequencing technology [27]. Total DNA was extracted using a DNA extraction kit (M5635-02, Omega Bio-Tek, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The integrity of the extracted DNA was verified by 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified by PCR and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using the SP Reagent Kit. Bioinformatic analyses of the sequencing data were performed using QIIME2 version 2019.4, following the official tutorial with necessary modifications to improve the analysis process. Sparse curves were generated to evaluate whether the sequencing depth was sufficient to cover all groups. Species richness was assessed using the Simpson index. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) was performed based on weighted Unifrac distances between samples. Biomarker identification was conducted using operational taxonomic unit (OTU)–based analyses and visualized through Venn diagrams. The taxonomic composition of each sample was determined at different classification levels, and relative abundance variations among groups were graphically illustrated.

Non-targeted metabolomic analysis of intestinal bacteria

On day 28, cecal contents were collected from three randomly selected mice per group for non-targeted metabolomic profiling [28]. Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis was performed by Pasenno Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) following a previously described method [29]. The analysis was conducted using an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 chromatographic column (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm×100 mm) coupled to a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer. Raw LC–MS/MS data were converted to mzXML format using ProteoWizard 3.0.8789 software, and subsequent peak alignment, retention time correction, and peak area extraction were conducted with XCMS (version 3.12.0). Metabolite identification and data preprocessing were performed using XCMS outputs, and data quality was verified before downstream analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and multivariate statistical analysis were conducted using the R package ropls to identify differential metabolites based on the criteria of fold change (FC) > 1 and p < 0.05. Identified metabolites were annotated by querying the Features, Metabolites, and Human Metabolome Database (HMDB). Associated metabolic pathways were determined using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). Correlation analysis was applied to assess data reliability and confirm that results met quality control requirements.

Correlation analysis of non-targeted metabolomics with 16S rRNA sequencing of gut microbiota

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between metabolomic profiles and microbial abundance were calculated using Mothur software (v1.46.1; University of Michigan, USA; https://mothur.org/). The correlation coefficient (ρ) ranges from − 1 to 1, where values between − 1 and 0 indicate a negative monotonic correlation, and values between 0 and 1 indicate a positive monotonic correlation between variables. A ρ value of 0 indicates no monotonic association. The p-value represents the statistical significance of each correlation, with smaller p-values reflecting higher confidence in the observed relationship. Finally, heat maps were generated using R software to visualize correlations between metabolomic features and the relative abundance of gut microbial taxa.

Statistical analysis

All data were statistically analyzed using SPSS Statistics 17.0 (IBM, USA) and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences among groups were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 and highly significant at p < 0.01.

Results

Identification and growth characteristics of C. alimentarius Y4

A bacterial strain, designated Y4, exhibiting strong antibacterial activity against ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella C1, was isolated from fermented pickles. Morphological observation showed that strain Y4 formed white, round colonies and was identified as a Gram-positive, acid-producing bacterium (Supplementary Fig. 1). Phylogenetic analysis of its 16 S rRNA gene sequence revealed 100% similarity with C. alimentarius DSM 20,249 (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Antimicrobial spectrum and growth kinetic curve

The antimicrobial activity of the C. alimentarius Y4 supernatant was tested against 12 common pathogenic bacteria (Table 1). The inhibition zone diameter against Pseudomonas aeruginosa Z1, Salmonella H9812, Acinetobacter baumannii C1, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), Escherichia coli K88, and Salmonella C1 ranged from 26 to 32 mm. For Shigella castellani Z1, Acinetobacter baumannii JS1, Enterococcus faecalis, and Staphylococcus aureus 25923, the diameter of the inhibition zone ranged from 21 to 24 mm. These results indicate that C. alimentarius Y4 has broad-spectrum antibacterial activity against both Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, with particularly strong inhibition of enteric pathogens such as E. coli and Salmonella. The growth kinetic analysis (Fig. 2A) showed that C. alimentarius Y4 entered the logarithmic growth phase at approximately 6 h and reached a stationary phase around 20 h. During the logarithmic phase, the culture pH declined rapidly, reaching approximately 4.79 at 20 h, which suggests substantial acid production. The pH stabilized after 24 h, indicating the completion of active fermentation and acid accumulation. (Fig. 2A).

Resistance of C. alimentarius Y4 to simulated gastrointestinal conditions

The tolerance of C. alimentarius Y4 to simulated gastrointestinal environments was evaluated under acidic and bile salt conditions. After 4 h of incubation at pH 3.0, the strain maintained a relative growth rate of 80%, indicating good acid resistance. Bile salts are known to disrupt bacterial cell membranes and adversely affect viability [30]. Therefore, assessing bile tolerance is essential for predicting probiotic colonization and persistence in the host intestine. Experimental results demonstrated that C. alimentarius Y4 exhibited strong tolerance to 0.3% bile salts after 4 h of incubation, maintaining a relative growth rate of 87.13%. These findings suggest that C. alimentarius Y4 possesses sufficient acid and bile resistance to survive passage through the gastrointestinal tract.

Antibiotic sensitivity and hemolytic activity

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of C. alimentarius Y4 against 14 antibiotics is presented in Table 2. The strain was resistant to oxacillin, ceftazidime-clavulanic acid, gentamicin, neomycin, vancomycin, and streptomycin. Whereas it exhibited moderate sensitivity to ciprofloxacin and was susceptible to florfenicol, doxycycline, carbenicillin, amoxicillin, cefoperazone, lincomycin, and rifampicin. Hemolytic activity testing revealed that, unlike the Staphylococcus aureus positive control exhibiting clear hemolysis, C. alimentarius Y4 displayed γ-hemolysis, indicating the absence of hemolytic activity and confirming its safety as a non-hemolytic probiotic candidate (Fig. 2B and C).

Proteolytic enzyme stability, cell surface hydrophobicity, and autoagglutination assay

The stability of C. alimentarius Y4 antibacterial activity following proteolytic enzyme treatment is shown in Table 3. Enzymatic digestion exerted minimal influence on the antibacterial efficacy of the C. alimentarius Y4 fermentation supernatant. Among, the untreated control group showed 100% activity, while residual antibacterial activities following catalase, trypsin, pepsin, and protease K treatment were 98.8 ± 0.24%, 98.8 ± 0.15%, 98.8 ± 0.20%, and 100 ± 0.41%, respectively. These findings indicate that C. alimentarius Y4 indicate a high potential for adhesion to intestinal epithelial surfaces.

Effects of C. alimentarius Y4 on clinical manifestations and intestinal colonization in mice

After infection with Salmonella C1, the mice developed varying degrees of diarrhea, depression, weight loss, loss of appetite, and increased water intake (Fig. 3A and B). By the end of the modeling period, the diarrhea rate had reached 100%, confirming the successful establishment of the Salmonella C1 mouse model. It should be noted that all mice in the STM group were euthanized and samples were collected on Day 7 post-infection. Therefore, all subsequent comparisons involving the STM group including clinical symptoms, histopathology, inflammatory cytokines, gut microbiota, and metabolites were based on the acute infection baseline of the same-day group rather than a true natural recovery control group at Day 28. During the treatment period, mice treated C. alimentarius Y4 or the probiotic control Bifico (PC group) showed progressive alleviation of diarrhea symptoms, improved appetite, and significant recovery of body weight. Among the treatment groups, those receiving MY4 group and HY4 group doses of C. alimentarius Y4 exhibited the most pronounced weight gain compared with the negative NC group. Fecal samples collected from each group were analyzed for viable LAB counts (Fig. 3C). On day 14, LAB counts in the NC, HY4, MY4, LY4, and PC groups were 7.84 ± 0.0031, 8.22 ± 0.013, 8.54 ± 0.039, 8.07 ± 0.058, and 8.07 ± 0.058 log₁₀ CFU/mL, respectively. By day 21, corresponding values increased to 7.81 ± 0.0033, 8.68 ± 0.33, 8.72 ± 0.045, 8.58 ± 0.1938, and 8.49 ± 0.0056 log₁₀ CFU/mL, respectively. Compared with the NC and PC groups, all C. alimentarius Y4-treated groups (LY4, MY4, and HY4) exhibited higher LAB counts, indicating effective colonization and proliferation of C. alimentarius Y4 within the intestinal tract.

Effect of C. alimentarius Y4 on cecal tissues

The effects of C. alimentarius Y4 on cecal tissues are shown in Fig. 3D and E. The cecal tissue in the NC group displayed normal histological architecture. In contrast, on the 7th day after infection, the STM group exhibited severe pathological changes, including extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, mucosal and submucosal edema, atrophy or shedding of intestinal mucosal epithelial cells, and significant thickening of both the mucosal layer and intestinal gland depth. On day 28, following treatment with C. alimentarius Y4, the HY4, MY4, and LY4 groups showed varying degrees of recovery. However, mild inflammatory infiltration, edema, and limited epithelial atrophy or shedding persisted in the LY4 group. With increasing doses of C. alimentarius Y4, the thickness of the mucosal layer and the depth of intestinal glands decreased significantly. Notably, the cecal tissue in the HY4 group may exhibit a recovery pattern most comparable to that of the NC group, indicating a dose-dependent protective effect.

Effects of C. alimentarius Y4 on serum levels of inflammatory cytokines

The effects of C. alimentarius Y4 on serum inflammatory cytokines in Salmonella C1–infected mice are presented in Fig. 3H–K. Compared with the NC group, the STM group showed a significant decrease in the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (p < 0.05) and marked increases in the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α (p < 0.05). By day 14, treatment with C. alimentarius Y4 (LY4, MY4, and HY4 groups) and the PC group resulted in decreased serum levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, accompanied by increased IL-10 levels. Interestingly, compared with the PC group, the HY4, MY4, and LY4 groups showed significantly lower levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (p < 0.005). By day 21, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels in the MY4 and LY4 groups were significantly lower than those in both the NC and PC groups (p < 0.05), indicating that C. alimentarius Y4 treatment may have a sustained anti-inflammatory effect.

Effect of C. alimentarius Y4 on the composition and diversity of gut microbiota

To evaluate the effect of C. alimentarius Y4 on the intestinal microbiota of mice, MiSeq 16 S rRNA sequencing was performed on microbial DNA extracted from intestinal contents. Multiple sequences were randomly selected from each sample, and the number of species represented by these sequences was determined. A dilution curve was then constructed based on the number of sequences and species. As shown in Fig. 4A, the curve increased sharply at first and gradually plateaued, indicating high microbial diversity in the mouse intestines and sufficient sequencing depth to capture the majority of bacterial species. A Venn diagram (Fig. 4B) was used to illustrate the number of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) across the five groups. A total of 271 species were shared among all groups. The numbers of OTUs identified in the NC, STM, HY4, MY4, and LY4 groups were 1,559, 1,314, 1,536, 1,322, and 1,265, respectively. The NC, HY4, and MY4 groups exhibited higher OTU counts than the STM group, indicating greater microbial diversity. Notably, no significant difference in OTU numbers was observed between the HY4 and NC groups. Bray–Curtis distance analysis revealed significant differences in microbial community structure among the groups. The STM group showed a distinct separation from the NC group, confirming the successful establishment of the infection model. Treatment with C. alimentarius Y4 partially restored the microbial community structure toward that of the NC group (Supplementary Fig. 3A).

At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were dominant across all groups. Compared with the NC group, the STM group showed an increase in Firmicutes and a decrease in Bacteroidetes, resulting in an elevated Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio. In addition, Salmonella C1 infection increased the relative abundance of Deferribacterota while reducing Actinobacteriota. Treatment with C. alimentarius Y4 decreased the relative abundance of Firmicutes and increased Bacteroidetes in the HY4 and MY4 groups (Fig. 4D). At the genus level, Salmonella infection increased the relative abundance of Duncaniella and Alistipes while decreasing COE1 (an unclassified genus within Lachnospiraceae) and CAG-485 (an unclassified genus within Muribaculaceae). Following treatment with C. alimentarius Y4, the relative abundances of Duncaniella and Alistipes decreased, whereas those of Muribaculum COE1 and CAG-485 increased in the HY4, MY4, and LY4 groups (Fig. 4C). Further analysis using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe; LDA > 4) identified 10, 20, 2, 11, and 9 microbial biomarkers in the NC, STM, HY4, MY4, and LY4 groups, respectively (Fig. 4E and F). In the STM group, dominant taxa included p-Firmicutes A, c-Clostridia, and o-Oscillospirales. In the HY4 group, the enriched taxa were c-saccharimonadia and o-Erysipelotrichales. The MY4 group showed enrichment in p-Firmicutes D, c-Bacilli, and o-Lactobacillales, while the LY4 group was dominated by f-Bacteroidaceae and f-Lachnospiraceae.

Analysis of metabolites in the cecum

To further investigate the differences in cecal content metabolites among the NC, STM, and experimental groups, LC-MS/MS was employed to determine and analyze the metabolites in the cecal contents. A total of 2,884 distinct gut microbiota–derived metabolites were identified. Most of these metabolites belonged to lipids and lipid-like molecules (39.1%), organic acids and derivatives (25.2%), organoheterocyclic compounds (11.9%), and benzenoids (8.6%) (Fig. 5A). PCA was used to evaluate the overall metabolite differences among the NC, STM, LY4, MY4, and HY4 (Fig. 5B and C). In positive and negative ion modes, the two principal components, PC1 (Principal Component 1) and PC2 (Principal Component 2), accounted for 43.2% and 15.1% of the total variance, respectively. The NC and STM groups showed distinct clustering and separation along the PC2 axis, indicating significant metabolic differences following Salmonella infection. Volcano plots were generated to visualize differential metabolite changes. Compared with the NC group, the STM group exhibited 186 upregulated and 471 downregulated metabolites (p < 0.05, fold change > 1) (Fig. 5D). After C. alimentarius Y4 treatment, the HY4 group showed 490 upregulated and 195 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5E), the MY4 group had 464 upregulated and 182 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5F), and the LY4 group displayed 578 upregulated and 269 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 5G).

Discussion

The growing incidence of drug-resistant Salmonella infections resulting from frequent antibiotic use has drawn considerable attention. Probiotics have emerged as promising alternatives because they enhance host defense against gastrointestinal pathogens, restore gut microbiota balance, and modulate immune function [31, 32]. LAB is recommended as a sustainable alternative to antibiotics. In this study, C. alimentarius Y4—a strain isolated from kimchi that exhibits strong inhibitory activity against E. coli B2, Salmonella H9812, E. coli K88, and Salmonella C1—was evaluated for its biological characteristics and therapeutic effects in a mouse model of intestinal infection. Notably, no prior studies have investigated the immunomodulatory potential of this bacterium.

Probiotic tolerance to acidic and bile conditions is a key indicator of gastrointestinal survival [33, 34]. Since food typically passes through the small intestine within four hours, where the average bile salt concentration is about 0.3%, tolerance to these conditions is crucial for colonization and functionality [35]. Under pH 3.0 and 0.3% bile salt conditions, C. alimentarius Y4 maintained relative growth rates of 80% and 87.13%, respectively, demonstrating strong acid and bile resistance. Furthermore, probiotic high self-aggregation and hydrophobicity suggest a strong capacity to adhere to intestinal epithelial cells, supporting its potential to colonize and exert probiotic effects [36, 37]. Our study found that C. alimentarius Y4 showed good hydrophobicity and self-aggregation ability, suggesting that it can effectively colonize the gastrointestinal tract and perform probiotic functions. In addition, proteolytic enzyme stability tests demonstrated that C. alimentarius Y4 retained its antibacterial activity after hydrolysis by proteinase K, pepsin, catalase, and trypsin. Considering the safety evaluation required for probiotic candidates, the antibiotic susceptibility of C. alimentarius Y4 was assessed against 14 antibiotics [38]. The strain Y4 was found to be sensitive to florfenicol, carbenicillin, cefoperazone, lincomycin, and rifampicin, indicating that C. alimentarius Y4 can be regarded as a safe probiotic strain.

In this study, infection with Salmonella C1 induced characteristic symptoms such as weight loss, reduced food intake, and cecal tissue damage, confirming the successful establishment of a diarrhea model caused by ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella in mice [39]. Administration of C. alimentarius Y4 may contribute to the mitigation of Salmonella-associated intestinal pathology, which is consistent with the findings reported by Liu et al. [40], who reported that C. alimentarius Y4 can mitigate Salmonella-associated intestinal pathology. Fecal colony counts revealed that on days 14 and 21, intestinal LAB levels in C. alimentarius Y4-treated mice were significantly higher than those in the NC group. Notably, the HY4 group exhibited greater interindividual variation (8.68 ± 0.33 log₁₀ CFU/mL) on day 21, a common observation in probiotic research that may reflect host-specific differences in intestinal colonization resistance. Although this variability underscores the complexity of host–microbe interactions, C. alimentarius Y4 consistently promoted LAB proliferation across all treatment groups. This observation aligns with findings by Tannock GW et al. [41], who demonstrated that probiotic colonization improves following recovery from intestinal inflammation, further supporting the strong colonization potential of C. alimentarius Y4. Serum cytokine levels are closely correlated with mucosal inflammation and are useful biomarkers for evaluating intestinal disease [42]. In this study, C. alimentarius Y4 treatment significantly reduced serum levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β and increased the level of the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, indicating a marked anti-inflammatory effect. These results are consistent with those of Li et al. [43], who demonstrated that LAB could alleviate pathogen-induced intestinal inflammation in mice by decreasing the levels of intestinal pro-inflammatory response and increasing the levels of anti-inflammatory response. It should be noted that all mice in the STM group were sampled on day 7 to confirm acute Salmonella infection, whereas C. alimentarius Y4-treated groups were evaluated after continuous intervention during the recovery phase. Therefore, the reduced cytokine levels observed in the treatment groups likely reflect both the natural resolution of infection and the immunomodulatory effects of C. alimentarius Y4. Importantly, the LY4, MY4, and HY4 groups—especially the HY4 group—exhibited more pronounced reductions in pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α and greater increases in IL-10 compared with the PC group, suggesting that higher doses of C. alimentarius Y4 may enhance systemic immune regulation. Moreover, extended C. alimentarius Y4 administration produced time-dependent improvements in the cytokine balance, with IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-10 showing significant modulation, while TNF-α exhibited a less pronounced trend. These findings indicate that sustained C. alimentarius Y4 treatment facilitates a more stable restoration of intestinal immune homeostasis.

The intestinal microbiota is a critical component of intestinal defense, playing an essential role in nutrient absorption, immune regulation, and maintenance of the mucosal barrier [44]. Disruption of the gut microbiota contributes to the development of various inflammatory diseases by impairing immune responses [45]. Invasive pathogens can disturb the microbial ecosystem and compromise the intestinal barrier, leading to diarrhea [46]. Restoring microbial balance is therefore critical for alleviating such symptoms. In this study, the impact of C. alimentarius Y4 on the gut microbiota of Salmonella C1–infected mice was investigated. Treatment with C. alimentarius Y4 may contribute to restoration of microbial diversity in infected mice, as evidenced in the LY4, MY4, and HY4 groups, where gut microbial richness and evenness improved markedly. At the phylum level, C. alimentarius Y4 administration decreased the relative abundance of Firmicutes and Deferribacterota, while increasing the proportion of Bacteroidetes. This shift suggests that C. alimentarius Y4 supplementation contributes to reestablishing a healthy microbial balance. Moreover, Salmonella C1 infection delayed the natural transition of dominant gut bacteria from Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes, whereas C. alimentarius Y4 treatment reduced the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio. This finding is consistent with Li et al. [47], who reported that a decreased F/B ratio is indicative of restored intestinal homeostasis [48]. At the genus level, the LAB richness in the MY4 group was significantly higher than in the NC, LY4, and STM groups. Notably, the abundances of COE1 and CAG-485 significantly increased (p < 0.05). These findings are consistent with those of Zhao et al. [49], who demonstrated that CAG-485 is associated with gut homeostasis and the production of anti-inflammatory metabolites such as butyrate. Furthermore, Zhao et al. [50] reported that Prevotellaceae_NK3B31, a member of the COE1 genus, can alleviate intestinal inflammation in mice. Taken together, these results suggest that C. alimentarius Y4 may contribute to enhances the abundance of beneficial bacteria in the intestines of Salmonella-infected mice, thereby contributing to microbial and immune restoration.

An imbalance in gut microbiota significantly impacts the host’s metabolic phenotype and can lead to various diseases [51]. Metabolomics provides valuable insights into metabolic alterations and therapeutic effects during intestinal infection. In this study, LC-MS/MS analysis was employed to evaluate the effects of C. alimentarius Y4 on intestinal metabolites across the NC, STM, and treatment groups. Multivariate statistical PCA analysis indicated strong data reproducibility and clear separation between the control and treatment groups in both positive and negative ionization modes. Compared with the NC group, the STM group exhibited substantial alterations in the composition and abundance of cecal metabolites, consistent with the findings of Leleiwi et al. [52], who found that Salmonella infection disrupts gut microbiota and metabolism by altering the composition of metabolites in the colon contents of mice. Among the altered pathways, the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, amino acid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathways were identified as key processes in maintaining gut homeostasis. ABC transporters, constituting one of the largest families of transmembrane transporters, are present in all organisms and are involved in the transport and accumulation of various substances, including the excretion of compounds that interfere with cell growth [53]. Both amino acid biosynthesis and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathways are particularly critical during infection, as they influence nutrient competition, immune modulation, and the maintenance of intestinal integrity [54, 55]. Notably, C. alimentarius Y4 appeared to restore intestinal metabolic balance primarily through modulation of these pathways. Damage to the intestinal mucosal barrier is a key factor in the development of intestinal inflammation. Amino acids, an essential nutrient for intestinal mucosal cells, contribute to the synthesis and repair of tight junction proteins, effectively preventing the translocation of harmful substances like bacteria and toxins into the submucosa and reducing inflammation, and promoting mucus secretion [56]. In this study, differential metabolite and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses revealed that C. alimentarius Y4 restored intestinal metabolite balance by upregulating amino acids such as L-leucine, L-lysine, and tryptophan. These findings align with previous reports that tryptophan regulates intestinal immune responses through microbial metabolism, alleviating inflammatory bowel disease, while L-lysine plays a critical role in rebalancing the Treg/Th17 response and repairing mucosal barrier damage [57]. In addition, Zhao et al. [58] demonstrated that dietary L-leucine enhances intestinal barrier function in hybrid catfish by improving humoral immunity, antioxidant capacity, and tight junction protein expression, consistent with the upregulation of L-leucine observed in this study. In summary, C. alimentarius Y4 may appear to enhance the abundance of beneficial genera such as COE1 and CAG-485 and to upregulate metabolites including L-leucine, L-lysine, and tryptophan through its involvement in ABC transporter activity, amino acid biosynthesis, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathways, thereby alleviating Salmonella-induced intestinal inflammation and promoting gut homeostasis. However, one recognized limitation of this study is the absence of a “natural recovery” control group at the 28-day sampling time point. As a result, the potential contribution of spontaneous host recovery over time could not be completely excluded, and the observed improvements cannot be unequivocally attributed solely to the C. alimentarius Y4 intervention. Our study only found that the magnitude and consistency of the improvements observed in mice treated with different doses of C. alimentarius Y4 support a beneficial role for this intervention.

Conclusions

In this study, C. alimentarius Y4, isolated from kimchi, exhibited strong antibacterial activity against enteric pathogens, including ciprofloxacin-resistant Salmonella C1. The strain demonstrated excellent acid and bile salt tolerance, as well as strong hydrophobicity and self-aggregation capacity, suggesting high survivability and colonization potential in the gastrointestinal tract. In a mouse inflammatory model, C. alimentarius Y4 may contribute to reduced serum levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation. Moreover, treatment with C. alimentarius Y4 facilitated the restoration of intestinal microbial community structure and function, as well as the repair of intestinal metabolic disorders. These findings indicate that C. alimentarius Y4 is a promising probiotic candidate that may contribute to reduced gut inflammation caused by Salmonella infection. However, this study has certain limitations. We have only established the Salmonella infection model group (STM group) without setting up a natural recovery control group on day 28. As a result, we can only speculate that C. alimentarius Y4 at different doses may alleviate Salmonella-induced intestinal inflammation. In addition, in future animal model studies, a natural recovery control group should be established, and samples should be collected at the same time point for comparison to validate these findings. Collectively, this study provides a theoretical foundation for developing C. alimentarius Y4 as a functional probiotic and offers new insights into probiotic-based interventions for antibiotic-resistant enteric infections.
