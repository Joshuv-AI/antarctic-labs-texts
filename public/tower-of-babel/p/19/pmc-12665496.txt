Alloxan attenuates glucosamine-induced NF-κB activation and proinflammatory gene expression under normoglycemic conditions
Authors: Sang-Min Kim, Chanhaeng Lee, Dong Yeol Kim, Inn-Oc Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glucosamine (GlcN), a critical substrate in the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway, is known to modulate inflammatory responses in macrophages depending on extracellular glucose concentration. In hyperglycemic conditions (25 mM glucose), GlcN suppresses the production of nitric oxide (NO) and decreases the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS). Conversely, under normoglycemic conditions (5 mM glucose), GlcN paradoxically enhances lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced iNOS expression, NO production, and the upregulation of additional proinflammatory mediators. In this study, we examined the effect of alloxan, a known O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) inhibitor, on GlcN- and/or LPS-mediated inflammatory responses in RAW264.7 macrophage cells. Under hyperglycemic conditions, alloxan exhibited little effect on the LPS-induced or LPS plus GlcN-induced expression of iNOS, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). In contrast, under normoglycemic conditions, alloxan significantly inhibited the induction of these inflammatory genes in response to LPS plus GlcN. At the mechanistic level, alloxan reduced NF-κB DNA-binding activity and prevented its recruitment to the iNOS promoter. In addition, alloxan attenuated GlcN-induced increase in O-GlcNAcylation of the NF-κB subunit p65. Collectively, these results indicate that OGT-mediated O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB is critical for GlcN-induced proinflammatory signaling under normoglycemia. Our work highlights the glucose dependency of O-GlcNAc cycling in macrophage responses and provides new perspectives on the metabolic regulation of innate immune responses.

INTRODUCTION

Macrophages are pivotal effector cells of the innate immune system, serving essential functions in both the initiation and regulation of inflammatory responses via well-orchestrated and precisely regulated signaling cascades. Nutrient-sensing pathways are crucial in aligning cellular metabolism with immune activity (1), and the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP) has emerged as an important metabolic regulator that links nutrient status with inflammatory signaling in macrophages (2, 3). The HBP integrates glucose, amino acid, fatty acid, and nucleotide metabolism to produce essential glycosylation substrates (4, 5). An essential step within this pathway involves the conversion of fructose-6-phosphate and glutamine into glucosamine-6-phosphate, catalyzed by the rate-limiting enzyme glutamine:fructose-6-phosphate amidotransferase (GFAT) (3). Glucosamine-6-phosphate is subsequently metabolized to generate uridine diphosphate N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), which is the end product of the HBP and serves as the obligatory donor substrate for O-linked N-acetylglucosaminylation (O-GlcNAcylation) (3). O-GlcNAcylation constitutes a reversible and dynamic post-translational modification of nuclear and cytosolic proteins, modulating numerous cellular processes such as transcription, signal transduction, protein stability, and adaptation to cellular stress (6). Exogenous glucosamine (GlcN) bypasses the GFAT-catalyzed step, is taken up via GLUT-1, GLUT-2, and GLUT-4 transporters, and rapidly increases intracellular UDP-GlcNAc levels (7, 8). This GlcN-driven increase in O-GlcNAcylation has been demonstrated to influence immune signaling pathways by acting on essential regulatory proteins (9, 10).

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that GlcN exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects at both systemic and central nervous system contexts, predominantly through modulation of O-GlcNAc cycling (10-13). In a range of animal models of inflammatory diseases—such as rheumatoid arthritis, experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis, and sepsis—GlcN administration has been demonstrated to reduce disease progression, which is associated with the inhibition of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) activation and lower levels of proinflammatory cytokine production (11, 14, 15). In the central nervous system, GlcN treatment mitigates neuroinflammatory responses by suppressing microglial activation and decreasing the expression of proinflammatory factors, highlighting its potential in regulating neuroimmune function (12, 13, 16). At the cellular level, GlcN inhibits the nuclear translocation of NF-κB induced by interleukin-1β (IL-1β) in chondrocytes and suppresses the increase in nitric oxide (NO) production and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression stimulated by LPS in both macrophages and microglia (15, 17, 18). Mechanistic studies have shown that GlcN promotes O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB subunits, including c-Rel and p65, resulting in reduced DNA-binding capacity and transcriptional activity, which leads to downregulation of inflammatory molecules such as iNOS, cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) (19). Notably, emerging studies indicate that the immunomodulatory effects of GlcN are strongly influenced by the availability of extracellular glucose. While GlcN suppresses the expression of inflammatory mediators under hyperglycemic conditions (25 mM glucose), it paradoxically promotes LPS-induced production of NO, iNOS, and proinflammatory cytokines in a dose-dependent manner under normoglycemic conditions (5 mM glucose) in macrophage (20). This apparent dichotomy highlights a bidirectional regulatory function of O-GlcNAc cycling, whereby the equilibrium between O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT)-mediated addition and O-GlcNAcase (OGA)-mediated removal of O-GlcNAc modifications governs the overall inflammatory response. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms responsible for this glucose-dependent switch in GlcN’s immunomodulatory activity are still not fully elucidated.

Alloxan, originally identified as a uric-acid metabolite with oxidative β-cell toxicity, is also recognized as an OGT inhibitor that reduces global O-GlcNAcylation across a variety of cell types (21, 22). Although its diabetogenic properties through ROS generation and GLUT2-mediated transport have been thoroughly explored, the effects of alloxan’s O-GlcNAc inhibitory function on immune cell signaling remain inadequately understood. In this study, we examine the impact of alloxan on LPS- and LPS ＋ GlcN–stimulated inflammatory gene expression in RAW264.7 macrophages under normoglycemic and hyperglycemic conditions. Our data demonstrate that alloxan specifically eliminates the GlcN-enhanced upregulation of major proinflammatory mediators—including iNOS, COX-2, IL-6, and TNF-α—when cells are under normoglycemic conditions, but shows limited influence on macrophage responses to LPS alone or under hyperglycemic environments. Mechanistic analyses associate this inhibitory effect with reduced O-GlcNAcylation and diminished DNA-binding activity of the NF-κB subunit p65, highlighting the critical function of OGT activity in regulating macrophage activation according to metabolic context.

Taken together, these observations advance our understanding of glucose-regulated innate immune signaling mediated by O-GlcNAc cycling and emphasize the importance of NF-κB O-GlcNAcylation as a potential therapeutic target for controlling inflammatory responses.

RESULTS

Alloxan suppresses GlcN-enhanced inflammatory responses in macrophages under normoglycemic, but not hyperglycemic conditions

Our previous studies have shown that glucosamine (GlcN) exerts differential regulation of inflammatory responses in macrophages depending on glucose availability: under normoglycemic conditions (5 mM glucose), GlcN amplifies LPS-induced inflammatory responses, whereas under hyperglycemic conditions (25 mM glucose), it suppresses these responses (20). To determine whether inhibition of O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) by alloxan is involved in this context-specific modulation, we evaluated how alloxan affects GlcN-influenced nitric oxide (NO) production and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) expression in RAW264.7 macrophages. Cells were pretreated with GlcN (5 mM) or vehicle, followed by stimulation with LPS (0.1 μg/ml) for 24 h in the presence or absence of alloxan (10-20 mM). Under normoglycemic conditions, GlcN significantly increased the production of NO in response to LPS, as shown by higher nitrite levels in the culture supernatant (Fig. 1A). This effect was paralleled by increased expression of iNOS and elevated global protein O-GlcNAcylation (Fig. 1B), consistent with greater hexosamine pathway activity. Notably, co-treatment with alloxan reduced the NO production, iNOS expression, and O-GlcNAcylation enhancement induced by GlcN in a dose-dependent manner. Supporting the physiological relevance of these findings, preliminary experiments in mouse peritoneal macrophage similarly showed that alloxan inhibited LPS plus GlcN–induced NO production under normoglycemic conditions (Supplementary Figure). By contrast, alloxan exposure in high-glucose conditions failed to significantly impact NO production, iNOS expression, or global O-GlcNAcylation (Fig. 1A, B), suggesting a reduced involvement of OGT activity in GlcN-mediated responses within this metabolic context.

Alloxan abolishes GlcN-enhanced transcription of proinflammatory genes under normoglycemic conditions

To further examine how alloxan influences the regulation of inflammatory genes, we measured mRNA expression levels of major proinflammatory mediators—including iNOS, COX-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)—in RAW264.7 macrophages using quantitative real-time PCR. In normoglycemic conditions, GlcN alone led to a modest elevation in the basal expression of these five inflammatory genes. Upon co-administration with LPS, GlcN substantially intensified LPS-induced transcriptional activation, resulting in distinctly higher mRNA levels of iNOS, COX-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1 (Fig. 2A). Notably, the addition of alloxan (10 or 20 mM) completely abrogated the GlcN-driven enhancement of LPS-induced gene expression, highlighting the requirement of OGT activity for GlcN-mediated transcriptional amplification under normoglycemic conditions. However, under hyperglycemic conditions (25 mM glucose), GlcN diminished LPS-induced expression of all analyzed proinflammatory genes. Importantly, alloxan co-treatment did not further affect this GlcN-induced suppression, indicating that OGT-dependent O-GlcNAcylation may not play a major role in regulating inflammatory gene transcription in hyperglycemic settings (Fig. 2B).

Alloxan blocks GlcN-induced enhancement of NF-κB promoter binding and transcriptional activity under normoglycemic conditions

To investigate the mechanism through which alloxan dampens GlcN-potentiated inflammatory signaling in normoglycemic environments, we assessed NF-κB activation and DNA-binding dynamics with electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA), biotinylated DNA pulldown, and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays. EMSA results indicated that GlcN alone did not further increase NF-κB DNA-binding activity compared to LPS stimulation alone, but combined treatment with alloxan markedly decreased the formation of NF-κB–DNA complexes in LPS and GlcN-treated cells (Fig. 3A). To further characterize the NF-κB complexes assembled at the iNOS promoter, we carried out biotin pulldown assays using a probe containing the canonical κB binding motif. This approach revealed that GlcN enhanced recruitment of NF-κB subunits p65, c-Rel, p52, and RelB, yet alloxan treatment substantially reduced these interactions. Notably, p50 subunit binding was largely unaffected by alloxan (Fig. 3B, C), indicating a selective interference with particular NF-κB dimers. These observations were supported by ChIP analyses targeting the proximal (–15 to –159) and distal (–904 to –1058) NF-κB binding regions within the iNOS promoter. GlcN significantly increased p65 occupancy at both binding sites following LPS exposure, an effect that was effectively reversed in the presence of alloxan (Fig. 3D).

Alloxan abolishes GlcN-induced O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB p65

To determine whether alloxan inhibits the O-GlcNAc modification of the NF-κB p65 subunit under normoglycemic conditions, RAW264.7 cells were exposed to LPS in the presence or absence of GlcN, with or without concurrent alloxan treatment. Enrichment of O-GlcNAc–modified proteins was accomplished by wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) pull-down assays, followed by immunoblotting for the p65 subunit of NF-κB (Fig. 4). GlcN treatment, both alone and in combination with LPS, resulted in a marked elevation of p65 O-GlcNAcylation compared to vehicle-treated controls. Notably, alloxan exposure decreased p65 O-GlcNAc levels to near-basal states under all tested conditions, demonstrating that OGT inhibition effectively blocks GlcN-stimulated O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB p65.

Proposed mechanism of glucose-dependent NF-κB regulation by GlcN and alloxan

To synthesize our major findings, we developed a schematic model that outlines the regulatory roles of GlcN and alloxan in NF-κB–dependent iNOS expression under normoglycemic conditions (Fig. 5). Within this framework, GlcN increases cellular UDP-GlcNAc availability, which consequently augments OGT-driven O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB subunits and stimulates enhanced iNOS gene expression and NO synthesis. Convertsely, inhibition of OGT by alloxan curtails the O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB, resulting in attenuated iNOS transcription.

DISCUSSION

This investigation underscores the essential function of protein O-GlcNAcylation in determining the glycemic context–dependent immunomodulatory actions of glucosamine (GlcN) in macrophages. By employing pharmacological blockade of O-GlcNAc transferase (OGT) with alloxan, we furnish strong evidence that the proinflammatory effects of GlcN observed under normoglycemic conditions (5 mM glucose)—such as increased NO production, heightened iNOS expression, amplified NF-κB DNA-binding activity, and upregulated proinflammatory gene transcription—require functional O-GlcNAc cycling. Alloxan administration robustly eliminated these GlcN-driven proinflammatory responses, signifying that OGT-catalyzed protein O-GlcNAcylation is crucial for enhancing inflammatory pathways in this context. In contrast, when exposed to hyperglycemic conditions (25 mM glucose), GlcN produced inhibitory effects on LPS-induced inflammation, evidenced by lower NO output and suppressed iNOS levels. Importantly, alloxan did not negate these anti-inflammatory outcomes, implying that GlcN-induced immunosuppression during hyperglycemia occurs predominantly through mechanisms independent of O-GlcNAcylation. This glycemic context-dependent divergence highlights the bidirectional immunomodulatory potential of GlcN and suggests the existence of a metabolic threshold that governs its shift from proinflammatory to anti-inflammatory function.

These findings align with, and expand upon, previous studies that have described both anti- and proinflammatory actions of GlcN. Initial research primarily associated the anti-inflammatory effects of GlcN with elevated intracellular UDP-GlcNAc concentrations and the subsequent O-GlcNAcylation of key transcription factors, notably the NF-κB subunits p65 and c-Rel, leading to decreased expression of proinflammatory genes (17, 19). In contrast, recent studies have identified an unexpected phenomenon in which GlcN enhances LPS-induced inflammation under normoglycemic conditions, but reduces these responses in hyperglycemic environments (20). These observations provide further support for the existence of a glucose-dependent regulatory switch and identify the O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB as a key mechanistic node underlying the bidirectional regulation of inflammation.

At the mechanistic level, our results show that GlcN increases O-GlcNAcylation of the NF-κB p65 subunit, thereby promoting the formation of NF-κB complexes, enhancing DNA-binding capacity, and facilitating recruitment to the iNOS promoter. Alloxan administration reversed these effects, highlighting the necessity of OGT enzymatic function for GlcN-driven NF-κB activation. Importantly, alloxan selectively inhibited the association of specific NF-κB subunits—including p65, c-Rel, p52, and RelB—while p50 interaction remained unaffected. This result suggests that p50 is either minimally O-GlcNAcylated or participates in complexes that lack transcriptional activity. Despite the precise mechanisms remaining uncertain, these subunit-specific distinctions point toward additional regulatory complexity within NF-κB signaling. Further investigation focusing on the site-specific O-GlcNAc modification of individual NF-κB subunits will be required to elucidate these pathways.

An important outstanding question arising from our data is why identical O-GlcNAcylation leads to contrasting outcomes depending on glucose availability. Possible mechanisms may involve glucose-dependent regulation of OGT/OGA expression or enzymatic activity, changes in subcellular localization, or distinct patterns of O-GlcNAc site occupancy on NF-κB. Comprehensive mapping of O-GlcNAcylation patterns under normoglycemic and hyperglycemic conditions, preferably through proteomic analyses, will be essential to elucidate this paradox and clarify the context-specific immunomodulatory roles of GlcN. Collectively, these points illustrate the complexity underlying O-GlcNAc–mediated immune modulation and highlight the necessity for integrated studies incorporating genetic, biochemical, and in vivo methodologies.

Despite these advances, several caveats must be noted. Most significantly, the use of alloxan as an OGT inhibitor introduces interpretative complexities. While alloxan efficiently reduces both global and p65-specific O-GlcNAcylation, it is also known to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) (23), which may independently affect NF-κB signaling and cytokine expression. Additionally, alloxan lacks complete specificity for OGT and may interact with other glycosyltransferases or cellular targets. The requirement for high millimolar concentrations for effective inhibition in vitro further restricts its translational applicability, and variable uptake among cell types could influence both efficacy and selectivity. Moreover, although the primary data were obtained using RAW264.7 macrophages, with initial results from mouse peritoneal macrophages, future studies will need to address whether glucose-dependent NF-κB O-GlcNAcylation and its proinflammatory functions are replicated in primary macrophages and in vivo models. Such validation is critical to establish the physiological significance of our findings and determine their potential for clinical translation.

Finally, we concentrated our analysis on the NF-κB pathway, despite the potential involvement of other O-GlcNAc–modified regulators, including STATs, MAPKs, and AP-1 components, in mediating the effects of GlcN. Future research should address these limitations through the application of genetic strategies (such as OGT knockdown or CRISPR-mediated gene editing) or by utilizing highly selective small-molecule inhibitors to precisely delineate the specific role of OGT. Assessing GlcN–OGT interactions in primary macrophages and in vivo models under rigorously controlled glycemic states will be essential for validating the physiological significance of our results. Furthermore, defining the O-GlcNAc-modified proteome in macrophages exposed to variable glucose environments may reveal additional factors underlying nutrient–immune interplay. Analyzing the temporal dynamics of O-GlcNAc cycling throughout distinct phases of inflammatory responses could provide greater insight into its regulatory function in immune activation and resolution.

In summary, our findings demonstrate that O-GlcNAcylation of NF-κB is essential for the proinflammatory functions of GlcN during normoglycemia, while its modulation may be distinctly altered during hyperglycemic states. This work underscores the importance of metabolic status in determining innate immune signaling and highlights O-GlcNAc cycling as a promising therapeutic target for context-specific control of macrophage-driven inflammation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents

Unless otherwise indicated, all reagents were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Cell cultures

RAW264.7 murine macrophage cells (from the Korean Cell Line Bank) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Mouse peritoneal macrophage isolation and treatment

Peritoneal macrophages were isolated from 8-12-week-old C57BL/6J mice. In brief, mice were euthanized in accordance with institutional animal care protocols, then the peritoneal cavity was lavaged with 5-10 ml of ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to collect peritoneal cells. The harvested cells were centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 minutes, resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well in 6-well plates. After incubation for 2 hours at 37°C, non-adherent cells were gently washed away with PBS, and adherent macrophages were used for subsequent experiments. For treatments, peritoneal macrophages were maintained in normoglycemic medium (5 mM glucose). Cells were pretreated with GlcN (5 mM) or vehicle for 30 minutes, then stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 0.1 μg/ml) for 24 hours, with or without alloxan (20 mM).

Nitrite assay

Nitric oxide (NO) production was evaluated by quantifying nitrite using the Griess reaction. In brief, 50 μl of culture supernatant was combined with 50 μl of Griess reagent (1% sulfanilamide, 0.1% N-[1-naphthyl]ethylenediamine dihydrochloride, and 5% phosphoric acid). The absorbance at 540 nm was determined using a microplate reader. Nitrite concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated with sodium nitrite (NaNO2).

Reverse transcription PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cells with TRIzolTM reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using GoScriptTM Reverse Transcriptase (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). PCR amplification was performed using primers specific for mouse GAPDH, F, TCATTGACCTCAACTACAGGT, R, CTAAGCAGTTGGTGGTGCAG; iNOS, F, GCATCCCAAGTACGAGTGGT, R, CCATGATGGTCACATTCTGC; COX-2, F, GCTGTACAAGCAGTGGCAAA, R, GTCTGGAGTGGGAGGCACT; IL-6, F, CCGGAGAGGAGACTTCACAG, R, TGGTCTTGGTCCTTAGCCAC; TNF-α, F, GACCCTCACACTCAGATCAT, R, TTGAAGAGAACCTGGGAGTA. MCP-1, F, GTCCCTGTCATGCTTCTGGG; R, GAAGACCTTAGGGCAGATGCAG. PCR products were visualized on a 1% agarose gel. For quantitative real-time PCR, cDNA was subjected to amplification with SYBR Green Real-Time PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan). Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH expression.

Wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) pull-down

Cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 0.5% Nonidet P-40) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails to inhibit protein degradation and dephosphorylation. For enrichment of O-GlcNAc-modified proteins, 1 mg of total protein lysate was incubated with wheat germ agglutinin (WGA)-agarose beads (Vector Laboratories, Albany, CA, USA) at 4°C for 1 hour with gentle rotation. Following incubation, the beads underwent thorough washing to eliminate proteins that were non-specifically bound prior to elution. The bound glycoproteins were released by heating in SDS-PAGE sample buffer for 5 min, followed by analysis via SDS–PAGE and Western blotting with specific antibodies.

Western blotting

Equal amounts of protein samples (20-40 μg) were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham Biosciences, Piscataway, NJ, USA). To minimize non-specific interactions, membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk or 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against O-GlcNAc (CTD110.6; Covance, Berkeley, CA, USA), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS; BD Transduction Laboratories, Lexington, KY, USA), cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2; Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI), NF-κB subunits p65, c-Rel, p50, p52, RelB (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) as a loading control. After three washes with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Amersham Biosciences) diluted 1:10,000 in TBST for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Amersham Biosciences) as described by the manufacturer. For quantitative comparisons, band intensities were determined by densitometric analysis using appropriate imaging software.

Streptavidin-agarose pulldown assay

Streptavidin-agarose pulldown assays were performed in accordance with previously described protocols (19) to quantitatively evaluate the interaction between transcription factors or other relevant proteins and a designated DNA sequence. A 20-nucleotide biotinylated oligonucleotide probe containing the NF-κB binding site (5’-GCTAGGGGGATTTTCCCTCT-3’), representing the −957/−977 region of the iNOS promoter, served as the target sequence. Total protein lysates were generated using a lysis buffer consisting of 10 mM HEPES (pH 8.0), 1.5 mM MgCl2, 200 mM sucrose, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 10 mM KCl, and protease inhibitors. The isolates were then suspended in PBS containing 1 mM EDTA (pH 8.0) and subjected to sonication to ensure nuclear protein extraction. For the pulldown procedure, 2 μg of the biotinylated iNOS-κB DNA probe was combined with 25 μl of streptavidin-conjugated agarose beads and nuclear protein extracts at 4°C for 1 hour with consistent agitation to enable efficient formation of DNA-protein complexes. Following thorough washes to eliminate non-specifically associated proteins, the bead-bound complexes were eluted and subjected to Western blot analysis with antibodies targeting the proteins of interest.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

A double-stranded DNA oligonucleotide probe containing the consensus NF-κB binding sequence (Promega) was end-labeled using T4 polynucleotide kinase (New England Biolabs, Beverly, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For the binding assays, 10 μg of nuclear protein extract was combined with the labeled oligonucleotide probe in a binding buffer containing 50 mM KCl, 12.5 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 6.25 mM MgCl2, 0.05 mM EDTA, 0.5% Nonidet P-40, 0.5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 5% glycerol, and 2 μg of poly(dI-dC) to minimize nonspecific interactions. This mixture was kept on ice for 15 minutes to facilitate DNA-protein complex formation. After incubation, probe-protein complexes were separated using a 4.5% native polyacrylamide gel under non-denaturing conditions. The gel was then processed to visualize shifted bands representing DNA-protein complexes.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays were conducted as described previously (19), with slight modifications. In short, cells were fixed by adding 1% formaldehyde directly to the culture medium to cross-link protein-DNA interactions, followed by quenching. Cells were lysed, and chromatin was fragmented by sonication on ice with a Bioruptor device (COSMO Bio Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) to yield DNA fragments of suitable size. The lysate supernatant was precleared by incubation with 20 μg sheared salmon sperm DNA, 5 μg normal IgG, and 50 μl protein G-conjugated agarose beads for 2 hours at 4°C to minimize nonspecific binding. Chromatin immunoprecipitation was achieved by incubating the precleared sample with 5 μg anti–c-Rel antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. Immunocomplexes were isolated using protein G beads, followed by thorough washing to eliminate nonspecific material. Chromatin complexes were subsequently eluted from the beads by elution buffer treatment, and cross-links were reversed by an overnight incubation with RNase A at 65°C. Thereafter, protein removal was accomplished by treating with EDTA, 1 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.5), and proteinase K at 42°C for 1 hour. Purification of DNA was performed using a commercial DNA purification kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The enrichment of the iNOS promoter region in the immunoprecipitated DNA was evaluated by PCR amplification with primers specific for the NF-κB binding site: forward primer (−904), 5’-GTGTACCTCAGACAAGGGC-3’, and reverse primer (−1058), 5’-CACACATGGCATGGAATTTT-3’. The amplified PCR products were analyzed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis or quantified by real-time PCR to assess relative enrichment.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments. Statistical analyses involving multiple groups were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Scheffé’s post hoc test for pairwise group comparisons. For comparisons between two groups, paired Student’s t-tests were performed. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were executed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA).
