Association Between Time Spent With Low Posttransplant Glomerular Filtration Rate and Access to Relisting and Retransplantation
Authors: Elaine Ku, Timothy P. Copeland, Charles E. McCulloch, Puneet Sood, Kirsten L. Johansen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rationale & ObjectiveFor transplant recipients with low estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), early preparation is needed to optimize the transition back to dialysis or retransplantation. Our objective was to determine if the amount of time spent with low eGFR was associated with a higher likelihood of relisting or retransplantation within 1 year of graft failure.Study DesignRetrospective cohort study.Setting & ParticipantsWe included adults who developed graft failure between 2010 and 2021 according to the US Renal Data System.ExposureWe modeled the time spent with eGFR between 20 and 5 mL/min/1.73 m2 using available serum creatinine values reported annually (henceforth referred to as time with low eGFR).OutcomeRelisting or retransplantation within 1 year of graft failure.Analytical ApproachWe used Fine-Gray models accounting for the competing risk of death to determine the association between time spent with low eGFR (as a categorical variable) and outcomes of interest.ResultsWe included 14,680 patients; median age at graft failure was 53 years (IQR, 42-61). Within 1 year of graft failure, ∼18% (n = 2,690) of patients were relisted and 13% (n = 1,854) were retransplanted. In adjusted models, spending 2-4 years (SHR, 1.43; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.16-1.75) or >4 years (SHR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.04-1.57) with low eGFR was associated with a higher likelihood of relisting (vs spending <2 years with low eGFR). Similarly, spending 2-4 years (SHR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.09-1.72) or >4 years (SHR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.37-2.19) with low eGFR was also associated with a higher likelihood of retransplantation within 1 year of graft failure.LimitationsResidual confounding; observational nature of study.ConclusionsTimely preparation of patients with low eGFR for retransplantation could increase patients’ likelihood of early relisting and retransplantation.

The incidence of dialysis initiation after allograft failure has been increasing steadily.1, 2, 3 Transitioning to dialysis after experiencing improvements in health-related quality of life due to restored kidney function from an allograft can be a challenging journey for patients.3,4 In the United States, the majority of patients with a failed allograft are treated with in-center hemodialysis, and only 15% receive a second kidney transplant within 1 year of graft failure.5,6 The reasons for low rates of retransplantation among those with graft failure are unknown, even though these patients were previously deemed appropriate candidates for their initial transplant and had navigated the process of waitlisting successfully in the past.

One factor that may be important for relisting and retransplantation is the rapid loss of allograft function, resulting in insufficient time for patients to be referred and complete their evaluation for relisting or identify potential living donors before the onset of graft failure. Nationally, patients are not eligible for waitlist registration until their estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) falls to 20 mL/min or lower. Using the trajectory of allograft function loss to estimate how much time may be available between this eGFR threshold for relisting and graft failure may help transplant centers and referring nephrologists prioritize the patients who should start the reevaluation and relisting process in a timely manner to increase the likelihood of early retransplantation.7,8 Our objective was to identify how much time kidney transplant recipients spend with low eGFR and whether a longer time spent with low eGFR would be associated with early relisting and retransplantation.

Methods

Study Population

We included patients aged 18-70 years who developed graft failure between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2021. To identify patients with progressive loss of eGFR, we required at least 1 serum creatinine value that corresponded to an eGFR between 35 and 20 mL/min/1.73 m2 followed by an eGFR of <20 mL/min/1.73 m2 according to the US Renal Data System (USRDS). Serum creatinine values were derived from the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) 2728 Form as well as the USRDS “Transplant” files, which include data derived from annual reporting of serum creatinine values to the United Network for Organ Sharing by transplant centers. We included data starting from an eGFR of 35 mL/min/1.73 m2 to ensure the inclusion of a sufficient number of data points to construct our trajectories. Patients with primary nonfunction of their transplant, less than 90 days of graft survival, cancer diagnosis before graft failure, and residence in US territories were excluded (see Figure S1). This study was reviewed by the University of California San Francisco Institutional Review Board and was deemed exempt from human subjects research.

Demographic Covariates and Comorbid Condition Ascertainment

Age, sex (male or female), race, ethnicity, insurance, and ZIP code of residence were determined using the “Patients” file of USRDS.2 Patients’ comorbid conditions such as obesity, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, and coronary artery disease were derived from the CMS-2728 Form submitted at the time of graft failure, and if missing, values from the first CMS-2728 Form submitted at the time of dialysis initiation or initial transplantation were carried forward. The history of rejection was defined using data from the “Transplant” files. The median annual neighborhood household income, adjusted for inflation to 2023 dollars, was estimated using statistics from the American Community Survey and the patient’s ZIP code of residence in the year their graft failure occurred.

Modeling the eGFR Trajectory to Predict the Time Spent With Low eGFR (Primary Exposure)

To model the trajectory of eGFR decline, we included all serum creatinine values available before the onset of graft failure, including the GFR estimated at the time of dialysis initiation after graft failure or preemptive retransplantation. Linear mixed models with patient-specific random intercepts and slopes for time and an unstructured covariance were applied to model the trajectory of eGFR change. We used the 2012 CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) equations to estimate GFR, because these estimates would have been used by providers for decision making during our study timeframe.9 We reviewed the fit of these linear mixed models with the actual eGFR values, and the fit was deemed to be satisfactory. We avoided overextrapolation of the eGFR trajectory beyond twice the total time available between the first to last serum creatinine value measurement after patients met our inclusion criteria. We excluded patients with positive slopes in their eGFR trajectory because they would be unlikely to prepare for graft failure (see Figure S1). While constructing our trajectories, we identified a subset of patients for whom the modeled time when an eGFR of 20 mL/min/1.73 m2 would be reached was after the actual graft failure date (signifying a more rapid loss of eGFR than was predicted based on linear mixed modeling of earlier trajectories, see Figure S2 for sample trajectories). We categorized and analyzed these patients as a separate group and henceforth refer to these patients as individuals with “accelerated graft failure.”

Next, we determined the time with a low eGFR between 20 and 5 mL/min/1.73 m2 via extrapolation from the linear mixed models we built above because we were interested in the maximal accrual time that would theoretically be available for relisting or retransplantation as in our prior work.7,8 This approach minimizes the influence of variations in practice in the timing of dialysis reinitiation or preemptive retransplantation by modeling trajectories to a common endpoint.10, 11, 12, 13 We extrapolated this time from our models in years (which reflects the rapidity of eGFR loss and the time available for workup for relisting) and used it as the primary exposure in all subsequent analyses, categorized as <2, 2-4, and >4 years to define this exposure. Hence, our exposure (henceforth referred to as time with low eGFR) was a 4-category variable (<2 years, 2-4 years, >4 years, and accelerated graft failure).

Outcome Determination

We extracted transplant dates using the initial and, if applicable, second transplant dates from the USRDS “Patients” and “Transplant” files. Transplant failure dates were derived from the “Patients” file. The primary outcomes were relisting and retransplantation within 1 year of graft failure. The initial and subsequent donor source (living or deceased) was determined using USRDS “Patients” and “Transplant” files, and in secondary analysis, we examined the outcome of retransplantation from a living versus deceased donor source within 1 year separately. We abstracted death dates from the USRDS “Patients” file.

In additional analysis, we also examined the outcomes of preemptive relisting or preemptive retransplantation as separate outcomes of interest.

Statistical Analysis

We used Fine-Gray models in our primary analysis to examine the association between the primary exposure (categorical time spent with low eGFR before graft failure) and time to relisting or retransplantation within 1 year of graft failure using separate models for each outcome and treating death as a competing risk. In these models, time started at graft failure; therefore, the time spent with low eGFR was determined before time started in these analyses. If waitlisting occurred before graft failure or preemptive retransplantation was the treatment modality at graft failure, time was set at 0.5 days for these outcomes. These models were adjusted for age at graft failure, sex, race/ethnicity, median neighborhood income based on ZIP code of patients’ residence, obesity, history of rejection, coronary artery disease, hypertension, heart failure, and diabetes. We considered only variables that would potentially influence the exposure and outcome as covariates; hence, variables such as transplant center size were not considered. Patients who died during follow-up contributed eGFR data until their time of death. We censored follow-up at 1 year or the date of administrative censoring because relisting and retransplantation would ideally occur shortly after graft failure. We checked models for violations of the assumption of proportionality, and models were deemed proportional in primary models.

In secondary analysis, we also examined living and deceased donor retransplantation as separate outcomes of interest, treating death and deceased (or living) donor retransplantation as competing risks. In sensitivity analysis, we repeated all analyses using Cox proportional hazards regression models.

In additional analysis, we used logistic regression models adjusted for the same covariates as above to examine the odds of preemptive relisting and preemptive retransplantation.

All analyses were conducted using Stata 17 (Statacorp LLC).

Those who spent <2 years with low eGFR were more likely to be of Black race or Hispanic ethnicity, more likely to have Medicaid insurance at graft failure, less likely to have diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, and obesity as comorbid conditions, and more likely to have had a history of rejection compared with those who had a longer time with low GFR (Table 1).

Association Between Time Spent With Low eGFR (eGFR Between 20 and 5 mL/min/1.73 m2) and Relisting or Retransplantation Within 1 Year of Graft Failure

Approximately 18% (N = 2,690) and 13% (N = 1,854) of the cohort were relisted or received transplantation within 1 year of graft failure, respectively. Approximately 7% of the cohort (N = 1,051) died during follow-up before retransplantation. In adjusted models accounting for the competing risk of death, spending 2-4 years (subhazard ratio [SHR], 1.43; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.16-1.75) or >4 years (SHR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.04-1.57) with low eGFR was associated with a higher likelihood of relisting within 1 year of graft failure compared with spending <2 years with low eGFR (Table S1 and Figure 1). In adjusted models, spending 2-4 years (SHR, 1.37; 95% CI, 1.09-1.72) or >4 years (SHR, 1.73; 95% CI, 1.37-2.19) with low eGFR was associated with a higher likelihood of retransplantation within 1 year of graft failure compared with spending <2 years with low eGFR. For the group with accelerated graft loss, the likelihood of a second transplant was not statistically significantly different from that of the reference group (SHR, 1.10; 95% CI, 0.88-1.38; Figure 1 and Table S1). These results were generally consistent when evaluating outcomes separately based on living versus deceased donor transplant (Figure 1 and Table S1).

When we used Cox proportional hazards models in lieu of Fine-Gray models, results were also similar (Table S2).

Time With Low eGFR (20-5 mL/min/1.73 m2) and Odds of Preemptive Waitlisting and Transplantation

A longer time with low eGFR was associated with higher odds of preemptive waitlisting (Table S3). Although a similar trend in the odds ratio was noted for preemptive retransplantation, findings only achieved statistical significance for those who spent >4 years with low eGFR compared with the reference group (<2 years; Table S3). Of note, accelerated graft loss was associated with lower odds of preemptive relisting (odds ratio, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.47-0.66) and a tendency toward lower odds of preemptive retransplantation (odds ratio, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.57-1.24; Table S3), although findings did not achieve statistical significance.

Discussion

In this study of patients who developed graft failure in the United States, we found that patients with a functional allograft were estimated to spend a median of 3.7 years with an eGFR of <20 to 5 mL/min/1.73 m2, although ∼2 in 5 patients developed graft failure earlier than would have been expected based on our models and experienced accelerated graft loss. A longer time spent with low eGFR was generally associated with a higher likelihood of relisting and retransplantation in adjusted analyses accounting for the competing risk of death, and having at least 2 or more years of time with low eGFR was associated with better access to retransplantation.

There has been significant concern that lack of access to medical care, recognition of kidney disease, and timely referral to transplant centers for waitlist consideration may contribute to delays in access to kidney transplantation among patients with nondialysis requiring chronic kidney disease.14, 15, 16 However, these concerns are substantially attenuated in patients who have already successfully received a kidney transplant and who have experience with navigating the candidacy evaluation process and awareness of their kidney disease. Our findings suggest that one potential factor that may be important in terms of relisting and retransplantation after graft failure is the rapidity with which patients lose kidney function after they meet the eGFR threshold for relisting (eGFR ≤ 20 mL/min). Because patients with low eGFR may be seeing nephrology providers who are not at a transplant center to prepare for the need for dialysis and because some transplant centers may no longer be routinely following such patients,17,18 fragmentation of care may lead to delays in referrals for preparation for relisting or retransplantation.4,18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Longer periods of time spent with low eGFR may allow patients to better reconnect with the transplant center for relisting, navigate the complex transplant reevaluation process, identify (additional) living donor candidates, and accrue greater preemptive wait time. It is concerning, however, that those with the most rapid decline in eGFR (spending <2 years with low eGFR) were younger patients who would likely benefit from retransplantation but who were less likely to be relisted or retransplanted in a timely manner.

We note that the group of individuals who had accelerated graft loss (much shorter time between eGFR 20 mL/min/1.73 m2 and actual graft failure than their eGFR trajectory would have suggested, see Figure S1) did have a statistically significantly higher likelihood of relisting compared with those spending <2 years with low eGFR. The reasons for these findings are unclear, but many of these patients had relatively stable trajectories of eGFR until the last 1-2 years of follow-up as seen in Figure S2. We surmise that these patients may have suffered acute kidney injury, which may have triggered patients and providers to prioritize these patients for reevaluation and relisting with a greater sense of urgency those with a more stable but constant trajectory of eGFR loss.24 However, this group of patients did not have better access to retransplantation, particularly from a deceased donor source compared with those who spent <2 years with low eGFR. This may also be a reflection of severity of illness and issues with adherence; whether this group should be prioritized for relisting is unclear.

Several limitations of our study should be noted, including the observational nature of the data and the potential for selection bias. Only those with serum creatinine values in the USRDS database were included for study, and patients who developed sudden acute graft failure may not have had sufficient data for us to model their trajectories. Of note, we used the 2012 CKD-EPI equations to estimate eGFR, and the disparities observed in access to repeat transplantation based on race may have been attenuated with the introduction of the 2021 CKD-EPI equation. However, our study was not designed to specifically examine racial disparities in access to transplantation with the use of different eGFR-estimating equations, which is an issue that has been examined by others.25 In addition, misclassification of comorbid conditions may have occurred, and not all patients included for analysis may have been candidates for either relisting or retransplantation, although this would have biased findings toward the null. We also do not have data about the type of providers (transplant vs nontransplant nephrologists) who were providing care to these individuals and did not consider the size of the transplant center or allocation region, although these factors should not directly influence the trajectory of eGFR loss at the patient level. Data on the reasons for graft failure were missing for many individuals in this cohort, and hence we did not examine reasons for delays in repeat transplantation that may have been related to the cause of graft failure. Finally, we do not have granular data on living donor availability for retransplantation.

Our study suggests that spending more than 2 years with low eGFR was associated with higher access to relisting and retransplantation. For those with accelerated loss of kidney function leading to graft loss, access to retransplantation may be suboptimal, even if these patients are relisted in a timely fashion for a second transplant. Further studies are needed to identify and predict risk factors for rapid posttransplant GFR decline to ensure that high-risk patients are recognized early and referred for transplant evaluation in a timely manner.
