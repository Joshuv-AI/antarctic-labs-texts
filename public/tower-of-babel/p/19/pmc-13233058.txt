Assessing Diagnostic Accuracy of a Handheld Point-of-Care Device for Quantifying Neonatal Bilirubin Levels: The BEAT Jaundice @Home Study
Authors: Lauren E.H. Westenberg, Jasper V. Been, Andrei Tintu, Jolande Y. Vis, Helene A. Bouma, Daan Nieboer, Peter H. Dijk, Henk Groen, Marten J. Poley, Erwin Ista, Eric A.P. Steegers, Irwin K.M. Reiss, Berthe A.M. van der Geest, Christian V. Hulzebos, for the BEAT Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe Bilistick is a handheld point-of-care device for measuring total bilirubin levels in small blood volumes. We assessed its diagnostic accuracy and user convenience in near-term neonates cared for at home.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted in nine Dutch community midwifery practices. Neonates ≥35 weeks’ gestation were eligible if they were at home between postnatal days 2–8 and had not received phototherapy. A Bilistick version 2.0 was used in parallel to laboratory-based bilirubin (LBB) quantification when significant visible jaundice was observed or the transcutaneous bilirubin reading was elevated.ResultsA total of 2,314 neonates were included in the study, with 423 blood samples analyzed across 13 laboratories. On 203 occasions, the Bilistick was not used. Among the remaining 220 Bilistick readings, 104 failed, and 2 lacked corresponding LBB results. A Bland-Altman plot of 114 paired measurements of Bilistick and LBB showed a mean difference of +9.7 µmol/L (0.57 mg/dL) with corresponding 95% limits of agreement of −179.7 to +199.2 µmol/L (−10.5 to 11.7 mg/dL). The positive predictive value of a Bilistick reading for having a total serum bilirubin level above the phototherapy threshold was 36.4%. The negative predictive value was 90.1%, sensitivity 60%, and specificity 77.6%. Hemolysis (24%) contributed to overestimations by the Bilistick. Community midwives expressed multiple barriers related to user convenience.ConclusionDiagnostic accuracy of the Bilistick when used in the home setting was limited. Its use was further hindered by a significant proportion of failed readings and low user convenience when operated by midwives.

Introduction

Accurate quantification of total serum bilirubin (TSB) levels is essential for diagnosing and monitoring neonatal hyperbilirubinemia. Treatment initiation is based on gestational age, the hour-specific TSB level, and the presence of risk factors for bilirubin neurotoxicity. Usually, a laboratory-based bilirubin (LBB) measurement is considered the gold standard to guide clinical decision making for phototherapy and exchange transfusion [1, 2].

Recently, handheld point-of-care (POC) devices for measuring TSB in whole blood have been developed to address the challenges associated with LBB quantification [3–10]. For example, the turnaround time – from blood collection to receiving the result – can be several hours for LBB quantification, especially outside clinical settings [11]. Handheld POC devices can improve healthcare, especially in regions with limited access to clinical laboratory resources, which can contribute to delayed diagnosis [12]. Additionally, POC devices require less blood volume and no need for a fully equipped laboratory. The Bilistick (Bilimetrix, Trieste, Italy) is a handheld POC device for TSB quantification in whole blood based on spectrometry [5, 13].

Here, we performed a prospective multicenter cohort study to assess the diagnostic accuracy and user convenience of the Bilistick in near-term neonates cared for at home. Findings from this study will help determine whether the Bilistick version 2.0 is useful for managing hyperbilirubinemia outside clinical settings.

Methods

This prospective multicenter cohort study adhered to the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (STARD) Reporting Guideline [14]. A more comprehensive description of the methods can be found in our previous published protocol [15].

Study Design

The Better Assessment of Neonatal Jaundice at Home, the BEAT Jaundice @home study, was a prospective multicenter cohort study conducted in nine Dutch community midwifery practices [15]. A Bilistick version 2.0 device (index test) was used when LBB quantification was indicated based on either the presence of significant visible jaundice and/or an elevated transcutaneous bilirubinometer (TcB) reading (i.e., at or above a customized TcB-based nomogram, which includes a safety margin of 50 μmol/L [2.9 mg/dL]), according to the community midwives [15].

Participants

Neonates were eligible for inclusion if they were born at a gestational age of 35 weeks or more, were at home between days two and eight of life, and received their first midwife visit before the 6th postnatal day. Neonates were excluded if they had previously received phototherapy or if their parent(s)/caregiver(s) were unable to understand the patient information sheet due to insufficient Dutch language proficiency. Written informed consent was obtained during the first home visit.

Test Methods

The Bilistick is a conformité européenne (CE) certified handheld POC device for TSB quantification in whole blood based on spectrometry (photometric-based in two wavelengths at 465 and 570 nm). Approximately 500 µL of blood was collected and sent to a nearby site for LBB quantification (reference test) according to standard clinical practice. The blood samples were wrapped in aluminum foil for protection from light during transport. Additionally, all laboratories participated in the Dutch external quality assessment program, thereby aiming to ensure minimal bias and reliability of TSB results in clinical care [16–19]. Immediately after the blood draw for LBB, 35 µL of blood (i.e., one or two drops) from the same heel prick was used to measure TSB in whole blood using the Bilistick test strip and reader. After each use of the Bilistick, the reader was cleaned using the recommended cleaning strips and disinfectant solution. The sample transfer pipettes, and test strip were used only once. According to the manufacturer’s instructions, the Bilistick system accepts blood samples with a hematocrit range of 25%–70%. The result from the conventional LBB quantification guided treatment decisions according to the current Dutch Management of Hyperbilirubinemia guideline [2]. Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia requiring treatment (defined as LBB above the treatment threshold) was the target condition. If the Bilistick reading exceeded the exchange transfusion threshold, the neonate was immediately referred to a pediatrician while awaiting the LBB result to avoid any treatment delay. The community midwives reported data on whether the Bilistick reading indicated hemolysis, as per the device’s results, either by the Bilistick reader or test strip. The Bilistick reader is equipped with an optical system designed to detect hemoglobin in the sample and will show an alert on the screen when hemolysis is likely to influence TSB results or the nitrocellulose membrane of the test strip will turn red/orange in case of hemolysis [20]. Manual calibration was required twice during the BEAT study and was carried out by the study team. The community midwives were trained in the use and interpretation of the Bilistick results by the BEAT study team, who were available 24/7 to answer any questions.

Intended Sample Size

The prespecified sample size calculation of 2,100 neonates was designed to evaluate the two primary hypotheses of the TcB screening device, which was the primary index test undergoing evaluation in the BEAT Jaundice @home study. The diagnostic accuracy of the Bilistick was a secondary aim of the BEAT study, and therefore, the sample size calculation was not based on the Bilistick evaluation [15, 21].

Statistical Analyses

The Bland-Altman plot was used to calculate the mean difference and corresponding 95% limits of agreement (LoA) between the Bilistick and LBB. We used 2 × 2 tables to calculate positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity, and specificity. Secondary outcomes included the proportion of failed readings (i.e., the Bilistick did not display a TSB result) using the Bilistick, and difference in turnaround time between Bilistick and LBB (in minutes).

Additionally, post hoc analyses were performed to further examine emerging patterns not specified a priori. One sensitivity analysis excluded samples assessed in one out of 13 hospital laboratories, which was found during the study to have systematically overestimated LBB values. Another sensitivity analysis excluded samples with hemolysis according to either the Bilistick reader or the test strip.

User Convenience

The midwives were interviewed to gain insights into their experiences using the Bilistick device.

Results

Between July 11th, 2021, and June 9th, 2023, a total of 2,323 neonates were screened for inclusion. Parents of 2,314 provided written informed consent. Four hundred twenty-three blood pricks were performed in 326 neonates (Fig. 1). Samples were analyzed across 13 different laboratories. Based on 423 measurements, the median (interquartile range [IQR]) LBB level was 238 (202–292) μmol/L (13.9 [11.8–17.1] mg/dL). Based on 116 measurements, the median (IQR) Bilistick level was 234.5 (193–303) μmol/L (13.7 [11.3–17.7] mg/dL). In 203 instances, the Bilistick was not used for various reasons (online suppl. E supplement 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551324). Of the remaining 220 readings, 47% resulted in Bilistick test failures, producing no readings. Ultimately, the Bilistick yielded 116 valid bilirubin measurements, with a median (IQR) of 230 (193–303) µmol/L (13.5 [11.3–17.7] mg/dL). Figure 2 shows a Bland-Altman plot of 114 paired measurements with a mean difference of +9.7 µmol/L (+0.57 mg/dL) and 95% LoA of −179.7 to +199.2 µmol/L (−10.5 to +11.6 mg/dL). Seventy-eight out of the 2,314 (3.4%) neonates had a TSB level above the treatment threshold, of which 20 had a Bilistick reading (Fig. 1). The PPV of the Bilistick reading for having a TSB level above the treatment threshold was 36.4%. The NPV was 90.1% (Table 2). Sensitivity was 60.0% and specificity was 77.6%, respectively.

Secondary Outcomes

Proportion of failed readings as displayed by the Bilistick reader was 47% (online suppl. E supplements 2). One of the nine devices was returned to Bilimetrix because it was malfunctioning and was subsequently replaced.

The Bilistick displayed a test result in under 2 min. LBB quantifications had a median (IQR) turnaround time of 145 (110–212) minutes based on 269 LBB quantifications. In 70.7%, the blood sample transfer relied on the partner/family member; in the other cases, the blood transport was handled by the community midwife or blood test service.

Post hoc Analysis

During the study, we noted that one of the 13 participating laboratories systematically overestimated LBB values. Consequently, the respective laboratory revised its analytical methods. Subsequently, we excluded blood samples assessed at this laboratory in our post hoc sensitivity analysis; hence, 70 paired measurements remained (online suppl. E supplements 3). The Bland-Altman plot of 70 paired measurements showed a mean difference of +10.1 µmol/L (+0.59 mg/dL) with a corresponding 95% LoA of –181.1 to +201.4 µmol/L (−10.6 to +11.8 mg/dL) (online suppl. E Supplement 4). The PPV of a Bilistick reading for having a TSB level above the treatment threshold was 17.6%. The NPV was 96.2%. Sensitivity was 60% and specificity was 78.5%, respectively.

The Bilistick device detected hemolysis in 24% of measurements. Excluding these hemolyzed samples, 91 paired measurements remained for the sensitivity analysis (online suppl. E Supplemental 5). The PPV of a Bilistick reading for having a TSB level above the treatment threshold was 52.6%. The NPV was 90.3%. Sensitivity was 58.8% and specificity was 87.8%, respectively. The Bland-Altman plot of 91 paired measurements showed a mean difference of −6.6 µmol/L (−0.39 mg/dL) with a corresponding 95% LoA of –182.3 to +169.0 µmol/L (−10.7 to +9.9 mg/dL) (online suppl. E Supplement 6).

User Convenience

We interviewed 11 midwives. First, some found the Bilistick pipette difficult to use and were not always able to obtain sufficient amounts of blood for measurements. Second, the device would sometimes stop working unexpectedly or display a low battery warning, even though it had just been fully charged, especially during low ambient temperature. Third, the midwives struggled with determining the most efficient use of the Bilistick device as the Bilistick reader required some time to power up and become ready for measurements. Additionally, the midwives reported occasionally having to measure multiple times with the Bilistick to obtain a valid reading.

Discussion

In this large prospective multicenter diagnostic study, the Bilistick 2.0 device demonstrated a mean difference of +9.7 µmol/L (+0.57 mg/dL), with 95% LoA ranging from −179.7 to +199.2 µmol/L (−10.5 to +11.6 mg/dL) compared with LBB values. The PPV was 36.4% for identifying a TSB level above the treatment threshold, while the NPV was 90.1%. Sensitivity and specificity were 60% and 77.6%, respectively. Additionally, the Bilistick had a high failure rate of 47%. When the Bilistick displayed a bilirubin reading along with a hemolysis warning, the TSB value tended to be overestimated. The low user convenience of the Bilistick appears to have contributed to the number (n = 203) of cases in which the Bilistick was not used. Although the Bilistick is marketed as providing rapid TSB readings, its clinical use in a well-equipped setting seems unreliable considering its test performance and wide LoA when compared with LBB measurements. If treatment decisions were based solely on Bilistick results, both over- and undertreatment could have occurred.

Our study is a prospective evaluation of the Bilistick in a home setting, whereas previous studies have been conducted in clinical environments, despite its potential for use in areas with limited medical infrastructure. Sundaram et al. [22] also evaluated the Bilistick 2.0 in 137 paired measurements and found a mean difference of 2.2 µmol/L (0.13 mg/dL) with LoA of −61.0 to +65.7 µmol/L (−3.6 to +3.8 mg/dL). They reported a test failure rate of 10.5%. Our findings indicate an even higher inaccuracy of the Bilistick in this setting as compared with that reported in our recent published meta-analysis of data from 11 effect estimates from eight studies. We found a pooled mean difference of −17 μmol/L (1.0 mg/dL), with a pooled 95% LoA ranging from −91 to 57 μmol/L (−5.3 to +3.3 mg/dL) [13]. This indicated that applicability in the home setting may be limited and that performance has not improved from Bilistick 1.0 to 2.0. The Bilistick device accepts blood samples within a hematocrit range of 25%–70%. Low hematocrit levels are typically linked to hemolytic diseases, where the elevated concentration of hemoglobin can interfere with TSB measurements, resulting in an overestimation of TSB levels. In contrast, when hematocrit exceeds the Bilistick test-strip limit of 70%, inadequate saturation of the nitrocellulose membrane may occur due to insufficient serum volume, potentially leading to falsely low bilirubin readings.

Another handheld POC device, the BiliSpec, now called BiliDX (Maternova Inc, Sausalito, USA), requires 75 µL of blood and has been evaluated in three studies [10, 23, 24]. The latest study evaluated a new lateral flow cassette in hospital settings, with over 95% of blood samples below 340 μmol/L (19.9 mg/dL). The Bland-Altman plot of 446 paired measurements showed a mean difference of 12.8 μmol/L (0.8 mg/dL), and 95% LoA of −43.9 and 69.6 μmol/L (−2.6 to +4.1 mg/dL) [10].

The variety of the applied routine LBB quantification might have introduced bias in our results. Despite this, sensitivity analyses showed consistency with the primary outcomes, suggesting that the findings remained robust even with these differences in laboratory assays. This may also be related to the participation of all laboratories in the national external quality assessment program, ensuring the accuracy of bilirubin results [19]. We did not formally assess hemolysis (in vivo or in vitro), for example, using end-tidal carbon monoxide or laboratory hemolysis indices. Nonetheless, this does not undermine our results and is consistent with clinical practice. Another limitation of the study is that midwives are often not specifically trained to operate POC devices, which may have contributed to the failure rate. Simultaneously, this real-world setting contributes to the external validity of our findings. A key strength is the prospective design conducted in the home setting involving midwives, maternity care assistants, and data of over 2,000 neonates. Only one other prospective study has been conducted evaluating the Bilistick [6]. Additionally, we used the latest version of the Bilistick at the onset of the BEAT study.

Since criteria for analytical performance specification based on either Milan I (outcome studies) or Milan II (biological variation) are unavailable for neonatal bilirubin, the maximum allowable bias was determined by expert opinion by the Dutch Foundation for Quality Assessment in Medical Laboratories (SKML), setting it at 5% [25].

POC devices are used in a different context than routine in vitro diagnostic methods; for example, in settings where conventional laboratory methods are not readily available. POC devices should not be evaluated as if they were designed to replace laboratory testing in all circumstances, but they must still meet high standards of quality. Hence, future research should focus on defining acceptable analytical performance biases for handheld POC bilirubin devices. Additionally, if implemented in everyday clinical practice, an external quality assessment program specific for handheld POC devices would be recommendable.

Conclusion

The diagnostic accuracy of the Bilistick version 2.0 in the BEAT Jaundice @home study was limited when applied by community midwives in the home setting, partly due to the impact of hemolysis, according to Bilistick. Additionally, its utility was further hindered by a high incidence of failed readings and a low perceived user convenience. As such, in its current form, Bilistick is not suitable for replacing LBB measurements for detecting the need for treatment in the home setting.
