Assessment of CD3+ T cell density in the intestinal mucosa of dogs with inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Grégory Bouillet, Tristan Méric, Gary Gonel, Elodie Darnis, Beatriz Mendoza, Rodolfo Oliveira Leal, Maria Riquelme Galmes, Coralie Bertolani, Marina Dominguez Ruiz, Olivier Toulza, Marine Hugonnard, Cyril Duperrier-Simond, Odile Sénécat, Amandine Drut, Moez Rhimi, Anne-Charlotte Barrot, Thibaut Larcher, Juan Hernandez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundT lymphocytes play a central role in the intestinal immune response in dogs with immunomodulator-responsive enteropathy (IRE), but the clinical relevance of mucosal T-cell infiltration remains unclear.Hypothesis/ObjectivesCompare mucosal CD3+ T-cell density between dogs with IRE and healthy comparison dogs across intestinal segments, and assess associations with clinical, biochemical, and histologic severity markers.AnimalsForty-two client-owned dogs with IRE and 12 healthy research dogs.MethodsRetrospective multicenter study. Archived endoscopic biopsy samples from the duodenum, ileum, and colon were evaluated using the Simplified Histopathological Scoring System. Immunohistochemistry for CD3 was quantified by digital image analysis (QuPath) and expressed as relative CD3+ density (RCD3). Group comparisons, receiver operating characteristic curve analyses, mixed linear models, and correlation analyses were performed.ResultsRelative CD3+ density was significantly higher in dogs with IRE than in comparison dogs in the duodenum (median, 6.34% vs 1.42%; P = .03), ileum (9.79% vs 0.85%; P < .001), and colon (3.88% vs 1.32%; P = .002). The area under the curve values were 0.833, 0.896, and 0.811, respectively. The RCD3 was not correlated with clinical indices, serum albumin concentration, or histologic scores, but ileal histologic score was negatively correlated with RCD3 (ρ = −0.37; P = .04).Conclusions and clinical importanceMucosal CD3+ T-cell density is increased in dogs with IRE but is not associated with clinical or overall histologic disease severity. Quantification of CD3+ should not be used as a solitary monitoring tool but may contribute to immunophenotyping.

Introduction

Chronic inflammatory enteropathies (CIEs) are a group of non-infectious gastrointestinal disorders in dogs, defined by persistent or recurrent gastrointestinal signs for at least 3 weeks and histopathologic evidence of intestinal inflammation. These conditions are thought to result from an inappropriate immune response to luminal antigens, including dietary components and intestinal microbiota, in genetically susceptible individuals.1–4 Immunomodulator-responsive enteropathy (IRE) represents a subset of CIEs that fail to respond to dietary or microbiota-directed treatments and require immunomodulators.1 Lymphoplasmacytic infiltration of the intestinal mucosa is a common histologic feature in dogs with CIE. Among the inflammatory cell types involved, T lymphocytes, key effectors of mucosal immunity, have been a major focus of investigation.

T cells contribute to intestinal homeostasis and immune surveillance, but their dysregulation is implicated in chronic inflammation by the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interferon γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), and interleukin-17 (IL-17), which can disrupt epithelial barrier integrity and promote mucosal damage.5,6 Immunohistochemical quantification of CD3-positive (CD3+) T lymphocytes therefore has been proposed as a potential marker of disease activity in dogs with IRE. However, published data on T-cell infiltration in dogs with IRE remain inconsistent. Several studies have reported increased mucosal T-cell densities compared with healthy controls,7–10 whereas others failed to identify significant differences or even reported lower T-cell counts in affected dogs.11–13 Notably, 1 study that reported lower mucosal T-cell density, included exclusively normoalbuminemic (NA) dogs with overall milder clinical disease, which may partly explain these discrepancies.12 Beyond these differences in T-cell density, the clinical relevance of mucosal CD3+ T-cell infiltration also remains uncertain. Most studies have failed to identify a significant association between CD3+ T-cell density and clinical disease severity, but a limited number of studies have reported correlations with clinical indices or treatment response.14–16

These discrepancies raise important questions regarding the biological and clinical relevance of mucosal CD3+ T-cell infiltration. In particular, it remains uncertain whether CD3+ cell density reflects disease presence, disease severity, or specific immunologic phenotypes within the heterogeneous spectrum of IRE in dogs.

These inconsistencies likely reflect variations in study design, biopsy site selection, case definitions, disease severity, and treatment protocols. Additionally, the clinical heterogeneity of IRE in dogs, including the presence or absence of hypoalbuminemia, may reflect distinct pathophysiologic phenotypes, further complicating the interpretation of mucosal immunophenotyping data.

We hypothesized that dogs with IRE would exhibit increased mucosal CD3+ T-cell density compared with healthy controls, and that CD3+ T-cell infiltration could be associated with clinical, biochemical, and histologic markers of disease severity. The aims of our study were1 to compare the density of CD3+ T lymphocytes in the intestinal mucosa of a large group of dogs diagnosed with IRE to that of healthy dogs, and2 to evaluate associations between T-cell infiltration and clinical, biochemical, and histologic markers of disease severity.

Materials and methods

Case selection criteria

Archived intestinal mucosal biopsy samples collected between January 2020 and December 2023 from client-owned dogs diagnosed with IRE by board-certified internal medicine specialists at 5 veterinary hospitals (Oniris VetAgroBio, Nantes, France; CHV Frégis, Arcueil, France; University of Lisbon, Lisbon, Portugal; Hospital Veterinari Canis, Palma de Mallorca, Spain; and AniCura Aquivet Referral Hospital, Eysines, France) were retrospectively included. Only dogs for which mucosal biopsy samples had been obtained from duodenum, ileum, and colon during endoscopic examination were eligible for inclusion.

The diagnosis of IRE was established based on the presence of chronic gastrointestinal signs lasting for ≥3 weeks, that were unresponsive to sequential dietary and microbiota-modulating interventions, and after exclusion of extra-intestinal, infectious, or neoplastic diseases. Exclusion of other diagnoses was based on fecal analysis for parasitic and infectious agents, CBC, serum biochemistry, urinalysis, and abdominal ultrasonography. Additional diagnostic tests, including pancreatic lipase measurement, liver function tests, or endocrine testing, were performed when clinically indicated. Although diagnostic testing was not fully standardized across all cases because of the retrospective nature of the study, all diagnoses were established by board-certified internal medicine specialists and clinical follow-up supported the final diagnosis. Regarding dietary trials, dogs were required to have failed to respond to at least 2 nutritional interventions, including 1 hydrolyzed protein diet and 1 fiber-enriched diet, each administered for a minimum of 2 weeks. Regarding microbiota-modulating treatment, dogs were required to have received either (1) at least 1 probiotic formulation containing ≥5 bacterial strains at high viable counts (≥109 colony-forming units per strain per day), administered for a minimum of 2 weeks, or (2) fecal microbiota transplantation, delivered PO in capsule form or by enema. Exclusion of other diagnoses was based on fecal parasitology, CBC, serum biochemistry, urinalysis, and abdominal ultrasonography, as clinically indicated.

Comparison specimens consisted of archived intestinal mucosal biopsy samples from 12 healthy research dogs. These animals served as the control group in a separate study and did not receive treatments expected to affect intestinal physiology or immune function. They had no history of gastrointestinal clinical signs, and intestinal samples were collected at necropsy after the end of unrelated research protocols, allowing full-body examination and minimizing the likelihood of undetected systemic or gastrointestinal disease. Healthy dogs included in the study were not drawn from the same source population as affected dogs and should therefore be considered a comparison group rather than a true control group.

Data collection

Clinical data, laboratory findings, and histopathologic results were extracted from medical records for each dog with IRE. The Canine Inflammatory Bowel Disease Activity Index (CIBDAI) and the Canine Chronic Enteropathy Clinical Activity Index (CCECAI) were retrospectively calculated based on available clinical information.17,18

For exploratory purposes, serum albumin concentrations were used to stratify IRE-affected dogs into 2 subgroups: hypoalbuminemic IRE (HA-IRE) and NA-IRE. To account for inter-laboratory variability in reference ranges, albumin concentrations were normalized using the following formula: corrected albumin = (measured albumin × 29.5)/(midpoint of the reference interval). Hypoalbuminemia was defined as corrected serum albumin concentration <20 g/L.

Histologic analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded biopsy specimens were sectioned and stained with hematoxylin–eosin-saffron following standard protocols. Histologic evaluation was performed by a single board-certified pathologist (T. Larcher), who was blinded to clinical information. Lesions were assessed according to the Simplified Histopathological Scoring System (SHSS), as previously described.19 The evaluated criteria are summarized below. For duodenal and ileal samples, the following histologic features were assessed: villous atrophy, crypt dilatation, lacteal dilatation, surface epithelial injury, and lamina propria infiltration by lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, or a combination of these leukocytes. In colonic samples, the criteria included crypt dilatation, fibrosis, goblet cell numbers, surface epithelial injury, and lamina propria infiltration by lymphocytes, plasma cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, macrophages, or a combination of these leukocytes. Each feature was graded semi-quantitatively on a 4-point scale, where 0 indicated absence of lesion, 1 mild lesions, 2 moderate lesions, and 3 severe lesions. A cumulative histologic score was calculated for each intestinal segment.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed on additional specimen sections for detection of T lymphocytes. Slides were deparaffinized by incubation at 69 °C for 1 h, followed by clarification in 3 successive xylene baths (total duration, 24 min). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating at 98 °C for 40 min in Roche retrieval buffer. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked using 3% hydrogen peroxide for 8 min. Sections were incubated with a rabbit polyclonal anti-CD3 antibody (#A0452; dilution, 1:300; Dako, Denmark), followed by a secondary antibody (#E0432; dilution, 1:200; Dako, Denmark) and horseradish peroxidase-streptavidin conjugate (#432323; dilution, 1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Canada). Detection was achieved using the DAB Quanto detection system (Epredia, Japan), and slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. For each staining series, negative controls (primary antibody omitted) and positive controls (splenic tissue) were included.

Digital slide analysis was performed using QuPath software (version 0.5.1).20 The classifier was trained to identify both CD3-positive cells and total nucleated cells using the positive cell detection tool under pathologist supervision. For each gastrointestinal segment, 3 regions of interest were manually selected a priori by a single blinded observer, including large areas of lamina propria and epithelium while minimizing inclusion of intestinal lumen, areas of erosion, or areas with tissue-folding artifacts. The use of three regions of interest per segment was chosen to ensure representative sampling of the mucosa while maintaining consistency across samples.

The relative density of CD3+ lymphocytes (RCD3) was calculated as the ratio of CD3-positive cells to total nucleated cells.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (R Core Team. R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing; 2021). Normality of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Correlations between quantitative variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Correlation analyses were exploratory and not based on predefined hypotheses. Therefore, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied, and results were interpreted with caution. Correlations were assessed within the IRE group.

The discriminatory performance of RCD3 to differentiate dogs with IRE from the comparison group was assessed by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve according to the area under the curve (AUC) as follows: excellent when AUC was ≥0.9, good for AUC values between 0.8 and 0.9, fair between 0.7 and 0.8, poor between 0.6 and 0.7, and considered a fail when AUC was < 0.6.21 The optimal cutoff value was determined using Youden’s index. A mixed linear model was used to analyze the associations between intestinal segment and group (IRE vs. comparison group) and RCD3 results, with individual dog considered a random factor. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Ethical considerations

Regarding IRE-affected dogs, all samples used in this study originated from surplus material collected during routine diagnostic procedures performed as part of standard clinical care. No additional sampling was conducted for research purposes. Written informed consent was obtained from owners for the use of residual biological materials and associated data for research purposes.

Regarding comparison dogs, all procedures were conducted in accordance with national and institutional animal welfare regulations. Dogs were bred and housed in a research facility and were sampled during necropsy at the conclusion of unrelated research studies. The intestinal biopsy samples used in the present study were surplus samples obtained from these prior research protocols and were not collected specifically for the study.

Results

Forty-two dogs diagnosed with IRE between 2020 and 2023 were retrospectively included in the study based on predefined clinical and histologic criteria. Dogs were recruited from 5 referral hospitals with the following distribution: Oniris VetAgroBio, Nantes, France (n = 19), CHV Frégis, Arcueil, France (n = 13), Hospital Veterinari Canis, Palma de Mallorca, Spain (n = 4), University of Lisbon, Lisbon, Portugal (n = 4), and AniCura Aquivet Referral Hospital, Eysines, France (n = 2). For each dog, a set of archived intestinal mucosal biopsy samples (duodenal, ileal, colonic, or some combination of these) was available for analysis. In addition, archived intestinal biopsy sample from 12 healthy research dogs with no history of gastrointestinal disease or clinical signs were included as comparison group.

Histologic findings

Using the SHSS, intestinal lesions were overall more severe in dogs with IRE compared with healthy controls. Duodenal scores were markedly increased in IRE dogs compared with controls (5; IQR, 4-6.75 vs. 0; IQR, 0-2.25; P < .001). Differences at the level of the ileum (5; IQR, 3-8 vs. 4; IQR, 3-4; P = .06) and colon (7.5; IQR, 5-9 vs. 6; IQR, 5-6; P = .05) did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2).

Comparison of segmental histologic scores (duodenum, ileum, colon) between comparison group (CG, n = 12) and IRE-affected dogs (n = 42). Medians are indicated by horizontal black lines. Asterisks denote P-values from statistical comparison: *P < .05; **P < .01; ***P < .001.

Exploratory subgroup analyses comparing HA-IRE and NA-IRE dogs identified no significant differences in segmental histologic scores (Supplementary Material 1).

Analysis of individual histological criteria identified specific lesions as being more prominent in IRE dogs compared with controls. In the ileum, crypt dilatation (2; IQR, 1-3 vs. 1; IQR, 1-1; P = .03), lacteal dilatation (1; IQR, 0.75-2 vs. 0.5; IQR, 0-1; P = .02), and lamina propria cellular infiltrates (2; IQR, 1-2 vs. 1; IQR, 1-2; P = .03) were significantly more severe in IRE dogs. The infiltrate was predominantly lymphoplasmacytic, with only 2 dogs showing a moderate eosinophilic component. In the colon, crypt dilatation also was significantly increased in IRE compared with controls (2; IQR, 1-3 vs. 1; IQR, 1-2; P = .05). Other features, including fibrosis and goblet cell depletion, did not differ significantly between groups. Epithelial injury in both duodenum and ileum could not be reliably assessed because of technical artifacts, and therefore was excluded from statistical comparisons.

Measurement of RCD3 was not possible in 28 sections from 26 individuals (20 IRE-affected dogs and 6 controls) because of insufficient staining, tissue-folding artifacts or inadequate quality of digitized slides. Importantly, missing measurements concerned individual sections rather than entire cases, and most dogs remained represented by at least one intestinal segment. Tissue surface areas analyzed ranged from 1 × 106 to 2 × 106 μm2 in most cases, with smaller areas analyzed (<1 × 106 μm2) in 6 measurements because of tissue folding artifacts. Diagnostic performance of RCD3

Receiver operating characteristic curve analyses suggested that RCD3 has potential utility in discriminating IRE-affected dogs from controls across all intestinal segments. The AUC was 0.833 for the duodenum, 0.896 for the ileum, and 0.811 for the colon (Figure 5). Optimal RCD3 cut-off values, determined by Youden’s index, were 3.36% for the duodenum, 6.05% for the ileum, and 2.70% for the colon, achieving 100% specificity and sensitivities of 69.2%, 69.7%, and 55.9%, respectively.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves evaluating the ability of relative CD3+ T lymphocyte density (RCD3) to discriminate IRE-affected dogs from comparison group in duodenal, ileal, and colonic mucosal biopsies.

Statistical modeling (mixed linear model results)

A linear mixed model was used to assess the effects of intestinal segment and clinical group (IRE vs. control) on RCD3, with individual dog included as a random effect to account for repeated measures. The colon and the control group served as reference categories for the fixed effects.

Compared with the colon, duodenal samples showed significantly higher RCD3 values (estimate: +2.96; P = .003), as did ileal samples (estimate: +4.22; P < .001). Dogs with IRE also had significantly higher RCD3 values than comparison dogs (estimate: +5.94; P < .001). These findings indicate a consistent increase in RCD3 values across intestinal segments and between groups.

Random-effect variance attributed to individual dogs was 15.73 ± 3.97, whereas the residual (intra-individual) variance was 19.91 ± 4.38.

Correlation analyses

No significant correlations were found between clinical indices (CIBDAI, CCECAI) or biochemical variables and either RCD3 values or histologic scores. However, a significant negative correlation was observed between ileal histologic scores and CD3+ T lymphocyte density in IRE-affected dogs (ρ = −0.3665, P = .04). Exploratory subgroup analyses suggested that this association may be more pronounced in some subsets of dogs, but these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of these comparisons.

Discussion

We demonstrated a significant increase in CD3+ T lymphocyte density in the intestinal mucosa of dogs with IRE compared with healthy controls, across all 3 intestinal segments (duodenum, ileum, and colon). In our sample, RCD3 values discriminated IRE dogs from comparison dogs with good separation. However, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of diagnostic accuracy in a clinically relevant setting, because intestinal biopsies are not performed in asymptomatic dogs. Rather, this analysis provides an exploratory assessment of the discriminatory capacity of CD3+ T-cell density between groups under controlled conditions. These results corroborate previous reports of increased mucosal T cell infiltration in IRE-affected dogs7–10 and extend those observations to a large, multicenter group.

However, discrepancies persist in the literature, with some studies reporting no difference or even decreased T cell densities in IRE.11–13 These inconsistencies likely reflect methodological heterogeneity, including differences in immunohistochemistry protocols, quantification techniques (eg, flow cytometry vs histology), case selection, and disease severity. By employing a standardized immunohistochemical protocol and digital quantification method (QuPath), our study adds clarity to this debate while minimizing observer bias and enhancing reproducibility.22–24

Moreover, RCD3 did not correlate with clinical indices (CCECAI, CIBDAI), biochemical variables, or overall histologic scores. Exploratory subgroup analyses, including comparisons between HA-IRE and NA-IRE dogs, were not part of the primary study objectives and therefore should be interpreted with caution. This analysis suggests that mucosal CD3+ infiltration reflects the presence of intestinal inflammation rather than disease severity or activity per se, consistent with prior studies showing inconsistent changes in T cell density after clinical improvement.14–16

Interestingly, a moderate negative correlation was observed between ileal histologic scores and CD3+ density in IRE dogs. This association appeared more pronounced in some exploratory subgroup analyses, but these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of these comparisons. This unexpected inverse relationship might reflect local immune exhaustion, tissue remodeling, or segment-specific immunologic dynamics. It further emphasizes the need to interpret immunologic markers within a broader clinical and histologic context.

In addition to these findings, the increased crypt dilatation observed in colonic samples represents an interesting feature. Although its biological relevance remains uncertain, it may reflect mucosal remodeling in response to chronic inflammation. Crypt dilatation could be associated with altered epithelial turnover, mucus accumulation, or impaired crypt emptying in a persistently inflamed or dysbiotic environment, rather than representing a lesion specific to a distinct disease phenotype.25

More broadly, these findings emphasize the complexity and heterogeneity of mucosal immune responses in dogs with IRE. The substantial inter-individual variability observed in CD3+ T-cell density, including overlap between IRE dogs and controls, further supports the concept of marked immunologic heterogeneity within IRE in dogs. Such heterogeneity has been increasingly recognized in both dogs and humans with inflammatory bowel disease and likely reflects differences in underlying immune pathways, host–microbiota interactions, and genetic susceptibility rather than methodological factors alone.3,4,26 As such, the variable CD3+ signal observed across dogs may reflect distinct immunologic endotypes rather than a uniform inflammatory process, consistent with emerging endotype-based frameworks proposed for chronic enteropathies.13,27 In this context, endotypes refer to biologically distinct disease subtypes defined by specific underlying pathophysiologic mechanisms, rather than by clinical presentation alone.

Taken together, these findings suggest that increased mucosal CD3+ T-cell density primarily reflects immune activation within the intestinal mucosa rather than the intensity of clinical disease or histologic severity. In this context, CD3+ infiltration should be interpreted as a marker of immune involvement rather than a surrogate for disease activity or prognosis. This distinction is essential, because it may explain why CD3+ density increases in IRE compared with controls, yet remains poorly correlated with clinical indices or biochemical severity markers.

From a clinical perspective, these results argue against the use of CD3+ T-cell density as a standalone diagnostic or monitoring tool. Instead, CD3+ quantification may contribute to the characterization of immunologic phenotypes within IRE in dogs, particularly when integrated with other histologic, molecular, or microbiota-related variables. In this context, immunologic phenotypes refer to distinct underlying immune response patterns (eg, Th1-, Th17-, or regulatory T cell-driven responses) that may contribute to disease heterogeneity. Such an approach aligns with emerging concepts of disease endotyping in both humans and dogs with IRE.28

From a methodologic standpoint, the use of QuPath software for automated T cell quantification represents a methodological strength of our study. Compared with manual counting, this digital approach enables high-throughput, standardized analysis across large tissue areas, improving reproducibility and minimizing observer bias.22–24 Manual adjustment of detection thresholds on a slide-by-slide basis ensured consistent signal optimization while decreasing background noise.

A key limitation of our study is the use of healthy dogs as the sole control group. Although this design allowed us to establish a clear baseline for mucosal CD3+ T-cell density and to demonstrate increased immune infiltration in IRE, it does not reflect the most clinically relevant diagnostic scenarios. In clinical practice, the main challenge lies in differentiating among subtypes of chronic enteropathy and predicting treatment response. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as supporting the use of CD3+ T-cell density as a standalone diagnostic marker. Future studies should prioritize comparisons across clinically relevant enteropathy phenotypes to better define the role of mucosal immunophenotyping. Beyond this conceptual limitation, several methodological constraints also should be acknowledged. In addition, although dogs were recruited from multiple referral centers, the distribution of cases across centers was uneven, with a majority of cases originating from a limited number of institutions. This imbalance may have introduced center-specific biases related to case selection, diagnostic approaches, or clinical management. However, the multicenter design likely enhanced the external validity of the study by capturing a broader spectrum of clinical practices and disease presentations. No center-specific analysis was performed because of the limited number of cases per site. In addition, inclusion required the availability of biopsy samples from the duodenum, ileum, and colon for each dog, which may have introduced selection bias. This criterion likely favored the inclusion of dogs with more extensive or clinically complex disease, in which a complete endoscopic evaluation was performed. Consequently, dogs with milder or more localized forms of enteropathy may have been underrepresented in the study population. Epithelial injury, a key component of the histologic scoring, could not be reliably assessed in a substantial proportion of samples because of technical artifacts and therefore was excluded from statistical analysis. This factor may have led to an underestimation of overall histologic severity, particularly in dogs with more advanced mucosal damage. However, other histologic features, including inflammatory infiltration and architectural alterations, were consistently evaluated and still identified significant differences between groups. The retrospective nature of the study led to incomplete datasets, particularly for some biochemical and histologic variables, which may have limited statistical power. In addition, the study lacked longitudinal follow-up, preventing assessment of treatment response. Third, spatial distribution of T cells (whether intraepithelial or lamina propria) was not evaluated. Such localization could yield insights into pathophysiology, because prior work suggests differential compartmentalization of CD3+ cells in healthy versus diseased dogs.29 Finally, we did not assess CD3+ cell subsets, despite growing evidence that alterations in T helper, cytotoxic, and regulatory T cell populations contribute to IRE pathogenesis in dogs.9–11,30 In addition, although control specimens were derived from healthy research dogs, their standardized housing and diet may have differed from those of client-owned dogs, potentially influencing baseline immune profiles. Nonetheless, ethical constraints limit the feasibility of obtaining endoscopic biopsy samples from healthy, privately-owned animals. In addition, tissue quality limitations resulted in incomplete quantification of CD3+ cells in a subset of samples and, in some cases, a decreased number of analyzable regions per section, which may have affected the robustness of the measurements. Comparison with earlier studies evaluating CD3+ T-cell density in food-responsive enteropathy (FRE) should be interpreted with caution. Changes in clinical practice, particularly the systematic use of structured dietary trials before endoscopic evaluation, likely result in fundamental differences between historical FRE cohorts and contemporary populations undergoing biopsy, thereby limiting direct comparability. Although low-grade intestinal inflammatory changes have been reported in some colony Beagles used as healthy controls, their interpretation remains uncertain, because comparable biopsy data from clinically healthy privately-owned dogs of diverse breeds are extremely limited.

Conclusion

Our study provides robust evidence of increased mucosal CD3+ T-cell density in dogs with IRE compared with healthy controls, supporting a role for T lymphocytes in disease pathogenesis. However, the absence of association between CD3+ density and clinical, biochemical, or overall histologic severity emphasizes the limitations of CD3+ quantification as a marker of disease activity or prognosis.

Rather than serving as a diagnostic or monitoring tool, mucosal CD3+ T-cell density appears to reflect immune engagement within the intestinal mucosa and may contribute to the identification of immunologic phenotypes within the heterogeneous spectrum of IRE in dogs. The substantial inter-individual variability observed in CD3+ T-cell density further supports the existence of heterogeneous immune activation states across dogs, which may reflect distinct underlying immunologic phenotypes rather than differences in disease severity alone. These findings emphasize the need for integrative approaches combining immunologic, histologic, microbiota-related, and clinical data to better capture the complexity of disease heterogeneity.

Future studies should focus on spatial and functional characterization of T-cell subsets, longitudinal assessment of immune dynamics, and integration with clinical outcomes to refine disease stratification and guide targeted therapeutic strategies.
