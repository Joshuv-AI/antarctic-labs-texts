Sexual and Reproductive Health Service Utilization and Associated Factors Among Youth With Disability in Northwest Ethiopia: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Hana Sewalem, Tigist Abuhay, Zemenu Shiferaw Yadita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAdolescents with disabilities are often perceived as sexually inactive and thus overlooked in sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, particularly in low‐resource settings like Ethiopia. Despite having the same SRH needs as their non‐disabled peers, they face significant marginalization. This study aimed to assess SRH service utilization among adolescents with disabilities in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia, and identify key factors influencing access.MethodsA community‐based cross‐sectional survey was conducted among 371 adolescents with disabilities selected through systematic random sampling. Data were collected via a pre‐tested structured questionnaire using Epicollect5 and analyzed using SPSS version 27. Binary logistic regression, both bivariable and multivariable, was used to determine factors associated with SRH service use. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe overall utilization of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services among youth with disabilities was 29.4% (95%CI: 25.3–33.5). Youths who had acquired their disability at an early age of childhood [AOR = 2.87, 95%CI: 1.46–5.62], having physical impairment [AOR = 4.14, 95%CI: 2.12–8.10], good knowledge [AOR = 2.94, 95% CI: 1.40–6.15], being one's own source of income [AOR = 0.180, 95%CI: 0.05–0.31], unfavorable attitude [AOR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.22–1.06], and lack of respectful services [AOR = 0.12, 95%CI: 0.05–0.31] were found to be significantly associated with SRH service use.ConclusionSRH service utilization among youth with disabilities remains low and far below national and global targets. Factors such as impairment type, age at disability onset, income source, knowledge, and attitude significantly influenced service use. The findings highlight the need to enhance SRH awareness, foster positive attitudes, promote economic empowerment, and ensure inclusive, respectful healthcare delivery for young people with disabilities. Targeted interventions are crucial to bridge the accessibility gap and uphold the SRH rights of this underserved group.Keywords: Bahir Dar, disability, Ethiopia, reproductive health, sexual health, youth

Introduction

Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) refers to physical, emotional, mental, and social well‐being in all aspects of sexuality and reproduction—not just the absence of disease. For youth with disabilities (YPWD), SRH is both a health issue and a matter of human rights. YPWD remain among the most vulnerable groups, facing discrimination, poverty, social isolation, and limited access to education and employment [1]. Globally, around 1 billion people live with disabilities (15.6% of the population), including 180–220 million young people—nearly 80% of whom live in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs). In Ethiopia alone, about 15 million people have disabilities. Despite these numbers, many, especially youth, are excluded from health services, education, and jobs. Harmful myths, such as the belief that people with disabilities are asexual, contribute to stigma and denial of SRH services. Ensuring access to SRH information and care for YPWD is essential for promoting their health, dignity, and social inclusion [2, 3, 4].

Sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service accessibility remains a major issue for young persons with disabilities (YPWD), who are often left out of health education and promotion due to inaccessible communication and neglect of their needs. Exclusion results in unequal access to screenings, preventive care, and treatment, which sustains health inequities. Article 25 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) guarantees the right to the highest attainable standard of health, including SRH services, without discrimination for persons with disabilities. Similarly, Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.7 guarantees universal SRH education and care, requiring disability‐inclusive policy incorporation into national health agendas [1, 5, 6]. Globally, women with disabilities represent about 10% of women, with the majority living in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [7, 8, 9]. In Africa, even though 42% of women in the reproductive age group use contraceptives, 22% of them have yet to have their SRH needs met, most notably in Sub‐Saharan Africa. In Ghana, 87% of YPWD report poor access to SRH services, while in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, only 26.1% of YPWD had accessed such services [10, 11, 12]. Limited access leaves them vulnerable to unsafe abortions, unplanned pregnancies, and STIs. Alarmingly, 62.5% of pregnancies with disabilities among Addis Ababa young women were unwanted and 87.5% sought unsafe abortion [1, 13, 14, 15].

There are various barriers to SRH access among YPWD. These include physical inaccessibility of health facilities, healthcare worker stigma, lack of disability‐friendly communication (e.g., the use of sign language), parental opposition, and general SRH information overall tailored to their needs [7, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. The Ethiopian National Adolescent and Youth Health Strategy (2016–2020) recognizes the SRH needs of young people with disabilities [21], but in the lack of dedicated data on the use of SRH among them, interventions will not be effective and non‐reflective of their real issues [22]. Secondly, YPWD are often excluded from education systems, contributing to illiteracy, joblessness, and fixed economic disadvantage [23, 24]. This gap in information is further intensified by the continuity of incomplete, youth‐unfriendly, and inaccessible education in SRH, coupled with the absence of public awareness programs that reinforce social stigma and marginalization [22, 25, 26, 27].

Despite increasing recognition of the SRH needs of disabled youth, little has been documented about this, especially in specific urban Ethiopian settings such as Bahir Dar City. The present study therefore, aimed to establish the level of SRH service utilization and its associated factors among disabled youth in the city. Closing this knowledge gap is crucial to assist in informing inclusive, equitable, and effective SRH interventions for one of Ethiopia's most marginalized groups of youth.

Methods

Study Design, Setting and Period

A cross‐sectional study was conducted from 27th January to 27th February 2025, in Bahir Dar City Administration, the capital of the Amhara Region, 560 kilometers northwest of the national capital, Addis Ababa. The city health facilities consist of two government specialized hospitals, one primary hospital, five private hospitals, and ten health centers. According to 2021 data, the Bahir Dar population is 406,433, and of this number, 214,393 are females and almost 1,500 have disabilities.

Population

The source population comprised all youths aged 15 to 24 years with disabilities residing in Bahir Dar City, Northwest Ethiopia. The study population included all youths within this age group who were registered with disability associations in Bahir Dar City.

Inclusion Criteria: Youth aged 15–24 years with physical, hearing, or visual impairments who had lived in Bahir Dar City for at least 6 months were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Exclusion Criteria: Youth with mental disabilities, those with both hearing and visual impairments, and individuals who were critically ill or unable to respond to the interview were excluded from the study.

Sample Size Determination

The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula based on the following assumptions: 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and 36.22% estimated proportion of SRH service utilization from a previous study [7]. A 10% additional sample was added in anticipation of non‐responses, bringing the total sample size to 389.

Sampling Procedure

The systematic random sampling technique was utilized in the selection of the study subjects. The sampling frame was a list of all the eligible youths with disabilities that was obtained from disability associations and Kebele administrations. Participants were selected based on gender and type of disability to be able to have balance and diversity. The sampling interval (K) was determined using the formula K = 1316/389 ≈ 5, where 1316 is the total number of participants eligible and 389 is the calculated sample size. A random starting point was selected, and every 5th individual on the list was included in the study until the required sample size was obtained.

Measurements and Variables

Variables

The study variables are both independent and dependent. The dependent variable is utilization of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, which was categorized into Yes or No. The independent variables incorporate a wide range of factors from socio‐demographic and economic characteristics. = Other independent variables are knowledge, attitude, and personal characteristics, health facility related characteristics as well as SRH issues discussion.

Operational Definitions and Concepts

Youth: Individuals aged between 15 and 24 years, as classified by the United Nations and previous studies [7, 19, 22].

Youth with Disabilities: Young people disabled due to an accident, trauma, inherited disorder, or sickness that limits one or more abilities, such as mobility, hearing, or vision [24].

History of Sexual Exposure: Those who have ever had sexual intercourse during their lifetime are to be considered as having a history of sexual exposure; those who have not are to be categorized as having no history of sexual exposure [25].

Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) Knowledge: Assessed using 14 knowledge items, and the scores are 0 to 36. Individuals who score above 50% are ranked as having good knowledge, while individuals who score 50% or below are labeled as having poor knowledge [28, 29].

Attitude towards SRH: Measured with the assistance of seven items on matters related to SRH on a five‐point Likert scale (“strongly agree” to “strongly disagree”). A sum score is obtained, and participants scoring over or equal to the mean are considered to have an acceptable attitude and those below the mean are categorized as having an unacceptable attitude [30].

Overall SRH Service Utilization: Adolescent respondents who accessed at least one of the four priority SRH services—SRH information and education, family planning services, VCT, or STI diagnosis and treatment—within the past 12 months are included in SRH service utilizers. Those who did not access any of them are non‐utilizers [14, 17, 25, 28, 31].

Kebeles—the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia.

Data Collection Tool and Procedure

Data was collected using an interviewer‐administered structured questionnaire using the Epicollect5 mobile data collection tool. The questionnaire covered key issues such as socio‐demographic and economic profiles, attitudes and knowledge regarding sexual and reproductive health (SRH), personal characteristics, and health facility factors. The tool was initially designed in English, then Amharic, local language and back‐translated into English to standardize, make clear, and ensure consistency. The data collection team consisted of eight data collectors who were trained and three supervisors. Two professional sign language interpreters were also included to accommodate participants with hearing impairment. Gender matching was ensured during interviews: female interviewers interviewed females and male interviewers interviewed males. A pilot study was conducted 2 weeks prior to the actual collection of data in Injibara town, with 5% of the total sample size (15 participants). For ensuring quality data, a pre‐tested and high‐quality questionnaire was utilized. Data collectors and supervisors were trained, with orientation on tool administration and utilizing the Epicollect5 system. Supervision was ongoing by supervisors and the principal investigators throughout the time of data collection to ensure completeness, accuracy, and protocol adherence.

Data Processing and Analysis

Following data collection using Epicollect5, the dataset was cleaned for completeness and accuracy. The data cleaned were exported into Microsoft Excel and imported into SPSS version 27 for analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were computed to describe data. Cross‐tabulations were also conducted to explore relationships between dependent and independent variables. To identify the association between independent variables and the outcome variable—use of SRH services—binary logistic regression analysis was performed. Bivariable logistic regression was conducted first in order to choose candidate variables for multivariable analysis. Variables with a p‐value of < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were fitted in the multivariable logistic regression model for adjustment of potential confounders and also for assessing the independent contribution of each predictor to the outcome. In the multivariable model, variables that were statistically significant had a p‐value of < 0.05. Strength of associations was expressed as adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals. Goodness‐of‐fit of the final model was checked (Hosmer and Lemeshow test), revealed a p‐value of 0.76.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical approval was sought from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of GAMBY Medical and Business College. A letter of permission was also officially obtained from Amhara Public Health Institute (APHI). City and sub‐city officials of all selected kebeles were also given further permission before data collection. All the participants were provided with sufficient information about the purpose of the study, data collection procedures, and potential benefits. All the participants gave written consent, and voluntary participation was strictly adhered to. Confidentiality was ensured by not recording or documenting any personal identifiers such as participants' names, at any stage of the study.

Socio‐Demographic Characteristics

A total of 371 individuals participated in the survey, giving a response rate of 95.3%. Of these, 68.2% were male. The mean age of the study participants was 19.26 years (1.14 Standard deviations). The majority (53.9%) had physical impairments, and disability was most typically either congenital/from birth (36.4%) or childhood (37.7%). In terms of education, 38.3% possessed primary education, and most of the participants were single (74.7%) (Table 1).

Knowledge of Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH)

Overall, only 24% of respondents had good knowledge regarding SRH. Nevertheless, some areas of comprehension were quite good. For instance, 90% identified at least one method for preventing pregnancy. When asked to cite the most fertile period during the menstrual cycle, 43.1% responded after menstruation. Knowledge about modern contraceptives was not even. Oral contraceptive pills (24.0%) and injectables (25.1%) were the best known. About 93.5% of the respondents knew STDs.

Awareness of HIV/AIDS was also considerable, with 94.6% of the interviewees reporting having heard about the disease. Unsafe sex was the major means of transmission (59.8%), followed by contact with infected blood (17.8%) and sharing needles (14.3%). Prevention practices most highly recommended involved abstinence (40.2%), followed by condom use (21.6%). (Table 2).

Attitude on Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH)

Overall, only 33.2% of the participants had a favorable attitude towards SRH. Despite this, the majority stated that they were in favor of SRH education among youth. Specifically, 69% agreed that young people need information on SRH. About attitudes toward HIV‐related stigma, 69% of the respondents disagreed with the view that HIV is a punishment from God. SRH Service Utilization and Reproductive History

Among the young people with disabilities surveyed, overall sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service use was 29.4% (95% CI: 25.3%–33.5%). Out of the 107 young people with visual impairments, 42.0% used SRH services. Only 25.0% of the 64 young people with hearing impairments had used the services. Of the 200 youths with physical impairments, only 24.0% had used SRH services. Out of the 118 female children with disabilities, 32.2% had used SRH services, whereas 28.0% of the 253 male children with disabilities had used them.

In terms of sexual activity and utilization of health services, 35.8% ever had sexual intercourse, of whom 17% had done so in the past year. Only 17.8%, however, used contraceptives in the past year, and 14.3% were using a method now—largely pills, injectables, and implants. Coverage of HIV testing was also extremely low at only 12.7% ever had VCT, although 72.8% reported that they would like to test for HIV in the future. Despite these levels of awareness and interest, a large proportion of youths with disabilities faced significant barriers to accessing SRH services. A large proportion, 74.4% of them were not satisfied with the services they received. A staggering 82.5% of the participants never spoke to their parents or guardians about SRH issues. Additionally, 39.9% of the participants required support to reach SRH services, and only 29.4% reached any SRH services.

Health Facility Service‐Related Factors

Only 37.5% of the respondents reported that there is good communication of SRH services at local health facilities, 42% did not agree, and 20.5% reported that it is occasional. There were available communication materials for disabled individuals, reported by 38.3%, unavailable to 31.3%, and occasionally available to 30.5%. For provider interaction, 31.8% said they were treated with respect and dignity, 37.5% disagreed, and 30.7% had mixed experiences. Similarly, 31.3% said providers were willing to adjust for disabilities, 29.9% disagreed, and 38.8% had mixed responses. Provider‐negative attitudes were witnessed by 44.2%, while 28.6% disagreed and 27.2% had both. Physical accessibility remains limited: 13.5% reported mobility‐friendly entrances, 32.1% disagreed, and 54.4% experienced variability. Available toilets for those with disabilities were only reported by 25.3%.

Factors Associated With SRH Service Utilization

In the multivariable logistic regression model, six variables remained statistically significant (p ≤ 0.05): onset of disability, type of disability, having a source of income, respectful service provision, attitude toward SRH, and knowledge of SRH.

Youth who acquired their disability during early childhood had higher odds of SRH services utilization than those who acquired it since birth [AOR = 2.87; 95% CI: 1.46–5.62]. Similarly, those with hearing impairments [AOR = 3.09; 95% CI: 1.37–6.97] or physical impairments [AOR = 4.14; 95% CI: 2.12–8.10] had higher odds of service utilization compared to those youths with visual impairment. Good knowledge of SRH was a strong positive predictor, with knowledgeable youth being nearly three times more likely to use SRH services [AOR = 2.94; 95% CI: 1.40–6.15]. Conversely, having an unfavorable attitude toward SRH services 52% reduced the likelihood of utilization [AOR = 0.48; 95% CI: 0.22–1.06], as did lacking a personal source of income [AOR = 0.180; 95% CI: 0.05–0.31] were 80.2% less likely to use SRH services. Those youths who had previous experience of receiving disrespectful care were 87.6% less likely to use SRH service than their counterparts [AOR = 0.12; 95% CI: 0.05–0.31], (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the utilization of Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) services and identify associated factors among youths with disabilities in Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia. The utilization of young people with disabilities in Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia, to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services was also significantly low at 29.4% reporting use of the services. In addition, a low percentage of only 24% of the participants reported satisfactory knowledge of SRH, and only 33.2% reported positive attitudes towards these services. These results point to significant deficits in knowledge, health literacy, and attitudinal acceptability, each of which is a probable cause of SRH service underuse in this group. Against other studies done in Ethiopia's other regions—Southern Ethiopia (40.5%) and Dessie City (42%)—this study's recorded utilization rate stands much lower. Also, the insignificant percentage of youths who had positive attitudes is as observed, in keeping with previous studies done in Gondar and Eastern Ethiopia that also noted minimal numbers of positive views among disabled youths towards SRH services. The consistency of such observations across different regions in the country shows systemic problems among disabled youth in the country [27, 32, 33, 34, 35]. The very low levels of knowledge and positive attitudes in Bahir Dar can be attributed to contextual factors like limited access to people with different types of disabilities‐inclusive health education, social stigma, and communication barriers among youths with different types of disabilities. Such barriers may even be more intense in this urban setting because of inadequate disability‐friendly infrastructure or the lack of well‐trained health personnel sensitive to the needs of persons with disabilities. In addition, social exclusion and prevailing cultural stigmas about sexuality and disability might deter service‐seeking behavior even more. Children who acquired disabilities during early childhood were more likely than children with congenital disabilities to make use of SRH services—a trend also observed in Gondar [27, 36]. When disabilities occur earlier in life, it may be easier for children to be included in school and exposed to sexual and reproductive health (SRH) information, which can affect how likely they are to use SRH services later on. The type of disability also had a key role in service use. Adolescents with hearing or physical disabilities were more utilized than those with vision disabilities, consistent with evidence from the Amhara Region and Gondar [37]. Only a few visually impaired adolescents indicate they have SRH information in an accessible form, e.g., Braille or audio [37, 38].

Economic status was also important. Adolescents without their own source of income were significantly less likely to utilize SRH services, echoing data from Eastern Ethiopia where economic self‐sufficiency was correlated with greater service use [7, 30, 34]. The association between income generation and higher service use suggests that economic dependence may limit youths' ability to access care, either by restricting their financial means or reinforcing power imbalances that reduce autonomy in health‐related decision‐making. Respectful treatment by providers was an important enabler. Young people who reported respectful treatment were more likely to use care, consistent with Addis Ababa and Amhara Region evidence highlighting the deterrent role of discriminatory provider attitudes [39, 40]. The significance of respectful provider treatment highlights the pressing need for disability‐inclusive provider training that fosters empathy, communication skills, and anti‐discriminatory practices. Disrespectful or dismissive interactions not only deter future service use but can also deepen feelings of exclusion and mistrust among youths already facing marginalization. This underscores that health systems must invest in creating inclusive service environments—characterized by trust, confidentiality, and physical accessibility—while also establishing accountability mechanisms to address discriminatory behaviors.

Knowledge of SRH issues was also one of the significantly associated factors for SRH service use. Individuals with good knowledge were nearly three times more likely to use SRH services, as in line with the Gondar and Eastern Ethiopia studies [27, 34]. Awareness enables people to know their needs and speak on their behalf for care. A favorable attitude towards SRH services was associated with service use. Adolescents with positive attitudes were more likely to access services, a trend also seen in studies in Uganda and Kenya, where positive attitudes and cultural openness enhanced use among adolescents with disabilities [41, 42]. Unfavorable attitudes are typically driven by stigma and myth regarding disability and sexuality. Cross‐country international evidence in Ghana and Tanzania also suggests that community‐based interventions such as peer support and education‐inclusive disability can have an instrumental effect on facilitating SRH service use among youth with disabilities [43, 44].

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

This study presents valuable data on the use of sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services by disabled adolescents, a group typically overlooked in research, policy, and programming. It highlights significant gaps in terms of services and informs us of the need for more inclusive approaches. The cross‐sectional study design limits causal inference, however, and excluding adolescents with mental disabilities or dual visual and hearing impairments reduces generalizability. Future research must be larger‐scale and more funded to be able to access these groups with trained interviewers and assistive communication aids. In addition, the utilization of self‐reported data may result in recall and social desirability bias since the topic is extremely sensitive.

Conclusions and Recommendations

Utilization of Sexual and Reproductive Health (SRH) services among youth with disabilities remains significantly low, falling short of both national and global targets. This study identified several key determinants influencing SRH service uptake, including the nature of the impairment, early age at onset of disability, access to a personal source of income, level of SRH knowledge, and attitudes toward SRH services. To address these gaps, a multi‐faceted approach is recommended. Key steps include improving SRH knowledge and attitudes of disabled youth, economically enabling them, and ensuring inclusive, accessible, and nondiscriminatory SRH services. Collaboration with Disabled Persons' Associations is key in peer education, advocacy, and referral of services. A coordinated rights‐based approach is indicated to increase equal access to SRH. Future large‐scale studies, including qualitative research, are indicated for further understanding YPWD's views and needs.
