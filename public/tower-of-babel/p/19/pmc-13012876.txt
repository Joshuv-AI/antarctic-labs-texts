Sequence and structure analysis for chemosensory proteins and odorant-binding proteins in Nilaparvata lugens (Hemiptera: Delphacidae)
Authors: Xiaoyi Dou, Russell Jurenka, Xiao-Qiang Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemosensation is crucial for insect behavior and physiology, involving several molecules. Chemosensory proteins (CSPs) and odorant-binding proteins (OBPs) transport volatile and lipophilic semiochemicals to activate the chemosensory system. Nilaparvata lugens Stål, is a monophagous pest of rice plants, causing significant economic losses worldwide. To date, 15 CSPs and 13 OBPs have been characterized, but their functions have not been fully investigated especially regarding their binding affinity with various ligands. This study compares the commonalities and differences between the 15 CSPs and 13 OBPs regarding sequence similarity, phylogeny, structural identity, and binding affinity with 3 different ligands. As expected, all 15 CSPs contain 4 conserved cysteine residues and 6 α-helical domains, whereas 13 OBPs have at least 6 cysteine residues and 6 α-helical domains. CSP1, 2, 8, and 14 cluster together in the phylogenetic tree, showing high structural similarity and similar binding affinity to the 3 ligands. OBP9 and 10 form a clade with high structural conservation; however, molecular docking indicates OBP9 exhibits a high binding affinity for the insecticide thiamethoxam. Three OBPs, OBP56d, OBPunknown1, and OBPunknown2 have been characterized and they display no significant preference for the 3 ligands. Overall, our integrated sequence and structural analysis reveals both functional redundancy and specific ligand preferences within the N. lugens chemosensory system. These findings provide a critical foundation for understanding the molecular basis of chemoreception in N. lugens and may inform the development of novel behavioral interference strategies.Keywords: chemical reception, ligand, structural alignment, molecular docking

Introduction

Chemical communication plays crucial roles in insect behavior, including food source search, mating, and oviposition. Insects perceive these chemical signals including pheromones and semiochemicals through a sophisticated olfactory system (Hildebrand 1995). Several proteins and steps are involved in this process. Odorant-binding proteins (OBPs) and chemosensory proteins (CSPs) are primarily responsible for the initial chemical detection (Vogt et al. 2002, Pelosi et al. 2006). These chemicals are then transported to receptors such as gustatory receptors, odorant receptors, and ionotropic receptors, inducing potential behavioral responses. Sensory neuron membrane proteins contribute to pheromone detection, while odorant-degrading enzymes help inactivate the signal, enhancing insect olfactory sensitivity. Since CSPs and OBPs are the first gatekeepers for chemical signals, their interaction with these chemicals is a key determinant of olfactory perception. Many CSPs and OBPs have been identified in various insect models, yet the differences between CSPs and OBPs in sequence, structure, and binding affinity have not been thoroughly analyzed.

Both CSPs and OBPs are low molecular weight proteins, approximately 12 to 20 kDa, but they share no sequence similarity (Pelosi et al. 2005, Sun et al. 2019). CSPs are found not only in the antennae but also in the wings, legs, pheromone glands, and proboscis, indicating their involvement in various functions such as larval development, tissue regeneration, and immunity, beyond chemoreception. OBPs are usually expressed in the antennae, playing roles in plant odorant reception, pheromone signal transduction, host selection, and mating behavior (Xu et al. 2005, Gholizadeh et al. 2015).

Nilaparvata lugens Stål (Hemiptera: Delphacidae), is a serious pest of rice due to its monophagous feeding habits. The identification of hosts involves the sensing of volatile organic compounds produced by the plant (Xu et al. 2002, Cheng et al. 2013). Indeed, pest infestation alters this chemical landscape by inducing certain repellent volatiles (Zhang et al. 2021). This signals the development of complex chemical dialogues in which plant defense signaling is one step ahead of the insect chemosensory system. Thus, the whole process of host selection, from initial attraction to active repellents, occurs at chemosensory level. Under these conditions, strong dependence of N. lugens on this sensory modality means the interactions with its hosts will make the chemosensory system one important target for innovative pest management strategies.

Due to the importance of chemosensation in N. lugens, 15 CSPs and 10 OBPs have been identified using various methods (Zhou et al. 2014, Deng et al. 2025b). Both CSPs and OBPs are regarded as chemosensory molecules in olfactory neurons, recognizing different chemical messages (Rutzler and Zwiebel 2005). However, the differences between these 2 protein types are not well understood. In this study, we characterized an additional 3 new OBPs and compared CSPs and OBPs in terms of sequence, structure, and binding affinity to various chemicals in N. lugens. The ligands in this study include the sex pheromone bombykol from Bombyx mori, methyl salicylate from plants, and thiamethoxam, a neonicotinoid insecticide. We utilized these 3 compounds as different structural ligands. Bombykol is a primary alcohol utilized as a sex pheromone for the moth B. mori Linnaeus. This serves as a type of control since there is no reason that N. lugens would recognize this molecule. Methyl salicylate was utilized because it is one of the main volatile repellents for N. lugens (Zhang et al. 2021). Thiamethoxam was utilized because it is one of the major neonicotinoid insecticides used in the control of N. lugens and it can develop high resistance to it, and recent studies have shown that neonicotinoid resistance could be caused by chemosensory mechanisms (Zhang et al. 2022).

Materials and Methods

Sequence Alignment

The sequences of 15 CSPs and 13 OBPs from N. lugens were retrieved based on homology to 20 CSPs and 52 OBPs from Drosophila melanogaster Meigen. Homology searches were conducted against the nonredundant protein sequences and Transcriptome Shotgun Assembly databases at the National Center for Biotechnology Information. Among these, the sequences for CSP12–15 were obtained from a previous study (Deng et al. 2025b). Two proteins, designated OBPunknown1 and OBPunknown2, were identified as uncharacterized proteins containing a PBP_GOBP domain. OBPunknown2 also possesses a large tegument protein UL36 domain; this domain was excluded from subsequent sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis. The GenBank accession numbers are shown in Table 1. The alignment was performed using the ClustalW program and modified using the Jalview application (Waterhouse et al. 2009).

Phylogenetic Analysis

Two phylogenetic trees were constructed using the full-length amino acid sequences of N. lugens CSPs and OBPs, with the exception of OBPunknown2. Additional sequences from D. melanogaster, B. mori, and Tribolium castaneum Herbst were included for comparison. All sequences were aligned using the ClustalW program. The trees were built with MEGA X (Stecher et al. 2020) with 1,000 bootstrap replicates using the maximum likelihood algorithm and were modified using the EvolView online program (He et al. 2016).

Structural Visualization

The amino acid sequences of all CSPs and OBPs were queried in the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database (Varadi et al. 2024). The Uniprot accession numbers are shown in Table 1. For proteins with available models, the PDB files containing the three-dimensional (3D) structures were downloaded and visualized using the PyMOL program (Schrodinger 2010). However, predicted structures for CSP11-15, OBP56d, OBPunknown1, and OBPunknown2 were not available in the database. Therefore, the 3D structures were generated by AlphaFold Server (Abramson et al. 2024), and the models with a predicted template modeling score above 0.5 were selected for subsequent analysis. The alignment of 3D structures was also conducted using the PyMOL program, and the root mean square deviation (RMSD) and alignment scores were recorded.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina (Eberhardt et al. 2021) to evaluate the binding affinities of 3 distinct ligands against OBPs and CSPs, which began with the preparation of the ligands by first drawing their 2D structures in ChemDraw and subsequently generating their 3D conformations using Chem3D. The protein structures were prepared by removing water molecules and adding polar hydrogens, after which a grid box was defined to encompass the known binding pocket of each protein for the docking simulation. The resulting poses for each ligand–protein complex were then visualized in PyMOL to identify the most favorable binding mode based on the calculated affinity score and to analyze potential hydrogen bonds sites.

Results

Sequence Alignment of CSPs and OBPs

Our analysis showed that both CSPs and OBPs in N. lugens were small proteins of about 200 amino acids with a molecular weight ranging from 10 to 20 kDa (Fig. 1). The 15 CSP sequences were diverse but had 4 conserved cysteine residues (Fig. 1a), a feature of the CSP family proteins, which were linked to form 2 disulfide bridges (Angeli et al. 1999). Additionally, 1 proline (Pro) residue was conserved across all CSPs. Some CSPs also shared certain conserved amino acids, including leucine (Leu), arginine (Arg), glycine (Gly), glutamine (Gln), and tyrosine (Tyr).

Sequence alignment of (a) CSPSs and (b) OBPs from Nilaparvata lugens. The alignment was conducted with ClustalW. The color shaded amino acids are identical conserved regions. The blue colored residues are cysteine. The other-colored residues are other conserved amino acids.

Sequence alignment from 13 OBPs in N. lugen revealed that while these OBPs were widely divergent, all possessed the 6 highly conserved cysteine residues typical of this protein family (Fig. 1b). We classified them based on their cysteine patterns: OBP1-8, OBP56d, OBPunknown1, and OBPunknown2 were classic OBPs with only 6 cysteines, while OBP9 and OBP10 were Plus-C OBPs, possessing additional cysteines. Furthermore, we found that OBP4, OBP9, OBP10, OBPunknown1, and OBPunknown2 lacked an N-terminal signal peptide, a feature presented in the other OBPs which had peptides of approximately 20 amino acids.

Phylogenetic Analysis of CSPs and OBPs

Only 1 domain was found in each CSP or OBP (Fig. 2) because of the small size, except for OBPunknown1, which contained 2 domains. All CSPs in N. lugens belong to the insect pheromone-binding A10/OS-D superfamily, which was thought to be involved in insect chemosensation. All CSPs were classified into 7 groups and were closely related to CSPs in different insects (Fig. 2a). CSP1, CSP2, CSP8, and CSP14 were classified together and were closely related to T. castaneum CSPs (TcasCSPs), while CSP11, CSP12, CSP13 were close to B. mori CSP12 (BmorCSP12). CSP3, CSP4, and CSP5 were grouped together with TcasCSP2, TcasCSP5, and TcasCSP7, respectively. CSP6, CSP7, and CSP10 were grouped together with TcasCSP1, TcasCSP8, and BmorCSP16. However, CSP9 and CSP15 were independent from other CSPs in N. lugens, T. castaneum, and B. mori, but CSP15 was more closely related to the groups containing CSP1 and CSP11. None of the CSPs in N. lugens were closely related to CSPs in D. melanogaster.

Three-Dimensional Structure Modeling and Alignment of CSPs and OBPs

Structures were visualized using PyMOL (Figs 3 and 4). All CSPs contained at least 6 α-helices and N-terminal signal peptides (Fig. 3). CSP5, CSP7, CSP9, and CSP11 also contained an α-helix within the signal peptide. We aligned the structures of CSPs to each other and found CSP1, CSP2, CSP8, CSP14 to be nearly identical, with the alignment scores of 410, 487, and 389, and the RMSD values of 0.4, 0.2, 0.5 Å, respectively (Table 2). Moreover, CSP12 and CSP13 are identical, with an alignment score of 598 and RMSD of 2.09 Å. Pairwise alignments of the remaining CSPs showed low identity, with either low alignment scores or high RMSD values.

Docking of N. lugens CSPs and OBPs to the Three Ligands

Three compounds, including the sex pheromone bombykol from B. mori, plant volatile methyl salicylate, and the insecticide thiamethoxam, were used as ligands for binding site prediction. The dissociation constant representing binding affinity is shown in Table 4. Lower scores indicate higher affinity. All CSPs and OBPs showed low binding affinity to bombykol, while their affinities for the other 2 ligands varied. Methyl salicylate had high binding affinity for CSP4 and OBP3, and thiamethoxam had strong binding affinity for CSP14 and OBP56d. The binding site and structures for these high-affinity interactions were shown in Fig. 5. Clearly, bombykol bound to the surface of CSP4 and OBP3, and the cavity of CSP14 and OBP56d, but did not have a specific hydrogen binding site with the proteins. Methyl salicylate bound within the cavity of the 4 proteins, but only CSP4 and OBP56d had specific hydrogen binding sites. It formed a hydrogen bond with GLN-95 of CSP4 at a distance of 2.9 Å and with PHE-145 of OBP56d at a distance of 1.8 Å. Thiamethoxam bound to the surface of the CSP4 and OBP3, and within the cavity of CSP14 and OBP56d, forming specific hydrogen bonds with each protein. It bound to the amino acids GLN-149 and ARG-96 of CSP4 with distances of 3.4, 3.4, and 3.0. It also bonded to GLN-80 of CSP14 at a distance of 3.1 Å. Thiamethoxam formed a single hydrogen bond with OBP3 and OBP9, interacting with GLN-133 of OBP3 at a distance of 3.4 Å, while no specific hydrogen bond was observed with OBP56d.

Discussion

Chemosensory processes in insects involve numerous molecules, with CSPs and OBPs playing crucial roles in binding odorants and activating signals. To date, CSPs and OBPs have been reported in many insect species across different orders, including Lepidoptera (Qiao et al. 2013), Hymenoptera (Briand et al. 2002), Blattodea (Kitabayashi et al. 1998), Orthoptera (Zhou et al. 2013), and Hemiptera (Gu et al. 2012). While most OBPs were identified in the antennae, CSPs can be detected in various tissues, including antennae, sex pheromone glands (Jacquin-Joly et al. 2001, Dou et al. 2019), female reproductive organs (Zhou et al. 2013), and male ejaculatory bulbs (Dyanov and Dzitoeva 1995), indicating the distinct roles of CSPs and OBPs. In this study, we used N. lugens as the insect model and bioinformatics tools to analyze the differences between the 15 CSPs and 10 OBPs for which the genes have been identified (Zhou et al. 2014), and 3 uncharacterized OBPs that are homologous to those in D. melanogaster. We selected 3 ligands for molecular docking based on the ecological and physiological relevance to N. lugens. First, we used methyl salicylate, a major volatile compound from its exclusive host plant, rice, to represent plant odorants. Second, we included the sex pheromone bombykol from B. mori as a model for pheromone-binding interactions, despite its likely irrelevance to N. lugens behavior, to provide a comparative baseline. Finally, given the emerging link between chemosensory proteins and insecticide resistance (Zhang et al. 2022), we also tested the neonicotinoid insecticide thiamethoxam as a ligand.

OBPs and CSPs both have conserved cysteine motifs. CSPs generally have 4 cysteines forming 2 disulfide bridges (Zhou et al. 2006), while classic OBPs present 6 conserved cysteines (Hekmat-Scafe et al. 2002). Plus-C OBPs are distinguished by at least 2 additional cysteines and a conserved proline after the sixth cysteine (Xu et al. 2003, Zhou et al. 2004). In this study, all 15 CSPs contained 4 cysteines and conserved motifs for asparagine, arginine, and proline. Other conserved amino acid motifs such as leucine, glycine, glutamine, tyrosine, and proline were identified among CSPs, indicating their conserved functional roles. Our results clearly showed that the 13 OBPs are diverse, particularly OBP9 and OBP10. OBP1-8, OBP56d, OBPunknown1, and OBPunknown2 contain 6 cysteines and are classified as classic OBPs, while OBP9 and OBP10 have 3 additional cysteines and belong to the Plus-C type. The diverse sequences and different types of OBPs suggest a broader functional diversity in N. lugens.

Based on an integrated analysis of sequence alignment, phylogeny, protein structure, molecular docking, and published functional data, we classify the N. lugens CSPs into 5 functional groups. Accordingly, the clade of CSP1, CSP2, CSP8, and CSP14 comprises most probably chemosensory and mating-associated proteins. They are tightly clustered within a phylogenetic tree with similarly low structural divergence (RMSD≤0.8 Å), thus reflecting a high degree of evolutionary conservation. In this vein, there are several binding modes concerning the proteins, which do not appear to be particularly effective at binding any of the three tested ligands. The expression profiles support functions in mating and host-seeking: CSP1 is specific to the male abdomen, CSP2 shows high expression in the female (Yang et al. 2014), and CSP8 is found in mated brachypterous female wings and males’ legs and has been shown to bind to rice volatiles nerolidol and hexanal (Waris et al. 2018). Indeed, it was only leg-enriched CSP14 (Deng et al. 2025b) that showed significant in silico affinity for thiamethoxam and methyl salicylate, which implies that its role is linked to leg-mediated chemical detection. Second, CSP4, CSP5, CSP12, and CSP15 are also candidate proteins for insecticide resistance and odor detection. These ubiquitously expressed proteins are associated with detoxification; hence, their knockdown reduces detoxification gene expression and increases mortality upon imidacloprid exposure (Deng et al. 2025b). This is in line with our docking prediction that relative to thiamethoxam, CSP4, CSP12, and CSP15 have relative high binding affinity. Third, CSP9 is developmental. It has low binding affinity for our test ligands, and its expression peaks in the early nymphal instars and declines sharply in the fifth instar (Yang et al. 2014), during which time it may also contribute to nymphal development or metamorphosis. Fourth, CSP10 seems to have evolved a specialization in detecting plant volatiles. An antennal tissue-enriched protein known to bind the repellent cis-3-hexenyl acetate (Waris et al. 2020), its predicted moderate-to-high affinity for methyl salicylate during this work corroborates a function in the perception of plant defense signals. The remaining CSPs (CSP3, CSP6, CSP7, CSP11, and CSP13) had low-to-moderate affinities for the ligands tested but show widely different putative functions. The predicted stronger affinity of CSP3 for methyl salicylate rather than bombykol supports its classification as a general semiochemical transporter. However, the data obtained for CSP6 and CSP7 showed weak binding. A previous report stated that CSP7 does not bind to 17 rice volatiles and 12 other ligands (Yang et al. 2014). Interestingly, however, knockdown of CSP7 increases susceptibility to insecticides (Deng et al. 2025b), indicating its role in resistance and not olfaction. CSP11 and CSP13 are highly expressed in the abdomen and legs, respectively, with low ligand affinity, and are not overexpressed in resistant strains (Deng et al. 2025a, 2025b). Therefore, their precise roles remain to be elucidated.

Unlike CSPs, OBPs predominantly occur in the antennae and are believed to be primarily involved in olfactory signal transduction. Standard assignments divide OBPs into classes based on functional specialization. These are PBPs and GOBPs (Krieger et al. 1996, Gong et al. 2009). Phylogenetic analysis clustered OBP1, OBP7, and OBPunknown1 together, yet divergent structure indicates several different folding mechanisms and possibly other functions. OBP1 seems to be involved in repellent detection because it is one of the most highly expressed genes in antennae, and this protein is implicated in the reception of geraniol (Ke et al. 2025), which is aligned with our predicted moderate binding affinity for methyl salicylate. OBP7 is also antennal-specific and transcriptionally upregulated by nitenpyram and sulfoxaflor (Zhang et al. 2022). It is also known to be associated with chlorpyrifos resistance (Deng et al. 2025a). Our docking showed low affinity for thiamethoxam, but other insecticides need to be tested to define its resistance role. OBP2, OBP5, OBP6, and OBPunknown2 are grouped into another phylogenetic cluster. OBP5, highly expressed in the head, has a strong binding affinity for chlorpyrifos (Deng et al. 2025a). Transcriptomic data suggest that OBP6, OBP8, OBP9, and OBP10 are responsive to the main herbivore-induced rice volatile linalool (Zhang et al. 2025a, 2025b, 2026). The moderate-to-high affinity of OBP6 for methyl salicylate supports its involvement in plant volatile perception. OBP8 is implicated in host-seeking behavior (He et al. 2019); however, its affinity for methyl salicylate was not detected in this assay. Therefore, the protein may interact with other volatiles released by rice. Although OBP3 did not show any strong ligand preference in our assay, it has already been established to work in olfaction and resistance (He et al. 2011, Zhang et al. 2022). Thus, our predicted hydrogen bond with GLN-133 remains consistent with a functional binding site. OBP9 seems to be multifunctional: it is downregulated by geraniol (Ke et al. 2025), overexpressed in nitenpyram-resistant strains (Zhang et al 2022), and has high in silico affinity for both methyl salicylate and thiamethoxam—meaning that this protein may also act in odorant recognition and interaction with insecticides. The new OBP56d, OBPunknown1, and OBPunknown2 will have to be characterized further. OBP56d is relatively highly specific for all 3 ligands, with a predicted hydrogen bond to PHE-145 for methyl salicylate, which may indicate an odorant-detecting role. OBPunknown1 has 2 PBP domains and shows very high binding affinity, which may indicate complicated or dual function. OBPunknown2 clusters phylogenetically with D. melanogaster OBP69a, which is under sexually dimorphic regulation (Bentzur et al. 2018), and thus may also have noncanonical roles besides general olfaction.

Sequence, phylogenetic, structural, and ligand-binding analyses put together show that although CSPs and OBPs are mainly involved in chemical detection, they exhibit various functions. CSPs and OBPs are quite different from each other in sequence, structure, and ligand-binding profile. While some have specific roles in detecting pheromones, plant volatiles, or bitter compounds, our data maintain the prevailing view that many are involved in plant volatile detection and insecticide resistance. Ligand panels should expand in the future to include a broader range of host plant compounds, defensive volatiles, and insecticides to fully realize these complicated binding spectra. This study provides a foundational bioinformatic comparison of the CSP and OBP proteins in N. lugens. Predictions made here lay a framework for crucial future experimental validation. Proposed functions of each protein must be confirmed via detailed in vitro binding assays and functional bioassays. High-resolution structures, in turn, have to be solved for a full understanding of the molecular bases leading to functional divergence in ligand interactions.
