Relationship Between Cytokines and Face Skin Symptoms in Newborns in Two Japanese Cities
Authors: Kaori Yonezawa, Sachi Higuchi, Satsuki Shimizu, Takeo Minematsu, Megumi Haruna
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimSeveral skin disorder symptoms may appear in infants, each resulting from a different inflammatory response. In this study, we investigated the relationship between skin cytokine levels and skin symptoms in newborns.MethodsThis cross‐sectional study was conducted in Tokyo and Oita, two Japanese cities. The participants were healthy, 1‐month‐old infants and their parents. Symptoms including erythema, papules, dryness, and exudate/yellow scaling on infant faces were evaluated as outcomes. Cytokine levels (interleukin [IL]‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and IL‐17) were measured by skin blotting. A multilevel analysis using a mixed‐effects model was conducted to account for regional differences.ResultsA total of 231 infants (119 from Tokyo and 112 from Oita) participated in this study. Erythema, papules, dryness, and exudate/yellow scaling were present in 59 (25.5%), 133 (57.6%), 37 (16.0%), and 16 (6.9%) of the infants, respectively. In terms of the associations between symptoms and cytokines, there was a significant association between papules and IL‐8 positivity (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 1.94, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.09–3.47) even after adjustment for differences in barrier function, area, and skin care.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that cytokines were linked to skin conditions, even after accounting for regional differences and genetic factors. This suggests that different symptoms point to the involvement of various cytokines in skin conditions in neonates, with mechanisms varying based on the symptoms. These findings could aid in developing specific preventive strategies in the future.Keywords: cytokines, infant, newborn, papules, skin diseases

Introduction

The prevention of skin conditions during the neonatal period is essential because they can cause pain and itching in newborns, leading to percutaneous sensitization of the site, and are associated with the development of allergies later in life [1]. Skin conditions also reduce the quality of life of caregivers [2]. Therefore, the development of prevention methods is necessary.

Facial skin conditions are among the most commonly observed skin problems during the neonatal period [3]. Conditions such as infantile eczema, seborrheic eczema, neonatal acne, and sweat rashes can lead to facial skin issues. However, neonatal skin conditions are often transient, and visiting a dermatology specialist during the early postpartum period is challenging. Therefore, non‐dermatologist often provide advice on skin conditions, making accurate diagnosis challenging. Thus, focusing on symptoms remains crucial.

Each skin conditions presents distinct symptoms based on its mechanism. Conversely, different skin symptoms involve various mechanisms and inflammatory responses. Therefore, it is crucial to understand the mechanism underlying each symptom rather than focusing solely on the name of the diagnosed disorder. Skin blotting is a non‐invasive method for observing skin inflammation [4]. This method is well established in adults for examining skin cytokines around symptomatic areas. A previous study showed that it can also be used in newborns [5]. Identifying the association between cytokines and symptoms could help clarify the mechanism by which symptoms appear and may be helpful in future prevention and treatment. Furthermore, skin conditions are associated with a family history of atopic dermatitis and impaired skin barrier function. Nevertheless, previous studies are limited to a single site; investigations at multiple sites are needed.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between skin cytokines and skin conditions in newborns in two regions. Since skin conditions may be linked to skin barrier function, genetic factors, and skin care or environmental factors, our objective was to determine if there is an association between cytokines and symptoms despite adjusting for these factors.

Methods

This cross‐sectional study was conducted in two Japanese cities: Tokyo and Oita. The Tokyo‐based study was conducted between April and November 2021, whereas the Oita‐based study was conducted between October 2021 and March 2022.

The participants were healthy, 1‐month‐old infants and their parents. The inclusion criteria were infants whose parents were over 20 years of age and could speak Japanese. Infants with congenital skin conditions were excluded from this study. In both areas, participants were recruited during the 1‐month checkup at the hospital. Owing to the difficulty in recruiting sufficient participants, we used websites, flyers, and snowball sampling in Tokyo. Data were collected from hospital rooms, participants’ homes, or university rooms. Two researchers, one each in Tokyo and Oita, conducted the measurements. They were midwives caring for newborns with well‐established experience in neonatal skin research and skin barrier function measurements.

Outcome

This study evaluated four skin symptoms (erythema, papules, dryness, and exudate/yellow scaling). Skin conditions were observed at three facial sites on the right and left foreheads, as well as around the eyebrows and eyes, using the infant facial skin assessment tool, a scale for assessing facial skin problems in newborns, as a reference [6].

Cytokines

Albumin and interleukin (IL)‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and IL‐17 levels were measured using skin blotting. A nitrocellulose membrane (Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) was hydrated with saline and applied to the skin for 10 min to allow the non‐invasive transfer of skin cytokines and albumin to its surface, where they could then be evaluated by staining. The nitrocellulose membranes were stored at 4°C until further analysis. The collected protein was immunostained using the SNAP i.d. 2.0 protein detection system (Merck Millipore, as described previously) [7].

The nitrocellulose membrane was treated for 10 min with a blocking agent (Blocking One; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan), followed by staining for each biomarker. Albumin was detected after reacting for 10 min with a primary antibody (A80‐229AP; Bethyl Laboratories Inc., Montgomery, TX, USA). IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and IL‐17 levels were detected after reaction for 10 min with primary antibodies, including anti‐IL‐4 (12227S; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA), anti‐IL‐6 (12153S; Cell Signaling Technology), anti‐IL‐8 (WH0003576M5; Merck KGaA), and anti‐IL‐17 (PRS4887; Merck KGaA) antibodies, followed by reaction with a secondary antibody labeled with alkaline phosphatase (ALP) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) or horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Jackson ImmunoResearch). Chemiluminescence was achieved by the addition of ALP substrate (Chemiluminescent AP Substrate; BioFX Laboratories, Owings Mills, MD, USA) or HRP substrate (Luminata Forte; Merck KGaA).

We used the same protocol at both sites (Tokyo and Oita), prepared identical antibodies simultaneously and followed the same staining process as closely as possible. The only difference was in the imaging instruments used in the final step: Lumicube (Liponics, Tokyo, Japan) in Tokyo and ChemiDoc XRS+ (Bio‐Rad, California, USA) in Oita. However, because this study only focused on the qualitative evaluation of positivity or negativity, the influence of the different instruments was considered minor. In both cases, a qualitative assessment of positivity or negativity was performed using −2 standard deviations (SD) of the negative control value as the cutoff.

Other Variables

We collected skin barrier function measurements, information on genetic factors (whether parents had a family history of atopic dermatitis), and information on the environmental factors of skin care practices (moisturizer application) as adjustment variables. Skin barrier function was measured as transepidermal water loss (TEWL) (VapoMeter SWL‐5001; Delfin Technologies, Kuopio, Finland) using the same instrument at both sites. Parents were interviewed using a questionnaire or verbal communication to determine if either parent had a family history of atopic dermatitis and whether they used moisturizers as part of their daily skincare regimen.

Statistical Analysis

We conducted a multilevel analysis using a mixed‐effects model to determine whether there was an association between symptoms and cytokines after adjusting for regional differences and previously identified factors. Cytokines were used as the dependent variable, and the region as a random effect, using the presence or absence of each of the four symptoms (erythema, papules, dryness, and exudate/yellow scaling) as the outcome. TEWL, family history of atopy, and the skincare regimen were entered as fixed effects. In addition, differences in patient background by region were also examined using a Chi‐square test. A two‐tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 29.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Ethical Considerations

Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of the infants before their participation in the study. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine at the University of Tokyo (Approval Number: 2020349NI) and Oita University of Nursing and Health Sciences (Approval Number: 21–55).

Results

A total of 119 neonates from Tokyo and 112 from Oita participated in the study. There were no significant regional differences in birth weight or gestational age at birth among the participants. There was no difference in the incidence of skin problems during the first month of life, as reported by the caregivers (64.7% in Tokyo and 65.4% in Oita). The number of infants with a family history of allergies was higher in Tokyo than in Oita (98/119 [82.4%] vs. 59/110 [53.6%], respectively; p < 0.001).

The prevalence of skin symptoms on the day of the survey differed, with 53.8% in Tokyo and 81.2% in Oita. Erythema, papules, dryness, and exudate/yellow scaling were present in 59 (25.5%), 133 (57.6%), 37 (16.0%), and 16 (6.9%) of the infants, respectively. Discussion

We examined the association between skin cytokines and skin disorder symptoms in newborns and observed a relationship between IL‐8 levels in the skin and the presence of papule symptoms. IL‐8 is a chemokine induced by the inflammatory stimulation of various cells. Its levels in the stratum corneum may be associated with the severity of atopic dermatitis [8]. IL‐8 can cause other target cells to translocate to the site of infection. Therefore, it can be assumed that it was present at the site of inflammation. IL‐8 is associated with skin symptoms [9]; however, despite adjusting for barrier function and environmental factors in the current study, IL‐8 remained associated with skin symptoms. These observations suggest that IL‐8 may be involved in the development of various skin conditions. Nevertheless, as this was a cross‐sectional study, the relationship between IL‐8 levels and the onset of papules before and after the study was unclear. In the future, we aim to examine the long‐term associations and their potential implications for skin condition development.

IL‐17 (IL‐17A in this study) functions as an immune enhancer, particularly in the fungus Malassezia, which specifically induces IL‐17 [10]. Malassezia fungi cause opportunistic infections, although they are known to cause seborrheic eczema in neonates. Malassezia infection is involved in the IL‐17 response; IL‐17 exerts an immunoprotective effect against Malassezia while simultaneously causing atopic skin‐like inflammation [11]. The present study identified a related trend between crusting/exudation and IL‐17 levels. Crusting/exudation is a severe skin issue and is most likely a symptom of seborrheic eczema in the patients in this study. Given that Malassezia is linked to the development of seborrheic eczema, its association with IL‐17 is a compelling finding. However, the relationship between seborrheic eczema and Malassezia in infants remains unclear [7]. Further studies should examine how Malassezia infections trigger an IL‐17 response and lead to long‐term inflammatory reactions.

In this study, we examined the effects of geographical regions on skin conditions. To date, no study has assessed skin barrier function or cytokine levels in these two regions in the same manner. Notable differences were observed in the skin barrier regimens between the regions. Different recruitment methods could explain the higher family history of atopic dermatitis in Tokyo; different survey periods may also affect skin barriers. In addition, compared to Tokyo, Oita is always warmer, has more precipitation, and is more humid. Previous studies have shown that seasonal differences affect the skin barrier [12]. Such climatic effects may have influenced the skin barrier function of newborns, potentially leading to skin problems.

A key strength of this study is that comparisons were made between measurements obtained at multiple sites using similar methods. However, the incidence of skin conditions perceived by mothers in the two regions was nearly identical (approximately 65%). This suggests that two‐thirds of newborns in Japan experience some type of skin disorder, though there are regional differences in the types of disorders and skin barrier function. Future studies should account for regional differences in the mechanisms underlying skin conditions, and prevention methods should be developed accordingly.

This study also had several limitations. Regional differences, including seasonal differences, were not considered. In addition, the measurement environment was not constant, and some staining instruments differed. Nevertheless, we established a shared procedure, used the best possible identical instruments, and ensured comparability between measurement results at the two sites. The unclear pre‐ and post‐occurrence relationships between skin conditions and symptoms remain a significant limitation. Future prospective, long‐term studies starting early in life are needed to clarify these causal relationships.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that different symptoms indicate that different cytokines are involved in particular skin conditions in neonates, and that the mechanisms differ depending on the symptoms. In the future, the association between cytokine levels and development of skin conditions should be clarified longitudinally to develop specific preventive strategies.
