Safety and Efficacy of Bronchoscopic Pulsed Electric Field Ablation for Lung Cancer and Metastases
Authors: Kamran Mahmood, Coral X Giovacchini, Trey C Mullikin, Jeffrey M Clarke, Christopher Kelsey, Scott L Shofer, Michael Dorry, Cristina Salmon, Emily Sanders, Joseph Mammarappallil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLocal treatment options for progressive lung cancer or metastases located in the central or middle thirds of the lungs are limited, especially if progression occurs after radiation. In these patients, local thermal ablative therapies are fraught with complications. We investigated the safety and efficacy of local control in progressive lung and metastatic cancer by bronchoscopic pulsed electric field (PEF) ablation.MethodsWe performed an analysis of prospectively collected data of all the patients with lung and metastatic cancer to the lungs treated by bronchoscopic PEF ablation at our institution between October 2024 and April 2025.ResultsSeven lesions in six consecutive patients were treated by PEF ablation using robotic bronchoscopy with three-dimensional imaging. There were three patients with NSCLC, and three with colorectal cancer, urothelial cancer, and thyroid cancer. All patients had progressed on standard oncologic therapies, and five had received radiation to the lesions. The median tumor size was 2.1 cm (range, 1.1–4 cm), and all were located in the central or middle-thirds of the lungs. At the median follow-up of 9.7 (interquartile range, 5.4–10.1) months, four (57.1%) lesions increased in size, two (28.5%) decreased, and one (14.3%) resolved. The decreased size or resolution of lesions was seen in the three patients with NSCLC. No direct complications due to PEF were encountered, but one patient required prolonged mechanical ventilation related to anesthesia.ConclusionsIn patients with lung cancer or lung metastases, bronchoscopic PEF ablation is safe and associated with mixed local control.Keywords: Lung cancer, Bronchoscopy, Pulsed electric field ablation, NSCLC therapy

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, and other primary malignancies with lung metastases have a poor prognosis.1,2 Despite recent oncologic advances, including immunotherapy, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, and stereotactic body radiation therapy, the outcomes for patients with progressive lung cancer and metastases remain poor.3, 4, 5 In patients with progressive local lung cancer or oligometastatic cancer in the lungs, local thermal ablation therapies, including radiofrequency ablation, microwave, and cryoablation, have been used with varying success and safety.6,7 The lesions in the central and middle-thirds of the lungs can be challenging to access percutaneously, and central blood vessels increase the risk of excessive bleeding.8 Bronchoscopy, especially with robotic navigation platforms and three-dimensional (3D) imaging, may provide a safe and effective access to these lesions.9 But the experience with bronchoscopic thermal ablation, including radiofrequency ablation and microwave, has been fraught with multiple complications, including life-threatening hemorrhage and death.7,10,11 Pulsed electric field (PEF) ablation offers a safer, nonthermal alternative in which high-voltage electrical pulses are passed through a lesion, leading to alteration in cell transmembrane potential and cell apoptosis.12,13 In addition, it releases nondenatured proteins into the tumor microenvironment, which may enhance a native immunogenic response.14, 15, 16 Because the cell death occurs slowly and the extracellular matrix is preserved, the risk of complications is lower when used in close proximity to critical structures such as blood vessels, bronchioles, and pleura as compared with other locally delivered ablative therapies.17,18 The U.S. Food and Drug Administration cleared a flexible needle (INUMI Flex endoscopic needle, Galvanize Therapeutics Inc., Redwood City, CA) that can be used with the Aliya electrosurgical system to perform bronchoscopic PEF ablation of lung lesions.19 To-date, the short-term safety of bronchoscopic PEF has been favorable as described in small studies; however, there has been no report on the efficacy for local control of malignancy.17, 18, 19, 20 The objective of this study is to describe the long-term clinical safety and early efficacy data for local control by bronchoscopic PEF ablation in patients with progressive NSCLC and metastatic cancer to the lungs.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

We prospectively collected data on consecutive patients treated clinically with bronchoscopic PEF at our institution between October 2024 and April 2025. All the patients had lung cancer or metastatic cancer to the lungs from other primaries, had been treated previously with radiation with or without systemic therapy, and had experienced radiographic cancer progression. To be eligible for PEF ablation, these patients were discussed by their multidisciplinary clinical team and referred for evaluation by interventional pulmonology. All the patients provided consent for the clinical procedure. The study was approved by the institutional review board (Pro00116887), and informed consent for enrolment in the research project was waived. The safety and local efficacy data were collected along with other relevant information. The short-term and long-term safety were defined as adverse events or complications within and beyond 30 days, respectively.

Bronchoscopy

All patients underwent bronchoscopy using a shape-sensing robotic platform (Ion, Intuitive Surgical, Sunnyvale, CA) with 3D imaging (Cios Spin, Siemens Medical Solutions, Malvern, PA) for tool-in-lesion confirmation. Patients underwent general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. A flexible bronchoscope was first introduced in the airway for inspection. It was followed by insertion of a robotic bronchoscope catheter with navigation to the target lesion using 3D imaging assistance. A diagnostic sample was obtained with a flexible needle, reviewed by rapid on-site evaluation, and malignancy was confirmed before ablation was performed.

PEF Ablation

For ablation of the lung lesions, Aliya PEF System (Galvanize Therapeutics, Inc., Redwood City, CA) was used with an INUMI Flex Needle, which delivers short-duration, high voltage (3kV), biphasic electrical pulses. INUMI Flex needle is a 160-cm long flexible catheter with a distal adjustable 21-gauge, partially insulated needle, 3.1 cm in total length with 0.5 cm active distal tip. Per instructions for use for INUMI Flex needle on the basis of preclinical studies, the volume of ablation zone is approximately 1.4 (± 0.2) × 1.1 (± 0.1) × 1.1 (± 0.1) cm3 or 0.88 cm3. Once the needle is in the target, the stylet of the catheter is removed. An electrode filament is inserted into the proximal catheter and attached to the generator. The generator is connected to a cardiac monitor, which is attached to the patient by standard four chest leads to monitor the cardiac rhythm. Aliya system is monopolar and requires a grounding pad attached to the patient. When the system is activated by pressing a foot pedal, one treatment consisting of 100 pulses or packets of energy synchronized to the R-wave on the electrocardiogram is delivered over 5 minutes.

Aliya INUMI Flex needle was inserted through the robotic bronchoscope catheter into the target lesion. The needle was extended out of the protective sheath and adjusted to reach the distal border of the lesion using 3D imaging. The PEF treatment was then applied. The needle was withdrawn back 0.5 cm at a time, using two-dimensional imaging, until the most proximal part of the lesion was treated. For a large lesion and if technically feasible, the needle was completely withdrawn and re-inserted into the target lesion using a different vector with a goal of maximal tumor volume ablation. For a different vector placement, tool-in-lesion confirmation was obtained using 3D imaging. The same steps were performed until the maximal tumor volume was treated.

The total procedure time was calculated from the bronchoscope insertion to removal time, the anesthesia time was from the induction to extubation, and the PEF ablation time was the time utilized for ablation.

Lesion Response

The size of the treated tumor was assessed using the radiology reports or measured by electronic calipers on the chest computed tomography (CT). The volume of the tumor was calculated using three dimensions. The percentage of tumor volume ablated was estimated on the basis of the number of PEF treatments applied and INUMI Flex Needle ablation zone volume compared with the total tumor volume. The location of the lesion was determined by drawing a line through the lesion from the pleura to the mediastinum, and was assigned in the central, middle, or outer third of the lung, depending on where the center of the lesion was located on the line. The change in the size of the tumor and 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on positron emission tomography (PET)–CT scan was assessed on the 3-month and subsequent follow-ups. Response Evaluation Criterion in Solid Tumors version 1.1 was used to evaluate the overall treatment response.21

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range (IQR) or range (minimum–maximum). Categorical variables were summarized using percentages.

Results

Six patients underwent bronchoscopic PEF of seven lesions (Table 1). Median age (IQR) was 75 (70–77.5) years, and half of the patients were females. There was one (16.7%) former and two (33.3%) current smokers. Three (50%) patients had NSCLC, one (16.7%) had rectal, one (16.7%) thyroid, and one (16.7%) had urothelial cancer. The patient with thyroid cancer had two lung lesions. Two (33.3%) patients with NSCLC had recurrent stage I disease, whereas other patients (66.6%) had stage IV disease. All patients had progressed on standard oncologic therapies. Five patients (83.3%) had received previous radiation, whereas the patient with thyroid cancer had two lesions and was treated with radioactive iodine previously, but no radiation. Previously, one (16.7%) patient had received chemoimmunotherapy, one (16.7%) had chemotherapy, one (16.7%) had a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, and one (16.7%) had radioactive iodine. One (16.7%) patient received concurrent and adjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Two (33.3%) other patients received adjuvant chemoimmunotherapy on disease progression, 3 months after PEF. After three months of PEF ablation, two (33.3%) patients received chemotherapy, and one was treated with immunotherapy (16.7%).

The median tumor size was 2.1 cm (range, 1.1–4 cm) as presented in Table 2. Six (85.7%) lesions were located in the central-third and one (14.2%) in the middle-third of the lung. All patients had diagnostic biopsies confirming malignancy on rapid on-site evaluation in the same procedure before the PEF ablation was performed. The median (IQR) number of initial PEF treatments per tumor was 3 (2–3.5). The median number of treatment vectors was 1 (IQR, 1–1.5). The median total procedure time was 68 (IQR, 63.5–80) minutes, the median anesthesia time was 82 (IQR, 79.5–101) minutes, and the median PEF ablation time was 15 (IQR, 10–17.5) minutes. The median tumor volume ablation percentage was 100 (IQR, 80.1–100). Some lesions did not achieve 100% ablation because of large lesion volume and technical inability to insert the PEF needle in a different optimal vector. One patient with tumor progression after PEF underwent a second PEF ablation. However, there was continued tumor progression on follow-up PET-CT.

The patients were followed up with PET-CT scans for a median of 9.7 (IQR, 5.4–10.1) months. The PET-CT scans were done every 3 months in all patients. On the final follow-up, four (57.1%) lesions increased in size, two (28.5%) decreased, and one (14.3%) resolved (Table 3, Figure 1, Figure 2 and 2A–D). The median increase in the largest diameter for the lesions that grew was 0.7 (0.5–1) cm, and the median decrease in the largest diameter of the lesions that regressed was 1.1 (0.5–1.2) cm. Both lesions increased in size in the patient with thyroid cancer. The decreased size or resolution of lesions was seen in the three patients with NSCLC. Only one of these patients received concurrent and adjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. All other patients received previous stereotactic body radiation therapy or radioactive iodine for thyroid cancer, one patient received previous chemotherapy, and one patient had previous chemoimmunotherapy. On the last follow-up PET scan, FDG-uptake increased for five (71.4%) lesions, decreased for one (14.3%), and resolved for one (14.3%). CT and PET scan changes were concordant in all patients, except one patient with NSCLC, in which the nodule decreased in size, but FDG-uptake on PET scan increased. Later clinical follow-up after the end of the study period revealed a continued increase in the size and FDG-uptake on PET-CT. On the basis of the Response Evaluation Criterion in Solid Tumors version 1.1 at the final follow-up, four (66.6%) patients progressed, one (16.7%) remained stable, and one (16.7%) had a complete response. No abscopal effect was seen in the only patient with multifocal disease in which the index lesion was treated.

No direct PEF-related complications were observed within or beyond 30 days. There were no significant cardiac arrhythmias, bleeding, or pneumothorax. All the patients were alive at the time of final follow-up. One patient was reintubated after the procedure for prolonged sedation but extubated after 6 hours, discharged within 24 hours, and continued to do well afterward.

Discussion

This is an early report of long-term safety and local control efficacy of bronchoscopic PEF ablation used in a clinical setting for patients with progressive lung cancer and cancers metastasized to the lungs. At a median follow-up of about 9 months, PEF ablation led to mixed local control but was safe with no direct complications.

The cohort of PEF ablation patients was particularly challenging, as they had tumor progression on standard of care therapy, and had limited treatment options as determined by a multidisciplinary team. All the patients, except one, had received radiation with recurrence in the previous field, and further radiation was ruled out by the treating physicians. In this context, for local control with PEF, four (57.1%) lesions increased in size, two (28.5%) decreased, and one (14.3%) resolved. The favorable response was seen only in patients with NSCLC, in which treated lesions decreased in size or FDG uptake, whereas all of the metastatic lesions increased in size or FDG avidity. The NSCLC lesion that resolved was the smallest in the cohort, with the largest diameter of 1.1 cm. Lesion size may be an important predictor of response, with smaller lesions responding better to PEF ablation, and this should be evaluated in larger studies. The FDG avidity and tumor size changes were concordant on PET-CT scans, except in one patient with NSCLC, in which tumor size shrank but FDG uptake increased. This patient exhibited continued tumor progression afterward. Therefore, increased FDG uptake after 3 months of PEF ablation may be a reasonable and sensitive marker for tumor persistence or progression.

Several small studies have been recently published about the safety of bronchoscopic PEF, but, to our knowledge, ours is the first one to report local control in progressive lung cancer and metastases to lungs.17, 18, 19 A prospective study evaluated the safety of PEF ablation using robotic bronchoscopy or percutaneous approach of 44 lung lesions in 30 treatment-naive patients before the standard of care oncologic therapy.19 Over a follow-up of 30 days, one pneumothorax was encountered with a percutaneous procedure. Unrelated to PEF, there was one case each of transient ischemic attack, respiratory failure related to anesthesia, and cervical spondylosis with radiculopathy. In another prospective multicenter study evaluating the safety of PEF ablation, 36 patients with NSCLC underwent bronchoscopic or percutaneous ablation about 20 days before the surgical resection.17 These patients were compared with eight patients who only had a biopsy before the resection. Adverse events included two pneumothorax, one case of hemoptysis, and one patient with chest pain. Histopathologic assessment of the resected tumor revealed a cellular depletion zone with decreased or absent tumor cellularity surrounded by a transition zone with decreased cellularity compared with the untreated part of the tumor. Arterioles and bronchioles in the treatment zone were intact, and the pleura was not damaged when the treatment was performed close to the pleural surface. In addition, aggregates of CD20–positive B cells—tertiary lymphoid structures—were identified in and around the treatment zones, which were considered to promote local immune response against cancer cells.

There is more data on the safety and efficacy of the percutaneous PEF. In a multicenter, retrospective study, 41 patients with advanced lung cancer, progressive on standard therapy, were treated with percutaneous PEF ablation, and the outcomes were compared with a propensity-matched cohort.22 Four patients had pneumothorax requiring chest tube, and one had hemoptysis. The PEF group’s 1-year progression-free and overall survival were 63.2% and 74.3% compared with 11.8% and 33% for the control group, respectively. Another retrospective study described the percutaneous PEF experience for 30 progressive tumors in 17 patients; two had NSCLC, and nine had metastatic tumors in the lungs.23 A total of 15 (50%) of the 30 treated lesions remained stable or decreased in size at a median follow-up of 3 months. Both treated NSCLC lesions remained stable. There were 12 (50%) out of the 24 nontreated lesions which remained stable or decreased as well. Concurrent systemic chemoimmunotherapy was used in 14 (82%) of the 17 patients. There were five cases of pneumothorax, three pleural effusions, and one perihepatic hematoma, which did not require drainage. One hemothorax required chest tube placement. In another retrospective study, 11 patients with 13 different metastatic lesions were treated with percutaneous PEF.24 Three patients had metastases to the lungs, but no participant had primary lung cancer. Residual disease and local or distant progression were seen in 10 out of 11 patients, and no abscopal effect was seen. One patient developed a gastric artery pseudoaneurysm and hemoperitoneum, requiring vascular embolization. Another patient developed brachial plexopathy attributed to pressure because of body positioning for the procedure. The safety and feasibility of percutaneous PEF in tumors of multiple organs were assessed in 155 patients, including 77 with lung cancer.25 Pneumothorax was seen in 21.5% patients with lung treatment, and 11.3% required a chest tube. One patient developed self-limiting hemoptysis, and another patient with preexisting hemoptysis died of massive hemoptysis within 48 hours of the procedure. Our study adds to the current literature by reporting both the safety and local control efficacy of bronchoscopic PEF ablation. Not only was the bronchoscopic PEF safe in patients with progressive tumors located in central- and middle-thirds of the lung, but patients with NSCLC exhibited modest local control.

PEF ablation is suggested to improve the immune response against the tumor. Immune synergy of PEF with chemoimmunotherapy in a murine cancer model has been exhibited, in which PEF, along with cisplatin and checkpoint inhibitor (α programmed cell death protein 1 inhibitor), increased the CD4+, cytotoxic CD8+, and CD4+ and CD8+ central memory T cells, slowed tumor growth, and improved survival.16 Another murine model study reported that PEF and immunotherapy, compared with radiofrequency ablation and immunotherapy, enhanced recruitment of M1 macrophages, natural killer cells, and dendritic cells and decreased regulatory T cells, leading to slowed tumor growth and better survival.15 Our study did not assess the immune markers, and we did not see an abscopal effect in the only patient with multifocal disease.

The strengths of our study include, to our knowledge, the first study to report on safety and local control efficacy of bronchoscopic PEF ablation in patients with progressive malignancies in the central and middle-thirds of the lung. We found that PEF is safe but has mixed efficacy, with a potential benefit in patients with NSCLC. Our findings are similar to the study by Moore et al.22 in which patients with progressive NSCLC who were treated with percutaneous PEF exhibited improved survival compared with propensity-matched controls. Kim et al.23 also reported the stability of patients with progressive NSCLC treated with percutaneous PEF. We speculated that the potential efficacy in lung cancer seen in our cohort might be related to the unique immunologic characteristics of lung cancer, which has been found to be one of the most responsive cancers to checkpoint inhibitors.3,26 The immune responsiveness of lung cancer, synergized by PEF mechanisms described above, may be responsible for the selective effectiveness of PEF in lung cancer. Limitations of our study include a small sample size and a heterogeneous cohort with no control group. Given that most of our patients did not receive concurrent or immediate adjuvant systemic chemoimmunotherapy, we were unable to assess the correlation between immunotherapy and PEF response. A randomized controlled trial is warranted to assess the role of PEF ablation in lung cancer and metastatic cancer to the lung, with immunologic markers and synergy evaluation with systemic immunotherapy.

In conclusion, bronchoscopic PEF ablation is safe but has mixed local control efficacy. Future studies are needed to assess the impact of PEF on outcomes of patients with lung and metastatic cancer to the lungs.
