The Anterior Belly of the Digastric Muscle as a Novel Target for Needle Electromyography in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
Authors: Tatsuya Fukumoto, Yusuke Osaki, Kenta Hanada, Koji Fujita, Ryuji Kaji, Yuishin Izumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsNeedle electromyography (EMG) in the bulbar region aids in the diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) but remains technically challenging. The genioglossus muscle (GM) is commonly examined; however, pain and poor relaxation may limit its evaluation. The trapezius is an alternative target but is innervated by the spinal accessory nerve. We investigated the utility of the anterior belly of the digastric muscle (ABDM), innervated by the trigeminal nerve, for bulbar EMG evaluation.MethodsIn this unblinded, prospective observational study, consecutive patients with suspected ALS underwent needle EMG of the ABDM, GM, masseter muscle (MM), and trapezius. Relaxation levels, EMG abnormalities, and pain (100‐mm visual analog scale [VAS]) were assessed.ResultsTwenty‐seven clinically diagnosed ALS patients were analyzed. Relaxation was better in the ABDM than in the GM (p < 0.001) and comparable to that in the MM and trapezius. Fasciculation potentials were detected more often in the ABDM (52%) than in the GM (0%) and MM (22%; p = 0.039) and were comparable to the trapezius (59%). Chronic denervation occurred at similar frequencies across muscles (33%–59%). The combination of abnormal spontaneous activity and chronic denervation was most frequent in the ABDM (37%). Pain was lower with the ABDM (median VAS score of 21) than with the GM (28; p = 0.0052) and MM (32; p < 0.001) and was similar to the trapezius (23).DiscussionThe ABDM may be a practical additional target for bulbar EMG in ALS, with favorable relaxation, lower pain, and potentially improved electrodiagnostic yield.

Introduction

Needle electromyography (EMG) plays a key role in the diagnosis of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Furthermore, the detection of denervation findings in the bulbar region by EMG provides supportive evidence for ALS and facilitates its differentiation from other disorders [1]. Despite its clinical value, EMG evaluation of the bulbar region remains challenging, with only a few muscles considered suitable for routine assessment, such as the genioglossus muscle (GM) and masseter muscle (MM) [2, 3]. The GM is typically selected, but the examination is frequently associated with substantial discomfort, and adequate relaxation is difficult to achieve [4]. These limitations hinder the reliable assessment of fasciculation potentials (FPs) and fibrillation potentials or positive sharp waves (Fib/PSWs), which are particularly important markers of early denervation [5]. The trapezius and sternocleidomastoid muscles have also been reported to be useful targets [6, 7]. However, these muscles are innervated by the spinal accessory nerve, whose motor neurons reside in the upper cervical cord; therefore, high cervical cord lesions may confound the interpretation of EMG findings [6, 8]. These considerations highlight the clinical need for purely cranial nerve‐innervated muscles that allow a reliable assessment of FPs and Fib/PSWs with a minimal burden on patients.

The anterior belly of the digastric muscle (ABDM) is a suprahyoid muscle innervated by the trigeminal nerve and involved in mouth opening and swallowing [9]. It lies superficially beneath the submandibular skin as a paired muscle (Figure 1A) and is readily palpable, allowing easy needle insertion. However, the utility of the ABDM for an EMG assessment of the bulbar region has not been systematically investigated. The aim of this study was to compare relaxation levels, EMG abnormalities, and procedural pain of the ABDM with those of conventional bulbar target muscles in patients with ALS.

Methods

Participants

This unblinded, single‐arm observational study enrolled patients with suspected ALS. Patients who visited Tokushima University Hospital for suspected ALS between October 1, 2024, and October 31, 2025, underwent needle EMG as part of the standard diagnostic evaluation. Patients were required at follow‐up to meet the revised El Escorial criteria (REEC), the updated Awaji criteria (UAC) [10], or the Gold Coast criteria (GCC) for ALS. Exclusion of alternative diagnoses was confirmed by experienced neurologists. Bulbar involvement was defined as the presence of dysphagia or dysarthria, either subjectively or objectively. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tokushima University Hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Needle Electromyography

Needle EMG was performed in the ABDM, GM, MM, and trapezius, and relaxation levels and electrophysiological findings were evaluated for each muscle. The GM was included as a standard bulbar muscle, whereas the MM was selected as a trigeminal nerve‐innervated muscle clinically used to evaluate bulbar involvement, allowing comparison with the ABDM. The trapezius was selected as part of our routine EMG evaluation of suspected ALS, based on its relatively consistent relaxation at rest in our practice and previous findings comparing the trapezius with the sternocleidomastoid muscle [6]. All examinations were performed by one of two board‐certified neurologists (T.F. and Y.O.), each with more than 8 years of experience in EMG, using a standard EMG system (Nicolet EDX, Natus Neurology Incorporated, Middleton, WI, USA) and a 30‐gauge concentric needle electrode. For the ABDM examination, the needle was inserted via a submandibular approach under ultrasound guidance (Vscan Air CL, GE Vingmed Ultrasound AS, Horten, Norway) with the patient in the supine position. A representative sonographic image of the ABDM is shown in Figure 1B, and the procedure is demonstrated in the Video S1. The examiner stabilized the mandible with the contralateral hand and instructed the patient to open the mouth slowly against resistance to evaluate the motor unit potentials (MUPs) of the ABDM. The GM was approached percutaneously beneath the mandible.

Muscle relaxation was graded on a three‐point ordinal scale based on a previous report [6]: complete (2), partial (1), and no relaxation (0). Complete relaxation was defined as the absence of voluntary MUPs for more than 10 s. Partial relaxation was defined as the presence of some voluntary MUPs while Fib/PSWs could still be distinguished by careful attention. Fib/PSWs were assessed at 10 sites per muscle and considered present if they were detected at more than two sites and persisted for more than 3 s. FPs were evaluated over a total of 30 s per muscle and recorded as present or absent; FPs were assessed only under complete relaxation. Abnormal spontaneous activity was defined as the presence of Fib/PSWs or FPs. MUPs were evaluated qualitatively. MUP changes were defined as an increased amplitude or duration, or polyphasic MUPs with instability. Chronic neurogenic change was defined as MUP changes with reduced recruitment. EMG findings from the ABDM and trapezius were not used for the diagnosis of ALS. Trapezius findings were excluded to avoid ambiguity regarding whether the muscle should be assigned to either the bulbar or cervical region. In an exploratory analysis, we examined whether including ABDM abnormalities would provide additional evidence for fulfilling the UAC and GCC in patients who did not meet these criteria at the initial examination.

Pain

Given that procedural pain is a recognized limitation of bulbar EMG and can affect the tolerability of the procedure, pain severity was assessed. Patients were asked to rate their pain immediately after each muscle examination using a 100‐mm visual analog scale (VAS), where 0 indicated no pain and 100 indicated the worst possible pain [11, 12].

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed in the overall cohort to compare relaxation levels, frequencies of abnormal EMG findings, and pain severity among muscles. The frequencies of abnormal EMG findings were compared using exact McNemar's tests, and relaxation levels and VAS scores were compared using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests with continuity correction. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subgroup analyses were descriptive only.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Population

A total of 37 patients were screened, 27 of whom were included in the final analysis; their characteristics and diagnostic categories at the initial examination are summarized in Table 1. Sixteen patients (59%) were examined within 12 months of symptom onset. By the final analysis, 26 patients (96%) met the GCC. Fifteen patients (56%) met the definite, probable, or probable laboratory‐supported criteria according to the REEC or UAC. Seven patients (26%) were classified as possible ALS according to the REEC and UAC, including one patient who did not meet the GCC. Among the five patients who did not meet the REEC or UAC but met the GCC, one required noninvasive positive pressure ventilation, one underwent gastrostomy, and three lacked upper motor neuron signs. At the initial examination, three patients (11%) did not meet the REEC, UAC, or GCC; two had disease restricted to the bulbar region, and one had disease restricted to the cervical region. By the final analysis, all three patients met the GCC; one was classified as probable ALS and another as possible ALS under the UAC.

Needle Electromyography

The relaxation levels and EMG findings are summarized in Table 2. Relaxation was better in the ABDM than in the GM (p < 0.001) and did not differ from that in the MM or trapezius. FPs were detected more often in the ABDM than in the GM and MM and at a similar frequency to that in the trapezius. Fib/PSWs were infrequent in all four muscles, with no statistically significant differences among them. MUP changes were observed at similar frequencies across muscles. Reduced recruitment was most frequent in the ABDM and was significantly higher than that in the GM and trapezius, but not different from the MM. The frequencies of abnormal spontaneous activity, chronic neurogenic change, and their combination are shown in Figure 2. Abnormal spontaneous activity was significantly more frequent in the ABDM than in the GM and was comparable to that in the trapezius. The frequency of chronic neurogenic change did not differ significantly among the four muscles. The combination of both abnormalities—meeting the electrodiagnostic criteria of the UAC and GCC—was most frequent in the ABDM and significantly higher than in the GM, with no significant differences compared with the MM or trapezius.

Subgroup analyses were performed to determine whether the frequency of abnormalities differed depending on bulbar involvement, the site of onset, tongue atrophy, or diagnostic category. The results are summarized in Table S1. Abnormal EMG findings of the ABDM, GM, MM, and trapezius were generally more frequent in patients with bulbar involvement. Both abnormal spontaneous activity and chronic neurogenic change were present in the ABDM in approximately half of the patients with bulbar involvement and of those with bulbar‐onset ALS. These findings were less consistently observed in the other examined muscles. Among patients with bulbar‐onset ALS without tongue atrophy, both abnormalities were present in the ABDM of four of five patients.

An exploratory analysis showed that incorporating findings from the ABDM allowed three patients with ALS who did not initially fulfill diagnostic criteria to meet ALS diagnostic criteria. In two patients, ABDM findings provided evidence of lower motor neuron involvement in the bulbar region, together with an exaggerated jaw jerk, leading to classification as possible ALS under the UAC and fulfillment of the GCC. In the remaining patient, ABDM findings provided evidence of lower motor neuron involvement in addition to that in the cervical region, allowing the patient to meet the GCC. Pain

The VAS scores are shown in Figure 3. The median VAS scores were lowest for the ABDM and significantly lower than those for the GM and MM.

Visual analog scale (VAS) scores. The median (IQR) VAS scores were 21 (9.5–28.5) for the anterior belly of the digastric muscle (ABDM), 28 (18.5–51) for the genioglossus muscle (GM), 32 (14.5–59) for the masseter muscle (MM), and 23 (12.5–33.5) for the trapezius. VAS scores were significantly lower for the ABDM than for the GM (*p = 0.0052) and MM (**p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study suggests that the ABDM may serve as a target muscle for an EMG evaluation of the bulbar region in patients with ALS and may complement conventionally examined muscles. The ABDM achieved better relaxation than the GM, with relaxation comparable to that of the MM and trapezius. Pain was lower in the ABDM than in the GM and MM and similar to that in the trapezius. Furthermore, FPs were detected more frequently in the ABDM than in the GM and MM, and chronic neurogenic change was observed at frequencies comparable to those in the other muscles. Overall, these results support the ABDM as a practical additional muscle for a bulbar EMG assessment, with favorable procedural feasibility and a potentially improved electrodiagnostic yield.

Limitations of the EMG assessment in cranial nerve‐innervated muscles have been widely recognized. In these muscles, Fib/PSWs are reported to be uncommon [13], making FP detection relatively more important [14]. Although the GM has traditionally been used for bulbar evaluations, the reported FP frequencies vary considerably across studies (1%–39%) [2, 6, 13, 15, 16]. This variability likely reflects technical factors—differences in approach methods, difficulty in maintaining adequate relaxation, and pain—in addition to cohort differences such as the sample size, the proportion of patients with bulbar‐onset ALS, and the disease duration [6, 15, 16, 17]. Sonoo et al. highlighted these limitations by demonstrating poor relaxation and reporting an FP frequency of only 1% in the GM [6], a finding that was closely replicated in our study. In this regard, the low FP frequencies observed in both the present study and the study by Sonoo et al. [6] may be partly attributable to the use of a submandibular approach. This possibility is consistent with an ultrasonographic study reporting more frequent detection of tongue fasciculations using a transoral than a submandibular approach [18]. On the other hand, facial muscles such as the frontalis and mentalis are relatively easy to examine, but the detection rates of FPs and Fib/PSWs are generally lower than in the GM, and controlling isometric contraction during the MUP evaluation can be difficult [2, 16, 19]. An analysis of the MM may help overcome some of these technical issues, yet the reported frequencies of EMG abnormalities remain lower than those of the GM [2, 3, 16], and our data indicate that pain is comparable to that of the GM examination. In contrast, the ABDM showed better relaxation and less pain while maintaining a high yield of abnormal EMG findings. Compared with the trapezius, the ABDM showed a similar overall detection rate of abnormalities. As a purely cranial nerve‐innervated muscle, the ABDM may therefore represent a practical alternative to the trapezius for bulbar EMG assessments.

Both abnormal spontaneous activity and chronic neurogenic change were observed more frequently in the ABDM than in the GM, MM, and trapezius. In the subgroup analysis, these abnormalities in the ABDM appeared relatively frequent in patients with bulbar‐onset ALS, although the sample size was small. These findings were also observed in some patients without tongue atrophy, a group in whom early EMG abnormalities can be especially informative. Notably, the incorporation of ABDM findings allowed three patients (11%) who initially did not fulfill the UAC or GCC to meet these criteria. Electrophysiological evidence of lower motor neuron involvement in the bulbar region is clinically meaningful in patients with suspected ALS, especially when available clinical and conventional EMG findings are insufficient to support a diagnosis of ALS or to distinguish ALS from other disorders. Thus, evaluation of the ABDM may contribute to an earlier diagnosis of ALS by providing additional electrodiagnostic evidence of bulbar involvement. Consistent with this interpretation, recent studies using surface EMG suggest early involvement of the ABDM in patients with ALS and an association between ABDM dysfunction and bulbar symptom progression [20, 21]. Interestingly, the ABDM also showed abnormalities more often than the MM, despite their shared trigeminal innervation. One possible explanation is the fiber‐type composition: the MM predominantly consists of type I fibers, whereas the ABDM contains a more even distribution of type I, type IIa, and type IIb fibers [9]. Given that type II fibers are reported to be more vulnerable to ALS [22, 23], this difference may partly account for the higher frequency of abnormalities observed in the ABDM.

Our cohort included a relatively high proportion of patients with early‐stage ALS, with 59% of patients examined within 1 year of symptom onset, and the high mean ALSFRS‐R score indicated mild functional impairment. This result likely reflects our role as an ALS referral center, where patients are often evaluated early in the disease course, and our inclusion of patients with possible ALS and those who did not fulfill diagnostic criteria at the initial examination to evaluate the utility of EMG in early‐stage disease. Even in this early‐stage cohort, abnormal EMG findings were detected in the ABDM at rates comparable to those of the other examined muscles, supporting its potential role as an additional muscle for evaluating early or clinically subtle bulbar involvement.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was modest; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Larger cohorts are needed to clarify how EMG evaluation of the ABDM may contribute to the diagnosis of ALS. Second, healthy or disease control groups were not included. Third, the unblinded qualitative EMG assessment may have affected the interpretation of EMG findings. Recruitment patterns in the ABDM should be interpreted cautiously because cranial nerve‐innervated muscles may have relatively high recruitment frequencies [24]. Nevertheless, our findings from the GM, MM, and trapezius were consistent with those of previous reports, supporting the validity of our results. Fourth, the ABDM was examined under ultrasound guidance in this study to ensure accurate needle placement, which may limit the direct applicability of our findings to settings without ultrasound guidance. Because the ABDM arises from the digastric fossa near the midline of the inferior mandible and courses toward the hyoid bone, the muscle belly can often be localized by palpation in the submental region during mouth opening. However, ultrasound guidance may be preferable when palpation is difficult because of marked atrophy or anatomical variation [25].

In conclusion, the ABDM may represent a feasible additional target for a bulbar EMG evaluation in patients with ALS, with favorable procedural tolerability and a potentially improved electrodiagnostic yield. Further studies are warranted to confirm its clinical value in patients with ALS.
