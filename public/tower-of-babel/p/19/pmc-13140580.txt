White matter hyperintensities mediate a greater proportion of age‐related lower cognitive scores in non‐Hispanic White participants compared to non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic participants: HABS‐HD
Authors: Cellas A Hayes, Samika Prasad, Anhiti Dharmapuri, Roland J Thorpe, The Health and Aging Brain Study (HABS‐HD) Study Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWhite matter hyperintensities (WMH) are robust markers of vascular brain injury and predictors of poorer cognition, but their contributions may differ across racial and ethnic groups.METHODSWe analyzed 3585 participants from the Health and Aging Brain Study–Health Disparities cohort (1264 non‐Hispanic White [NHW], 1003 non‐Hispanic Black [NHB], 1318 Hispanic). Baseline demographics, vascular risk factors, apolipoprotein E ε4, magnetic resonance imaging–derived WMH, and domain‐specific cognitive z scores were assessed. We used moderated mediation models to evaluate whether WMH mediated the age–cognition relationship by race/ethnicity.RESULTSGreater WMH volume was associated with poorer memory, executive function, processing speed, and language (all p < 0.01). Mediation by WMH varied across groups: WMH explained ≈ 30% of the age–cognition association in NHWs, 20% to 28% in NHBs, and 12% to 19% in Hispanics.DISCUSSIONWMHs consistently mediated age‐related lower cognition, but group differences highlight the contribution of cardiometabolic, neuropathological, and social determinants beyond WMHs in NHB and Hispanic populations.Keywords: aging, cognition, mediation analysis, racial and ethnic disparities, vascular risk factors, white matter hyperintensities

INTRODUCTION

In the order of magnitude, across racial and ethnic groups, non‐Hispanic Blacks (NHBs) have the highest risk and prevalence of dementia and Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia, followed by Hispanics, and then non‐Hispanic Whites (NHWs). 1 , 2 , 3 Approximately 45% of the worldwide dementia burden can be attributed to cardiometabolic conditions such as diabetes (2%), hypertension (2%), and dyslipidemia (7%). 4 However, certain populations, including NHBs and Hispanics, have higher prevalences of these risk factors compared to NHWs, which may contribute to the increased dementia prevalence and underlying contributing pathologies in these groups. 5 , 6 These aforementioned cardiometabolic risk factors serve as critical chokepoints as upstream contributors to neuropathology and cognition. 7 In addition, the hypothetical removal of certain risk factors including hypertension and diabetes can significantly improve cognition, especially for NHB and Hispanic racial ethnic groups. 8

Vascular contributions to cognitive impairment (VCID) have become an increasingly important focus within the dementia spectrum of AD and related dementias (ADRD). 9 A major contributor to cognitive decline and dementia is white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), a neuroimaging biomarker of cerebral small vessel disease and vascular brain injury. 10 , 11 , 12 AD and WMHs have shared risk factors including age and hypertension, as well as other cardiometabolic conditions and behaviors such as dyslipidemia, obesity, and smoking. 5 , 6 , 13 Given that certain populations such as NHBs and Hispanics have a higher burden of these risk factors, they may also be expected to have greater WMH volumes and subsequently be associated with earlier or greater cognitive impairment and decline.

Previous research has shown that NHBs have higher WMH volumes than NHWs, 14 although findings are inconsistent, with some studies reporting no significant racial or ethnic differences based on study design and inclusion. 5 , 6 Several studies consistently demonstrate that increased WMH volumes are associated with poorer cognitive performance across multiple domains. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 However, these studies are often limited by small sample sizes, underrepresentation of diverse racial/ethnic groups, and a focus on global rather than domain‐specific cognition. In our previous work using data from the Health and Aging Brain Study–Health Disparities (HABS‐HD), we found that diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and tobacco use were associated with greater WMH volume, with no evidence of interaction by sex. 19 Similar patterns emerged in a subcohort of females, 6 with the exception that diabetes and Hispanic ethnicity were linked to greater WMHs. In males, diabetes and Hispanic ethnicity also showed significant associations, along with an opposing interaction for dyslipidemia. 5 Regardless, age remains the strongest predictor of WMH, and the presence of co‐pathologies including vascular lesions has been linked to earlier onset and greater severity of cognitive decline. 20

There is little to no evidence on whether racial and ethnic differences exist in the associations among age, WMHs, and cognitive outcomes, particularly across distinct cognitive domains. Previous research has reported that WMHs mediate a percentage of the association between age and cognitive impairment using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). 21 A follow‐up study has also confirmed those findings; however, these studies were limited by relatively small sample sizes and the lack of inclusion of NHB and Hispanic racial ethnic groups. 22 Building on our previous findings and limitations identified in previous studies, we investigated whether the association between WMH volume and cognitive performance differs by racial and ethnic group (NHW, NHB, and Hispanic) and evaluated the extent to which WMHs mediate the relationship between age and cognition at baseline in the HABS‐HD cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

HABS‐HD cohort description

HABS‐HD began as the Health & Aging Brain Among Latino Elders (HABLE) study and is a continuing longitudinal community‐based cohort led by the Institute for Translational Research at the University of North Texas Health Science Center. 23 , 24 It is one of the largest AD investigation to date enrolling NHW, NHB, and Hispanic older adults, designed to address the amyloid/tau/neurodegeneration (ATN) framework. Study procedures were approved by the University of North Texas Health Science Center Institutional Review Board and follow the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Demographics

Participants reported their age, sex/gender, years and level of education, and self‐identified racial/ethnic background (NHW, NHB, or Hispanic).

Apolipoprotein E

Apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε2/ε3/ε4 genotyping was performed using TaqMan SNP assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific). 23 , 25 For analyses, those with at least one ε4 allele were categorized as ε4 carriers, while participants without were considered non‐carriers.

Cardiometabolic risk factor definitions

Hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, and tobacco dependence were identified through a combined review of self‐reported medical history, medication use, and clinical or laboratory findings. 6 , 26 Hypertension was defined by self‐reported diagnosis, current antihypertensive treatment, or elevated blood pressure readings at the study visit. Diabetes was defined by self‐report, use of glucose‐lowering medication, or elevated HbA1c levels. Dyslipidemia was identified by self‐report, lipid‐lowering treatment, or elevated cholesterol/triglyceride values. Obesity was defined by body mass index ≥ 30 kg/m2, and tobacco dependence was determined by self‐reported current use of tobacco products. These definitions align with prior HABS‐HD studies on WMHs. 5 , 6 , 26

Magnetic resonance imaging acquisition and WMH quantification

Neuroimaging methods and WMH processing followed previously published HABS‐HD protocols. 23 , 27 All scans were acquired on a 3‐Tesla Siemens magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system using a harmonized protocol. 23 , 28 High‐resolution T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient echo and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences were collected, and WMHs were segmented from co‐registered T1 and FLAIR images using the Lesion Growth Algorithm from the SPM Lesion Segmentation Toolbox (www.statisticalmodelling.de/lst.html). 29 WMH volumes were log‐transformed, adjusted for intracranial volume (ICV), and residualized to produce the primary WMH outcome measure. 30 , 31 Outliers were removed using standard interquartile range (IQR) criteria (values beyond 1.5× IQR above Q3 or below Q1). Hence, the WMH volume outcome in this study is representative of an ICV‐adjusted, log‐transformed version of raw WMH volume. 5 , 6 , 19

Neuropsychological assessment and cognitive domains

Participants completed a standardized neuropsychological battery assessing multiple cognitive functions, which has been previously described. 23 , 32 Memory was assessed with Logical Memory and the Spanish‐English Verbal Learning Test (SEVLT). Executive function was measured with the Digit Span and Digit Symbol Substitution Test. Processing speed and attention were evaluated with Trail Making Test Parts A and B (inverted to reflect worse cognition), and language abilities with semantic and phonemic fluency tasks. Raw scores for each test were converted to z scores, which were then averaged to create composite scores for four cognitive domains: episodic memory, executive function, processing speed, and language. 32

Statistical analysis

Cross‐sectional sample comparisons

Demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized separately for each racial/ethnic group (NHW, NHB, and Hispanic). Continuous measurements are reported as means with standard deviations, and categorical measures as raw counts and percentages. For categorial variables compared across racial ethnicities, we used a Pearson chi‐squared (χ 2) test when all expected cell counts exceeded five; otherwise, a Fisher exact test was applied. For continuous outcomes, we used a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and compared within‐group differences using the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD).

Elastic net regression

To identify key demographic and clinical predictors of ICV‐adjusted WMH volume, we applied an elastic net regularization approach. 33 The analysis included participants with complete data for WMH volume and all candidate predictors: age (scaled in 5‐year increments), sex, years of education, race/ethnicity (NHW, NHB, Hispanic), APOE ε4 carrier status, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity, tobacco use, and MRI scanner type. Predictors were dummy‐coded when categorical and standardized to unit variance. A 10‐fold cross‐validation (cv.glmnet, α = 0.5) was used to select the penalty parameter (λ_min) that minimized the mean squared error under a Gaussian distribution. Predictors with non‐zero coefficients at λ_min were considered the most robust correlates of WMHs. The final set of variables included in subsequent statistical analyses included age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, APOE ε4, hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use, and MRI scanner.

Moderated mediation

We used a moderated mediation framework to test whether the indirect pathway linking age to cognition through WMHs varied by racial ethnic group. 34 The mediator model regressed ICV‐adjusted, log‐transformed WMH volume on age (per 5 years) and covariates (sex, education, race/ethnicity, APOE ε4, hypertension, diabetes, tobacco use, and MRI scanner type). For each cognitive domain (memory, executive function, processing speed, language), the outcome model regressed the z scored cognitive domain scores on age, WMH volume, the same covariates, and a WMH × racial ethnicity interaction term. Both models were passed through the mediate() function and mediation (R Package for Causal Mediation Analysis), with age specified as the treatment variable and WMH volume as the mediator. We sequentially set the racial ethnicity variable to NHW, NHB, and Hispanic, running 1000 bootstrap replications to estimate group‐specific average causal mediation effects (ACME), average direct effects (ADE), total effects, and proportions mediated. Statistical significance was defined as a bootstrap 95% confidence interval for ACME that excluded zero, and all analyses were reproducible using a fixed random seed.

The moderation mediation model framework specification permits effect modification of the WMH–cognition association by racial ethnic group, such that the indirect effect of age on cognition through WMHs is allowed to vary across groups within a unified modeling framework. Accordingly, the estimated ACME values represent moderated indirect effects, and differences in these estimates across racial and ethnic groups should be interpreted as variation in the strength of the WMH–cognition pathway linking age to cognitive performance, rather than differences arising from separate model specifications.

Statistical analyses were conducted in R v4.2.3.

RESULTS

Participant characteristics

Participant characteristics varied significantly by racial ethnic groups (Table 1). NHW participants were older on average (68.9 [8.7] years) than NHB (63.4 [7.8] years) and Hispanic (63.5 [8.0] years) participants (p < 0.001). Diabetes prevalence was highest among Hispanics (35.0%), followed by NHBs (25.8%) and NHWs (14.0%; p < 0.001), whereas hypertension was most common in NHBs (79.2%), then Hispanics (63.7%) and NHWs (58.5%; p < 0.001). WMHs differed across groups, with raw WMH volumes of 4.10 (7.0) mL in NHWs, 4.46 (12.1) mL in NHBs, and 2.48 (5.0) mL in Hispanics (p < 0.001). Normalized WMH volumes showed a similar pattern (1.06 [1.57] in NHWs, 0.93 [1.65] in NHBs, 0.65 [1.62] in Hispanics; p < 0.001). Cognitive performance also varied significantly. In the memory domain, NHW had a mean z score of 0.31 (0.92), compared to −0.13 (0.85) in NHBs and −0.21 (0.81) in Hispanics (p < 0.001). Executive function scores were 0.40 (0.75) in NHWs, 0.09 (0.78) in NHBs, and −0.47 (0.83) in Hispanics (p < 0.001). For processing speed, scores were 0.28 (0.72), 0.04 (0.87), and −0.36 (1.10), respectively (p < 0.001). Language scores were 0.27 (0.87) in NHWs, 0.04 (0.88) in NHBs, and −0.29 (0.82) in Hispanics (p < 0.001).

Race/ethnicity moderates the WMH–cognition associations

Neither the age × NHB interaction (β = −0.06; 95% confidence interval [CI] −0.14 to 0.03; p = 0.18) nor the age × Hispanic interaction (β = −0.01; 95% CI −0.08 to 0.06; p = 0.84) was significantly associated with WMH volume (Table 2). Higher WMH volume was associated with lower memory score (β = −0.10; 95% CI −0.13 to −0.07; p < 0.01). The WMH × NHB interaction did not reach statistical significance (β = 0.04; 95% CI −0.01 to 0.08; p = 0.08), whereas the WMH × Hispanic interaction was significant (β = 0.07; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.11; p < 0.01). For executive function, both age (β = −0.10; 95% CI −0.12 to −0.09; p < 0.01) and WMH (β = −0.11; 95% CI −0.13 to −0.08; p < 0.01) were associated with lower scores, and the WMH × Hispanic interaction was significant (β = 0.06; 95% CI 0.03 to 0.09; p < 0.01). Similar to the other domains, older age and more WMHs were associated with lower scores in the processing speed and language domains. However, for processing speed, the WMH × NHB interaction was modestly significant (β = −0.06; 95% CI −0.10 to −0.01; p = 0.01), whereas the WMH × Hispanic interaction was not significant (β = −0.01; 95% CI −0.05 to 0.03; p = 0.69). Conversely, for the language domain, the WMH × Hispanic interaction was significant (β = 0.06; 95% CI 0.02 to 0.10; p < 0.01).

Moderation mediation

Across all three racial/ethnic groups, older age was equivalently and strongly linked to greater WMHs (Path A β ≈ 0.46 per 5‐year increment, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.49, p < 0.001) and, in turn, higher WMHs predicted poorer performance in every cognitive domain (Path B β between −0.11 and −0.07, all p < 0.001; Figures 1, 2, 3). In NHW participants, the mediated proportions were the largest: WMHs accounted for 29.9% (95% CI 20.6%–39.0%) of the age–memory association, 31.7% (23.9%–40.0%) of the age–executive function, 17.1% (9.2%–26.0%) of age–processing speed, and 32.0% (21.3%–43.0%) of age–language (all ACME p < 0.001) associations (Figure 1A–D). In NHB participants, WMHs mediated 20.5% (9.5%–31.0%) of lower memory scores (ACME p = 0.002), 26.3% (16.3%–35.0%) of executive dysfunction, 28.0% (19.3%–36.0%) of lower processing speed, and 18.8% (11.2%–26.0%) of language deficits (all other ACME p < 0.001; Figure 2A–D). WMHs still significantly mediated the association between age and cognitive domain performance in Hispanics despite accounting for a lower percentage: WMH explained 12.5% (1.7%–22.0%, p = 0.02) of lower memory scores, 17.3% (9.4%–25.0%, p < 0.001) of poorer executive dysfunction score, 18.8% (11.2%–26.0%, p < 0.001) slower processing speed, and 15.3% (2.8%–27.0%, p = 0.02) of language deficits (Figure 3A–D.

DISCUSSION

The objective of this study was to examine whether WMHs mediate the relationship between age and cognition across racial and ethnic groups in a large, diverse, community‐based cohort. Across the entire cohort, greater WMH volume was strongly associated with poorer performance in memory, executive function, processing speed, and language, reaffirming WMHs as a robust marker of vascular brain injury and cognitive aging. Importantly, these associations varied by racial and ethnic group: WMH–cognition relationships were weaker in Hispanics for memory, executive function, and language, whereas NHB participants demonstrated a somewhat stronger effect for processing speed. Mediation analyses further confirmed WMHs as a central mechanism linking age and cognition. Older age was consistently associated with higher WMH volume, and in turn, WMH partially explained age‐related lower cognitive scores in every group. However, the proportion of mediation differed: WMH accounted for nearly one third of age‐related lower cognitive scores in NHW participants, intermediate effects in NHBs, and the smallest effects in Hispanics. While WMHs consistently emerged as a key mediator of age‐related lower cognition across groups, the variability in mediation proportions underscores the importance of other contributors such as cerebral small vessel disease not captured by WMHs, AD pathology, or social and structural determinants of health, particularly in underrepresented populations.

Prior large‐scale and longitudinal studies have shown that WMHs accumulate rapidly with advancing age, are detectable as early as midlife, and reliably predict cognitive decline. 35 , 36 , 37 Consistent with these findings, we identified age as the strongest determinant of WMHs, and higher WMH volumes independently predicted worse performance in memory, executive function, processing speed, and language.

The magnitude of the associations between WMHs and cognition was not uniform across NHW, NHB, and Hispanic racial ethnic groups. WMH volume showed weaker associations with cognition in Hispanics for memory, executive function, and language, while NHB participants demonstrated a somewhat stronger link between WMH and processing speed. These results indicate that while WMHs are a shared risk pathway, the impact on cognitive domains may vary depending on racial and ethnic context. These findings align with our recent work using population intervention modeling, which demonstrated that the higher prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors such as diabetes and hypertension in NHB and Hispanic groups contributes to disproportionately worse cognitive outcomes compared to NHWs, in whom these risk factors are less common. 8 Hence, the findings from this study support the hypothesis that the higher prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors in NHB and Hispanic participants contributes not only to greater WMHs but also to direct cognitive impairments, which may explain the smaller proportion of age‐related lower cognitive scores mediated by WMHs in these groups.

Our findings extend the prior literature by demonstrating that WMHs mediate the association between age and cognition across multiple cognitive domains and within specific racial and ethnic groups. Previous studies have largely focused on global cognition, often measured by screening tools such as the MoCA, which limited the ability to examine domain‐specific pathways. 21 , 22 Large cohort studies such as Atherosclerosis Risk in Communuties and the Cardiovascular Health Study established that vascular risk factors, particularly hypertension, are strongly linked to WMH accumulation, with effects often more pronounced among Black participants. 38 , 39 Similarly, the Washington Heights and Inwood Community Aging Project and other community‐based cohorts have shown that Black and Hispanic older adults carry a disproportionate WMH burden, partially attributable to elevated vascular comorbidity. 14 , 40 More recently, prospective efforts like the Diverse Vascular Contributions to Cognitive Impairment study were explicitly designed to address these disparities by integrating vascular, neuroimaging, and biomarker measures in racially and ethnically diverse populations. 41

Beyond vascular risk, population‐based cohorts across the lifespan including the Dunedin Study, the Meta‐Analyses on Strategic Lesion Locations for Vascular Cognitive Impairment Using Lesion–Symptom Mapping consortium, and work by Garnier‐Crussard et al. have demonstrated that WMHs emerge as early as midlife, increase exponentially with age, and consistently predict declines in executive function, processing speed, and working memory. 35 , 36 , 42 Midlife vascular exposures, particularly cumulative blood pressure, have also been linked to WMH‐related changes in brain connectivity and cognition, reinforcing the role of early vascular injury in shaping later‐life trajectories. 43 Mediation analyses from Gronewold et al. further suggested that periventricular WMH can account for more than half of the association between blood pressure and cognition, particularly in memory and executive domains. 44

Within this context, our study advances the field by showing that the proportion of age‐related lower cognitive scores explained by WMHs differs substantially across racial and ethnic groups. WMHs accounted for nearly one third of age‐related lower cognitive scores in NHW participants but substantially less in NHB and Hispanic participants, underscoring that cardiometabolic risks, non‐vascular neuropathologies, and social and structural determinants of health may play a larger role in cognitive aging in specific groups. Two prior studies reported that WMHs mediate part of the association between age and cognition; however, cognition was assessed only with the MoCA, highlighting a major gap in domain‐specific evaluations. 21 , 22 Our findings extend this previous literature by not only investigating multiple cognitive domains but using moderated mediations within specific racial ethnic groups.

The attenuated mediation effects in NHB and Hispanic participants suggest that other processes contribute more prominently to cognitive aging in these groups. Higher prevalence of hypertension and diabetes in these populations may exert both direct and indirect effects on cognition beyond WMHs considering they are modifiable risks of dementia. 4 Social and structural determinants of health including disparities in health‐care access, chronic stress, and cumulative disadvantage likely compound these biological risks. 45 Importantly, these differences emerged in a cohort that was largely cognitively normal, underscoring that racial and ethnic disparities in the drivers of cognitive impairment are detectable even prior to severe dementia. Taken together, our findings highlight both the shared role of WMHs as a robust biomarker of age‐related lower cognitive scores and the distinct pathways through which vascular, neurodegenerative, and social mechanisms contribute to cognitive aging across populations. Future work should also incorporate social determinants of health into longitudinal mediation and moderation frameworks to quantify the extent to which structural and contextual factors account for racial and ethnic differences in WMH burden and cognitive outcomes. Such approaches will be critical for disentangling the proportion of variability attributable to vascular, social, and environmental exposures, thereby advancing a more comprehensive and mechanistic understanding of heterogeneity in brain aging across populations.

Strengths

Foremost is the use of a large, community‐based, and racially/ethnically diverse cohort, which included substantial representation of NHB and Hispanic participants. The breadth of comprehensive neuropsychological testing allowed us to evaluate domain‐specific associations rather than relying on global measures. From a methodological perspective, our use of elastic net regression for covariate selection minimized issues of multicollinearity and promoted model parsimony, thereby increasing the robustness of the results. Finally, the application of a moderated mediation framework enabled simultaneous examination of indirect effects of WMHs on cognition and racial/ethnic moderation, providing novel insights into both shared and differential pathways of brain aging across groups.

Limitations

At the same time, certain limitations should be acknowledged to contextualize these findings and their interpretations. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the mediation results should be interpreted as statistical rather than causal pathways. Residual confounding from unmeasured vascular, genetic, or lifestyle factors cannot be excluded. Additionally, WMHs were quantified as a global volume without consideration of lesion location, which may differentially affect specific cognitive domains based on anatomical distributions. Although APOE ε4 status and other genetic risk factors were included as covariates, they were not explicitly examined as moderators, which could further refine understanding of gene–environment and gene–vascular interactions. Finally, although the cohort was racially and ethnically diverse, subgroup sample sizes particularly in the context of interaction terms may have limited statistical power to detect differences across groups.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, WMHs emerged as a robust mediator of the association between age and cognition in this large, diverse community‐based cohort. The proportion of mediation was greatest in NHW participants, intermediate in NHB, and lowest in Hispanic participants, highlighting both shared and distinct pathways of cognitive aging. These findings emphasize that WMHs contribute meaningfully to age‐related lower cognitive scores across all groups. At the same time, other mechanisms such as the higher prevalence of cardiometabolic risks and the enduring influence of structural and social determinants of health also play critical roles in shaping cognition in older adults. Future work should prioritize longitudinal designs to establish temporal relationships among vascular risk, WMH accumulation, and poorer cognition with the addition of more advanced statistical methods focused on causal inference.
