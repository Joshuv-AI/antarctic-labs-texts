Manufacture of Clostridioides difficile Spores for Experimental Infection of Human Volunteers
Authors: Annefleur D O Hensen, Céline Harmanus, Christine Voorvelt, Anoecim R Geelen, Maryam El Hamdioui, Ed J Kuijper, Laura Pattacini, Pim Schipper, Inge M van Amerongen-Westra, Pauline Meij, M Y Eileen C van der Stoep, Meta Roestenberg, Wiep Klaas Smits
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfections with Clostridioides difficile remain a significant global healthcare problem for which novel (preventive) treatment strategies are urgently needed. Controlled human infection models (CHIMs) can contribute to a better understanding of the disease and accelerate (novel) medicinal product development.MethodsA robust production process for C difficile spores for experimental human administration was implemented. Direct-release capsules containing viable C difficile spores were formulated. The manufacturing process aligns with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) guidelines, including quality controls and release by a Qualified Person (QP).ResultsSelected nontoxigenic and toxigenic C difficile strains were used to create a master cell bank and working cell bank from which multiple batches of purified active substance were produced. Substance was then used to manufacture the final active product. The active product and its intermediate products passed all quality controls for identity, potency, purity and safety, and were individually released by the QP. Stability of active substance and product is confirmed up to 12 months.ConclusionsAlthough there are no clear standardized international guidelines for challenge material production, we demonstrate that it is feasible to produce C difficile human challenge material for small-scale application in CHIM trials. This application of GMP principles to an unconventional production process of a bacterial spore-forming anaerobic challenge agent is an example for the production of challenge material as described in the auxiliary medicinal product guidelines of the European Medicines Agency and the US Food and Drug Administration.

Clostridioides difficile is the leading cause of healthcare-associated diarrhea, responsible for an estimated 15 600 deaths and 284 000 disability-adjusted life-years globally [1]. Concerningly, the incidence of community-acquired C difficile infection (CDI) has nearly doubled in the past years [2]. As such, CDI causes a substantial clinical, social, and economic burden [3].

Due to high relapse rates following conventional antibiotic treatment and rising antimicrobial resistance [4], alternative treatment strategies are urgently needed. Microbial restoration therapies show promise for high-level sustained prevention of recurrent CDI [5–7]. However, the complex interactions between C difficile and the human microbiome remain poorly understood. Additionally, animal models cannot fully replicate these interactions, complicating preclinical optimization of novel medicinal products and highlighting the need for innovative approaches.

Controlled human infection models (CHIMs), in which human volunteers are deliberately inoculated with an infectious organism (so-called “challenge agent”) under controlled conditions, are a valuable tool to rapidly evaluate medicinal products for efficacy in early-phase clinical development [8, 9]. CHIMs have been developed for a wide variety of pathogens (including parasites, viruses, and bacteria) over the past 200 years and have even led to the registration of a novel cholera vaccine [10]. The development of a C difficile CHIM, in which healthy adult volunteers are experimentally exposed to C difficile spores in a controlled environment, would provide the opportunity to identify microbiota and immunological targets for novel medicinal products, and ultimately test the preliminary efficacy of such products (eg, vaccines or microbial restoration therapies). The challenges of developing such a model have recently been highlighted [11].

One such challenge relates to the production of C difficile challenge material. To ensure safety of CHIM participants, a challenge agent that complies with regulatory requirements for human use must be available. Within the European Union (EU), challenge agents are classified as auxiliary medicinal products (AxMPs) by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), defined as a medicinal product used for the needs of a clinical trial as described in the protocol, and should be manufactured according to Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) equivalent standards. GMP guidelines describe the minimum requirements that medicinal products must meet in their manufacturing process to ensure that the medicinal product is (1) of consistent high quality, (2) appropriate for their intended use, and (3) meets the requirements of the marketing or clinical trial authorization [12].

Aligning the manufacturing process of C difficile challenge material with GMP guidelines presents several key challenges, particularly in meeting the quality attributes of identity, purity, potency, and safety, as outlined in the technical white paper on quality considerations for CHIM challenge agent production [9]. Here, we address these challenges and describe a robust manufacturing process for producing C difficile challenge material in an academic laboratory by trained personnel with an appropriate quality control (QC) strategy. This method for producing encapsulated viable C difficile spores exemplifies the manufacture of AxMPs under the EMA or US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Manufacturing a C difficile Challenge Product

Production was performed in a dedicated laboratory within the Experimental Bacteriology research group in the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC) under the quality management system of LUMC's Clinical Pharmacy and Toxicology department and the Center for Cell and Gene Therapy. The production process of C difficile spores was established using known and established methods and protocols at the LUMC [13].

The manufacturing process is generic, developed to be adaptable to diverse C difficile strains, including both nontoxigenic C difficile (NTCD) and toxigenic C difficile (TCD) strains. Here, we exemplify the process by producing challenge material from the NTCD strain L-NTCD03 (polymerase chain reaction [PCR] ribotype 416, sequence type [ST] 39, clade 4, complete genome GenBank NZ_OX637968.1) and the TCD strain L-TCD-01 (PCR ribotype 020, ST2, clade 1, complete genome GenBank NZ_OY731254.1). Spores were produced in a dedicated anaerobic isolator cabinet (Whitley A135GMP, Don Whitley Scientific, Bingley, UK) in a restricted-access biosafety level 2 laboratory, using food-grade culture media (Soy Peptone A3X [SA3X], Organotechnie, La Courneuve, France) in batch fermentations in sterile single-use polyethylene terephthalate glycol–modified (PETG) flasks (Sterile Disposable PETG Flask 2000 mL, Thermo Scientific, Rochester, NY, USA). Food-grade SA3X was selected as it does not contain animal-derived components and sustains robust growth and high spore yields (Supplementary Figure 1). Spores were purified by aerobic washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Fresenius Kabi, Graz, Austria) containing 0.1% Tween 80 (Polysorbatum 80, Duchefa, Haarlem, The Netherlands). Dose-adjusted 500-µL aliquots (104 and 107 colony-forming units [CFU]) C difficile spores in buffered 70:30 glycerol (Duchefa, Haarlem, The Netherlands):PBS were filled into size 0 direct-release capsules (Vcaps Plus Capsules, Lonza, Colmar, France) using an autoclavable capsule-filling system (ProFiller1100, Torpac, Heerlen, The Netherlands). A detailed risk assessment of the C difficile manufacture process and impurities was conducted. All raw materials, disposables, and excipients were quarantined until release was done by a QC officer. The qualification of all compendial and noncompendial reagents is listed in Supplementary Table 1. Noncompendial reagents were qualified based on vendor Certificate of Analysis and met predefined acceptance criteria (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). The production process was formalized in standard operating procedures (SOPs), authorized by the QC officer, Qualified Person (QP), and Head of Production.

The QCs and in-process controls (IPCs), detailed below, were based on well-established protocols of the Dutch National Expertise Center for CDI [14–16] as well as European guidance documents [13 , 17, 18]. All IPCs and QCs were validated or qualified for their intended use. Where possible, IPCs and QCs were performed according to GMP requirements or, if not possible, under ISO15189 standards at the clinical microbiology laboratory of the LUMC. QC analyses during culturing were performed in a Whitley A35 anaerobic cabinet (Don Whitley Scientific). All QC results were independently reviewed by a QC officer, after which the master cell bank (MCB), working cell bank (WCB), active substance (AS), and active product (AP) were released by a QP. To verify the robustness of the manufacturing process, ≥3 preclinical test runs were performed per challenge strain.

A product dossier was created according to EMA/CHMP/BWP/534898/2008 Revision 2. for pharmacological products. The product was characterized as an AxMP (EU regulation No 536/2014 Clinical Trials Regulation). The principles of GMP as outlined in EU Regulation 536/2014 along with the white paper “Considerations on the Principles of Development and Manufacturing Qualities of Challenge Agents for Use in Human Infection Models” [9] were applied to the production process wherever possible and audited as such.

RESULTS

Master Cell Bank and Working Cell Bank

The C difficile challenge strains were selected based on previously published criteria; the strain should be a recent isolate, representative for commonly circulating strains, nonepidemic, susceptible to optional rescue antibiotics, exhibit robust growth and sporulation in production medium, and be verified by sequencing [11]. The NTCD strain was isolated from fecal material of an asymptomatic nursing home resident and the TCD strain from fecal material of a symptomatic patient with uncomplicated CDI. The challenge strains were used to form an MCB, the starting material for the production of the active product (Figure 1). The MCB was prepared under Good Laboratory Practice conditions according to the standard operating procedures of the Dutch National Expertise Center for CDI, an ISO15189-certified laboratory. In short, the MCB was created through enrichment culture in C difficile enrichment modified broth (Mediaproducts BV, Groningen, The Netherlands), inoculated onto C difficile selective agar plates (CLO, bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France), and recultured on a tryptic soy sheep blood (TSS) plate (bioMérieux), after which a colony was transferred to storage medium at −80°C.

Active Substance

To produce purified spores in solution (Figure 1), which constitutes the AS, the WCB was recovered on an SA3X agar plate and restreaked on a fresh SA3X plate to increase cell yield. Subsequently, a starter culture was generated in liquid SA3X medium under anaerobic conditions using the anaerobic isolator cabinet. The optical density (OD) of the cultures was measured and the cultures were diluted in fresh SA3X medium to a starting OD of 0.05 followed by incubation in shaking Erlenmeyer flasks for 14 days to produce spores. In the production medium, spore yield increased modestly between 7 and 14 days (from 0 CFU/mL to >106/mL); thus, a 14-day time frame was selected (Supplementary Figure 1). We found no significant difference in spore yield between different culture volumes, demonstrating robustness of the method (Supplementary Figure 1). After 14 days, cells were pelleted by centrifugation and washed 5 times with PBS/0.1% Tween 80 to remove residual vegetative cell debris. All AS batches yielded a high spore count (Figure 3), meeting the acceptance criterion for spore count (>1.6 × 106 spores/mL). The AS was stored at −80°C.

The AS was released for further processing after undergoing successful QC testing (Figure 2).

Active Product

The AP consists of a 500-μL suspension containing 104 or 107 CFU C difficile spores in capsules for oral administration. The doses of 104 and 107 CFU spores were selected based on previously published experimental NTCD studies [19, 20].

To produce the AP, the AS was thawed and adjusted to an appropriate concentration in formulation buffer (70%/30% [v/v] glycerol and PBS) after which 500-μL volumes were dispensed in direct-release Lonza Vcaps capsules. Capsules were stored at room temperature (15°C–25°C) and quarantined until release.

The AP was released after undergoing successful QC testing (Figure 2).

Stability of AP

Stability testing was performed on the AS and the AP at multiple timepoints according to a predefined stability plan (1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months). Stability testing consists of (1) identity testing by gluD PCR and ribotyping PCR; (2) potency testing by spore enumeration (Figure 4); (3) purity testing by visual inspection of colonies on a TSS plate, colorimetric measurement of protein/DNA content, and aerobic plating; and (4) capsule controls by visual inspection of capsule integrity and measurement of fill weight and volume (Figure 2).

Spore yield (colony-forming units [CFU]/mL) stability of multiple batches of active product (capsules) over time (nontoxigenic Clostridioides difficile [NTCD] and toxigenic Clostridioides difficile [TCD]). The NTCD strain used is L-NTCD03 and the TCD strain used is L-TCD-01.

We observed stability, with limited or no decrease in plating efficiency over a period of 12 months so far, for the NTCD and the TCD AP (Figure 4). All other stability tests also met the predefined criteria (Figure 2) at these timepoints. Stability of AS conforms with the stability observed of AP. The observed stability at multiple timepoints across different production batches confirms robustness of stability across the batches. The expiry date of the (N)TCD AP is adjusted to the stability data. Based on these observations, we expect that the actual shelf life of the AP extends well beyond the current stability data. Together this demonstrates that the AP has shelf life compatible with use in challenge studies.

In-Process Controls and Quality Controls

The IPCs and QCs performed to ensure identity, potency, purity, and safety during the C difficile challenge material manufacturing process are indicated in Figures 1 and 2 and described below.

Identity

Species PCR

The presence of C difficile cells/spores was confirmed by gluD PCR on total DNA. The gluD PCR uses specific primers to amplify a 158-bp fragment of the glutamate dehydrogenase (gluD) gene, which is then detected with a gluD-specific probe in a real-time PCR assay [13].

Ribotyping

To identify and confirm the PCR ribotype of the C difficile challenge material, capillary PCR ribotyping was performed. The 16S-23S intergenic spacer regions were amplified and visualized by capillary gel electrophoresis [15]; the resulting banding pattern was matched to reference profiles using BioNumerics 7.6 (bioMérieux).

Complete Genome Sequencing

A reference sequence of the challenge strains was generated using long-read circular consensus sequencing (Pacific Bioscience Sequel II) and automated assembly using established protocols [21, 22].

Short-Read Sequencing

Short-read NGS (Illumina MiniSeq, NextSeq 500, or NovaSeq Xplus) was additionally performed on the WCB and the AS. Core genome analysis using Ridom SeqSphere+ (Ridom GmbH, Münster, Germany) confirmed that the strain's clade (clonal complex) and complex type matched the reference genome (Supplementary Table 4).

Potency

Total Cell Count

Viable bacterial cells in the MCB and the WCB were enumerated by culturing serial dilutions on brain-heart infusion (BHI) plates. Total cell counts were calculated as the geometric mean minus 2 standard deviations of log-transformed CFU values.

Total Spore Count

Viable spore counts were determined by plating serial dilutions of ethanol-shocked suspensions on BHI plates with 0.1% of the germinant sodium taurocholate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Lancashire, UK) and enumerating CFUs after 48 hours. Total spore counts were calculated as the geometric mean minus 2 standard deviations of log-transformed CFU values. The AS production yield was targeted at >1.6 × 106 spores/mL, but typically yielded >107 spores/mL.

Purity

Visual Appearance of Colonies

Clostridioides difficile cells or spores were routinely cultured anaerobically on TSS plates for 72 hours. Plates were visually inspected to ensure all colonies were morphologically consistent with C difficile, by 2 independent persons.

Contamination With Aerobic Bacteria

Absence of aerobic bacterial contaminants (<10 CFU/mL) was confirmed by aerobic culture on nonselective TSS agar plates.

Measurement of Protein and DNA Content

Free protein or DNA in the AS and the AP, derived from residual vegetative C difficile cell components, was quantified using colorimetric measurements (Qubit dsDNA HS Assay and Qubit Protein Assay, LifeTechnologies, Eugene, OR, USA). Limits were set at approximately 10-fold the minimum value observed after repeated washing (Figure 2).

Safety

Presence of Toxin Genes

Multiplex PCR targeting toxin A (tcdA), toxin B (tcdB), and binary toxin genes (cdtA and cdtB) was performed on total DNA from the samples, with 16S rRNA and gluD genes included as DNA isolation controls.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility

Susceptibility to optional rescue antibiotics was assessed by agar dilution [23] for fidaxomicin (Selleckchem, Köln, Germany) and Etest (bioMérieux) for metronidazole and vancomycin. Breakpoints were defined according to European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing criteria [24].

Microbial Contaminants of the MCB

Total DNA from the MCB was tested by qPCR for gastrointestinal pathogens, including the bacteria Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, Aeromonas, and Plesiomonas shigelloides, as well as the viruses adenovirus, norovirus, rotavirus, sapovirus, and astrovirus.

NGS for Bacterial Adventitious Agents

A nontargeted metagenomic analysis was performed to identify potential contaminants. Short-read NGS data were analyzed using GenomeDetective tool (EmWeb bv, Herent, Belgium) to confirm identity (assignment: C difficile) and assess levels of selected bacterial pathogens [25]. To account for potential spurious identifications from shared DNA segments, acceptance criteria were validated using simulated reads from the reference genome.

Capsule Controls

Capsule Integrity

Capsules from the beginning, middle, and end of the AP production were visually inspected for integrity, ensuring both capsule parts were aligned, undeformed, and free of leakage when tilted.

Fill Weight and Volume

The fill weight of the AP capsules was measured on an analytical balance; each 500-µL capsule was expected to weigh 700 ± 20 mg.

DISCUSSION

To summarize, we demonstrate feasibility of producing C difficile challenge material for CHIM studies at a relevant scale, following GMP principles with appropriate QCs and release. This application of GMP principles to an unconventional production process of a spore-forming anaerobic bacterial challenge agent exemplifies the manufacture of bacterial challenge agents as outlined in EMA and FDA AxMP guidelines.

Challenge material is not classified as a medicinal product and specific international challenge material production guidelines are lacking. The EMA identifies challenge agents as AxMPs and states that when the AxMP is not authorized it shall be manufactured according to GMP or at least an equivalent standard [26]. In the United Kingdom, the manufacturing of challenge agents is not regulated [27]. In the United States, the challenge agent is considered a biological product and is subject to regulation under federal law by the FDA. The challenge agent must therefore (1) be manufactured under GMP conditions where possible; (2) satisfy the FDA regulations of safety, purity, and potency; and (3) have detailed information on the provenance and manufacture as part of the required Investigational New Drug Application [27].

To ensure identity and safety of the C difficile challenge material, NGS was applied for full characterization of the challenge strain. Additionally, the application of NGS for the detection of adventitious agents enhances QC testing of challenge material, facilitating the identification of potential other bacterial pathogens in the AP beyond European Pharmacopoeia requirements. To safeguard the level of contamination with adventitious agents, acceptance criteria must be predefined. This can be challenging, as metagenomic analysis is prone to spurious identifications (eg, due to DNA segments shared between bacterial species). This is particularly relevant for C difficile challenge material, where DNA isolation from spores is difficult and typically yields low-biomass DNA samples. Acceptance criteria for detecting adventitious agents by NGS were established using simulated reads from the reference genome and a well-characterized pipeline, setting a cut-off of <0.3% of mapped reads aligning to pathogenic species. Such criteria may require adaptation for other species. In the case of spurious NGS identifications, quantitative 16S micelle PCR [28] can provide additional value by enabling both identification and quantification of bacterial composition.

Second, to further ensure safety, the production of challenge material requires the use of food-grade media and GMP-grade raw materials, avoiding animal-derived products where possible to mitigate risks such as transmissible spongiform encephalopathies. However, standard C difficile culture media contain animal-derived components, and conventional spore purification methods often use reagents (eg, nonionic X-ray contrast agents) unsuitable for spore production or oral administration. Consequently, during process development, the standard BHI medium was replaced with SA3X medium, and spore purification was adapted to washing with PBS-0.1% Tween. Moreover, the dedicated manufacturing area was adapted to include appropriate control measures, equipment validation, and a cleaning protocol to prevent cross-contamination. Cleaning protocols should align with regulations for the production site, and in our case included the use of VirkonS as a sporicidal disinfectant. Although GMP guidelines may stipulate production in a controlled environment (eg, a clean room), the inherent resistance and persistence of C difficile spores present significant challenges for decontaminating such facilities. Notably, the guidance on challenge agent production [9] states that a clean room is not mandatory; however, the manufacturing area should form part of the contamination control strategy, with environmental controls for particulate and microbial contamination tailored to the production step, the contamination risk of starting materials, and the safety of the final batch. Because C difficile requires anaerobic conditions, production was conducted in a qualified anaerobic isolator cabinet (GMP grade A) to ensure a robust and reproducible manufacturing process. The isolator underwent full qualification (installation, operational, and performance qualification). Thereby this manufacturing area provided not only the necessary environment for spore production but also ensured environmental control, including regulation of particulate and microbial contamination through controlled, high-efficiency particulate air–filtered, unidirectional airflow.

Third, to ensure potency, the challenge agent's administration route, storage, and transport conditions must be carefully considered. Oral capsules were selected to mimic the natural route of infection, while simultaneously controlling the number of spores reaching the gastrointestinal tract. As C difficile spores need exposure to primary bile acids in the duodenum for germination [29, 30], liquid-filled direct-release capsules (instead of enteric capsules) were chosen as they release the spores in the stomach or shortly after. As the formulation buffer, 70%/30% (v/v) buffered glycerol/PBS was selected to balance spore suspension feasibility and prevent capsule leakage. Capsules were preserved at room temperature (15°C–25°C) to avoid the structural changes in the content and integrity of the capsules that occur upon freezing. Extensive testing showed that Lonza Vcaps liquid-filled direct-release capsules provided the best performance for secure containment, stability, and leakage prevention at room temperature.

Applying GMP principles to C difficile spore challenge material for clinical use highlights the need for collaboration and thorough risk analysis among various disciplines, including pharmacists, microbiologists, clinicians, and technicians. This collaboration has led to a highly controlled production of C difficile spore challenge material in an academic setting. Building on these efforts, the C difficile challenge material has received Medical Research Ethics Committee approval for proof-of-concept clinical trials in healthy volunteers, using NTCD spores for colonization (NCT05693077) and TCD spores for infection (NCT06702345), to find a safe and infectious dose, while addressing the key challenges highlighted in our previous report [11]. Establishing a CHIM for C difficile will be a critical advancement in improving our understanding of CDI as well as accelerating (novel) product development to battle the rising incidence and global burden of this complex disease.
