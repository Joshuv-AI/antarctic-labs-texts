Rewiring the unfolded protein response for plant growth recovery after stress
Authors: Dae Kwan Ko, Raz Danieli, Federica Brandizzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The unfolded protein response (UPR) is a highly coordinated signaling network that alleviates endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, a condition induced by diverse environmental challenges in plants. Over the past two decades, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the genetic and molecular mechanisms of ER stress sensing and signal transduction in plants, largely through studies in the model plant Arabidopsis thaliana. These advances have established the UPR as a central regulator of proteostasis and underscored its broader relevance to plant growth and development and crop productivity under stress conditions. Despite this progress, critical knowledge gaps remain, particularly concerning the downstream biological processes required for growth recovery once ER stress has subsided and how these processes are coordinated by UPR regulators. Recent systems-level and integrative studies have begun to reveal critical roles of UPR signaling in pathways governing growth re-establishment and homeostasis of nutrient allocation and energy metabolism. In this review, we highlight recent findings on the functional roles of the plant UPR in recovery from ER stress, with a focus on mechanisms mediated by UPR regulators and downstream biological pathways that enable the transition from stress mitigation to growth restoration. Although this research area is still emerging, accumulating evidence supports a model in which the UPR functions as a dynamic regulatory network that actively coordinates post-stress physiological recovery to support plant fitness.

Introduction

Plants possess a remarkable capacity to withstand unfavorable and often fluctuating environmental conditions. Some challenges occur predictably, such as daily or seasonal changes in light intensity and temperature, whereas others arise unexpectedly, including diverse abiotic and biotic stresses (Zhang et al., 2022). Because plants are sessile, they must adapt to fluctuating environmental conditions by deployingmulti-layered molecular mechanisms that detect stress and coordinate metabolic and physiological processes across space and time. One of the most conserved of these systems is the unfolded protein response (UPR), which comprises interconnected signaling pathways that safeguard endoplasmic reticulum (ER) protein folding capacity. ER stress occurs when the burden of unfolded or misfolded secretory proteins surpasses the folding and quality-control capacity of this organelle (Karagoz et al., 2019; Pastor-Cantizano et al., 2020; Ko and Brandizzi, 2024). Activation of the UPR initiates a coordinated set of responses that limit further protein influx into the ER, increase folding capacity through the induction of molecular chaperones and foldases, remodel ER architecture, and remove terminally misfolded proteins through dedicated quality-control routes such as ER-associated degradation (ERAD) and ER-phagy (Werner et al., 1996).

Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in identifying key molecular regulators of the plant UPR, elucidating their interactions within highly interconnected regulatory networks, and defining the biological processes they govern to maintain ER homeostasis (Nawkar et al., 2017; Lai et al., 2018b; Ko and Brandizzi, 2020, 2022a, 2022b; Kim et al., 2022, 2025; Ko et al., 2023; Ahn et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). Collectively, these efforts have established the UPR as a central signaling hub that integrates environmental cues with cellular proteostasis and growth control. More recently, there has been growing interest in understanding how the UPR contributes not only to stress mitigation but also to the restoration of growth, fitness, and development following stress relief—a distinct physiological phase referred to as ER stress recovery (ERR) (Ruberti et al., 2018; Ko and Brandizzi, 2024). This conceptual shift is grounded in the recognition that organisms encounter transient environmental stresses throughout their life cycle, many of which induce reversible ER stress (Liu et al., 2007, 2020; Li et al., 2020; Adhikari et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025; Bajaj et al., 2026). Accordingly, the capacity to reestablish cellular and physiological homeostasis after stress is as essential for survival and productivity as the ability to endure stress itself.

Emerging evidence indicates that the UPR plays active and multifaceted roles in facilitating ERR through diverse biological pathways, including pathways that regulate root growth dynamics, circadian clock function, and developmental plasticity (Ruberti et al., 2018; Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a, 2022b; Ahn et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025). These findings reinforce the view that the UPR extends beyond a short-term damage-control system and operates instead as a dynamic regulatory network that coordinates post-stress recovery and long-term adaptation. Despite these advances, our understanding of how UPR-mediated signaling is reprogrammed during the recovery phase remains incomplete. It is increasingly apparent that ERR involves complex molecular interactions spanning multiple regulatory layers and interconnected biological systems rather than merely reversing stress-induced responses.

Given the rapid expansion of the UPR field, enabled in part by technological advances and its growing relevance to crop resilience and productivity, it is timely to synthesize recent discoveries on the role of the plant UPR in ERR. In this review, we provide an overview of current knowledge of the plant UPR network, with a focus on mechanisms that enable recovery from environmental stresses that trigger ER dysfunction in plants, emphasizing insights gained from Arabidopsis thaliana (hereafter Arabidopsis). We first summarize the updated regulatory landscape of the plant UPR with respect to growth restoration and then discuss established and emerging downstream pathways implicated in recovery processes, highlighting representative examples where appropriate. Finally, we consider the functional and physiological consequences of successful UPR-mediated recovery and outline key open questions and future research directions.

Rewiring the UPR for post-stress growth recovery

Growth re-establishment is a critical determinant of successful recovery after ER stress and broader environmental stresses that transiently disrupt ER function, including heat, salt, and elevated ambient temperatures, all of which can activate UPR signaling (Liu et al., 2007; Ruberti et al., 2018; Li et al., 2020; Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a; Zhang et al., 2025). In this section, we synthesize current knowledge of UPR regulators that support plant growth and fitness during and after ER stress, with particular emphasis on recent discoveries relevant to ERR. Because canonical UPR signaling, historical perspectives, and experimental designs for ERR assays have been discussed in detail elsewhere, we refer readers to previously published reviews for broader context (Hetz and Papa, 2018; Pastor-Cantizano et al., 2020; Ko and Brandizzi, 2024; Adhikari et al., 2025).

Core UPR regulators in ERR

Despite its capacity to modulate the expression of hundreds to thousands of genes in a highly context-dependent manner, the plant UPR is controlled primarily by a small number of core regulators operating through two major signaling branches (Liu and Howell, 2010; Deng et al., 2011; Nagashima et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2018). These regulators coordinate transcriptional and post-transcriptional responses in a spatiotemporally controlled fashion, allowing plants to fine-tune proteostasis and growth according to the severity, duration, and phase of ER stress.

The first and most evolutionarily conserved branch is mediated by inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), a type I transmembrane protein anchored to the ER that possesses kinase and endoribonuclease activities. In yeast and metazoan systems, IRE1 activation has been explained in part by a binding protein (BiP)-dissociation model, in which ER stress titrates BiP chaperones toward unfolded protein substrates, thereby permitting IRE1 oligomerization and autophosphorylation and activation of its RNase function (Bertolotti et al., 2000; Okamura et al., 2000; Pincus et al., 2010). In addition, structural and biochemical studies have proposed a direct-binding model in which unfolded or misfolded proteins interact with the luminal domain of IRE1 to promote its activation (Credle et al., 2005; Oikawa et al., 2009; Gardner and Walter, 2011). Although the extent to which these models apply to plant IRE1 remains to be fully established, they provide a useful mechanistic framework for understanding conserved modes of IRE1 activation. In Arabidopsis, three IRE1 isoforms have been identified: IRE1a, IRE1b, and IRE1c. Among these, IRE1a and IRE1b serve as the principal stress sensors (Noh and Kwon, 2002; Mishiba et al., 2019; Pu et al., 2019). Activated IRE1 initiates unconventional splicing of a 23-nt intron from the mRNA encoding the basic leucine zipper transcription factor bZIP60 (Deng et al., 2011; Nagashima et al., 2011). This splicing event induces a translational frameshift that generates an active transcription factor(TF), spliced bZIP60, which drives transcriptional reprogramming to alleviate ER stress. IRE1 also mediates the selective degradation of specific mRNAs through IRE1-dependent decay (RIDD), thereby modulating diverse biological pathways, including secretory trafficking, autophagy, and pathogen responses (Mishiba et al., 2013).

The second major UPR branch is mediated by bZIP28, a membrane-associated TF and type II transmembrane protein that interacts with BiPs under non-stress conditions. ER-stress-induced dissociation from BiPs triggers the translocation of bZIP28 to the Golgi apparatus, where it undergoes sequential proteolytic cleavage (Srivastava et al., 2012; Iwata et al., 2017). The released N-terminal fragment, which contains the bZIP DNA-binding domain, relocates to the nucleus and modulates UPR gene expression, often in concert with spliced bZIP60 (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a).

Functional redundancy between these two UPR transcriptional branches is evident: bzip28 and bzip60 single mutants exhibit little or no growth defect under ER stress, whereas the bzip28/60 double mutant displays severe growth impairment and a markedly altered transcriptomic profile (Deng et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2018). Furthermore, the embryo lethality of ire1a/b bzip28 triple mutants underscores the indispensable roles of these UPR regulators in stress tolerance and basal growth and development (Deng et al., 2013). Importantly, genetic analyses also demonstrate that these major UPR regulators are required for post-stress growth recovery. In ERR assays, both bzip28/60 and ire1a/b mutants fail to resume growth, consistent with their central roles in UPR signaling (Ruberti et al., 2018). By contrast, the bzip60 single mutant recovers significantly better than the bzip28 mutant, although its recovery remains less robust than that of wild-type plants (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). These findings indicate that bZIP28 and bZIP60 regulate partially overlapping yet functionally distinct genetic programs during ERR, particularly in roots (Figure 1). Non-canonical UPR regulators and modulators of ERR

In addition to the core UPR regulators, several other ER-associated proteins, including bZIP17; members of the NAC (No Apical Meristem, ATAF, and Cup-Shaped Cotyledon) family, such as NAC089 and NAC062; and B cell lymphoma 2-associated athanogene 7 (BAG7), have been proposed to function as ER stress sensors or modulators (Liu et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2014a, 2014b; Li et al., 2017). However, genetic analyses indicate that neither single nor higher-order mutants of these factors exhibit growth defects comparable to those observed in ire1a/b or bzip28/60 mutants, suggesting that they function as auxiliary or context-dependent regulators of the UPR. Importantly, their molecular roles and biological significance in ERR remain largely unexplored, highlighting a major gap in current understanding.

Modulators of ERR

Recent studies have begun to provide mechanistic insight into how such co-regulators modulate UPR activity to promote growth restoration during ERR. A systems-level analysis integrating RNA sequencing and TF–DNA interactome data identified G-class bZIP TF 2 (GBF2), a key component of abscisic acid (ABA) signaling (Song et al., 2016), as a negative regulator of ERR (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022b). Loss of GBF2 function enhanced root growth recovery relative to wild-type plants. Mechanistically, GBF2 suppresses the transcriptional activity of bZIP28 and bZIP60 by competing for shared DNA binding sites, thereby attenuating the expression of UPR marker genes.

Conversely, a genetic study of circadian clock mutants revealed that the oscillator component CIRCADIAN CLOCK ASSOCIATED 1 (CCA1) acts as a positive regulator of ERR by stabilizing the bZIP28 protein and thereby enhancing UPR-dependent transcriptional reprogramming required for growth restoration (Ahn et al., 2025). Given that ABA signaling and the circadian clock interface with numerous biological pathways (Nemhauser et al., 2006; Yang et al., 2021), these findings suggest that UPR-mediated transcriptional reprogramming during ERR is embedded within a complex and highly interconnected regulatory network. Hence, bZIP28- and bZIP60-dependent transcriptional outputs are fine-tuned by pathway-specific regulators, enabling precise and context-dependent modulation of downstream biological processes.

Beyond transcriptional control, successful ERR also requires returning UPR-induced proteins to pre-stress levels once ER homeostasis is restored, thereby restoring the balance between ER protein-folding demand and folding capacity. Recent work has highlighted ERAD as a key mechanism in this process. Specifically, the ER-stress-inducible luminal protein TUNICAMYCIN-INDUCED 1 (TIN1) was shown to undergo rapid ERAD-dependent degradation during ERR (Wang et al., 2025). Whether this mechanism applies more broadly to proteome remodeling during ERR or instead is restricted to a subset of UPR-induced proteins remains an important open question. Collectively, these findings indicate that UPR regulators function not only as emergency responders to ER stress but also as integral components of growth-regulatory networks that enable plants to resume normal development following stress relief (Figure 1).

Regulator-specific mechanisms driving ERR

Whereas stress attenuation primarily limits cellular damage and restores homeostasis, growth restoration requires coordinated activation of a broader range of biological pathways, including metabolic reprogramming, phytohormone signaling, and developmental control (Yang et al., 2015; Gupta and Senthil-Kumar, 2017; Huang et al., 2019; Illouz-Eliaz et al., 2025). Successful ERR is therefore expected to involve extensive crosstalk between ER proteostasis signaling and growth-regulatory networks. Emerging evidence indicates that the UPR achieves this coordination through regulator-specific mechanisms, in which individual UPR components perform specialized functions beyond their canonical roles in mitigating ER stress. In this section, we highlight recent advances that reveal how distinct UPR regulators contribute differentially and cooperatively to successful ERR.

bZIP28- and bZIP60-dependent regulation during ERR

Although bZIP28 and bZIP60 function largely redundantly in activating the transcriptional arm of the UPR during ER stress, increasing evidence suggests that these TFs regulate partly distinct signaling programs during ERR (Ruberti et al., 2018; Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). Early indications of such divergence were reported by Ruberti et al. (2018), who showed that ER-stress-induced expression of ENDOPLASMIC RETICULUM-localized DnaJ family protein ERdj3B (ERdj3B), which encodes an ER-localized DnaJ family foldase, is preferentially dependent on bZIP28, whereas induction of BiP3 relies more strongly on bZIP60. These findings provided initial evidence that bZIP28 and bZIP60 differentially regulate UPR target genes.

Phenotypic analyses further support this distinction. Differences in root growth recovery between bzip28 and bzip60 single mutants become apparent as early as 3 days after ER stress termination, suggesting that divergent regulatory outputs are already established during the early recovery phase. Consistent with this notion, the transition from stress to recovery is accompanied by rapid and extensive transcriptomic reprogramming. Recent transcriptome analyses have revealed a set of genes that respond specifically during ERR, distinct from those induced during active ER stress (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). Notably, the number of ERR-responsive genes dependent on bZIP28 or bZIP60 peaked at 12 h after stress removal (645 bZIP28-dependent genes and 653 bZIP60-dependent genes), compared with substantially fewer targets at 0 h (50 and 52 genes, respectively) and 24 h (216 and 218 genes, respectively). These results indicate that bZIP28 and bZIP60 coordinate ERR-associated gene expression in a temporally restricted and phase-specific manner.

Mechanistic insights from this study further suggest that bZIP28- and bZIP60-mediated regulation during ERR involves additional layers of transcriptional control (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). Promoters of bZIP28- or bZIP60-dependent ERR genes were enriched for non-bZIP TF-binding motifs, suggesting cooperative or hierarchical interactions with other regulatory factors. Moreover, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing performed during ERR revealed that, although genes bound by bZIP28 and bZIP60 overlap substantially at canonical ER-stress-related loci, each TF also binds a distinct subset of genes associated with different biological pathways. For example, bZIP28-exclusive targets were enriched for genes involved in biotic stress responses, whereas bZIP60-exclusive targets showed enrichment for abiotic-stress-related pathways. Together, these findings support a model in which bZIP28 and bZIP60 operate in partially independent transcriptional modules that collectively shape growth recovery.

Importantly, these molecular distinctions align with phenotypic outcomes. The bzip28 single mutant consistently exhibited more severe defects in post-stress growth recovery than the bzip60 mutant (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a), indicating that bZIP28-dependent pathways exert a greater influence on growth restoration. Together, these results indicate that functional redundancy between bZIP28 and bZIP60 during ER stress does not fully extend to ERR, where each regulator contributes distinct and temporally defined outputs.

IRE1-mediated regulation of growth recovery and fitness during ERR

Although IRE1 and bZIP60 are traditionally viewed as components of a linear signaling cascade during ER stress, this relationship does not fully explain their roles during ERR. Genetic evidence indicates that IRE1 possesses functions that are at least partly independent of bZIP60 and contribute to post-stress growth recovery (Ruberti et al., 2018). In ERR assays, the ire1a/b bzip60 triple mutant exhibits more severe growth defects than the ire1a/b double mutant, indicating that IRE1 and bZIP60 contribute to growth restoration through overlapping but non-identical pathways.

The molecular mechanisms underlying IRE1-mediated regulation of ERR remain incompletely understood; however, RIDD has emerged as a compelling candidate mechanism. Unlike bZIP60 splicing, RIDD represents a post-transcriptional output of IRE1 signaling and selectively degrades mRNAs involved in diverse cellular processes (Mishiba et al., 2013). The role of RIDD in growth regulation is supported by observations that ire1a/b mutants consistently display shortened primary roots even under non-stress conditions (Chen and Brandizzi, 2012; Bao et al., 2019; Ko et al., 2023), a phenotype that is absent in bzip60 mutants but retained in ire1a/b bzip60 plants (Deng et al., 2013). This phenotype has been linked to altered activity of TARGET OF RAPAMYCIN (TOR), a conserved serine/threonine kinase that functions as a master regulator of cellular metabolism and growth across eukaryotes (Angelos and Brandizzi, 2021).

Recent work by Reagan et al. has shown that IRE1 suppresses TOR signaling via RIDD, thereby modulating growth responses to environmental stress (Reagan et al., 2025). Although this study did not explicitly examine ERR, it provides a mechanistic framework through which IRE1 could regulate growth restoration independently of transcriptional UPR outputs. By selectively degrading mRNAs that promote TOR activity, IRE1 may impose a transient growth restraint during stress and facilitate the controlled reactivation of growth during recovery. This model offers a plausible explanation for the distinct contributions of IRE1 to ERR and underscores the importance of post-transcriptional regulation in coordinating proteostasis with growth.

Collectively, these findings argue against viewing the UPR as a single signaling module during ERR. Instead, bZIP28, bZIP60, and IRE1 deploy distinct yet integrated regulatory strategies, spanning transcriptional specialization and post-transcriptional control, to orchestrate the complex transition from stress mitigation to growth restoration. Understanding how these regulator-specific mechanisms are integrated remains a central challenge for future studies of ERR.

UPR signaling shapes ERR

The findings discussed in the previous section collectively indicate that ERR is not governed by a single linear signaling cascade but instead emerges from the coordinated, regulator-specific actions of core UPR regulators. By partitioning transcriptional and post-transcriptional outputs across bZIP28, bZIP60, and IRE1, the UPR differentially engages distinct biological programs that together enable growth restoration. A key unresolved question, therefore, is how these regulator-specific outputs are translated into downstream physiological and developmental pathways that drive recovery at the organismal level. In this section, we examine biological pathways operating downstream of, or in close coordination with, the UPR during ERR. We first discuss how environmental inputs, such as light, temperature, and circadian timing, shape UPR activity and recovery outcomes. We then consider how UPR signaling intersects with energy-sensing pathways, systemic communication, and phytohormone signaling to coordinate growth restoration at the organismal level.

Light, temperature, and circadian signaling in ERR

Plants rely heavily on light perception to adapt to environmental change, and extensive crosstalk between light signaling and the UPR has been documented. Increased light intensity enhances sensitivity to ER stress, and this response is mediated in part by HYPOCOTYL 5 (HY5), a key light-signaling bZIP TF. HY5 negatively regulates bZIP28 by suppressing its transcriptional activation activity (Nawkar et al., 2017). HY5 itself is ubiquitinated by the E3 ligase complex formed by CONSTITUTIVE PHOTOMORPHOGENIC 1 (COP1) and SUPPRESSOR OF PHYA-105 (SPA1), leading to its degradation by the proteasome (Osterlund et al., 2000; Holm et al., 2002). Notably, the ER stress hypersensitivity of cop1 mutants is fully rescued in the hy5 cop1 double mutant (Kang et al., 2021), demonstrating that HY5 mediates light-dependent modulation of the UPR. Additional regulatory complexity arises from phytochrome B (PhyB), which controls the nuclear localization of SPA1 (Zheng et al., 2013; Podolec and Ulm, 2018). Moreover, COP1 abundance in the nucleus increases under ER stress (Kang et al., 2021), suggesting feedback regulation between UPR signaling and light perception. PhyB also enhances UPR gene expression in a red/far-red ratio-dependent manner, indicating that light quality modulates UPR outputs during ER stress (Ahn et al., 2022).

Temperature represents another environmental cue with a direct influence on protein folding and proteostasis. PhyB phosphorylates PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTOR 4 (PIF4), a major regulator of thermomorphogenesis, thereby reducing PIF4 protein stability under noninductive conditions (Lorrain et al., 2008). Recent evidence has implicated bZIP17, bZIP28, and bZIP60 as upstream enhancers of PIF4-mediated thermomorphogenic growth (Zhang et al., 2025). At the transcriptional level, PIF4 recruits UPR-associated bZIP TFs to the promoters of growth-promoting genes under thermomorphogenesis, including the PIF4 locus itself. At the post-translational level, these UPR-associated bZIP TFs stabilize the PIF4 protein by competing with PhyB for PIF4 interaction. Interestingly, UPR activation during thermomorphogenesis precedes PIF4 induction and persists after the initial temperature exposure, supporting a role for the UPR in both mitigating proteostasis stress and sustaining adaptive growth under warm-temperature conditions.

The circadian clock further integrates environmental cues with UPR signaling. Time-of-day-dependent regulation has been shown to influence plant sensitivity to ER stress, and circadian control promotes ERR (Ahn et al., 2025). The morning clock component CCA1 plays a central role in this process by interacting with bZIP28 and enhancing its transcriptional activation activity. Loss of CCA1 impairs growth restoration during ERR, highlighting the importance of temporal regulation in post-stress recovery. Together, these findings demonstrate that UPR signaling is deeply integrated with light, temperature, and circadian pathways, not only to attenuate ER stress but also to coordinate growth restoration during ERR at the molecular and physiological levels.

The UPR and energy-sensing pathways

In mammalian cells, a link between the UPR and cellular energy metabolism has long been recognized because protein folding and chaperone activity are energetically demanding processes that require ATP and carbohydrates (Hernández et al., 2002; van der Harg et al., 2017). However, how UPR signaling intersects with energy-sensing pathways to maintain cellular viability remains incompletely understood. Although UPR activation increases the production of ER chaperones, global protein synthesis is simultaneously attenuated, leaving the net energetic cost of ER stress difficult to define (Depaoli et al., 2019).

In response to ER stress, the UPR constitutes a systemic signaling network in plants and animals (Han et al., 2013; Srivastava et al., 2018; Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). In plants, emerging evidence suggests that UPR outputs intersect with systemic energy homeostasis during ERR. In Arabidopsis, expression of the sugar transporter genes SWEET13 and SWEET14 is elevated in the bzip60 mutant relative to the wild type during ERR (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). An earlier study demonstrated that expression of the UDP-glucose/UDP-galactose transporter gene UTR1 is induced under ER stress and that loss of UTR1 results in elevated expression of UPR chaperones (Reyes et al., 2006). Together, these studies suggest a reciprocal relationship between UPR signaling and carbohydrate allocation, although the molecular mechanisms linking these processes remain largely unresolved. Understanding how systemic energy status contributes to UPR-mediated growth restoration represents an emerging frontier in ERR research.

To resume growth after environmental stress, plants must dynamically reallocate nutrient resources and coordinate energy homeostasis with growth programs. Central to this process are TOR and SUCROSE NON-FERMENTING1-RELATED KINASE 1 (SnRK1), two evolutionarily conserved protein kinases that translate nutrient and energy status into growth- and stress-related outputs by promoting anabolic growth programs and low-energy adaptive responses, respectively (Baena-González et al., 2007; Xiong et al., 2013; Mair et al., 2015; Nukarinen et al., 2016). Consistent with this model, TOR signaling is hyperactivated in ire1a/b mutants even under physiological conditions, resulting in growth defects relative to the wild type (Angelos and Brandizzi, 2021). These findings suggest that the UPR contributes to the sensing and regulation of cellular energy metabolism through TOR, both during stress and, potentially, during ERR. Whether the UPR–TOR axis directly contributes to growth restoration during ERR remains an open question. Notably, in Drosophila cells, TOR activity increases under ER stress in an ATF6-dependent manner (Allen and Seo, 2018), raising the possibility that plant UPR TFs, including bZIP28 and bZIP60, may also interface with TOR signaling. Given that SnRK1 antagonizes TOR activity in systemic energy regulation, it may also participate in UPR-mediated energy signaling during ERR. Dissecting how these energy-sensing pathways are integrated with UPR outputs will be essential for understanding growth restoration following ER stress.

Autophagy represents another critical component of the energy-sensing network. In plants, ER stress has been proposed to induce autophagy primarily through IRE1b, and this response can be suppressed by overexpression of BiP, indicating a direct link between ER folding capacity and autophagic activation (Yang et al., 2016). UPR-dependent autophagy can also be triggered by phosphate deficiency (Pu et al., 2017; Naumann et al., 2019), suggesting that this pathway integrates multiple stress signals. Importantly, ER-stress-induced autophagy is at least partly independent of TOR inhibition (Pu et al., 2017), highlighting the complexity of UPR–energy signaling interactions. How these pathways are coordinated during the transition from stress to recovery remains an important unresolved question.

Phytohormone signaling and systemic regulation during ERR

Although molecular links between the UPR and energy sensors such as TOR and SnRK1 are beginning to emerge, how these signaling events translate into physiological growth restoration during ERR remains unclear. One potential point of convergence is plasmodesmata-mediated cell-to-cell communication. Plasmodesmata are membrane-lined channels that connect adjacent cells and enable the systemic movement of signaling molecules, thereby coordinating developmental and stress responses across tissues (Lucas et al., 1995; Lim et al., 2016; Bahafid et al., 2026). In plants, UPR activation has been shown to propagate systemic signals toward the shoot through plasmodesmata (Lai et al., 2018a, Lai et al., 2018b), suggesting that plasmodesmata may serve as an important route through which ER stress information is transmitted beyond the initially stressed cells.

Several lines of evidence suggest that energy status, sugar availability, and stress signaling functionally converge on plasmodesmata regulation. TOR negatively regulates plasmodesmata permeability (Brunkard et al., 2020), whereas studies in potato have shown that dormancy release, a form of growth reactivation, is controlled by soluble sugar levels and promoted by knockdown of the SnRK1β subunit (Danieli et al., 2023). Although these findings come from distinct experimental systems, together, they suggest that plasmodesmata may act as a systemic integration hub where proteostasis, energy status, and growth-regulatory signals are coordinated. In this model, UPR signaling, TOR/SnRK1-dependent energy regulation, and sugar availability should be viewed not as components of a simple linear pathway, but rather as interconnected processes that converge on cell-to-cell communication during post-stress growth restoration.

Phytohormone signaling provides an additional layer of regulation at this potential convergence point. Phytohormones play central roles in coordinating the balance between stress responses and growth, root growth recovery, and developmental reprogramming after abiotic stress, making them strong candidates as mediators of growth restoration following ER stress (Achard et al., 2006; Skirycz et al., 2011; Geng et al., 2013; Shi et al., 2014). Among them, ABA is particularly relevant because of its established role in plasmodesmata regulation. ABA promotes callose deposition at plasmodesmata, thereby restricting their aperture and limiting cell-to-cell transport (Tylewicz et al., 2018). Recent work in the moss Physcomitrium patens further demonstrated that exogenous ABA restricts plasmodesmata transport and identified ABSCISIC ACID INSENSITIVE 5 (ABI5) as a central regulator of this process (Jinno et al., 2025). Thus, ABA-associated signaling components may influence ERR not only through transcriptional regulation but also by modulating systemic communication through plasmodesmata.

Consistent with this possibility, the plant UPR functions as a systemic signal that is modulated by plasmodesmata aperture (Lai et al., 2018a), and ABI5 functions as a co-regulator of bZIP28 and bZIP60, enhancing their transcriptional activation of UPR target genes through conserved G-box motifs (Ko et al., 2023). Conversely, another ABA-associated TF, GBF2, negatively regulates bZIP28 and bZIP60 during ERR by competing for DNA binding sites (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022b). Together, these findings suggest that ABA-associated regulators may fine-tune UPR outputs at multiple levels, including transcriptional control and, potentially, plasmodesmata-mediated systemic signaling. Importantly, ABA levels themselves do not appear to be altered by ER stress or UPR deficiency (Ko et al., 2023), suggesting that ABI5, GBF2, and related ABA-associated components may contribute to ERR through mechanisms that are partly independent of canonical ABA accumulation. Whether ABA signaling directly contributes to ERR, or whether selected ABA-associated regulators have been co-opted into UPR regulatory networks, remains an important open question.

Beyond ABA, emerging evidence suggests that ethylene signaling may also contribute to ERR. Ethylene is perceived by a family of ER-localized histidine kinase receptors, including ETHYLENE RESPONSE 1 (ETR1), which interact with CONSTITUTIVE TRIPLE RESPONSE 1 (CTR1), a negative regulator of ethylene signaling (Chang et al., 1993; Kieber et al., 1993; Hua and Meyerowitz, 1998). In tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), exogenous ethylene induces the expression of UPR regulators and marker genes, including SlIRE1a, SlIRE1b, SlbZIP60, and SlBiP, whereas ethylene receptor mutants show attenuated induction of SlIRE1b and SlBiP under ER stress (Czékus et al., 2022). These findings suggest that ethylene signaling modulates UPR activation, although the functional connections between ethylene pathway components and the core UPR regulators bZIP28, bZIP60, and IRE1 remain to be defined.

Supporting a role for ethylene in ERR, transcriptomic analyses revealed significant enrichment of ethylene-related Gene Ontology terms in co-expression modules induced specifically during ERR but not during active ER stress (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a). This observation raises the possibility that ethylene contributes to growth restoration rather than stress attenuation per se. Other phytohormones are also likely to participate in ERR. Gibberellins and cytokinins, which antagonize ABA in multiple developmental contexts (Wang et al., 2011; Guan et al., 2014; Li et al., 2021), may function within interconnected hormonal networks during recovery. Auxin represents another promising candidate: numerous auxin transporter genes are differentially expressed during ERR (Ko and Brandizzi, 2022a), and during thermomorphogenesis, UPR-associated bZIP TFs promote PIF-dependent expression of the auxin biosynthesis gene YUCCA8 (YUC8) and the auxin-responsive gene INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID-INDUCIBLE 19 (IAA19) (Zhang et al., 2025). Together, these findings suggest previously unrecognized crosstalk between UPR signaling and auxin-mediated growth regulation during recovery.

Taken together, these findings suggest that phytohormone-associated regulators and systemic signaling routes may cooperate with core UPR outputs to coordinate growth restoration during ERR. Rather than acting as a single linear pathway, UPR-bZIP TFs and IRE1-mediated RIDD, TOR/SnRK1-dependent energy signaling, plasmodesmata-mediated communication, and hormone-associated regulators appear to form an interconnected network that links proteostasis to systemic growth control (Figure 1). Concluding remarks and outlook

Research on the UPR as a central adaptive response to ER stress in plants has advanced substantially over the past two decades. In contrast, the role of the UPR in ERR has only recently begun to be explored, despite its strong potential to uncover biologically important downstream processes and inform strategies for enhancing crop resilience. Accumulating evidence now indicates that the UPR is not merely activated to mitigate stress-induced damage but is also intricately configured to enable rapid and coordinated restoration of growth following stress relief.

To date, investigations into UPR-mediated growth restoration during ERR have relied primarily on classical genetic, biochemical, and omics-based approaches. Moving forward, deeper mechanistic insight will require the integration of innovative, multidisciplinary methodologies that capture the complexity of UPR signaling across scales. These include high-throughput genetic and chemical screens, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, large-scale network modeling, and machine-learning-based approaches for predicting gene function and protein structure. Such strategies will accelerate the discovery of novel regulators and regulatory principles governing UPR-mediated growth restoration during ERR.

A key experimental caveat in defining ERR-specific functions is that many current conclusions are derived from loss-of-function mutants of core UPR regulators. Because these mutants are also impaired in mounting a proper ER stress response, growth or transcriptomic defects observed during recovery may partly reflect carryover effects of impaired stress mitigation rather than direct roles in the recovery phase itself. Future studies should therefore distinguish stress-phase and recovery-phase functions using temporally controlled genetic perturbation, inducible complementation, phase-specific transcriptomics and proteomics, and recovery assays in which ER stress intensity is normalized across genotypes. Such approaches will be critical for distinguishing ERR-specific regulatory mechanisms from secondary effects inherited from impaired stress responses.

Most experimental studies of ERR have employed chemical ER stress inducers because of their robustness and reproducibility (Olden et al., 1979; Kaufman, 1999; Koizumi et al., 1999). However, translating insights from these controlled systems to agricultural contexts will require broader experimental frameworks that incorporate physiologically relevant stress regimes. Manipulating UPR signaling through genome editing, engineered chaperones, or conventional breeding strategies could offer promising avenues for enhancing growth restoration following recurrent abiotic stresses, such as heat, drought, or flooding.

One particularly compelling translational opportunity lies in improving resilience to nutrient stress. Root growth responses to phosphate availability are closely linked to the stability and activity of TOR, and recent evidence indicates that the UPR inhibits TOR signaling through RIDD-mediated cleavage of RAPTOR1B mRNA (Reagan et al., 2025). This finding raises the possibility that UPR signaling coordinates root growth and nutrient allocation during ERR. Determining how TOR activity is dynamically regulated throughout the recovery phase will be critical for understanding this regulatory axis and for evaluating its potential utility in crop improvement.

Although a wide range of environmental stresses are known to activate the UPR (Deng et al., 2011; Adhikari et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2025), it remains unclear whether UPR-mediated growth restoration represents a universal recovery strategy across stress types. Different stressors engage distinct UPR branches (e.g., bZIP17 in salt stress or pathogen-induced accumulation of unspliced bZIP60) (Liu et al., 2008; Adhikari et al., 2024), suggesting that UPR outputs may be fine-tuned to specific stress contexts. Systematic comparisons of UPR regulator function during recovery from diverse environmental stresses will be essential for defining common and stress-specific recovery programs.

Finally, emerging insights into UPR-mediated growth restoration may inform our understanding of agronomically important traits such as seed dormancy. Dormancy represents a tightly regulated state of growth arrest that is critical for plant survival and agricultural productivity (Bentsink et al., 2006; Footitt et al., 2011; Nakabayashi et al., 2012). Given that the UPR modulates energy metabolism and allocation during ERR (Figure 2; Table 1), similar mechanisms may contribute to the establishment, maintenance, or release of dormancy. Consistent with this idea, expression of UPR marker genes correlates with the progression of seed and bud dormancy in peach (Prunus persica) (Fu et al., 2014), pointing to previously unexplored roles of UPR signaling in developmental transitions beyond stress recovery.
