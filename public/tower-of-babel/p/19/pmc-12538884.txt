Characterizing drought-resilient cotton genotypes through morpho-physiological and biochemical traits at flowering stage
Authors: Muhammad Yousaf Shani, Allah Ditta, Muhammad Kashif Riaz Khan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrought is a major abiotic stressor significantly reducing global crop productivity by disrupting key morpho-physiological and biochemical processes, including stomatal conductance, photosynthetic efficiency, and water use dynamics. This study systematically assessed the effects of water scarcity on fifty genetically diverse cotton genotypes under three different conditions: normal field capacity (100% FC, monitored by soil moisture weight), moderate drought stress (60% FC), and severe drought stress (40% FC) during the flowering stage, a critical period for crop yield. Key morphological (shoot length, root length, biomass), physiological (relative water content, stomatal conductance), and biochemical traits (antioxidant enzyme activity, oxidative stress markers) were evaluated across all treatments. The results revealed a gradual decline in trait response as drought severity increased, with significant genotype-dependent variations. Histograms captured frequency distribution trends across different stress levels, while principal component analysis (PCA) differentiated trait expressions among genotypes. Pearson’s correlation analysis highlighted strong positive and negative relationships among the traits based on their drought responses. The multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) proved to be a valuable tool, identifying G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) as the most drought-resistant genotypes. These genotypes showed superior performance and maintained higher water status, minimized oxidative damage, and activated effective defense mechanisms under stress. These findings provide valuable insights into the morpho-physiological and biochemical traits associated with drought resilience and lay the groundwork for targeted breeding programs to develop drought-tolerant cotton varieties. Introduction

Groundwater resources are rapidly depleting due to climate change, reduced rainfall, and increasing global water demand, severely impacting crop productivity [1]. Crops are vulnerable to significant yield losses from abiotic stresses such as drought, heat, and salinity, posing a major threat to global food security [2]. Among these stresses, drought, driven primarily by erratic and insufficient rainfall, drastically affects crop productivity, physiology, and growth [3]. Water deficit is the foremost abiotic stress affecting 28% land area globally, with different levels of water scarcity [4]. Therefore, the dire need is to develop drought-tolerant crop varieties to cope with water deficit and ensure global food security. Among the cotton-producing countries, Pakistan ranked seventh, accounting for a 3.4% contribution to global cotton productivity after China, India, the United States, Brazil, Australia, and Turkey. The global average cotton yield is approximately 849 kg per hectare [5]. The cotton industry generates a significant economic impact of around $600 billion worldwide [6, 7]. In recent decades, a 14% decline in cotton productivity has been noticed, with a dramatic decline from 13.96 to 11.93 million bales in Pakistan, mainly due to erratic rainfall patterns and harsh climatic regimes [8]. Severe drought stress compromises photosynthesis by inhibiting chlorophyll synthesis, damaging essential enzymes, and increasing lipid peroxidation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and electrolyte leakage [9]. In response to these challenges, plants produce both enzymatic antioxidants (such as peroxidase, catalase, and ascorbate peroxidase) and non-enzymatic antioxidants (including proline, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds) to mitigate oxidative stress and damage [10]. Drought-induced water stress disrupts the balance between ROS and antioxidants, leading to oxidative damage that harms vital cellular components and reduces mitochondrial function [11].

In cotton species, Gossypium hirsutum, which accounts for 90% of global cotton production, is particularly sensitive to drought stress, which negatively impacts root architecture, physiological functions, and lint yield [12]. While cotton plants are moderately drought-tolerant during the vegetative stage, and highly sensitive to water deficit during the flowering stage [13]. Drought at this critical stage can cause flower drop and boll abscission, with ethylene synthesis triggering boll shedding and overall productivity decline. Additionally, drought impairs electron transport and reduces photosystem efficiency, further hampering growth and boll development [14]. Cotton fiber, representing 35% of global fiber production, is the second-largest oil-producing crop after soybeans. The ongoing global water crisis emphasizes the urgent need for drought-resistant cotton varieties [15]. To cope with the detrimental effects of drought stress, cotton plants employ various strategies such as recovery, avoidance, escape, and tolerance, making it essential to understand plant responses in water-limited environments [16]. Morphological traits like plant height, flower retention, and boll weight are key indicators of drought tolerance. Root morphology, high leaf water content, and reduced stomatal conductance and transpiration rates also contribute to drought resilience, providing valuable insights into identifying drought-tolerant genotypes [17].

Among the traits that can distinguish drought-tolerant from susceptible cotton genotypes are shoot length, root length, flower retention, leaf area, and boll number per plant [18]. Understanding the morpho-anatomical characteristics of roots is crucial for deciphering drought stress mechanisms [19]. Drought-tolerant genotypes often exhibit higher leaf water content in water-limited environments. Under drought stress, cotton plants accumulate osmolytes like proline, soluble sugars, and total free amino acids to lower cellular osmotic potential, maintaining leaf turgor and relative water content (RWC) [20]. These compounds also protect cellular structures from dehydration damage while sustaining metabolic activity. Enhanced osmotic adjustment capacity is a key determinant of drought tolerance in high-yielding cotton cultivars [21]. Studies have shown that in water-limited environments, genotypes that maintain higher proline and relative water contents (RWC) levels are considered drought-tolerant genotypes that could be used to develop drought-tolerant varieties in cops such as mungbean [22].

Osmoregulation plays a crucial role in enhancing drought tolerance in plants. It involves the active adjustment of the osmotic potential of plant cells to retain water under conditions of limited availability [23]. The prevalence of extreme water deficit stimulates the synthesis of soluble sugars, polyphenols, proline, amino acids, and flavonoid contents, whereas it declines the carotenoids, photosynthetic rate, relative water content, plant biomass, and water potential. The accumulation of compatible solutes like proline, sugars, total free amino acids, and polyols aids plants in maintaining cellular turgor pressure and restricting water loss. These solutes also play a role in stabilizing proteins and cellular membranes under stress. In addition, the synthesis of osmotic regulators is often tightly coordinated with antioxidant systems, providing a dual defense against oxidative damage and water loss. In drought-sensitive plants, the failure to effectively regulate osmotic balance under water stress can lead to cellular dehydration, disrupted metabolic processes, and ultimately plant death. Conversely, plants with efficient osmoregulatory systems (higher solute accumulation) can maintain cellular metabolic activities, relative water content, higher proline concentration, and growth even in water-deficient conditions, offering a crucial adaptive advantage under drought stress.

Drought stress also substantially declines the photosynthesis rate and stomatal conductance, making them essential parameters for distinguishing drought-sensitive from drought-tolerant genotypes [24]. The response of cotton genotypes to normal versus water-limited environments varies considerably, underscoring the importance of regularly screening germplasm to enhance crop productivity and adaptability in fluctuating climatic conditions [25]. Furthermore, drought stress negatively affects photosynthetic efficiency, transpiration rate, and relative water content in drought-sensitive genotypes, leading to stomatal closure and impaired carbon dioxide fixation [26].

This study aims to enhance genetic resources by evaluating drought-resilient cotton genotypes, with a particular focus on the critical flowering stage. The novelty of this research lies in its comprehensive approach, incorporating advanced morpho-physiological and biochemical markers, especially during the reproductive phase for two weeks consecutively. Unlike previous studies that primarily concentrate on vegetative growth, this research emphasizes the flowering stage, where drought stress significantly impacts yield potential and fiber quality. By employing a multi-trait genotype-ideotype-based index (MGIDI), the study refines drought tolerance screening and provides novel insights into the physiological and biochemical adaptations that contribute to drought resilience. This approach aims to accelerate the development of drought-resistant cotton varieties, addressing the pressing need for sustainable cotton production in the face of global water scarcity. In the current study, we also emphasized osmoregulation by sustaining the higher solutes, including proline, soluble sugars, amino acids, and phenolic compounds, that maintain cellular osmotic balance during water stress, highlighting its role in conferring drought tolerance and enhancing plant survival under water-limited conditions.

This research aimed to investigate the effects of water scarcity on fifty diverse cotton genotypes sourced from various research institutes in Pakistan, including the Nuclear Institute for Agriculture and Biology (NIAB), Ayub Agriculture Research Institute (AARI), Cotton Research Institute (CRI), Central Cotton Research Institute (CCRI), and the University of Agriculture Faisalabad, Pakistan (Table 1). The study was in glasshouse conditions using a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications for each treatment, and each genotype to ensure statistical reliability.

In addition, irrigation treatments were carefully managed to impose three distinct water stress conditions: normal field capacity (100% FC), moderate drought stress (60% FC), and severe drought stress (40% FC). The experimental setup allowed for precise monitoring of soil moisture and plant responses under each treatment, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of drought-induced physiological and biochemical changes. The genotypes were monitored throughout the growing season, with particular focus on key stages such as flowering and boll development, to capture the impact of water stress on cotton productivity and resilience.

Experiment detail

To initiate the experiment, seeds from the collected genotypes were planted in plastic pots (28 × 65 cm) in August 2023. Each pot was filled with a soil mixture consisting of 9 kg of clay and 1 kg of sand, for a total of 10 kg. The pots were then irrigated with tap water and allowed to reach the desired field capacity before sowing. The seeds were soaked overnight for 12 h, and the following morning at 7:00 am, four to five seeds were sown at a depth of 2 cm in each pot. Once germination was successful, thinning was carried out to leave one seedling per pot. During the experiment, the glasshouse temperature was controlled to remain between 34 and 38 °C during the day and 26–28 °C at night using a heating and cooling system. Twenty-one days after germination, each pot received 100 kg N ha−1 as urea and 70 kg P2O5 ha−1 as DAP. The seedlings were regularly irrigated with tap water until the first flowers appeared. Drought stress was introduced at the flowering stage (45 days after sowing) by maintaining three distinct soil moisture regimes: 100% field capacity (FC) as control, 60% FC for moderate drought stress, and 40% FC for severe drought stress conditions in growing cotton genotypes. The irrigation treatments were precisely regulated through daily gravimetric measurements using a digital balance (WT10002A, WT-A, China), with water supplementation adjusted to maintain the target moisture levels throughout the 14-day stress period during flowering. Following this controlled drought imposition, all the morpho-physiological and biochemical attributes were appraised to assess genotype-specific responses to water deficit conditions.

Soil moisture measurement

Soil field capacity refers to the amount of water retained in the soil after it has been saturated and allowed to drain freely under the influence of gravity. To determine the soil field capacity (FC) used in this experiment, thoroughly mixed soil was used. Two hundred grams of dried soil (Wd) were placed into a one-liter plastic beaker with drainage holes covered with filter paper at the bottom, water was added slowly and evenly until it became fully saturated. This beaker was placed on another beaker with a 500 ml capacity. After the watering, the soil was given 1 h to fully absorb it because the soil was dry, and it took time for the water to penetrate thoroughly. When the soil was fully saturated, it was allowed to drain freely through the drainage hole for 48 h, so all the gravitational water was drained out. After the drainage period, the container with the soil was weighed to determine the wet weight. This weight includes both the soil and the water it holds, and the container weight. After deducing the beaker and filter paper weight, the soil weight with the moisture it retained was determined on a digital electrical balance as a wet weight (Ww), which was 256 g. The amount of water the soil holds at field capacity was calculated using the difference between the wet and dry weights of the soil:

Field Capacity (%) =[(Ww − Wd)/Wd] ×100 = [(256 − 200)/200]x100.

In the present case, it was 28%.

For 100% FC, 280 g or mL of water is required for 1000 g–1 kg of soil, and it was soil + water is 1280 g.

For 60% FC [28/100) x 60] 168 g or mL of water required for 1000 g–1 kg of soil, and it was soil + water 1168 g.

For 40% FC [28/100) x 40] 112 g or mL of water required for 1000 g–1 kg of soil, and it was soil + water 1112 g.

Morphological attributes

After applying drought stress, various morphological attributes were measured, including shoot length (SL), root length (RL), shoot fresh weight (SFW), shoot dry weight (SDW), root dry weight (RDW), and root fresh weight (RFW). Shoots from all genotypes were thoroughly rinsed with tap water, followed by two washes with distilled water to remove any contaminants. Roots were carefully excised using a sharp blade, and both root and shoot lengths were measured and recorded in centimetres. To minimize root loss during the uprooting process, the pots were filled with a 3:1 mixture of clay soil and sand, which improved soil porosity and facilitated better root recovery. The roots were gently uprooted by flooding the pots with water, ensuring minimal damage and consistent recovery across all treatments. Careful washing and sieving of the roots were performed to retrieve even the fine roots, ensuring the most accurate measurements possible. Once collected, both root and shoot samples were dried in an oven (EB-4 A) at 72 °C for four days to determine their dry weights. Additionally, flower retention (FR) in cotton was assessed by tracking the number of flowers retained over time. Healthy, uniform plants were tagged at flowering, and newly opened flowers were marked with color-coded small badges for easy identification. Flower retention was monitored every 8–10 days until boll formation. After 25 days post-anthesis, the number of retained flowers was counted, and the percentage of flower retention was calculated using the formula provided in [13]. Furthermore, the number of bolls per plant was assessed by counting the bolls on each plant before harvesting.

Physiological attributes

To assess the physiological parameters of the growing cotton genotypes, an InfraRed Gas Analyzer (IRGA) model Ci-340 (USA) was used to measure critical plant functions, including stomatal conductance (SC), photosynthetic activity (Pn), and transpiration rate (E). These parameters were measured after two consecutive weeks (14 days) of irrigation treatments applied to the growing cotton genotypes, and these observations are essential for understanding how plants respond to different water availability levels, particularly relevant under drought stress conditions.

The study involved three distinct treatments: control (100% field capacity, FC), moderate drought stress (60% FC), and severe drought stress (40% FC). Field capacity (FC) refers to the amount of water held in the soil after it has drained and represents optimal soil moisture for plant growth. Each treatment was applied to replicate cotton plants, and measurements were conducted on sunny days to ensure consistent light and environmental conditions, specifically between 9:30 a.m. and 12:00 p.m. These timeframes were chosen to capture peak photosynthetic activity when the plants are fully exposed to sunlight, as this is when physiological processes like photosynthesis and transpiration are most active. For each treatment, the data were collected from the topmost maturing leaf, which was fully exposed to sunlight. This leaf is typically the most representative of the plant’s overall health and physiological status. By focusing on this leaf, we aimed to minimize variation caused by other leaves, ensuring reliable and consistent measurements across genotypes.

Finally, the leaves were oven-dried at 70 °C for 48 h to obtain their dry weight. Relative Water Content (RWC) was determined using the formula described by [27].

Biochemical attributes

Several biochemical markers associated with drought stress were assessed to evaluate drought-resilient and drought-susceptible cotton genotypes. These markers provide important insights into how the plants respond at the biochemical level to water stress. The following methods were employed to measure various biochemical parameters:

Antioxidants and reactive oxygen species

Superoxide Dismutase (SOD) Activity was determined using the method described by Giannopolitis and Ries [28]. This enzyme plays a crucial role in protecting plants from oxidative damage by catalyzing the dismutation of superoxide radicals into oxygen and hydrogen peroxide. Peroxidase (POD) and Catalase (CAT) activities were measured using the protocol proposed by Chance and Maehly [29]. Both enzymes are involved in the detoxification of reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated during drought stress. POD primarily aids in the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide, while CAT decomposes hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen. Ascorbate Peroxidase (APX) activity was measured following the method outlined by Erel [30]. This enzyme also helps in scavenging hydrogen peroxide, playing a key role in the antioxidant defense system during oxidative stress. Malondialdehyde (MDA), a product of lipid peroxidation, was quantified according to the technique provided by Cakmak and Horst [31]. Elevated MDA levels indicate increased oxidative stress and membrane damage, a common response to drought stress. Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2) Content was quantified following the protocol described by Velikova [32]. Hydrogen peroxide is another ROS that accumulates under stress conditions, and its accumulation can signal oxidative damage to plant cells.

Osmolytes

Total soluble proteins (TSP) were quantified using Bradford’s protocol [33]. The total protein content is a key indicator of the plant’s overall metabolic activity and cellular function under stress conditions. In addition, total soluble sugars (TSS) were analyzed following the method described by Riazi et al. [34]. Sugars are essential for energy production and osmotic regulation, and their levels typically increase in response to water stress as the plant adapts to maintain turgor pressure. Moreover, total free amino acids (TFA) were determined according to the procedure outlined by Hamilton et al. [35]. Amino acids play a vital role in osmotic adjustment and the plant’s stress response mechanisms. Despite this, proline content (Pro) was estimated using the method established by Bates and Waldren [36]. Proline is an osmolyte that accumulates in response to stress, particularly drought, and helps in protecting the plant cells from dehydration. Total phenolic content (TPC) was determined using the protocol described by Ainsworth EA, Gillespie [37]. Phenolic compounds are involved in the plant’s antioxidant defense system, and their levels typically increase in response to environmental stresses like drought. In addition, total flavonoid content (TFC) was measured following the method of Zhishen et al. [38]. Flavonoids are a group of polyphenolic compounds that have antioxidant properties and are important in protecting plants against oxidative damage.

Photosynthetic pigments

The concentrations of chlorophyll a (Chl.a), chlorophyll b (Chl.b), total chlorophyll (T.chl), and carotenoids (Car) were extracted using the protocol described by Arnon [39]. These pigments are essential for photosynthesis, and their content directly indicates the plant’s ability to capture light and perform photosynthesis, especially under stress conditions. Multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) calculation and analysis

The MGIDI analysis was employed to rank the studied accessions and identify genotypes with superior traits. This process involved evaluating each genotype’s proximity to an ideal drought-tolerant ideotype by setting optimal values for traits associated with drought resilience. Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses, including descriptive statistics, analysis of variance (ANOVA), histograms, principal component analysis, and Pearson’s correlation analysis, were performed to evaluate the response of genotypes through morpho-physiological and biochemical attributes under varying drought stress regimes. Before conducting these multivariate analyses, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were evaluated. Visual inspection of histograms for each trait across treatments indicated that the data were approximately normally distributed with relatively consistent variances that guided our decision to proceed with parametric methods. To control for potential inflation of Type I error due to multiple trait comparisons, a false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the p-values obtained from two-way ANOVA tests across all measured traits. The FDR adjustment was conducted using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure at a significance level of 5% [42]. This approach ensures that the probability of falsely declaring significant results remains controlled while maintaining statistical power. Additionally, Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test was used for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Histograms were developed for individual traits using Python software (version 3.12.8) to ascertain the frequency distribution of all the cotton accessions for individual parameters against imposed water stress treatments. In addition, for each treatment, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed using R software (version 4.4.2) to elucidate the interaction among genotypes and observed attributes. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to interpret the large, intricate datasets to ascertain the variability in analyzed traits under each imposed treatment. Despite this, MGIDI analysis was performed to discern the superior genotypes based on their mean performance, selection gain, and heritability percentage against imposed water deficit treatments. A Monte Carlo simulation was employed to evaluate the effectiveness of the index, testing various scenarios for different genotype counts, traits, and correlation structures to determine the likelihood of achieving desired trait improvements [8].

Physiological responses to drought stress

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) values revealed that drought stress significantly (P ≤ 0.05) influenced the physiological processes, including relative water contents (RWC), excised leaf water loss (ELWL), photosynthesis rate (Pn), and transpiration rate (E), in growing cotton genotypes. Under T0, the relative water content (RWC) was 78.76 ± 9.94, which dropped to 71.60 ± 9.54 under T1 and 65.87 ± 13.56 under T2. excised leaf water loss (ELWL) increased from 0.44 ± 0.17 under T0 to 0.54 ± 0.25 under T1, and 0.60 ± 0.37 under T2, reflecting dehydration-induced cellular damage. Similarly, photosynthetic efficiency and transpiration rate declined under drought stress. The transpiration rate (E) was 5.16 ± 1.58 under T0, decreasing to 1.92 ± 0.91 under T1 and 1.08 ± 0.41 under T2, indicating reduced stomatal activity in response to water deficit. Similarly, the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) dropped from 22.87 ± 3.70 at T0 to 17.51 ± 3.52 under T1, and further to 13.08 ± 2.28 under T2, reflecting a progressive decline in photosynthetic capacity under drought conditions.

Biochemical responses to drought stress

The induction of water deficits depicted significant (P ≤ 0.05) alterations in all the studied biochemical attributes. Carotenoids (Car) decreased from 0.43 ± 0.06 (T0) to 0.24 ± 0.03 (T1) and 0.12 ± 0.03 (T2). Chlorophyll a (Chl.a) dropped from 0.25 ± 0.05 (T0) to 0.16 ± 0.06 (T1) and 0.11 ± 0.04 (T2). Chlorophyll b (Chl.b) decreased from 0.18 ± 0.02 (T0) to 0.11 ± 0.02 (T1) and 0.10 ± 0.03 (T2), while Total Chlorophyll (T.chl) declined from 0.43 ± 0.06 (T0) to 0.28 ± 0.08 (T1) and 0.21 ± 0.08 (T2). Total soluble proteins (TSP) were reduced from 7.93 ± 2.67 (T0) to 6.05 ± 2.28 (T1) and 4.75 ± 1.02 (T2). In contrast, Total soluble sugars (TSS) increased from 2.70 ± 0.64 (T0) to 4.02 ± 1.17 (T1) and 5.58 ± 1.65 (T2), while total free amino acids (TFA) rose from 9.34 ± 1.71 (T0) to 25.92 ± 2.92 (T1) and 33.88 ± 3.06 (T2). Proline (Pro) increased from 105.28 ± 35.24 (T0) to 232.4 ± 71.71 (T1) and 307.75 ± 71.47 (T2). Total Phenolic Content (TPC) rose from 3.33 ± 0.57 (T0) to 4.41 ± 0.87 (T1) and 5.83 ± 0.75 (T2), and Total Flavonoid Content (TFC) increased from 2.13 ± 0.46 (T0) to 2.94 ± 0.63 (T1) and 3.83 ± 0.57 (T2) (Fig. 1; Table 3).

Under control conditions (T0), the mean values recorded for antioxidant enzymes along with standard deviation were superoxide dismutase (SOD) at 307.9 ± 78.95, peroxidase (POD) at 396.3 ± 92.07, catalase (CAT) at 270.7 ± 109.5, and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) at 248.9 ± 57.5, with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) at 1.72 ± 0.35 and malondialdehyde (MDA) at 0.35 ± 0.07. However, under moderate drought stress (T1), SOD increased to 456.3 ± 117.4, POD to 739.2 ± 136.9, CAT to 502.5 ± 198.3, and APX to 478 ± 126.4, while H2O2 rose to 3.14 ± 0.65 and MDA to 0.40 ± 0.06, indicating heightened oxidative stress. Under severe drought stress (T2), SOD, POD, CAT, and APX escalated further to 935.7 ± 265.4, 845.43 ± 184.5, 692.7 ± 266.61, and 659.97 ± 169.8, respectively, while H2O2 increased to 4.38 ± 0.92 and MDA to 0.52 ± 0.06, reflecting intensified oxidative damage as drought stress intensified (Fig. 2; Table 3).

Discussion

Drought is a major abiotic constraint that significantly reduces crop productivity and poses a serious threat to global food security, particularly in water-scarce regions [43]. To improve cotton productivity and lint quality under water-limited conditions, breeders have focused on developing drought-resistant genotypes. This study assessed fifty diverse cotton genotypes under normal, moderate, and severe drought stress to identify drought-tolerant accessions based on morpho-physiological and biochemical traits. Cotton genotypes G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) demonstrated desirable traits such as ideal shoot length, root length, shoot biomass (fresh and dry), and root biomass (fresh and dry), indicating their resilience to drought. In contrast, other genotypes exhibited stunted growth and impaired flowering due to increased leaf abscission, poor mitosis, and germination issues [44]. This is consistent with previous studies on maize, where similar effects of drought stress on plant growth were reported [45]. Water stress impedes cell elongation, which limits biomass accumulation and overall growth (Fig. 4).

Key physiological traits, including relative water content (RWC), transpiration (E), and excised leaf water loss (ELWL), serve as critical indicators of drought tolerance. Drought stress reduces soil water retention, which lowers RWC and osmotic potential, resulting in reduced leaf turgor, stomatal closure, and diminished transpiration rates [46]. Tolerant genotypes such as G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) maintained higher RWC and lower ELWL, reflecting their ability to sustain cellular hydration and reduce water loss. The drought-tolerant genotypes maintained higher RWC and lower ELWL, reflecting their capacity to sustain cellular hydration and minimize water loss. These traits are essential for selecting drought-resilient cotton genotypes, with the ability to preserve RWC and reduce ELWL (Fig. 4). Histogram analysis for morphological (Fig. 4), physio-biochemical (Fig. 1), and enzymatic antioxidants (Fig. 2) traits across different water stress regimes further highlighted these genotypic differences through alterations in recombination frequency across studied cotton accessions under moderate and severe drought [47]. Similar patterns have been observed in wheat, where drought disrupts water relations and impairs essential physiological processes [48].

Osmoregulation emerged as a critical drought tolerance mechanism in the evaluated cotton genotypes, with drought-tolerant accessions including G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) exhibiting significantly higher accumulation of compatible osmolytes such as proline, total soluble sugars (TSS), and free amino acids, relative water contents (RWC) compared to susceptible cotton lines. This aligns with previous findings [49], demonstrating that osmolyte concentrations are enhanced under the prevalence of water deficit, preserving turgor pressure and sustaining stomatal conductance and relative water content under stress. Proline accumulation under drought stress assists in maintaining the protein structure, crucial enzyme functions, and cellular ion homeostasis [50]. In addition, the accumulation of endogenous proline arising from proteolysis and reduced protein synthesis acts as a crucial adaptive response in drought-tolerant cotton genotypes under severe water deficit. This accumulation contributes to improved water relations, enhanced dry biomass production, and maintenance of chlorophyll contents [4]. Additionally, severe water deficit conditions suppress the proline degradation pathway, leading to enhanced proline accumulation, particularly in the root tips of sensitive genotypes [51]. This accumulated proline acts as a reservoir that supports recovery and promotes growth following stress alleviation. Our findings are consistent with [52], which has highlighted the critical osmoprotective role of proline in drought-tolerant fennel genotypes, manifested through higher proline accumulation, improved leaf water potential, and greater relative water content (RWC) compared to sensitive lines. In addition, our results align with [53], confirming that genotypes exhibiting elevated levels of proline and total soluble sugars demonstrate superior water retention capacity under drought stress. Furthermore, in drought-tolerant cotton genotypes, elevated relative water content (RWC) and increased synthesis of soluble sugars demonstrate a positive relationship with enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, energy supply, flower retention, higher number of bolls per plant, and reduced excised leaf water loss. These observations are consistent with previous studies on fennel genotypes exposed to water-deficit conditions, where elevated levels of proline and soluble sugars were characteristic of drought-tolerant lines [54]. Moreover, osmotic adjustment plays a crucial role in boosting enzymatic antioxidants including SOD, POD, CAT, and APX stabilizes cellular structures and mitigate oxidative damage highlighting their dual role in drought adaptation [55]. The osmoregulatory response was complemented by enhanced antioxidant activity in tolerant genotypes. Despite this, higher concentrations of non-enzymatic antioxidants (total flavonoids, phenolics, and soluble proteins) also effectively scavenged ROS, preserving cellular integrity [56]. These mechanisms collectively supported physiological function, as evidenced by improved flower retention and stress recovery in tolerant genotypes. Similar observations in sugarcane [57] underscore the conserved role of osmolyte accumulation in maintaining productivity under drought stress.

Drought-tolerant genotypes also maintain adequate water potential and osmotic balance under stress, facilitating the synthesis of osmo-protectants. These genotypes displayed more efficient water management mechanisms, such as higher osmotic potential and reduced oxidative stress. Photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, and chlorophyll content all decline with increasing drought stress [58]. Water deficits primarily disrupt physiological processes due to reduced water availability in xylem vessels, thereby impairing photosynthesis and glucose production [21, 59]. Severe drought stress can cause photo-oxidation of pigments, damaging photosystem I and II, which leads to stunted growth [60]. Despite this, drought-tolerant genotypes including G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) continued to show stable photosynthesis, stomatal conductance, and transpiration rates, along with consistent chlorophyll content and efficient CO2 fixation, which enabled growth under stress.

The activity of key enzymatic antioxidants including superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), and catalase (CAT) increased significantly under drought stress, demonstrating their critical role in ROS scavenging. SOD catalyzed the dismutation of superoxide radicals (O₂⁻) into hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) [61]. which is subsequently detoxified by CAT (producing H₂O and O₂) and APX (using ascorbate to reduce H₂O₂ to H₂O) [62]. Drought-tolerant genotypes including, G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB), exhibited superior physiological resilience, maintaining higher photosynthetic rates, sustained stomatal conductance, reduced excised leaf water loss, and elevated relative water content. This resilience correlated with the activation of drought-responsive mechanisms, including enhanced antioxidant production (SOD, CAT, APX), accumulation of osmoprotectants (proline, total soluble sugars, total free amino acids), and preservation of photosynthetic efficiency [63, 64]. In contrast, susceptible genotypes accumulated elevated H₂O₂ and malondialdehyde (MDA), reflecting severe oxidative damage. The excess H₂O₂ likely initiated iron-mediated Fenton reactions, generating hydroxyl radicals (OH•) that aggravated lipid peroxidation, thereby increasing MDA levels and compromising membrane integrity [65].

Traditional drought tolerance indices such as the Stress Tolerance Index (STI) and Stress Susceptibility Index (SSI) have long served as useful tools in plant breeding. However, their reliance on yield-based or single-trait evaluations poses significant limitations, as they often fail to capture the multifaceted and coordinated nature of drought responses involving morphological, physiological, and biochemical traits [66]. These univariate approaches may overlook genotypes that possess favorable combinations of drought-adaptive traits mediated by diverse physiological mechanisms. In contrast, the Multi-Trait Genotype-Ideotype Distance Index (MGIDI) addresses these limitations through a robust multivariate framework. By employing factor analysis to account for trait intercorrelations and calculating the distance of each genotype from an ideotype representing optimal trait values, MGIDI enables a more holistic and unbiased genotype ranking [67]. In our study, MGIDI effectively identified drought-tolerant cotton genotypes including G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) that combined high osmoregulatory capacity (proline, soluble sugars, relative water content) with stable photosynthetic efficiency and membrane integrity. This underscores the value of MGIDI in supporting selection decisions in breeding programs targeting broad-spectrum drought resilience. While traditional indices still hold relevance for a specific trait, the multivariate structure of MGIDI offers a more comprehensive and statistically sound approach to genotype selection. Our findings are consistent with earlier reports that successfully applied MGIDI for identifying stress-tolerant accessions across diverse crops [67, 68]. Future integration with machine learning approaches may further enhance the precision and scalability of genotype selection under complex stress conditions [69].

The effective exploitation of MGIDI aids in ascertaining drought resilience based on multiple traits and simulations, comparing MGIDI’s accuracy with both novel and classical indexes, demonstrating its utility in selecting genotypes with desired traits [70]. The index, validated through Carlo simulations, revealed that genotypes G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) are consistently drought-resilient under varying stress conditions (Figs. 1 and 2). By integrating multiple traits into a single performance score, the MGIDI identified G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) as drought-tolerant, demonstrating a high degree of heritability for growth, physiological, and biochemical traits. The robust heritability and selection differential observed for the analyzed traits indicate that the favourable characteristics for selected cotton accessions revealed their genetic stability and consistency expressed under stress conditions. MGIDI proved valuable in identifying these genotypes for future breeding endeavours to establish drought-resistant cotton cultivars (Supplementary Table S1).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was conducted to evaluate the responses of cotton accessions to different drought stress levels (Fig. 7). Interestingly, under moderate and severe drought stress, genotypes G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) grouped, underscoring strong positive interactions with key traits such as shoot and root fresh weight (SFW, RFW), shoot and root dry weight (SDW, RDW), as well as antioxidant enzymes SOD, POD, and CAT, as indicated by the PCA biplot [71]. This positive interplay among genotypes, plant biomass, and antioxidant enzymes highlights the resilience character of these cotton accessions under moderate to severe water deficit situations.

Pearson’s correlation analysis underscores the complexity of trait interactions under different water deficit conditions. Under control conditions, strong positive correlations among traits such as chlorophyll a (Chl.a) with total chlorophyll (T.chl) and flower retention (FR) with number of bolls per plant (NBP) reflect the coordinated functioning of photosynthetic and stress response mechanisms. However, the negative correlations between peroxidase (POD) and total soluble proteins (TSP) and catalase activity (CAT) with TSP highlight the potential relationship between antioxidative enzymes and soluble sugar accumulation [72]. In moderate drought stress, several traits, including superoxide dismutase activity (SOD) and peroxidase activity (POD,) fostering strong positive correlations, suggesting enhanced relationship in antioxidative defense mechanisms under stress, whereas negative associations, such as between hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and peroxidase activity (POD), indicate stress-induced inhibition of certain enzymatic activities. Under severe water stress, the positive correlations between SOD and POD as well as CAT and APX reflect the critical role of antioxidant enzymes in mitigating oxidative damage. However, the negative correlations, especially between H₂O₂ and SOD, revealed the severe impact of extreme drought stress on metabolic activities inside the cell. These findings suggest that drought stress induces alterations in trait interactions, with varying degrees of positive and negative correlations, which could inform breeding strategies for drought resilience [73, 74].

Moderate and severe drought stress significantly reduces cotton lint yield and fiber quality, affecting attributes such as fiber length, strength, micronaire, and fineness [75]. The early stages of fiber elongation, particularly within the first 15 days after anthesis, are highly sensitive to water scarcity [15, 76]. Environmental changes disrupt cellulose formation, impairing fiber maturation and elongation, which ultimately leads to a decline in fiber quality [77]. Genotypes G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) have demonstrated superior drought tolerance and hold great potential as parent materials for future cotton breeding programs aimed at enhancing drought resilience. Cultivating these selected cotton genotypes in drought-prone regions could help sustain agricultural productivity by serving as a valuable genetic resource for developing drought-tolerant cultivars. These genotypes may eventually be approved as commercial varieties, providing farmers with a realistic approach to mitigate drought stress. Additionally, hybridization efforts could facilitate the introgression of drought-tolerant traits into other cotton varieties, facilitating the widespread transmission of these valuable traits and ensuring the widespread distribution of desired traits. The findings of current research highlight the importance of identifying and developing drought-tolerant genotypes, particularly at the flowering stage, to sustain cotton cultivation under altering climatic patterns.

A potential drawback of the current study is that the evaluations were conducted under glasshouse conditions, which may not fully capture the complexities of field environments. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of drought stress and its effects, it is crucial to integrate molecular-level investigations alongside morpho-physiological and biochemical assessments. Long-term research should focus on exploring the interaction between abiotic factors and genetic traits to provide deeper insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying drought tolerance. Moreover, future studies will validate the selected drought-resilient genotypes through multi-location and multi-season trials under field conditions to ensure their stability and adaptability across diverse environmental conditions. Although the use of pots allowed for precise maintenance of soil field capacity and consistent drought stress application under control conditions, but did not fully replicate field performance owing to restricted root growth, potentially influencing genotype-specific stress tolerance. For a more thorough understanding of drought resilience, cotton genotypes could be further characterized genetically through protein quantification, gene expression profiling, genome-wide association studies (GWAS), quantitative trait loci mapping (QTLs), metabolomic studies, and marker-assisted breeding at the molecular level. Furthermore, to confirm the drought tolerance of selected drought-tolerant genotypes, field trials across different environmental regimes are necessary to validate their practical effectiveness and contribution to developing more drought-resilient cotton varieties.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating morpho-physiological, biochemical, and statistical screening tools for identifying drought-tolerant cotton genotypes. Among the fifty genotypes evaluated, G1 (FH-189), G2 (FH-453), G3 (N-5016), G7 (N-868), G26 (N-1011), G44 (FH-941), and G47 (135 − 44/BB) emerged as the most resilient, validated through morpho-physiological traits (e.g., biomass production, relative water content) and biochemical markers (e.g., antioxidant enzyme activities, total phenolics, and proline content). These genotypes exhibited strong defense mechanisms against drought-induced oxidative stress, which effectively mitigated reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage. The multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) and biplot analysis provided robust multi-trait selection, further supported by high selection differentials and heritability values. These tools offered comprehensive insights into genotype performance under drought stress, enabling reliable identification of superior accessions. The findings underscore the importance of selected drought-tolerant cotton genotypes as valuable genetic resources for breeding drought-resilient cotton varieties. Their adoption can significantly enhance cotton productivity and stability under water-limited conditions, offering practical solutions for farmers in drought-prone regions. By combining physiological and biochemical trait evaluation with advanced screening methods, this study provides a strong platform for future breeding programs. These results contribute toward the long-term goal of securing cotton yield resilience and promoting sustainable cotton production globally in the face of increasing water deficits and climate variability.
