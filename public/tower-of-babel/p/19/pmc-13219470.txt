Advancing early detection of chromophobe renal cell carcinoma: a Bayesian optimization approach to machine learning models
Authors: Yupeng Chen, Qidong Zhou, Lal Hussain, Yongbao Wei, Jiawen Wang, Tian Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (ChRCC) prediction for a more accurate diagnosis is the aim of this research. This research focuses on enhancing ChRCC detection via image analysis, thereby overcoming the huge difficulty of obtaining and early and accurate diagnosis, given the terrible reality of kidney cancer, particularly ChRCC, and the critical role that early detection and diagnosis play in patient survival. Conventional imaging methods could not be definitive, which could result in a delayed or incorrect diagnosis. The efficacy of conventional machine learning and feature extraction techniques in precise diagnosis is limited by the hidden nonlinear dynamics that ChRCC imaging patterns frequently display. The detection and prognosis of ChRCC were improved by the computation of the most pertinent features, hybrid feature extraction, key feature selection, and optimization of machine learning algorithms through innovative Bayesian optimization. This allowed for effective early decision-making and improved decision support systems in general. This research is organized into multiple stages. Image enhancement methods and data augmentation were used to improve imaging pre-processing in the initial stage. In the second stage of feature engineering, the most pertinent features were found using the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM). In the third stage, Bayesian optimization was used to optimize the machine learning algorithms’ hyperparameters. Lastly, strong assessment indicators were used to assess the model’s performance. With 99.02% accuracy, 98.19% F1-Score, 98.04% Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), and 0.9989 Area Under the Curve (AUC), a large neural network yielded the best results. Similar results were obtained using a medium neural network, which had an accuracy of 98.55% and an AUC of 0.9976. These outstanding results demonstrate that the suggested approach has the potential to revolutionize the treatment of kidney chromophobe carcinoma by improving the condition’s detection, diagnosis, and comprehension, which would eventually enhance patient outcomes and the efficiency of the healthcare system.Keywords: Chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (ChRCC), Kidney cancer, Feature extraction, Neural network, Bayesian optimization, Gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM)Subject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Although less common than other RCC subtypes, chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (ChRCC), commonly referred to as chromophobe kidney cancer, poses a serious health risk. Early detection is essential for better outcomes, but it is often challenging to do using conventional clinical methods1. One or both kidneys may be affected by this malignancy, which arises from healthy kidney cells and forms unchecked growths, or tumors. Chromophobe, a subtype of ChRCC, and clear cell ChRCC are the most prevalent types seen in adults2–4. Compared to other, more common tumors, chromophobe receives less research attention despite being the sixth deadliest cancer for men and the eighth for women worldwide. An estimated 14,770 persons died from kidney cancer between 2007 and 2016 (9,820 males and 4,950 women), according to statistics. Chromophobe in particular needs more research, even though the medical profession is continuously investigating and implementing new cancer diagnosis and treatment strategies5–7. In an effort to remove all doubt about cancer, researchers are committed to creating cutting-edge techniques for early and precise diagnosis. Unchecked cell development is the hallmark of cancer, which is a serious threat to human life. We can improve our capacity to combat this dangerous illness by concentrating on chromophobe research.

The diagnosis, management, and prognosis of ChRCC, a very uncommon cancer, are extremely difficult. Surgical resection is the most common treatment; however, there are frequently no effective targeted medications for advanced or recurrent cancer, and the tumor’s unpredictable behavior makes the prognosis unclear. Early and accurate detection is more challenging because conventional imaging methods frequently result in delayed or inaccurate diagnoses. To address these problems, we present a novel machine learning (ML)-based approach. This approach finds complex patterns in imaging data using the Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) and other high-level feature extraction approaches. Additionally, Bayesian optimization is used to fine-tune the ML algorithms, improving diagnostic quality and enabling better patient care. Researchers are using ML-driven automated systems more frequently to enhance cancer prediction from medical imaging.

Surgeons must precisely extract pertinent information from such images in order to facilitate timely intervention and treatment planning, which in turn saves lives through early cancer diagnosis, including the elusive ChRCC8–11. Thoennes originally identified ChRCC as a distinct diagnostic issue in 1985 12. It is more frequent in men and makes up more than 5% of all renal cell carcinomas (12). It is typified by chromosomal abnormalities, such as deletions or missing DNA sections, and comes from particular kidney cell types. Higher frequencies are found in North America, Europe, Australia, Japan, China, Africa, and India, according to geographic distributions.

Chromosome abnormalities are increasingly linked to its development, despite the fact that its origin is unknown13. Risk factors include high blood pressure, smoking, exposure to cadmium and arsenic, and certain chemicals such as nitrosamines14. With over 80% of cases, clear cell carcinoma is the most prevalent type of kidney cancer. This type is produced from the kidney’s tubular component and may be identified under a microscope by its transparent, clear cytoplasm14. In the end, better patient outcomes and survival depend on early detection and informed treatment decisions.

Two crucial radiological techniques for identifying subgroups of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) are computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)15. CT provides vital information on tumor size, vascularity, and the extent of metastases and is the primary modality utilized for staging and surgical planning15. However, due to its superior soft-tissue resolution and efficient use of contrast material, MRI offers a number of benefits that improve the characterization of the RCC subtypes15. To classify the many histological subtypes of RCC, guide particular treatments, and improve patient prognosis by determining the unique clinical course and prognostic outcome of each subtype, more sophisticated imaging modalities like CT and MRI are needed.

By employing CNNs to extract spatial information from high-contrast images, the Kidney Ensemble-Net + LR model outperforms other methods in classification. Materials and methods

Dataset

The Cancer Genome Atlas Kidney Chromophobe (TCGA-KICH) dataset, which is available at https://wiki.cancerimagingarchive.net/display/Public/TCGA-KICH#1f25bee1662c4ba1a29b0c4499bf9059, was used in this study. The dataset, which was made available for research purposes, includes a mixed set of CT scan pictures associated with kidney cancer patients as well as pertinent microRNA (miRNA) information. Using random sample techniques, we selected 15 patients and 15 trials, yielding a total of 5530 individuals, and the same patients are split across training/test sets. This ultimate cohort contained 2649 abnormal and 2881 normal images. The detailed dataset demographic information is detailed in https://www.cancerimagingarchive.net/collection/tcga-kich/. To develop and test the models stringently, this entire dataset of 5530 images was carefully split. 70% of the data, or about 3871 photos, were delivered to the training set. The model found the innate patterns and correlation between the GLCM qualities and cancer labels with the help of this important variable. The validation set consisted of about 830 images, or 15% of the total data. In order to prevent overfitting during the learning phase, this data was crucial for adjusting the hyperparameters and model parameters using an objective performance criterion. The final 15% of the data, or about 829 photos, was retained as an unseen test batch. This set-aside test set was maintained over the entire model development and fine-tuning procedure in order to accurately assess the optimized model’s real generalization performance. Because it ensures that the claimed performance measurements genuinely reflect the model’s resilience and ability to generalize to truly unique input, this valid 70%-15%-15% partitioning technique is crucial for reproducibility.

Pre-processing

The following image pre-processing methods were utilized on ChRCC images.

Image resize

We used “inter-area” interpolation to produce accurate and seamless photo scaling. A computer vision technique called interpolation uses the surrounding area of a pixel to calculate its value47. By averaging the pixel value of the relevant area in the original image, the “inter-area” approach calculates the new pixel value. Because it considers a large number of pixel values and produces smoother and more accurate results than other interpolation techniques, it is particularly useful for reducing the size of photos through pixel reduction. Because it effectively removes a variety of noise types, including Gaussian, Poisson, speckle, and paper noise, this pre-processing method is essential for pre-conditioning images for analysis48. By removing these artifacts, image quality is significantly improved, which is crucial for effectively extracting useful features to assist additional analytical work49.

Data augmentation

We used two general strategies—data augmentation and stratified splitting—to mitigate any limitations of our data collection. Through the creation of synthetic replicas of existing samples, data augmentation let us expand our data set. As a result, the model became more resilient to real-world fluctuation and functioned interactively to prevent overfitting. In order to preserve an equitable representation of all classes, the procedure used methods like adding noise, carrying out several transformations, and creating synthetic data. In order to maintain the original data’s class distribution in both the training and test sets, stratified splitting was also used to accomplish this balance of representation during the data division procedure.

Feature extraction

Accurate categorization in machine learning depends critically on the creation of useful features. Texture, shape, geometry, and even intricate dynamic properties have all been extracted from data in the past using a variety of methods50–55. In this work, we particularly used the kidney chromophobe dataset to extract GLCM characteristics.

Gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM)

The computational GLCM56. This method reveals underlying patterns in the image by carefully analyzing the spatial interactions between nearby pixels with different grey levels56. Because of its adaptability, it has been widely used in many machine learning and image analysis domains. In theory, the GLCM divides an image into a grid and precisely determines the frequency with which nearby pixels display specific grey levels for each pixel. To successfully get comprehensive textural changes, this test is typically performed in four cardinal positions: 0°, 45°, 90°, and 135° 57–59. Energy, Inverse Difference Moment60, Entropy, Contrast61, and other homogeneity measures62. are among the GLCM descriptors computed from ChRCC pictures. Each of these characteristics captures a distinct facet of the texture of the image. These characteristics are very helpful in image classification and segmentation tasks because they serve as unique “fingerprints” that describe the texture63. The decision to use GLCM (Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix) features as the primary input modality is prompted by its established ability to capture textural heterogeneity, which is a characteristic of tumor microarchitecture in renal cell carcinoma. GLCM-based handcrafted descriptors quantify second-order statistics such as contrast, correlation, energy, and homogeneity, providing interpretable measures of local intensity relationships that can be directly related to tissue characteristics. Although GLCM descriptors are a subset of texture-based radiomics features, they are more compact and computationally efficient than radiomics features, which reduces the risk of overfitting when working with small datasets. Compared to deep feature embeddings obtained using CNNs or transformer-based architectures, GLCM features are easier to understand and require less data, and they usually exhibit robust performance across variations in imaging techniques, noise levels, and scanner kinds. Although CNN- or transformer-derived embeddings are capable of capturing extremely complex and hierarchical patterns, their features are typically less obvious, and they require large-scale datasets and careful regularization to generalize well. Because GLCM features strike a balance between interpretability, discriminative power, and robustness, they are an excellent choice for studies with intermediate sample sizes and diverse image sources.

Machine learning classification

This study leverages innovative ML classifiers to enhance the detection of ChRCC. The goal is to accurately categorize individuals based on their observed characteristics. The following classification techniques are employed.

Support vector machine (SVM)

SVMs are a robust supervised learning method applied to data classification. They proceed by identifying a hyperplane, roughly speaking, a dividing line or plane in more than two dimensions, which best separates the data points of the various classes from one another. This is done by maximizing the margin, that is, the distance between the hyperplane and the nearest data points of the respective classes. The margin is the minimum distance between the decision boundary (hyperplane) and each other’s closest data points of the two classes. The issue with SVMs is that there are several possible hyperplanes that are able to separate the data. One way to deal with this is to select the hyperplane minimizing classification error, i.e., the number of misclassified data points. Different algorithms may be used to solve this not-so-trivial problem67.

The use of GLCM features in the analysis of kidney imaging and tumor characterization is strategically warranted as detailed in recent studies18,68–72 because they have a special capacity to measure second-order texture information that is essential for differentiation of subtle yet important pathological changes. In contrast to more basic first-order statistics that simply characterize pixel intensity distributions, GLCM carefully inspects the spatial relations between pixels by determining how frequently given pairs of gray-level values occur at specified distances and angles. Although there are other texture-capturing feature types such as Local Binary Patterns (LBPs) or wavelet features, GLCM provides a robust, interpretable paradigm with which researchers can identify numerical features with obvious textural attributes (e.g., increased GLCM entropy suggesting increased disorder). Additionally, GLCM definitions are standardized, helping assure reproducibility of research. Although deep learning models are capable of learning complicated features end-to-end, handcrafted features of GLCM provide a complementary and interpretable source of information, particularly useful in cases where data are limited or when feature interpretability is essential for clinical translation. Therefore, GLCM’s ability to describe the complex spatial distribution of pixels makes it an invaluable tool for strong and informative kidney tumor characterization.

K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN)

The KNN is a non-parametric supervised learning algorithm commonly used for classification tasks in ML 73–75. Its goal is to use the neighbors of data points to classify them into different classes. KNN predicts class labels for fresh, unseen data by calculating the distance and similarity between data points, whether it is used for regression or classification. It is a popular option in machine learning because of its ease of use and quick computation time. For example, KNN is quite good at matching and recognizing patterns in images76,77. In this case, “K” stands for the average of the training data points that are closest to one another. KNN frequently uses Heuristic and Euclidean Distance methods to calculate the distances between continuous variables.

For analyzing gene expression data, Correlation Coefficients are commonly used. Researchers actively seek to enhance KNN’s classification performance. Various algorithms have been proposed in the literature, including Large Margin, Nearest Neighbor, and Neighborhood Components Analysis. Additionally, Metric Learning Algorithms, a supervised technique specifically designed for KNN, have shown significant improvements in classification performance. The Hamming Distance Technique (HBT) is commonly used for analyzing text or discrete variables.

Neural networks (NN)

Neural networks differ in architectural variations, primarily the number of layers and the number of neurons. Slim networks with few layers and neurons are best suited for less complex tasks. Medium networks present a good balance between depth and width for achieving acceptable performance at moderate computational cost. Large networks, with lots of neurons in every layer, can learn very complicated patterns but are thereby more likely to overfit. Two-hidden-layered networks, and especially those with two hidden layers, provide intermediate complexity and can be applied to a broad range of tasks. Lastly, the selection of the optimum architecture also relies on factors like complexity in the data, available computational resources, and the specific needs of the task to be executed.

The continuous parameters’ search space is specified using ranges (i.e., (0.0001, 0.1) for learning rate), whereas categorical parameters are specified using lists of discrete values. For continuous variables, we often use uniform distributions over their given intervals as prior distributions during the initial sampling step, prior to the surrogate model (e.g., Gaussian Process) sharpening its knowledge of the objective function landscape. The Gaussian Process, as our surrogate model, naturally models the objective function as a random process under control of a mean function and a covariance function (kernel), with the capability to probabilistically forecast unseen hyperparameter settings. The Expected Improvement (EI) or Upper Confidence Bound (UCB) acquisition functions therefore direct the search by trading off exploration of uncertain areas against exploitation of promising ones, rendering the hyperparameter tuning process much more efficient than grid or random search.

The Bayesian optimization process was carefully crafted to effectively and efficiently optimize the hyperparameters of our machine learning models. As the foundation of the optimization, we used Expected Improvement (EI) as the acquisition function. EI was selected because it has been shown to be effective in trading off the exploitation of regions of the hyperparameter space that were not previously visited (in the hope of discovering better optima) with the exploitation of regions that are forecasted to provide high performance (to improve on good solutions that have already been found). The optimization was configured for 20 iterations total, of which the first 5 were random evaluations for seeding the surrogate model. This number of iterations was found experimentally to offer a reasonable balance between computational expense and the probability of converging towards an optimal set of hyperparameters within the given search spaces. A fixed number of iterations acted as the main stopping criterion, with implicit convergence being seen as the improvement in the objective function in later iterations began to gradually level off. Essentially, cross-validation was tightly woven into the hyperparameter tuning workflow to guarantee solid and unbiased performance estimation of every candidate set of hyperparameters. In particular, a five-fold stratified CV was used. The model was trained and assessed five times on alternate folds of the training set for each set of hyperparameters recommended by the Bayesian optimizer’s acquisition function. The Bayesian optimization method was applied to the performance over these five folds (e.g., mean F1-score or mean accuracy) as the objective function value. By reducing the significant risk of overfitting to a single train-validate split during hyperparameter tuning and encouraging the selection of truly robust hyperparameter settings, this cross-validation technique provides a more accurate evaluation of the model’s generalization performance on new data.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve

The ROC curve, sometimes called the relative operating characteristic curve, is a useful visual aid for assessing the effectiveness of binary classification models with various decision thresholds78,79. It shows the trade-off between sensitivity (true positive rate, TPR) and specificity (true negative rate, FPR). The ROC curve is a helpful tool for evaluating the performance of machine learning classification algorithms. Plotting the model’s capacity to distinguish between the positive and negative classes in terms of true positives and false positives is made simple as a result. By looking at the ROC curve, practitioners can ascertain the optimal threshold for a particular classification problem.

K-fold cross validation

A simple method for extensively assessing machine learning model performance is cross-validation. To do this, the available data must be split into two separate sets, one for model testing and the other for training. Five-fold cross-validation is a common implementation that divides the data set into five pieces of equal size. Here, the one held-out fold is used to train and test four of them in turn80. Each of the five folds is guaranteed to be utilized as the only test set once over the five repetitions. Finally, the overall model performance is determined as an average of the outcomes from the five test runs. This method yields a stronger and more stable measure of model performance but provides a complete evaluation without being computationally expensive. Cross-validation is not only a means of assessment but also an important factor against overfitting. Overfitting happens when a model overfits the training data and results in a model with a bad generalization performance on new, unseen data81.

By means of systematic training and testing of the model across different subsets of the data, cross-validation prevents this issue82. These repeated trainings and testings over sets of different data yield a better estimate of the actual predictive ability of a model. Cross-validation is also very useful for selecting the optimal complexity of a model because it permits training and testing models with different configurations over different partitions of data83.

Cross-validation, as reflected in Fig. 2, is a basic method of fighting overfitting and yielding stable model estimation, particularly important when faced with small or imbalanced datasets. It operates by iteratively dividing the data into training and validation folds so that every data point becomes part of both the training and evaluation set at some iteration. Compared to a single train-test split, this approach offers a more accurate and less biased approximation of a model’s real generalization performance, helping to ascertain whether a model is only memorizing the training set. Stratified k-fold cross-validation is essential when working with imbalanced classes since it preserves the original class ratios across folds to prevent the model from overemphasizing the majority class. If training instances are extremely limited, Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) can achieve the most training data per iteration. Furthermore, inside the training folds of a cross-validation loop, resampling techniques (such as SMOTE for oversampling or undersampling) were deliberately used to balance class distributions so that the model could appropriately learn from underrepresented classes without data leaking. Finally, by performing hyperparameter adjustment in an inner cross-validation loop, nested cross-validation adds an extra layer of robustness, making the assessment of model performance on truly unseen data even more objective. Together, these specialized cross-validation methods verify that a model’s strong performance accurately reflects its ability to generalize, even in challenging data situations.

K-fold cross validation method for training/ testing data formulation.

Nested Cross-Validation (NCV), the gold standard for obtaining an unbiased evaluation of a model’s generalization performance while hyperparameter tuning is attempted, was key to the multifaceted method used to prevent overfitting during hyperparameter tuning. Supporting NCV, intrinsic regularization methods were implemented and tuned within the neural network architectures themselves; these included dropout, which probabilistically shuts down neurons during training to avoid co-adaptation, and L2 regularization (weight decay), which penalizes big network weights in order to encourage simpler models. Additionally, early stopping was applied in training iterative models such as neural networks, stopping training when performance on a held-out validation set no longer improved, to avoid the model learning noise from the training data. This thorough collection of techniques as a whole guarantees that the high performance levels are not just due to overfitting to the tuning or validation sets but instead show the models’ actual capacity to generalize to new, unseen clinical data.

The performance of our machine learning models, such as the different neural network, KNN, and SVM settings, was assessed purely through 5-fold cross-validation. This standard internal evaluation method consisted of dividing the resultant dataset into five equivalent-sized subsets. For each of the five runs, four successive subsets were utilized for training models, while the remaining one-fifth was used as a validation set for measuring performance. The last reported performance values (sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, accuracy, F1-score, MCC, and p-value) are the mean of the results calculated for these five different training and validation splits. Although this method greatly improves the perceived generalizability of the models within the limits of our current dataset by making all data points contribute to both training and validation, there is a point to be made that the evaluation was not carried out on a completely unseen or external dataset. Hence, although the 5-fold cross-validation strongly supports the consistency and performance of the models across various divisions of our sampled material, their actual generalizability to completely novel, independently acquired patient cohorts or those from other institutions would need to be verified on such external data.

Discussions

Youssef et al. 89 illustrated a sensitivity of 96% and accuracy of 90% employing quantitative reverse transcription and microarray analysis, which are alternative methodological techniques emphasizing gene expression instead of image features.

In addition to accuracy, the suggested approach also demonstrates an AUC of 0.9989 and a high F1-score of 98.19%, signifying outstanding overall performance in both precision and recall and strong discriminative power between classes. Such further metrics confirm the outstanding performance of the suggested approach in ChRCC detection, implying a great improvement in diagnostic power.

The results are computed using traditional ML and optimized neural network algorithms. The shown results convincingly prove the higher performance of Neural Network (NN) models compared to conventional machine learning (ML) algorithms like KNN and SVM in the chromophobe cancer detection problem using hand-engineered GLCM features. A side-by-side comparison of the best-performing models in each class makes this difference clear: the Wide NN always produces the highest results, including a very high accuracy of 0.9902, an F1-score of 0.9819, an MCC of 0.9804, and an impressive AUC of 0.9989. On the other hand, the top-performing KNN model, Fine KNN, had an accuracy of 0.9866, an F1-score of 0.9744, an MCC of 0.9732, and its best AUC (Medium KNN) was 0.9976. Likewise, the top SVM model, Cubic SVM, had an accuracy of 0.9814, an F1-score of 0.9648, an MCC of 0.9627, and its best AUC (Cubic/Fine Gaussian SVM) was 0.9976.

The neural network’s superior capacity to distinguish between chromophobe cancer and normal is demonstrated by its consistent outperformance on all important parameters. Neural networks’ deep, multi-layer architecture allows them to learn intricate, non-linear hierarchical models and complex feature interactions from the input GLCM features, which typically capture subtle textural patterns in medical images. These design strengths are the source of this performance advantage. Unlike KNN, which relies solely on local similarity, or SVM, which looks for the optimal hyperplane, NNs can learn more discriminative and abstract patterns that aren’t always defined in the initial feature set. The F1-score and MCC show that these NN models were successfully optimized, most likely using Bayesian optimization as mentioned, which further enabled them to make better use of the high-dimensional information in the GLCM features with increased sensitivity, specificity, and precision-recall balance. Consequently, the empirical result clearly confirms that neural networks are a more effective approach for this difficult medical image classification problem due to their greater capacity and adaptive learning nature.

The performance of the suggested model is compared to previous research in the area of lung infection categorization in Table 5. Compared to the majority of the current approaches, the suggested model, which makes use of GLCM features and a wide neural network optimized with Bayesian optimization, achieves a noticeably greater accuracy (99.02%). The model’s excellent performance in terms of precision and recall is further supported by the high F1-score (98.19%) and AUC (0.9989). For this issue, previous research used conventional feature-extraction techniques. To capture the various ChRCC imaging features, we suggested a GLCM feature-extracting method. Additionally, we used Bayesian optimization to optimize the parameters of machine learning algorithms. The suggested model’s high accuracy and resilience point to its potential for useful applications in clinical settings. In locations with limited resources, the approach may help with early identification and appropriate treatment of ChRCC.

A few crucial factors regarding the type of medical imaging data and the task at hand—the distinction of tumor subtypes—led to the decision to use a WNN optimized using Bayesian optimization rather than the more common machine learning classifiers like SVM and RF. Neural networks, particularly wide ones, provide special advantages for complex pattern recognition tasks in medical imaging, even though SVMs and RFs are powerful and widely used algorithms.

First of all, neural networks excel at learning hierarchical characteristics and complex, non-linear correlations from raw or minimally processed picture data, which is essential for recognizing subtle morphological and textural variations among RCC subtypes. Because they provide a comprehensive, quantitative description of the image, the GLCM texture features—which encode statistical texture information—make excellent neural network inputs. While RFs can approximate non-linear functions and SVMs can also handle non-linear data using kernel tricks, neural networks—especially those with multiple layers, even if they are “wide” rather than “deep”—are better at extracting and fusing these features in a highly abstract and discriminative manner. They perform better in applications like tumor classification, where subtle visual signals are crucial, because of their innate ability to acquire complex patterns and representations without direct feature creation, aside from the initial GLCM extraction.

Second, neural networks are highly adaptable and may adjust their parameters to achieve optimal performance for a given task, particularly when combined with optimization methods like Bayesian optimization. Compared to manual optimization or grid search, Bayesian optimization produces an even better and more effective model configuration by thoroughly scanning the hyperparameter space. The network’s ability to learn the most discriminative features for ChRCC from the GLCM inputs can be significantly increased by this optimization process, leading to increased accuracy and generalization. The increased degree of complexity and unpredictability in neural network architecture typically benefits more from advanced optimization techniques, even though SVMs and RFs also require hyperparameter tuning.

Thirdly, our proposed GLCM Wide Neural Network achieved an excellent 99.02% accuracy, 98.19% F1-score, and an AUC of 0.9989 by direct comparative analysis, as shown by Table 5 data. This performs significantly better than other published methods, such as various deep learning and machine learning techniques. For instance, the second-best accuracy for identifying ChRCC was 95% (Tander et al. using a neural network) and 95.2% (Ali et al. using neighborhood component analysis). While other studies using SVM or RF for medical image classification have shown promising results in other contexts, their accuracy for comparable tasks is typically lower than that of highly sophisticated neural network models, especially when classifying complex textural content. Neural networks, for example, tend to perform better than RFs and SVMs in high-performance and highly flexible tasks, particularly in complex domains like image recognition, even though they may require more data and processing capacity.

The classification of ChRCCs has significantly improved with the use of this suggested paradigm. Its exceptional performance and practicality highlight the benefits of combining state-of-the-art machine learning with conventional image processing. Future research should concentrate on refining the model, fixing its flaws, and examining its use in real-world clinical contexts. To evaluate the model’s generalizability in situations with little data, more experiments with progressively smaller training subsets (e.g., 25% of the original dataset) should be carried out. This study will assess the degree of performance degradation, highlight possible overfitting risks, and show sensitivity to restricted data availability.

Because it methodically looks for hyperparameters that steer clear of overly complicated models and encourage improved generalization, Bayesian optimization is crucial in these specific situations. Similar regularization techniques that prevent overfitting include dropout layers, which randomly deactivate neurons during training, and early stopping mechanisms, which halt training whenever validation performance reaches a plateau. Together, these strategies increase the model’s stability and utility in real-world clinical settings by ensuring that the feature extraction and classification pipeline is dependable even in scenarios where training data is scarce.

Before putting the suggested Bayesian-optimized feature extraction and classification pipeline into practice in clinical settings, it is crucial to recognize certain significant limitations despite the pipeline’s huge translational promise for practical diagnostic tools. First, the small sample size inherently reduces statistical power and increases the risk of overfitting, particularly in high-dimensional texture-based analysis. Second, generalizability to broader clinical contexts where collecting parameters vary significantly may be hampered by dataset homogeneity, which results from images obtained on a limited number of scanners, protocols, and patient groups. Third, without external validation, it is challenging to evaluate the model’s stability and effectiveness over different multi-institutional cohorts.

Future research should use multi-center datasets with a range of acquisition characteristics, conduct external and temporal validation, and conduct head-to-head comparison studies with qualified radiologists to determine whether the model increases diagnostic confidence or efficiency in order to get around these limitations. To evaluate the dependability, repeatability, and clinical value of the suggested pipeline within standardized radiology procedures, prospective clinical testing and the incorporation of real-world imaging variances will be required. The significant adaptability and enhanced therapeutic relevance of the suggested Bayesian-optimized feature extraction and classification method can be advantageous for many relevant biomedical imaging applications. The framework can be applied to other renal tumor subtypes, such as clear cell RCC (ccRCC) and papillary RCC (pRCC), which also show heterogeneous spatial patterns, microstructural variations, and morphologic irregularities on imaging, because it combines automated hyperparameter tuning with reliable texture-based descriptors and machine-learning classifiers95. GLCM or extended radiomics features can be used to capture the unique textural fingerprints of some subtypes, such as ChRCC. The best feature combinations and classifier setups for each subtype can be methodically found via Bayesian optimization.

In addition to kidney tumors, the pipeline can be used for more general histopathological and imaging-based cancer classification tasks, such as lung, gastrointestinal, brain, and breast diseases96–98. Bayesian optimization provides scalable customization for a range of datasets, imaging modalities (e.g., CT, MRI, and histopathology slides), and classification objectives (e.g., disease staging, grading, or subtyping). The pipeline’s modular design allows for the addition of increasingly complex feature encoders, such as transformer-based representations, deep CNN embeddings, or wavelet radiomics, while maintaining the efficacy of Bayesian search for model selection.

Conclusions

A thorough approach to the difficulties of renal chromophobe carcinoma image analysis is provided by this study. We want to greatly increase the accuracy and efficiency of ChRCC recognition by combining picture enhancement techniques, advanced feature engineering approaches, efficient data augmentation procedures, and reliable model evaluation processes. In particular, GLCM features are extracted to capture complex spatial pixel correlations, and Bayesian optimization is used to fine-tune machine learning models to further improve detection performance. On key criteria including accuracy, F1-score, MCC, and AUC, the final models—most notably the Wide Neural Network—perform better. This significant development in kidney cancer detection has the potential to totally change the industry by facilitating earlier diagnosis, more accurate treatment planning, and ultimately improved patient outcomes.

Limitations and future directions

The primary goal of this study was to demonstrate the technical performance of our proposed AI framework, an enhanced Wide Neural Network (WNN) trained on GLCM features using Bayesian Optimization, in accurately diagnosing Chromophobe Renal Cell Carcinoma (ChRCC). In our initial attempt, we deliberately focused on methodological improvement rather than directly comparing our model with radiologists. These comparisons were excluded due to the study’s narrow focus, the substantial effort required to gather a sufficiently large and diverse expert-labeled dataset, and the methodological difficulties of developing a fair reader study that accounts for variations in expertise, workflow, and interpretation. Future studies will overcome these limitations. Future study will use prospective or retrospective reader studies to directly compare the AI model to experienced radiologists, evaluate its impact on diagnostic efficiency and consistency, and explore the benefits of human-AI collaboration. We also wish to conduct extensive external validation to further show the model’s clinical generalizability. With a 99.02% accuracy for ChRCC identification, our solid preliminary results offer a good basis for future clinical validation, despite the constraint of not having a radiologist comparison. Although this study extracts a variety of GLCM features to represent complex texture patterns of kidney malignancies, we have not yet employed explicit dimensionality reduction approaches such as PCA or LASSO. In order to make sure that only stable attributes were employed for model construction, we subsequently concentrated on meticulous feature extraction and redundancy filtering using correlation analysis and repeatability tests. We recognize that in order to enhance interpretability and generalizability, feature-space optimization is required. Future studies will employ methods such as Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), which continuously eliminates non-informative variables, and LASSO, which does embedded feature selection. These techniques will lessen overfitting, increase model resilience across a variety of datasets, and help identify the most economical and discriminative feature subsets.

It should be mentioned that this study has certain inherent limitations despite its outstanding performance. The most significant is that because of the incredibly small dataset size, the model is more susceptible to selection bias. A limited dataset increases the possibility of learning patterns unique to the cohort or imaging method rather than genuinely generalizable characteristics, even while 5-fold cross-validation reduces overfitting. Furthermore, when applied to images from various clinical scenarios, the lack of comprehensive documentation on imaging acquisition parameters—such as scanner type, slice thickness, contrast regimens, and reconstruction algorithms—introduces potential unpredictability that could lower model dependability. To achieve multicenter robustness, protocol-specific biases must be reduced and imaging heterogeneity must be resolved.

To build on the promising performance of the proposed model, a number of research options are proposed. First, validation on much bigger and multi-center datasets is necessary to confirm generalizability across a range of patient demographics and imaging scenarios. Second, multimodal imaging data (such as CT with DWI, perfusion MRI, or PET) may enhance lesion characterization by integrating texture, cellularity, vascularity, and metabolic markers. Third, adding relevant clinical information, such as genetic markers, test findings, or demographics, may improve risk stratification and diagnosis accuracy even further. Automated tumor-segmentation pipelines using complex designs such as transformer-based models or U-Net variants would enable a fully automated diagnosis process.

The current work used statistical methods such as the Kruskal-Wallis test to identify discriminative features in terms of interpretability. The complex interactions seen in neural network models cannot be fully captured by these methods, although they are useful for preliminary filtering. Future studies will use model-agnostic interpretability tools like SHAP and LIME to quantify both local (per-case) and global (overall) feature contributions. These tools will improve transparency, foster clinician trust, and enhance understanding of the anticipated imaging findings of ChRCC. Although the model worked well on the TCGA-KICH dataset, external validation is still required because TCGA-KICH is a single-sourced cohort with particular imaging standards and demographics. Thus, future research should evaluate the model using independent datasets such as KiTS19 or other multi-center RCC cohorts. Domain adaptation techniques like transfer learning and adversarial feature alignment can also reduce distribution shifts between datasets. Prospective evaluation in real clinical procedures and quantitative assessment of model uncertainty would be essential to ensure safe and reliable adoption.

Lastly, by combining radiomics signatures with GLCM features and evaluating performance against contemporary deep learning architectures like ResNet, DenseNet, Swin Transformer, and Vision Transformer (ViT), future research will broaden the methodological scope. When there is a class imbalance, ROC and precision-recall curves are helpful tools for comprehending sensitivity-specificity trade-offs. Combining deep feature embeddings with manually created texture descriptors may lead to enhanced biological interpretability, better diagnostic performance, and complementary advantages.
