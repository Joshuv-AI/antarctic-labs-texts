Stage-specific regional distribution of amyloid and tau deposition across the Alzheimer’s disease continuum revealed by tau-to-amyloid ratio imaging
Authors: Kazunari Ishii, Takahiro Yamada, Kohei Hanaoka, Yasuyuki Kojita, Atsushi Kono, Hayato Kaida, Takashi Nihashi, Keita Sakurai, Takashi Kato, Akinori Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAmyloid-β and tau deposition follow distinct spatial and temporal trajectories across the Alzheimer’s disease (AD) continuum. Amyloid accumulation occurs early in the disease course, whereas tau pathology is more closely associated with neurodegeneration and clinical progression. Characterizing stage-specific regional divergence between amyloid and tau deposition may refine biomarker-based disease staging and improve prognostic assessment.MethodsWe analyzed participants from the BATON study who underwent amyloid PET with 18F-flutemetamol, tau PET with 18F-MK-6240, and 3D MRI within a three-month interval. Participants were classified into cognitively normal amyloid-negative controls (CNA, n = 101) and the AD continuum (n = 102), comprising preclinical AD (PCA, n = 47), mild cognitive impairment due to AD (MCA, n = 24), and AD dementia (ADD, n = 31). Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated using the Centiloid and CenTauR frameworks, and voxel-wise tau-to-amyloid ratio (TAR) images were generated. Voxel-wise and region-of-interest analyses were performed to compare regional patterns of amyloid deposition, tau accumulation, and TAR across disease stages.ResultsAmyloid deposition was already widespread at the PCA stage, involving the frontal, posterior cingulate/precuneus, and temporal cortices. In contrast, tau deposition in PCA was largely confined to the medial temporal lobe. With progression to MCA and ADD, tau burden increased substantially and extended to the lateral temporal, parietal, and frontal cortices. TAR analysis demonstrated high values in the medial temporal cortex at the PCA stage, followed by decreasing TAR in frontal regions during MCA and a tau-dominant neocortical pattern in ADD.ConclusionsAmyloid and tau exhibit distinct and stage-dependent regional dissociation across the AD continuum. TAR imaging effectively captures this divergence, reflecting early medial temporal tau predominance and subsequent neocortical tau expansion. These findings support the utility of integrated amyloid–tau PET metrics for refined disease staging and longitudinal therapeutic monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00259-026-07935-x.Keywords: Alzheimer’s disease, amyloid, tau, PET

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the abnormal deposition of amyloid-β (A), tau deposition (T), and subsequent neural injury (N). The framework of the AT(N) classification, proposed by the National Institute on Aging and Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA), has provided a biological definition of AD, categorizing individuals based on biomarker evidence for amyloid deposition, tau pathology, and neurodegeneration [1]. More recently, this system has been extended to ATNIVS, which incorporates additional markers of inflammation (I), vascular pathology (V), and α-synuclein (S), reflecting the multifactorial nature of AD and related dementias [2].

One of the critical insights from PET imaging studies is that the temporal and spatial progression of amyloid and tau deposition is not uniform across the brain. Amyloid plaques tend to first accumulate in the neocortex, particularly in the precuneus and posterior cingulate regions, years before clinical symptoms appear [3]. In contrast, tau deposition, as detected by second-generation tau PET tracers such as 18F-MK-6240 and 18F-PI-2620, begins in the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus, spreading to associative neocortices as the disease advances, closely paralleling cognitive decline [4, 5].

Importantly, recent longitudinal PET studies have demonstrated that the regional dissociation between amyloid and tau deposition influences the rate of neurodegeneration and clinical progression [6]. For instance, while amyloid positivity is necessary for the initiation of tau spread, the burden and topography of tau deposition are more strongly correlated with clinical symptoms and disease severity [5]. These findings underscore the importance of characterizing stage-specific regional patterns of amyloid and tau pathology, which may refine disease staging and improve prognostic accuracy. Previously, we have proposed glucose metabolism images normalized to amyloid or tau deposition and have reported how the accumulation of abnormal proteins affects regional reductions in glucose metabolism [7, 8]. The aim of this study was to elucidate the spatial ratio and stage-dependent divergence of amyloid and tau deposition in AD continuum using amyloid PET, tau PET, and tau-to-amyloid ratio (TAR) images.

Methods

Subjects

For the purpose of this study, we selected the subjects who were collected for the BATON study (https://jrct.mhlw.go.jp/en-latest-detail/jRCTs031210288) at National Center for Geriatrics and Gerontology. At the time of enrollment, the subjects gave written informed consent for data collection and completed questionnaires approved by each participating site’s ethical committee. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the “Ethical Guidelines for Medical and Health Research Involving Human Subjects” issued by the Ministry of Health, Labor, and Welfare in Japan. All participants underwent a battery of neuropsychological tests, including the Mini-Mental State Examination, Alzheimer Disease Assessment Scale-Japanese version and Logical Memory I and II scores from the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised. Based on clinical assessments by outpatient physicians, participants were clinically categorized into AD, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), non-AD dementia (nonAD), and cognitively normal (CN). Participants with any significant medical, neurologic, or psychiatric diseases such as cerebrovascular diseases, brain tumors, and mood disorders other than AD and MCI based on the medical records, laboratory tests, neuropsychological tests, and MRI were excluded from the selection. The 238 subjects were selected from those who underwent 3D-MRI, amyloid PET and tau PET. Amyloid and tau PET scans, 3D-MRI scans and psychological tests were performed in three months. From the 238 subjects we selected 101 CN subjects who were negative for amyloid deposition on amyloid PET (CNA) as controls, together with 102 AD continuum subjects including 31 AD dementia subjects (ADD), 24 mild cognitive impairment (MCI) due to AD subjects (MCA) and 47 CN subjects considered preclinical AD (PCA). All the AD continuum subjects were positive on amyloid PET. Flutemetamol amyloid PET images of each subject were visually assessed separately by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians who were blinded to clinical data [9]. Amyloid positivity was evaluated in the bilateral frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes, posterior cingulate, and striata. Each amyloid PET image was classified as amyloid positive or negative, with amyloid positivity defined as equal or greater accumulation in the gray matter (GM) compared to the white matter (WM). Images were independently interpreted by three expert readers, and amyloid deposits positivity or negativity was determined according to their evaluations [9]. In cases of disagreement among the readers, the final classification was based on a majority vote. Inter-rater agreement was assessed using Fleiss’ kappa to evaluate overall agreement among multiple raters, and Conger’s kappa was additionally calculated as a generalized form of Cohen’s kappa applicable to multiple raters. The overall inter-rater agreement was excellent, with both Fleiss’ kappa and Conger’s kappa yielding identical values of 0.921 (95% CI: 0.88–0.96), indicating almost perfect agreement according to the Landis and Koch classification. The demographic data of each subject group are shown in Table 1. There was a significant difference in age only in the ADD group (p < 0.05). The MMSE scores of ADD group was significantly lower than those of other groups and those of MCA group was significantly lower than those of PCA and CNA groups (p < 0.05). The ADAS scores of ADD group was significantly higher than those of other groups and those of MCA group was significantly higher than those of PCA and CNN group (p < 0.05).

Imaging protocol

All enrolled participants underwent 18F-flutemetamol-PET for amyloid imaging, 18F-MK6240-PET for tau imaging and MRI scans at their respective facilities, in accordance with the study protocol. PET imaging was conducted using PET-CT cameras (Biograph 16 True Point and Biograph 64 Vision 600, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany, GE Discovery IQ.x; GE Healthcare, USA) [10].

For flutemetamol-amyloid PET and MK6240-tau PET, a 20-min scan (5 min x 4 frames) was performed 90 min after injection of 185 MBq 18F-flutemetamol or 18F-MK6240.

Data analysis

Anatomical normalization of the 3D-MRI T1WI, amyloid PET, and tau PET images was performed using Statistical Parametric Mapping version 12 (SPM 12; Wellcome Department of Cognitive Neurology, London, United Kingdom). For amyloid PET and tau PET, all individual images were co-registered to the individual 3D-MR image and then transformed into standard stereotactic anatomical space using the DARTEL technique [11]. Amyloid and tau images were spatially normalized to a standard brain using anatomical standardization technique based on each subject’s 3D T1-weighted MRI. Details of the normalization procedure have been described elsewhere [8]. The standardized amyloid PET images were normalized by the counts from the Centiloid cerebellar VOI [12], while the tau PET images were normalized by the counts from the CenTauR cerebellar VOI [13]. To generate an image representing the ratio of tau to amyloid deposition, the standardized tau image was divided by the amyloid PET image, yielding the tau-to-amyloid ratio (TAR) image, which was further normalized using counts from the CenTauR cerebellar VOI.

For cortical SUVR measurements, the CenTauR VOI was segmented into regions: medial temporal, lateral temporal, parietal and posterior/precuneus cortical regions. Regional SUVRs were calculated as the ratio of each regional VOI count to the cerebellar VOI count. Notably, the CenTauR VOI covers a broad cerebellar region, while in tau PET images, marked meningeal uptake was observed, which could potentially affect cerebellar counts through spill-in [14]. Therefore, an additional VOI restricted to the central cerebellum, unaffected by spill-in from meningeal uptake, was defined, and cortical CenTauR SUVRs were recalculated using this restricted VOI for normalization.

For the voxel-wise comparisons, all amyloid PET, tau PET and TAR images were smoothed using an isotropic 8-mm Gaussian kernel to increase the signal-to-noise ratio and compensate for differences in gyral anatomy between individuals. Then, voxel-wise comparisons among ADD, MCA, PCA and CNA subjects were performed using ANOVA analysis by SPM12. The threshold for significance was set to p < 0.05 corrected for family-wise error (FWE) due to multiple comparisons, and the voxel extent threshold was set to 200.

Regional SUVR by VOI analysis

Regional Standardized Uptake Value Ratios (SUVRs) were calculated using predefined cortical volumes of interest (VOIs). VOIs for the frontal, lateral temporal, lateral parietal cortices, and posterior cingulate / precuneus (PCC) were derived from the Centiloid VOIs, whereas the medial temporal lobe VOI was defined according to the CentTauR framework. Two reference regions were applied: the Centiloid whole cerebellum and the restricted cerebellum.

Regional analyses were performed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with the ADD, MCA, PCA and CNA groups as the main factor and age, sex, and PET scanner as covariates. These covariate-adjusted models were used as the primary analyses. For each tracer/reference combination, post hoc comparisons were performed for ADD, MCA, and PCA versus the CNA group. To account for multiple testing across regions, the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure was applied to the 18 post hoc comparisons within each tracer/reference combination (6 regions × 3 contrasts). Group-wise regional values are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For each comparison versus CNA, adjusted mean differences with 95% confidence intervals and standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. Overall ANCOVA results are reported as F statistics, p values, and partial η².

Correlation and agreement analysis between reference methods

To quantitatively assess the impact of different cerebellar reference regions, correlation and agreement analyses were performed between measurements derived using whole cerebellum normalization (CL) and restricted central cerebellar normalization (reCb). Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated for each region of interest (ROI) as well as for pooled data across all ROIs. 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were estimated using Fisher’s z-transformation. Agreement between the two methods was further evaluated using Bland–Altman analysis, including estimation of the mean difference (bias) and the 95% limits of agreement (LoA; mean difference ± 1.96 × standard deviation). These analyses were performed for both tracer-specific and pooled datasets.

Cognitive correlation analysis

In addition, to evaluate the associations between amyloid, tau, and TAR images and cognitive function, voxel-wise correlation analyses were performed using SPM12. Correlations with MMSE and ADAS scores were assessed while adjusting for age, sex, and scanner type as covariates.

Agreement Between CL and reCb

Amyloid SUVR-CL and Amyloid SUVR-reCb demonstrated strong correlations across all VOIs (r = 0.92–0.97, all p < 0.001). Bland–Altman analysis revealed a systematic negative bias, with CL-based values being consistently lower than reCb-based values (mean difference = − 0.08). The 95% limits of agreement ranged from − 0.25 to 0.09, indicating moderate variability between the two methods. Similarly, TAR-CL and TAR-reCb showed very strong correlations across all Amyloid SUVR VOIs (r = 0.96–0.99, all p < 0.001). When pooled across all regions, the correlation remained high (r = 0.988, 95% CI: 0.985–0.991). Bland–Altman analysis again demonstrated a consistent negative bias of CL relative to reCb, suggesting systematic underestimation associated with whole cerebellum normalization.

Overall, these results indicate that although CL- and reCb-based measurements are highly correlated, they are not directly interchangeable due to systematic differences between reference region definitions.

Cognitive correlation analysis

There was no significant correlation between amyloid images and either MMSE or ADAS scores. In contrast, tau images and TAR images showed a significant negative correlation with MMSE scores and a significant positive correlation with ADAS scores across the entire cerebral cortex, except for the primary sensorimotor and occipital cortices (FWE-corrected, p < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated distinct regional differences in the distribution of amyloid and tau deposition across the AD continuum. Amyloid deposition was widely distributed across association cortices, including the frontal cortex, posterior cingulate/precuneus, and temporal regions, even in the PCA group. In contrast, tau deposition in PCA was predominantly localized to the medial temporal lobe, with more extensive involvement of neocortical regions observed in the MCA and ADD groups.

These observations accord with prior PET imaging studies. For example, Pereira et al. [15] found that tau accumulation is most frequent in medial temporal regions initially, only later spreading to lateral temporal and other cortices. Similarly, Fantoni et al. [16] reported that in moderate amyloid-positive individuals, although amyloid deposition is diffuse (including frontal and parietal regions), tau PET signal remains more localized until later stages.

Amyloid vs. tau deposits and regional differences

One of the key findings is that amyloid deposition in the frontal cortex is strong even by PCA, while tau in the frontal region is not as abundant. This suggests that amyloid pathology may precede tau deposits into frontal regions, consistent with the hypothesis that amyloid sets the stage or primes the cortex for subsequent tau propagation. Studies such as “Spatial Patterns of β-Amyloid Accumulation” by Collij et al. showed early amyloid accumulation in frontal and default-mode network regions before significant tau deposition in these areas [17]. Importantly, our results should be interpreted as reflecting stage-associated differences rather than direct evidence of temporal progression, given the cross-sectional design of this study.

Tau dominance in later stages and TAR behavior

We found that in the ADD stage, tau deposition becomes very strong—often exceeding amyloid levels in those same regions. As a result, the TAR in the frontal cortex may decline, since although amyloid is also high, tau’s relative increase overshadows amyloid. This pattern aligns with neuropathologic staging [4], where tau deposits into neocortical regions later but intensifies in amount. Likewise, Iaccarino et al. have described patterns where tau shows less diffuse deposits until later, but then becomes dominant in several neocortical areas [18]. In MCA and ADD groups, TAR values increased in several cortical regions, reflecting increased tau burden relative to amyloid. However, TAR behavior varied across regions and disease stages, indicating that this metric is influenced by both numerator (tau) and denominator (amyloid) dynamics. Therefore, TAR should be interpreted as a relative index rather than a direct measure of pathological burden.

Medial temporal lobe: A zone of early tau despite low amyloid

An interesting nuance is that in the medial temporal lobe, we observed high tau deposition early (in PCA) even though amyloid levels there are comparatively lower. This leads to particularly high TAR values in medial temporal cortex in PCA, as tau deposition outpaces amyloid in this region. This finding resonates with work by Wuestefeld et al., who reported age-related and amyloid‐independent tau deposition heavily concentrated in medial temporal areas among older individuals [19].

Region of deposition by disease stage

Putting this together, a model emerges:

PCA stage: Amyloid has broadly deposited in many regions (frontal, PCC, temporal), but tau remains medial temporal. High TAR in medial temporal cortex because tau is more than amyloid in that region and in others amyloid is already high.

PCA stage: Amyloid has broadly deposited in many regions (frontal, PCC, temporal), but tau remains medial temporal. High TAR in medial temporal cortex because tau is more than amyloid in that region and in others amyloid is already high.

MCI stage: Tau begins to deposit in lateral temporal and perhaps parietal regions; TAR begins to decrease in frontal region because amyloid burden is already high and tau hasn’t caught up there as much.

MCI stage: Tau begins to deposit in lateral temporal and perhaps parietal regions; TAR begins to decrease in frontal region because amyloid burden is already high and tau hasn’t caught up there as much.

ADD stage: Tau becomes more widespread and intense, especially in neocortical regions, overtaking or adding to amyloid, so that TAR in frontal and other neocortical regions may show different patterns (lower or plateaued relative to temporal regions).

Cognitive correlation analysis

The observed relationships among amyloid PET, tau PET, TAR, and cognitive measures (MMSE and ADAS) in the present study are consistent with prior studies [5, 20]. Notably, the pattern of correlation between TAR and cognitive performance closely mirrors that of tau PET. This finding can be mechanistically explained by the lack of a significant association between amyloid burden and neuropsychological measures, as repeatedly demonstrated in previous voxel-wise and region-based studies. Given that TAR is defined as the ratio of tau to amyloid deposition, the absence of a meaningful contribution from the amyloid component suggests that the observed association between TAR and cognition is predominantly driven by the tau signal in the numerator. In this context, TAR may be interpreted as a tau-weighted metric, particularly in stages where amyloid deposition has plateaued or shows limited variability. These findings further support the notion that tau pathology, rather than amyloid burden, is more directly linked to cognitive impairment and clinical severity in AD.

Limitations and future directions

There are limitations to our study. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is based on cross-sectional data, and therefore cannot establish temporal trajectories of amyloid and tau accumulation. Longitudinal studies are required to validate the sequence of regional changes suggested by our findings.

Second, ratio-based metrics such as TAR may be sensitive to variability in the denominator, particularly in regions or stages with low amyloid signal. This may introduce instability and should be considered when interpreting regional differences.

Third, although age, sex, and scanner type were included as covariates in all analyses, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. In particular, age differences across groups may influence medial temporal tau signal.

Fourth, despite the application of false discovery rate (FDR) correction across regions, the large number of statistical comparisons remains a potential source of type I error.

Fifth, scanner-related variability and differences in image acquisition or reconstruction may affect quantitative PET measures, even though scanner type was included as a covariate.

Our findings suggest that TAR may provide complementary information regarding the spatial relationship between amyloid and tau deposition. However, its clinical utility should be interpreted cautiously. The present study does not include formal analyses of incremental predictive value, and therefore conclusions regarding its utility for diagnosis or prognosis remain preliminary.

Conclusions

In summary, amyloid deposition was widely distributed across cortical regions in the AD continuum, whereas tau deposition showed a more regionally restricted pattern, particularly in earlier stages. The TAR metric reflects these spatial differences, particularly highlighting relative tau predominance in medial temporal regions. However, given the cross-sectional design, methodological considerations, and potential limitations of ratio-based measures, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further longitudinal and predictive studies are required to clarify the clinical relevance of TAR and its role in AD imaging.
