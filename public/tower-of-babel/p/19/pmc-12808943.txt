COMPADRE: Combined pedigree-aware distant relatedness estimation for improved pedigree reconstruction
Authors: Grahame F. Evans, James T. Baker, Lauren E. Petty, Alexander S. Petty, Hannah G. Polikowsky, Ryan J. Bohlender, Hung-Hsin Chen, Che-Yu Chou, Kathryn Z. Viljoen, Janet M. Beilby, Shelly Jo Kraft, Wanying Zhu, Joshua M. Landman, Autumn R. Morrow, Dayi Bian, Alyssa C. Scartozzi, Chad D. Huff, Jennifer E. Below
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryDesigning powerful and unbiased genomic studies requires accurate assessment of familial relatedness even when this information is not captured from participants. Characterization of pairwise degrees of relatedness from participants’ genetic data enables reconstruction of pedigrees, and several pedigree reconstruction tools have emerged in the last decade. However, limitations of these tools include high computational burden in large datasets, reliance on external information, reduced accuracy in admixed populations, and most notably, an inability to accurately reconstruct pedigrees when only a subset of family members is represented in the genetic data. To improve pedigree reconstruction in large-scale data and in pedigrees with high proportions of ungenotyped individuals, we have developed a tool, combined pedigree-aware distant relatedness estimation (COMPADRE), that leverages pairwise relationship estimates informed by both averaged genome-wide identity-by-descent (IBD) sharing estimates and shared IBD segments’ length and distribution. COMPADRE reduces the number of candidate pedigrees compared to pedigree reconstruction and identification of a maximum unrelated set (PRIMUS), resulting in lower resource utilization and fewer high-likelihood pedigrees reconstructed, with the greatest improvements observed in pedigrees with high sample missingness. In 20- and 40-person pedigree simulations, COMPADRE also increases the proportions of correctly reconstructed pedigrees compared to PRIMUS, Bonsai, and CLAPPER. In 15,478 individuals of African ancestry from the BioVU biobank, COMPADRE achieved greater precision relative to PRIMUS. COMPADRE represents a marked improvement in both the confidence and accuracy of pedigree reconstruction relative to existing tools. COMPADRE integrates updates to the PRIMUS algorithm with the functions of estimation of recent shared ancestry (ERSA) and PADRE within a single tool and is freely available online.

Main text

Accurate characterization of familial relationships is critical for a wide variety of applications across epidemiologic, genetic, and genealogic analyses. For example, pedigrees can be used to estimate trait heritability,1,2 perform family-based linkage3,4,5 and association analyses,6 determine genetic model and penetrance,7 determine familial relative risk,8 conduct pedigree-aware genotype imputation9 and phasing,10,11 and identify and reunify families.12 In familial and population-based studies, poor characterization of relatedness can cause both false negative and false positive results and, alternatively, can provide a substantial increase in power when true pedigree structures are correctly modeled.13,14

Computational approaches that derive pedigree information from genetic data leverage measures of identity by descent (IBD) between pairs of individuals. PRIMUS (Pedigree Reconstruction and Identification of a Maximum Unrelated Set)15 was the first tool to enable assemblage of pairwise relationship information into accurate real-world, multi-generational pedigrees that could include gaps (e.g., ungenotyped parents) and half-siblings, without prior knowledge of family structure. The PRIMUS algorithm first constructs networks or non-directional graphs of related individuals from averaged genome-wide IBD sharing derived using PLINK under the assumption that all genetic markers are independent. It then estimates the degree of relationship between individuals using a kernel density estimation (KDE) function and iteratively reconstructs pedigrees in phases by degree of relatedness. PADRE (Pedigree-Aware Distant Relatedness Estimation)16 was subsequently developed to connect multiple pedigrees through distant relationships between founders.

Since the introduction of PRIMUS, several tools have emerged to address computational speed and efficiency in relatedness detection and pedigree reconstruction. CLAPPER (Composite Likelihood Approach to Pedigree Reconstruction), for example, first connects all sampled individuals into a “guess” pedigree and then stochastically evaluates possible structural rearrangements using simulated annealing. It then reports the most likely pedigree among all iterations.17 DRUID (Deep Relatedness Utilizing Identity by Descent), on the other hand, begins with an empty graph, adding nodes and edges for full sibling relationships, then parent-child relationships, and so on, allowing for inference of IBD-sharing profiles of ungenotyped ancestors and relatedness between distant relatives.18 Bonsai is targeted at linking distantly related individuals in large, sparsely sampled pedigrees.19 To accomplish this task, it infers small pedigrees of closely related individuals by systematically exploring the total pedigree space (akin to PRIMUS), the efficiency of which can be optimized through modifiable hyperparameters. The resulting “small Bonsai” pedigrees are then assembled into larger “big Bonsai” pedigrees using a generalized version of the DRUID algorithm.18 Most recently, PONDEROSA (Parent-Offspring Pedigree Inference Robust to Endogamy) was developed to expand the range of relationships that could be identified to include endogamous and consanguineous relationships.20

Despite these innovations, we observe that current methods often fail to reconstruct the true pedigree. A core driver of this observation is the overlap in IBD-sharing proportion ranges between incremental degrees of relatedness, which functionally lowers the potential resolution of pedigree reconstruction methods that rely on these estimates (Figure S1).15 Similarly, extant methods struggle to reconstruct pedigrees with high proportions of ungenotyped individuals, a situation often observed in biobanks,21 and because of susceptibility to bias in the presence of population structure (e.g., recent admixture),22,23 data heterogeneity (e.g., comprising sequence data of varying read depth, libraries, or multiple arrays),24 and diversity (e.g., spanning multiple distinct populations).25 For example, the method-of-moments approach to pairwise relatedness estimation in PLINK26 requires allele frequency information, which can be estimated from either the data itself or an external source, resulting in lower estimation accuracy for more ancestrally heterogeneous datasets.25,27 Heterogeneity of allele frequencies across populations or subpopulations within a dataset can cause an increase in variance around the expected mean proportion of IBD sharing.25 This phenomenon can lower relationship likelihood confidence levels and result in more ambiguity in pedigree reconstruction as measured by the number of possible pedigrees generated and the number of failed reconstructions.

A potential solution to these problems is the use of relatedness estimates for pedigree reconstruction that are derived from the length and distribution of contiguous genomic segments’ shared IBD, which are more robust to genetic heterogeneity.25 ERSA (Estimation of Recent Shared Ancestry)28 performs a composite likelihood ratio test on metrics of IBD segment length and distribution to accurately estimate pairwise relatedness up to the limit of detection (9th degree). We hypothesized that pairwise relationship estimates derived from IBD segment data have the potential to markedly improve pedigree reconstruction because ERSA can estimate 2nd- and 3rd-degree relationships with higher confidence and less dependence on population allele frequencies than averaged genome-wide IBD-sharing estimates (e.g., by PLINK26).

Indeed, PADRE already leverages distant relationships estimated by ERSA (≥3rd degree) to improve the accuracy of the estimation of the degree of relatedness between founders of pedigrees reconstructed by PRIMUS. PADRE requires input from both ERSA and PRIMUS; however, executing ERSA, PRIMUS, and PADRE each as standalone software tools is cumbersome and inefficient and can lead to errors.16 A single tool that more efficiently and effectively leverages IBD segment data to reconstruct pedigrees and combines them through distant founder relationships is needed to maximize the use of familial structures, especially in large, diverse, genetic data sources.

We therefore introduce Combined PADRE (COMPADRE), a tool that simultaneously brings together the functions of ERSA, PRIMUS, and PADRE. COMPADRE integrates information from IBD segments and global IBD-sharing estimates to improve pairwise relationship assignments and, consequently, pedigree reconstruction. To demonstrate improvements in completion rate, accuracy, and runtime, we tested COMPADRE on simulated genomic data with founder genotypes drawn from 1000 Genomes Project samples corresponding to the European (“EUR”) and admixed American (“AMR”) ancestry superpopulations.29 We also assessed performance on real data from both participants of genetically inferred African ancestry from Vanderbilt University Medical Center’s biobank, BioVU,30,31 as well as in a large, clinically validated pedigree. We find that COMPADRE’s improved efficiency enables pedigree reconstruction on biobank-scale genomic datasets and that COMPADRE outperforms PRIMUS and other popular tools, such as Bonsai and CLAPPER, in terms of reconstruction accuracy.

PRIMUS uses a trained KDE function to assign pairwise relationship likelihoods based on the clustering of externally estimated IBD proportions generated from genotype data (e.g., by PLINK,26 REAP,23 or PC-Relate22) with IBD data from simulated pedigrees. These likelihoods are used to build a probability vector, p, for six relationship types: parent-offspring (ppo); full sibling (pfs); half-sibling, avuncular, and grandparental (p2); first cousin, great grandparental, great avuncular, and half-avuncular (p3); distantly related (p4+); and unrelated (pu).15,32 To construct relatedness probability vectors with greater precision, COMPADRE incorporates two sources of relatedness data. For 1st-degree (parent-offspring and full sibling) relationships, COMPADRE maintains the same approach as PRIMUS, estimating the probability of ppo and pfs from the averaged genome-wide sharing estimates of IBD0 and IBD1 derived using PLINK’s method-of-moments approach. For the four other relationship types (p2, p3, p4+, and pu), COMPADRE derives relationship probabilities from the length and distribution of IBD segments using likelihoods generated by the ERSA algorithm.28 Thus, COMPADRE requires IBD segment input data, which can be generated using existing tools such as iLASH,33 hap-IBD,34 RaPID,35 or GERMLINE.36

Starting with our initial relationship probability vector p, we re-calculate p2, p3, p4+, and pu from ERSA likelihoods under the assumption that ppo and pfs are the optimal relationship probabilities given the IBD data and cannot be improved upon with segment-derived probabilities. We transform the ERSA likelihoods of the IBD segment data conditioned on the hypothesized relationship (pi) to the probability of the hypothesized relationship conditioned on the IBD segment data using Bayes’ rule, such that p2 + p3 + p4+ + pu = 1 − (ppo + pfs). Specifically, we convert from likelihoods to probabilities by applying p(relationship | IBD) = p(IBD | relationship) p(relationship)/p(IBD) and applying a uniform prior. We assume that ppo, pfs, p2, p3, p4+, and pun comprise the extent of relationship possibilities and subsequently handle the p(IBD) denominator by re-normalizing all p(relationship | IBD) values such that they sum to 1. COMPADRE integrates ERSA-based estimates for all pairs of individuals not inferred to be likely 1st-degree relatives, or ppo + pfs < 0.4. When a pair of individuals shares no IBD segments, pu is set to 1. Pedigree reconstruction is then performed as in PRIMUS, with the modified pairwise relationship probability vectors as input. COMPADRE adopts the same internal quality control pipeline as PRIMUS (referred to in both tools as the “prePRIMUS pipeline”), with the only analytical difference being an update to the ancestral population assignment approach that now leverages a support vector machine (SVM) algorithm. We find that this approach improves population assignment accuracy relative to the principal-component analysis (PCA)-based clustering method previously used by PRIMUS (Figure S2). Users can optionally select to automatically evaluate reconstructed pedigree results using the PADRE algorithm, which stitches together pedigrees via distant (up to 8th-degree) relationships between founders.

We benchmarked COMPADRE against other tools of interest using simulated genomic pedigree data. As in the Staples et al. papers,15,16,32 we used the approach developed by Morrison25 (Figure S3) to simulate pedigree data after modifying the software to improve usability and scalability (see data and code availability). We selected unrelated founders from 1000 Genomes Project EUR or AMR superpopulation reference files29 to drop in genotypes to the simulated pedigree structure(s). We then performed haplotype phasing for each individual simulation file set using SHAPEIT537 (providing a reference panel comprising population-specific samples from phased BioVU genotype data using the --reference option and default settings otherwise) and IBD segment detection using GERMLINE2 under default settings.36 We additionally estimated phase-unaware IBD segments for use in Bonsai directly from each simulated genomic data file set using IBIS38 with a 5 cM segment inclusion threshold and default settings otherwise.

We also simulated a sample age for input to Bonsai. The process begins by assigning generations to each sample, identifying pedigree founders (individuals with both parents coded as “0” in the FAM file) as generation 0, and then recursively assigning their children to generation 1, grandchildren to generation 2, and so on. A refinement step then works backwards from the deepest generation to ensure parents are consistently placed one generation above their children. Next, the script generates ages by giving each generation a base age such that age = 100 − (generation ∗ 25) years, with random variation added from a normal distribution (μ = 0, σ = 5 years). The algorithm then iteratively adjusts these ages through up to 100 iterations to satisfy biological constraints: maintaining parent-child age gaps of at least 14 years and assigning spouses similar ages (within ±2 years of their mean age plus random noise of −2 to +2 years). Ages are bounded between 0 and 100 years to provide maximum variability for the depth of pedigrees simulated. The final output appends both the simulated ages and generation assignments as new columns to the original pedigree data. The corresponding source code for age simulation is available in the COMPADRE GitHub repository (see data and code availability).

We additionally masked genotype data for a portion of the samples within each simulated pedigree using the approach described in Staples et al.15 to create missing individuals (Figure S3). Only individuals with children were eligible to be masked, and individuals were only masked when omitting their data did not create a gap larger than a 3rd-degree relationship (which would effectively split the pedigree that can be reconstructed). This iterative masking approach increases the number of 2nd- and 3rd-degree gaps, and, in turn, pedigree reconstruction complexity as more eligible individuals are masked, while maintaining the constructability of the original pedigree topology (Figures S4 and S5). We note that most modern tools, including PRIMUS, show comparable reconstruction accuracy when pedigree leaves are removed and nodes are not masked additively (Figure S6), as in Jewett et al.,19 even at high levels of sample missingness. Because the Jewett et al.19 approach results in reduced reconstruction complexity with increasing missingness (relative to the Staples et al.15 approach) and potentially reshapes the constructable topology (Figure S5), it can produce simpler, smaller pedigrees even as the proportion of individuals eligible to be masked approaches 100% (Figure S4B).

We additionally performed analysis on real data from BioVU. We performed sample and variant filtering for quality control ahead of running PRIMUS and COMPADRE using PLINK v.1.9.26 Initial filtering thresholds excluded duplicate variants and indels (insertions and deletions), duplicate samples (the duplicate sample with a lower call rate was removed), and variants that deviated strongly from Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE; p <1 × 10−15). We also used a minor-allele-frequency filter of 1%, a variant missingness filter of 3%, and a genotype missingness filter of 5%.

We evaluated COMPADRE performance against PRIMUS, Bonsai, and CLAPPER, all under default settings, by comparing the results of reconstructing family simulations stratified by pedigree size (size n = 20 and n = 40), proportion of sample missingness ([m] = 0–0.2), and ancestral background (1000 Genomes EUR and AMR population data). In total, we evaluated the four tools across 4,900 unique simulations, or 175 simulations per size-missingness-population stratum (Figure S7). All four methods of interest (Bonsai, CLAPPER, COMPADRE, and PRIMUS) return a PLINK-formatted FAM file or similar, where each line in the file corresponds to an individual in the suggested pedigree structure and the individual’s parental IDs.27 Bonsai and CLAPPER return a single pedigree17,19; in contrast, PRIMUS and COMPADRE reconstruct and return all high-likelihood pedigrees in both text and visual format (for details, see Staples et al.15). When more than one pedigree can be reconstructed, PRIMUS and COMPADRE rank them in a summary file by a score equal to the sum of the log likelihood value of each relationship in the pedigree.15 All data simulation and software benchmarking were performed using an x86_64 compute cluster and Simple Linux Utility for Resource Management (SLURM) for job distribution.39 Compute nodes feature 64+ cores and 512+ GB RAM per node. We supplied 4 cores, 64 GB RAM, and 3 days of runtime for all benchmarking jobs. We carried out all compute allocations in accordance with the protocol in Staples et al.15 We additionally implemented a fallback consistent with the original PRIMUS benchmarking of re-evaluating partially reconstructed pedigrees at a 2nd-degree maximum-level relatedness (the default is 3rd degree).15 We used the GNU Project command-line time utility to collect runtime and resource utilization data for each tool.40

We evaluated and compared simulated pedigree reconstructions in COMPADRE and PRIMUS and found that COMPADRE reconstructs the true pedigree as the only possible pedigree as often or more often than PRIMUS, with greater improvements in performance for pedigrees with higher numbers of missing individuals (Figure 1; Table S2). In addition, COMPADRE reconstructed fewer incomplete pedigrees and successfully reconstructed a large proportion (70%, EUR; 61%, AMR) of the simulations where PRIMUS failed to reconstruct the full pedigree (Figure S8). We defined a “successful reconstruction” as ranking the true pedigree as most likely for COMPADRE and PRIMUS and as reconstruction of the true pedigree structure for Bonsai and CLAPPER, which only reconstruct a single pedigree for each network. Bonsai, CLAPPER, and PRIMUS closely matched COMPADRE’s reconstruction success in simulations with few to no missing individuals, but COMPADRE increasingly outperformed other tools as the proportion of missing individuals increased (Figure 2; Table S2). PRIMUS performance generally mirrored that of COMPADRE but at a diminished level of reconstruction success (up to 20% lower in n = 40, m = 0.2 pedigrees), notably as the proportion of missing individuals increased. Both Bonsai and CLAPPER demonstrated a modest but overall decrease in performance compared to COMPADRE and PRIMUS when averaged across all simulation varieties. Notably, as sample missingness increased, CLAPPER’s performance gap widened, but Bonsai’s performance converged toward that of COMPADRE and PRIMUS, even outperforming PRIMUS in n = 40, m > 0.15 pedigrees. Because COMPADRE and PRIMUS reconstruct all likely pedigrees, improvements in precision can be achieved by reconstructing fewer candidate pedigrees while retaining the true pedigree among the candidates. For pedigrees that were correctly reconstructed in both COMPADRE and PRIMUS, COMPADRE identified fewer candidate pedigrees on average relative to PRIMUS (Figure S9). For simulations with n = 20, we observed up to an 11% decrease in the number of candidate pedigrees reconstructed in COMPADRE relative to PRIMUS. In simulations with n = 20, we also observed a larger proportion of reconstructions with COMPADRE where the true pedigree was the only pedigree detected (EUR, +10%; AMR, +11%) (Figure 1; Table S2). The greatest improvement in pedigree resolution was in simulations with n = 40, where we observed up to a 25% decrease in the number of candidate pedigrees generated in COMPADRE relative to PRIMUS and a 22% increase in the number of simulations where the true pedigree was the only pedigree detected (Figure S9; Table S1). This improvement is due to greater precision in pairwise relationship estimation, with fewer pairs of people that require reconstructions of pedigrees reflecting multiple degrees of relatedness (e.g., reconstructing all possible pedigrees where a given pair is represented as 2nd and 3rd due to the inability to adequately distinguish between these degrees using the PRIMUS KDE approach15). Ambiguous relationship assignments occur whenever there is more than one degree of relationship with a probability >0.1, a threshold optimized in the original PRIMUS implementation.15 Integration of IBD-segment-derived relationship estimates in COMPADRE led to a marked reduction of ambiguity in pairwise relationships across both pedigree sizes and ancestral superpopulations (EUR n = 20, +21%; EUR n = 40, +55%; AMR n = 20, +25%; AMR n = 40, +39%).

In addition to simulation benchmarks, we applied COMPADRE to real data from BioVU, specifically, individuals previously genotyped on the MEGAEX array41 (n = 15,478) with genetic similarity to the 1000 Genomes African superpopulation (AFR)31 as assessed by PCA.42 We performed phasing and IBD detection as previously described and reconstructed pedigrees using default settings in both PRIMUS and COMPADRE. COMPADRE generated an overall 15% lower average number of candidate pedigrees per successful reconstruction and up to 24% fewer average candidate pedigrees in networks with 11–15 genotyped individuals (Figure 3). COMPADRE also extended the total set of successful reconstructions by reconstructing an additional 5% of the pedigrees where PRIMUS failed to fully reconstruct. We compared runtime (as defined by total CPU time) as well as memory utilization (as defined by maximum RAM usage) in Bonsai, CLAPPER, COMPADRE, and PRIMUS. In simulated pedigrees, COMPADRE demonstrated decreased runtime compared to PRIMUS as sample missingness increased, with the greatest improvement seen in n = 20, m = 0.2 pedigrees, where the COMPADRE runtime was 61% faster on average (Figure S11). COMPADRE and PRIMUS used comparable RAM across simulation varieties with zero to low sample missingness (COMPADRE, average 1.1 GB; PRIMUS, average 1.2 GB). In m ≥ 0.05 simulations, COMPADRE used 20% less RAM on average in n = 20 simulations, with the greatest difference seen in n = 40, m = 0.075 simulations, where COMPADRE used 41% less RAM than PRIMUS (COMPADRE, average 2.0 GB; PRIMUS, average 3.4 GB). Bonsai outperformed PRIMUS and COMPADRE in both CPU time and RAM usage in both n = 20 (average 1.0 min, 0.1 GB RAM) and n = 40 (average 2.6 min, 0.2 GB RAM) simulations. CLAPPER also outperformed PRIMUS and COMPADRE in CPU time across all simulation varieties (average 1.3 min). CLAPPER’s RAM usage was consistent across all simulation varieties (average 1.4 GB), representing lower utilization than in PRIMUS, COMPADRE, and Bonsai in n = 40, m > 0.025 simulations and higher utilization in all n = 20 simulations. We additionally benchmarked our real BioVU data analysis of 1,653 family networks reconstructed from 15,478 individuals of African ancestry with genomic data. COMPADRE ran 25% faster than PRIMUS with comparable RAM usage.

While Bonsai, CLAPPER, COMPADRE, and PRIMUS are all capable of reconstructing accurate pedigrees with few to no family members missing, performance diverges in large pedigrees with high proportions of missing individuals, which are the predominant scenarios in biobank data and large case-control studies. Through the integration of ERSA relationship likelihoods into the PRIMUS algorithm, COMPADRE markedly extends the accuracy and efficiency of pedigree reconstruction, demonstrated by its performance in both simulated and real data. Moreover, improvements to pedigree reconstruction can now, in turn, improve optional linkage of discrete pedigree outputs via PADRE (Figure S12). These improvements make comprehensive pedigree reconstruction a tractable problem even in the largest biobank settings across diverse populations. Additionally, as the availability of whole-genome sequencing data in large biobanks continues to increase, new opportunities are arising for family-based genetic approaches to again contribute to the genomic discovery renaissance,41 and the identification and reconstruction of pedigrees will be essential first steps in such efforts. Furthermore, as genetic data generation costs continue to decline, tools for pedigree reconstruction are also becoming increasingly valuable in non-human studies,43,44,45 where complex pedigrees with missing family members are the norm.

In summary, COMPADRE offers clear improvements in pedigree reconstruction while bringing together key relatedness and pedigree inference tooling in a single program. These improvements will be especially valuable to investigators working with large-scale genomic datasets, where sparse representation of families is commonplace and the availability of scalable computational approaches is paramount to successful investigation.

Data and code availability

Both COMPADRE and the modified Morrison pedigree simulation tool are freely available to download from source via GitHub (see web resources). COMPADRE is also supported via accompanying Docker and Singularity images. Full installation and execution instructions, as well as example input data, are on GitHub.
