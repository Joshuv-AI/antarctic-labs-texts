Red cell distribution width (RDW), RDW to albumin ratio (RAR), and long‐term mortality in diabetic neuropathy: A NHANES analysis, 1999–2019
Authors: Jui‐Chu Huang, Pey‐Jium Chang, Yi‐Lun Chiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsWe aimed to explore associations between Red cell distribution width (RDW), RDW to albumin ratio (RAR), and long‐term all‐cause and cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in adults with diabetes and DN.MethodsThis study included adults aged ≥40 years with DN from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey from 1999 to 2004, followed through 2019. Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations between RDW, RAR, and all‐cause and CVD mortality. Predictive accuracy was determined using area under the curve (AUC) by receiver operating characteristic analyses.ResultsData of a total of 624 participants with DN were analyzed. Each unit increase in RDW significantly increased all‐cause mortality by 12% (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.20) and CVD mortality by 15% (aHR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.02–1.31), while each unit increase in RAR increased all‐cause mortality by 73% (aHR = 1.73) and CVD mortality by 93% (aHR = 1.93). In the highest versus lowest quartiles, RDW (aHR = 1.89) and RAR (aHR = 2.91) were associated with higher all‐cause mortality risk, and even higher risk for CVD mortality (RDW: aHR = 2.86; RAR: aHR = 4.84). The AUC for 5‐year predictions was RDW 0.825 and RAR 0.846 for all‐cause mortality, and RDW 0.811 and RAR 0.814 for CVD mortality.ConclusionsRDW and RAR are strong predictors of long‐term mortality in individuals with DN. RAR demonstrates stronger associations and higher predictive accuracy than RDW, particularly in predicting CVD mortality.

INTRODUCTION

Diabetic neuropathy (DN) is a prevalent and debilitating complication of diabetes mellitus, affecting nearly 50% of individuals with long‐term diabetes 1 , 2 . This condition manifests primarily as damage to the peripheral nerves, resulting in distressing symptoms such as pain, numbness, and tingling, especially in the extremities 3 , 4 . The chronic nature of DN not only diminishes the quality of life significantly for those affected but also increases the risk of morbidity and mortality substantially among adults with diabetes. The development of DN is often associated with other complications of diabetes, leading to myriad health issues that complicate diabetes management 5 , 6 .

In recent years, red cell distribution width (RDW), a measure of variations in red blood cell size, has gained attention as a potential biomarker for predicting adverse outcomes in various chronic conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, sepsis, and diabetes 7 , 8 . Elevated RDW levels have been associated with increased inflammation and oxidative stress, both of which are critical factors in the pathogenesis of diabetic complications. Additionally, the RDW to albumin ratio (RAR) has also emerged as an important indicator of disease severity and systemic inflammation 9 , 10 . Elevated RAR values have been linked to poorer prognostic outcomes, highlighting the potential utility of RAR in clinical settings 11 .

Despite the growing body of literature supporting the prognostic value of RDW and RAR in various chronic diseases, the specific associations between these biomarkers and mortality in people with diabetes who develop peripheral neuropathy remain underexplored. Understanding these associations is crucial, particularly given the complex interplay between diabetes, neuropathy, and other comorbid conditions that may exacerbate health outcomes. Therefore, this study aimed to explore associations between RDW, RAR, and long‐term all‐cause and CVD mortality in adults with diabetes and DN.

METHODS

Study design and data source

The present cross‐sectional, population‐based, observational, retrospective study was an analysis of secondary data extracted from The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database. NHANES data are collected by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (http://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/). The ongoing survey is conducted in two‐year cycles to evaluate the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the US. It uses a complex, multistage design to collect and analyze data representative of the national, non‐institutionalized population of the US. The data are released for research purposes, and permission to use the data is granted to researchers by the NCHS. Participants in NHANES complete a household interview and are invited to undergo an extensive examination in a mobile examination center, including a physical examination, specialized measurements, and laboratory tests. Consequently, evaluating subjects within the NHANES database provides reliable and multidimensional data, which can be equated to a population‐level assessment.

Ethics considerations

NHANES was approved by the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided signed informed consent prior to their participation in the survey. Because the present study involved analysis of secondary data that were de‐identified, no additional ethical approval or informed consent was required. The data used in this study remained anonymous throughout the analyses.

Study population

The present study utilized data from the NHANES database collected between 1999 and 2004. Adults aged 40 years and older with DN were included in the analysis. Participants with both diabetes and peripheral neuropathy were classified as having DN. Participants with a history of stroke, as well as those with missing data on sex, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), mortality, sample weights, RDW, and serum albumin were excluded from the cohort.

Confirmation of diabetes

Participants were classified as having diabetes if they met any of the following criteria: self‐reported diagnosis of diabetes by a healthcare professional, reported current use of anti‐diabetic medications, an HbA1c level of ≥6.5%, a fasting plasma glucose level of ≥126 mg/dL, or an oral glucose tolerance test result of ≥200 mg/dL.

Confirmation of peripheral neuropathy

Participants who responded positively to either of the following questions were classified as having peripheral neuropathy: “During the past 3 months, have you had a painful sensation or tingling in your hands or feet? Do not include normal foot aches from standing or walking for long periods” and “During the past 3 months, have you had numbness or loss of feeling in your hands or feet, other than from your hands or feet falling asleep?”

Demographic, lifestyle, and clinical characteristics of participants

Demographic data of participants' included age, sex, race/ethnicity, education level, and poverty‐to‐income ratio. Cigarette smoking status (the only lifestyle characteristic measured) was obtained by standard questionnaires through in‐person home interviews conducted by trained NHANES' interviewers using the Family and Sample Person Demographics questionnaires and the Computer‐Assisted Personal Interviewing system (Confirmit Corp., New York, NY, USA). Collected data were weighted according to the NHANES protocol. Body mass index (BMI) value was obtained from the NHANES examination measurements, calculated as body weight (kilograms) divided by height (meters squared). Body weight was measured using an electronic load cell scale, and standing height was measured with a fixed stadiometer.

Additionally, data on comorbid chronic diseases were gathered from the medical condition questionnaire. Comorbidities examined in this study included hypertension, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic respiratory diseases, and cancer. Laboratory measurements, including total cholesterol, triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), albumin, HbA1c, and fasting glucose, were obtained from standard NHANES biochemistry profiles analyzed from blood samples collected at the time of participants' enrollment.

Statistical analysis

The NHANES database employs a complex, multistage, probability sampling design to ensure national representation. The analysis incorporated sampling weights, pseudo‐strata, and pseudo‐cluster identifiers provided by NHANES and instructed by the NCHS. For continuous variables, weighted means and standard errors were calculated and presented, while categorical variables were summarized using unweighted counts and weighted percentages. Differences in continuous variables across groups were compared using analysis appropriate for survey data, and differences in categorical variables were evaluated using the Rao‐Scott chi‐square test designed for complex survey samples.

Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to estimate associations between RDW, RAR, and mortality. The results were presented in hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kaplan–Meier plots were depicted, and the log‐rank test was used to compare overall survival across the quartiles of RDW and RAR of the participants. Multivariable regressions adjusted for potential confounders (variables found to be significant in univariate analysis), including age (in years), sex, race/ethnicity, BMI, CVD, and CKD. Performance of RDW and RAR in predicting all‐cause and CVD mortality was measured using the area under the curve (AUC). A two‐sided P‐value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS statistical software (version 9.4; SAS Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Study population selection

The study sample selection process is shown in Figure 1. A total of 31,126 subjects were identified in the NHANES 1999–2004 data cycles, where there were 822 participants aged 40 years and older with both diabetes and reported to have peripheral neuropathy. Participants with stroke (n = 86) and those with missing information on HbA1c, RDW, or RAR (n = 112) were excluded. Finally, 624 participants were included for the study. Based on the sample weights provided by the NHANES, this sample represents a population of 1,839,891 participants in the entire US (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of study population selection.

Characteristics of the study population

Patients' characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The mean age of the study population was 61.6 years, the male/female ratio was approximately equal, and most participants were non‐Hispanic White (61.7%) based on NHANES' categories of race/ethnicity. Significant differences were observed in age, race/ethnicity, CVD, CKD, LDL‐c, and fasting glucose between participants with and without all‐cause mortality, as well as those with and without CVD mortality. Compared to the participants who survived, the deceased participants had higher mean RDW and RAR. Mean RDW was 13.3 and 12.8 in deceased and survived groups, respectively (P < 0.001). Mean RAR was 3.3 and 3.1 in deceased and survived groups, respectively (P < 0.001). For CVD mortality, mean RDW was 13.4 and 12.9 for participants with and without CVD mortality (P = 0.005), and mean RAR was 3.3 and 3.1 for participants with and without CVD mortality (P = 0.003) (Table 1).

Associations between RDW, RAR, and mortality outcomes

Tables 2 and Table S1 present associations between RDW, RAR, and mortality outcomes. After adjustment, for each additional unit of RDW and RAR, the risk of all‐cause mortality increased significantly (RDW: adjusted HR [aHR] = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.20, P = 0.002; RAR: aHR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.41–2.11, P < 0.001). Compared to those in quartile 1 of RDW, participants in quartile 4 of RDW had a 1.89 times higher risk of all‐cause mortality (95% CI: 1.17–3.05, P = 0.010). Similarly, compared to those in quartile 1 of RAR, participants in quartiles 3 and 4 had a higher risk of all‐cause mortality (aHR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.48–2.85, P < 0.001; aHR = 2.91, 95% CI: 2.02–4.17, P < 0.001, respectively) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

The present study explored associations between RDW, RAR, and long‐term mortality outcomes, including CVD mortality. We found that both RDW and RAR are strong predictors of long‐term mortality in adult participants from the NHANES with DN. Participants in the highest quartiles of RDW and RAR had a higher risk of all‐cause mortality than those in the lowest quartile, with risk increasing by 89% for RDW (aHR = 1.89) and 191% for RAR (aHR = 2.91) for all‐cause mortality, and by 186% for RDW (aHR = 2.86) and 384% for RAR (aHR = 4.84) for CVD mortality. Associations were even stronger for CVD mortality. Additionally, the results of the present study show definitively that RDW and RAR are reliable biomarkers, with excellent predictive accuracy, particularly for CVD mortality. RAR demonstrates the strongest associations and outperforms RDW in predictive accuracy. Stratified analyses by CVD history, CKD status, and obesity status revealed consistent results in which RAR had the strongest association with CVD mortality among various subgroups.

Previous studies included a comprehensive review of DN, reporting it to arise from nerve barrier dysfunction 1 . That review focused on the development of DN and current therapies and the need for more effective therapies since DN is largely responsible for the increased morbidity and mortality in adults with diabetes. Yang et al. 12 reported progress in the treatment of DN, explaining that standard current therapies include blood glucose control, nutritional supplements, antioxidants, and stress reduction.

Other studies have evaluated RDW and RAR and reported promising results for their predictive capabilities in individuals with diabetes and diabetic complications. The results of Liu et al. 9 are compatible with the present study results and were also generated using data of NHANES participants, showing that the predictive value of RAR is more accurate than that of RDW for projecting the prevalence of diabetes, while RAR also exhibits strong long‐term prognostic power. Liu et al. 9 also showed that survival time was reduced as RAR increased for those aged ≤60 years among female patients with diabetes. Also, similar to the results of the present study, results of a cohort study by Hao et al. 10 found that higher baseline RAR values were associated with increased risk of all‐cause and cause‐specific mortality in the general population. Those authors suggested that RAR may be a simple, reliable, and inexpensive indicator for identifying adults with diabetes and DN who are at high risk for mortality. Associations have also been found between RDW, RAR, and mortality in individuals with diabetes and complications other than DN, including Diabetic Lower Extremity Ulcers and diabetic retinopathy 13 , 14 . However, despite advances in the diagnosis and management of diabetes and its complications, no readily accessible prognostic tool is available currently to help identify individuals at high risk for mortality. Results of the present study appear to confirm that RAR is an appropriate tool by which to identify adults with diabetes and DN who are at high risk for all‐cause and CVD mortality.

Although the current analysis demonstrates associations and predictive values, it cannot address the underlying mechanisms based solely on epidemiological data. However, it is important to elucidate these mechanisms to better understand the findings. Associations between elevated RDW and RAR and higher mortality in adults with diabetes and DN is likely driven by the strong link between RDW and systemic inflammation, as demonstrated in several fairly recent studies including Fu et al. 14 and Joosse et al. 15 Elevated RDW reflects ineffective erythropoiesis and chronic inflammation, both of which are more pronounced in patients with long‐standing diabetes and its complications such as DN. Adults with diabetes and DN tend to have more advanced disease and higher levels of systemic inflammation compared to adults with diabetes in the general population, which may account for the greater predictive value of RDW and RAR in this population, as shown in the present study.

Albumin also plays a role in anti‐inflammatory response and overall health status 16 , and thus a higher RAR suggests increased inflammation and poorer nutritional status. This may help to explain why RAR outperformed RDW in predictive accuracy for CVD mortality in the present cohort. The inflammation likely contributes to vascular damage and accelerates atherosclerosis, further increasing the risk of mortality 17 , 18 , 19 . In summary, building on the results of these previous studies, findings of the present study demonstrate that RAR may fill the need for a prognostic marker for mortality in this population with diabetes and DN.

Going forward: implications for clinical practice

RDW and RAR, both derived from standard blood tests and cost‐effective to implement, can easily be incorporated into routine clinical practice for risk stratification in individuals with diabetes who have already developed neuropathy. RAR may be especially valuable in identifying high‐risk patients who may benefit from more intensive management of cardiovascular risk factors. Given the high mortality rates observed in patients with elevated RDW and RAR, it is suggested that healthcare providers should consider closer monitoring and potentially earlier interventions.

Strengths and limitations

This study utilized a large, nationally representative sample from the NHANES database, assuring generalizability of its findings across the US population on associations between RDW, RAR, and long‐term mortality in adults with diabetes and DN. However, this study still had several limitations.

First, this cross‐sectional, population‐based, observational study design limits generalization of results to other populations, may include selection bias, and does not allow inferences of causality. Linkage to the US national death data enabled a longitudinal follow‐up, allowing for an in‐depth analysis of 5‐ and 10‐year mortality outcomes, emphasizing RDW and RAR as potential prognostic markers. Second, self‐reported data may underestimate the prevalence of asymptomatic neuropathy, which is common in diabetic patients (approximately 30–50% may be asymptomatic) 2 . Future studies should incorporate objective tests, such as the Semmes‐Weinstein 10‐g monofilament test, to capture both symptomatic and asymptomatic cases.

Additionally, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors may still influence the results. The RDW and RAR values were collected only once at the initial entry into the study, and the study was unable to track changes in these markers over time. This limitation may affect the understanding of how fluctuations in RDW and RAR could influence long‐term mortality outcomes in diabetic patients with neuropathy.

CONCLUSIONS

Results of this study demonstrate that both higher RDW and RAR are strong predictors of long‐term mortality in adults with DN. RAR shows the strongest associations, outperforming RDW in predictive accuracy, especially for CVD mortality. Particularly, our focus on DN patients highlights the amplified prognostic value of RAR in this high‐risk subgroup, where hazard ratios for mortality are substantially higher compared to general populations. This underscores the clinical relevance of RAR as a prognostic tool specifically for DN patients. Although RDW and RAR are clearly reliable prognostic indicators of long‐term mortality in this population, further research tracking longitudinal changes in these markers is warranted to strengthen their clinical applicability.
