Interpretable Deep Learning Radiomics for Differentiating Pleomorphic Adenoma and Warthin Tumor
Authors: CHUYUAN MA, YUNXIA HUANG, ZIHENG HUANG, JIALI XU, SIYAO HU, YANXIA ZHANG, ZIKANG ZHAO, XIUHONG GUAN, GUOJUN ZHAO, GUANGMING LI, ZIYAN WANG
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimPreoperative differentiation between pleomorphic adenoma (PA) and Warthin tumor (WT) is essential for optimizing surgical strategies. This study aimed to develop and validate an interpretable machine learning framework integrating clinical data, traditional radiomics, and deep learning features to enhance diagnostic precision via conventional computed tomography (CT).Patients and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 171 patients (84 PA and 87 WT). A total of 1,561 radiomic and 2,048 deep learning features were extracted from preoperative CT scans. Following LASSO-based feature selection, models were constructed and evaluated on a 70:30 training-validation split (n=119 and n=52, respectively). Diagnostic performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Model interpretability was achieved through SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis.ResultsThe combined model significantly outperformed individual clinical or radiomic models. The XGBoost classifier exhibited the highest efficacy, yielding a validation AUC of 0.961 (95%CI=0.916-1.000). In the training cohort, the clinical model and the radiomics-deep learning model achieved AUCs of 0.902 (95%CI=0.843-0.962) and 0.95 (95%CI=0.91-0.99), respectively. SHAP analysis indicated that the deep-learning score, patient age, and gender were the most influential predictors.ConclusionAn interpretable model combining deep learning radiomics and clinical attributes provides robust accuracy in distinguishing PA from WT. The integration of SHAP values offers clinicians transparent insights, supporting personalized treatment planning.

Introduction

Pleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most frequent benign neoplasm of the parotid gland, accounting for 85%-90% of salivary gland tumors, followed by Warthin tumors (WTs) which comprise 6%-10% of cases (1, 2). Accurate preoperative differentiation is clinically imperative due to their divergent biological trajectories. PAs exhibit “borderline” characteristics; although initially benign, they can transform into potentially fatal pleomorphic adenocarcinoma (3, 4). Consequently, PAs necessitate extensive resection to mitigate a high risk of recurrence or malignant transformation, whereas WTs–rarely malignant–often allow for conservative observation, particularly in elderly or frail patients (5). Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) offers diagnostic clarity but carries risks of tumor seeding or facial nerve injury (6).

Conventional imaging often fails to provide a definitive diagnosis due to the significant phenotypic overlap between these entities. Radiomics facilitates a non-invasive, high-throughput extraction of quantitative phenotypes, revealing biological characteristics invisible to the human eye. Although multi-sequence imaging has shown significant diagnostic promise (7-10), traditional workflows frequently lack standardized automation, and the clinical deployment of robust preprocessing tools remains an ongoing challenge (11). Deep learning radiomics (DLR), utilizing convolutional neural networks (CNNs), provides a sophisticated alternative by quantifying high-dimensional phenotypes beyond manual perception (12). However, integrated research combining handcrafted radiomics with deep transfer learning for parotid tumors remains sparse, and the inherent “black box” nature of these models hinders clinical trust. We therefore introduced the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) framework to enhance diagnostic transparency and elucidate feature importance (12).

Patients and Methods

Study subjects. A total of 171 patients diagnosed with salivary gland tumors who were admitted to Qingyuan People’s Hospital from June 2017 to March 2024 were selected through the electronic medical record system. All patients underwent routine preoperative CT scans, and the diagnoses were confirmed through postoperative follow-up and pathology reports. Among these patients, there were 87 cases of WT and 84 cases of PA. Inclusion criteria were: (i) All patients underwent preoperative CT scans at our hospital, and the images were standardized and clear; (ii) All cases were confirmed by postoperative pathology, and clinical data were complete; (iii) No history of puncture or radiotherapy; (iv) For patients with multiple lesions, only the largest lesion was included. Exclusion criteria were: (i) Incomplete or unclear imaging data; (ii) Significant imaging artifacts, such as those from dentures or motion; (iii) Presence of other tumors.

The patients included in this study were randomly divided into a training set and a validation set in a 7:3 ratio. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Qingyuan People’s Hospital (Ethics Approval Number: IRB-2024-076) and complies with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki. This retrospective study was exempted from obtaining informed consent from the subjects by the ethics committee.

Imaging data acquisition. CT examinations were performed using a Toshiba 320-slice spiral CT scanner (Model: Aquilion One; Toshiba Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan). The scanning range extended from the external auditory canal to the mandibular angle. Scanning parameters included a tube voltage of 120 kV, automatic tube current, a pitch of 1.0, and a slice thickness of 2 mm.

Clinical and CT imaging feature model construction. Imaging data for all included patients were obtained from the hospital’s Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) system. Two radiologists with ten years of experience (H.Z. and M.L.) evaluated the clinical and CT characteristics while maintaining blinding to the clinical pathology data. The specific features assessed were as follows: (i) Sex (male/female); (ii) Age; (iii) Smoking status (yes/no); (iv) Alcohol consumption (yes/no). The CT images were analyzed to record (v) tumor distribution (left/right); (vi) Tumor shape (regular/irregular); (vii) Tumor margin (well-defined/ill-defined); (viii) CT value; (ix) Maximum tumor diameter; (x) Deep involvement of the parotid gland (yes/no); (xi) Enlarged lymph nodes (present/absent); (xii) Presence of cystic changes, necrosis, and calcification. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to compare the clinical and CT features between PAs and WTs. Indicators that showed significant differences were used to construct the clinical model.

Image segmentation and feature extraction. Image segmentation of the parotid gland was performed layer by layer using 3Dslicer software (version 4.11), with Physician A responsible for the completion. Physician B randomly re-delineated the images of 30 patients one week later, and Physician A re-delineated the regions of interest (ROIs) on the CT images of these 30 patients.

Radiomics feature extraction was conducted using the RIAS medical imaging preprocessing tool, which is developed based on the Pyradiomics library and supports file preprocessing, ROI extraction, radiomics feature extraction, and data preprocessing functionalities (11). Deep learning feature extraction was performed using the open-source software Python 3.8.19, with all parotid gland CT images from the included patients serving as training data. The classification labels were PA and WT. The ResNet50 neural network model was trained using mini-batch stochastic gradient descent, with a batch size set to four (batch_size=four) and an initial learning rate of 1e-4 (learning_rate=1e-4). The model convergence was achieved after 50 complete training epochs (epochs=50). The trained ResNet50 model served as a feature extractor, outputting the feature values from the average pooling layer prior to the fully connected layer of the neural network, resulting in a total of 2048 deep learning features extracted.

Feature selection and model construction. The inter-class correlation coefficient and intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) were employed for evaluation to ensure that the selected features exhibited good consistency and reproducibility. An ICC greater than 0.8 indicates high consistency. Features with ICC >0.8 were retained and combined with deep learning features. Since deep learning features are high-dimensional numerical vectors rather than manually defined, they did not require consistency testing (12).

Radiomics features and deep learning features were normalized using Z-scores to avoid the influence of differences in dimensionality and value ranges among features. Using the standardized feature data from the training set, a Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model with 10-fold cross-validation was applied for feature dimensionality reduction, determining the final feature set and corresponding coefficients to eliminate redundant features and retain only those with the highest predictive value. Additionally, the weighted coefficients from the LASSO model were used to calculate the deep radiomics scores (Rad_and_DL scores) for patients in the training set, thereby constructing a radiomics deep learning feature model. The aforementioned process derived from the training set was directly applied to the validation set data, which did not participate in any parameter calculations in the previous steps.

Construction and performance evaluation of machine learning models. Models were constructed using Logistic regression based on clinical features, radiomics deep learning features, and a combination of both feature sets, with nomograms generated accordingly. Feature classification was implemented using R software (version 4.4.2). The performance of the models was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were provided for the results. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to assess the clinical utility of the models by determining the net benefits at various threshold probabilities for the training and testing datasets. Calibration curves were utilized to validate the calibration of the models. After selecting the model with the best performance among the three, the corresponding features were used to establish predictive models with seven classifiers, including logistic regression (LR), decision tree (DT), random forest (RF), extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), K-nearest neighbor (KNN), and Naive Bayes model (NBM). The performance of the models was compared using the AUC of the ROC curve.

Model interpretation and feature importance. Machine learning models are often regarded as black boxes due to the difficulty in explaining why a particular algorithm can provide accurate predictions for specific patient populations. Therefore, we introduced SHAP values in this study. SHAP, proposed by Lundberg and Lee, is a unified framework for interpreting machine learning predictions, representing a novel method for explaining various black box machine learning models. Its interpretability performance has been previously validated. Based on its quantifiable impact on model predictions, this study applied SHAP to quantify the contribution of each variable to the prediction outcomes and to highlight the most influential variables. Statistical methods. Data analysis was performed using the R software package (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables included in the study’s clinical characteristics were age, maximum diameter, and CT values. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median (interquartile range) [M (P25, P75)] and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables included in this study were sex (male/female), smoking status (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no), tumor distribution (left/right), tumor shape (regular/irregular), tumor margin (clear/blurred), maximum tumor diameter, deep involvement of the parotid gland (yes/no), presence of enlarged lymph nodes (yes/no), presence of cystic changes, necrosis, and calcification (yes/no), which were expressed as frequencies and percentages (%), and analyzed using the χ2 test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Baseline descriptions in the training and validation sets were performed using the compareGroups package, with automatic identification; statistical tables were generated using the CBCgrps package.

Variables that were significant in univariate logistic regression analysis were included in multivariate logistic regression analysis (glm {stats} package), employing a backward stepwise method (backward Wald method) to select the final predictive variables included in the model. A nomogram model was constructed based on the regression coefficients of the predictive variables. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was used to evaluate the model’s discrimination; calibration curves were used to assess the model’s accuracy; and DCA was employed to evaluate the clinical applicability of the model.

For feature selection, LASSO regression was conducted using the glmnet package. Machine learning algorithms included decision trees using the rpart package; random forests using the randomForest package; naive Bayes and SVM using the e1071 package; k-nearest neighbors (KNN) using the kknn package; and XGBoost using the xgboost package, with the SHAPforxgboost package specifically utilized to interpret the outputs of the XGBoost model.

All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software package (version 4.4.2) and DCPM (V5.49, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, PR China). In this study, the R software package (version 4.4.2) and DCPM (V5.49, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) was used for the drawing of the figures.

Results

Patient characteristics. A total of 171 patients were included in this study, comprising 112 males and 59 females, with ages ranging from nine to 78 years, and a mean age of 52.08±15.21 years. Among the 171 patients, there were 87 cases of Warthin tumors (51%) and 84 cases of PAs (49%). All patients were divided into a training group (n=119) and a validation group (n=52), with a ratio of 7:3. The training group included 56 patients (47%) with PAs and 63 patients (53%) with WTs, while the validation group consisted of 28 patients (54%) with PAs and 24 patients (46%) with WTs (Table I).

Establishment of clinical models. The comparative results of clinical and imaging features between the PA group and the WT group in the training and validation sets are detailed in Table II. Univariate logistic regression analysis revealed statistically significant differences in cystic/necrotic/calcified conditions, smoking status, CT values, maximum diameter, age, and sex between the two lesion types in both the training and validation sets (all p<0.05). Establishment and performance evaluation of the combined model. After integrating the clinical model with the radiomic-deep learning score model, the AUC of the combined model in the training set was 0.968 (95%CI=0.939-0.998) (Figure 2A).

The nomogram indicates that the radiomic-deep learning score holds significant weight in distinguishing the risk levels between the PA group and the WT group (Figure 2G). Calibration curves (Figure 2C and D) demonstrate a good fit for the nomogram. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed that both the training and validation sets did not deviate from perfect goodness of fit (p=0.463 for the training set and p=0.796 for the validation set). DCA for each model (Figure 2E and F) indicates that the combined model provides better clinical benefits in differentiating between the pleomorphic adenoma group and the Warthin tumor group. Therefore, the combined model was selected for the subsequent establishment of multiple machine learning models.

Establishment of machine learning models. Utilizing the combined clinical-imaging features and deep-imaging features, seven classifiers were employed to construct machine learning models, and their performance was evaluated using ROC curves and AUC values (Figure 5A and B). A radar chart was utilized to depict the differences among these models across five key metrics: accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, precision, and F1 score (Figure 5C). Among the classifier models, XGBoost performed the best in the validation set, achieving an AUC of 0.961 (95%CI=0.916-1.000) (Figure 2B), and was therefore selected as the optimal output model for this study.

Discussion

Clinical significance and pathogenic factors. Surgical planning hinges on the 2%-25% malignant transformation risk associated with PAs, contrasted with the predominantly benign nature of WTs (13). Conventional imaging remains subjective, and the invasive nature of biopsies can cause patient harm (14). In our cohort, WTs showed a stark 7:1 male-to-female ratio and an older demographic, mirroring epidemiological trends (1, 15). The nexus between tobacco and WT is robust; historic data indicates that nearly 75% of WT patients are active smokers (15, 16). Mechanistically, smoke irritants likely induce glandular proliferation, delayed hypersensitivity, or oxidative stress-mediated mitochondrial DNA damage (17-21). Furthermore, our results confirm that CT values exceeding 45 HU strongly indicate WT (16). This density difference likely reflects the transition from the lymphoid-rich architecture of WT to the mucin-heavy stroma characteristic of PA (22).

Radiomics, ResNet-50, and model synergy. Radiomics transforms radiological pixels into a mineable, high-resolution data space (23). While recent studies have utilized deep learning for parotid segmentation and MRI-based classification (24-26), our CT-based approach utilizes the ResNet-50 framework to mitigate common training pitfalls like gradient vanishing or explosion (12, 27). The global average pooling layer within ResNet-50 effectively reduces parameter counts, thereby minimizing overfitting risks (27). By fusing handcrafted radiomics with deep transfer learning, we outperformed single-model architectures (12, 28, 29). This synergy integrates complementary information across diverse feature sets, capturing subtle phenotypic variations that elude visual assessment.

SHAP interpretability and trust. Clinical adoption of machine learning is often stymied by a lack of interpretability (30). Unlike traditional importance metrics, SHAP provides consistent global and local explanations, quantifying the weight of each predictor for specific patient outcomes (30, 31). In our analysis, the radiomics-DL score emerged as the paramount predictor (30). This transparency–visualized through feature contribution plots–allows clinicians to verify the model’s rationale (e.g., predicting WT based on advanced age and elevated CT values), bridging the gap between artificial intelligence and surgical intuition.

The promising results of our interpretable deep learning framework are consistent with recent advancements in various radiomics applications. For instance, deep learning and radiomics models have shown significant potential in predicting pathological complete response in breast cancer (32), predicting the growth of vestibular schwannomas (33), and evaluating treatment response in esophageal cancer (34, 35). These studies further validate that radiomics, combined with advanced learning algorithms, provides a robust and non-invasive tool for clinical decision-making and prognosis evaluation across different tumor types.

Study limitations. This study has limitations. First, the relatively small sample size from a single center precluded external validation. Second, the retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Finally, region-of-interest (ROI) segmentation was performed manually, which is labor-intensive; automated segmentation might improve future workflows.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we successfully developed an interpretable machine learning model. The study demonstrates that a combined model based on clinical signs, radiomics, and deep learning features outperforms other models in the differential diagnosis of WT and PA. Furthermore, by utilizing SHAP to explain model outputs, our approach offers high accuracy and interpretability, potentially serving as an important tool in clinical settings to assist physicians in formulating personalized medical plans.
