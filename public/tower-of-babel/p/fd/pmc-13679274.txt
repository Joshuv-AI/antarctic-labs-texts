Systematic review and meta-analysis of lung metastases incidence in cervical cancer patients
Authors: Kimia Pakdaman, Amin Esmaeilnia Shirvani, Kasra Pakdaman, Hossein-Ali Nikbakht, Mosua Yeminfroz, Pouyan Ebrahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer (CC) is a common and aggressive malignancy in women. The lung is its most frequent site of distant metastasis, yet the incidence and risk factors remain underexplored. This study aimed to estimate the incidence of lung metastases (LMs) in CC and characterize their clinical and pathological patterns.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar up to May 8, 2025, for studies reporting LMs incidence in CC patients. Eligible studies were assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) tool. Data were analyzed with STATA version 17 using a random-effects model. Pooled estimates were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe global pooled incidence of LMs in CC was 4.31% (95% CI: 3.25–5.37), with the highest regional rate observed in Africa (7.42%). Neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) demonstrated the highest histology-specific LMs incidence at 16.91% (95% CI: 11.06–22.76), markedly exceeding adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. LMs risk was substantially elevated in advanced stages, rising to 15.75% (95% CI: 4.92–26.58) in stage IV compared to 3.12% (95% CI: 1.74–4.51) in stage I, with an average post-metastasis survival of 32.56 months (95% CI: 7.57–57.54).ConclusionOur results show that the lung is a common site for metastasis in CC, often alongside liver and bone metastases. LMs risk is higher in neuroendocrine carcinoma and advanced stages. These findings can help improve prevention and treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16579-x.Keywords: Uterine Cervical Neoplasms, Lung Neoplasms, Neoplasm Metastasis, Incidence, Systematic review

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) is among the most common and aggressive malignancies in women, ranking second only to breast cancer in incidence [1]. It can invade surrounding tissues through multiple pathways, including direct extension, involvement of pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes, and hematogenous spread via cancer cells traveling through the bloodstream to distant organs [2, 3]. Typically, CC advances through local invasion and lymphatic dissemination; however, hematogenous metastasis plays a key role in cases of distant spread [4, 5]. Common sites for hematogenous metastases include the liver, lungs, and bones. Notably, hematogenous metastases are associated with a significantly poorer prognosis, with affected patients facing a 5.3-fold increased risk of death compared to those with only lymphatic involvement [6]. Approximately 15% of CC patients develop distant metastases during the course of their disease [7]. Unlike patients with localized disease who generally respond well to conventional treatments such as surgery and chemotherapy, those with metastatic CC lack standardized therapeutic protocols and have a poor prognosis [8]. Five-year survival rates stand at 91.5% for localized CC, compared to only 16.5% for metastatic cases [9].

The lung is the most common site of distant metastasis in CC [10]. Most patients with lung metastases (LMs) are asymptomatic, and these metastases are often detected incidentally during routine chest imaging, such as X-rays or computed tomography (CT) scans, in outpatient follow-up visits. Therefore, early identification of patients at high risk for LMs and timely intervention are crucial for effective treatment [11]. Management of CC patients with LMs typically involves surgical resection, chemotherapy, or radiotherapy, with a median survival time of approximately 18 months [8]. Pulmonary involvement can present as either a solitary pulmonary nodule or multiple metastases. In cases of a solitary lung nodule, distinguishing it from primary lung cancers and carcinoid tumors is of significant clinical importance [12].

Previous studies investigating the incidence of LMs in CC patients have generally reported rates below 5%. For example, Shan et al. estimated a LMs incidence of approximately 3.8% among over 12,000 CC patients using Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database data [13]. Similarly, Ki et al. reported a prevalence of 3.6% for LMs in this population [14]. Their study also demonstrated that patients with three or fewer metastatic lung lesions had significantly longer overall survival compared to those with more than three lesions (40 months versus 25 months). Given the relatively low reported incidence of LMs in CC, the clinical and pathological characteristics of CC patients with LMs have not been comprehensively studied across diverse populations.

Although conventional treatments for LMs in CC often have limited efficacy and the prognosis remains poor, data on the incidence and risk factors for this type of metastasis are still scarce and fragmented. Currently, there is no comprehensive study that thoroughly examines and characterizes the occurrence of LMs in CC patients across different regions worldwide. Therefore, the aim of this study is to estimate the incidence of LMs in CC patients and to describe the clinical and pathological features of those affected.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed and conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines [15] (PROSPERO 2025 registration code: CRD420251060348).

Search strategy and data sources

The search strategy for this study was developed through a multi-step process involving the formulation and refinement of keywords and search methods. This strategy employed a combination of controlled vocabulary derived from Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) in the PubMed database alongside specialized free-text keywords relevant to the topic. Key terms included “Uterine Cervical Neoplasms,” “Lung Neoplasms,” “Lung,” “Neoplasm Metastasis,” “Metastases,” and “Incidence” (Table S1). Subsequently, five major databases, PubMed/Medline, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar, were searched without any time or language restrictions up to May 8, 2025. Additionally, gray literature searches were conducted to identify unpublished or non-indexed studies.

Study selection process

Following the completion of the database searches, all retrieved articles were imported and organized using EndNote 2019 (version X9; Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA). Duplicate records were identified and removed using the “Find Duplicate” tool. Subsequently, two independent researchers screened the remaining articles based on the inclusion criteria, with any disagreements resolved by a third reviewer (the principal investigator). Only studies that clearly and explicitly reported the incidence of LMs in CC patients, providing specific data on the total number of patients and cases, were included in the analysis. Additionally, selected studies were required to present precise quantitative data on the incidence rate of LMs. Studies were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) focusing solely on stage IVb or M1 patients, (2) data derived from overlapping or duplicate databases, (3) failure to distinguish the primary gynecologic cancer type in LMs reporting, (4) narrative reviews, conference abstracts without extractable data, in vitro and in vivo laboratory studies, book chapters, case reports, and case series, and (5) articles without accessible full texts, where attempts to obtain them from authors were unsuccessful. Notably, several included studies were based on SEER (Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results) database data. Among these, only studies that met criteria for comprehensiveness, accuracy, time frame, and adherence to inclusion standards were selected to provide reliable information on the incidence of LMs in CC [16].

Data extraction and variables

After identifying eligible studies and obtaining their full-text PDFs, data extraction was independently performed by two researchers using Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Any discrepancies or uncertainties during data extraction were resolved by the principal investigator. The data extraction file included multiple variables such as: first author’s name, year of publication, data collection period, mean or range of patient ages, study design, country of origin, and geographic region categorized according to the World Health Organization’s six-region classification (Africa, Eastern Mediterranean, Europe, Southeast Asia, Americas, and Oceania) [17]. Additionally, the Human Development Index (HDI) and income level of the countries were recorded [18], along with cancer stage based on FIGO classification (stages I to IV) [19], and histological subtype of CC including squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), adenocarcinoma (ADC), and neuroendocrine carcinoma (NEC) [8, 9]. In addition, information on LMs was collected, including the number of single and multiple metastases and their total count, as well as concurrent involvement of other organs, as reported in the original articles. Clinical symptoms such as cough, hemoptysis, fever, dyspnea, and chest pain were also documented. The type of lung nodules (solitary versus multiple) was recorded. Survival-related variables were defined by two main indicators: (1) survival time from diagnosis of LMs to death, and (2) the interval between initial CC diagnosis and the occurrence of LMs [20, 21].

Quality appraisal (Risk of bias assessment)

To assess the quality of studies included in this systematic review, we utilized the validated ‘Checklist for Prevalence Studies’ developed by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI). This checklist consists of nine critical items evaluating methodological domains such as sampling strategy, sample size adequacy, validity of condition measurement, statistical analysis, and the clarity of reporting. Each item was scored as 1 if the criterion was clearly met and 0 if it was not met or unclear, yielding a total possible score ranging from 0 to 9. Based on the total score, studies were categorized as follows: high quality (low risk of bias) for scores of 7–9, moderate quality for scores of 5–6, and low quality (high risk of bias) for scores below 5. This scoring approach is consistent with established methodological guidance and has been widely applied in systematic reviews of prevalence and incidence data [22, 23].

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using STATA version 17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). To estimate the overall incidence of LMs in CC patients, a random-effects model based on the Restricted maximum likelihood method was applied; this approach accounts for clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies, enabling more accurate generalizability. Effect sizes were calculated using untransformed proportions and reported alongside 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I² statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [24, 25]. Forest plots were employed for visual inspection of result dispersion and pooled incidence estimates. Also, to explore potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted based on variables such as publication year, data collection start year, study country, income level, cancer histology, FIGO stage, and risk of bias. Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out method was performed to evaluate the robustness of the results. Meta-regression analyses were further carried out to assess the impact of continuous variables, including publication year, HDI, mean patient age, and data collection year, on incidence estimates. Concurrently, the incidence of primary findings such as clinical symptoms, metastasis patterns (lung-only versus with other organs), number of lung lesions, and patient survival status were examined. Meta-analysis of continuous outcomes was used to calculate mean survival time and interval from initial diagnosis to LMs [26, 27]. For studies reporting descriptive data as medians with ranges or interquartile ranges (IQR), or as case-by-case information, the “Meta-Analysis Accelerator” tool was utilized to estimate means and standard deviations required for meta-analysis [28]. Finally, publication bias was evaluated visually using funnel plots and statistically via Egger’s test, with p-values below 0.05 indicating significant bias [29]. Additionally, the trim-and-fill method was applied under a random-effects model to estimate the impact of potentially missing studies on the pooled effect size [30]. All analytic procedures were conducted in accordance with established guidelines for incidence meta-analyses to ensure accuracy, transparency, and reproducibility of findings [23].

Results

Study selection

Following the implementation of the predefined search strategies, a total of 3,797 article titles were identified through five major databases. After removing 820 duplicate records, 2,977 unique studies proceeded to the initial screening phase based solely on titles. During this step, 2,423 records were excluded due to obvious irrelevance, including studies on non-cervical cancers, metastases other than to the brain, and preclinical or basic science investigations. The remaining 554 studies were subjected to a second-level screening that considered both title and abstract content. In this stage, 512 articles were excluded for one or more of the following reasons: lack of brain metastasis data, focus on other cancers, being review or experimental studies, or fell outside the scope of the established inclusion criteria. As a result, 42 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. All full texts were accessible either directly or through university institutional access, and no study was excluded due to unavailability. Of these, 21 studies were excluded for reasons including duplicate data (n = 4), irrelevance to outcomes (n = 7), wrong population (n = 3), unsuitable study design (n = 3), lack of total sample size (n = 2), or focus on metastasis-only samples (n = 2). Ultimately, 21 studies that were methodologically and content-wise aligned with the objectives of this review were selected for the final analysis. Data from these studies, comprising a total of 65 records, were organized into a structured Excel file and utilized for statistical analyses (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics and quality appraisal

In this systematic review, 21 studies encompassing 75,399 CC patients with 2,662 reported LMs from diverse geographic regions and varying study designs were included in the final analysis. The data collection periods in these studies ranged from 1948 to 2022. Participating countries included the United States [20, 31–37], China [38–40], India [41, 42], Japan [43, 44], South Korea [35, 45], Iran [46], Turkey [21], Austria [47], South Africa [48], and Sri Lanka [49], reflecting broad geographic coverage across multiple continents. Except for one prospective study [42], all others were retrospective. Most studies assessed LMs without stratifying by specific cancer stage or histology. However, nine studies [20, 21, 33, 37, 38, 40, 42, 43, 49] and six studies [32, 34, 35, 39, 43, 49] specifically focused on stages I–IV and histological subtypes SCC, ADC, and NEC, respectively. Reported patient ages ranged from 19 to 88 years, with mean ages typically between 50 and 58 years. Sample sizes varied considerably, from 189 patients up to over 26,000. The number of reported LMs cases ranged from 5 to 1,058, indicating significant variability in prevalence and study design (Table 1).

Results of the Meta-analysis

Global incidence of LMs from patients with CC

Based on data from 21 studies including 75,399 patients, the global pooled incidence of LMs among CC patients was reported as 4.31% (95% CI: 3.25–5.37). The highest incidence was observed in the African region at 7.42% (95% CI: 6.02–8.82), while the lowest incidence was reported in Southeast Asia at 2.13% (95% CI: 0.41–3.85). Among individual countries, Austria had the highest reported incidence at 8.99% (95% CI: 4.92–13.07), and Sri Lanka the lowest at 1.83% (95% CI: 1.25–2.41). The Americas and Europe also exhibited relatively high incidences of LMs, with rates of 5.32% (95% CI: 3.55–7.08) and 6.45% (95% CI: 2.48–10.43), respectively (Table 2).

Subgroup meta-analysis of LMs incidence based on priori variables

In subgroup analyses, the highest incidence of LMs was observed in studies published before 1995, with a rate of 5.65% (95% CI: 3.89–7.40), whereas more recent studies (2011–2025) reported a lower incidence of 3.84% (95% CI: 2.83–4.86). Similarly, studies that began data collection before 1980 showed a higher incidence of 5.43% (95% CI: 3.51–7.35), compared to those starting in later decades, which had the lowest incidence at 3.51% (95% CI: 2.32–4.70). Regarding study quality, no significant differences were noted across high, moderate, and low risk-of-bias groups, with incidence rates remaining around 4% in all categories. However, when stratified by income level, high-income countries reported the highest incidence at 4.82% (95% CI: 3.27–6.37), while low- to middle-income countries reported the lowest at 2.13% (95% CI: 0.41–3.85) (Table 3).

Information of LMs patients (Co-occurrence, Symptoms, number of LMs, and survival outcomes)

Analysis of data from 11 studies revealed that the majority of patients (60.34%, 95% CI: 45.71–74.98) presented with multiple LMs or metastases involving other organs, while 39.66% (95% CI: 25.02–54.29) had solitary lung involvement. Among concurrent metastases, the liver was the most frequently involved site at 24.06% (95% CI: 11.26–36.85), followed by bone at 19.45% (95% CI: 9.44–29.45). The brain showed the lowest incidence at 2.04% (95% CI: 0.56–3.53). Regarding clinical symptoms, cough was the most common, reported in 63.24% (95% CI: 45.04–81.45) of patients, followed by dyspnea at 33.79% (95% CI: 23.96–43.63) and chest pain at 29.70% (95% CI: 1.57–57.84). The mean survival time following diagnosis of LMs was 32.56 months (95% CI: 7.57–57.54), while the interval from initial CC diagnosis to LMs occurrence was 36.34 months (95% CI: 30.24–42.44) (Table S3).

Subgroup analysis of pooled incidence of LMs based on stage and histology of CC

Analysis of LMs incidence by histological subtype of CC revealed that NEC exhibited the highest rate at 16.91% (95% CI: 11.06–22.76), compared to ADC at 4.63% (95% CI: 2.53–6.74) and SCC at 4.00% (95% CI: 2.83–5.17) (Fig. 2). Additionally, LMs incidence varied significantly across cancer stages, with stage IV patients showing a rate of 15.75% (95% CI: 4.92–26.58), nearly three times higher than stages II (5.13%, 95% CI: 3.04–7.22) and III (5.51%, 95% CI: 2.29–8.73), and approximately five times higher than stage I (3.12%, 95% CI: 1.74–4.51) (Fig. 3).

Meta regression of incidence of LMs

Meta-regression results showed no significant association between temporal, demographic, or development-related variables and the pooled incidence of LMs. Analysis of trends based on the start year of data collection (1948–2020) revealed a non-significant decrease in incidence (Regression Coefficient = − 0.036; 95% CI: − 0.092 to 0.019; p = 0.189). A similar non-significant trend was observed for publication year (1967–2024) (Regression Coefficient = − 0.035; 95% CI: − 0.099 to 0.029; p = 0.270). Evaluation of mean patient age also supported the absence of a significant relationship with incidence, as age increases from 49.15 to 58.90 years were associated with a very slight, non-significant upward trend (Regression Coefficient = 0.029; 95% CI: − 0.623 to 0.679; p = 0.915). Furthermore, analysis based on the HDI showed a non-significant positive association with incidence (Regression Coefficient = 7.495; 95% CI: − 4.890 to 19.879; p = 0.221) (Fig. 4).

Sensitivity analysis and Publication bias

Sensitivity analysis using the leave-one-out approach demonstrated that excluding any single study did not significantly alter the pooled incidence estimate of LMs. The overall estimate remained stable across all scenarios, with only minor fluctuations that did not affect the final analysis trend (Figure S1).

Publication bias was assessed both visually and statistically. As shown in the funnel plot (Figure S2), the asymmetric distribution of studies around the pooled estimate suggests the presence of publication bias. This was further supported by Egger’s regression test, which revealed significant small-study effects (bias = 3.84, SE = 0.943, p < 0.001). To evaluate the potential impact of this bias on the pooled estimate, we conducted a further analysis using Duval and Tweedie’s trim-and-fill method. As shown in Figure S3, no additional studies were imputed on either side of the funnel plot, and the pooled effect size remained unchanged (observed = 4.309; observed + imputed = 4.309; 95% CI: 3.252–5.366). This suggests that, despite evidence of publication bias, the primary meta-analytic results are likely robust and not substantially influenced by missing studies (Figure S3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis involving 75,399 CC patients found that the incidence of LMs was 4.31%. Moreover, over half of these LMs were either multiple lung lesions or accompanied by metastases in other organs, with the (24.06%) and bone (19.45%) being the most common concurrent sites. Chen et al. conducted a study using data from the United States’ SEER cancer database [11] involving 19,377 CC patients between 2010 and 2015, reporting LMs in 4.33% of cases. Their univariate and multivariate analyses revealed a strong association between LMs and the presence of liver and bone metastases; multivariate analysis showed that patients with liver metastases had approximately an eightfold increased risk, and those with bone metastases had about a fourfold increased risk of developing LMs. Also, Henglian et al. investigated LMs following laparoscopic radical hysterectomy (LRH) in CC patients with clinical stages IA1 to IIA2 in China [39]. Among 2,149 patients treated between 2007 and 2015, LMs occurred in 3.39%. Another study by Ki et al. in South Korea reported a 3.6% incidence of LMs among CC patients treated from 1990 to 2014 [14]. Zhou et al. analyzed metastatic site patterns in 1,347 metastatic CC patients using the SEER database [50]. In this study, approximately 40% of LMs cases occurred alongside metastases to other sites. Among these, the most common two-site metastases involved the lung and liver, followed by the lung and bone. The next most frequent multi-organ metastatic pattern was three-site metastases involving the lung, liver, and bone. One possible explanation for the higher incidence of LMs compared to other organs in CC is that the lung microenvironment provides a favorable niche for the survival and proliferation of cancer cells, while CC cells adapt through cellular changes to facilitate metastasis to the lung [51]. Metastases to the lung, liver, and bone all occur via hematogenous spread [8]. Since many patients do not report lung symptoms at the time LMs are diagnosed [14, 42], it is likely that by the time LMs are detected on routine follow-up imaging, hematogenous spread to other organs has already occurred, explaining the high rate of concurrent metastases in other organs among patients with lung involvement. Our study also found that the reported incidence of LMs in CC tended to be higher in countries with greater development indices, although this trend was not statistically significant. This finding is likely due to better access to diagnostic facilities, more rigorous follow-up protocols, and more comprehensive patient registry systems in developed countries [52]. It should be also noted that comparisons across studies should be approached cautiously due to potential differences in population demographics and healthcare infrastructure that may contribute to between-study variability. Interestingly, we also observed that the highest pooled incidence of LMs was reported from African countries (7.42%), despite only one study from this region being included in the meta-analysis. This finding should be interpreted with considerable caution, as it may be influenced by the limited number of studies, smaller sample sizes, and differences in diagnostic, staging, and reporting practices across regions. Furthermore, in low-resource settings, limited access to imaging modalities such as CT scans or PET may result in underdiagnosis in routine practice, whereas in selected tertiary centers conducting research, detection rates may be relatively inflated. In addition, the burden of comorbidities such as HIV, which is highly prevalent in many parts of Africa, may accelerate cancer progression and metastatic behavior, including to the lungs. Previous studies have reported a possible link between HIV-associated immunosuppression and increased metastatic potential in CC patients [53, 54]. These findings highlight the urgent need for more epidemiological and clinical research from underrepresented regions, particularly low- and middle-income countries, to improve the global understanding of metastatic patterns and to reduce geographic bias in pooled estimates.

Histologically, NEC of the cervix exhibits the highest incidence of LMs, with 16.91%. In contrast, LMs rates for ADC and SCC types are approximately 4%. Neuroendocrine tumors of the cervix tend to invade lymph nodes earlier than ADC and SCC, leading to earlier clinical presentations with both local and distant metastases [55]. Also, these findings, while informative, may be influenced by study-specific biases and limited case numbers in rare histological subtypes, contributing to heterogeneity in reported rates. A study by Park et al. analyzed and compared metastasis patterns of NEC versus other tumor types using SEER data from 2000 to 2019 (34). Their analysis showed that at diagnosis, metastasis incidence was significantly higher in NEC (43%) compared to SCC (13%) and ADC (10%). Among metastatic cases, LMs incidence ranged from 30% to 40% across all three histologies, indicating that while NEC has a higher overall metastatic tendency, this is not exclusively limited to LMs. Another study by Li et al. reviewed SEER data from 2000 to 2018 focusing on NEC of the cervix [56], reporting that 56% of 142 metastatic cases involved the lungs. The aggressive nature of neuroendocrine cervical tumors resembles that of small cell lung carcinoma, which is often diagnosed at advanced or metastatic stages [57]. Given that neuroendocrine histology accounts for only about 1% of CC cases, fewer studies have focused on optimizing treatment for this subtype compared to the more common ADC and SCC [58]. These factors contribute to the increased incidence of LMs observed in neuroendocrine cervical carcinoma.

Our study demonstrated that stage IV CC has the highest incidence of LMs, with 15.75%. LMs rates in stages II and III were approximately 5%, while stage I showed about 3%. Gulseren et al. examined LMs patterns in 890 CC patients in Turkey between 1991 and 2017 [21]. Their study reported LMs rates below 10% for stages I to III, whereas stage IVB patients exhibited a 50% incidence (6 metastases among 12 patients). Another study by Tellis et al. reviewed 243 CC patients at a U.S. hospital and reported 22 cases of LMs [37]. The incidence of LMs was 57% in stage IV patients, compared to only 4% in stage I. Similarly, Gunasekera et al. studied 2,075 CC patients in Sri Lanka, finding a 10% LMs rate in stage IV patients, while stages II and III had significantly lower rates between 1% and 2%, and no LMs were observed in stage I patients [49]. These findings consistently highlight the significantly higher LMs rates in advanced CC, particularly stage IV. In advanced stages, factors such as increased tumor burden, more aggressive tumor cell behavior, and compromised immune function facilitate the hematogenous spread of cancer cells to organs including the lungs [59]. Nonetheless, variability in staging definitions and diagnostic workup across studies introduces potential heterogeneity that should be considered when interpreting these stage-specific estimates.

Although this systematic review and meta-analysis provides a comprehensive evaluation of LMs in CC patients and their clinicopathological findings, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. Most included studies originated from countries with high development indices, which may bias the findings toward data from these regions. For instance, only one study each from the African and Eastern Mediterranean regions was included, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions about these areas. Another limitation of our meta-analysis is the scarcity of survival-related metrics. Only two studies reported mean and standard deviation for overall survival and diagnostic-to-metastasis intervals, precluding a robust pooled survival analysis or computation of hazard ratios. This underscores the need for future longitudinal cohort studies with time-to-event data and standardized outcome reporting to establish the prognostic implications of LMs in CC. Also, the presence of publication bias, as evidenced by Egger’s test and funnel plot asymmetry, suggests the possibility of overestimation in our pooled incidence estimate. Although the Trim-and-Fill method indicated a minor downward adjustment, the results should be interpreted cautiously due to potential limitations in the method itself, especially in the context of heterogeneity. In addition, significant heterogeneity among the included studies, stemming from variations in study design, sample size, population characteristics, and data reporting, limits the generalizability of the findings and necessitates cautious interpretation. Moreover, the lack of detailed demographic data in several of the included studies, particularly regarding age, ethnicity, HIV status, and comorbidities, limited our ability to perform patient-level subgroup analyses or assess how these variables might influence the incidence and patterns of LMs. This gap also constrains the interpretation of observed heterogeneity and underscores the need for more comprehensive reporting in future research.

Conclusion

The findings of this study indicate that the lung is a common site of metastasis in patients with CC and is frequently involved alongside metastases to other organs such as the liver and bone. Our results also highlight the importance of active surveillance for LMs in high-risk groups, including patients with NEC of the cervix and those with advanced disease stages. These findings may inform the development of more effective prevention and treatment strategies in the future.
