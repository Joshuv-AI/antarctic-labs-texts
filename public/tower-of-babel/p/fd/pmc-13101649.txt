Sarcopenia in Oncological Patients Diagnostics, Screening, and Impact on Quality of Life, Fatigue, Handgrip Strength, and Physical Activity (Sarco‐Detect Study)
Authors: Friederike Hilpert, Lisa Jochem, Lea Feline Kraack, Ilka Ratjen, Friederike Stölzel, Thore Wulff, Anne Letsch, Thorsten Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe main objective of the Sarco‐Detect study was to evaluate diagnostic tools—bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and handgrip strength (HGS)—for determining sarcopenia compared with computed tomography (CT). The secondary aim was to evaluate the SARC‐F questionnaire as a screening tool and to investigate the impact of muscle loss on quality of life, fatigue, HGS, and the association between previous physical activity and muscle loss.MethodsThe study was conducted at the University Cancer Center Schleswig‐Holstein. To assess muscle mass status, BIA and HGS were measured in patients with cancer and compared with CT. Additionally, EORTC QLQ‐C30, FACIT‐Fatigue Scale, BSA, and SARC‐F questionnaires were administered.ResultsOne hundred thirty one patients were recruited. Fourteen patients showed a CT‐SMI below the reference values by Van der Werf et al. (2018). Thirteen patients showed reduced HGS. CT‐SMI and HGS correlated significantly (r = 0.492, p = 0.001). The phase angle of the BIA correlated significantly with CT‐SMI (r = 0.441, p = 0.001) and HGS (r = 0.493, p = 0.001). Patients with reduced HGS presented significantly higher appetite loss compared with patients with preserved HGS. Patients with preserved HGS showed a higher quality of life than patients with reduced HGS. FACIT‐Fatigue scores differed significantly between patients with reduced HGS and those with preserved HGS (F(1, 104) = 3.991, p = 0.048; U = 321.000, Z = −2.088, p = 0.037). Activity index correlated moderately with fatigue symptoms and quality of life. The SARC‐F showed a sensitivity of 18.8% and specificity of 96.6% for HGS, and a sensitivity of 31.3% and specificity of 90.7% for CT‐SMI.ConclusionsCT‐SMI and HGS correlated positively and significantly. The BIA phase angle correlated with CT‐SMI and HGS. Reduced HGS is associated with increased appetite loss, higher fatigue, and lower quality of life. SARC‐F questionnaire appears suitable for identifying patients without sarcopenia.Trial RegistrationGerman Clinical Trials Register: DRKS00035258

Introduction

Sarcopenia (Definition, Diagnostic Tools)

Sarcopenia is a muscle disease defined by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP2, 2018) as a combination of low skeletal muscle strength and low skeletal muscle mass or quality [1]. The decline in skeletal muscle mass, in addition to imposing an economic burden on the healthcare system and generating societal costs, is associated with increased mortality among affected individuals [1]. Particularly in vulnerable groups such as cancer patients, the diagnosis of sarcopenia is associated with an increased risk of adverse prognostic outcomes and negatively affects both overall survival and progression‐free survival [1, 2, 3, 4]. The current gold standard for diagnosing sarcopenia is computed tomography (CT), which is associated with high costs and substantial time and personnel requirements [1]. Dynapenia describes the loss of muscle strength and can result from malignant disease [5, 6]. Implementing other noninvasive, low‐cost, and easy‐to‐use methods, such as bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and the measurement of handgrip strength (HGS), could facilitate earlier and more regular estimation of body composition, thereby promoting the introduction of preventive measures such as nutritional support and strength training [1, 7, 8].

Quality of Life and Fatigue

Reduced muscle mass and its potential negative effects on health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) and fatigue have become increasingly important to understand [2, 9]. In recent years, sarcopenia in cancer patients has been shown to be associated with reduced quality of life and the occurrence of fatigue, making it a critical factor to address in cancer treatment [10, 11]. Regarding muscle strength, lower HGS has been linked to reduced HRQOL [12, 13]. The European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Core Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ‐C30) version 3.0 questionnaire was developed by the European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) to evaluate quality of life and related factors in cancer patients. Its reliability and validity were first tested in 305 patients with nonresectable lung cancer in 1993 [14]. Currently, it represents one of the most widely used standard instruments for assessing quality of life and symptom severity in cancer patients [15]. The FACIT‐Fatigue Scale questionnaire, published by the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (FACIT), aims to evaluate the level of fatigue experienced during the previous week among cancer patients [16, 17]. Therefore, further investigation of self‐assessed HRQOL and fatigue status in oncological patients could help improve and implement supportive treatment options [18].

Physical and Sports Activity

The importance of physical activity for maintaining a healthy life has been established by numerous studies. Reduced physical activity, among other factors, is associated with an increased risk of developing sarcopenia [19]. Secondary sarcopenia due to cancer, in particular, can negatively affect mortality, lead to a higher likelihood of complications, and reduce tolerance to the side effects of chemotherapy [4, 20, 21, 22]. To accurately estimate the physical activity levels of individuals, numerous questionnaires have been proposed and reviewed [23, 24]. A validated self‐assessment tool in German‐speaking regions is the BSA questionnaire [25]. It aims to assess physical activity using the Frequency–Intensity–Time–Type (FITT) method [26]. Physical therapy and strength training have been shown to support the treatment of sarcopenia [27, 28]. These interventions may also be beneficial for sarcopenic cancer patients and highlight the need for further research. In this study, physical activity will be examined in relation to existing muscle mass and strength. Furthermore, it will be investigated whether there is a positive correlation between quality of life and fatigue symptoms in relation to the respective muscle mass and strength.

Screening for Sarcopenia

To prevent the abovementioned adverse effects of sarcopenia, a reliable and accurate screening tool could be advantageous. The EWGSOP2 recommends the SARC‐F, a short five‐question self‐assessment instrument that has been tested worldwide [1, 29, 30]. Previous studies have shown that the SARC‐F can be beneficial for identifying patients with severe sarcopenia because of its relatively low sensitivity and high specificity [31, 32]. To further contribute to the existing evidence and validate the SARC‐F, the questionnaire was included to screen for sarcopenia in this oncological cohort in Kiel, northern Germany. The efficiency and accuracy of the diagnostic pathway can be improved by using a valid screening tool [33]. This study aims to evaluate the questionnaire in an oncological cohort to assess its suitability for routine use and to compare the results with those of previous studies (oncological and non‐oncological), thereby complementing the current body of research.

In view of the above, this research project aimed to investigate muscle mass status using two diagnostic tools. A further aim was to determine the reliability of a screening measure and to explore the relationship between a low skeletal muscle index (CT‐SMI) and reduced HGS with quality of life, fatigue, and previous physical activity. A study that focuses on all the aspects mentioned above could prove beneficial for establishing supportive interventions in the form of physical or psychological therapy, as well as nutritional support for oncological patients.

Materials and Methods

Ethics Statement

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Clinical Studies of the Medical Faculty of the Christian‐Albrechts‐University of Kiel (approval number AZ D613/22). The study was registered in the German Clinical Trials Register under the number DRKS00035258. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients after a detailed verbal explanation of the study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and a report presenting the results and conclusions will be submitted to the Ethics Committee.

Study Design

The Sarco‐Detect study was conducted as a cross‐sectional study using a convenience sample at the interdisciplinary outpatient clinic of the University Cancer Center Schleswig‐Holstein (UCCSH) in Kiel between January and May 2023 (Monday to Friday). A convenience sample was used due to the structural conditions of the interdisciplinary outpatient clinic. A risk of selection bias may have occurred due to the chosen convenience sampling design.

Participants and Inclusion Criteria

Oncological patients (≥ 18 years) who had received, were currently receiving, or were scheduled to receive treatment at the UCCSH outpatient clinic were included in this study. Additional inclusion criteria were the availability of a CT image at the level of the third lumbar vertebra that was no older than 28 days at the time of the BIA measurement to ensure comparability between the two methods. Exclusion criteria included electronic implants, active prostheses, connection to portable electronic medical devices, and pregnancy, as these conditions could pose potential risks or cause significant inaccuracies in BIA measurements. Interested patients received an information sheet containing all relevant details about the study, and any questions were addressed verbally by the study staff. The proportion of patients who declined participation after receiving sufficient information about the study was not recorded. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained before the beginning of data collection. As long as the required inclusion criteria were met, the informed consent form was signed, and the extent of therapy was not affected by participation, patients in the hematological outpatient clinic were selected using a convenience sample. A homogeneous level of participation in the questionnaires was expected. In the case of heterogeneous participation levels, separate analyses for each subgroup will be conducted.

Computed Tomography

According to the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2) guidelines, CT imaging is considered the gold standard for diagnosing sarcopenia. Therefore, in this study, CT imaging was used as the reference method for detecting the loss of skeletal muscle mass [1]. CT images in the axial plane at the level of the third lumbar vertebra, with fully visible transverse processes, were selected. To correctly identify areas of skeletal muscle mass, a Hounsfield unit range from −29 to +150 HU was applied according to van der Werf et al. [34]. Using the brush tool option, skeletal muscle was then manually selected, and the skeletal muscle cross‐sectional area (SMA) in cm2 was calculated. The height‐normalized skeletal muscle index (SMI) in cm2/m2 was estimated by dividing the SMA by the square of body height. The SMI was compared with gender‐, age‐, and BMI‐specific cutoff values provided by the study of van der Werf et al. [34].

Handgrip Strength

To assess muscle strength, HGS was measured. According to Cruz‐Jentoft et al. (2019), lower grip strength is associated with poorer outcomes, longer hospital stays, and reduced quality of life. The hand dynamometer SH5002 (Saehan, 23–29 Bongamgongdan 13‐gil, Republic of Korea) was used according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The dynamometer was positioned so that, for each participant, the second finger joint was at the level of the handle. Grip strength was then gradually and uniformly increased without sudden shocks or abrupt changes in movement. To avoid an increase in intrathoracic pressure, patients were advised to exhale deeply during the measurement. Measurements were obtained while the patients were seated, with three measurements for each patient and a pause of at least 30 s between measurements. Only the maximum recorded value was used for analysis. As evaluation criteria, cutoff values recommended by the EWGSOP2 and reported by Dodds et al. [14] were used. Reduced HGS was defined as values of < 16 kg for women and < 27 kg for men [1].

Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis

BIA allows for a noninvasive and accurate measurement of body composition. In this study, the medical Body Composition Analyzer 525 from the company Seca was used. In addition to assessing overall body composition, the phase angle can also be directly determined [35]. During BIA measurement, a weak alternating current is passed through the body, allowing total body resistance, also known as impedance, to be determined at the skin electrodes. Impedance is composed of the resistance of electrolyte‐containing fluids (resistance, R) and the resistance generated by the cell membrane (reactance, Xc). At the cell membrane, a time delay occurs between the applied current and the voltage. This phase shift can be measured in degrees and expressed as the phase angle. The phase angle is also described as a marker of vitality and provides insight into the health and nutritional status of body cells [36]. A low phase angle indicates that a larger proportion of the measured current flows through the extracellular mass, which may be caused by factors such as vitamin and nutrient deficiencies or muscle loss.

Questionnaires

The questionnaires assessing quality of life, fatigue, and physical activity were distributed in person and were either returned directly after completion or sent back by mail. The screening questionnaire was distributed and returned by mail or administered by telephone.

European Organization for Research and Treatment of Cancer Core Quality of Life Questionnaire (EORTC QLQ‐C30)

The questionnaire consists of a total of 30 items, including five multi‐item functional scales (physical, role, social, emotional, and cognitive functioning), three multi‐item symptom scales (fatigue, pain, and nausea), and five single‐item symptom scales (dyspnea, insomnia, appetite loss, constipation, and diarrhea). The last two questions assess global health status/quality of life. The questionnaire is designed as a self‐assessment regarding functioning and symptoms during the previous week. The first 28 questions are answered on a scale ranging from “1 = not at all” to “4 = very much,” with the intermediate options “2 = a little” and “3 = quite a bit.” The quality of life questions range from “1 = very poor” to “7 = excellent.” The EORTC provides a scoring manual that converts the responses to a linear scale ranging from 0 to 100. High scores on the functional scales indicate a high level of functioning, whereas high scores on the symptom scales indicate greater symptom severity. Missing data were handled according to the scoring manual [37]. To compare the results, the study by Waldmann et al. was used as a reference, based on the clinical significance of differences in mean values determined by Cocks et al. [38, 39].

FACIT‐Fatigue Scale (FACIT‐Fatigue)

The FACIT‐F is a 13‐item self‐assessment questionnaire developed by the FACIT. It was designed to assess quality of life and fatigue during daily activities over the previous 7 days [16]. The response options range from “0 = not at all” to “4 = very much,” with the intermediate options “1 = a little bit,” “2 = somewhat,” and “3 = quite a bit.” According to the FACIT‐Fatigue scoring guidelines, the responses are transformed to a linear scale ranging from 0 to 52, where higher scores represent higher quality of life and lower levels of fatigue [40]. A cutoff value below 34 indicates the presence of fatigue [41]. Piper and Cella [42] determined that a score of 30 or less is clinically significant for fatigue. Tennant et al. [17] reached the same conclusion, indicating that a score below 30 represents severe fatigue.

BSA‐F

To assess physical activity, the questionnaire developed by Fuchs et al. (2015) was used. The questionnaire includes nine questions assessing work‐related physical activity, the exercise index for leisure time, and the sports index, each reported in minutes of exercise per week [25]. Due to incomplete responses to the questions, an adequate assessment of weekly exercise minutes according to the American College of Sports Medicine recommendations for oncological patients was not possible [43]. For improved evaluation, a separate activity index was created consisting of reported sporting activity over the last 5 years, over the entire life span, and over the last 4 weeks. The evaluation of this index yields scores between three and six, with six indicating consistent subjective sports activity across all life phases and three indicating consistent inactivity.

SARC‐F

To screen for sarcopenia, the EWGSOP2 recommends the use of the SARC‐F questionnaire to efficiently guide further diagnostic evaluation [1]. The questionnaire consists of five questions, each with three response options assessing the level of limitations in daily life. The questions relate to carrying five kilograms of weight, walking on level ground, rising from a chair or bed, climbing 10 stairs, and the history of falls during the past year. The response categories are “no difficulty,” “some difficulty,” and “a lot of difficulty/unable.” The number of falls is categorized as zero, one to three, and four or more falls [44]. A total score is calculated from the responses, with a maximum of 10 points; a score of four points or higher suggests the need for further diagnostic evaluation [29].

Statistical Analysis

Before the study began, the study protocol was discussed with the Institute for Medical Statistics at Kiel University. The study was planned and conducted as a feasibility study. In consultation with the Institute for Medical Statistics, it was determined that calculating an exact sample size was not necessary. It was recommended that the cohort size be determined based on case numbers reported in comparable studies in the literature and the time frame planned for the study. The statistical analysis was performed using Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics for Macintosh, Version 29.0.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The mean ± standard deviation (SD), minimum, and maximum values are presented for HGS and CT‐SMI. Correlations between variables were determined using Spearman's and Pearson's correlation analyses. A normal distribution was assumed based on the sample size and the central limit theorem and was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests, which confirmed a normal distribution. To determine whether muscle loss had an impact on quality of life and fatigue, a one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. Levene's test was conducted in advance to evaluate the homogeneity of variance so that the Welch test could be applied if necessary. Nonparametric testing using the Mann–Whitney U test was subsequently performed to confirm the results of the parametric analyses. All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. A result of p < 0.01 was considered highly significant, and a result of p < 0.001 was considered very highly significant. Due to variability in sample sizes, these are reported in the Results section.

Results

Study Population

As the present study was designed as a feasibility study aimed at the exploratory assessment of acceptability and effectiveness, no a priori sample size calculation or formal power analysis was performed. A total of 131 individuals were recruited, of whom 115 returned the questionnaires. Due to varying response rates for each questionnaire and missing data, the appropriate sample size (n) required for further analyses was first determined. This resulted in different sample sizes for each assessment method and could not have been anticipated. Of the 115 patients who returned the questionnaires, 114 patients presented a valid HGS result, and the CT images of 102 patients met the required criteria, resulting in a valid CT‐SMI. Due to logistical limitations and the availability of the BIA device, BIA measurements could only be performed in 67 patients (Figure 1).

Computed Tomography

CT images from 102 patients (40 female, 62 male) were evaluated. The mean age was 66.67 ± 11.07 years, ranging from 24 to 85 years. The mean BMI was 25.54 ± 4.64 kg/m2, with a minimum of 15.04 kg/m2 and a maximum of 38.16 kg/m2. Of the 102 CT images, seven were acquired in the native phase, 16 in the arterial phase, and 79 in the venous contrast phase. The mean CT‐SMI for the entire cohort was 43.65 ± 7.73 cm2/m2. The mean CT‐SMI for men was approximately 14% higher than that of women (45.90 ± 7.17 cm2/m2 vs. 40.18 ± 7.36 cm2/m2). According to van der Werf et al., the average value for men corresponds to the 10th percentile of the healthy male Caucasian population aged 20–82 years. The mean CT‐SMI for women was close to the 50th percentile.

Fourteen patients (2 female, 12 male) presented CT‐SMI results below the reference values established by van der Werf et al. These patients had an average age of 68.07 ± 7.33 years and a mean BMI of 26.23 ± 5.25 kg/m2. The average CT‐SMI was 36.97 ± 7.14 cm2/m2, with a mean HGS of 32.31 ± 8.38 kg. Two men fell below both the CT‐SMI and HGS reference values and could therefore be classified as sarcopenic. The mean deviation from the age‐ and BMI‐specific reference values was 6.66 ± 6.41 cm2/m2 (women: 8.29 ± 5.68 cm2/m2; men: 5.51 ± 6.66 cm2/m2). A significant correlation between CT‐SMI and HGS was observed (r = 0.492, p = 0.001). Similarly, a significant correlation was observed between CT‐SMI and the phase angle (r = 0.441, p = 0.001) (Figures 3 and 4, Tables 1 and 2).

Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis

BIA was performed in 67 patients (29 female, 38 male). The cohort showed a mean age of 66.01 ± 11.71 years and a mean BMI of 25.76 ± 4.45 kg/m2 and did not differ from the overall cohort. The mean phase angle was 4.87° ± 0.82°, with a minimum of 1.6° and a maximum of 6.4°. The mean phase angle for women was 4.62° ± 0.88°, which was lower than that for men, who had an average phase angle of 5.07° ± 0.72°. Of the 67 measured individuals, 61 were below the reference values established by Bosy‐Westphal et al. (27 female, 34 male) [40]. Further evaluation of the BIA results revealed a significant correlation between the measured phase angle and HGS (r = 0.493, p = 0.001) (Figure 5).

EORTC QLQ‐C30

For each category of the EORTC QLQ‐C30 questionnaire, a different sample size was available. In the categories of physical functioning, fatigue, nausea and vomiting, and pain, 110 questionnaires were returned. A total of 109 questionnaires were returned for role functioning and insomnia, 107 for emotional functioning, constipation, diarrhea, and cognitive functioning, 106 for social functioning, 108 for dyspnea and appetite loss, and 104 for financial difficulties and global health status. For each scale, the lowest possible score was 0 and the highest possible score was 100. The lowest mean score, 11.52 ± 22.09, occurred in the “nausea and vomiting” scale. The highest mean score, 79.12 ± 25.61, was observed in the “cognitive functioning” scale. The mean scores in the functioning scales were “physical functioning” (64.79 ± 24.80), “role functioning” (51.53 ± 36.05), “emotional functioning” (65.29 ± 26.32), and “social functioning” (53.30 ± 35.51). In the symptom scales, the following mean scores were observed: “fatigue” (49.85 ± 28.70), “pain” (29.85 ± 32.02), “dyspnea” (37.96 ± 36.02), “insomnia” (32.11 ± 33.00), “appetite loss” (28.40 ± 36.69), “constipation” (17.76 ± 30.14), “diarrhea” (16.51 ± 27.23), and “financial difficulties” (18.91 ± 30.38). The mean score for global health status/quality of life was 50.64 ± 21.55.

EORTC QLQ‐C30 Results Compared With Reference Studies

To estimate how much the results of this oncological cohort differed from those of the general population, reference data from Waldmann et al. [39] and Schwarz and Hinz [45] were used. Waldmann et al. [39] provided age‐ and gender‐adjusted reference values for the general population in Germany. As an additional reference, cohort data from Schwarz and Hinz derived from responses of the general population in Germany were used [45]. To determine clinical significance, moderate mean differences (in points) defined by Cocks et al. [38] were applied. Compared with the general female population reported by Waldmann et al. [39], female oncological patients presented significantly greater limitations in the scores for “physical functioning” (62.18 ± 26.13 vs. 77.00), “role functioning” (49.20 ± 39.26 vs. 73.00), and “social functioning” (52.48 ± 35.88 vs. 79.20). These patients also showed significantly higher symptom scores for “fatigue” (51.22 ± 28.53 vs. 34.30), “nausea and vomiting” (16.26 ± 28.26 vs. 4.50), “dyspnea” (34.19 ± 35.45 vs. 22.40), “appetite loss” (34.17 ± 38.85 vs. 9.20), and “diarrhea” (24.79 ± 30.32 vs. 9.60), as well as an overall lower quality of life (48.91 ± 20.60 vs. 69.20). Male oncological patients presented clinically significantly greater limitations in the scores for “physical functioning” (66.33 ± 24.03 vs. 81.00), “role functioning” (52.94 ± 34.20 vs. 75.70), and “social functioning” (53.80 ± 35.55 vs. 79.70) compared with the male general population reported by Waldmann et al. [39]. These male patients also showed significantly higher symptom scores for “fatigue” (49.03 ± 28.98 vs. 30.70), “dyspnea” (40.10 ± 36.42 vs. 21.80), “appetite loss” (25.00 ± 35.21 vs. 7.80), and “constipation” (20.59 ± 32.08 vs. 8.30), as well as an overall lower quality of life (51.15 ± 21.99 vs. 64.70) (Figures 6 and 7).

EORTC QLQ‐C30 and CT‐SMI

No significant correlations (Spearman's) were found between the EORTC QLQ‐C30 scores and CT‐SMI. To determine whether patients with a CT‐SMI below their reference value showed significant differences in the functioning and symptom scales compared with those whose CT‐SMI was above the reference value, a one‐way ANOVA was performed. The scales “constipation” (p = 0.033) and “diarrhea” (p < 0.001) showed significant results in Levene's test; therefore, Welch's test was additionally applied. The ANOVA revealed no significant results in any of the scales. Welch's test identified a significant difference for the scale “diarrhea” (t = −3.081, df = 43.657, p = 0.004, n = 94). The cohort with a CT‐SMI above the reference value (n = 80) presented more frequent diarrhea than the cohort with reduced CT‐SMI (n = 14) (18.75 ± 28.48 vs. 4.76 ± 12.11). Nonparametric testing using the Mann–Whitney U test showed no significant differences.

EORTC QLQ‐C30 and HGS

The scale “appetite loss” (Spearman's ρ = −0.227, p = 0.019) and “global health status/quality of life” (Spearman's ρ = 0.233, p = 0.018) showed weak correlations with HGS according to Cohen (1988) [46]. All other scales showed no significant correlations. To determine differences between the EORTC QLQ‐C30 scores of patients with HGS below and above the reference value, a one‐way ANOVA was performed. Levene's test showed no significant result. Welch's test was additionally performed because of its robustness. Patients with HGS below the reference value presented significantly higher appetite loss compared with patients whose HGS was preserved (54.55 ± 40.20 vs. 25.69 ± 35.37, F(1,105) = 6.389, p = 0.013, n = 107). Patients with preserved HGS showed a higher quality of life than patients with reduced HGS (52.90 ± 20.98 vs. 29.55 ± 13.62, F(1,101) = 12.923, p < 0.001, n = 103). The Mann–Whitney U test confirmed the results of the ANOVA and Welch's test. A normal distribution was present in both groups according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (appetite loss: p = 0.089; global health status/quality of life: p = 0.220). A significant difference in “appetite loss” was found between the group with preserved HGS (Mdn = 0.00) and the group with reduced HGS (Mdn = 66.67) (U = 320.000, Z = −2.353, p = 0.019, r = −0.227). The quality of life of patients with reduced HGS (Mdn = 33.33) was significantly lower than that of patients with preserved HGS (Mdn = 50.00) (U = 182.500, Z = −3.484, p < 0.001, r = 0.343).

FACIT‐Fatigue

Of the 115 returned questionnaires, 107 (40 female, 67 male) could be evaluated further. The lowest achieved score was 1, and the highest achieved score was 52. The mean score of the cohort was 33.29 ± 12.55. The mean score of the male group was slightly higher (33.86 ± 12.61) than that of the female group (32.35 ± 12.57). Fifty‐five patients (21 female, 34 male) had a score below 34 and were therefore classified as presenting fatigue, whereas 52 patients (19 female, 33 male) achieved a score above 34 and were therefore classified as not presenting fatigue according to the cutoff value proposed by Van Belle et al. [41]. According to the report by Tennant et al. [17], a score below 30 indicates severe fatigue. Thirty‐nine patients (15 female, 24 male) had a score of 30 or below, indicating severe fatigue, whereas 16 patients (6 female, 10 male) had a score between 30 and 34. When compared with the normative percentiles reported by Montan et al. [47], 83 patients (31 female, 52 male) presented a FACIT‐Fatigue Scale score below their age‐ and gender‐specific percentile, indicating higher fatigue and lower quality of life than their peers. In contrast, 24 patients (9 female, 15 male) showed a FACIT‐Fatigue Scale score above their age‐ and gender‐specific percentile, indicating lower fatigue and higher quality of life compared with their peers.

FACIT‐Fatigue and CT‐SMI

No significant correlation was found between the FACIT‐Fatigue Scale and CT‐SMI (Spearman's ρ = −0.092, p = 0.380). The mean FACIT‐Fatigue score of patients with CT‐SMI above the reference value was higher than that of patients with CT‐SMI below the reference value (33.85 ± 12.53 vs. 29.07 ± 13.42; n = 94). The female cohort with reduced CT‐SMI showed a lower mean score than females with CT‐SMI within the normal range (18.50 ± 19.09 vs. 33.20 ± 11.62; n = 35). Similarly, the male cohort with CT‐SMI above the reference value presented a higher mean score than males with CT‐SMI below the reference value (34.31 ± 13.23 vs. 30.83 ± 12.49; n = 59). Levene's test showed no significant result (p = 0.842). The ANOVA revealed no significant difference between the FACIT‐Fatigue scores of the group with reduced CT‐SMI and the group with preserved CT‐SMI (F(1,92) = 1.697, p = 0.196). The Mann–Whitney U test confirmed the results of the ANOVA, showing no significant difference between the scores of patients with CT‐SMI above the reference value (Mdn = 35) and those of patients with CT‐SMI below the reference value (Mdn = 32) (U = 447.500, Z = −1.195, p = 0.232).

FACIT‐Fatigue and HGS

FACIT‐Fatigue Scale scores showed a weak correlation with HGS (Spearman's ρ = 0.197, p = 0.043) according to Cohen (1988) [46]. The mean FACIT‐Fatigue score of patients with preserved HGS was higher than that of patients with reduced HGS (34.03 ± 12.48 vs. 26.14 ± 11.75; n = 105). Both the male and female cohorts with preserved HGS showed higher mean scores compared with their peers with reduced HGS (male: 33.98 ± 13.03 vs. 31.64 ± 9.45, n = 66; female: 34.11 ± 11.69 vs. 16.50 ± 9.33, n = 40). Levene's test showed no significant result (p = 0.586). The ANOVA revealed a significant difference between the FACIT‐Fatigue scores of patients with reduced HGS and those with preserved HGS (F(1,104) = 3.991, p = 0.048). The Mann–Whitney U test also showed a significant difference between the group with reduced HGS (Mdn = 26) and the group with preserved HGS (Mdn = 35) (U = 321.000, Z = −2.088, p = 0.037). These results indicate that HGS is associated with fatigue and quality of life.

BSA‐F

The questionnaire was evaluated for a total of 100 individuals (37 female, 63 male). Due to missing or inaccurate responses, the minutes of physical activity per week could not be determined. Instead, a new activity index consisting of three questions was created from the evaluable items. Twenty‐one individuals reported being regularly physically active during the past 4 weeks. Fifty‐three individuals reported regular physical activity during the past 5 years, and 40 individuals considered themselves to have been generally physically active throughout their lives. In the activity index, 32 individuals answered all three questions with “no,” resulting in a score of zero. Seventeen individuals reported one active phase, scoring 1 point; 36 individuals reported two active phases, scoring 2 points; and 15 individuals reported being physically active in all phases, scoring 3 points. When examining the correlation between the activity index and CT‐SMI evaluated in CT, no significant correlation was found (r = −0.048). Similarly, no monotonic relationship was observed between the activity index and HGS (r = 0.047). However, a significant correlation was found between the activity index and fatigue symptoms (r = 0.231, p = 0.022). Additionally, a significant correlation was observed between the activity index and quality of life (r = 0.261, p = 0.01).

SARC‐F

Of the 115 possible participants, 75 questionnaires could be used for further analysis (30 female, 45 male). Of these 75 patients, 70 presented a viable CT image. The Spearman correlation between the questionnaire score and HGS (kg), as well as the test performance in relation to HGS and CT‐SMI, was calculated. The Spearman correlation analysis was based on the hypothesis of an inverse relationship between HGS (kg) and the total score of the SARC‐F questionnaire. The analysis yielded a correlation coefficient of −0.265, which was significant at the 0.05 level (p = 0.022). Thus, the alternative hypothesis was accepted, indicating a decreasing SARC‐F score as HGS increased. The strength of the correlation was moderate. The test performance of the questionnaire was evaluated separately for HGS and CT‐SMI. Sensitivity and specificity were calculated for both HGS and CT‐SMI. Regarding HGS, the questionnaire showed a sensitivity of 18.8% and a specificity of 96.6%. Regarding CT‐SMI, the sensitivity was 31.3% and the specificity was 90.7%. The examination of significant differences in the medians of the total SARC‐F score between genders (Mann–Whitney U test) showed no significant difference (median females = 1.5; median males = 1; U = 584.500; Z = −1.004; p = 0.319).

Discussion

CT/HGS

The evaluation based on the EWGSOP showed that muscle mass and strength were, on average, higher in men than in women, and that the average age of the affected individuals was higher, which represents the most significant risk factor [1]. Fewer patients showed skeletal muscle values below the reference values reported by van der Werf et al. [34] than expected. Based on CT‐SMI, the prevalence of pre‐sarcopenia, defined as a reduction in skeletal muscle mass alone, was 13.72% in this study. According to the EWGSOP definition, sarcopenia was present in only three men among the 102 patients, resulting in a prevalence of 2.94%. This prevalence is considerably lower than expected and is even below the nationwide prevalence of sarcopenia [1] in healthy German men, which is currently estimated at 4% [48]. Other studies have reported a wide prevalence range of sarcopenia among patients with cancer, from 11% to 74% [2]. In this study, the variability and deviation from the reference threshold are relevant for interpreting the results. The average deviation was 6.66 ± 6.41 cm2/m2 and was even lower among men. These results suggest that many patients were borderline sarcopenic and therefore at risk of receiving a pre‐sarcopenia or sarcopenia diagnosis. Muscle mass loss in these patients appears to be already advanced, although the strict diagnostic threshold has not yet been reached. The low prevalence of sarcopenia observed in this study may also be due to the relatively small cohort and the lack of randomization. The correlation between muscle mass and strength indicates that, even without applying strict cutoff values, a linear relationship exists between reduced muscle mass and reduced muscle strength. This relationship suggests that HGS measurements obtained during routine examinations, when interpreted appropriately, can provide an indication of a patient's skeletal muscle status.

Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis

Ninety‐one percent of the measured patients showed a reduced phase angle. This high prevalence can be attributed not only to muscle mass loss but also to other factors such as age, fluid imbalance, and wasting diseases, which are likely present concurrently in this study population and may sometimes interact with each other [49]. The correlation results indicate that measurement of the phase angle using BIA, when interpreted correctly, can provide insight into the patient's muscle mass and strength. In addition to assessing muscle status, the phase angle has also been shown in other studies to be a predictor of overall survival in patients with cancer [50]. BIA is easier to integrate into routine care, more patient‐friendly in application, and associated with fewer side effects. Our results suggest that early use of BIA diagnostics could provide indications of potentially necessary interventions and the patient's prognosis, as well as an overview of the general condition. However, further studies with larger sample sizes and a higher prevalence of sarcopenia are necessary to establish this procedure in clinical practice.

EORTC QLQ‐C30

The entire cohort reached a mean HRQOL score of 50.64 ± 21.55. The reference value provided by the EORTC is 61.3 ± 24.2, suggesting reduced HRQOL in our oncological cohort [51]. A comparison of the functional scores of the entire cohort with the reference data from the general population reported by Waldmann et al. [39] shows that both female and male oncological patients had significantly lower scores in the physical, role, and social functioning scales, as well as in HRQOL, according to the criteria proposed by Cocks et al. [38]. The cohort also showed a higher prevalence of fatigue, dyspnea, and appetite loss. Male patients reported more constipation, whereas female patients reported more diarrhea, nausea, and vomiting. When compared with the reference study by Schwarz and Hinz [45], using the point difference thresholds defined by Cocks et al. [38], our cohort additionally showed significantly higher levels of pain and financial difficulties. Female patients also reported more insomnia, whereas male patients reported more diarrhea and lower cognitive functioning [45]. These results are consistent with findings reported by Nolte et al. [52], although our cohort showed lower physical, role, and social functioning, as well as lower quality of life. In addition, higher levels of fatigue, dyspnea, and appetite loss were observed compared with the results reported by Nolte et al. [52]. Across the reference studies, the scales with reduced functioning scores and elevated symptom scores largely overlap. Given the presence of oncological disease in our cohort, such differences would be expected.

EORTC QLQ‐C30 and CT‐SMI

Regarding our original research question of whether HRQOL is worse in oncological patients with muscle mass loss, it is important to emphasize the small number of individuals who scored below their CT‐SMI reference value (n = 14). The ANOVA showed no significant differences across the scales between patients with CT‐SMI below the reference value and those with CT‐SMI above the reference value. Only in subsequent analyses did Welch's test indicate a higher level of diarrhea among patients with CT‐SMI above the reference value. The observation that the patient group with CT‐SMI above the reference value reported more diarrhea may be explained by the numerical imbalance between the two groups. Nipp et al. [11] reported reduced HRQOL and higher levels of depressive symptoms among sarcopenic cancer patients. Our cohort did not demonstrate such differences in HRQOL. This finding may be due to the relatively small total sample size, which may not have been sufficient to include a substantial number of patients with muscle loss compared with those without muscle loss.

EORTC QLQ‐C30 and HGS

Low HGS has recently been shown to negatively affect overall survival and mortality rates in cancer patients with and without cancer cachexia [21, 53, 54]. In our cohort, patients with reduced HGS displayed lower HRQOL and greater appetite loss compared with patients with preserved HGS. Previous studies support these findings [13, 55, 56]. Kilgour et al. [55] found that patients with advanced cancer and reduced HGS had lower HRQOL, although HRQOL was assessed using the BFI and MQoL questionnaires. Norman et al. [57] also reported an association between reduced HGS and lower HRQOL in patients with cancer. Similarly, Kim et al. [56] found lower HRQOL among female cancer patients with reduced HGS using the EQ‐5D‐3L questionnaire. Regarding the EORTC QLQ‐C30, Barbosa et al. [58] reported that lower HGS in colorectal cancer patients was associated with reduced HRQOL irrespective of frailty and sarcopenia status. Determining whether HGS is reduced may therefore aid in assessing HRQOL in cancer patients and may provide an incentive to implement physical training interventions. Malnutrition leading to cachexia in cancer patients, together with appetite loss, is a well‐known complication of malignant disease [59]. O'Callaghan et al. [13] reported similar findings in their study: oncological patients with weaker HGS reported significantly greater appetite loss, which aligns with our results. To maintain healthy muscle mass, nutrients such as protein, vitamin D, and omega‐3 fatty acids have been associated with supporting muscle function [60]. Adequate protein intake, in particular, has been linked to better HGS [61, 62]. Therefore, assessing appetite and dietary intake in patients with lower HGS may help guide dietary interventions aimed at preventing further deterioration of muscle function.

FACIT‐Fatigue

Cancer‐related fatigue is a well‐known symptom of malignant disease and can severely impair the daily life of patients [10, 63]. In our cohort, 83 patients (31 female, 52 male) presented with a FACIT‐Fatigue score below their age‐ and sex‐adjusted normative percentile rank according to Montan et al. [47]. In contrast, 24 patients (9 female, 15 male) displayed a FACIT‐Fatigue score above their respective percentile rank. The observation that the majority of cancer patients in our cohort exhibited higher fatigue levels compared with their healthy peers [47] is therefore not unexpected. In their study, Butt et al. [64] identified fatigue as the most important symptom among cancer patients receiving chemotherapy. These results are consistent with findings reported by Cella et al. [65], who showed that cancer patients with and without anemia in the USA experienced significantly higher levels of fatigue than the general population. Our results confirm the importance of identifying fatigue in cancer patients in order to implement measures aimed at reducing fatigue symptoms.

FACIT‐Fatigue and CT‐SMI

When comparing the FACIT‐Fatigue results of patients with normal CT‐SMI values to those with abnormal CT‐SMI values, no significant differences were found. However, when examining the 14 patients with CT‐SMI below the reference value (29.07 ± 13.42) and the 80 patients with CT‐SMI above the reference value (33.85 ± 12.53), it can be observed that the patients with CT‐SMI below the reference value could be classified as experiencing severe fatigue according to Tennant et al. [17]. Although research by Kilgour et al. [66] reported an association between cancer‐related fatigue (CRF) and muscle mass, our study did not demonstrate such an association. Another study by Neefjes et al. [67] highlighted that, particularly in men, higher muscle mass at the third lumbar vertebra (L3) is associated with lower CRF. These findings were not observed among women in the same study [67]. It is important to note that Neefjes et al. [67] reported that more than half of the male patients presented with low CT‐SMI in a total cohort of 233 patients. The absence of significant differences between the two groups (CT‐SMI above the reference value vs. CT‐SMI below the reference value) in our study may be explained by the relatively small sample size, the low prevalence of sarcopenia, and the imbalance between the two groups.

FACIT‐Fatigue and HGS

Our cohort showed a significant difference in FACIT‐Fatigue scores between patients with preserved HGS and those with reduced HGS (34.03 ± 12.48 vs. 26.14 ± 11.75), using the cutoff value proposed by Van Belle et al. The group with preserved HGS did not meet the criteria for fatigue according to this cutoff [41]. These results are consistent with those reported by Kilgour et al. [66], who found that lower HGS levels were associated with higher fatigue in patients with gastrointestinal and non‐small‐cell lung cancer. The correlation identified between fatigue and HGS in our study can be described as weak. Our data are consistent with previous literature, which has not identified a strong correlation between fatigue and HGS in cancer patients to date [68, 69]. To further understand the relationship between fatigue and HGS in cancer patients, additional research is required.

BSA‐F

Although the proportion of patients completing the questionnaire can be considered satisfactory, several issues arose during the use of the BSA questionnaire. The intended evaluation of minutes of physical activity per week could not be performed for a substantial number of participants due to insufficient information regarding the frequency and duration of the reported activities. As a result, it was not possible to assess these data in accordance with the reference recommendation [43] of 150 min per week. The analysis was therefore limited to simple decision questions, from which a custom physical activity index was created. The index was derived from the evaluable questions; further statistical validation or comparability with other studies using this questionnaire is not available, but it provides a rough approximation of the patient's activity level. However, questions regarding an individual's physical and sporting activity are highly subjective and prone to social desirability bias, which can lead to distorted responses and decreases the internal validity of the results. The BSA‐F is a measurement and screening tool designed to assess physical activity in work, leisure, and sports among adults [25]. However, based on the present results, the use of the complete BSA‐F questionnaire in an oncological population may be questionable. Many oncological patients are not employed during treatment. Furthermore, due to their illness, their range of motion is often limited, and their level of physical activity is frequently reduced. These aspects might be better captured through selectively chosen questions and a more individualized adaptation of the BSA‐F questionnaire.

SARC‐F

The results indicate that the SARC‐F questionnaire may serve as a suitable screening tool in an oncological cohort. High specificity with respect to the parameters of muscle mass and muscle strength reduces the risk of false‐positive results. This high specificity may also prevent overdiagnosis, which has positive implications for the efficiency of the diagnostic algorithm. A similar outcome using the same cutoff value of 4 was reported in the study by Erbas et al. [70]. A meta‐analysis published in 2021 concluded that the questionnaire is more suitable for identifying patients without sarcopenia than for detecting patients who have sarcopenia [71]. The sensitivity of the questionnaire is relatively low; however, several approaches have been proposed to increase sensitivity by combining the SARC‐F with additional parameters, as implemented in the SARC‐CalF, for example [72]. This study aimed to evaluate the SARC‐F questionnaire without any extensions because additional parameters would require further data collection. For routine clinical use, a questionnaire that can be completed without personnel assistance is more practical, which is the reason the SARC‐F was used without modifications in the Sarco‐Detect study. Nevertheless, several studies have reported improved sensitivity when additional parameters are incorporated [71, 73, 74]. Contrary to the initial assumption, the prevalence of sarcopenia in our cohort was not higher than that observed in non‐oncological cohorts, which is consistent with findings reported in studies involving non‐oncological populations. The questionnaire may therefore support the appropriate application of diagnostic procedures by reducing unnecessary testing rather than primarily facilitating case identification. The specificity of the questionnaire with respect to HGS and CT‐SMI was similar (96.6% vs. 90.7%), although slightly higher for HGS. This finding suggests that muscle strength may be screened more accurately than muscle mass using this questionnaire. It remains unclear whether the higher specificity regarding muscle strength may be explained by the fact that absolute values of muscle strength are more closely related to daily functional abilities than muscle mass. Because the questionnaire focuses on everyday functional tasks, this may represent a possible explanation for the differences observed between the two parameters. This interpretation is supported by the significant inverse correlation between the SARC‐F score and HGS. Additional studies are required to validate these findings. In particular, given the unexpectedly low prevalence of sarcopenia in our oncological cohort, it would be valuable to assess the screening performance of the SARC‐F in an oncological population with a higher prevalence of sarcopenia, as initially anticipated.

Limitations

This study presents important findings in several areas and contributes to current research on screening, diagnostics, and the impact of sarcopenia on quality of life. However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. The findings have limited generalizability, particularly due to the small cohort size. Furthermore, the prevalence of sarcopenia in this study was unexpectedly low, which reduces the interpretative power of the results. Additionally, the activity index used in this study may introduce bias because it lacks prior validation. Multiple statistical analyses were performed without applying a correction for multiple testing, which may increase the risk of false‐positive findings. Moreover, the study design and the varying participation rates in the different assessments resulted in several sub‐cohorts, which complicate the overview and comparison of results. The comparison of multiple subgroups with differing sample sizes may also reduce the internal validity and statistical power of the analyses. When interpreting comparisons between specific subgroups, the limited homogeneity within these groups should also be considered. Overall, the study highlights several relevant aspects of screening, diagnostic options, and quality of life in the context of sarcopenia. Nevertheless, additional studies are required to confirm these findings and strengthen their validity.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated significant correlations between CT‐SMI and HGS. The BIA phase angle also showed significant correlations with CT‐SMI and HGS. CT‐SMI was not significantly associated with lower HRQOL or increased fatigue in cancer patients. In contrast, reduced HGS was associated with greater appetite loss, higher fatigue levels, and lower HRQOL in cancer patients. No correlation was identified between the activity index and CT‐SMI or HGS. However, a significant correlation was observed between the activity index and fatigue, as well as between the activity index and HRQOL. The SARC‐F questionnaire demonstrated high specificity but relatively low sensitivity for detecting reduced muscle strength and muscle mass.

The number of patients with values below the reference ranges for BIA and CT‐SMI is currently quite low. To further investigate these findings, studies with a larger sample size and a higher prevalence of reduced CT‐SMI are necessary. A larger dataset would increase the robustness of the correlation analysis between BIA and CT‐SMI and allow for a more detailed evaluation of the relationship between decreased CT‐SMI, HRQOL, and fatigue. This would also help clarify the overall clinical relevance of reduced CT‐SMI in cancer patients. In this study, HGS and the BIA phase angle allowed a reliable estimation of muscle mass status. The use of these cost‐effective and easy‐to‐apply tools for documenting muscle mass status in a clinical setting—for example, before starting therapy and during treatment monitoring—should therefore be considered. This could enable earlier interventions aimed at improving the HRQOL of cancer patients. This study also showed that HGS is closely associated with typical symptoms of cancer and, when assessed regularly, can provide valuable information about a patient's general condition. These findings should be confirmed and further investigated in larger oncological cohorts to better understand the role and clinical relevance of HGS in the daily lives of cancer patients. The evaluation of the BSA‐F questionnaire in this study proved to be challenging, and it became evident that the questions need to be more specifically tailored to the needs and characteristics of oncology patients in order to provide more reliable insights. The SARC‐F questionnaire can be considered a valuable tool for identifying patients without sarcopenia; however, it is less effective for detecting patients with sarcopenia. To draw more precise conclusions about differences between oncological and non‐oncological patients with sarcopenia, studies involving cohorts with a higher prevalence of sarcopenia are required.
