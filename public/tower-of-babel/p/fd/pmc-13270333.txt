Total Pedicle Preservation Mastopexy: Technique, Safety, and Long-term Results
Authors: Radu Ionescu, Theodor Mareș, Daniel Dima, Mădălina Corina Totir, Cristian Radu Jecan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMastopexy aims to correct breast ptosis and restore contour and position, but existing techniques may compromise nipple–areola complex (NAC) vascularity, sensitivity, or lactation potential. Reported limitations include recurrent ptosis, bottoming out, and perfusion-related complications.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to evaluate the long-term safety, stability, and effectiveness of total pedicle preservation mastopexy (TPPM) in women undergoing mastopexy.MethodsA prospective case series of 488 women (cup size A-D, grade I-II ptosis) underwent TPPM between 2012 and 2022, performed by a single surgeon. Patients were followed at 1, 6, 12, and 24 months. Primary outcomes included sternal notch-to-nipple (SN-N) distance, nipple-to-inframammary fold (N-IMF) distance, breast density (Shore O Durometer), and areolar diameter. Complications were recorded.ResultsMean SN-N distance decreased from 25.22 cm preoperatively to 20.65 cm at 24 months (P < .001). N-IMF distance increased modestly from 7.2 cm postoperatively to 8.1 cm at 24 months, corresponding to a 52.9% relapse of the initial correction with 47.1% of the initial correction maintained. Breast density increased from 7.6 to 8.8 Shore O units at 24 months (P < .001), remaining above baseline. Areolar diameter decreased from 64.2 mm preoperatively to 42.0 mm at 24 months. The overall complication rate was 2.5%. 12 events in 488 patients with no cases of NAC necrosis, hematoma, wound dehiscence, or infection.ConclusionsTPPM is a safe, reproducible mastopexy technique that achieves stable long-term results. It offers an effective alternative for patients seeking durable, natural outcomes without implants or major tissue excision.Level of Evidence: 4 (Therapeutic) For image description, please refer to the figure legend and surrounding text.

Throughout history, female breasts have been depicted as symbols of love and fertility and have been deeply tied to a woman's sense of self.1 For many, breasts represent an important sign of femininity, making breast-lift surgeries, or mastopexies, a key procedure to restore and reshape ptotic breasts, improving their contour and position without altering volume or adding foreign implants.2

The success of breast surgery heavily depends on understanding the breast's vascular anatomy. Key sources of blood supply to the nipple–areola complex (NAC) include the internal thoracic, lateral thoracic, anterior intercostal, and acromiothoracic arteries.3 Among these, the internal thoracic artery is the most reliable, often providing perforating branches from the second intercostal space, with less frequent contributions from the first, third, and fourth spaces.4,5

Würinger et al described a horizontal fibrous septum extending from the pectoral fascia along the fifth rib toward the nipple, which helps guide these branches as they move toward the NAC.6 Michelle le Roux et al observed that the arterial supply runs superficially, ∼1.03 cm beneath the skin at the edge of the NAC.7 As the vessels extend laterally, they travel deeper into the breast parenchyma after passing through the pectoralis major, converging toward the NAC at around 1.42 cm depth at a distance of 3 cm from the NAC, as reported by the same study.7 Additionally, other findings suggest a depth of about 1.5 cm ± 0.4 cm.5 Venous drainage follows a similar pattern, with superficial pathways medially and deeper routes laterally. Near the NAC, veins are located at an average depth of 2.6 mm, whereas at a distance of 3 cm laterally, they are found at ∼3 mm depth.7 In terms of innervation, the anterior intercostal nerves (2-5) supply the breast, and branches of the fourth and fifth nerves innervate the NAC, becoming superficial within 2 mm of depth as they approach the nipple.7

Despite these general patterns, variations in vessel pathways because of embryologic development can lead to unpredictable blood supply to the NAC, making it difficult for surgeons to accurately anticipate vascular distribution.

To address these challenges, we propose the total pedicle preservation mastopexy (TPPM), a technique that preserves all major vascular pedicles, ensuring reliable blood supply to the NAC and reducing complication risks.

METHODS

Study Population

This study included female patients operated on and followed up exclusively by the first author, R.I., over a 10-year period (2012-2022). The inclusion criteria were as follows: female patients with breast cup sizes ranging from A to D, with grade I or II ptosis, no history of breast surgery, and no intention to receive breast implants. Patients were excluded if they had undergone previous breast surgery, expressed a desire for breast implants, presented with breast sizes larger than D cup, had grade III or pseudo-ptosis, or were lost to follow-up at any point during the 24-month monitoring period.

All patients provided written informed consent for the anonymous use and analysis of their data, as well as for the publication of photographs and videos. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Preoperative Data Collection

We collected preoperative data including age, BMI, smoking status, number of pregnancies, and breastfeeding history. We measured the sternal notch-to-nipple (SN-N) and nipple-to-inframammary fold (N-IMF) distance in centimeters preoperatively and during the 24-month period at certain time points. Breast density was assessed using the “Shore O Durometer” (Milton), a handheld spring-loaded probe that measures the resistance of soft tissues to indentation, providing a numerical value of 0 to 100 Shore O units; higher readings indicate firmer tissue (Supplemental Figure 1).

Follow-up Protocol

The patients were followed for 24 months by the first author R.I., with clinical assessments performed at 1, 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively. At each follow-up visit, key outcome parameters were recorded, including SN-N and N-IMF distances, breast density, and areolar diameter.

Preoperative Markings

A breast taping method is used preoperatively to simulate mastopexy results, allowing patients to preview the expected outcome during their initial visit.8 With the patient in a standing, upright position, the mid-sternal line is first marked. The breast meridian is then identified by drawing a vertical line starting from the midpoint of the clavicle and extending downward to the inframammary fold (IMF), following the natural alignment of the breast.

An important refinement involves Pitanguy's point, which is used to mark the upper edge of the areola rather than the nipple position, allowing optimal postoperative positioning by accounting for the glandular reshaping and avoiding a high-riding NAC. Additionally, the IMF is marked with the patient in a supine position, because the fold is naturally situated slightly higher than it appears when standing. By gently pressing the breast on the chest wall, the correct fold position becomes apparent.

The patient is subsequently placed again in a standing position after marking the IMF, and a keyhole template (mastopexy pattern marker) is then applied to outline the periareolar incision. Following this, the breast is manipulated by applying controlled force to displace the tissue superior-laterally and superior-medially. This step allows for the precise identification and marking of the medial and lateral pillars of the incision by connecting the lower edges of the keyhole contour with the IMF. During this process, careful attention is paid to the amount of force applied, avoiding over-resection of tissue and excessive tension on the closure (Figure 1).

Preoperative breast markings on a 26-year-old female patient in standing position. Bilateral keyhole mastopexy pattern is outlined. Red X marks indicate Pitanguy's point, defining the upper edge of the areola.

Although the final result resembles an inverted-T mastopexy, the horizontal component is added intraoperatively based on direct assessment, keeping the vertical pillar within an interval of 4.5 to 6 cm depending on the breast size (Video 1).

Operative Technique

Under general anesthesia, with the patient in the supine position and the arms abducted at 90°, tumescent solution was injected intradermally into the area to be deepithelialized. The NAC is superficially circumscribed using an areola marking device of 42 mm. Surgical markings are then incised, followed by complete deepithelialization of the outlined area. (Figure 2)

Circumvertical pattern incision and complete deepithelialization of the marked area.

Using electrocautery, incisions are made to a depth of 3 mm along the margins: circular incisions for the upper half and triangular along the vertical portion of the breast-lift pattern. This approach ensures precise dissection, limiting the incision to the dermal layer while preserving the underlying adipose tissue and vascular structures (Video 2).

At the lower pole of the breast, the superficial Scarpa's fascia is released down to the level of the deep pectoral fascia over a width of 2 to 3 cm. This release technique allows for elevation of the gland to a higher position while maintaining the stability of the IMF (Video 2).

In the lower pole, a running locking suture using 2-0 Vicryl sutures (Ethicon, Inc., Raritan, NJ) is applied, starting at the cranial point of the dermal triangle and extending caudally along its full length (Video 2). Within the released section of the gland, a deep bite is taken from the deep pectoral fascia in a higher position using the running suture (Figure 3). To ensure skin margin congruence at the top of the vertical pillar, an additional 2-0 Vicryl suture is placed.

Placement of running locking sutures on the inferior dermal triangle and fixation to deep pectoral fascia with a running 2-0 Vicryl™ suture. Medical illustration by Rodrigo Tonan.

The running suture continues after passing through deep pectoral fascia in a subcuticular fashion to the upper edge of the vertical pillar. Initially, the suture is placed in the deep dermis (reticular dermis) within the first 3 to 4 cm of the caudal vertical pillar and becomes more superficial afterward. This ensures that when the horizontal deepithelialization is performed later on, the suture remains intact (Video 2). Once the vertical pillar suturing is completed, the suture is tied together with the free end of the previously placed 2-0 Vicryl sutures (Ethicon, Inc.) suture at the top of the pillar to secure fixation (Figure 4A). Two additional subcuticular 3-0 Monocryl sutures (Ethicon, Inc.) are then placed—1 above and 1 below the secured fixation point.

A) Final aspect after the vertical pillar closure. Medical illustration by Rodrigo Tonan. B) Areolar closure using an interlocking 2-0 Monotex® suture (Riverpoint Medical, Portland, OR, USA). Medical illustration by Rodrigo Tonan.

An interlocking suturing technique is performed around the NAC using a nonabsorbable 2-0 Monotex suture (Riverpoint Medical, Portland, OR) with a straight triangular needle (Figure 4B).10 In the initial series of cases, 2-0 Prolene (Ethicon, Inc.) was used for this step, but it was later replaced by Monotex to reduce the risk of suture extrusion. The areolar diameter is measured before closure and adjusted to 38 to 40 mm. The Monotex suture is secured with 5 double knots to prevent slippage, given the suture's slippery nature. After knotting, one end of the suture is trimmed, leaving a 4 to 5 mm tail, whereas the other end, with the needle still attached, is passed through the skin and exited 3 to 5 cm laterally to the NAC before being cut. A 4-0 Monocryl suture (Ethicon, Inc.) is then used to complete the areolar closure with an intradermal technique. (Video 3)

The patient is elevated to a near-sitting position on the operating table to assess breast symmetry and shape (Figure 5). Any excess skin in the lower pole is adjusted using tailor-tacking and marked for excision, resulting in N-IMF distances between 6.5 and 8.5 cm, depending on breast volume and shape.

Intraoperative evaluation of breast symmetry and contour with the patient in a near-seated position.

The operating table is returned to a horizontal position, and the length of the vertical pillars is measured from the IMF to the lower edge of the areola and determined based on breast size, typically ranging between 4.5 and 6 cm. The excision is then marked, and the staples are carefully removed. In contrast, the N-IMF distance measured postoperatively represents the distance from the nipple apex to the IMF.

The marked area is then deepithelialized, and the dermis is excised to facilitate better wound approximation, except for a central vertical strip, which remains partially secured by the running suture of the vertical pillars (Figure 6 and Video 4). The horizontal segment is then sutured using 2-0 Vicryl sutures (Ethicon, Inc.), with the first 2 stitches placed on the left and right sides of the inverted T junction. Additional 5-0 Prolene sutures (Ethicon, Inc.) are placed as simple stitches at the inverted T junction and the areolar-vertical pillar junction to secure and approximate the skin edges, completing the procedure (Figure 7).

Deepithelialization and dermalexcision of the marked horizontal area, preserving a central vertical dermal strip attached by the running suture. Medical illustration by Rodrigo Tonan.

Final closure of the mastopexy incisions with an inverted-T pattern.

Postoperative Management

Patients were discharged on the first postoperative day and instructed to wear a supportive compression bra continuously for 45 days. During this period, patients were advised to avoid lifting the elbows above shoulder level, performing any strenuous activity, or carrying weights exceeding 3.5 kg. Direct trauma or pressure to the breasts was also to be avoided. Sleeping in a supine position was recommended for the first 30 days to minimize strain on the surgical area. Oral antibiotic prophylaxis with amoxicillin–clavulanate is administered for 7 days postoperatively, and analgesics are prescribed as needed.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Python (SciPy library). Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation. A paired t-test was used to compare preoperative and postoperative values of Suprasternal Notch to Nipple-Areola Complex (N-NAC) distance and breast density at different time points (1, 6, 12, and 24 months) to assess the stability and effectiveness of the technique.

Additionally, an independent t-test was used to compare smokers and nonsmokers in terms of postoperative N-NAC distance and breast density at 24 months. To determine whether smoking was associated with a higher complication rate, a χ2 test was performed.

A P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

RESULTS

We analyzed a cohort of 488 patients to assess the outcomes and stability of the TPPM technique over a 24-month follow-up period. The mean age of the patients was 35.6 ± 5.02 years (range, 25-46 years), with a mean BMI of 22.6 ± 2.28 kg/m2. Among the patients, ∼15.5% was smokers, and the average number of pregnancies per patient was 1.8 ± 0.73. Additionally, 56% of the patients had a history of breastfeeding. Preoperatively, the mean N-NAC distance was 25.22 cm ± 0.77 cm. At 1 month postoperatively, the mean distance was significantly reduced to 19.11 cm ± 0.40 cm, P < .001. At 6 months, the mean N-NAC distance remained stable at 19.6 cm ± 0.60 cm, P < .001. After a 12-month period a minimal increase to 20.21 cm ± 0.64 cm was noted, and by 24 months, the mean distance was 20.65 cm ± 0.67 cm, P < .001. Overall, the mean increase in the N-NAC was 1.54 cm over the 2-year follow-up period, indicating long-term preservation of the lifting effect.

Breast density was another key parameter assessed in this study, which was significantly enhanced over time. Preoperatively, the mean breast density, as measured using the Shore O scale, was 7.6 ± 0.63 units. Postoperatively, there was an immediate and significant improvement, with the mean density increasing to 10.4 ± 0.67 units (P < .001) at 1 month, corresponding to a 36.84% mean enhancement. At 6 months, the mean density was 9.8 ± 0.65 units (P < .001); at 12 months, its mean was measured at around 9.2 ± 0.52 units; and at 24 months, the mean density was 8.8 ± 0.59 units (P < .001).

The mean preoperative N-IMF distance was 8.9 ± 0.54 cm (range, 8.0-9.8 cm) and was reduced to 7.2 ± 0.41 cm at 1 month (P < .001). A modest, progressive increase was then observed: 7.5 ± 0.46 cm at 6 months (P < .001 vs preoperative; P = .019 vs 1 month), 7.9 ± 0.48 cm at 12 months (P < .001 vs preoperative; P = .008 vs 6 months), and 8.1 ± 0.52 cm at 24 months (P < .001 vs preoperative; P = .079 vs 12 months). The largest mean increase from the immediate postoperative measurement was 0.9 cm, corresponding to a 52.9% relapse of the initial correction, with 47.1% of the initial correction maintained at 24 months. Two patients (0.41%) did meet the clinical criteria for bottoming out, with N-IMF distances exceeding 9.5 cm at 24 months. Although these values fall within the anatomical range measured preoperatively, they deviate from the expected postoperative trajectory and are therefore reported as complications. The areolar diameter showed a significant and stable reduction following surgery. Preoperatively, the mean diameter was 64.24 ± 6.59 mm, decreasing to a mean of 39.96 ± 0.35 mm (P < .001) immediately postoperatively, representing a 38% reduction. At 6 months, the mean diameter was 40.48 ± 0.30 mm, and at 12 months, it was 41.18 ± 0.30 mm. Over the 24-month follow-up, a gradual and minor increase was observed, reaching 41.97 ± 0.32 mm (P < .001).

Regarding postoperative complications, the overall complication rate was low at 2.5%. 12 events in 488 patients means 2.5%. Specific complications included 4 cases of Prolene (Ethicon, Inc.) extrusion (0.82%), 2 cases of bottoming out (0.41%) and minor asymmetries in 6 patients (1.22%). Notably, no cases of hematoma, infection, skin necrosis, and wound dehiscence were reported. No significant difference was found between smokers and nonsmokers in terms of N-NAC distance at 24 months (P = .8169) or breast density at 24 months (P = .4256).

Additionally, a χ2 test was performed to examine whether smoking increased the likelihood of complications, and results suggest that it was not a risk factor for complications in this study population (P = 1.0).

DISCUSSION

The TPPM presents multiple advantages, offering surgeons a safe, effective, and reproducible technique. This is supported by our findings, where no cases of NAC necrosis were observed among the 488 patients, and the overall complication rate was limited to 2.5%, 12 events in 488 patients. The preservation of major vascular sources, except for the subdermal vascular plexus interrupted by the circumferential incision, contributes to this low complication rate. Importantly, most venous drainage is maintained through deeper accompanying veins, preventing venous congestion, as highlighted by Michelle le Roux et al.10 This study demonstrated the extensive venous anastomoses within the NAC, which facilitate the redirection of drainage to the primary draining veins. Crucially, preserving at least one of the main veins is sufficient to ensure proper venous drainage of the NAC. The findings also emphasize that the lateral veins are located deeper, whereas the medial veins are more superficial.9

The TPPM technique differs from the central mound mastopexy in a few key aspects. First, TPPM preserves all major pedicles, including internal mammary, lateral thoracic, intercostal, and thoracoacromial perforators, thus maximizing vascularity and sensory innervation of the NAC. In contrast, the central mound technique relies predominantly on a posterior pedicle, with potential sacrifice of peripheral perfusion pathways.11 Second, TPPM leaves the parenchyma virtually untouched and prioritizes inferior pole dermal fixation, which supports sustained lower-pole suspension and nipple elevation, as demonstrated by limited change in N-IMF distance at 24 months. Conversely, the central mound mastopexy technique involves extensive subcutaneous undermining and parenchymal resection, which makes it suitable for use in augmentation mastopexy procedures.12 In contrast, TPPM is not compatible with breast implants, because the combination tends to produce an overly conical breast shape and increases the risk of developing a waterfall deformity.

The TPPM technique demonstrates significant and sustained lifting potential. Preoperatively, the mean N-NAC distance was 25.22 cm, which was significantly reduced to 19.11 cm at 1 month postoperatively, indicating an effective initial lift. This correction was largely maintained over the 24-month follow-up, with only a slight increase of 1.54 cm in N-NAC distance. Long-term stability of the horizontal scar within the IMF was consistently observed, with no evidence of recurrent glandular herniation beneath it. This clinical observation was supported by objective measurements of the N-IMF distance, which showed a 0.9 cm increase (52.9% relapse of the initial correction) over 24 months, confirming the sustained lower-pole support (Figures 8-10).

Regarding breast density, Ohmaru et al have reported that natural age-related changes can result in a decrease of ∼25% in mammographic breast density over a decade because of tissue relaxation.13 In contrast, the TPPM technique resulted in an initial 36.84% enhancement in breast density at 1 month postoperatively. Although there was a gradual decrease over time, the density remained about 15.8% higher at the 24-month mark compared with preoperative levels, confirming that TPPM maintains a firmer breast structure over time.

The technique's versatility allows it to be effectively applied to patients with breast sizes ranging from cup A to cup D, providing flexibility in skin excision and contouring for optimal aesthetic outcomes. Additionally, it is straightforward for surgeons to implement and is associated with a low complication rate, supporting patient safety.

Despite these benefits, the technique has certain limitations. It is not suitable for patients with larger breast volumes (cups greater than D), grade III ptosis, or pseudo-ptosis, because these patients are more susceptible to bottoming out because of excessive breast weight that typically requires parenchymal modification or reduction, which TPPM does not provide. This limitation is seen in the 2 cases observed in our study, which involved patients with cup sizes larger than D. For such cases, we recommend a breast reduction procedure, given that TPPM does not involve large tissue excision. However, small resections of the lower pole or combining the technique with liposuction or lipofilling can be performed to improve volume symmetry and conceal defects. In our experience, TPPM can also be applied in massive weight loss patients who meet the same breast volume and ptosis criteria, without the need for additional stabilizing procedures. Furthermore, because the technique maintains good breast vascularization and limits inflammation by restricting deep dissection within the mammary gland, an inflammation that could otherwise favor early fat resorption, making lipofilling safe and effectively utilized to achieve a fuller upper pole.

Lastly, to address the issue of suture extrusion associated with the previously used Prolene sutures (Ethicon, Inc.) for the interlocking suture, we transitioned to 2-0 Monotex sutures (Riverpoint Medical), and no further cases of suture extrusion have been reported.

This study has several notable strengths, consisting of a large sample size of 488 patients and a long-term follow-up period of 24 months, with comprehensive statistical analysis, confirming that TPPM maintains a significant reduction in N-NAC distance, sustained lower-pole suspension, and an increase in breast density over time. Additionally, the low overall complication rate of 2.5%, 12 events in 488 patients and the absence of significant differences between smokers and nonsmokers strengthen the safety profile of the technique.

Preservation of nipple–areola sensitivity was confirmed clinically during follow-up; however, no objective assessments such as galactography or sensory-threshold testing were performed. Given that the technique avoids deep glandular dissection and preserves the galactophorous ducts and vascular and neural supply, impairment of lactation is unlikely, but this remains an inferred benefit requiring confirmation by future studies with validated objective tests.

However, although the results demonstrate a 15.8% sustained increase in breast density at 24 months, it remains unclear whether this effect would persist beyond this period. The method of assessing the density with Shore O Durometer provides an objective measure of tissue firmness, but it does not directly quantify glandular or adipose composition. Future studies using mammographic or MRI-based density assessments could offer a more precise characterization of the structural changes induced by TPPM.

This study also has methodological limitations. It represents a single-surgeon cohort, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, no validated aesthetic outcome measures or patient-reported outcome instruments were used, which restricts the ability to objectively assess aesthetic perception and patient experience. Future directions should also incorporate patient-reported outcome measures such as BREAST-Q to better evaluate satisfaction, quality of life, and functional impact from the patient's perspective.

CONCLUSIONS

The TPPM technique effectively lifts and reshapes the breast while preserving all main vascular pedicles. It is particularly suited for patients with breast sizes ranging from cup A to cup D who do not require tissue reduction or concomitant breast augmentation, offering reliable, natural-looking results with minimal complications. The procedure is simple, reproducible, and has demonstrated stable outcomes at 24 months, making it a valuable and consistent option in aesthetic breast surgery.
