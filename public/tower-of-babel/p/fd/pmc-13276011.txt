Co‐Creation of an Injury Prevention Implementation Strategy for a Girls' Football Team in a Community‐Based Club
Authors: Christian Victor Otte Odgaard, Kristine Sørensen, Carsten Møller Mølgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aim of this study was to investigate (1) Football coaches' and players' experiences with a co‐created injury prevention (IP) implementation strategy, (2) Football coaches' and players' visions for improving the implementation of IP; and (3) to which extent the initial co‐created implementation strategy increases the use of IP exercises. A mixed‐method design consisting of observations and interviews. An implementation strategy was co‐created with five youth girls' coaches in a community‐based football club. The process was evaluated through semi‐structured focus group interviews with five coaches and eight youth players. Observations of football sessions were conducted before intervention, 5 weeks after and 7 months after implementation. Interviews were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis. Five themes were generated from the coaches' interview: (1) use of IP during intervention period, (2) challenges during intervention period, (3) positive experiences during the intervention, (4) responsibility and other stakeholders, and (5) visions for future implementation. Four themes were generated from interviewing the players: (1) experiences with IP during the intervention, (2) injuries and consequences, (3) motivation, and (4) visions for implementation. Observations showed increased duration of IP and increased number of IP exercises after 5 weeks, but limited changes after 7 months. The co‐created strategy led to a limited increase of IP exercises used. While coaches had mixed experiences with IP during the intervention, the players generally had positive experiences. The participants' visions for improving the implementation were to engage multiple stakeholders, change the culture of the club, and gain knowledge.

Introduction

In football an anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury stands out as one of the most severe knee injuries [1], with female adolescent athletes being particularly at risk [2, 3]. Injury prevention exercise programs (IPEP) have shown to reduce overall injuries, including ACL‐injuries, under controlled settings when implemented in women's football [4]. However, the implementation of IPEPs in real‐world settings remains a challenge [5]. Coaches play a pivotal role in this process, and several barriers have been identified among coaches across team sports [6, 7]. To overcome these barriers and to bridge the gap between theory and practice, involving the end‐users (e.g., Coaches) is essential. Co‐creation is one of the methods that engages end‐users in the process which have been used in injury prevention studies [8, 9]. Co‐creation can foster ownership and provide valuable insights that contribute to the development of feasible IPEPs and implementation strategies [9]. However, the iterative and cyclical nature of co‐creation can be resource‐intensive, particularly in terms of time and financial costs. This demand for resources can pose a significant barrier for community‐based clubs, highlighting the need for alternative, less resource‐intensive approaches that still retain some level of end‐user involvement [8]. This study adopts a pragmatic approach to co‐creating and implementing IP (injury prevention) in a community‐based football club, aiming to explore three key aspects: (1) Football coaches' and players' experiences with a co‐created injury prevention implementation, (2) Football coaches' and players' visions for improving the implementation of injury prevention; and (3) to which extent the initial co‐created implementation strategy changes the content of a football session in at 5 weeks and 7 months after implementation.

Method

Study Design

A mixed‐method design consisting of focus group interviews and observations was used. In this action research study, the approach was a pragmatic co‐creation of knowing with people instead of knowing about people [10]. The Research Ethics Committee in the North Denmark Region determined that, based on the study design used, the project did not require approval (Ref. No. 2–1–02–1‐25). The study was approved by The Danish Data Protection Agency (K2024‐074) and the written consents were archived at Aalborg University.

Context and Participants

A local community‐based football club in northern Denmark was recruited. Participants included two U13/14 girls' coaches and three U15/17 girls' coaches. During the season, U13/14 practiced as one team and U15/17 as another. The teams were non‐elite and practiced twice weekly with one match at the weekend. The project took place during the off‐season where the teams practiced on artificial turf or indoors. All meetings with the participants were held within the football club premises, either on‐field or in a designated meeting room.

The five coaches, all female, ranged in age from 20 to 28 years. Their coaching experience varied from 6 months to 5 years, with two of the coaches holding coaching certifications. All coaches were active football players. Eight players (four from U13–U14 and four from U15–U17) took part in the player focus groups. CO and KS were responsible for all data collection and were both authorized physiotherapists with experience as coaches and players in team sports.

Interviews, Interview Guide and Analysis

To evaluate the process, three separate semi‐structured focus group interviews were conducted; one with the coaches, and one each with the U13/14 and U15/17 teams. Interview guides were developed following the phases described by Kallio et al. [11] and pilot tested with two youth coaches. All interview participants, including the players' parents, were informed about the project and provided informed written consent.

The focus group interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed in accordance with Braun and Clarke reflexive thematic analysis [12]. The analysis involves six phases: familiarization, generating codes, identifying themes, reviewing themes, defining themes, and producing the article. Using NVivo 1.7.1, CO and KS conducted the initial coding and thematization independently of each other. Subsequently, the codes and themes were homogenized. Lastly, the findings from each interview were synthesized using matrix analysis to explore tensions, agreements, and divergences [13].

To structure football coaches' and players' visions for improving IP implementation, their responses were analyzed using the Implementation Drivers Framework. This framework identifies competency, organizational, and leadership drivers that together support effective, high‐quality implementation of evidence‐based practices [14, 15].

Observations, Observation Protocol and Analysis

To explore the injury prevention content and potential changes, football sessions with the teams were observed before intervention, and 6 weeks and 7 months after intervention. The coaches and players were informed about the observations beforehand. An observation protocol was developed based on guidelines for preventing ACL injuries [16]. This protocol was used to document the components included in the sessions and whether coaches provided feedback and explained the purpose of exercises, in a binary fashion (yes/no). Additionally, the content of the football sessions, the time spent on each exercise or drill and the overall atmosphere within the teams were noted. A timer was set on a smartphone to track the duration of exercises. The timer started when the players began an exercise and stopped when the coach instructed the players to change exercises or take a break. The timer continued running if the coach paused the exercise to provide instructions or feedback on technique. The coaches were aware they were being observed (Data S1).

The Co‐Creation and Implementation Process

Our process followed Padua's seven‐step framework for developing and implementing preventive training programs [17].

Step 1–3: Establishing Contact, Developing the Team, Identifying Barriers and Solutions and Development of the Initial Implementation Plan

Using convenience sampling, the head of youth at a local football club was contacted by phone, who agreed to arrange a meeting with the coaches from the girls' youth department. Due to long response time and challenges finding a suitable date, 2 months passed from the initial contact to the meeting with the coaches.

The meeting with the coaches was a semi‐structured focus group interview. The first 15‐min was a presentation on injuries in women's football, using examples from the 2023 FIFA Women's World Cup, followed by principles of injury prevention (duration, frequency, exercise selection, and feedback on technique) [16]. Subsequently, the coaches' use of IP exercises, barriers and facilitators encountered, and their visions for implementing IP within their teams were explored (Data S2). Following the meeting, football sessions with the teams were observed to gain deeper insight into their context.

The initial meeting led to the development of a co‐created implementation plan by incorporating coaches' visions and guideline recommendations for injury prevention (Figure 1).

Development of the IPEP and implementation plan using a solution‐oriented process by incorporating coaches' visions and guideline recommendations for injury prevention.

Step 4: Design the IPEP and the Flyer

Guided by the coaches' visions, observations and recommendations from National Athletic Trainers' Association [16], a 20‐min long IPEP and an informational flyer were prepared (Data S3 and S4). The FIFA 11+ running exercises the team already performed in the IPEP were retained. Copenhagen adductor drill (strength), vertical jumping (plyometric), and one‐legged ball throwing (balance), as well as running with partner resistance and competitive agility drills with a ball, were added. Furthermore, suggestions for incorporating plyometrics or agility exercises into their existing football drills were provided, and pictures of good and bad landing technique, adapted from FIFA 11+, were added. Most exercise explanations emphasized external cueing, such as “imagine landing on eggs that must not break” or “land with as little sound as possible” [16].

The flyer included feedback on how to meet the guideline recommendations based on the coaches' current practice, with explanations of what and why. Additionally, it included exercise variations (Nordic hamstring curl, jumping lunges and plant‐and‐cut) and online evidence‐based inspiration sources for IP exercises (e.g., Skadefri/Fittoplay.org).

Step 5: Train the Trainers and Users

As envisioned by the coaches, all teams were merged for the first on‐field introduction to the IPEP. The IPEP was instructed by the researchers while the coaches observed. The coaches were provided with a paper handout of the program (Data S3). The purpose and the benefits of IPEPs (e.g., reducing injuries, enhancing performance, and team winnings) were explained to the players and coaches on‐field. Each new exercise was introduced with an explanation of its football‐specific purpose and benefits (e.g., “Makes you better at sprinting”, “protects your ankle”). Players and coaches were encouraged to provide feedback on the content and implementation of the IPEP. For example, the coaches believed that players followed instructions better in smaller groups, so additionally one separate on‐field session for each team was held. The researchers attended a final session for 30 min (with both teams on the same field) to address any questions about IP exercises. The implementation was concluded with the flyer being emailed to the coaches. The coaches were encouraged to be creative with the exercises as long as they followed the guidelines in the flyer.

Step 6 and 7: Fidelity Control and the Next Step

To evaluate the process, the coaches and players were interviewed 5 weeks post‐intervention and observations of the football sessions were conducted 5 weeks and 7 months post‐intervention.

Results

Football Coaches' and Players' Experiences With a Co‐Created Injury Prevention Implementation (Figure 2 and Data S5)

Overview of coaches and players' experiences and attitudes from the interview, and their visions applied in the Implementation Drivers Framework.

Coaches

Coaches expressed varied attitudes toward the use of IPEPs and reported variation in implementation as well as several practical barriers. U13/14 coaches adhered closely to the prescribed program, while U15/17 coaches applied selected components or modified exercises to fit football drills and time constraints. Coaches who implemented IP training less frequently cited low player engagement, limited parental support, and found the exercises generally boring. In contrast, coaches who used the program consistently reported stronger support from both parents and players.

Across teams, challenges included managing large groups, maintaining exercise quality, and engaging players who lacked motivation or understanding of IP's purpose. Limited injury knowledge and time constraints, particularly among volunteer coaches, further hindered consistent implementation.

Despite these challenges, the coaches described the co‐creation process as a positive experience and found it meaningful to be involved from the start. They valued the external expertise introduced by the research team, which increased the perceived legitimacy of IP. They reported gaining useful tools for training, observing greater body awareness among players, and perceiving IP as a potential team‐building activity. However, limited structural support and inconsistent expectations across the club constrained sustained use.

Players

Players expressed generally positive attitudes toward the IPEP and the implementation. U13/14 players reported using the exercises as instructed, while U15/17 players used selected components. Exercises were described as fun, different, and more meaningful when introduced by external instructors, which increased their perceived seriousness. Warm‐ups were viewed as both social and functional, helping players prepare physically while bonding with teammates. Exercises were considered easy to perform and adaptable, though repetition reduced engagement. Players appreciated physically demanding tasks and reported fewer discomforts during the intervention.

Players understood injuries as both physical limitations and social disruptions affecting team dynamics. They emphasized warming up as key to prevention and noted that previous injuries or current pain increased motivation to engage in IP, whereas uninjured players often viewed it as less relevant.

Football Coaches' and Players' Visions for Improving the Implementation of Injury Prevention (Figure 2 and Data S5)

Coaches

Coaches emphasized the need to embed IP within the club's culture and structure to ensure long‐term adherence. They called for leadership engagement and alignment across age groups. Suggested strategies included seasonal workshops combining theoretical and practical content, top‐down support from the Danish Football Association (DBU) to include IP in coaching education and club standards, and better communication with parents to enhance buy‐in. Coaches also recommended developing accessible digital resources, for example an app or platform with football‐specific IP exercises.

Overall, coaches envisioned a coordinated, multi‐level approach involving clubs, parents, and governing bodies to make IP a routine and sustainable part of football practice.

Players

Players preferred IP to be integrated into regular football training rather than performed separately. They highlighted the need for variation and coach guidance during exercises, while acknowledging their own responsibility for proper execution. Players highlighted the early introduction of IP as important for creating a habit.

Players suggested involving other stakeholders, including parents and DBU, to strengthen implementation. They proposed that DBU provide instructional materials, such as videos and workshops for coaches, and incorporate IP into youth football initiatives. Overall, players envisioned IP as a collaborative, engaging, and routine part of football practice supported by coaches, clubs, and governing bodies.

Structured observations, supported by the focus group interviews, revealed an increase in IPEP adherence at 5 weeks post‐intervention, particularly among the U13/14 team. Coaches of this group closely followed the prescribed IPEP, integrating all components (plyometric, strength, agility, balance, and flexibility) into a 22‐min warm‐up routine. In contrast, the U15/17 coaches demonstrated partial adherence, incorporating only selected components for a shorter duration (~10 min) and providing limited instructional feedback. However, this effect was not sustained at 7 months post‐intervention. By that time, both teams had largely reverted to their pre‐intervention practices, with observations indicating minimal inclusion of IPEP components, an absence of coach feedback, and no explanation of exercise purpose. Discussion

In this study, researchers and coaches co‐created the early steps in an implementation plan for IP in a women's youth department in a community‐based football club. Taking a pragmatic approach, the implementation process was led by the coaches' perspectives, utilizing existing evidence‐based IP tools. The observations and interviews 5 weeks after implementation indicated increased use of IP exercises, duration, and frequency. However, observations 7 months after implementation showed no or limited changes in use of IP exercises. While coaches had mixed experiences with IP during the intervention, the players generally had positive experiences. Furthermore, the coaches and players had several suggestions for improving the implementation of IP. The results from the interviews were applied in “Implementation Drivers Framework” which can be used as a foundation for the next steps in implementing IP at club level.

Several barriers and facilitators described by the coaches during the preparation meeting and 5 weeks post intervention have also been reported in other studies [6, 7]. Although the intervention was designed to address these barriers and adapt to the context, challenges with sustained used of the IPEP remained, despite initially positive coach attitudes—consistent with earlier research [18]. Discrepancies also emerged between players' and coaches' perceptions of the IPEP. While all players expressed positive attitudes toward the exercises and the concept of IP, some coaches perceived limited player interest, understanding and engagement. This mismatch suggests that coaches may underestimate players' motivation or misunderstand their perspectives on IP. Addressing this communication gap could be crucial for improving adherence and optimizing program delivery. Although workshops and supporting materials remain the most common implementation strategies [19], they may be insufficient to foster a shared understanding of IP across all stakeholders. Building on participants' suggestions, future initiatives should promote collaboration between coaches, players, parents, club management, and external stakeholders (e.g., DBU) to align expectations and embed IP within club culture, supporting long‐term sustainability.

This study highlights that even within the same environment, the coaches can experience varying barriers to implementing IPEPs. The coaches' differences in experience and expertise affect the way they interpret and implement evidence, emphasizing the need for adaptable solutions [20]. Time constraints further complicated IP adoption, as coaches, all volunteers, reported difficulty finding time for initiatives (e.g., workshops) to improve IP implementation. The coaches requested better coaching courses and workshops, which can be beneficial [21], but the setup in the club and the coaches' limited time is contradictory. Moreover, exercises are only one component of IP; IPEPs could be enhanced by embedding psychological components (e.g., stress management, self‐awareness) into physical training routines as described by Ageberg et al. in 2025 [22]. However, this would add further complexity to coaches' responsibilities. A simple program may therefore be appealing as a first step toward IP because it is easier to implement for the coaches. To improve the use of IP training, the culture in the club might have to change, making IP mandatory and already initiated among the youngest football players.

The coaches in this study indicated at the 5‐week follow‐up that sustaining long‐term use of the IPEP would be challenging, which was confirmed at the 7‐month follow‐up. Similar challenges with compliance and maintenance have been reported previously [23]. Researcher involvement in this study likely enhanced adherence in the short term but also limits the feasibility of this approach in a real‐world setting, as ongoing support may require financial resources that most community‐based Danish football clubs do not have. Evidence suggests that external support throughout the season may be necessary to maintain high fidelity and compliance [23]. Although this presents a practical barrier, implementation of IPEPs has demonstrated cost‐effectiveness and societal benefits by reducing ACL injuries and subsequent risks of arthrosis [24, 25, 26], suggesting a sound return on investment. Bringing in external professionals, such as physiotherapists, to focus on preventive rather than reactive care could help reduce long‐term injury‐related costs. Ultimately, a multi‐ecological approach that combines financial investment with attention to key implementation drivers is essential for integrating IPEPs into community sports and ensuring their long‐term success.

Strengths and Limitations

A significant strength of the study is its focus on co‐creation, with coaches and researchers working together to implement the intervention plan. Involving end‐users in the entire process, considering context, and utilizing research evidence can effectively bridge the gap between theory and practice [22, 27]. Additionally, we triangulated the findings by including observations and interviews with coaches and players, which increases the validity of our findings and provides a nuanced understanding of the challenges associated with implementing IP training [28].

However, a limitation to the study is the relatively short follow‐up period and the lack of information about the number of injuries before and after the intervention. On the other hand, this is often the reality in community clubs, making the method a good example of a practical approach.

Although we successfully recruited all the girls' youth teams and the majority of coaches within the girls' youth department, the overall sample size remains small. This limited sample, coupled with the inclusion of only a single club, may constrain the generalizability of our findings. However, the generalizability of our findings will always rely on the co‐creation process, which should reflect the individuals involved. Finally, there is a risk of desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect, as participants might have provided the answers they thought the researchers wanted while also being aware that they were being observed.

Perspectives

Our findings show that, even within the same club, coaches and players experience different obstacles and motivations when using IP exercises, reinforcing that implementing IPEPs in community sport is challenging. Similar to recent implementation research by Ageberg et al. in youth handball [22], even co‐creation efforts may not ensure consistent practice, as coaches can struggle to translate intentions into action due to established habits, limited time, or a low perceived return on investment. External support, such as ongoing education, organizational policies, and simplified resources, may therefore be necessary to sustain use across team sports. Furthermore, co‐creation may not improve adherence or fidelity more than traditional programs [22], suggesting that deeper cultural and structural changes are required. Sustainable IPEP use will likely depend on long‐term organizational commitment rather than short‐term enthusiasm from individual coaches or players. Embedding IP into coach education, strengthening club policies, and involving multiple stakeholders, consistent with the Implementation Drivers Framework, may help create lasting behavioral change. Future research should explore how other stakeholders perceive their roles and responsibilities in supporting the implementation and long‐term sustainability of injury‐preventive training within football clubs.
