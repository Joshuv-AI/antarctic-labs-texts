In Silico and in vitro antiviral activity of FDA approved drugs from ZINC database against RNA-dependent RNA polymerase of dengue virus
Authors: Alia Uzma, Adiba, Ammara Kausar, Muhammad Waseem Aslam, Liaqat Ali, Mahwish Ali, Asma Saleem Qazi, Abdul Haq, Deeba Amraiz, Emad Rashad Sindi, Taha Alqahtani, Magdi E. A. Zaki, Molalign Assefa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dengue is a frequent mosquito borne infection with no antiviral medications approved and drug repurposing is an economical and time-saving method of creating an efficient therapy. This research has chosen RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) of non-structural protein 5 (NS5) of dengue virus serotype 2 (DENV-2) as a therapeutic. PyRx software was used to perform virtual screening of FDA-approved drugs available in the ZINC database based on their structure. Out of the top15, doxorubicin and rifamycin were selected as candidate drugs to undergo molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) studies. Docking showed binding energies of − 8.9 kcal/mol for doxorubicin and − 8.6 kcal/mol for rifamycin. Simulations of MD confirmed that interactions of doxorubicin with the palm domain of RdRp occur in a stable way, which may result in the inability to promote viral replication. In Vero cells, cytotoxicity tests showed that concentrations below 12.25 µg/mL of doxorubicin were not toxic, whereas higher concentrations had morphological effects with a CC50 of approximately 150 µg/mL. MTT-based cell viability assays in DENV-2–infected Vero cells showed dose-dependent effects on cell viability, with an IC50 of 86.52 µg/mL. Conclusively, doxorubicin exhibited indirect, cell viability–based effects in DENV-2–infected Vero cells at non-cytotoxic concentrations, however, a low selectivity index (SI ≈ 1.7) and cytotoxicity highlights a narrow therapeutic window, limiting its potential for direct clinical use. The development of safer derivatives, optimized dosing and targeted delivery systems, e.g., nanocarriers should be considered in future research to improve efficacy and decrease toxicity.

Introduction

The arthropod borne viral disease known as dengue fever emerges from infections caused by the four antigenically distinct serotypes of the dengue virus (DENV-1 to DENV-4). Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes act as vectors for dengue virus transmission [1]. These mosquitos demonstrate high efficiency in spreading disease since they feed during daytime hours and bite people intensely during morning and evening hours thus raising contact rates. Global warming creates conditions where mosquito vectors expand beyond typical inter-tropical zones thereby increasing the risk of dengue infection spreading to new areas. The dengue virus undergoes two essential life cycle phases which include its replication within mosquitoes followed by humans, causing symptoms like high fever, strong headache, joint pain thus name break bone fever. In the severe cases, disease may result the dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF), which is potentially fetal. DHF is marked by internal bleeding and acute reduction in the number of platelets [2]. Currently, dengue fever is endemic in over 100 countries in all five regions of the WHO, and almost half of the global population reside in risk areas. Current WHO estimates note that there are millions of cases of dengue each year globally with large numbers of severe cases and fatalities. Dengue transmission patterns are becoming more affected by climatic changes and increases in temperature, allowing Aedes mosquitoes to survive longer, increase their geographical range, and become active earlier in the year [3, 4]. After giving the widespread impact of dengue it is essential to understand the virus at molecular level. Exploring the structure of virus is essential for the developing effective treatment strategies.

The dengue virus belongs to the family Flaviviridae along with other medically relevant viruses like Zika virus, West Nile virus and yellow fever virus. The dengue virions have a spherical shape measuring 50 nm across. The DENV genome possesses a single-stranded RNA molecule that measures about 10.7 kilobases (kb) in length constituting a positive-sense virus. DENV has three essential structural proteins which are capsid (C), pre-membrane (prM) and envelope (E). An icosahedral nucleocapsid core lies inside the DENV virions which contains an outer lipid bilayer envelope that derives from host cell membranes. The outer part of the virus contains one hundred and eighty copies of envelope proteins (E) and membrane proteins (M) distributed like a herringbone pattern [5]. The viral RNA genome exists within the nucleocapsid core complexed with various C protein copies. Virion’s stability together with infectivity and immune response evasion depends on its specific structural organization. The seven non-structural proteins are NS1, NS2A, NS2B, NS3, NS4A, NS4B, and NS5 [6]. The structural proteins are crucial in the virion’s creation and interaction with host cells. While the non-structural proteins are essential for viral multiplication, assembly, and immune evasion.

Though the dengue cases have been increasing over the years, an approved antiviral therapy is still lacking, which explains the dire necessity to devote attention to research on antiviral agents as well as vaccines. CYD-TDV (Dengvaxia) is the first certified vaccine but it is not recommended to be used in seronegative individuals because it causes an increased risk of severe dengue disease, owing to antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE), so serological screening must be performed before vaccination which is logistically difficult to achieve at a large scale [7]. The strategies that are available are restricted to the management of mosquito vectors to avert the spread of the virus, and effective therapeutic measures are urgently needed. Conventional drug discovery is the time consuming and very expensive activity that in most cases takes years. Drug repurposing, or drug repositioning, provides a useful alternative to these limitations by searching new indications to the existing FDA approved drugs. This strategy has become preferred in dengue studies because of its economic advantages, reduced timeline of development, safety records, and proven data of the involved drugs [8]. Many studies have been important in the discovery of potential drug candidates and new therapeutic targets aimed at enhancing treatment of dengue.

Recent advances in molecular and structural virology have aided in the discovery of viral targets that can be used to develop new therapies [9]. Of these categories, the DENV genome identifies conserved regions encoding non-structural enzymes, mainly viral polymerases and proteases, as promising antiviral drug targets. The most frequently targeted non-structural proteins are NS3 protease, NS3 helicase, NS4B and NS5 [10]. Among them, NS5 has attracted the most attention, having several crucial functions during viral replication and concerning the interaction with cellular machineries [11]. The structure of the NS5 protein consists of two functional enzymatic regions with an N-terminal methyltransferase (MTase) domain and a C-terminal RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) that are also required in the viral replication of the genome [12, 13]. More than enzymatic functions NS5 has interactions with various host proteins to limit host immune response in infection.

Markedly, NS5 protein is one of the largest and the most preserved protein in the DENV genome and it retains over 75% sequence identity in all the four dengue serotypes [14, 15]. The importance of such a high conservation level as the target of the development of broad-spectrum antiviral therapy against dengue is emphasized. Use of the in-silico methods such as molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulations and the pharmacokinetic profiling has significantly stimulated NS5 RdRp inhibitors discovery by facilitating high throughput screening, analysis of the most critical contacts, and lead compound optimization. The tools make the drug discovery faster, cheaper, and more precise in generating candidates to be validated in the experiments.

This study aimed to identify potential antiviral candidates targeting dengue virus RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) using structure-based virtual screening of FDA-approved drugs and to evaluate the cytotoxicity and effects on cell viability of selected compounds in dengue virus serotype 2–infected Vero cells.

Materials and methods

Preparation of protein

The crystal structure of RdRp available on Protein Data Bank was retrieved with PDB ID 6IZX, 10.2210/pdb6IZX/pdb. After the structure of the protein was downloaded, other substances connected to the protein were removed by the use of an open-source system Pymol (version 2.5.5) [16].

Structure- based virtual screening

Using the ZINC library that comprised of 3109 FDA-approved drugs, the molecular docking against the dengue virus RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) was done through PyRx software (version 0.8) [17]. After screening, next step was to find the lead compound. The lead compound was identified after applying the Lipinski rule of five which includes.

After the analysis the next step was the study of pharmacokinetic and toxicity properties. The weak candidates of the drug would be eliminated by virtual screening. The remaining candidates can be selected as by optimizing RdRp through ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity) investigations, possible treatments for the condition can be developed which is absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion related to human body was done. ADMET properties were checked through online tool Pkcsm (http://biosig.unimelb.edu.au/pkcsm/) [18].

Ligand’s preparations

The 3-dimensional structures of selected ligands were retrieved from the PubChem database maintained by the National Center for Biotechnology Information. After obtaining the ligand structure their energy was minimized by Chem 3D pro (version 3.2). The optimized structures were then saved in PDB format for further analysis.

Molecular Docking

Protein and the selected ligands were redocked by using software called cavity detection guided blind docking (CB dock) (http://clab.labshare.cn/cb-dock/) to identify best binding sites and refine their binding positions. This tool automatically identifies potential binding sites on the protein and calculates the center and size of the docking box based on the ligand structure. CB dock offered high accuracy while focusing on cavity base binding. The 3D structures of ligand and protein were uploaded in SDF and PDB format to initiate the docking process. This tool generates five possible binding poses and the best pose is selected on lowest vina score in kcal/mol. The results include interactive 3D visualization of most favorable interactions [19]. The software known as Discovery studio was used to examine particular interactions and orientation of the chosen drugs when attached to the desired proteins.

Molecular dynamic simulation

The molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) were conducted by using GROMACS (v.2023.3) software, which is a commonly used and powerful molecular dynamics simulation tool. To simulate a natural biologic environment, complex of protein-ligand was incorporated into the box of water model with the TIP3P. TIP3P is a water solvation model that uses the steepest gradient approaches and has parameters like density of the 0.997 g/L, 25 °C temp, and the pressure of 1 atm [20]. For the neutralization of the system, ions were used. Initially, system was energy minimized to eliminate bad contacts if any. Equilibration was then performed in two stages; temperature was equilibrated with the constant Number of particles, Volume, and Temperature (NVT) ensemble and the pressure was equilibrated with constant number of particles, constant temperature, and constant pressure (NPT) ensemble. MD simulations were conducted for 100 ns under standard physiological conditions. Temperature (300 K) and pressure (1 bar) were used.

Analysis of molecular dynamics simulation

Once the 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation was conducted in the GROMACS, several trajectory analyses were carried out in order to perform the assessment of dynamic behavior and stability of the RdRp-Ligand complex. Structural stability of the protein and ligand as a whole during the simulation was determined through the root mean square deviation (RMSD). Flexibility of amino acid residues in protein was measured as the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), detecting the area of high or low mobility. Radius of gyration (Rg) was estimated to assess the compactness of structure of the protein as a consequence of time. Also, the principal component analysis (PCA) was done for gaining insights on main movement in the protein, where PC1 and PC2 will be the leading movements of conformational change. These analyses revealed a wholesome picture of structural behavior of the complex under simulated physiological conditions.

In vitro study

Cell lines and culture conditions

In the present study, Vero cells (African green monkey kidney epithelial cells; ATCC 1-CCL-81) were chosen as in vitro model of cytotoxicity and MTT experiment. Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) was used to culture cells as it contains vital nutrients, amino acids, vitamins, and glucose. This medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) which added growth factors and proteins to ensure optimal cell proliferation. To avoid contamination with bacteria, PEN-STREP (antibiotic) 100 µg/ml were added. The cultures were held at 37 °C in a humidified incubator in 5% CO2. Subculture was done at near 80–90% confluency by application of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA reagent, followed by centrifugation and seeding to use in the experiment.

Preparation of compound dilutions

The candidate compound was dissolved in sterile water and made up as a stock solution (1 mg/mL) and diluted in complete DMEM immediately prior to each experiment.

Cytotoxicity assay

African green monkey kidney epithelial cells (Vero cell) were grown in Dulbecco Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) that consisted of 10% of fetal bovine (FBS) and 1% of penicillin- streptomycin. Cells were kept in humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% of CO₂. For cytotoxicity evaluation, the cells were seeded in the 96-well plates at the density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and incubated for 24 h to allow proper attachment. Further, the cells were exposed to increasing concentrations of Doxorubicin, 0 (control), 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL. Untreated negative controls as well as vehicle control groups were also included. Following 24 h of drug exposure, MTT assay was performed for assessing the cell viability. Each well was filled with 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Then the supernatant was discarded and 100 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. A microplate reader was utilized to measure the absorbance at 570 nm. Viability of cells was measured as a percentage against negative control population. GraphPad Prism software (nonlinear regression analysis) was used to obtain the dose-response curve and CC₅₀ (50% cytotoxic concentration).

MTT based cell viability assay

The activity of Doxorubicin against dengue virus serotype 2- infected vero cells was evaluated by measuring cell viability as an indirect indicator of the antiviral effect. Vero cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. These cells were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% of CO₂. The cells were seeded into the 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and then incubated for 24 h to allow cell attachment. For infection, 100 µL of the dengue virus type 2 (DENV-2)-positive serum sample was used as the source of the virus, with 10⁴ TCID 50 per well, and left to adsorb during 1 h of incubation at 37 °C. The inoculum was removed after the adsorption period, and fresh medium was added and post infection treatment was conducted by addition of various concentrations of Doxorubicin 0 (infected control) 3.125, 6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, 100 and 200 µg/mL. Every treatment was performed in triplicate and appropriate controls were included, virus only (untreated, infected) were also used as a control of reduction in cell viability due to viruses, the drug only (untreated, infected) to determine any cytotoxic activity of doxorubicin independent of viral infection and negative control (untreated, uninfected) was included to assess baseline cell viability.

After infection and 48 h of incubation,, the effects of the drug on cell viability in DENV-2–infected Vero cells were evaluated using the MTT assay, which measures cell viability and provides preliminary information on drug-associated cytotoxicity in infected cells and potential cell-protective effects. It is important to note that MTT assay does not directly quantify viral replication, infectivity, or viral load. After incubation, 20 µL of MTT (5 mg/mL) reagent was added in each well and plates were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. The supernatant was removed after incubation and 100 µL of DMSO was added to dissolve the formazan crystals. A microplate reader was applied to measure absorbance at 570 nm. The protection percentage was determined by reading the absorbance of the treated infected cells against the viral control. The IC₅₀ (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) was calculated using GraphPad Prism software through nonlinear regression analysis of the dose–response curve.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data of cytotoxicity, and MTT assays are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) in three independent experiments, where each experiment has three technical replications. Graph pad Prism was used to perform statistical analysis. Two-way ANOVA was used to complete group comparison to determine the combined effects of drug concentration and infection status on cell viability. The statistical significance was set to a p-value below 0.05.

Results

Preparation of RdRp for molecular Docking

As the virus remains common, there is no successful medicine available and the number of dengue cases is growing, it is crucial to discover and provide new drugs. Drug repurposing is one of the practical and fast approach adopted in this study to deal with this serious health issue [21]. Non-structural protein 5 (NS5) of the dengue virus plays important function in the life cycle of virus with the main tasks being as an RdRp and is well-conserved part in virus reproduction. The replication of the virus genome requires RdRp activity, that is why RdRP is the most attractive target for drug development against denuge. Molecular docking has become a critical computational method in drug discovery process, as it helps the researcher to determine how the small molecules binds or interacts with the biological targets especially proteins. Molecular docking allows predicting three-dimensional structures and binding affinities by acting as simulations of ligand-receptor interactions. The PDB contains a large amount of data regarding the protein-ligand complexes. In current study the RdRp 3D structure was obtained from protein data bank named with 6IZX 10.2210/pdb6IZX/pdb (Fig. 1).

The protein structures were optimized using Pymol in order to remove any irrelevant molecules. The linear chain consisting of range 2492–3391 amino acids was kept referring as the A chain and remaining all the constituents of the protein were eliminated so that further process was done effectively.

ADMET prediction

In the initial process of drug discovery, in silico ADMET prediction is important as it is an early assessment of drug-related pharmacokinetic properties that help in the hit-to-lead and the lead development [29]. The accuracy of these predictions has been further increased with advance pyrotechnics through machines provided by machine learning. In the current project, the deep scan into pharmacokinetic profiles of the two selected drugs was provided by the analysis of ADMET properties.

The pharmacokinetics parameters showed that the two ligands had good distributions. Their respective VDss implies that they are well distributed in the tissues and there is no high concentration in plasma. Also, the binding parameters of the two compounds in the serum were satisfactory with a balance being observed between unbound and bound states. Both ligands have a logBB and logPS within the safe range, subjecting them to cross the blood-brain barrier with no hazard to the central nervous system. Both ligands are in safe range regarding distribution (Table 5). Cytochrome P450 (enzyme) held for the detoxification in liver. Many drugs get deactivated by this enzyme but certain drugs can be activated. Inhibitors of this enzyme can directly affect the metabolism of drug hence should not be used. Similarly, Drug metabolism is mostly dependent on CYP2D6 and CYP3A. Inhibition to these affects the pharmacokinetics of the drug in use. Table 5 presents the metabolic properties i.e., CYP2D6, CYP1A2, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP3A4 these are absent in both ligands. CYP3A4 it is only present in rifamycin.

The Renal OCT2 substrate acts as a transporter that helps in clearing the drugs and other compounds. Total clearance indicates hepatic clearance which means the drug is metabolized and renal clearance indicates the drug is excreted. Doxorubicin has highest excretion value while rifamycin has low renal clearance. The toxicity values of rifamycin and doxorubicin are given below. Doxorubicin has a high MRTD value than rifamycin (Table 5). All other test values are in the safe range, that shows that both are not the cause for AMES toxicity and have a safe tolerated dose. They both are the hERG I and II inhibitors. They both have a safe toxic rate with respect to test on rat and on T. pyriformis with that they are not toxic to liver and does not provide any sensitivity to skin. Doxorubicin is toxic to liver and rifamycin is hERG II inhibitor while rest parameters are in safe range.

Molecular Docking analysis of selected compounds with DENV-2 RdRp

Molecular docking was carried to see the interaction between ligands and the RdRp protein of dengue virus with the help of CB dock. 3D and 2D structures were generated using UCSF Chimera and Discovery studio respectively, and these were analyzed to see how each ligand fits in the active site of the protein and what kind of interactions can be formed. The UCSF Chimera-based 3D docking visualization indicated that doxorubicin interacts with the palm domain of dengue virus RdRp domain (Chain A). Doxorubicin binds to a particular surface pocket of the polymerase which implies its possible blocking function. Doxorubicin showed stable binding to DENV RdRp with a binding energy of −8.9 kcal/mol, making contact with residues such as ASP808, MET809, TRP833, SER763, LYS756, as well as TYR832 (Table 7). Molecular dynamic simulation

The protein-ligand complex’s dynamic properties and stability under in silico physiological condition was also tested through MD simulations. The simulations have been done on the GROMACS (Groningen Machine for Chemical Simulations) software package, which is an open-source platform highly employed in the simulation of biomolecular systems due to its high efficiency.

Analysis of simulation

Various kind of analysis were undertaken in order to get a clear picture of the simulation.

Root mean square deviation

Root mean square deviation (RMSD) provides information on how the structure of protein or ligand has changed throughout the simulation. A low RMSD (less than 3.0 Å) is an indication that the structure did not change significantly and it is stable (42). The RMSD plot for the RdRpwith doxorubicin complex over 100 ns shows an initial rapid rise in RMSD to around 1.6 to 1.8 ~Å within first few nanoseconds followed by gradual increase and fluctuation between 2.0 and 2.8 ~Å until around 60 ns which indicates ongoing conformational changes and system equilibrium. Moreover, after 60 ns the RMSD begins to decrease slightly and stabilizes between 2.0 and 2.4 ~Å which shows that complex exhibits moderate structural deviation implying some flexibility but ultimately achieving relatively stable state (Fig. 4).

The RMSD plot for RdRp with rifamycin complex over a 100 ns molecular dynamics simulation indicates that the system undergoes an initial equilibrium phase with RMSD rapidly increasing to 1.5 ~Å in the first few nanoseconds followed by moderate fluctuations between 1.4 and 1.8 ~Å up to around 40 ns. However, the RMSD gradually rises and fluctuates around 2.0 to 2.5 ~Å suggesting some conformational flexibility or structural adaptation likely influenced by ligand binding. The complex remains relatively stable throughout simulation with no signs of significant unfolding or instability (Fig. 5). This implies that no significant alterations in the structure occur and a rigid, stable complex of rifamycin-RdRp is formed. Both complexes are stable, the RMSD values are relatively low in the acceptable range of < 3.0 Å, although rifamycin seems to cause a somewhat more rigid structure, whereas for doxorubicin the flexibility is intermediate.

Root mean square fluctuation

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) depicts where (which residues) there was the most movement in the protein. RMSF values tend to be large in flexible areas. The analysis by root means square fluctuation revealed tiny fluctuations in key binding sites except at the terminal and loop areas where flexibility was expected. The higher the peaks the more flexible that region will be implying moving and flexible segments of the protein structure. The RMSF for the RdRp doxorubicin complex reveals that most residues exhibit relatively low flexibility with fluctuations typically ranging from 0.8 to 1.8~Å which indicates that stable regions across the majority of the protein structure. Moreover, some residues specifically around positions 140, 310, 450 and closer residue 540 show significant higher fluctuations with peak reaching nearly 6 ~Å. However, these elevated fluctuations likely correspond to loop regions or terminal segments that are inherently more flexible or solvent exposed possibly playing role in ligand interaction or conformational adjustments during the simulation (Fig. 6). The RMSF plot of the RdRp rifamycin complex displays low flexibility across most residues with fluctuations ranging from 1 to 2 ~Å indicating stable protein ligand interaction. Moreover, the significant peaks are observed at the N terminal residue 1 and specifically around residue 540 which exceeds 10 ~Å. The highly flexible or disordered regions at these positions. These elevated fluctuations could be due to terminal regions, flexible loops or regions not directly stabilized by ligand binding and may contribute to local conformational dynamics crucial for protein function or ligand binding (Fig. 7). Finally, both complexes are stable and there is localized flexibility in certain sections which are important to interact with the ligands and activity of the protein.

Radius of gyration

The radius of gyration (Rg) shows the compactness of the protein. Lower Rg value indicates that atoms of proteins are closely packed which shows that protein is well folded and is stable (Neurosnap, 2021). Nevertheless, slightly lower Rg values are exhibited by doxorubicin that varies between 25.2 A and 25.8 A, which points to a highly compact structure and implies that the protein is in a well-folded, active form (Fig. 8). Alternatively, the Rg values of rifamycin lie in the range of 25.4 A to 26.0 A indicating slight structural flexibility without any significant change in conformation. These data indicate that the two complexes do not lose their structure and that doxorubicin has a somewhat more stable, compact structure than rifamycin (Fig. 9).

Cytotoxicity of doxorubicin

Effects of doxorubicin on Vero cells was establish to check the Cytotoxicity of drug. Earlier studies indicated that doxorubicin displays a high degree of cytotoxicity having an IC50 of about 12.8 +/- 0.8 µg/mL. Likewise, a replicon-based assay showed a 50% cytotoxic range of 13.76 µg/mL. In current study, the cytotoxicity of Doxorubicin on Vero cells was evaluated using morphological observations and the MTT assay after 24 h of exposure to various concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 µg/mL. The untreated Vero cells showed normal, healthy spindle-shaped morphology and a confluent monolayer, indicating no cytotoxic effects. A clear dose-dependent cytotoxic response was observed with increasing concentrations of Doxorubicin. At lower concentrations (3.125–6.25 µg/mL), the cell viability remained above 95%, indicating that Doxorubicin had little to no toxic effect on the cells at these doses. The morphology of cells also remained largely intact at these concentrations, with minimal structural disruption.

However, signs of cytotoxicity began to emerge from 12.5 µg/mL, where moderate morphological changes such as cell rounding, shrinkage, and partial disruption of the monolayer became evident. This corresponded with a reduction in cell viability to approximately 85%, suggesting early onset of cytotoxic effects. At 25 µg/mL, cytotoxicity became more pronounced, as evident from more extensive cell detachment, disorganization, and decreased cell density, with viability dropping to around 70%. Further damage was observed at 50 and 100 µg/mL, where cells displayed severe morphological alterations and loss of structural integrity, with viability decreasing to approximately 60% and 50%, respectively. The most significant cytotoxic effect was observed at 200 µg/mL, where almost complete cell detachment and destruction of the monolayer were observed. Cell viability at this concentration dropped below 40%, and the majority of cells appeared rounded and fragmented, indicating extensive damage. The quantitative results from the MTT assay supported the morphological observations (Fig. 13). Doxorubicin induced a significant, dose-dependent decrease in Vero cell viability. While viability remained above 95% at 3.125 and 6.25 µg/mL, it dropped to around 85% at 12.5 µg/mL and further declined to 70% at 25 µg/mL. Viability continued to decrease to approximately 60% at 50 µg/mL, 50% at 150 µg/mL, and reached as low as 38% at the highest concentration of 200 µg/mL. The calculated CC₅₀ (the concentration required to reduce cell viability by 50%) was approximately 150 µg/mL, indicating a moderate level of cytotoxicity.

These findings confirm that Doxorubicin exerts a significant cytotoxic effect on Vero cells in a concentration-dependent manner. While lower concentrations appear relatively safe, higher doses (≥ 12.5 µg/mL) cause substantial reduction in viability and noticeable morphological damage. This suggests that although Doxorubicin may be a potent therapeutic agent, its dosage must be carefully controlled to minimize host cell toxicity.

Treatment with Doxorubicin resulted in a gradual and significant increase in cell viability with increasing drug concentrations. At lower concentrations (3.125–12.5 µg/mL), a modest improvement in viability was observed, ranging from ~ 38% to ~ 40%. However, at 25 µg/mL, cell viability increased more notably to approximately 43%, and further rose to ~ 45% at 50 µg/mL. The 50% cell viability was observed at 86.52 µg/mL, where at 200 µg/mL, viability peaked at around 60%, suggesting strong cell viability at this concentration. It may be attributed to the drug’s cytotoxic effects at high doses, as shown in previous cytotoxicity assays. These findings suggest a concentration-dependent cell viability response in DENV-infected cells; however, the observed effects reflect MTT-based measurements of cell survival rather than direct assessment of viral replication.

Based on the observed increase in viability of infected cells with drug treatment, the IC₅₀ was estimated to be approximately 86.52 µg/mL. The IC₅₀ value was calculated as the concentration required to restore 50% cell viability relative to virus control, as determined by the MTT assay. Cytotoxicity analysis showed a CC₅₀ of approximately 150 µg/mL. Combined, these values produced a selectivity index (SI = CC50/IC50) of approximately 1.7, with a highly narrow therapeutic index. Even though there was an improved viability of infected cells on drug treatment at moderate concentrations (50–100 µg/mL), the low SI indicates that the antiviral potential of Doxorubicin is closely associated with cytotoxic effect. This limits its applicability for systemic antiviral use, reinforcing the need for careful dose optimization or structural modification to improve safety and selectivity.

Discussion

Dengue virus (DENV) has been a significant health problem in the world particularly in South and southeast Asia regions such as Pakistan, as seasonal outbreaks of this deadly virus have been associated with high disease burden [31]. Although its influence is increasing, there is no approved antiviral therapy available, and new drug repurposing is a fast and convenient approach to finding new potential candidates, particularly with high-conserved viral components. The RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) domain of the non-structural protein 5 (NS5), which is a vital enzyme of viral genome replication and a highly conserved component of all strains of DENV-2, is one of the most promising targets [21].

In the current study, structure-based virtual screening and drug repositioning were used to identify the potential inhibitors of dengue virus 2 [32]. Structure-based virtual screening represents the process of locating possible inhibitors based on the high-resolution crystal bi-products of viral proteins to predict and simulate atomic interactions [33]. Structure based virtual screening was done by shortlisting the top 15 compounds from ZINC library on the basis of binding affinity. These compounds were further screened on the basis of in silico ADMET profiling (pkCSM descriptors), molecular weight, intestinal absorption, BBB permeability, AMES mutagenicity, hepatotoxicity and skin sensitization [27, 28]. A number of high affinity compounds were eliminated because of predicted intestinal absorption was very low (0–17 per cent), extremely high molecular weights (> 900 Da) of some compounds or because of predicted mutagenicity. Among these compounds, known approved drugs, doxorubicin and rifamycin, were selected against the RdRp domain of DENV-2 (PDB ID: 6IZX) on the basis of availability, binding affinities, ADMET properties and their nature i.e. both are nature compounds, antibiotics and antivirals [24, 40]. Doxorubicin and rifamycin both exhibited favourable docking scores (–8.9 kcal/mol and − 8.6 kcal/mol, respectively), and acceptable ADMET properties, such as a high predicted intestinal absorption rate (~ 62–65%), AMES non-mutagenicity, low BBB penetrations (logBB − 1), and lack of predicted skin sensitization, and rifamycin also lacks predicted hepatotoxicity. The selection of doxorubicin and rifamycin as the specific antiviral agents to be subjected to docking and MD simulations and of doxorubicin as the agent to be tested in vitro was based on these criteria in combination with their clinical availability and prior reports of antiviral or flavivirus-affinity.

In silico study revealed doxorubicin and rifamycin to be the best RdRp binders, with doxorubicin showing a higher docking affinity (–8.9 kcal/mol) than rifamycin. The computing analysis of the doxorubicin in the present research showed that it has a high binding affinity to the catalytic core of the dengue RdRp and it interacts with other conserved amino acids such as Asp540, Lys693 and Ser710 of the palm domain of RdRp [41].

Simulations of MD revealed that both complexes of the ligands and RdRp were stable, and the RMSD value was less than 3.0 A showing stability, and the RMSF change was limited to the loops that were flexible. Rg analysis also verified that the two complexes retained compact and well-folded conformations, but doxorubicin had slightly reduced Rg values, suggesting that the structure is tighter. PCA showed that rifamycin induced a more constrained pattern of protein movement as compared to doxorubicin that permitted regulated flexibility in the catalytic site-as would be anticipated of an efficient inhibitor binding. These computational findings are supported by past facts that indicate that anthracyclines have the capability of inhibiting nucleic acid polymerases [38, 39]. Doxorubicin is an antineoplastic antibiotic, antiviral agent that has proven to have broad anticancer effects [34–36] and antiviral effects against HIV, herpes simplex virus, Rauscher leukemia virus, and the avian myeloblastosis virus [37, 40].

On the basis of in silico study particularly the docking score, MD stability matrices and availability of drug, doxorubicin was identified as most suitable drug for further in vitro analysis.

It was critical to establish the cytotoxic effects of doxorubicin on Vero cells. Earlier studies indicated that doxorubicin displays a high degree of cytotoxicity having an IC50 of about 12.8 +/- 0.8 µg/mL. Likewise, a replicon-based assay showed a 50% cytotoxic range of 13.76 µg/mL. Results of the current research indicated that Doxorubicin exhibited a dose-dependent cytotoxic effect on Vero cells. Low concentrations (3.125–6.25 µg/mL) were relatively non-toxic, maintaining over 95% cell viability. However, higher doses (≥ 12.5 µg/mL) caused significant reductions in viability, dropping to 50% at 100 µg/mL and as low as 38% at 200 µg/mL. The calculated CC₅₀ was approximately 150 µg/mL, indicating moderate cytotoxicity. The range of doxorubicin that could be safely used on vero cells was 3.125–12.5 µg/mL as at these concentrations cell viability was greater than 85%, and above these concentrations it leads to cytotoxicity (CC₅₀ ≈150 µg/mL).Cell viability based assays were performed with the same concentration range of doxorubicin in dengue virus infected vero cells through MTT based cell viability approach, and the findings showed that doxorubicin may have potential antiviral associated effect as reflected by changes in cell viability. These effects were characterized by change in cell morphology, clumping of cell and cell detachment to the culture substrate at the end of the incubation period. Microscopic cytopathic effects of DENV-2 infection were not identified. Morphological changes occur only at the concentration of doxorubicin above 12.6 µg/mL. On the basis of such observations, the concentrations of below 12.6 µg/mL were generally safe to be used in further antiviral assays. Doxorubicin showed dose-dependent effect on cell viability in dengue virus 2 infected Vero cells, with an estimated IC₅₀ of 86.52 µg/mL based on MTT assay. While effective at inhibiting the virus, higher doses were also cytotoxic, indicating the need for careful dose optimization and further in vivo evaluation. Compared with the previous evidence on doxorubicin and other repurposed antivirals, such as remdesivir and favipiravir, doxorubicin showed improved cell viability in infected vero cells as measured by the MTT assay, which is an indirect demonstration of inhibition of virus replication. Also its poor selectivity index (SI = 0.7) and established cytotoxicity make it less attractive as a direct clinical repurposing choice as an antiviral therapeutic. Unlike doxorubicin, remdesivir and favipiravir both are nucleotide analogs specifically engineered to target RdRp with greater and more directed antiviral effects with lesser severe side effects. This selectivity virulence makes remdesivir and favipiravir more viable against viral infections than doxorubicin which is a primarily a chemotherapy agent that has only experimental and indirect antiviral potential based on MTT derived cell viability data. The potential of doxorubicin as antiviral has been demonstrated through in silico stability with RdRp, although the mechanism of action is not understood because of the indirect antiviral readouts in the assays. The low selectivity index and toxicity prevents its application as a targeted antiviral, indicating that off target interactions might be contributing factor. The low selectivity index indicates that the observed effects are closely associated with cytotoxicity, thereby limiting translational relevance and supporting the interpretation of this study as exploratory rather than predictive of therapeutic efficacy. Derivatives or specific target delivery should be considered in future researches in order to enhance selectivity and minimize toxicity. Doxorubicin treatment increased the viability of infected cultures upto 12.5 µg/mL, suggesting a slightly protective effect of doxorubicin on infected cells; however, direct evidence of its antiviral action is still needed through specific virological endpoints such as plaque assay or focus-forming assay to quantify infectious viral output, qRT-PCR of viral RNA in supernatants or cells, or immunostaining/ELISA for viral antigens. Accordingly, all antiviral-related conclusions drawn in this study should be considered preliminary, as they are based solely on host cell viability measurements rather than direct virological endpoints. One of the limitations of this study is lack of additional orthogonal cytotoxicity assays beyond MTT-based viability measurements, which reduces the comparative strength of the in vitro findings. One of the limitations of the present study is the lack of a positive control and additional orthogonal cytotoxicity assays, which would have strengthened comparative interpretation and validation of the observed effects. Future studies incorporating these assays and additional in vivo studies will be important to further support and the extend the current findings.

Conclusion and future prospects

The in-silico study suggests that doxorubicin may interact with the molecular targets relevant to DENV serotype 2. However, MTT assay indicate slightly increase in cell viability of infected Vero cells at low concentration of doxorubicin While the compound exhibited dose dependent cytotoxicity at higher concentrations. Molecular docking and dynamic simulation showed intense interaction areas, complemented by the fact that the drug has the potential to disrupt nucleic acid pathways. Such in silico discoveries open the path to subsequent in vitro verifications, and, provided to be confirmed in the latter, they may become instrumental in the design of effective antivirals against DENV and possibly other flaviviruses. Doxorubicin demonstrated indirect, cell viability based effects in DENV-2 infected vero cells; however, the low selectivity index and cytotoxicity severely limit its clinical applicability. Given the low selectivity index and cytotoxicity-associated effects, the findings of this study should be regarded as exploratory, providing preliminary insights rather than predictive evidence of therapeutic efficacy. Due to the low selectivity index and toxicity of doxorubicin, it is concluded that, the future studies need to concentrate on the development and testing of its derivatives to reduce the side effects. Further investigations using less cytotoxic concentrations or alternative assays e.g. plaque assay, viral RNA quantification, or antigen detection are required to accurately assess its antiviral potential. Alternatively, doxorubicin can be modified structurally to enact changes such as increasing specificity and decreasing toxicity, so it may be more efficiently used as an antiviral agent. The strategy might be effective, which would further help in developing new antiviral therapeutics against dengue and other RNA viruses.
