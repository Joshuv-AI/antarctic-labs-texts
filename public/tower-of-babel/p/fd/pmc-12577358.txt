Background

Moebius syndrome (MS) is a rare congenital, non-progressive, symptomatic disorder characterized by loss of facial expression (absence of mimicry and strabismus), inability to close the eyelids, and various oral diseases due to congenital bilateral paralysis of the sixth and seventh cranial nerves (CN), in which the third, fourth, fifth, ninth, tenth and twelfth cranial pairs may also be affected [1]. Craniosynostosis is a head deformity that develops after premature closure of one or more cranial sutures.

In this article, we present a previously unreported association of Moebius Syndrome with sagittal synostosis to contribute to the clinical manifestations of the syndrome in the literature.

Material and method

Permission was obtained from the Local Ethics Committee. In 2023, the Pediatric Neurosurgery Department operated on a sagittal synostosis case associated with Moebius syndrome, retrieved from the neurosurgery archive.

The “literature” was scanned by including studies on human subjects of all ages published in English, using the MEDLINE/PubMed and EMBASE electronic databases. A comprehensive literature search was conducted, including studies on human subjects of all ages published in English, using the MEDLINE/PubMed and EMBASE electronic databases. The primary search utilized the keywords “endoscopy,” “sagittal synostosis,” “Moebius syndrome,” and “cranial vault surgery.” However, no previously reported cases of synostosis associated with Moebius syndrome were identified.

Case presentation

A 6-month-old girl who could not close her eyes and mouth after birth was followed up in another hospital with the diagnosis of Moebius Syndrome and was referred to us because of cranial deformity. The patient had a typical clinical appearance of scaphocephalic synostosis with a narrow, long head and prominent frontal curvature. On neurologic examination, general condition was good, Moro reflex was weakly positive, eye closure was present only during crying on the right side, lip and eye closure was present in routine situations, and facial asymmetry was present (Fig. 1).

In further investigations, CN VII agenesis on the left side and accompanying sagittal suture synostosis were reported on brain MRI, and REV3L gene mutation was found to be associated with Moebius Syndrome in genetic tests. In the REV3L gene mutation, a mistake was detected in the amino acid p.Glu387Gly. Open Cranial vault surgery was planned because the patient was syndromic.

Surgical procedure

Under general anesthesia in the supine position, the skin was opened after a bicoronal skin incision between both ears. Endoscopic treatment was not deemed appropriate for the patient who may have had minor cranial sutures affected secondary to this condition. The sagittal suture line was lifted as a bar in the midline, 1.5 cm right and 1.5 cm left. Then, osteotomies extending temporally and occipitally with 2 cm intervals were made laterally. The posterior border was opened to the posterior fontanelle. Lambdoid sutures were visualized, and osteotomies were performed. The coronal suture was preserved, and osteotomies were made at equal intervals from the frontal bone. Stretching was applied to the opened barrel osteotomies, a green stick fracture was created from the bottom, and the lateral diameter of the calvarium was increased (Fig. 2). The skin was closed after bleeding control. The patient was discharged on the 4th postoperative day without additional problems and is still being followed up without any new pathologic condition.

Discussion

Most cases of MS, a congenital paralysis of the cranial nerves, are sporadic and cause problems with sucking, swallowing, breathing, and phonation due to cranial nerve involvement and are sometimes associated with other malformations [2]. In some cases, associations with multiple malformations such as hand and finger anomalies, mental retardation and autism, and other syndromes such as Poland syndrome, Pierre Robin sequence, Carey-Fineman-Ziter, Klippel-Feil anomaly have also been reported [2, 3].

First described by von Graefe and Saemish in 1880, the syndrome was later developed by Paul Julius Moebius in 1888 and named Moebius syndrome [4]. In 1998, Abramson first proposed the abbreviation CLUFT to describe the syndrome: C, cranial nerve; L, lower extremity; U, upper extremity; F, face; T, thorax, to define the grading of the disease and to describe the heterogeneity of clinical features [3].

The prevalence of MS is approximately 1 in 125,000 live births, affecting both sexes equally [5]. The exact mechanism of occurrence is still unknown. In most cases, it occurs sporadically, with a family history or during pregnancy.

Studies suggest that both environmental and genetic factors, such as alcohol, cocaine, and exposure to various teratogenic agents, are among the etiologic causes. It is thought to be a significant factor affecting the development of the cranial nuclei secondary to impaired brain vascularity due to thrombosis, stenosis, and lack of formation of the vertebrobasilar system, which develops primarily in the first trimester of pregnancy [4].

The genetic molecular cause of MS is diverse. Defects in the PLXND1 gene, which is predicted to be involved in several processes, including endothelial cell migration by activating protein domain-specific binding activity; nervous system development; and regulation of angiogenesis; and the gene encoded by the REV3L gene, representing the catalytic subunit of DNA polymerase zeta involved in translesion DNA synthesis, have been implicated as the primary cause of Moebius syndrome [2].

In addition, Mederos-Mollineda et al. showed that mutations in MBS1, MBS2, and MBS3 (Moebius Syndrome-associated genes 1–2) genetic loci contribute to the development of this syndrome through various pathways. HOX genes from the family encoding homeobox domains have also been suggested to be effective in the genetic background of the syndrome because they play a role in abnormal brain development [2].

In radiologic imaging, the absence of cisternal-canalicular segments of the facial nerve (Fig. 3), bilateral facial colliculi and cisternal segments of bilateral abducens nerves, and flattened base of the fourth ventricle on brain MRI are specific findings. Other findings include brain stem hypoplasia, hypoplasia of the extraocular muscles, calcification in the pons in the region of the abducens nuclei, and cerebellar hypoplasia [6].

Sagittal synostosis is the most common form of isolated craniosynostosis. It accounts for approximately half of all synostosis cases, with an estimated incidence of 1 in 2000 [7]. Sagittal synostosis is often considered an isolated health event that is not associated with other medical conditions. Clinically, it is called scaphocephaly, which is caused by premature closure of the sutura sagittalis, resulting in a narrow-long skull with frontal bossing.

Fibroblast Growth Factor receptor genes (FGFR3 and FGFR2) are involved in syndromic craniosynostoses [8, 9]; the protein encoded by these genes binds to heparin and has broad mitogenic and angiogenic activities. It has been shown to play a role in various biological processes, such as nervous system development, wound healing, and tumor growth. Transcription factor genes (i.e., TWIST and MSX2) are these disorders’ most frequently mutated genes [10, 11]. In addition, bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) genes, TGFB2, ERF from the ETS transcription factor family, RUNX2, EFNB1, FAM20C, and LMX1B gene defects are other factors involved in the developmental stages of syndromic synostosis [12, 13]. Therefore, it may be useful to search for FGF and other genetic mutations in more detail in syndromic cases with head deformity.

However, we did not find any synostosis cases reported. We hope our case will contribute to the literature as the first case of synostosis in this respect and contribute to the scope of Moebius syndrome, which is still being studied today.

Conclusion

Moebius syndrome is a condition whose etiology and clinical spectrum remain incompletely understood and are continuously evolving with ongoing research. Therefore, it is crucial to thoroughly recognize the clinical findings in patients, guide them toward genetic studies when necessary, and remain attentive to newly identified manifestations that may contribute to advancing scientific knowledge in this field.
