Copper-catalysed dynamic kinetic (4+1) cyclization of 1,3-enynes for atroposelective arylpyrrole synthesis
Authors: Ya-Jun Zhong, Xiaoli Ren, Ting Qi, Ronggen Zhong, Qian Liu, Lei Hu, Wei Huang, Cheng Peng, Jun-Long Li, Bo Han, Gu Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Axially chiral arylpyrroles are a promising class of compounds with broad applications in catalysis, medicinal chemistry, and materials science. Herein, we report a copper-catalysed dynamic kinetic asymmetric (4 + 1) cyclization for the atroposelective synthesis of arylpyrroles bearing a single stereogenic axis or 1,2-diaxes from 1,3-enynes and amines. This dynamic kinetic asymmetric transformation employs a chiral Cu/Pybox complex, with air as the oxidant and DABCO serving as both a base and a proton shuttle. A key feature is the reversibility of the aza-Michael addition, which enables the dynamic formation/cleavage of distal C–N bonds. The 5-endo-dig cyclization serves as both the rate-determining and stereodetermining step, ensuring precise stereocontrol of the proximal stereocenters by the catalyst. This strategy overcomes long-standing challenges in the stereocontrol of distal C–N bond formation and enables the synthesis of atropisomeric DMAP catalysts with two stereogenic axes. DFT calculations provide insights into the mechanism of stereocontrol.

Introduction

Axially chiral compounds play a pivotal role in asymmetric catalysis1,2, drug development3–5, and materials science6–8. In particular, axially chiral arylpyrroles have garnered particular interest due to their unique structural features and functional properties. These compounds exhibit significant biological activities, making them promising candidates in drug discovery. For instance, the chiral 2-arylpyrrole derivative Esaxerenone (MINNEBRO®) is an approved antihypertensive drug9. Additionally, natural products featuring chiral arylpyrrole scaffolds, for example, marinopyrrole10 and neopyrrolomycin11,12, derived from actinomycetes, demonstrate potent antibacterial and anticancer properties. In addition to their medicinal applications, the incorporation of pyrrole rings into chiral frameworks has opened new avenues for the design of innovative chiral catalysts, further expanding their utility.

Considerable progress has been made in synthesizing optically pure pyrrole atropisomers13–16 employing strategies such as (dynamic) kinetic resolution17–20, desymmetrization of prochiral pyrroles21–24, and the de novo construction of pyrrole rings (Fig. 1a)25–27. Among these, de novo synthesis28–32 stands out for its distinct advantages. Unlike methods that rely on pre-formed pyrrole scaffolds, de novo approaches utilize simple and readily available starting materials, allowing for greater structural diversity. Despite these advantages, the de novo atroposelective synthesis of arylpyrroles remains underexplored. Notable advancements include Tan’s asymmetric Paal–Knorr reaction for C–N axial chirality33–36, asymmetric (3 + 2) annulations by Zhu, Chen, Lu, Mei, and Carretero37–42, Ye’s intramolecular diyne cyclization43,44, and Xu’s copper–vinylallenylidene-mediated annulation45–47. Nevertheless, the demand for new axially chiral arylpyrrole frameworks remains high, given their potential in catalyst design, drug discovery, and functional materials. The cascade (4 + 1) cyclization of 1,3-enynes with primary amines provides an efficient and environmentally friendly approach to pyrrole synthesis, offering 100% atom economy48–51. However, developing an asymmetric variant of this transformation poses a formidable challenge due to the inherent difficulties in stereocontrol.

To overcome these long-standing challenges, here we report a highly efficient Cu-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric (4 + 1) cascade cyclization for the construction of axially chiral arylpyrroles from readily available 1,3-enynes and aliphatic or aryl amines. Although dynamic kinetic asymmetric transformation (DyKAT) has been explored for the modulation of axial chirality52,53, its application to the de novo construction of axially chiral systems remains largely underdeveloped54–56, with most previous examples relying on pre-existing biaryl frameworks. In this work, a chiral Cu/Pybox catalyst, molecular oxygen from air as a green oxidant, and DABCO as a multifunctional additive are combined to enable efficient atroposelective pyrrole formation. This strategy allows precise control over axial chirality while addressing the intrinsic challenges associated with asymmetric (4 + 1) cyclization of primary amines with 1,3-enynes57–59.

Notably, this catalytic platform also provides a streamlined entry to axially chiral (dimethylamino)pyridine (DMAP) derivatives60–64, a class of Lewis base catalysts that remains largely unexplored in the context of axial chirality. Since the seminal development of centrally and planar chiral DMAP catalysts65–67, progress toward atropisomeric DMAP analogs has been limited, typically requiring multistep syntheses or resolution-based approaches68–72. The ability to access structurally diverse atropisomeric DMAP scaffolds through a modular cyclization strategy highlights the broader potential of this methodology in asymmetric catalysis and catalyst design.

Results

Reaction development

We began our study by using readily available 1,3-enyne73,74 1a and aniline 2a as model substrates to synthesize axially chiral 2-arylpyrrole 3a with a C–C axis (Table 1). To identify a suitable catalytic system, we first screened bifunctional tertiary amines, which have proven effective in reactions of hydroxycoumarin with 1,3-enynes75 (see Tables S1–S4 in the Supplementary Information for optimization details). Using air as a green terminal oxidant in dichloroethane (DCE) at room temperature, we tested squaramide catalysts C1 and C2 (derived from cinchona alkaloids and 1,2-cyclohexanediamine) as well as urea catalyst C3. While these catalysts facilitated complete conversion of 3a within 24 h, they resulted in poor stereoselectivity, highlighting the challenge of achieving stereocontrol in distal C–N bond formation (up to 60:40 er, entries 1–3). Given the role of Cu(II) complexes as both π-acidic catalysts and oxidation promoters, we next explored their use in the (4 + 1) cyclization. The addition of 10 mol% Cu(OTf)2 to the reactions significantly accelerated those catalyzed by C1–C3, yielding 3a within 3–5 h with improved yields and better atroposelectivity (up to 74:26 er, entries 4–6). However, using chiral Cu(II) complexes alone led to sluggish reactions, with ligands L1 and L2 affording 3a in low enantioselectivity (entries 7 and 8). In contrast, the Box ligand L3 provided some improvement, and the Pybox ligand L4 significantly enhanced enantioselectivity, achieving an er of 98:2 (entries 9 and 10).

Further optimization revealed that adding a basic additive could effectively promote the reaction, likely by facilitating proton transfer in the cascade. Among the various bases tested, DABCO (diazabicyclo[2.2.2]octane) emerged as the most effective, increasing the yield to 94% with an er of 95:5. Other organic and inorganic bases, such as K2CO3 or Et3N, reduced the stereoselectivity of the reaction (entries 11–13). Lowering the reaction temperature to –20 °C enhanced stereoselectivity (Table S4), while reducing it further to –30 °C improved the er value to 98:2 (entry 14). After examining various parameters, the optimal conditions were determined to be: Cu(OTf)2 (10 mol%), ligand L4 (12 mol%), and DABCO (1.0 equiv.) in DCE (1.0 mL) at –30 °C with air as the oxidant.

Substrate scope

With the optimized reaction conditions established, we first explored the substrate scope of different aryl amines in the Cu-catalyzed (4 + 1) cyclization of enyne 1a, yielding axially chiral pyrroles 3 products with diverse N-substituents. As shown in Fig. 2, aniline and phenylamines with different electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups substituted at the para-position were well tolerated, delivering products 3b–3h in 69–92% yields with 93:7 to 98:2 er. Para-substituted cyano and ester groups on the arylamine did not affect reactivity (3i, 3j), though the cyano group slightly lowered enantioselectivity (90:10 er). Similarly, meta-Cl and meta-CF3 substituents furnished products 3k and 3l with good yields and stereoselectivity, whereas meta-CH3 and –OCH3 groups caused a slight decrease in enantioselectivity (3m and 3n). Building on this success, we advanced the construction of axially chiral arylpyrroles with dual (C–C and C–N) axes. To our delight, reactions of ortho-substituted aryl and heteroaryl amines with enyne 1a furnished configurationally stable 1,2-diarylpyrroles (3p–3s) with excellent enantioselectivity (>96:4 er). All reactions also exhibited exclusive diastereoselectivity, demonstrating the successful transfer of the ligand’s chiral information to the distal C–N axis and underscoring the strategy’s versatility and effectiveness. The structure and relative configuration of the 1,2-diaxes in product 3p were identified by X-ray crystallography (CCDC 2411166). It is worth noting that N4,N4-dimethylpyridine-3,4-diamine (3-amino-DMAP) afforded product 3t in 87% yield with excellent enantioselectivity as a single diastereoisomer (98:2 er and >20:1 dr), enabling access to atropisomeric DMAP catalysts with dual stereogenic axes.

We then examined the scope of aliphatic amines in the construction of diverse axially chiral arylpyrroles (Fig. 2, bottom panel). Primary amines exhibited consistently high yields and good enantioselectivities. Linear and branched alkylamines, including 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-amine, isobutylamine, butylamine, pentylamine, and benzylamine, reacted smoothly to afford the corresponding products (4a–4e) with high efficiency. Cyclopropanamine (4f) and sterically hindered amantadine (4g) were also well tolerated, maintaining good yields and atroposelectivities. Functionalized amines, including mono- and polyfluorinated ethanamines (4h–4j) and chloro-substituted amines (4k, 4l), yielded products with excellent enantioselectivities (94:6 to 99:1 er). The structure and absolute configuration of product 4i were confirmed by X-ray crystallography (CCDC 2387480). Substrates 2m and 2n, bearing cyclic or linear ether groups (tetrahydropyran and benzyloxy groups), delivered good yields of 79% and 93%, respectively, with high enantioselectivities. Boc-protected azetidine-3-amine exhibited comparable reactivity, affording product 4o in 84% yield and an er of 97:3. A long-chain ethanolamine derivative with a carboxylic acid ester was compatible with the reaction, yielding product 4p (87% yield, 95:5 er).

Additionally, an aliphatic amine featuring a terminal alkyne moiety underwent cyclization to yield 4q in 85% yield with 95:5 er, demonstrating potential for late-stage diversification. Interestingly, using this method, enyne 1a could selectively undergo cascade (4 + 1) cyclization with the ε-amino group of N-terminal-protected lysine, yielding axially chiral arylpyrrole 4r with good yield and excellent diastereoselectivity. Moreover, two dipeptide substrates with lysine residues, 2s (with lysine at the C-terminus) and 2t (with lysine at the N-terminus), also underwent (4 + 1) cyclization smoothly under standard conditions, yielding the corresponding products 4s and 4t in excellent yields and stereoselectivities, respectively. These results underscore the compatibility of the reaction with a wide range of aliphatic amines, including those bearing various functional groups or steric profiles.

Next, we evaluated various nitro-enyne substrates with aniline 2a (Fig. 3). Alkynes bearing diverse aryl groups, including electron-rich and electron-poor substituents, successfully formed axially chiral arylpyrroles (5a–5f). Sterically hindered aryl groups (5g and 5h) and thiophenyl substituents (5i) were also well tolerated, giving products with high enantioselectivity (95:5–98:2 er). The reaction of enyne with a larger substituent, 3-deoxy-estrone, provided the corresponding axially chiral 3-arylpyrrole-deoxy-estrone 5j in high yield with excellent stereocontrol (98:2 dr). Notably, in addition to aryl-substituted enynes, alkyl-substituted enynes also underwent the dynamic kinetic (4 + 1) cyclization smoothly. Synthetic applications

To assess the scalability of this Cu-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric (4 + 1) cyclization, we performed the model reaction on a 1.0 mmol scale under standard conditions (Fig. 4a). The reaction demonstrated excellent practicality and efficiency, affording 438 mg of axially chiral product 3a (94% yield, 98:2 er). Subsequent synthetic transformations showcased the versatility of this method, enabling access to a diverse array of chiral arylpyrrole derivatives. For instance, treatment of 3a with N-bromosuccinimide (NBS) or N-chlorosuccinimide (NCS) selectively afforded brominated and chlorinated products 6a and 6b, respectively, at the pyrrole 3-position, while maintaining excellent stereoselectivity (99:1 er). Expanding on our interest and previous work in DMAP catalysis76–78, we efficiently synthesized atropisomeric DMAP catalysts 3t and 3u from commercially available starting materials via our (4 + 1) cyclization approach (Fig. 5c). A rotational barrier of 34.9 kcal mol–1 was obtained for the stereogenic axis in 3t, suggesting that racemization is highly unlikely and supporting its suitability as a platform for catalyst development. Furthermore, oxidation of 3t with meta-chloroperoxybenzoic acid (m-CPBA) afforded the novel DMAP N-oxide catalyst OC-5, an important Lewis base catalyst. To evaluate their catalytic potential, we applied these new catalysts in the recently developed (2 + 3) cycloaddition of yne-enones 16 with oxindole-derived Morita–Baylis–Hillman (MBH) carbonates 17 (Fig. 5d).79 Notably, atropisomeric DMAP catalyst 3t, bearing C–C and C–N 1,2-diaxes, exhibited excellent compatibility with PPh3AuCl, delivering superior stereoselectivity (18, up to >20:1 dr, 93:7 er) compared with conventional catalysts. In contrast, widely used DMAP catalysts, such as bulky proline-derived Connon type catalyst OC-6, displayed moderate stereoselectivity (70:30 er), while chiral ligands such as Sadphos led to racemic products with low yields. These findings underscore the remarkable efficiency and selectivity of our newly developed atropisomeric DMAP catalysts, paving the way for further advancements in asymmetric catalysis.

Mechanistic investigations

To elucidate the mechanism of the cascade (4 + 1) cyclization, we conducted a series of control experiments and computational studies (Fig. 6a). No reaction occurred in the absence of the copper catalyst, confirming its essential role. When the reaction was performed using deuterated aniline (aniline-d₇), the deuterium incorporation at the pyrrole core demonstrated that the pyrrole hydrogen atoms originate from the aniline nucleophile. To determine the enantiodetermining step, we designed control experiments to separate the cyclization and oxidation stages. First, the reaction was conducted under standard Cu(II)/L4 conditions in a glovebox, followed by the addition of stoichiometric Pb(OAc)₄, a known oxidant for dihydropyrroles39. Product 3a was obtained in 82% yield with 96:4 er, nearly identical to the standard aerobic conditions, indicating that the oxidation step does not impact stereoselectivity. Next, when racemic 3a (generated using organocatalyst C2, entry 2 in Table 1) was treated with L4/Cu(OTf)₂ under air, the enantioselectivity remained unchanged, confirming that the Cu catalyst does not influence chirality during oxidation. Similarly, when cyclization was performed using C1 (60:40 er, entry 1 in Table 1), subsequent treatment with either L4/Cu(OTf)₂/air or Pb(OAc)₄ produced 3a with nearly identical er values. These results unequivocally demonstrate that the 5-endo-dig cyclization, rather than oxidation, is the enantiodetermining step. This finding has a critical mechanistic implication: the C(sp³)–C(sp²) bond between the dihydropyrrole and naphthyl rings cannot freely rotate after cyclization. If rotation occurred before oxidation, racemization or erosion of enantiopurity would result, contradicting our experimental observations of excellent chirality transfer.

Subsequently, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to further elucidate the mechanism of the Cu-catalyzed asymmetric (4 + 1) cyclization reaction and central-to-axial chirality transfer, focusing on stereoselectivity control (see p. S72 in the Supplementary Information for detailed computational methods. The Cartesian coordinates of all optimized structures are provided in Supplementary Data 1). As illustrated in Fig. 6b, the reaction begins with the aza-Michael addition of the 2a–DABCO complex to the 1,3-enyne 1a–Cu(II)L4 complex, forming the first C–N bond via transition state TS1. The Si-face attack pathway (right side) through (S)-TS1 has a low activation barrier (ΔG‡) of 10.6 kcal mol⁻¹. An alternative DABCO orientation, (S)-TS1’, exhibits higher energy. This aza-Michael addition yields (S)-II, followed by DABCO-H⁺-assisted protonation to form intermediate (S)-III. The protonation transition state (S)-TS2 (ΔG‡ = 14.2 kcal mol⁻¹) for the Re-face attack of DABCO-H⁺ has a lower activation barrier than (S)-TS2’ (ΔG‡ = 17.1 kcal mol⁻¹), indicating that Re-face protonation is kinetically favored due to less steric hindrance.

Next, the reaction proceeds via a 5-endo-dig intramolecular nucleophilic attack of the amine group on the metal-coordinated triple bond, forming the second C–N bond while undergoing simultaneous deprotonation, leading to intermediate (S)-IV. This cyclization step represents the rate- and enantioselectivity-determining step with a ΔG‡ of 28.1 kcal mol⁻¹ relative to (R)-II. Finally, intermediate (S)-IV undergoes protonation to yield the pro-product (S)-V, while the chiral Cu catalyst is regenerated in the presence of DABCO.

Given that our control experiments demonstrated the C(sp³)–C(sp²) bond between the dihydropyrrole and naphthyl rings cannot freely rotate after cyclization, the configuration of the final axially chiral product is determined by the naphthyl orientation established during the cyclization step. To understand this critical aspect of central-to-axial chirality transfer, we systematically analyzed the naphthyl ring orientation in the enantioselectivity-determining transition states. Guided by literature precedents39, we reasoned that the phenyl substituent on the naphthyl ring would preferentially orient outward in (S)-TS3 and (S)-TS3’ (ultimately generating 3a) while orienting inward in (R)-TS3 and (R)-TS3’ (generating ent-3a), as these conformations should minimize steric hindrance.

To verify this conformational hypothesis, we located an alternative transition state (S)-TS3-conf, where the phenyl substituent on the naphthyl group adopts the opposite orientation through rotation around the C(sp³)–C(sp²) bond. The substantial energy difference (ΔΔG‡ = 5.3 kcal/mol, with (S)-TS3 at 22.6 kcal mol⁻¹ and (S)-TS3-conf at 27.9 kcal mol⁻¹) confirms our hypothesis that the naphthyl orientation is selectively determined during cyclization. Using the independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) analysis80 (Fig. 7a), we identified the key factors governing this selectivity. In the favorable (S)-TS3, the naphthyl group engages in significant π–π interactions and lone pair–π (LP–π) stacking with both the nitro group and the ligand, providing crucial stabilization. In contrast, in (S)-TS3-conf, rotation of the naphthyl ring causes the phenyl substituent to introduce destabilizing steric repulsion while simultaneously disrupting these beneficial non-covalent interactions. Quantitatively, buried volume (%VBuried) analysis reveals that (S)-TS3-conf exhibits significantly higher steric crowding (71.0%) compared to (S)-TS3 (65.1%), providing further evidence for the enhanced steric repulsion in the rotated conformation (Fig. 7b). Beyond naphthyl orientation, we investigated the origin of stereoselectivity at the forming stereocenter by analyzing non-covalent interactions in the competing transition states (S)-TS3 and (R)-TS3. As shown in Fig. 7c, the favored (S)-TS3 exhibits stronger π–π stacking and LP–π interactions. Furthermore, the adjacent stereogenic center of the naphthyl group adopts an S-configuration, minimizing steric repulsion with the nitro group. In contrast, in the disfavored (R)-TS3, the R-configuration of the naphthyl stereogenic center creates significant steric repulsion between the nitro group and both the aniline phenyl group and the oxazoline ligand. Buried volume analysis further reveals that (R)-TS3 exhibits higher steric crowding (69.5%) compared to (S)-TS3 (65.1%), resulting in a substantially higher activation energy (ΔΔG‡ = 6.3 kcal/mol).

Overall, computational results indicate that the aza-Michael addition steps (from I to III) are reversible, while the intramolecular 5-endo-dig cyclization via (S)-TS3 is exergonic and irreversible (from III to IV), establishing it as both the rate-determining and enantioselectivity-determining step. The unfavorable (R)-IIIb dynamically reverts to the reactant state and is subsequently converted into the more favorable (S)-IIIb. Thus, the entire cascade reaction achieves stereocontrol through a dynamic kinetic asymmetric transformation (DyKAT) strategy, where the reversible aza-Michael addition enables dynamic interconversion of intermediates, effectively addressing remote stereocontrol challenges. The chiral copper catalyst precisely governs both the formation of the stereocenter and the fixation of naphthyl orientation during the 5-endo-dig cyclization, representing a central-to-axial chirality transfer mechanism and leading to high stereoselectivity in the final axially chiral product. Besides, DFT calculations further revealed the role of Cu(II) species as the active catalyst in the rapid oxidation of the pro-product V to the final product 3a through a three-step pathway involving deprotonation, single-electron transfer (SET), and hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) (see Supplementary Fig. S4 for details).

Discussion

We have developed a Cu-catalyzed dynamic kinetic (4 + 1) cyclization of 1,3-enynes for the de novo synthesis of axially chiral arylpyrroles with a single stereogenic axis or 1,2-diaxes. This method offers excellent atroposelectivity, high yields, an environmentally friendly process, and broad substrate tolerance with various nitro-enynes and aliphatic or aryl amines, making it highly versatile for synthesizing diverse chiral arylpyrrole derivatives. We prepared a series of atropisomeric DMAP catalysts bearing 1,2-diaxial chirality, which demonstrated strong catalytic activity in challenging reactions, highlighting their potential in asymmetric catalysis. This work applies the DyKAT strategy to the de novo synthesis of axially chiral compounds, effectively addressing the long-standing challenge of asymmetric (4 + 1) cyclization of primary amines with 1,3-enynes.

Methods

General procedure for the synthesis of products 3, 4, and 5

In a 10 mL reaction tube, Cu(OTf)2 (3.6 mg, 0.010 mmol, 10 mol%) and ligand L4 (4.8 mg, 0.012 mmol, 12 mol%) were stirred in DCE (0.5 mL) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, 1,3-enyne 1 (0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) and DABCO (11.3 mg, 0.10 mmol, 1.0 equiv.) were added. A solution of amine 2 (0.12 mmol, 1.2 equiv.) in DCE (0.5 mL) was then added dropwise at −30 °C. The reaction mixture was stirred at −30 °C for 48 h. After completion, as monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC), the reaction mixture was concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography using petroleum ether/ethyl acetate as the eluent to afford the corresponding chiral products.
