Comparison of ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus growth hormone-releasing hormone neuron counterregulatory neurochemical marker gene expression in young adult versus middle-aged male and female rats
Authors: Rami Shrestha, Subash Sapkota, Karen P Briski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAging impairs glucose counterregulatory function by unknown mechanisms. Ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus/dorsomedial division (VMNdm) growth hormone-releasing hormone (Ghrh) neurons control counterregulation through Ghrh-modulated discharge of structurally diverse co-expressed neurochemicals. Current research used gene silencing and single-cell laser-catapult-microdissection/multiplex qPCR tools to investigate whether aging alters eu- and/or hypoglycemic patterns of co-produced counterregulatory-inhibiting (γ-aminobutyric acid) or -stimulating (Ghrh; glutamate; nitric oxide) neurotransmitter marker genes in this cell population. Data document basal Ghrh mRNA diminution in older, i.e., middle-aged rats of each sex and age-specific Ghrh transcriptional responses to hypoglycemia in females. Older rats showed Ghrh repression of glutamate decarboxylase67 (GAD67) and GAD65 mRNAs, a gain of inhibitory tone in female. In middle-age male rats, both GAD transcripts were refractory to hypoglycemia, an age-related response loss. In older females, hypoglycemia stimulated GAD65 gene expression, contrary to Ghrh-mediated repression in young adult animals. Ghrh suppressed glutaminase (GLS) mRNA in middle-aged rats of each sex, like young adults. Unlike young adult males, older males showed no GLS transcriptional response to hypoglycemia; older hypoglycemic females exhibited up-regulated GLS mRNA, differing from down-regulated expression in younger animals. Aging reduced neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS) gene profiles in females only; nNOS transcription was increased by hypoglycemia in middle-aged rats irrespective of sex, but was unopposed by Ghrh, indicating loss of this inhibitory tone. Results bolster the notion that by midde-age, aging may adjust, according to sex, multi-modal transmitter signaling by VMNdm Ghrh neurons to the brain glucose-regulatory network. Further research is justified to ascertain consequences of aging-associated patterns of co-expressed transmitter release on counterregulation in each sex.Key Words: Ghrh, insulin-induced hypoglycemia, GAD65, SF-1, glutaminase, sex differences

Introduction

The brain consumes a disproportionate amount of bodily energy to maintain essential, high energy-demand nerve cell functions, and is reliant upon uninterrupted provision of its primary metabolic fuel glucose. Hypoglycemia constitutes a potent threat of neurological dysfunction and injury to vulnerable central nervous system (CNS) neuron populations (Kalimo et al., 1985; Auer, 1986; Agardh et al., 1992; Auer and Siesjö, 1993). To achieve the critical physiological objective of preserving systemic glucose homeostasis, i.e., euglycemia, the brain utilizes sensory cues on central and peripheral cellular energy status to shape effector actions that regulate glucose uptake, utilization, synthesis, and storage; insulin and counter-regulatory hormone secretion; and food intake (Watts and Donovan, 2010). The hypothalamus, the hierarchal visceral motor center of the CNS, operates within an extensive network of fore-, mid-, and hindbrain structures to impose ultimate control of autonomic, neuroendocrine, and behavioral motor/counterregulatory responses to systemic glucose imbalance (Carey et al., 2013; La Fleur et al., 2014; Devarakonda and Mobbs, 2016; Leloup et al., 2016; Roh et al., 2016; López-Gambero et al., 2019; Yoon and Diano, 2021; Thorens, 2022).

Diabetes mellitus refers to a group of metabolic disorders that are characterized by dysregulated glucose metabolism resulting in elevated blood glucose levels, i.e., hyperglycemia. Strict control of diabetes-associated hyperglycemia by insulin pharmacotherapy is required to prevent widespread deleterious damage to multiple organ systems but is unfortunately complicated by unremitting iatrogenic insulin-induced hypoglycemia (IIH) (Cryer, 2013, Cryer, 2014, Cryer, 2015). Diabetes is a prevalent health problem in the elderly, causing acute and long-term complications that contribute to significant morbidity and mortality in those patients (Wong, 2015; Sircare et al., 2016; Longo et al., 2019). Diabetes in aged adults constitutes an escalating public health burden as these individuals comprise one of the swiftest growing segments of the diabetic patient population (Kalyani et al., 2017; Meneilly et al., 2018; Freeman, 2019). Elderly diabetic patients face an exacerbated risk of harmful effects of hypoglycemia as counterregulatory, i.e., corrective hormone outflow and neurogenic awareness are impaired with age (Muggeo et al., 1985; Marker et al., 1992; Ortiz-Alonso et al., 1994; Kalyani and Egan, 2013; Kalyani et al., 2017; Chia et al., 2018). At present, the mechanism(s) that underlies aging-related counterregulatory dysfunction remains unknown.

The dorsomedial division of the ventromedial hypothalamic nucleus (VMNdm) is the sole location in the brain wherein the nuclear transcription factor steroidogenic factor-1 (SF-1/NR5a1) is synthesized (Cheung et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2019). SF-1 is implicated in neural regulation of systemic energy balance and glucose homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2008; Kim et al., 2011; Xu et al., 2011; Choi et al., 2013; Garfield et al., 2014; Meek et al., 2016). Recent reports show that VMNdm growth hormone-releasing hormone (Ghrh) neurons exhibit hypoglycemia-sensitive SF-1 mRNA, and that Ghrh neurotransmission affects hypoglycemic patterns of counterregulatory hormone secretion (Sapkota et al., 2023). VMNdm Ghrh neurons also expresses gene transcripts that encode enzyme markers for characterized non-neuropeptide counterregulatory neurotransmitters, i.e., the labile gas nitric oxide (NO) and the amino acids γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and glutamine (Glu); these co-expressed neurochemical gene profiles are subject to neuromodulatory control by Ghrh receptor (Ghrh-R) signaling. VMNdm Ghrh neurons are evidently controlled, in part, by intrinsic metabolic sensory cues as these nerve cells express transcripts for requisite molecular machinery, i.e. the glucose sensor glucokinase (GCK) and the energy sensor 5’-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (Sapkota et al., 2024).

Present studies applied combinatory in situ immunocytochemistry/laser-catapult-microdissection/single-cell multiplex qPCR tools, within the context of a validated whole-animal IIH model, to investigate the hypothesis that the aging process between young adulthood and middle-age may affect VMNdm Ghrh neuron expression of gene transcripts that encode SF-1 and protein markers for glucose counterregulation-heightening or -inhibiting neurotransmitters. The VMN is a major constituent of a distinctive sex-dimorphic network in the brain that is established by estrogen receptor imprinting during the ‘critical period’ of central nervous system development (Simerly, 2002). The VMN exerts sex-specific control of numerous functions, including sexual and maternal behaviors, physical activity, and metabolic homeostasis (Musatov et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2013; Martínez de Morentin et al., 2014; Correa et al., 2015; Hashikawa et al., 2017). Our recent studies show that Ghrh imposes sex-dimorphic control of co-expressed glucose-regulatory transmitter gene profiles under conditions of eu- and hypoglycemia (Sapkota et al., 2023). To align with the current U.S. National Institutes of Health policy emphasis on evaluation of sex as a critical biological variable, the study design implemented here involved young and middle-aged adult rats of each sex to address the premise that attainment of this key milestone in the lifespan may be accompanied by changes in baseline and/or hypoglycemia-associated patterns of Ghrh and co-expressed transmitter marker gene expression in VMNdm Ghrh neurons according to sex.

Materials and methods

Animals

Young adult (2–3 months of age) and older, i.e., middle-aged (11–12 months of age) male and female Sprague Dawley rats were housed communally by sex in shoe-box cages (2–3 animals per cage), under a 14-h light: 10-h dark cycle (lights on at 05.00 h). Animals were accustomed to handling by daily gentling prior to initiation of experimentation and had unhampered access to standard laboratory chow (prod. no. Harlan Teklad LM-485; Harlan Industries, Madison, WI) and tap water. Study protocols and procedures were performed in conformity with the NIH Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, 8th Edition, under ULM Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approval.

Experimental design

On Study Day 1, middle-age rats of each sex were randomly assigned to four treatment groups (n = 4 animals/sex/treatment group). Young male (n = 4) and female (n = 4) rats were designated as age-associated controls. Animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 9.0 mg ketamine/1.0 mg xylazine/0.1 mL/100 g bw before bilateral injection (total injection volume: 1.0 µL; infusion rate: 3.6 µL) of Ghrh siRNA (500 pmol; Accell siRNA rat Ghrh, set of 4; prod. no. A-089046–16–0010; Horizon, Cambridge, UK) or scramble (SCR) siRNA (500 pmol; Accell Control Pool Non-Targeting; prod. no. D-001910–10–20; Horizon) to the bilateral VMN (total volume: 1.0 μL; infusion rate: 3.6 μL/min; coordinates: −2.5 mm posterior to bregma, 0.6 mm lateral to midline, 9.0 mm ventral to skull surface), as described (Sapkota et al., 2023). Injections were made using a 33-gauge Neuros syringe (prod. no. 53496; Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL), using a Neurostar stereotactic Drill and Injection Robot (Neurostar, Tubingen, Germany). Post-surgical treatments included ketophen (sc; Zoetis Inc., Kalamazoo, MI) and enrofloxacin (IM; Bayer HealthCare LLC, Animal Health Division, Shawnee Mission, KS) injections and topical 0.25 % bupivacaine application to closed incisions. After full recovery from anesthesia, animals were transferred to single-occupancy cages. On Study Day 7, male and female rats were injected sc at 09.00 hr with vehicle (V; sterile diluent; Eli Lilly & Co., Indianapolis, IN) or neutral protamine Hagedorn insulin (INS; 10.0 U/kg bw; Eli Lilly (Napit et al., 2019)); animals were sacrificed by rapid decapitation one hr post-injection. Individual brains were dissected whole, then snap-frozen by immersion in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane for storage at −80 °C.

Individual VMNdm Ghrh neuron laser-catapult-microdissection

Consecutive 10 µm-thick fresh frozen tissue sections of the VMN were collected between −1.80 to −2.3 mm posterior to bregma and mounted on polyethylene naphthalate membrane-coated slides (prod. no. 415190–9041–000; Carl Zeiss Microscopy LLC, White Plains, NY). Serial sectioning of each brain was initiated rostral to the retrochiasmatic area, at the approximate level of the optic chiasm, and continued until the VMN was reached; sections then cut through the rostral VMN were processed to acquire pure Ghrh nerve cell samples by in situ immunocytochemistry/laser-catapult microdissection for gene expression analysis. Several distinctive neuro-topographic features were used to verify the rostro-caudal progression of tissue sectioning, including continuity of the third ventricle with lateral ventricles, which occurs at the level of the suprachiasmatic nucleus; derivation of the lateral optic tracts from the midline optic chiasm; and rostro-caudal changes in curvature of the ventral surface of the hypothalamus. Sections were fixed with ice-cold acetone (5 min) prior to blocking (2 h) with 1.5 % normal goat serum (prod. no. S-1000, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS), pH 7.4, supplemented with 0.05 % Triton X-100. Tissues were then incubated (48–72 h; 4°C) with a rabbit primary antiserum against preproGhrh (prod. no. PA5–102738, 1:2500; Invitrogen, Waltham, MA), as described (Sapkota et al., 2023), followed by a horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1 h; prod. no. PI-1000, 1:1000; Vector Lab.). Ghrh immunoreactivity (-ir) was visualized with ImmPACT 3,3’-diaminobenzidine peroxidase substrate kit reagents (prod. no. SK-4105; Vector Lab.). Ghrh-ir-positive neurons were individually isolated and removed from tissue sections using a Zeiss P.A.L.M. UV-A microlaser IV system and collected into an adhesive cap (prod. no.415190–9181–000; Carl Zeiss) containing lysis buffer (4 µL; SingleShot Cell Lysis Kit prod. no. 1725080; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA) for single-cell multiplex quantitative reverse transcription PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis.

Single-cell multiplex RT-qPCR analysis

Complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis and amplification: For each animal, n = 3 Ghrh-ir neurons were collected for single-cell gene expression analysis. Single-cell lysates were centrifuged (3000 rpm; 4 °C; 5 min) prior to serial 25 °C (10 min) and 75 °C (5 min) incubations in a Bio-Rad iCyclerQ RT-PCR Detection System. RNA integrity, purity, and quantity was determined using a Bio-Rad Experion gel-based electrophoresis system. Single-cell mRNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA by addition of 1.5 µL iScript™ Advanced cDNA Synthesis Kit buffer prod. no. 1725038; Bio-Rad), followed by 46°C incubation (20 min) and 95 °C denaturation (1 min), as described (Ali et al., 2022a, Ali et al., 2022b; Alshamrani et al., 2022). Pre-amplification master mix was prepared by combining SsoAdvanced™ Pre-Amp Supermix (prod. no. 1725160; Bio-Rad) with individual target and housekeeping gene Bio-Rad PrimePCR™ PreAmp for SYBR Green Assays [Ghrh (prod. no. qRno-CID0007723), SF-1/Nr5A1 (prod. no. qRnoCID0001458), GAD1/GAD67 (prod. no. qRnoCID0004554), GAD2/GAD65 (prod. no. qRnoCID0003485), NOS1/nNOS (prod. no. qRnoCED0009301), GLS (prod. no. qRno-CID0007756), Ghrh-R (prod. no. qRnoCED0003825), GAPDH (prod. no. qRnoCID0057018)]. A 9.5 µL pre-amplification master mix aliquot was added to individual cDNA samples prior to initial 95 °C incubation (3 min), followed by 20 cycles of consecutive 95 °C (15 s) and 58 °C (4 min) incubations. cDNA samples were diluted with IDTE (185 µL; prod. no. 11–05–01–05; Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc., Coralville, IA). RT-qPCR analysis: PCR samples were prepared by combining target gene Bio-Rad primers [Ghrh (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCID0007723), SF-1/NR5A1 (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCID0001458), glutamate decarboxylase (GAD)67/GAD1 (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCID0004554), GAD65/GAD2 (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCID0003485), nNOS/NOS1 (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCED0009301), glutaminase (GLS) (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCID0007756), Ghrh receptor (Ghrh-R) (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCED0003825), GAPDH (0.5 µL; prod. no. qRnoCID0057018)], 2 µL cDNA sample, and 5 µL iTaq™ Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (prod. no. 1725121; Bio-Rad). PCR samples were added to individual wells of hard-shell 384-well PCR plates (prod. no. HSP3805, Bio-Rad) for analysis in a Bio-Rad CFX384™ Touch Real-Time PCR Detection System. Samples were initially denatured at 95°C (30 sec), then incubated over 40 cycles of initial 95 °C incubation (3 sec), followed by 30 sec incubation at 60 °C for GAD1, at 59.9 °C for GAD2, 59.1 °C for SF-1/Nr5A1, 58.8 °C for GLS, 58.5 °C for Ghrh or Ghrh-R, 58.0 °C for nNOS/NOS1, or 57.3°C for GAPDH. Melt curve analysis was performed to detection nonspecific products and primer dimer formation. Data were normalized to GAPDH using the comparative Ct (2-ΔΔCt) method.

Western blot analysis of Ghrh protein expression in laser-microdissected Ghrh-ir neurons

For each treatment group, three independent cell lysate pools (n = 50 cells/pool/treatment group) were created for Ghrh protein analysis, as described (Sapkota et al., 2023). Thus, cell lysates from each animal were included in each treatment group sample pool. Sample pool proteins were separated by electrophoresis in Bio-Rad TGX 12 % stain-free gels (prod. no. 1610185, Bio-Rad Laboratories Inc., Hercules, CA). Stain-Free imaging technology for total protein measurement was used as the loading control (Gilda and Gomes, 2015; Moritz, 2017). After separation, gels were activated by UV light (1 min) in a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc MP Imaging System for quantification of individual lane total protein. Proteins were transferred to 0.45-μm PVDF-Plus membranes (prod. no. 121639; Data Support Co., Panorama City, CA), for FreedomRocker™ Blotbot® (Next Advance, Inc., Troy, NY) automated wash and antibody incubation processing. Non-specific immunoreagent binding was abated by pretreatment blocking of membranes with Tris-buffer saline, pH 7.4, 10 mM tris hydrochloride, 50 mM sodium chloride (TBS) supplemented with 0.1 % Tween-20 and 2 % bovine serum albumin. Membranes were incubated for 36–42 h, at 4 °C, with a rabbit primary polyclonal antiserum raised against preproGhrh (prod. no. PA5–102738, 1:2000; Invitrogen). Membranes were next incubated with goat anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase-labeled secondary antibodies (1:5000; prod. no. NEF812001EA; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA), before exposure to maximum sensitivity SuperSignal West Femto chemiluminescent substrate (prod. no. 34096; ThermoFisherScientific, Rockford, IL). The chemiluminescence optical density (O.D.) value measured for each target protein band was normalized to total protein in that lane using ChemiDoc MP Image Lab™ 6.0.0 software. A proprietary trihalo compound that is directly incorporated into Bio-Rad Stain-Free gel chemistry lacks inherent fluorescence but renders all in-gel proteins fluorescent and thus measurable by optical density (O.D.) upon UV photoactivation. After summation of individual protein O.D. values in a single lane, the target protein band chemiluminescence O.D. value is normalized to summed total in-lane protein by ChemiDoc MP Image Lab™ 6.0.0 software. Figures presented here denote mean normalized O.D. measures at the Y axis. Each Western blot analysis employed precision plus protein molecular weight dual color standards (prod. no. 161–0374, Bio-Rad).

Glucose and counterregulatory hormone analyses

Plasma glucose levels were measured using an ACCU-CHECK Aviva plus glucometer (Roche Diagnostic Corporation, Indianapolis, IN) (Napit et al., 2019). Plasma corticosterone (ADI-900–097; Enzo Life Sciences, Inc., Farmingdale, NY), glucagon (EZGLU-30K, MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA), and GH (prod. no. KRC5311; Invitrogen/ThermoFisherScientific, Waltham, MA) levels were analyzed according to kit instructions, as described (Sapkota et al., 2023).

Statistics

Mean normalized mRNA, glucose, and counterregulatory hormone measures in old male and female rats were analyzed by three-way ANOVA and Student Newman Keuls post-hoc test. Data from young SCR siRNA/V versus old SCR siRNA/V control groups were evaluated by t test. Differences of p < 0.05 were considered significant. In each figure, statistical differences between specific pairs of treatment groups are denoted as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Results

The cause(s) of aging-associated glucose counterregulatory dysfunction is unclear. VMNdm Ghrh neurons impose sex-dimorphic control of glucose homeostasis in young adult rats. Here, combinative in situ immunocytochemistry/laser-catapult-microdissection/single-cell multiplex qPCR techniques were used to address the premise that at middle age, baseline and/or hypoglycemic patterns of VMNdm Ghrh neuron counterregulatory neurochemical marker gene expression may differ from young adults, according to sex. Inclusion of Ghrh gene knockdown tools in the current study design enabled investigation of potential aging effects on Ghrh neuromodulation of amino acid and labile gas transmission and systemic counterregulatory hormone profiles in each sex.

Discussion

The neural mechanisms that underlie age-associated glucose counterregulatory dysfunction are not known. VMNdm Ghrh neurons engage in multi-modal neurochemical signaling to control counterregulation. This cell population discharges counterregulatory-constraining and -enhancing neurochemicals of distinctive chemical structure and spatial/temporal release profiles, to provide integrated, dynamic input to the brain glucostatic regulatory network. Present studies used single-cell multiplex qPCR techniques to determine if transcription of one or more neurotransmitter marker genes characteristic of this neuron population is age-sensitive in one or both sexes. Current data document age-discordant hypoglycemic VMNdm neuron Ghrh mRNA expression patterns in female, but not male rats. Aging is associated with divergent GAD and GLS transcriptional responses to hypoglycemia in male (no change in GAD1, GAD2, or GLS mRNAs) versus female (up-regulated GAD2 and GLS mRNAs), yet nNOS gene expression was elevated in middle-aged hypoglycemic rats of each sex. Outcomes support the prospect that aging may cause sex-contingent alterations in a panel of glucose-regulatory neurotransmitter signals that originate from VMNdm Ghrh neurons. Ongoing work seeks to characterize the mechanisms which mediate aging effects on distinctive counterregulatory neurochemicals synthesized in these cells, and to discern the impact of individual and integrated release of age-sensitive neurotransmitters on counterregulatory function in each sex.

VMN Ghrh neurotransmission controls co-expressed VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 nerve cell neurochemical marker gene profiles and counterregulatory hormone secretion in young rats of each sex (Sapkota et al., 2023). Current data show that aging diminished VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neuron Ghrh gene expression in middle-aged male and female rats, resulting in higher baseline expression levels in the former sex. Here, Ghrh mRNA profiles in older adult males were unchanged in response to hypoglycemia, data that align with results reported for young male rats. In the female, however, hypoglycemia was seen here to down-regulate Ghrh gene expression in old females, which reflects an age-associated gain of transcriptional reactivity to this metabolic challenge in that sex. Additional studies are warranted to investigate effects of this female-only inhibitory neurotransmitter response on counterregulatory function in those animals. VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neurons express Ghrh-R, inferring that Ghrh may govern neurotransmission by these cells via autocrine regulatory mechanisms. The present project documents sex-specific aging effects on VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 nerve cell Ghrh-R gene profiles. Whereas young animals of each sex exhibit a lack of Ghrh control of Ghrh-R mRNA content, middle-aged rats exhibit neuropeptide stimulation of Ghrh-R gene expression. During hypoglycemia, older adult male rats exhibit an evident loss of positive Ghrh-R transcriptional response to hypoglycemia and middle-aged animals of each sex show a gain of positive Ghrh control of this gene profile. It should be noted that documentation here of adjustments in patterns of Ghrh-R gene expression owing to aging or Ghrh gene knockdown does not constitute definitive proof of corresponding changes in Ghrh neuropeptide expression and transmission. This supposition will require experimental validation by application of analytical methods of requisite sensitivity for protein quantification in single brain cell samples.

GAD catalyzes rate-limiting conversion of glutamate to GABA and is expressed in brain as 67 (GAD1/GAD67) and 65 (GAD2/GAD65) kDa molecular weight variants that are encoded by distinct genes. These enzyme exhibit differences in amino acid primary structure, nerve cell subcellular localization, and regulation. GAD1/GAD67 expression is subject to transcriptional and posttranscriptional control, whereas GAD2/GAD65 is controlled by transcriptional and kinetic mechanisms (Behar, 2009). The presence of GAD2/GAD65 protein in axon terminals and vesicles versus GAD1/GAD67 localization to cytoplasm points to the existence of distinct vesicular and cytoplasmic GABA pools and separate roles for GAD2/GAD65, i.e., neurotransmission, and GAD1/GAD67, i.e. cellular metabolism (Martin and Barke, 1998; Tavazzani et al., 2014; Schousboe and Waagepetersen, 2017). Data here show that progressive from young adulthood to middle age did not alter baseline VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neuron GAD1 or GAD2 mRNA profiles yet affected hypoglycemic gene transcription patterns according to sex. Aging abolished down-regulated patterns of GAD1 and GAD2 transcription observed in young INS-injected male rats yet promoted a switch from down- to up-regulated GAD2 mRNA content in hypoglycemic females. Current data infer that in male rats, Ghrh neuromodulatory repression of baseline GAD mRNA profiles is unperturbed by age, whereas middle-aged females exhibit an evident gain in Ghrh-mediated restraint of VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neuron GAD1 and GAD2 gene expression. Aging also imposes sex-specific effects on Ghrh regulation of hypoglycemic GAD2 mRNA profiles, causing a gain (male) or loss (female) of neuropeptide suppressive tone. It is of interest to speculate whether Ghrh signaling exerts primary control of hypoglycemic patterns of VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 GAD2 gene expression in each sex. GABA neurotransmission is reported to blunt counterregulatory endocrine function and down-regulated GABAergic signaling is implicated in hypoglycemic augmentation of hormone secretion. Present outcomes support the possibility aging may abolish, at least in VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neurons, negative GABAergic transmitter reactivity to IIH. Further research is needed to determine the impact of this neurochemical adaptation on diminished counterregulatory hormone profiles characteristic of old hypoglycemic male and female rats.

Present studies show that baseline VMN Ghrh/SF-1 nerve cell GLS gene expression aging is unaffected by aging in either sex, but this gene profile is higher in middle-aged female versus male rats. Outcomes here indicate that Ghrh inhibition of basal GLS gene transcription occurs in young and older adult animals of each sex. Importantly, aging does alter hypoglycemic patterns of GLS gene expression in a sex-dimorphic manner as this gene profile is refractory to INS injection in middle-aged male rats, in contrast to down-regulated GLS mRNA in young animals, and hypoglycemia stimulates VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 nerve cell GLS gene expression in older females, unlike inhibition of gene transcription in young adult animals. Data here infer that Ghrh regulation of this mRNA profile is determined by glucose status as impact of this neuropeptide signal is lost because of hypoglycemia. It is intriguing to consider whether attenuation of Ghrh signal control is responsible for positive GLS gene transcriptional reactivity to hypoglycemia. Baseline patterns of VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neuron nNOS gene expression did not differ here between middle-aged male and female rats. Data here show that Ghrh imposes a negative regulatory tone on this gene profile in older adult female, but not male animals; these findings align with reports for young animals (Sapkota et al., 2023). Here, as in young rats, hypoglycemia enhanced nNOS gene transcription; however, this stimulatory response was blunted by Ghrh input in young animals yet was unaffected by this neuropeptide signal in middle-aged rats. Thus, glucose status evidently shapes Ghrh regulation of this VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neuron gene profile in middle-aged female rats, i.e. determines a switch from inhibitory to no control during eu- versus hypoglycemia.

Within the context of the CNS ‘one neuron/one neurotransmitter’ model, it is customary to presume that changes in neurochemical production and synaptic vesicle storage correlate with adjusted, i.e., similar direction and magnitude of synaptic firing and neurotransmitter release. In the present circumstances where co-expressed transmitters exhibit variable transcriptional reactivity to aging and/or Ghrh gene silencing, it is intriguing to consider how either of these variables may affect synaptic release of individual neurochemicals, including those characterized by stimulus-associated downregulated gene transcription. It would be instructive to learn if Ghrh nerve cell counterregulatory transmitters released by exocytosis, i.e. Ghrh, GABA, and glutamate, are co-packaged within common synaptic vesicles for simultaneous co-release, or alternatively, if these neurotransmitters accumulate in separate vesicle populations for independent discharge in response to characteristic Ghrh control. It is intriguing to consider if and how subpopulations of Ghrh nerve cell synaptic vesicles may be selected for exocytosis while others remain undisturbed. It is, moreover, of interest to understand whether and how aging may affect individual neurochemical release. Ongoing studies seek to characterize the cellular targets and functional sequelae of Ghrh-controlled release of individual VMNdm Ghrh neuron counterregulatory neurochemicals, including individual counterregulatory hormone profiles, and to determine if the diffusible gas NO and co-expressed amino acid and neuropeptide transmitters act on common and/or different post-synaptic substrates to impose Ghrh control of glucose homeostasis.

The nuclear transcription factor SF-1 is expressed only in the VMNdm, where it acts to regulate whole body energy and glucose homeostasis. Young rats are characterized by Ghrh regulation of baseline SF-1 gene expression in females alone, and hypoglycemia-associated down-regulation of SF-1 transcription in both sexes (Sapkota and Briski 2024). Present data show that in the male, aging results in a gain of inhibitory Ghrh control of this gene profile during eu- and hypoglycemia and causes a loss of SF-1 transcriptional reactivity to hypoglycemia. Interestingly, in the middle-aged female, Ghrh regulation of SF-1 gene expression is evidently controlled by glucose status as control is lost due to hypoglycemia. Our recent studies show that VMN SF-1 governs expression profiles of counterregulatory neurochemical marker genes in VMN Ghrh/SF-1 nerve cell (Sapkota et al., 2024). Current work did not address the question of how age-associated changes in hypoglycemic patterns of SF-1 gene transcription in male rats may affect counterregulatory neurotransmitter signaling in that sex.

The molecular mechanisms that affect counterregulatory neurotransmission in middle-aged rats of each sex are not known. It is plausible to consider whether adjustments in neurochemical marker gene expression documented here are a consequence of aging effects on the metabolic status of VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neurons. These neurons are evidently capable of self-monitoring as they express genes that encode characterized biomarkers for metabolic sensing, namely the glucose sensor glucokinase and variants of the catalytic subunit of the ultra-sensitive energy monitor 5’-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) (Sapkota et al., 2024). The possibility that aging may affect net VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 nerve cell energy balance will require additional investigation, including development and application of analytical tools of requisite sensitivity for quantification of AMP and ATP molecules in single nerve cell samples. When such proof-of-principle is acquired, it would be reasonable to extend such findings by investigation of how age-related energy status may affect individual counterregulatory neurochemical production and release.

Synchronized quantification of individual target gene profiles by multiplex qPCR analysis is a valuable tool for evaluation of relative expression of diverse neurotransmitter marker mRNAs within a common cellular source. Here, compilation of relative target gene mRNA expression ratios for VMNdm Ghrh neurons acquired from euglycemic or hypoglycemic SCR siRNA-pretreated, V-injected middle-aged rats infers that aging may cause sex-specific adjustments in proportionate baseline or glucoprivation-associated expression of distinctive target gene profiles. These novel observations support the prospect that aging may affect relative rates of neurotransmitter synaptic discharge, in other words, may alter ratios of neurochemical signal volume received by post-synaptic elements, thereby affecting signal integration by downstream neurons during glucose homeostasis or deficiency. This supposition will require analytical verification that proportionate counterregulatory neurochemical release into synapses is indeed modified by age in one or both sexes during eu- or hypoglycemia. Present findings moreover identify aging effects on relative Ghrh-R and SF-1 gene transcription in eu- and/or hypoglycemic animals, which alludes to the possibility that proportionality of Ghrh-R or SF-1 versus Ghrh synthesis may be correspondingly affected, which could, in turn, alter cellular sensitivity to Ghrh input or SF-1 transcriptional regulatory action.

The present project is valuable for disclosure of sex-dimorphic aging effects on baseline counterregulatory hormone profiles, namely reductions in circulating corticosterone, glucagon, and GH levels in middle-aged male rats alongside decrements in the latter hormone profile in old versus young females. Outcomes are also important for documenting age-associated loss of corticosterone and GH secretory responses to hypoglycemia in each sex, and for noting that hypoglycemia hyperglucagonemia occurs in young and older adult rats of each sex. Because measurements of circulating hormone concentrations were conducted here at a single time after vehicle or insulin administration, it should be kept in mind that this ‘snapshot’ approach may mask distinctive aging effects on net hormone secretion over time, i.e. temporal patterns of release after those injections, in one or both sexes. It remains to be determined if there is a causal link between aging effects on release of one or more VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neuron counterregulatory neurochemicals and peripheral hormone secretion patterns under eu- or hypoglycemic conditions.

In summary, understanding of aging impairment of glucose counterregulation is elusive. VMNdm Ghrh/SF-1 neurons shape counterregulatory hormone release through Ghrh-modulated discharge of neurochemicals of diverse chemical structure and temporal/spatial release profiles. Research here investigated whether aging may alter, according to sex, eu- and/or hypoglycemic patterns of co-expressed counterregulatory-constraining or -enhancing neurotransmitter marker mRNAs in those nerve cells. Current outcomes infer that aging may impose sex-dimorphic effects on multi-modal transmitter signaling to the brain glucose-regulatory network, provided by a key metabolic-sensory ventromedial hypothalamic neuron population. Present data emphasize the need for ongoing efforts to determine where VMNdm Ghrf/SF-1 neurons project in the brain, characterize the neurotransmitter phenotype(s) of neurons that are innervated by this cell population, and discern aging-associated changes in baseline and/or hypoglycemic patterns of neurochemical signaling on target neuron and brain glucostatic network function.
