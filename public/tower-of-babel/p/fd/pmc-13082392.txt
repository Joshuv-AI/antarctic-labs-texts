A bio-inspired computational pipeline for antibody screening and repurposing
Authors: Junxin Li, Mark A Ige, Chao Zhang, Linbu Liao, Faiz Rasul, Xiaohu Ren, Xiaochun Wan, Youhai Chen, Haiping Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic antibody discovery is central to modern drug development, yet conventional methods such as hybridoma and phage display remain slow, inefficient, and costly. Computational approaches including site-saturation mutagenesis often yield limited affinity gains and expression liabilities, while deep learning and generative models expand sequence diversity but suffer from low validation rates. Here, we present a multi-scale computational screening pipeline inspired by key principles of in vivo immune selection. The framework integrates structure-based docking (ZDock), graph neural network–based interaction prediction, and accelerated molecular dynamics (MDs) with metadynamics free-energy profiling to enable high-throughput in silico prioritization of structure-resolved antibodies. Applied to Activin A, a pleiotropic cytokine implicated in fibrosis, oncology, and muscle-wasting disorders, the platform screened ~5000 antibody structures and identified 11 candidates. Experimental validation confirmed two binders, with Ab4 exhibiting sub-nanomolar affinity (KD = 0.38 nM) and potent neutralizing activity, underscoring therapeutic potential in fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP) and related diseases. Rather than performing full iterative affinity maturation, the present study focuses on the screening and repurposing stage, with affinity maturation positioned as a prospective extension. This work demonstrates the feasibility of integrating AI-driven interaction prediction with physics-based simulations to accelerate structure-guided antibody screening and repurposing, while conceptually paralleling selected stages of immune selection rather than fully recapitulating immune evolution.Keywords: human antibody virtual screening, deep learning, docking, Activin A, FOP

Introduction

Therapeutic antibodies have emerged as a cornerstone of modern medicine [1], with five antibody-based biologics ranking among the top 10 best-selling drugs globally in 2023 [2]. Despite their commercial and clinical success, critical challenges persist in developing novel antibodies with high affinity, patentability, and favorable developability profiles [3]. Beyond the labor-intensive biopanning process, conventional antibody discovery platforms face fundamental limitations: (i) hybridoma technology requires prolonged animal immunization and suffers from immunogenicity risks due to murine-derived sequences triggering human anti-mouse antibody (HAMA) responses [4]. (ii) phage display exhibits high false-positive rates and disruption of natural antibody pairing [5]. (iii) single B cell sequencing preserves native antibody pairs through direct amplification of immunoglobulin genes from individual B cells, its implementation remains constrained by low throughput, host immune history and specialized infrastructure [5]. Traditional approaches, such as computation or experiment based site-directed mutagenesis [6, 7], often yield incremental improvements but fail to generate structurally distinct candidates with robust intellectual property (IP) potential. Furthermore, these methods typically incur considerable costs and require lengthy experimental timeframes.​ Conversely, radical sequence modifications risk compromising expression efficiency [8] or introducing immunogenic liabilities [9].

Recent advances in deep learning and structural biology have fueled a paradigm shift. Platforms leveraging antibody–antigen interaction data (e.g. OAS [10], SAbDab [11]) now enable sequence-structure co-design. Current AI-driven generative models, such as AntiBERTy [12] RFdiffusion [13] and DiffAb [14], have advanced sequence-structure exploration, enabling the direct generation of antibody sequences or structures with potential antigen binding capabilities. However, these generation strategies still face substantial challenges in experimental validation and developability assessment. Achieving simultaneously high affinity and optimal drug-like properties (e.g. expression yield, stability, and humanization) remains a significant challenge, partly due to the lack of robust binding interface pattern recognition. While traditional docking methods prioritize antibody–antigen interface analysis, their predictive accuracy remains limited. In contrast, deep learning offers significant potential for accurately predicting interactions and binding affinity. For instance, geometric deep learning algorithms and pre-trained language models are particularly well-suited for high-throughput screening or guiding design by predicting interaction likelihood or affinity, thus potentially overcoming the limitations of current generative and traditional approaches.​.

To fully translate this predictive capability into the transformative strategy required for next-generation antibody discovery, we must look to biology itself. The immune system robustly generates high-affinity antibodies through two key evolutionary stages: (i) primary screening of naive B-cell repertoires (~7 × 1010–9 × 1010 variants) for initial antigen binding (yielding ~105–106 initial binders) [15, 16], followed by (ii) iterative +affinity maturation, driven by somatic hypermutation and selective pressure to enhance binding affinity [17, 18]. Inspired by selected high-level stages of in vivo immune selection, we designed a bio-inspired computational framework for large-scale screening and prospective refinement. As illustrated in Fig. 1, this bio-inspired workflow applies selected principles of immune selection by first performing large-scale in silico screening within a predefined epitope space, followed by prospective mutation-guided refinement. In the present study, we focus primarily on the screening and repurposing stage, leveraging deep learning for ultra-high-throughput prioritization of structure-resolved antibodies. While future extensions may incorporate iterative mutation cycles to provide a closer computational analogue of prospective affinity optimization, the current implementation translates selected principles of immune evolution into a computationally tractable and experimentally testable screening paradigm, rather than fully recapitulating the complete immune maturation process.

In the present study, we present a multi-scale computational pipeline addressing two intertwined challenges: (i) antibody discovery against target antigens and (ii) affinity maturation of lead candidates while preserving developability. Our approach integrates graph-based interaction prediction models with accelerated molecular dynamics (MDs) simulations and metadynamics-based free energy calculations to prioritize candidates. By coupling evolutionary constraints with binding-interface optimization, we implement a prospective mutation-guided refinement strategy in which initial candidates are further optimized through energetically guided mutations. In this study, we selected Activin A as our target antigen to screen potential high affinity antibodies. Activin A is a homodimeric cytokine of the TGF-β superfamily, composed of two inhibin βA subunits, which engages type II receptors (ACVR2A/2B) and recruits type I receptors (e.g. ACVR1B/ALK4) to initiate canonical SMAD2/3 signaling and, in parallel, non-Smad pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK/ERK [19]. In fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP), Activin A acts as the obligate ligand for the mutant ACVR1 receptor, driving heterotopic ossification; genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of Activin A prevents this process. Therapeutic antibodies against Activin A, exemplified by garetosmab, which has proceeded to phase III trials (NCT05394116)-neutralize the ligand, blocking its interaction with receptors and thereby suppressing pathological signaling in FOP [20] and other Activin A–mediated disorders.

Experimental validation showed that, among the 11 computationally screened antibodies, two displayed measurable binding activity, and one exhibited strong binding affinity (KD = 0.38 nM) and potent neutralizing activity in vitro. Together with our previous work on point mutation optimization [8, 21], we propose a bio-inspired computational framework consisting of two sequential steps—large-scale virtual screening followed by targeted mutation-guided refinement. This ‘screening + mutation’ paradigm is conceptually inspired by two high-level stages of antibody discovery and refinement. This framework conceptually translates selected principles of immune selection into a computational screening strategy, with the potential to prioritize candidates that may exhibit improved binding properties.

This work bridges a critical gap between AI-generated diversity and biophysical realism. Unlike prior methods relying solely on sequence statistics, our pipeline explicitly models antibody–antigen interfacial dynamics, enabling iterative optimization of both affinity and structural novelty. The framework’s versatility extends beyond therapeutic antibodies to T cell receptor-antigen (TCR-antigen) interaction prediction and ADC design, offering a blueprint for next-generation macromolecular therapeutics.

Methods

Data collection

A database of 5935 antibody–antigen complexes from SAbDab (The Structural Antibody Database) [11] (https://opig.stats.ox.ac.uk/webapps/newsabdab/sabdab/archive/all/) was collected and processed. Since this database contains antibody–antigen complex information, the extracted antibodies were guaranteed to have accurately defined binding interfaces. The antibody interface within 1 nm of the antigen was extracted as the binding site. The Activin A structure was obtained from B chain of PDB entry 1NYS. PDB entry 1NYS also contains an activin receptor, which we used to define the Activin A receptor-binding interface by extracting Activin A residues within 1 nm of the interface for subsequent docking.

Docking to obtain complexes

ZDOCK was employed to dock Activin A with each antibody. Binding regions were constrained to predefined local areas on Activin A corresponding to the receptor-binding interface, rather than performing global blind docking across the entire antigen surface [22]. Five conformational poses were generated for each pair, all of which were used for deep learning evaluation. It should be noted that this constrained docking strategy is designed for relative ranking within a predefined epitope region, rather than exhaustive exploration of all possible binding modes.

Graph neural network for binding prediction

Two previously developed deep learning models (DeepGCN_Anti and Sgraph_AB) [8, 21] together with a statistical potential were applied to evaluate binding propensity for the generated antibody–antigen complexes. For each antibody–antigen pair, five constrained docking poses were generated. A pair was classified as a predefined binding hit if at least one of the five poses produced a deep-learning–predicted interaction probability above a fixed threshold. The classification threshold was determined using a held-out validation set during model development and was fixed prior to large-scale screening and experimental testing. This ‘any-of-five’ decision rule was adopted to determine whether a plausible binding mode exists within the predefined epitope region, rather than requiring all poses to satisfy the cutoff. Local Molecular Dynamics and Metadynamics for Binding Validation.

To reduce computational costs and enhance screening efficiency, we designed a localized antibody–antigen simulation protocol (see workflow in Fig. S1), analogous to our previously developed pocket MDs approach [23]. The antibody pocket, located within 1 nm of the antigen, was isolated. ACE and NHE groups were applied to cap the C- and N-termini, respectively, to prevent the introduction of extraneous charges. However, as the antigen remained large, distant regions from the antibody (residues 0–14, 39–48, 77–83, 113–116) were manually excised.

Experimental validation of selected antibody candidates

Production of antibodies and antigens

Recombinant human Activin A protein and a human IgG1 isotype antibody were purchased from MedChemExpress LLC and Biolegend LLC, respectively. The genes encoding human IgG1 antibodies (Abs) were synthesized and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 expression vectors (Invitrogen). Recombinant antibodies were produced by transient transfection of 293F cells using polyethyleneimine (PEI), a non-viral transfection reagent (Sigma-Aldrich), as described previously [24]. Supernatants were collected after 5 days of culture to test the potential binding affinities. Later, for further experiments, the shortlisted candidate, Ab4, was purified by Protein A affinity chromatography (GE Healthcare).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plates (Thermo Scientific) were coated with 100 ng Activin A at 4 °C overnight followed by blocking with 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich). Supernatants for cells expressing different Abs were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, and then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-human IgG (Earthox), followed by the development with 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB, Sigma-Aldrich) substrate, and the reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid (Sigma-Aldrich). Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using M1000 plate reader (Tecan). An isotype-matched human IgG1 antibody was included as a negative control to assess non-specific binding and background effects at matched concentrations.

Determination of binding affinity (KD values)

Based on bio-layer interferometry (BLI), the Octet K2 instrument (Pall ForteBio) was used to measure binding affinities, namely KD values. The human antibody Ab4 in SD buffer containing PBS, 0.1% BSA, and 0.02% Tween 20 was loaded onto Protein A biosensors (Pall ForteBio), followed by measurement of the Kon value through incubation with serially diluted Activin A. The Koff value was subsequently calculated by incubation with SD buffer only. The ratio of Kon to Koff determined the KD value reported here.

Anti-Activin A antibody neutralization assay

HepG2 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher) at 37°C, 5% CO₂. To induce cytotoxic effects, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5000 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were treated with 100 ng/ml Activin A protein for 24 h. After Activin A treatment, cells were exposed to anti-Activin A antibody (Ab4) at concentrations of 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100 and 1000 ng/ml for 24 h. Cell viability was measured using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, Thermo Fisher) assay. Briefly, 20 μL of MTT reagent (5 mg/ml) was added to each well, and plates were incubated for 4 h at 37°C to allow formazan crystal formation. Binding detail analysis

For our shortlisted active antibody candidates, binding interactions with Activin A were analyzed using both the top docking poses and AlphaFold3-predicted complexes. Critical residues mediating the interactions were identified.

Results

Computational pipeline identifies high-affinity antibodies targeting Activin A

Our multi-stage computational pipeline (Fig. 2) systematically narrowed ~5000 initial antibody candidates to 11 high-affinity binders through iterative refinement. Initial docking-generated conformations were screened using statistical potential scoring and two graph-based deep learning models (DeepGCN_Anti and Sgraph_AB) [8, 21]. DeepGCN_Anti and Sgraph_AB differ in their dependence on structural context. DeepGCN_Anti, developed in our previous work, uses separate graph representations for the antibody and antigen binding regions, enabling interaction prediction without requiring explicit complex-level interface information. In contrast, Sgraph_AB, developed in this study, adopts a single graph representation that explicitly encodes the antibody–antigen interface within a complex, allowing more accurate evaluation of binding propensity for structure-resolved antibody–antigen pairs. Architectural details and benchmark performance of DeepGCN_Anti and Sgraph_AB are summarized in Supplementary section 1, Table S1, and Fig. S2. Subsequent 20 ns local MD simulations were carried out to roughly estimate binding stability; methods are shown in Fig. S1. Accordingly, docking and short-timescale simulations in this pipeline are used as qualitative filters to prioritize plausible binders, rather than as definitive validation of binding kinetics. Metadynamics-derived free energy landscapes further confirmed favorable binding thermodynamics, displaying the lowest energy basin at the Coordinate number corresponding to close binding (Fig. S3). The detailed MD simulation and metadynamics simulation are provided in Supplementary material section 2. It should be noted that given the relatively short simulation timescale used in this study (20 ns) and the use of a simplified, locally truncated antibody–antigen system, these metadynamics calculations are not sufficient to provide rigorously converged absolute binding free energies. In this work, the primary purpose of the metadynamics simulations was not to serve as a quantitative validation tool, but rather to assess whether the candidate antibody–antigen systems exhibit consistent and physically reasonable binding trends. As such, these simulations were intended to provide qualitative, supportive evidence for the screening results rather than definitive thermodynamic confirmation.

Experimental validation confirms computational predictions

Binding affinity assessment of selected antibodies with Activin A

To assess the binding activities of 11 Abs, ELISA was performed using the supernatants from 293F cells transfected with the expression vectors encoding the respective antibody genes. As shown in Fig. 3a, Ab4 exhibited high-affinity binding to recombinant Activin A, whereas Ab5 displayed low-affinity binding. The remaining nine antibodies displayed no significant binding under the tested conditions. Compared to an isotype control antibody, Ab4 demonstrated specific high-affinity binding (Fig. S4a). Ab4 (SL335) is a human antibody originally developed against human serum albumin (HSA) to extend drug half-life [25], while Ab5 is a SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibody targeting the spike protein trimer to block viral entry [26]. Ab4 leverages endogenous HSA as a carrier, minimizing immunogenicity risks, whereas Ab5 targets pathogen-specific antigens, avoiding cross-reactivity. Both antibodies therefore present low-risk profiles for therapeutic repurposing.

For detailed kinetic analysis, the IgG1 isoform of Ab4 was expressed and purified, and its binding affinity for Activin A was quantified via bio-layer interferometry (BLI). As shown in Fig. 3b, the concentration-dependent association and dissociation kinetics of Ab4 with Activin A revealed high-affinity binding in the nanomolar range, with a calculated equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) of 0.38 nM, confirming the strong interaction between Ab4 and Activin A.

Neutralizing activity of Ab4 against Activin A

Neutralization effects were interpreted relative to isotype controls, without claiming pathway-level mechanistic validation. To evaluate the neutralizing capacity of Ab4 against Activin A, we treated HepG2 cells with recombinant Activin A (0.1 μg/ml) for 24 h to induce cytotoxicity. Cells were then incubated with increasing concentrations of Ab4 for an additional 24 h. Cell viability assays demonstrated a dose-dependent rescue effect, with higher antibody concentrations correlating with greater protection against Activin A-mediated cytotoxicity, as shown in Fig. 3c. This neutralizing activity was specific, as the isotype control showed no rescue effect (Fig. S4b).

Activin A triggered cell cycle arrest through upregulation of CDK inhibitors and promoted apoptosis via modulation of Bcl-2 family proteins [27]. The ability of Ab4 to reverse these cytotoxic effects suggests potent neutralization against Activin A, likely through competitive inhibition of receptor binding and subsequent blockade of downstream apoptotic signaling pathways. However, pathway-level validation, such as SMAD2/3 phosphorylation assays in FOP-relevant cellular models, will be an important next step to directly confirm inhibition of canonical Activin A signaling.

Interface interaction comparison between different candidates

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the native binding interface between Activin and its receptor exhibits extensive amino acid interactions, with the 2D interaction plot further identifying four specific hydrogen bonds formed at the binding site. Our analysis revealed that both of the identified active antibodies (Ab4 and Ab5) demonstrate binding modes that maintain high structural similarity to the native activin-receptor complex in terms of binding site geometry and molecular conformation. Among the inactive candidates, only two antibody models showed binding patterns closely resembling the native binding conformation, while the remaining eight exhibited significant deviations in their binding modes. These results indicate that for antibody screening targeting this particular epitope, both binding to the correct epitope and maintaining the native-like conformational arrangement are critical determinants of functional activity. We propose that future screening efforts could significantly narrow candidate selection by prioritizing antibodies that preserve a native-like binding topology. We agree that large deviations from plausible receptor–antigen binding conformations are often associated with poor binding and that this information could, in principle, be leveraged as an early filtering criterion. In the current pipeline, docking poses were primarily used for downstream structural interpretation rather than as a hard filter, in order to avoid prematurely discarding candidates that might adopt alternative yet viable binding modes. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that pose-based deviation metrics could serve as a useful heuristic for early-stage triage, especially in large-scale applications. We have added a brief discussion in the revised manuscript highlighting this possibility and outlining how such geometric or conformational consistency checks could be incorporated as an optional early filtering step in future extensions of the pipeline.

Discussion

The proposed antibody screening strategy exhibits significant versatility, potentially applicable to antigen–antibody systems with well-defined epitopes. While the current study focuses on screening antibodies with pre-existing antigen complexes, its principles can be applied to diverse antibody libraries. Since antibody binding predominantly localizes to complementarity-determining regions (CDRs), this method can be universally applied to antibody libraries with structural data or those generated via computational modeling (e.g. AlphaFold-predicted structures). With advancements in antibody prediction accuracy and efficiency, constructing libraries of millions to tens of millions of antibodies becomes feasible. This scalability enables high-throughput screening to identify functional candidates, significantly enhancing hit rates. In the present study, the epitope on Activin A was defined based on its native receptor-binding interface, reflecting a deliberate, mechanism-driven design choice. By prioritizing antibodies that overlap with the receptor-binding surface, we aimed to enrich for candidates with a clear competitive neutralization mechanism and lower functional uncertainty. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept for mechanism-driven, epitope-constrained antibody screening, rather than an exhaustive exploration of all functional epitopes on Activin A.

Recent breakthroughs in generative AI models (e.g. AntiBERTy [12], DiffAb [14], and RFDiffusion [28]) have enabled the production of vast antibody libraries. However, experimental validation of these generated antibodies remains cost-prohibitive. Our screening framework addresses this bottleneck by computationally prioritizing candidates with high binding potential, reducing wet-lab validation efforts. In this study, we applied the pipeline to identify potential Activin A binders from a curated, structure-resolved antibody library. Notably, the success of this approach depends on both the quality of generative models (e.g. avoiding non-expressing or unstable designs) and iterative improvements in structural prediction accuracy.

In this study, the analogy to immune selection is limited to two high-level design principles: large-scale screening within a defined candidate space and subsequent prospective refinement of selected hits. The current implementation does not model repertoire-scale stochastic diversification, clonal competition, or iterative affinity-driven selection, and should therefore be interpreted as a bio-inspired screening and repurposing framework rather than a computational recapitulation of immune evolution.

The present study is limited to ~5000 structure-resolved antibodies from SAbDab, representing a narrow and biased subset of antibody space that does not support claims of exhaustive exploration of vast or highly diverse antibody repertoires. This bias likely enriches for experimentally tractable antibodies with favorable expression, stability, and overall developability, which is advantageous for proof-of-concept validation but constrains generalizability. Accordingly, the current work is best viewed as a validation of the downstream structure-based screening and refinement module in a tractable repurposing framework. Broader utility will require the two-stage workflow proposed here, namely rapid sequence-based pre-filtering followed by structure-based docking and physics-informed refinement.

The experimental validation rate observed here (2/11) should therefore be interpreted within a curated repurposing framework rather than compared directly with de novo generative antibody design studies. Because the source library is enriched for antibodies with prior structural characterization and favorable developability-related properties, this setting is not directly comparable to unrestricted de novo generation. The observed hit rate thus reflects performance within a biased but experimentally tractable screening space. Nonetheless, further improvements could be achieved by increasing library size and incorporating more scalable sequence- and affinity-guided pre-filtering strategies in future iterations of the pipeline.

From a simulation perspective, conventional MD simulations on the order of tens of nanoseconds may be insufficient to fully capture slow binding or unbinding events. To mitigate this limitation, short MD simulations were complemented with enhanced sampling techniques, specifically metadynamics, enabling estimation of free-energy landscapes and more robust assessment of binding stability. This combined strategy represents a practical compromise between computational cost and screening efficiency. With increased computational resources, extending simulation times and exploring additional enhanced sampling approaches may further improve accuracy in future studies.

The antibody screening library was constructed in a target-agnostic manner from the SAbDab database (2023 release), comprising ~5000 experimentally resolved antibody–antigen complexes. No manual curation or target-specific filtering was applied, and antibodies were not pre-selected based on antigen class, protein family, or relevance to the TGF-β superfamily. To exclude potential data leakage or bias, sequence homology analysis was performed between all original antigens in the library and Activin A using BLASTP [29], confirming the absence of significant similarity to Activin A or its close paralogs(all with BLAST scores below 80), as summarized in Supplementary Table S5. Moreover, the previously reported antigens of the two experimentally validated antibodies were found to be structurally and sequentially unrelated to Activin A, indicating that the observed binding cannot be attributed to antigen homology or target family overlap.

Accordingly, the identification of Activin A binders in this work is best interpreted as in silico antibody screening and repurposing rather than de novo antibody generation. For example, the lead candidate Ab4 (SL335) was originally developed as a human serum albumin binder, and its activity against Activin A reflects the discovery of a previously unrecognized binding specificity. This reframing emphasizes the practical utility of computational screening pipelines for uncovering hidden cross-reactivity and repurposing opportunities within existing antibody repertoires, rather than the generation of entirely new antibody sequences. Furthermore, our framework’s adaptability extends to various related fields, such as Nanobodies and Camelid Antibodies, Antibody-Drug Conjugates (ADCs), TCR-Antigen, and GPCR-Targeting Antibodies. The structural insights from VHH domains (e.g. SAbDab database) enable seamless adaptation to the Nanobodies and Camelid Antibodies development.

For Antibody-Drug Conjugates (ADCs) development, it helps optimize antibody specificity for payload delivery using epitope binning and stability profiling. For TCR-Antigen Interaction Prediction, we can retrain the model on TCR-pMHC complexes (e.g. RefineGNN) for immunotherapy applications.

Our novel antibody, Ab4, has demonstrated the ability to reverse the cytotoxic effects of Activin A in vitro. This suggests that Ab4 could be a valuable tool in conditions where Activin A contributes to pathological processes. For instance, in FOP, Activin A drives the formation of heterotopic ossification through the mutant ACVR1 receptor [30]. By neutralizing Activin A, Ab4 could potentially inhibit this aberrant signaling and reduce the formation of new bone lesions. This mechanism is similar to that of garetosmab, a well-studied Activin A-blocking antibody [20].

Given its mechanism of action, Ab4 holds therapeutic potential across multiple indications supported by current research and clinical insights. In fibrodysplasia ossificans progressiva (FOP), Ab4 could emulate the efficacy of garetosmab by neutralizing Activin A, thereby inhibiting heterotopic ossification and mitigating disease progression [20]. In oncology, Activin A exhibits context-dependent roles in tumorigenesis, making Ab4 a candidate for modulating the tumor microenvironment.

For example, in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where Activin A drives metastasis, Ab4 may suppress tumor dissemination [31]. Beyond oncology, Activin A’s immunomodulatory effects position Ab4 as a potential adjunct to immunotherapy or a treatment for autoimmune disorders by regulating T-cell and NK-cell activity [32]. Additionally, in cancer cachexia, Ab4 could counteract Activin A-induced IL-6 secretion and autophagy, reversing muscle wasting as observed in preclinical models (50–62% IL-6 reduction). Finally, in thalassemia, elevated Activin A contributes to hemolysis and bone loss; Ab4 may restore bone mineral density and ameliorate anemia by disrupting pathogenic Smad signaling [33]. Collectively, these applications underscore Ab4’s versatility in targeting Activin A-mediated pathways.

Computational requirements and scalability are important considerations for the practical deployment of antibody screening workflows. In the present study, the computational cost associated with each major stage of the pipeline—including docking, learning-based screening, and force-field–based refinement—was evaluated, together with the typical hardware requirements and overall runtime. While the current implementation was applied to a moderate-scale antibody library, several core components of the workflow are inherently amenable to parallelization. As a result, the pipeline can be readily scaled to larger datasets through distributed execution or high-performance computing resources, enabling efficient large-scale screening in future applications.

Large deviations from physically plausible receptor–antigen binding conformations are frequently associated with weak or nonproductive binding. Although such geometric deviations could, in principle, be exploited as an early-stage filtering criterion, docking poses in the current pipeline were primarily retained for downstream structural interpretation rather than enforced as a hard exclusion filter. This design choice was motivated by the desire to avoid prematurely discarding candidates that might adopt alternative yet viable binding modes not captured by a single docking configuration. Nevertheless, pose-based deviation metrics represent a useful heuristic for early-stage triage, particularly in large-scale screening scenarios. Accordingly, geometric or conformational consistency checks could be incorporated as an optional early filtering step in future extensions of the pipeline.

Future optimization

Although ASGBIE was not directly deployed for large-scale screening in this study, it has demonstrated exceptional precision in retrospective analyses—both herein and in prior work—particularly in quantifying residue-level energetic contributions. This capability positions ASGBIE as a powerful tool for binding affinity optimization (e.g. identifying electrostatically repulsive ‘cold spots’ in active antibodies to guide affinity maturation) and mutation impact prediction (profiling binding energy changes induced by point mutations) [34–38]. However, its current dependence on computationally intensive MDs simulations limits practicality for high-throughput applications. To address this bottleneck, future efforts will focus on generating comprehensive datasets of ASGBIE-calculated affinity values and mutation-induced energy shifts, then training machine learning models to predict affinity and mutational effects rapidly. Integrating such predictors into our computational pipeline will significantly enhance the toolkit for next-generation antibody engineering, bridging precision and scalability.

Currently, the framework does not incorporate downstream developability filters at this stage. Future enhancements could include the integration of explicit developability assessments, expansion toward multi-epitope targeting strategies, and incorporation of additional experimental feedback to further strengthen and refine the overall framework.

In addition, atom-level graph representations represent a promising future direction and could, in principle, enable higher-resolution modeling of antibody–antigen interactions. However, such representations are associated with substantially increased computational cost, which may limit their scalability in large-scale screening settings. In the present study, we therefore adopted residue-level graph representations as a deliberate trade-off, allowing efficient large-scale screening while still capturing the dominant structural and dynamic features relevant to binding. When reliable atom-level representations become available and computational resources permit, atom-level graph modeling could be incorporated as a future enhancement in scenarios where higher accuracy is required.

Conclusion

We present a bio-inspired, structure-guided computational pipeline for antibody screening and repurposing. Applied to Activin A, the framework identified two binders from a structure-resolved antibody library, including Ab4 with sub-nanomolar affinity and in vitro neutralizing activity. The present study should be viewed as a proof-of-concept for epitope-constrained screening and repurposing in a tractable structural space. Future expansion to larger and less biased libraries will require hierarchical pre-filtering, structure-based refinement, and additional developability-aware assessment.
