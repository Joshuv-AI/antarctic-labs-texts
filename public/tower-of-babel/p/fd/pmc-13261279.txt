Deep learning-derived pericardial adipose tissue by electrocardiogram-gated cardiac computed tomography predicts cardiovascular events beyond coronary calcium score
Authors: Zahra Esmaeili, Jose R. Medina-Inojosa, Elham Mahmoudi, Laura M. Ortega-Aviles, Betsy J. Medina-Inojosa, Bradley J Erickson, Francisco Lopez-Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPericardial adipose tissue (PAT) is an emerging imaging biomarker of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk. We evaluated whether PAT volume improves prediction of cardiovascular events beyond Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) score and the AHA Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT™) equation.MethodsWe analyzed data of 11,897 Olmsted County, MN, residents without known history of heart failure and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events who underwent non-contrast, ECG-gated CAC scans. PAT volume (cm3) and density (Hounsfield units) were measured using a validated automated deep learning (DL) segmentation model (Dice score 0.94). We stratified patients into groups based on PAT volume (cut-off: top quartile 319 cm3) and CAC score (cut-off: 100 Agatston units). Cox proportional hazard models evaluated associations with the composite outcome of total CVD (myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular death), while adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors, and CAC score across PREVENT™ risk categories.ResultsOver a median of 16.4 years, 9.6% experienced total CVD events. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated progressively lower CVD-free survival across PAT and CAC groups (p < 0.001). PAT volume stratified risk across CAC categories, with higher volume conferring incremental risk even at low CAC (HR = 1.24, 95% CI:1.05–1.48), and enhanced risk stratification within borderline and intermediate PREVENT™ categories.ConclusionPAT volume provides prognostic value for total CVD events beyond PREVENT™ and CAC scoring.

Introduction

Pericardial adipose tissue (PAT) refers to the metabolically active lipid-storing tissue compromising two anatomically distinct layers: the epicardial adipose tissue (EAT) located between the visceral pericardium and myocardium, particularly along the interventricular and atrioventricular grooves; and paracardial adipose tissue, which lies external to the parietal pericardium [1,2]. The pathophysiological significance of PAT extends beyond simple lipid-storage as it functions as a metabolically active endocrine organ capable of bidirectional signaling with cardiac tissue [3]. As EAT increases, it can extend between the ventricles and coronary arteries and result in myocardial infiltration due to absence of a physical barrier between EAT and the myocardium [1]. This proximity facilitates direct interaction that can potentially contribute to progressive coronary artery disease, secondary to atherosclerotic plaque formation leading to myocardial fibrosis and acute coronary syndrome [4,5].

Coronary artery calcium (CAC) scoring from electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated non-contrast computed tomography (CT) is an increasingly accepted tool for cardiovascular risk assessment and quantification of coronary artery calcification [6]. An elevated total CAC score (> 0 Agatston Units (AU)) has been shown to predict cardiovascular events independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Conversely, a CAC score of zero is associated with an excellent prognosis [12,13]. However, despite its robust predictive value, nearly one-third of cardiovascular events occur in individuals with zero CAC score [14], and a substantial proportion of patients with obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) have zero CAC score [15,16], highlighting the presence of residual cardiovascular risk not captured by coronary calcification alone.

Given the potential involvement of EAT and PAT in the pathophysiology of coronary atherosclerosis and other cardiac comorbidities, as well as growing evidence supporting its emergence as an imaging biomarker of cardiovascular disease [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]], it is possible that Artificial intelligence (AI)-based measurement of PAT may improve the risk stratification when predicting cardiovascular disease beyond CAD. Yet, the prognostic performance of AI-derived PAT measurements compared to established CAC scoring remains incompletely understood.

This study aims to evaluate the association of deep learning (DL)-based measurement of PAT with total cardiovascular disease (CVD) events beyond Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) score and the American Heart Association (AHA) Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events (PREVENT™) equation.

Methods

Study population

A historical cohort study was conducted using the resources of the Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP), a comprehensive medical record linkage system that includes medical records, vital signs, medications, laboratory values, and additional clinical information from health care providers in Olmstead County, Minnesota [22]. All consecutive adult patients (≥18 years of age) who provided research authorization and underwent non-contrast, ECG-gated CAC CT scans between 1997 to 2020 were included. For each participant, the first available CT scan was used to train the model, and the corresponding date was used to ascertain baseline clinical information. Patients were excluded if: 1) they had missing CAC scores, 2) were on statin therapy when undergoing CAC scan, 3) had known history of heart failure (HF) or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) events, defined by documented history of myocardial infarction (MI), or stroke, 4) did not provide research authorization. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Cardiovascular risk assessment

The 2023 PREVENT™ equation, developed by the AHA Cardiovascular- Kidney-Metabolic Scientific Advisory Group has been previously described in detail [23,24]. This risk calculator uses demographic information (e.g. age and sex), vital signs (e.g. systolic blood pressure), body mass index (BMI), behavioral information (e.g. smoking status), laboratory values [e.g. total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR)], medical history (e.g. diabetes), and use of anti-hypertensive and lipid lowering medications. It is the first risk tool to combine cardiovascular, kidney and metabolic health measurements to calculate 10-year risk of total CVD events categorized as (i) low: < 5%, (ii) borderline: 5–7.4%, (iii) Intermediate: 7.5–19.9%, and (iv) High: ≥ 20%.

Coronary artery calcium scoring

CAC scores were calculated using automated scoring software on 3-mm-thick continuous images from electron beam or multi-slice CT scanners [[25], [26], [27]]. We used Natural language processing (NLP) algorithms to extract the CAC score from the radiologist narrative reports in the electronic medical records. To achieve this, the research team first pinpointed the relevant terms related to scores in the radiologist reports. Each report was then analyzed to identify these keywords along with their numerical or alphanumeric values. A physician investigator (J.M.I), checked random samples of NLP outputs. During this internal review process, the investigator provided feedback to fine-tune the algorithm's performance and add more related terminology. An accuracy of 95% was used as a threshold for adequate NLP performance. The Kappa statistics were used to quantify the agreement between the investigator and final NLP outputs.

Cardiovascular disease outcomes

All subjects were followed from the index CT date until the occurrence of first CVD event or last available follow-up on 1 May 2023, following previously validated methodology [22,28]. Total CVD events, as defined by the PREVENT™ risk score [24], include a composite of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, heart failure, and cardiovascular death. All eligible records were initially screened electronically via the REP record linkage system using the selected diagnostic and procedural codes (Supplementary Table 1). Then a random subset of cases and non-cases, were independently reviewed by two investigators (Z.E and B.M.I) to validate event status as well as baseline clinical information.

DL-based PAT quantification

A previously validated machine learning model, trained on the same dataset using the nnUNET framework was used to segment the PAT on CT images [29]. PAT was defined as adipose tissue within the Hounsfield Unit (HU) range of -190 to -30 [30]. PAT volume was calculated as the number of voxels within the segmented region multiplied by the voxel size (mm3), and the PAT density was calculated as the mean HU of all voxels included in the segmented region. Model results were validated by an experienced cardiologist (Dice score: 94%). Statistical analysis

Data was summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous and proportions (frequency) for categorical variables. Median and interquartile range (IQR) was used to report the non-normally distributed variables. PAT volume was categorized into three groups based on the tertile cut points. Distribution of baseline characteristics across PAT volume categories was compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous and chi-square test for categorical variables. Pearson correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between PAT volume and density together and with other variables.

Cumulative incidence of total CVD events was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and the differences between the groups were compared by the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression analyses were conducted to assess the prognostic value of different parameters for total CVD events and identify the independent predictors. In consistent with the PREVENT™ risk score [24], participant age, rather than the calendar time to follow-up data was used as the time scale in Cox modeling, because age is the strongest predictor of incident CVD. Models where additionally adjusted for left truncation (people entering the study at different ages) [31].

Multivariable analyses were done in three different adjustment sets to evaluate the difference in hazard by adding predictors to the model: i) adjusted for sex; ii) adjusted for sex, and PREVENT™ risk factors including total and HDL cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, history of diabetes and hypertension, smoking status, eGFR, and BMI; iii) further adjusted for CAC score. To improve interpretability of hazard ratios (HR), standardized PAT volume, density, and log transformed CAC score were used in the Cox model. For other variables, HRs were reported for every 10-unit increase in the variable. Restricted cubic spline analysis was performed to test the dose-response relationship between PAT volume and CVD events. To assess the potential influence of temporal confounding arising from more than 20 years of enrollment window in our study, a sub-analysis was performed by incorporating the calendar year of CT scan as an additional covariate in Models 3 and 4.

To investigate the prognostic performance of PAT volume beyond CAC score, patients were stratified into four groups based on the PAT volume (high volume was considered as top quartile: > 319 cm3) and CAC score (cut-off: 100 AU). CAC score of 100 AU was used as the threshold because it is considered as the transitioning point of low to moderate risk [32]. Kaplan-Meier curves assessed the cumulative incidence of CVD events across these four groups and Cox regression models were used to compute the HRs in univariate and multivariable models, using the PREVENT™ risk factors as covariates.

To evaluate the incremental discriminative value of PAT metrics to the baseline features beyond PREVENT™ risk factors and CAC score, we compared C-statistics in three Cox proportional hazard models. Model 1 included baseline PREVENT™ risk factors as described above. Model 2 further included log-transformed CAC score. Model 3 incorporated PAT volume and density metrics. Model discrimination was assessed using Harrell’s C-statistics. To further investigate the clinical importance of PAT metrics, the category-based Net Reclassification Improvement (NRI) was calculated across CAC groups (0, 1–99, ≥100 AU) and using the clinically established PREVENT™ risk categories as the reclassification thresholds. Events were truncated to 10 years to meet the criteria for established PREVENT™ thresholds. Both events (NRI+) and non-event (NRI-) components and corresponding confidence intervals (CI) are reported separately.

PREVENT™ risk score for total CVD events was calculated for each individual using the PyPREVENT python package [33]. Then, patients were stratified into four groups based on their PREVENT™ risk scores, and Cox proportional hazard models evaluated the association of PAT volume with total CVD events across the categories.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.1) and Python (version 3.13). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

A total of 11,897 adult Olmsted County residents without prior CVD events who underwent calcium scoring were identified. The mean age of the overall cohort was 54.6 ± 9.46 years, with 66% male and 97% White. Participants with higher PAT volume were older, had higher BMI, and increased prevalence of diabetes and hypertension; were more categorized as high risk based on PREVENT™ risk score; and had higher calcium scores. Distributions of race, smoking status, and lipid profile were also heterogenous across PAT groups. Coronary calcium CT measurements: CAC and PAT

CAC was present in 6333 (52%) subjects at baseline. In patients with prevalent CAC, median CAC score was 59 (IQR:12–228) AU. Subjects who developed total CVD events after follow-up, had significantly higher baseline CAC score (378 vs 113 AU, p<0.001). During the follow-up, total CVD events rate increased progressively with higher CAC categories, ranging from 0.48 per 100 person-years in subjects without coronary calcium to 2.19 per 100 person-years in subjects with CAC ≥ 300 AU.

PAT volume and density were moderately and inversely correlated (r: -0.6. p<0.001). PAT volume was also moderately correlated with BMI (r: 0.6, p<0.001). In the total population, median PAT volume was 228.7 (158.7–319.3) cm3, and median density was -81.4 (-87 to -75.88) HU. Men had significantly higher PAT volume and lower density than women (p<0.001 for both). PAT and CAC as CVD predictors

PAT metrics were successfully measured for all included subjects. During a median follow-up of 16.4 years, the composite outcome of total CVD was observed in 1142 (9.6%) subjects (0.66 per 100 person-years), including 559 heart failure events, 431 ischemic strokes, 342 myocardial infarctions, and 163 cardiovascular deaths (subjects could have more than one event).

To evaluate the association of PAT volume with total CVD events, patients were stratified in three groups based on PAT volume tertiles (T1: <182 cm³, T2: 182–285 cm³, T3: >285 cm³). Participants in higher tertiles of volume had increased cumulative incidence of CVD events (log-rank p < 0.001, Fig. 3). Median time to CVD events decreased progressively across tertiles (T1: 16.3 years, T2: 15.9 years, T3: 14.9 years, p<0.001). In Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for PREVENT™ risk factors and CAC score, higher PAT volume was significantly linearly associated with increased risk of incident CVD (p for linear association = 0.005). Furthermore, the risk rose steadily with higher PAT, and there was no evidence for J- or U-shaped patterns, the restricted cubic spline analysis showed no evidence of nonlinearity (p = 0.218). In multivariable cox regression analysis with age as the time scale, participants in the highest tertile of PAT volume (> 285 cm3) had approximately double the risk of total CVD events compared to those in the lowest tertile, after adjusting for sex (HR= 2.05, 95% CI: 1.74–2.42); after further adjustment for potential confounders, the risk remained 36% higher (HR=1.36, 95% CI: 1.12–1.64); and additional adjustment with CAC Score resulted in 30% higher risk of CVD in the highest tertile of PAT volume compared to the lowest (HR= 1.30, 95% CI: 1.08–1.58) (Table 2). However, these associations were not significant for PAT density in the fully adjusted model (p > 0.05, Table 2). Furthermore, investigating the interaction between PAT volume and density showed that there is no interaction between these two variables and they have independent effects on CVD risk (HR= 0.99, 95% CI: 0.94–1.04). Sex stratified analysis showed that, in male participants higher PAT volume is significantly associated with CVD events after adjusting for PREVENT™ risk factors and CAC score (HR= 1.36, 95% CI: 1.04–1.78), this association was not significant in the fully adjusted model in female participants. To account for temporal confounding during the study period, such as changes in CT scanner technology, a sub-analysis was performed by incorporating calendar year of CT scan as an additional covariate in Models 3 and 4. Results were robust for the association between PAT volume and CVD events. Discrimination ability of PAT features for CVD detection

Discrimination of CVD events improved incrementally with addition of CAC score and PAT parameters. As demonstrated in Fig. 6A, adding PAT volume and density to a model containing baseline PREVENT™ risk factors and CAC score, improved C-statists from 0.72 to 0.76 and this improvement was statistically significant (ΔC = +0.04, 95% CI: 0.025–0.054, p < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). To complement the discrimination analysis, category-based NRI was calculated using PREVENT™ risk categories across CAC strata (Fig. 6B). Among individuals with CAC = 0, adding PAT metrics yielded a statistically significant NRI of 0.28 (95% CI: 0.17–0.40), driven primarily by appropriate downward reclassification of non-events (NRI- = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.19–0.27), while reclassification of events was minimal. The largest reclassification benefit was observed in patients with zero CAC. Discussion

This population-based cohort study demonstrates that an automated DL-derived measurement of PAT volume from a clinically indicated ECG-gated CAC CT scan is an independent predictor of total cardiovascular events beyond traditional risk factors and CAC score. Our findings show that: first, increased PAT volume, not density, is independently associated with an increased risk of CVD events. Second, among individuals with low CAC and those classified as borderline and intermediate risk using the PREVENT™ equation, DL-derived PAT volume enables additional risk assessment that may support clinical decision making. Third, DL-derived PAT volume and density (as measured by HU), provide incremental value beyond traditional risk factors for discriminating CVD events. Together, these findings highlight that DL-based quantification of PAT volume can serve as a complementary tool to routine non-contrast CAC scoring images for clinical assessment and risk stratification, without requiring additional imaging or manual interpretation.

While prior studies examined the association between DL-derived PAT volume with cardiovascular events [34,35], our findings extend this work showing a direct comparison of PAT volume across the most recent PREVENT™ risk equation and evaluating the incremental discriminatory value beyond CAC score in a large, US population-based cohort.

Our findings align with prior research evaluating the association of cardiac adipose tissue with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Results from the Early Identification of Subclinical Atherosclerosis (EISNER) trial demonstrated that after accounting for ASCVD risk score, there was a 35% increase in adverse cardiovascular events per SD increase in EAT volume [34]. Similarly, the Heinz Nixdorf Recall Study reported higher event rates with increasing EAT across volume quartiles [35]. However, these studies do not investigate the incremental value that this marker can provide to current risk assessment tools. Our study strengthens these observations by demonstrating that the association between PAT and cardiovascular events persists over a long-term follow-up and remains clinically informative among individuals classified as borderline or intermediate risk and those with low CAC scores. This finding addresses a critical gap in previous studies, by identifying traditionally low risk patients who are at risk for future adverse events. Complementing these, Liu et al. showed that EAT volume and density improved outcome prediction in post myocardial infarction patients [36]. By demonstrating similar findings in our larger cohort without prior CVD, we extend the clinical utility of EAT metrics from secondary to primary prevention.

Several biological explanations have been described in literature. The unique anatomic position of EAT and lack of a fascial barrier facilitate direct paracrine signaling to the coronary vasculature and myocardium [37]. Prior research shows that under pathological conditions, elevated expression of inflammatory mediators including IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-1 by cardiac adipose tissue promotes the activation of macrophages and local inflammation [4,37]. Compared to subcutaneous adipose tissue, EAT has been shown to express higher levels of leptin and inflammatory cytokines, alongside reduced adiponectin and interleukin-10, [38]. These changes can contribute to the development of a proatherogenic environment. Also, paracardial adipose tissue, has been associated with systemic inflammation and insulin resistance [1,2], suggesting a potential role as a marker of cardiometabolic risk [39].

Importantly, we found that high PAT volume stratified risk even in individuals with low CAC scores and could appropriately reclassify patients across PREVENT™ risk categories, especially in the lower CAC groups. This addresses a critical gap in identifying traditionally low risk patients, who are at risk of future adverse events. This increased risk, unidentified by CAC score alone, may arise from systemic metabolic dysfunction and presence of non-calcified plaques (NCP), which is an independent predictor of adverse events in the future [40]. Historically, patients with NCP and low CAC scores (<100 AU) have been categorized as lower risk for future adverse events and those with CAC score of zero may not receive preventive therapies with aspirin and statin in the absence of high risk features [[41], [42], [43]]. However, adverse cardiac events are now being increasingly identified in patients who are low risk based on current risk scores [40,44], highlighting that CAC score alone may not fully capture residual cardiometabolic risk. This warrants the importance of additional assessment tools in this group. Currently, increased CAC score in otherwise low-risk individuals warrants initiation of medication and lifestyle modifications [45]. In contrast, absence of coronary artery calcium can reclassify patients with elevated ASCVD risk scores as low risk for future events and warrant cessation of statin or aspirin therapy [34,46]. The 7.3% event rate we observed in zero CAC patients over 16 years of follow-up reflects well-recognized heterogeneity within this group and is consistent with previous long-term warranty data [12,47,48]. Lower CVD-free survival in higher PAT volume individuals observed within the zero CAC group, shows that PAT can help identifying this residual cardiometabolic risk. Our findings show that high PAT volume remains predictive in borderline and intermediate PREVENT™ risk categories, independent of CAC, suggests PAT could guide preventive therapy decisions in patients whose risk remains uncertain after standard assessments.

The automated DL measurement addresses the primary barrier to clinical implementation. Manual PAT segmentation requires 10–30 min of expert time with inter-observer variability, making population screening impractical [49]. Our approach using DL had high overlap with manual segmentation with processing under 30 seconds. This automation enables integration of PAT measurement into existing CAC scoring workflows without additional physician time, making PAT assessment feasible for routine clinical practice.

These findings raise the hypothesis that PAT may represent a modifiable risk factor responsive to pharmacological therapies and lifestyle interventions. Prior studies have reported that therapeutic agents for cardiometabolic prevention, such as glucagon-like receptor agonist (GLP-1), sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors and statins can reduce cardiac adipose tissue [50,51]. However, the clinical link between reducing cardiac adipose tissue to improved cardiovascular outcomes needs to be tested in prospective interventional studies.

From an epidemiological endpoint, our study’s strength stands out by using large population-based cohort with long-term follow-up capturing sufficient events for robust analysis, comprehensive outcome adjudication through REP registry and physician reviewers, little missing information on covariates of interest, and multivariable adjustment for potential confounders. Our DL approach could reduce the variability, reduce the barriers associated with the conventional CT assessment, and support clinicians for identifying and improving care to patients with inconclusive or borderlines results. These features support the PAT assessment as a modifiable risk factor, and as a clinical marker that can be easily integrated into existing practice. While automated PAT volume was independently associated with incident CVD and modestly improved discrimination beyond CAC, its influence regarding clinical decision-making remains unclear. Our findings show that PAT-derived metrics were helpful for reclassifying individuals in lower CAC groups, this highlights that their value is limited when individuals are in higher risk levels driven by increased CAC scores.

Despite the major contributions of the current study, there are key limitations that merit consideration. First, the DL algorithms are still under early stages of development and implementation limits the generalizability of our findings. As a result, the skepticism from patients and clinicians may be limiting its real-world clinical adoption. Nevertheless, our group has previously developed and validated multiple DL models that can accurately identify diseases that are not apparent to clinicians [[52], [53], [54]]. This highlights the significance of the active and evolving area of investigation conducted by our group. Second, the predominance of White individuals within the REP system in Olmsted County, Minnesota (97% of our study population) limits the extrapolation of these findings to other racial/ethnic groups. This is particularly relevant given the well-established differences in adipose tissue distribution and cardiovascular risk profiles across diverse populations [[55], [56], [57]]. Socioeconomic characteristics within the REP non-Hispanic white population also differ from those in other regions of the country [58]. Although, the REP has been recognized for its rigorous epidemiologic data system, supporting the relevance of these findings to the Midwest region and comparable to other U.S. populations [58]. Third, the comorbidities were gathered using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9th and 10th version system codes, which introduces a possible source of misclassification bias [22,59]. To mitigate this, random samples of extracted information were independently validated in duplicate by two investigators. Lastly, our segmentation model captures total pericardial adipose tissue compromising both the epicardial and paracardial components. Given the mechanistically different contributions of these fat depots to cardiovascular outcomes, it would be valuable to investigate in future studies, whether this association is driven predominantly by epicardial or paracardial component of PAT. Despite these limitations, our findings do not compromise the internal validity of our study. We conducted a large, population-based cohort study with an extended follow-up, using a DL model with a robust performance that supports the reliability and reproducibility of our results.

Conclusion

In this large, contemporary U.S. population-based cohort study, we demonstrated that PAT volume can be automatically quantified from a routine non-contrast cardiac CT scan using DL algorithms. PAT volume was independently associated with incident CVD events and provided modestly improved discrimination beyond CAC and PREVENT™ risk equation. These results warrant validation in external and more diverse populations.
