Association of Charlson–Deyo Comorbidity Index With Survival Following Upfront Laryngectomy for Laryngeal Squamous Cell Carcinoma
Authors: David Herz, Aman M Patel, Disha Patil, George S Bebawy, Ariana Shaari, Andrey Filimonov, Ghayoour S Mir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCharlson–Deyo Comorbidity Index (CCI) predicts 10‐year mortality for a patient based on a range of comorbid conditions. Objective preoperative risk assessment tools inform physician decision making. Our study examines the relationship between CCI and overall survival (OS) following upfront laryngectomy for laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC).MethodsA retrospective analysis of the 2004–2016 National Cancer Database (NCDB) was conducted to identify adult patients with pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC undergoing upfront laryngectomy. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards modeling were performed to evaluate the association between CCI and OS.ResultsOf the 25,720 patients satisfying inclusion criteria, 16,204 (63.0%) were CCI = 0, 6812 (26.5%) were CCI = 1, 1948 (7.6%) were CCI = 2, and 756 (2.9%) were CCI ≥ 3. On univariate analysis, CCI groups differed by age (65.1% were < 65 years old in CCI = 0 vs. 57.3%, 49.6%, and 48.9% in CCI = 1, 2, and ≥ 3; p < 0.001), sex (p < 0.001), and race (p < 0.001). Differences also existed across CCI groups in primary site, T‐stage, N‐stage, systemic therapy, and radiotherapy (p < 0.001). On Kaplan–Meier analysis, 5‐year OS for CCI = 0, 1, 2, and ≥ 3 was 55.3%, 47.8%, 39.5%, and 34.0%, respectively (p < 0.001). CCI = 1 (aHR 1.20, 95% CI 1.14–1.26, p < 0.001), CCI = 2 (aHR 1.57, 95% CI 1.45–1.71, p < 0.001), and CCI ≥ 3 (aHR 1.77, 95% CI 1.56–2.01, p < 0.001) were associated with worse OS than CCI = 0.ConclusionIn an adult cohort with pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC undergoing upfront laryngectomy, increasing CCI was associated with worse OS. During perioperative counseling, CCI should be optimized and considered as a factor influencing survival outcomes.Level of Evidence4.Keywords: Charlson–Deyo Comorbidity Index, laryngeal cancer, laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, National Cancer Database, survival

Introduction

Laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) represents one of the most common malignancies of the head and neck region, significantly impacting patient quality of life due to its critical functions in voice and swallowing [1]. While treatment paradigms for LSCC increasingly emphasize organ preservation strategies such as chemoradiation, upfront total laryngectomy remains a necessary and often curative option for patients with advanced disease or those unable to tolerate chemoradiation protocols [2, 3, 4].

Despite advances in surgical techniques and postoperative management, total laryngectomy is associated with significant morbidity, affecting speech, swallowing, and overall patient function [5, 6]. Thus, accurate risk stratification and individualized patient counseling are crucial components in the management of LSCC [7, 8].

Traditionally, prognostic assessment in LSCC heavily relies on TNM staging, a classification system focusing on tumor size, nodal involvement, and the presence of distant metastasis [9]. Although valuable, TNM staging alone does not fully capture individual patient risk, as patients with identical staging often exhibit diverse survival outcomes due to varying health statuses and comorbid conditions [10]. Recognizing this gap, recent literature has emphasized the need to integrate patient‐specific factors into preoperative counseling and treatment decision‐making, particularly in complex surgical cases like total laryngectomy.

The Charlson–Deyo Comorbidity Index (CCI) is a validated tool designed to quantify patient comorbidity burden, predicting 10‐year mortality across various disease contexts [11]. Previous studies using the National Cancer Database (NCDB) have established the prognostic utility of CCI in head and neck cancers. These studies consistently demonstrate that higher CCI scores correlate with poorer overall survival (OS) and higher complication rates [12, 13]. However, there remains a notable absence of research focused on evaluating the predictive value of CCI specifically in the context of upfront laryngectomy for LSCC.

Addressing this critical knowledge gap, the current study aims to explore the association between CCI and OS among patients undergoing upfront laryngectomy for LSCC. We hypothesize that an increasing comorbidity burden, reflected by higher CCI scores, will correlate significantly with reduced survival. By examining this relationship, our study seeks to improve preoperative counseling, refine risk stratification practices, and ultimately guide clinicians toward more tailored, patient‐centered approaches in treating LSCC.

Methods

Data Source

The NCDB is jointly sponsored by the American Cancer Society (ACS) and American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer (CoC). The NCDB collects data from > 1500 CoC‐accredited hospitals within the United States, capturing > 70% of newly diagnosed head and neck cancer [14, 15, 16]. The Rutgers New Jersey Medical School exempted our study because of the de‐identified nature of patient data. The ACS and CoC are not responsible for the conclusions derived herein.

Inclusion Criteria

The 2004–2016 NCDB was retrospectively reviewed for adult patients diagnosed with pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC undergoing upfront laryngectomy (Figure 1). American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 7th edition was utilized for this study. The transition from the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, seventh edition to the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, eighth edition occurred in October 2016, but classification criteria for pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC were unchanged. The study period was selected to provide a large contemporary national cohort with adequate follow‐up for 5‐year OS analysis while maintaining consistency in NCDB treatment and staging variables. LSCC was identified using International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD‐O‐3) histology codes 8070/3, 8071/3, 8072/3, 8073/3, 8074/3, 8075/3, 8076/3, 8078/3, behavior code 3 (behavior code 3: signifies an invasive or malignant tumor), and topography codes (C320 [glottis], C321 [supraglottis], C322 [subglottis], C323 [laryngeal cartilage], C328 [overlapping lesion of larynx], C329 [larynx, NOS]). Upfront laryngectomy was defined using NCDB surgical procedure codes 30–80, which capture subtotal, partial, total, radical, and pharyngolaryngectomy procedures, including laryngectomy NOS. Procedures coded as local excision, biopsy, polypectomy, or ablative therapies (codes below 30, such as laser ablation, cryosurgery, electrocautery, or photodynamic therapy), as well as NOS or unknown surgery codes were excluded, as these do not represent definitive laryngectomy. To fully characterize the patient demographics, clinicopathologic features, and treatment of pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC, strict inclusion criteria were implemented only for survival analyses. All patients included in the descriptive analysis underwent upfront laryngectomy, establishing a cohort in which treatment sequences reflect surgery followed by radiation and/or systemic therapy. Patients were excluded from survival analyses if they had a history of prior malignancy; distant metastasis (cM1); treatment with palliative intent; neoadjuvant RT or chemotherapy; or unknown vital status or follow‐up [17].

Variables

Patient data included age at diagnosis, sex, race, primary payer status, CCI, history of prior malignancy, histology, primary site, grade, pathological tumor (pT) classification, pathologic nodal (pN) classification, clinical metastasis (cM), surgical margin status, treatment, 90‐day mortality, vital status, and survival time. CCI was provided as a standardized variable in the NCDB, based on ICD‐coded comorbidities present at the time of diagnosis, excluding the primary malignancy. The index is truncated at a maximum score of 3 in the dataset. Consistent with prior studies using NCDB, CCI was treated as a categorical variable with values of 0, 1, 2, or 3+ [11, 18, 19]. While some studies use the age‐adjusted Charlson comorbidity index (ACCI), which adds one point per decade over age 50, we used the standard CCI as provided by the NCDB, which does not include age as a component. This approach is consistent with prior NCDB analyses and allowed for separate evaluation of age and comorbidity [20, 21]. Microscopic, macroscopic, or unspecified residual tumor were considered PSM. Patients with surgery followed by radiation were classified as having received postoperative RT. The primary outcome of our study was 5‐year OS. Survival time was calculated to 5 years of follow‐up.

Statistical Analysis

Patients undergoing surgical treatment in different CCI cohorts were compared with the chi‐square and likelihood ratio tests, as appropriate. Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed with the log‐rank test to estimate 5‐year OS. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard regression models handling missing data with listwise elimination and adjusting for all significant variables in univariable analysis were implemented to identify patient demographics, clinicopathologic features, and definitive treatments independently associated with OS. A total of 9980 cases were excluded from survival analysis due to predefined criteria. Specifically, 7164 patients had a history of prior malignancy, 346 had palliative or unknown treatment intent, 2430 had unknown vital status, and 746 had missing surgical margin data (Figure 1). The proportionality of hazards was evaluated using time‐dependent covariates and was not violated in any regression models. Sensitivity analyses were performed through subgroup analyses stratified by age ≥ 65 years to evaluate association between CCI and OS. This subgroup represents a cohort where competing comorbidity‐related mortality may have a greater influence on outcomes. The two‐sided threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. SPSS version 29 (IBM) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Patient Demographics, Clinicopathologic Features, and Treatment

A total of 25,720 patients with pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC undergoing upfront laryngectomy satisfied inclusion criteria. CCI groups differed significantly by age (65.1% were < 65 years in CCI = 0 vs. 57.3%, 49.6%, and 48.9% in CCI = 1, 2, and ≥ 3; p < 0.001), sex (p < 0.001), race (p < 0.001), primary site (p < 0.001), pT classification (p < 0.001), and pN classification (p = 0.001). Overall, 51.9% of patients received RT and 25.8% received systemic therapy, with both distributions varying across CCI groups (each p < 0.001). Negative surgical margins were achieved in 85.8% of patients and did not differ by CCI (p = 0.740). Ninety‐day mortality increased with comorbidity burden, rising from 2.6% for CCI = 0 to 8.7% for CCI ≥ 3 (p < 0.001). Readmission within 30 days occurred in 7.4% of patients and was also associated with higher comorbidity (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Cox Proportional Hazards Analysis

On multivariable modeling, higher CCI remained independently associated with worse OS: CCI = 1 aHR 1.197 (95% CI 1.136–1.262, p < 0.001), CCI = 2 aHR 1.574 (1.449–1.710, p < 0.001), and CCI ≥ 3 aHR 1.767 (1.556–2.007, p < 0.001) compared with CCI = 0. Other independent predictors of decreased OS included age ≥ 65 years (aHR 1.531, 95% CI 1.460–1.606, p < 0.001), Black race (aHR 1.099, 95% CI 1.035–1.166, p = 0.002), higher pT and pN categories (all p < 0.001), and positive margins (aHR 1.314, 95% CI 1.236–1.398, p < 0.001). Female sex was protective (aHR 0.812, 95% CI 0.764–0.863, p < 0.001). RT was independently associated with improved OS (aHR 0.805, 95% CI 0.761–0.851, p < 0.001), while systemic therapy was not significant (p = 0.735) (Table 2).

5‐Year OS by CCI

On Kaplan–Meier analysis, 5‐year OS declined with increasing comorbidity: 55.3% for CCI = 0, 47.8% for CCI = 1, 39.5% for CCI = 2, and 34.0% for CCI ≥ 3 (log‐rank p < 0.001). Stratified analysis showed this decline persisted across demographic and clinicopathologic subgroups. By age, patients < 65 years had higher survival (59.2% for CCI = 0 vs. 39.8% for CCI ≥ 3) compared to those ≥ 65 years (46.7% for CCI = 0 vs. 27.7% for CCI ≥ 3, p < 0.001). By sex, females demonstrated improved OS compared to males across all CCI groups (62.3% vs. 53.7% for CCI = 0; 33.3% vs. 34.3% for CCI ≥ 3, p < 0.001). Survival also varied by race, with White patients generally showing higher OS than Black patients (56.2% vs. 51.3% for CCI = 0, 33.8% vs. 33.3% for CCI ≥ 3, p < 0.001). Primary tumor site influenced outcomes, with true vocal cord tumors showing the best survival (59.2% for CCI = 0) and larynx NOS the worst (47.6% for CCI = 0). Survival progressively decreased with advancing T and N stage regardless of comorbidity. Negative margins conferred improved survival compared to positive margins across all CCI groups (56.7% vs. 45.9% for CCI = 0). On stratified analysis, untreated patients demonstrated higher crude survival rates, likely reflecting selection bias, whereas RT remained independently associated with improved OS on multivariable analysis (Figure 2, Table 3).

5‐Year OS by CCI, Stratified by Older Age

Among 5354 patients aged ≥ 65 included in the survival analysis, increasing comorbidity remained prognostic on multivariable analysis: CCI = 1 aHR 1.203 (95% CI 1.111–1.303, p < 0.001), CCI = 2 aHR 1.601 (1.492–1.795, p < 0.001), and CCI ≥ 3 aHR 1.705 (1.427–2.038, p < 0.001) compared with CCI = 0. In this older cohort, RT remained protective (aHR 0.829, 95% CI 0.754–0.912, p < 0.001), while positive margins conferred higher risk (aHR 1.233, 95% CI 1.119–1.357, p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis of KM curves showed survival declined steadily with higher CCI scores, though absolute OS was lower in older patients compared to the overall cohort (Figure 3, Table 4). The direction and magnitude of the association between increasing CCI and worse survival remained consistent with findings observed in the overall cohort, without major discrepancies identified on subgroup analysis.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the association between CCI and 5‐year OS in patients who underwent upfront laryngectomy for pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC. Our analysis revealed a significant decline in 5‐year OS with increasing CCI scores. Additional factors such as treatment modality, age, T‐stage, N‐stage, margin status, and sex were also found to significantly influence survival outcomes. CCI assigns a weighted score to comorbid conditions such as diabetes, heart failure, or renal disease and correlates the total sum of these scores with the risk of mortality [22]. Previous retrospective, single‐institution cohort studies have suggested CCI as a predictor of mortality in patients with surgically treated LSCC. One retrospective cohort study of 548 patients with T3‐T4 LSCC who received a total laryngectomy showed that a CCI score greater than 3 was significantly associated with a 70% increased 5‐year mortality risk [23]. Nesic et al. found that 177 patients with T1‐4 N0‐3 LSCC most frequently had cardiovascular and respiratory comorbidities, and higher CCI scores were again found to be significantly correlated with worse survival outcomes [24]. Another retrospective cohort study of 90 patients with surgically treated T1‐4 N0‐2 LSCC conducted by Castro et al. found that 4‐year survival dropped significantly with increasing CCI score [25]. In line with this evidence, the present study demonstrates that comorbidity burden is a significant determinant of post‐laryngectomy outcomes in a large, national cohort. On univariate analysis, perioperative risk rose sharply with comorbidity burden, with 90‐day mortality and readmission rates increasing significantly among patients with higher CCI scores, underscoring that comorbidities directly compromise surgical recovery (Table 1). On Kaplan–Meier analysis, 5‐year OS decreased from 55.3% to 34.0% as CCI increased from 0 to ≥ 3 (p < 0.001) (Figure 2, Table 3). Together, these findings suggest that CCI may capture surgical vulnerability, making it a critical factor in perioperative risk stratification and counseling.

Although prior studies in heterogeneous head and neck cancer populations have reported mixed findings regarding the prognostic value of CCI, our study focused specifically on patients undergoing upfront laryngectomy for LSCC, a population with substantial surgical and perioperative morbidity. In this setting, comorbidity burden may play a particularly important role in postoperative recovery, tolerance of adjuvant therapy, and long‐term survival. Our findings therefore support the utility of CCI within this specific surgical LSCC cohort. Furthermore, our sensitivity analysis included an older patient subgroup (≥ 65 years), in whom distinguishing the independent effects of comorbidity burden from age‐related physiologic risk is particularly challenging, further supporting the utility of CCI within this higher‐risk population. Alternative CCI thresholds and categorizations have been proposed in prior head and neck cancer studies. Our grouping strategy was selected both to remain consistent with prior NCDB‐based analyses and to further stratify patients with greater comorbidity burden, as higher CCI thresholds may better distinguish patients with substantial physiologic vulnerability, including differences in postoperative recovery, treatment tolerance, and overall mortality risk [26, 27, 28].

Comorbidities can decrease survival in patients after total upfront laryngectomy by complicating postoperative recovery and limiting the ability to tolerate adjuvant therapies such as radiation or chemotherapy. Additionally, comorbid conditions may increase the risk of complications and long‐term non‐cancer‐related mortality [29, 30]. In clinical practice, a higher CCI score may also lead to more conservative systemic treatment due to concerns about treatment‐related toxicity. For example, patients with multiple comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or renal insufficiency, are at a higher risk of experiencing adverse effects from aggressive systemic therapies [31, 32, 33]. As a result, clinicians may opt for less aggressive modalities, especially when the risks of toxicity outweigh the potential benefits of combined treatment for LSCC [23, 34, 35]. In the present study, while systemic therapy was not significantly associated with improved survival, radiation therapy was (Table 2). These findings potentially reflect a bias toward offering radiation to patients with better overall health and fewer comorbidities. Likewise, the lack of significant association between systemic therapy and survival may reflect the exclusion of patients with higher comorbidity burdens from chemotherapy due to safety concerns. In a study conducted by Fong et al. on T1‐4 N0‐3 LSCC, the proportion of patients who received single modality treatment increased as CCI increased [8]. This pattern was seen in our results as well, with a lower proportion of patients with CCI ≥ 3 receiving systemic or radiation therapy compared to those with a CCI of 0 (Table 1).

Predictably, the proportion of patients aged 65 or older increased as comorbidity burden increased. Age above 65 was also significantly associated with worse 5‐year survival outcomes (Table 2). Current literature on LSCC confirms this trend. A retrospective cohort study by Yang et al. showed that T1‐4 N0‐3 LSCC patients aged ≥ 60 had a higher likelihood of metastases and worse survival compared to patients under 60 years [36]. Furthermore, Liang et al. analyzed an elderly Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) cohort (≥ 60 years old) and found that advanced age, especially ≥ 80, was independently predictive of mortality within 6 months [37]. Additionally, a meta‐analysis with over 32,000 LSCC patients affirmed that age (≥ 60 years old) and comorbidity scores (CCI ≥ 1) were some of the strongest predictors of reduced OS, with CCI being second only to TNM stage in terms of its predictive ability [38]. Our paper further evaluates CCI by stratifying by age. We found that, in this older population, CCI is still a strong predictor, with increased 5‐year postoperative mortality (Table 4).

The present study found that most patients presented with advanced T stage, with T3 and T4 tumors comprising the majority of the cohort, while early‐stage tumors (T1–T2) were less common. Similarly, higher nodal burden was frequently observed, with a substantial proportion of patients staged as N2 disease, whereas N1 and N3 comprised smaller percentages (Table 1). These findings suggest that the majority of patients in this cohort were diagnosed at an advanced stage. Advanced T stage was also a significant predictor of outcomes. Compared to T1 tumors, patients with T2 and T3 tumors had worse outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have shown T stage is a predictor of OS in LSCC, with T4 stage associated with the highest risk of mortality due to invasion into surrounding structures [8, 38]. A retrospective cohort study of patients with T3–4 N0 glottic LSCC found that 5‐year OS was significantly lower in T4 patients compared to T3 patients [39]. Of note, this study also compared outcomes between patients treated with total upfront laryngectomy and partial laryngectomy. Patients who underwent partial laryngectomy had significantly better survival outcomes compared to those who received total laryngectomy. However, this difference was likely influenced by selection bias, as a greater percentage of total‐laryngectomy patients had T4 tumors.

No significant difference was found in margin status (positive or negative) across CCI scores in our analysis. Positive margin status was associated with significantly increased risk of mortality. A retrospective study of patients who underwent total laryngectomy for T1‐4 N0‐2 LSCC found that patients with positive margins experienced significantly worse disease‐free survival (DFS) compared to patients with negative margins [40]. In the same cohort, no significant survival difference was observed between patients with “close” margins (1–5 mm) and those with “clear” margins (> 5 mm), suggesting that achieving margins greater than 1 mm is sufficient after total laryngectomy, underscoring that staging and comorbidity remain more significant determinants of survival in this population.

The majority of patients across all CCI groups were male. Female sex was found to be significantly associated with improved survival. Several studies have identified sex as a prognostic factor in LSCC. In a SEER study of more than 47,000 LSCC patients, Wang et al. found that males had significantly worse 5‐year OS than females. Females were also found to have higher 5‐year cancer‐specific survival than males (65% vs. 59%) [41]. Similarly, Zhang et al. found that female sex was significantly associated with improved outcomes in a study on T1‐4 N0‐3 LSCC [42].

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the NCDB lacks data regarding the severity and management of comorbidities, which may affect the precision of CCI classification. Additionally, the NCDB is a hospital‐based registry derived from CoC‐accredited institutions and may therefore not fully represent outcomes across all treatment settings, limiting generalizability. The database also does not provide disease‐specific survival, recurrence outcomes, cancer‐specific mortality, or detailed functional outcomes such as voice quality and postoperative quality of life. Important behavioral variables including smoking, alcohol use, and substance use are also unavailable and could not be controlled for in the analysis. Finally, retrospective database studies remain susceptible to selection bias and residual confounding despite multivariable adjustment. Future prospective studies incorporating frailty indices, functional outcomes, and disease‐specific survival measures are warranted to further refine risk stratification in surgically treated LSCC patients.

Conclusion

In this study of patients with pT1‐4 N0‐3 M0 LSCC undergoing upfront laryngectomy, higher CCI scores were independently associated with worse 5‐year OS. These findings support incorporating comorbidity burden into preoperative counseling and multidisciplinary treatment planning. Patients with elevated CCI scores may benefit from medical optimization, closer perioperative monitoring, and individualized consideration of multimodal treatment strategies. Incorporating comorbidity indices such as CCI alongside traditional oncologic staging may improve individualized risk stratification in surgically treated LSCC.
