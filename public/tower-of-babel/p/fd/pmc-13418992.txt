Synergistic effects of ambient particulate matter and extreme weather events on arthritis risk: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yongbin Wang, Weihang Liu, Yongsheng Liao, Ruiting Zhao, Chunjie Xu, Fei Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of long-term exposure to a comprehensive panel of particulate matter (PM) size fractions and extreme weather events in the development of incident arthritis remains underexplored. We aimed to evaluate their independent, combined, and interactive effects on arthritis risk in a Chinese middle-aged and older population.MethodsThis prospective study utilized data from two nationally representative cohorts: The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study and the China Family Panel Studies. A total of 13,147 participants aged ≥ 45 years without baseline arthritis were included. Annual exposures to PM1, PM2.5, PM1−2.5, PM10, PM2.5−10, and extreme weather events (low/high-temperature days, extreme rainfall/drought days, and a composite Climate Physical Risk Index [CPRI]) were estimated using high-resolution datasets. Incident arthritis was identified via self-reported physician diagnosis. Time-updated Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations. Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression evaluated the mixture effect, and additive interaction models quantified synergies between PM and extreme weather. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) and threshold effect analysis were utilized to evaluate potential nonlinear associations.ResultsOver a median follow-up, 1,200 incident arthritis cases occurred. Each 1 µg/m³ increase in PM1, PM2.5, PM1−2.5, and PM10, was associated with hazard ratios (HRs) of 1.061 (95% CI: 1.030–1.093), 1.022 (95% CI: 1.011–1.033), 1.023 (95% CI: 1.009–1.037), and 1.004 (95% CI: 1.001–1.006), respectively. Additionally, each 1-day increase in extreme low-temperature day (LTD) was linked to HRs of 1.042 (95% CI: 1.019–1.065). The WQS mixture index yielded an HR of 1.719 (95% CI: 1.379–2.143), with PM2.5−10 contributing the largest weight (38.8%), followed by PM1−2.5 (34.7%) and LTD (25.4%). Dose-response curves revealed accelerating nonlinear relationships for PM1, PM2.5−10, LTD, extreme rainfall days (ERD), extreme drought days (EDD) and CPRI. In exploratory threshold analyses, above 40.862 µg/m³ for PM₁, the HR per 1 µg/m³ was 1.193 (95% CI: 1.131–1.259); for LTD above 17.624 days, the HR per additional day was 1.173 (95% CI: 1.147–1.199). For PM2.5₋₁₀, the threshold was 18.692 µg/m³, with a strong association below (HR = 3.276, 95% CI: 2.338–4.590) and a marginal one above (HR = 1.005, 95% CI: 1.000–1.009). Significant additive interactions were observed between LTD and both PM10 (Synergy Index [S] = 2.129, 95% CI: 1.393–2.865) and PM2.5 (S = 1.867, 95% CI: 1.208–2.526). The association between PM and arthritis was modified by smoking and geographic region.ConclusionsLong-term exposure to ambient PM (across multiple size fractions) and extreme low-temperature weather are independent and synergistic risk factors for incident arthritis in middle-aged and older Chinese adults. Public health strategies targeting arthritis prevention should adopt an integrated approach addressing both air quality and climate resilience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27983-0.Keywords: Arthritis, Particulate matter, Extreme weather events, Environmental exposure, Cohort study

Introduction

Arthritis encompasses a group of disorders characterized by inflammation and degeneration of joints and surrounding tissues, leading to pain, stiffness, swelling, and functional impairment [1]. This broad spectrum of conditions, including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, represents a leading cause of global disability and diminished quality of life [2]. The disease burden is substantial and growing. The Global Burden of Disease Study estimated 595 million individuals worldwide were living with osteoarthritis in 2020, a 132% increase since 1990 [2]. In China, the prevalence is similarly rising, with osteoarthritis cases increasing from 26.1 million in 1990 to 61.2 million in 2017 [3]. Concurrently, rheumatoid arthritis affects approximately 0.42% of the Chinese population, with over 5 million patients reported [4]. Beyond their impact on individuals, these conditions impose significant socioeconomic burdens through direct medical costs and indirect productivity losses [5].

Amidst this rising burden, the potential role of modifiable environmental risk factors warrants critical examination. Climate change and air pollution are paramount global public health challenges, and their intersection with chronic inflammatory conditions like arthritis is gaining scientific attention [6, 7]. Most epidemiological research to date has focused on fine particulate matter (PM2.5), linking it to an increased risk of inflammatory arthritis [8]. While cross-sectional evidence has associated PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 exposure with prevalent arthritis [9], there remains a notable scarcity of large-scale, prospective cohort studies investigating the impact of long-term exposure to a comprehensive panel of particulate matter (PM) size fractions on arthritis incidence. Crucially, the health effects of other understudied fractions—such as PM1−2.5, and PM2.5−10—on incident arthritis risk are largely unexplored. Simultaneously, in the context of a rapidly changing climate, the impact of recurrent extreme weather events—including extreme low-temperature days (LTD), extreme high-temperature days (HTD), extreme rainfall days (ERD), and extreme drought days (EDD)—on arthritis incidence is poorly understood. Furthermore, a comprehensive assessment incorporating a composite Climate Physical Risk Index (CPRI), which integrates multiple climate hazards, is notably lacking in the context of arthritis etiology.

Clinical observations suggest weather fluctuations can exacerbate symptoms [10], and biological plausibility exists for both pollutant- and weather-driven pathways involving systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and altered joint physiology [11–13]. Critically, populations are concurrently exposed to complex mixtures of these environmental stressors, which may interact synergistically or antagonistically. For instance, certain meteorological conditions can influence the concentration, distribution, and toxicity of airborne particulates. Yet, quantitative evidence on the potential interactions between specific PM fractions and extreme weather events on arthritis risk is scarce.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, we leveraged two large, nationally representative prospective cohorts in China. Our objectives were threefold: (1) to evaluate the independent associations of long-term exposure to a comprehensive panel of PM size fractions (PM1, PM2.5, PM₁₋2.5, PM10, and PM2.5₋10) and diverse extreme weather events (LTD, HTD, ERD, and EDD) and the CPRI with incident arthritis risk; (2) to assess the combined health effect of this multi-factorial environmental mixture; and (3) to quantitatively test for potential additive interactions between specific PM exposures and extreme weather events. By adopting an integrated environmental exposure perspective, this study aims to provide novel evidence on the common and interactive environmental etiology of arthritis to inform targeted public health strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

This prospective cohort study utilized longitudinal data from two independent, nationally representative surveys in China: The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the China Family Panel Studies (CFPS) [14, 15]. The combined baseline sample covered 28 provinces. We applied sequential eligibility criteria to establish a cohort for analyzing long-term environmental exposures and incident arthritis. Participants with prevalent rheumatoid arthritis at baseline were excluded, and individuals younger than 45 years were removed. Subjects with completely missing key covariates or time-varying exposure variables were excluded, and observations with missing city information were removed as geographic identifiers were required for environmental exposure assignment. Implausible BMI values and climate exposure values exceeding the theoretical upper limit resulted in censoring from that visit onward. Follow-up was truncated at gaps exceeding three years between visits to account for discontinuities in longitudinal follow-up. Participants with fewer than three follow-up visits were excluded from the final analysis. Missing covariate data were handled using multiple imputation. After applying these criteria, the final analytical sample comprised 13,147 participants who were arthritis-free at baseline (Figure S1). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of participants from each individual cohort (CHARLS and CFPS) are presented in Table S1. The study period spanned 2011–2020 for CHARLS and 2012–2020 for CFPS. The total accumulated follow-up time was 103,262 person-years (CHARLS: 55,337 person-years; CFPS: 47,925 person-years). A comparison of characteristics between included participants and those excluded solely due to insufficient follow-up is provided in Table S2.

Exposure assessment

Long-term exposures to environmental factors were assessed using two high-resolution, spatiotemporally matched datasets.

Air pollutants: Annual average concentrations of PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 were obtained from the China High-resolution Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset (https://weijing-rs.github.io/product.html). The CHAP dataset provides continuous, 1-km resolution gridded estimates across mainland China from 2000 onward, generated by integrating ground-based monitoring, satellite remote sensing, atmospheric modeling, and machine learning techniques [16–18]. The model performance for each PM fraction, assessed by 10-fold cross-validation on daily estimates, demonstrates high accuracy: for PM1, the cross-validation coefficient of determination (CV-R²) is 0.83, with a root-mean-square error (RMSE) of 9.50 µg/m³ and a mean absolute error (MAE) of 6.17 µg/m³; for PM2.5, CV-R² = 0.92, RMSE = 10.76 µg/m³, MAE = 6.32 µg/m³; and for PM10, CV-R² = 0.90, RMSE = 21.12 µg/m³, MAE = 11.22 µg/m³. These metrics confirm the high quality of the exposure estimates used in the present study. From these, we derived concentrations of intermediate size fractions: PM1−2.5 (PM2.5 - PM1) and PM2.5−10 (PM10 - PM2.5).

Extreme weather events: Data on extreme weather were sourced from the CPRI dataset [19, 20]. This dataset provides annual counts of days on which weather conditions exceeded location-specific historical thresholds for four event types: LTD, HTD, ERD, and EDD, covering the period 1993–2023. These thresholds are defined based on the historical distribution at each weather station over the baseline period 1973–1992: LTD uses the 10th percentile of daily mean temperature, HTD the 90th percentile, ERD the 95th percentile of daily rainfall, and EDD the 5th percentile of daily relative humidity. For each year from 1993 to 2023, the counts are calculated by comparing daily observations to these station-specific thresholds. Regional annual counts are obtained by averaging the counts across all stations within the region. Additionally, we utilized the composite CPRI, which integrates the standardized frequencies of these four extreme events using equal weights into a single annual measure of overall climate-related physical risk. Annual counts of extreme weather days for each city/province were directly sourced from the CPRI dataset. These indices are constructed by averaging daily meteorological observations from all stations within the region, without spatial interpolation. Participants were assigned exposure values based on their city of residence.

For each participant, residential addresses at each survey wave were geocoded. Long-term average exposures for each environmental metric were calculated for each participant-year by averaging data from the grid cells corresponding to their residence of city over relevant time windows aligned with the follow-up intervals.

Definition of arthritis

Arthritis status was ascertained in each wave using the standardized question: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have arthritis or rheumatism?” Participants who answered “no” at baseline but “yes” in a subsequent wave were classified as incident cases. The date of diagnosis was estimated as the midpoint between the last survey without a report and the first survey with a report.

Covariates

To control for potential confounding factors, the following covariates and their categories were included in the analysis: Demographic characteristics, including age (continuous), sex (male/female), marital status (married/other), and education level (illiterate/primary school/middle school/high school or above); Lifestyle and behaviors, including physical activity level (inactive/moderate/vigorous), smoking history (yes/no), and alcohol drinking history (yes/no); Anthropometric measure, specifically body mass index (BMI, continuous, kg/m²); Social and geographic factors, including residence type (urban/rural), city and cohort source; and Comorbidity status, including hypertension (yes/no) and diabetes (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for participant characteristics and environmental exposures were summarized for the total sample and stratified by arthritis status. Continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) as appropriate, and categorical variables as frequency (percentage). Differences were tested using t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or tests. Correlations between environmental exposures were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients.

The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated between time-varying environmental exposures and incident arthritis risk was analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression with a “start-stop” (or Andersen-Gill) data structure. This approach split each participant’s follow-up time into multiple intervals between consecutive surveys. For each interval, we defined the start time, stop time, and event status (arthritis diagnosed or not). Exposure variables were assigned as the average concentration over the specific interval (e.g., 1-year, 2-year, or 3-year average corresponding to the interval length). Time-varying covariates (BMI, physical activity, smoking, alcohol, hypertension, diabetes) were assigned their values from the survey at the start of the interval. Fixed covariates (sex, education, marital status, residence, city, cohort source) used baseline values. Follow-up time was used as the underlying time scale. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) depicting the assumed confounding structure and temporal ordering is provided in Figure S2.

We constructed three nested models. Model 1: Adjusted for environmental exposures only, with a cluster robust variance estimator to account for within-individual correlation across intervals. Model 2: Additionally, adjusted for core demographic and anthropometric factors: time-updated age, sex, and lagged BMI. Model 3 (Fully adjusted model): Further adjusted for all remaining covariates: lagged physical activity, smoking, alcohol drinking, hypertension, and diabetes status, plus fixed education level, marital status, urban/rural residence, city, and cohort source (CHARLS vs. CFPS).

Weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression was used to construct a mixture index of five environmental exposures: PM1, PM1−2.5, PM2.5−10, LTD, and ERD. Each exposure was first coded into quartiles (0–3). Participants were randomly split into a training set (60%) and a validation set (40%). The training set underwent 100 bootstrap resamples to estimate the mean and standard deviation of the component weights, which were then used to construct the WQS index. HRs represent the change in risk per one-unit increase in the WQS index, which corresponds to a simultaneous one-quartile increase in all five components. The association between the WQS index and incident arthritis was assessed in the validation set using a Cox model with robust standard errors clustered by participant. All analyses were conducted in R using the survival package.

Restricted cubic splines (RCS) with four knots (at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles) were incorporated into the fully adjusted Cox model to examine potential nonlinear dose–response relationships. All HRs were expressed relative to the median exposure (P50). For exposures showing nonlinear associations, we additionally performed exploratory piecewise linear models to identify potential thresholds. Stratified Cox models were run within subgroups defined by age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), sex, BMI category (underweight [BMI < 18.5 kg/m²], normal [18.5 ≤ BMI < 24 kg/m²], overweight [24 ≤ BMI < 28 kg/m²], obese [BMI ≥ 28 kg/m²] per Chinese standard), education, marital status, residence, region, smoking, alcohol use, hypertension and diabetes. Heterogeneity across subgroups was tested using the Z-test and Cochran’s Q test. To quantify interactions on the additive scale, we dichotomized pairs of exposures (e.g., PM10 and LTD) at their median values to create four categories: low-low (reference), high-low, low-high, and high-high. These categories were included in the fully adjusted Cox model. The relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the attributable proportion due to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (S) were calculated from the resulting HRs. RERI > 0, AP > 0, and S > 1 collectively indicate a synergistic interaction. The 95% CIs for the RERI, AP, and S were estimated using the delta method.

To examine the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we performed a nested case–control analysis using propensity score matching, in which each incident case was matched to up to three controls (1:3 matching) with a caliper width of 0.02 and without replacement. Propensity scores were estimated based on age, sex, education level, marital status, urban/rural residence, city, smoking history, and alcohol consumption history, all assessed at the last follow-up visit. Covariate balance before and after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.10 indicating adequate balance .Second, to assess potential over adjustment bias arising from time-varying covariates that may lie on the causal pathway, we sequentially refitted the fully adjusted model after excluding (i) hypertension and diabetes, (ii) physical activity, and (iii) all four potential mediators (BMI, hypertension, diabetes, and physical activity).Finally, to address potential residual confounding by secular calendar-time trends, we additionally included entry year as a covariate in the fully adjusted Model 3.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Description of demographic characteristics

A total of 13,147 participants were included in this study. After several years of follow-up, 1,200 individuals (9.1%) developed incident arthritis, while 11,947 individuals (90.9%) remained non-incident. Gender distribution showed a significant difference (P < 0.001), with males comprising 44.4% and females 55.6% in the arthritis group. Age distribution (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years) also differed significantly between the groups (P = 0.013), with 22.6% of the incident arthritis group aged ≥ 65 years. BMI categories showed a significant difference between the groups (P < 0.001), with an obesity proportion of 10.3% in the incident arthritis group. The distributions of education level, residential area (urban/rural), geographic region (north/south), physical activity level, alcohol consumption (P = 0.047), hypertension, and diabetes all demonstrated significant differences between the groups (all P < 0.001). However, marital status did not differ significantly (P = 0.908). For environmental exposure variables, the weighted averages for LTD, HTD, and CPRI were significantly different between the two groups (all P < 0.001). However, the weighted averages for various air pollutants (PM1, PM2.5, PM1−2.5, PM10, and PM2.5−10) as well as for ERD and EDD showed no significant differences between the groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). The long-term average level of environmental exposure was as follows: PM1: 27.87 ± 8.45 µg/m³, PM2.5: 49.28 ± 15.75 µg/m³, PM10: 84.62 ± 29.50 µg/m³, PM1−2.5: 21.41 ± 7.67 µg/m³, PM2.5−10: 35.33 ± 15.76 µg/m³. The annual averages for LTD, HTD, ERD, EDD, and CPRI were 18.76 ± 3.66 days, 45.19 ± 8.00 days, 22.35 ± 8.23 days, 19.47 ± 9.74 days, and 26.44 ± 3.28 respectively (Table 1). Table S3 presents the cumulative observations during the follow-up period. Throughout the study, a total of 61,503 follow-up visits were recorded. Among these, participants who developed new-onset arthritis during follow-up contributed 5,119 people‑visits (8.3%) accumulated from baseline onward, while those who remained free of arthritis contributed 56,384 people‑visits (91.7%).

Independent associations of PM and extreme weather events with incident arthritis

The correlation matrix of environmental exposures is presented in Table S4. Strong positive correlations were observed among all particulate matter size fractions (rs = 0.814–0.978, all P < 0.001). Among climate indices, LTD and HTD were moderately negatively correlated (rs = -0.541, P < 0.001). Correlations between air pollutants and climate indicators were weak (absolute rs < 0.15). The CPRI was positively correlated with both EDD (rs = 0.621, P < 0.001) and ERD (rs = 0.488, P < 0.001).

Results from the time-updated Cox proportional hazards models are shown in Fig. 1. In the fully adjusted model, long-term exposure to PM1, PM2.5, PM1−2.5, and PM10 was significantly associated with an increased risk of incident arthritis. For each 1 µg/m³ increase in concentration, HRs were 1.061 (95% CI: 1.030–1.093) for PM1, 1.022 (95% CI: 1.011–1.033) for PM2.5, 1.023 (95% CI: 1.009–1.037) for PM1−2.5, and 1.004 (95% CI: 1.001–1.006) for PM10. The association for PM2.5−10 was not statistically significant (HR = 1.003, 95% CI: 1.000-1.007, P = 0.091).

Among climate-related exposures, each additional annual LTD was associated with an HR of 1.042 (95% CI: 1.019–1.065). No significant associations were observed for HTD, ERD, EDD, or CPRI in the fully adjusted model (all P > 0.05).

Dose–response relationships are displayed in Fig. 2, and the HRs at the 10th (P10) and 90th (P90) percentiles relative to the median are summarized in Table 2. Nonlinear associations (P-nonlinear < 0.05) were observed for PM1, PM2.5, PM2.5–10, LTD, ERD, EDD, and CPRI. For LTD, the HR increased markedly from 0.700 (95% CI: 0.627–0.781) at P10 to 2.084 (95% CI: 1.901–2.284) at P90. For PM2.5, the corresponding HRs were 0.966 (95% CI: 0.870–1.074) at P10 and 1.375 (95% CI: 1.216–1.555) at P90. PM2.5−10 showed an HR of 0.703 (95% CI: 0.613–0.805) at P10, increasing to 1.005 (95% CI: 0.890–1.134) at P90. The associations for PM1−2.5 and PM10 were approximately linear, and HTD showed no consistent association. The relationship with ERD was also nonlinear, showing a fluctuating pattern.

Exploratory threshold analyses are reported in Table 3. PM1, LTD, and CPRI all exhibited clear threshold effects. Below their respective thresholds, no statistically significant associations were observed; however, risk increased significantly above the thresholds, with PM1 showing a significant association above 40.862 µg/m³ (HR = 1.193, 95% CI: 1.131–1.259), LTD above 17.624 days (HR = 1.173, 95% CI: 1.147–1.199), and CPRI above 30.641 (HR = 1.137, 95% CI: 1.058–1.222). Stratified analyses and effect modification

The stratified analysis indicates that the association between PM and the risk of arthritis is significantly modified by various factors, with smoking status and geographical region being the most prominent effect modifiers (Figs. 4 and S3-S6). First, smoking status significantly modifies the effects of PM1, PM2.5, PM10, and PM1−2.5 (all P for heterogeneity < 0.05). Specifically, among non-smokers, exposure to these pollutants is significantly associated with an increased risk of arthritis (e.g., PM1: HR = 1.019, 95% CI: 1.008–1.031), whereas among smokers, the corresponding associations are significantly weakened, or disappear. Second, geographical region also extensively and significantly modifies the associations for all particulate matter and the CPRI (P for heterogeneity < 0.05). Overall, the risk estimates for exposure to various PM are generally higher among residents in Southern China compared to those in Northern China (e.g., PM1−2.5: Southern HR = 1.042, 95% CI: 1.026–1.059; Northern HR = 1.013, 95% CI: 0.999–1.026). However, the association between CPRI and arthritis risk is stronger in Northern China (Northern HR = 1.045, 95% CI: 1.025–1.065; Southern HR = 1.012, 95% CI: 0.997–1.027). Additionally, BMI, education level, alcohol consumption, and hypertension status also significantly modify the effects of specific pollutants, to varying degrees. Notably, the harmful effects of long-term LTD exposure are highly consistent and significant across most examined subgroups, with generally elevated risk estimates and minimal variation (e.g., male: HR = 1.030, 95% CI: 1.010–1.050; female: HR = 1.078, 95% CI: 1.060–1.095).

Interaction between PM and extreme weather events

Joint exposures to high LTD and high levels of all examined PM fractions were consistently associated with substantially elevated risks, with joint-high HRs ranging from 3.440 (95% CI: 2.633–4.496) for PM1 to 4.004 (95% CI: 2.973–5.391) for PM1−2.5. All additive interaction measures demonstrated statistically significant departure from additivity. The largest synergistic effect was observed for LTD combined with PM10 (RERI = 1.440, 95% CI: 0.747–2.132; AP = 0.388, 95% CI: 0.198–0.577; S = 2.129, 95% CI: 1.393–2.865), followed by PM2.5 (RERI = 1.130, 95% CI: 0.524–1.736; AP = 0.329, 95% CI: 0.151–0.508; S = 1.867, 95% CI: 1.208–2.526), PM1 (RERI = 0.999, 95% CI: 0.409–1.588; AP = 0.290, 95% CI: 0.118–0.463; S = 1.693, 95% CI: 1.148–2.237), and PM1−2.5 (RERI = 0.789, 95% CI: 0.109–1.469; AP = 0.197, 95% CI: 0.027–0.367; S = 1.356, 95% CI: 1.012–1.701). Together, these results indicate a robust synergistic interaction between LTD-defined extreme weather events and particulate matter (Table 4).

Sensitivity analyses

In the 1:3 propensity score–matched nested case–control analysis, covariate balance was satisfactory, with all standardized mean differences below 0.10 after matching. Effect estimates for key environmental exposures were consistent with those from the primary Cox models in terms of direction, magnitude, and statistical significance (Table S5). Sensitivity analyses addressing potential over adjustment bias from time-varying covariates yielded similar results. After excluding hypertension and diabetes, the association between LTD and outcome remained significant (HR = 1.041, 95% CI: 1.018–1.065), and further exclusion of BMI and physical activity did not materially alter the estimates (HR = 1.043, 95% CI: 1.020–1.066). Across all models, associations for PM fractions remained stable, with no meaningful changes in direction or strength (Table S6). These findings suggest that the observed associations are robust to alternative assumptions regarding the causal role of intermediate health indicators. Finally, additional adjustment for entry year to account for potential calendar-time trends did not materially change the results, which remained consistent with the primary analysis (Table S7).

Discussion

In this large, prospective study of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, we found that long-term exposure to most measured size fractions of PM (PM1, PM2.5, PM1−2.5, and PM10) and to specific extreme weather events, particularly LTD, were independently associated with an increased risk of incident arthritis. Although the primary analyses based on continuous exposures were designed to provide concise and readily comparable average risk estimates, the RCS analyses indicated that the exposure–response relationships for PM1, PM2.5, PM2.5−10, and LTD may be non‑linear. For these exposures, the continuous HRs are better interpreted as the overall average gradient rather than a constant linear risk change across all exposure levels. Notably, we identified synergistic effects on an additive scale between joint exposure to LTD and PM1, PM2.5, PM1−2.5, and PM10, indicating that their combined health impact exceeds the sum of their individual effects. Our results were robust across sensitivity analysis and highlight the modifying roles of smoking and geographic region.

Our findings regarding the positive associations between PM2.5, PM10, and arthritis risk align with previous epidemiological studies. Cross-sectional study results from the CHARLS reported that long-term exposure to PM1, PM2.5, and PM10 was associated with increased odds of prevalent arthritis [9]. A large Canadian cohort similarly found PM2.5 exposure to be associated with rheumatoid arthritis (RA) onset [21]. However, a UK Biobank study reported significant associations only for PM2.5 with RA risk, but not for PM2.5−10 or PM10 [22]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the studied disease spectrum (all arthritis types in our study vs. specific RA), regional variations in pollution composition and levels, and distinct genetic or lifestyle backgrounds of the populations. Our study expands this evidence by demonstrating significant risks associated with PM1 and PM1−2.5, size fractions that have been less studied in the context of arthritis.

The robust association we observed for LTD is supported by a body of literature linking cold exposure to arthritis exacerbation and risk. Short-term time-series studies have shown that lower temperatures are associated with increased emergency visits and hospitalizations for RA [23–25]. Furthermore, occupational studies, such as the Swedish EIRA case-control study, have linked working in cold environments to an elevated risk of developing RA [26]. While some studies suggest temperature associations may be specific to RA rather than osteoarthritis (OA) [27], our findings across a broad arthritis spectrum suggest that cold may be a shared environmental risk factor, possibly through systemic pathways affecting joint health. Differences from studies focused solely on OA may arise from methodological variations, including our focus on long-term incident risk rather than short-term symptom exacerbation.

The observed associations are biologically plausible through interconnected pathways of systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation [23]. For extreme cold, potential mechanisms include: (1) Induction of a systemic inflammatory response, evidenced by elevated C-reactive protein levels [12]. (2) Direct activation of transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1) channels, which are implicated in pain and inflammation pathways and may be sensitized in arthritic conditions [28, 29]. (3) Peripheral vasoconstriction, reducing synovial blood flow and impairing the clearance of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), thereby potentiating local joint inflammation [30].

For PM, multiple pathways have been proposed [31]: (1) Oxidative stress and inflammation: Inhaled particles induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, activating the NF-κB pathway and leading to the systemic release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) [31–33]. This chronic, low-grade systemic inflammation can predispose to or exacerbate joint disease [33]. (2) Immune dysregulation: Specific components of PM (e.g., polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons) may interact with immune receptors like the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, potentially promoting autoimmune responses [34–36]. (3) Mitochondrial dysfunction: PM-induced oxidative stress can damage mitochondria in joint cells (e.g., chondrocytes), leading to the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, amplifying IL-1β production and creating a pro-inflammatory feedback loop that drives cellular dysfunction and apoptosis [37, 38].

The synergistic interaction we observed between LTD and coarse PM (PM10, PM2.5−10) may arise from several mechanisms. Cold conditions can alter respiratory physiology and mucociliary clearance, potentially increasing the deposition and retention of larger inhaled particles in the airways [39]. Furthermore, the combined stress from physical (cold) and chemical (particulate) insults may overwhelm shared adaptive and anti-inflammatory pathways, leading to a supra-additive inflammatory cascade. This interaction highlights the importance of assessing co-exposures in environmental health. In contrast, PM2.5 can penetrate into the alveolar region and enter the systemic circulation, inducing the release of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and TNF-α. However, extreme cold can also trigger systemic physiological stress responses, including sympathetic nervous system activation, peripheral vasoconstriction, and increased circulating inflammatory markers [40, 41]. The systemic inflammatory pathways activated by PM2.5 and cold overlap substantially, leaving limited residual biological capacity and thus hindering a strong synergistic effect. PM2.5 may act primarily as a non-specific systemic inflammatory burden, rather than as a pathway-specific trigger that strongly interacts with cold exposure, resulting in relatively weak synergy. In contrast, the effects of coarse particles are mediated mainly through airway-localized and neuro-immune pathways, which are more distinct from cold-induced systemic stress responses, thereby providing greater pathway complementarity and enabling a more pronounced interaction.

Stratified analyses revealed heterogeneity in the associations between various air pollutants and the risk of arthritis across different populations. The attenuation or even reversal of the association between particulate matter exposure and arthritis risk among smokers is one of the most suggestive findings of this report. This contrasts with most previous studies suggesting that smoking exacerbates the health effects of air pollution [42]. Several speculations may account for this phenomenon. Chronic smoking can induce sustained systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation [43], which may drive inflammatory pathways toward saturation, thereby creating a “threshold effect”. Under this scenario, smoking-induced inflammation becomes so dominant that the incremental inflammatory burden imposed by ambient particulate matter is relatively trivial, causing arthritis risk among smokers to reach a plateau earlier. Notably, acute smoking studies have shown that cigarette smoke exerts not only pro-inflammatory but also transient suppressive effects on certain inflammatory parameters [44], suggesting a complex, biphasic immunomodulatory response. Although this phenomenon does not directly prove that chronic smoking saturates the inflammatory response to environmental exposures, it provides biological plausibility for the hypothesis that the immune system of smokers no longer mounts a typical incremental response to subsequent particulate matter exposure. From an epidemiological perspective, this finding may also reflect a “depletion of susceptibles” within the smoking population. Since smoking is a voluntary behavior, individuals sensitive to tobacco smoke may never initiate smoking or may quit early due to discomfort, resulting in a current smoker population that is systematically enriched with individuals relatively resistant to inflammatory stimuli [45]. Furthermore, those most susceptible to inflammatory diseases may have already developed arthritis or experienced competing health events such as cardiovascular disease or death earlier in life, rendering high-risk subgroups underrepresented in the observed cohort. In addition, residual confounding, exposure misclassification, and survival bias cannot be fully excluded. A potential interaction between smoking and age may also play an important role in this pattern. Smokers tend to have a higher baseline inflammatory burden and undergo accelerated biological aging [46], which may mask or modify the detectable effect of ambient particulate matter exposure. This masking effect may be particularly pronounced in older age groups, where arthritis risk is already substantially elevated due to multiple coexisting risk factors. Concurrently, we observed significant North-South differences. Residents in southern China exhibited higher risks associated with particulate matter of all size fractions. This regional pattern may be attributable to the distinct chemical composition of PM in the South, where the pollution profile is dominated by industrial and traffic emissions and the warm, humid climate facilitates secondary particle formation. High relative humidity and strong solar radiation promote the atmospheric oxidation of gaseous precursors (SO2, NOₓ, and volatile organic compounds), leading to the abundant formation of secondary inorganic and organic aerosols that are predominantly enriched in fine particulate matter [47]. Owing to their high-water solubility and strong redox activity, these secondary components may induce more pronounced systemic oxidative stress and inflammatory responses on a per-mass basis, thereby plausibly contributing to the elevated arthritis risk observed among southern residents. In contrast, the association between the composite CPRI and incident arthritis was more pronounced in northern China. This divergence likely reflects region-specific climatic conditions characterized by more frequent and severe extreme weather events, particularly during the prolonged winter season. In northern China, extreme low-temperature days often coincide with the heating period, when emissions from coal and biomass combustion are relatively high, leading to increased ambient concentrations of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and metal-containing particles [48]. Although the CPRI itself is a meteorological index based on the frequency of multiple extreme weather events, these climatic extremes may co-occur with periods of intensified anthropogenic emissions in northern regions. Such co-occurrence may contribute to a combined environmental stress context that could exacerbate systemic inflammation and immune dysregulation, thereby potentially increasing susceptibility to arthritis. Furthermore, factors such as obesity and female sex were associated with higher risk estimates for particulate matter, which aligns with known biological contexts [49, 50]. It is noteworthy that in stark contrast to the heterogeneity described above, the harmful effect of long-term LTD exposure was highly consistent across all subgroups. This strongly suggests that LTD, as a cumulative environmental stressor, may induce health damage through more fundamental and universal pathways, sufficient to override susceptibility differences arising from individual characteristics. Consequently, this highlights its pervasive threat to public health and identifies it as a priority target for public health intervention.

These findings have direct public health implications. They argue for integrated, multi-level prevention strategies. At the policy level, environmental and meteorological agencies should develop coordinated “environment-health” monitoring and early warning systems, with regionally tailored interventions (e.g., targeting fine particles in the south and composite climate risks in the north). At the clinical and community level, long-term environmental exposure history should be considered in arthritis risk assessment, particularly for high-risk groups such as obese individuals and women. Public health messaging should clearly distinguish the long-term nature of these environmental risks from short-term weather-related symptom fluctuations to avoid misinterpretation.

This study has several strengths, including the use of two large, nationally representative prospective cohorts, which enhances generalizability within the Chinese context. We employed high-spatiotemporal-resolution exposure data for multiple particulate size fractions and diverse extreme weather indices. Analytically, the use of time-updated Cox models, WQS, and formal tests for additive interaction strengthens causal inference regarding long-term and combined exposures.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although high-resolution exposure data were used, assessment was performed at the community level and could not capture individual-level variations in indoor exposure, mobility, or occupational settings. This likely introduced non-differential misclassification and attenuated the effect estimates toward the null. Second, data on gaseous pollutants such as NO2 and ozone were not available for the full study period, limiting the characterization of the full air pollution mixture; future studies incorporating these pollutants are warranted. Third, the primary outcome was self-reported physician-diagnosed “arthritis or rheumatism,” precluding both clinical/imaging validation and reliable differentiation between OA and RA. Because OA and RA differ in etiological pathways, environmental drivers may differ across subtypes, and we cannot determine whether the observed associations are driven predominantly by one subtype or represent a shared environmental vulnerability. Any non-differential misclassification of the outcome would most likely bias effect estimates toward the null, so the direction of associations reported here remains informative. Future studies incorporating clinical diagnoses, serological or imaging data, or medication proxies (e.g., disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) are needed to disentangle subtype-specific environmental risks. Fourth, both cohorts experienced attrition, and detailed retention rates were not available. The requirement that participants complete at least three consecutive follow-up surveys may have introduced selection bias as well as survival bias. As shown in Table S2, compared with included participants, those excluded for insufficient follow-up were older and had higher baseline prevalence of hypertension and diabetes. This confirms that the three-consecutive-follow-up criterion selectively removed individuals who were more vulnerable at study entry. Because this subgroup would be expected to exhibit stronger exposure–arthritis associations, their exclusion likely biased the observed hazard ratios toward the null. Consequently, our effect estimates should be interpreted as conservative, and the true population-level risks may be larger than those observed. Fifth, the study population consisted of Chinese middle-aged and older adults; the generalizability of findings to other ethnicities, age groups, or populations operating under different environmental policies requires further investigation. Sixth, unmeasured confounders (e.g., occupation, diet, genetics, healthcare access) and residual confounding from imperfectly measured covariates may still partly account for the observed associations. Finally, the WQS approach assumes linear additive effects of the quantile‑transformed exposures on the log-hazard; future studies should apply more flexible methods such as Bayesian kernel machine regression to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

This study provides novel evidence that long-term exposure to ambient PM and extreme low-temperature weather are independent and synergistic risk factors for incident arthritis in a middle-aged and elderly Chinese population. These findings underscore the importance of adopting a composite environmental exposure perspective in understanding the etiology of arthritis. They highlight the need for integrated public health strategies that address both air quality and climate resilience to mitigate the growing burden of arthritis in aging populations.
