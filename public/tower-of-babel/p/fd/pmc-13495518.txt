The potential of TRPC channel-mediated autophagy in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury
Authors: Han Han, Yanru Wang, Shoutian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI) is a common complication in the treatment of acute myocardial infarction, which is often accompanied by high disability rate and mortality.Autophagy is a highly conserved cellular process widely observed in eukaryotic cells, serving as a critical mechanism for the removal of damaged, dysfunctional, or aged intracellular components. As a vital self-protective pathway, autophagy plays an essential role in maintaining cellular homeostasis. Accumulating evidence indicates that autophagy is implicated in calcium overload during MI/RI. The transient receptor potential canonical (TRPC) channels, which function as Ca²⁺-permeable non-selective cation channels, are known to participate in various Ca²⁺-related pathological processes.MethodsA comprehensive literature review was conducted using established scientific databases, including ScienceDirect, PubMed, and Google Scholar. This article focuses on the pathogenesis of MI/RI, the TRPC channel family, and their interrelationships. Relevant research findings were systematically summarized, and potential directions for future investigation were discussed.Main bodyThis review examines the key pathophysiological mechanisms underlying myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury, including inflammatory responses, calcium overload, and oxidative stress. Particular emphasis is placed on calcium overload, with a detailed exploration of its molecular mechanisms. Furthermore, autophagy and the associated signaling pathways—specifically the CaMKKβ/AMPK/mTOR axis—are discussed to elucidate the interplay between autophagy and calcium regulation, as well as the protective effects of autophagy in the context of MI/RI. Additionally, the structure, function, and current research progress on the TRPC channel family in relation to MI/RI are reviewed, providing insights into potential future research avenues.ConclusionCurrently, the effective treatment of myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury remains a significant clinical challenge. Evidence suggests that downregulation of TRPC channel expression may mitigate Ca²⁺ overload and thereby reduce cellular damage. However, whether this protective effect is mediated through autophagy requires further experimental validation and mechanistic investigation.

Introduction

Myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury (MI/RI), as a common complication during the treatment of acute myocardial infarction, is often accompanied by high morbidity and mortality, so how to effectively avoid MI/RI has become an important clinical issue [1]. However, as the pathophysiological mechanisms of MI/RI are complex and associated with calcium overload, oxidative stress (OS), neutrophil infiltration, and other factors, the mechanisms have not been fully elucidated [2–6]. Autophagy, as one of the important processes for maintaining cellular homeostasis and ensuring cell survival [7], can play a protective role in the myocardium [8]. Studies have shown that Ca2+ influx into the activated CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway can regulate autophagy [9]. The TRPC channels, as calcium ion-permeable non-selective cation channels, has frequently been involved in research on calcium ion-related diseases since their discovery, especially in ischemic cardiomyopathy. This article reviews the role of the CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway and its intermediate pathophysiological mechanisms in MI/RI, as well as the role of TRPC channels in MI/RI, and provides a theoretical reference for the study of the MI/RI mechanism.

Pathophysiological mechanisms of MI/RI

MI/RI was first proposed by Hearse in 1977, and it refers to the phenomenon that the myocardial blood supply is interrupted due to embolic vascular occlusion, vascular stenosis and other causes, which leads to damage to the myocardium due to ischemia and hypoxia after a period of time, and the damage to the myocardial tissue is further aggravated after the restoration of blood supply, which in turn results in irreversible myocardial injury [10, 11].

MI/RI is a complex pathological process involving multiple factors, and its mechanisms are being continuously explored, improved and deepened. In recent years, it has been found that the pathogenesis of MI/RI is mainly associated with inflammatory response, calcium overload, oxidative stress, impaired energy metabolism and cellular autophagy [12, 13].

Oxidative stress Reactive oxygen species (ROS) is a term referring to the oxygen metabolism associated with Oxygen-containing reactive substances (OCRS), including superoxide, hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radicals, and singlet oxygen species [14]. When ischemia-reperfusion occurs, the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane is inhibited, which induces ROS production. When excessive ROS production exceeds the body’s scavenging capacity, OS occurs, resulting in an imbalance between the oxidative and antioxidant systems, and further leading to cellular and tissue damage [15]. Oxidative stress induces vascular endothelial dysfunction, cardiomyocyte apoptosis and myocardial damage, which ultimately leads to cardiac dysfunction [15].

Intracellular calcium overload Ca2+ acts as an important messenger for maintaining cellular physiological functions when ischaemia/reperfusion.When perfusion injury occurs, calcium overload is triggered by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which leads to the disruption of intracellular calcium homeostasis [16]. Therefore, calcium overload is regarded as a key process in the development of MI/RI. The permeability of myocardial cell membranes to calcium ions is significantly increased during ischaemia/reperfusion, and the sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium pump is impaired, resulting in the failure of intracellular calcium ions to return to the calcium reservoir. During myocardial ischemia and hypoxia, cellular acidosis caused by abnormal cellular metabolism leads to an increase in Na+/H+ exchange, which in turn causes an increase in intracellular Na+ concentration. During reperfusion, in order to restore extracellular pH, excessive intracellular Na + is extruded through Na+/H+ exchange, which triggers reverse Na+/Ca2+ exchange and ultimately leads to calcium overload [17].

The inflammatory response is an important defense mechanism of the body, and excessive inflammatory response can also induce MI/RI. The early stage of MI/RI is characterized by inflammation, and a large number of inflammatory factors are produced during the injury process. In the injured myocardial endothelial tissue, a large number of neutrophils adhered to the myocardial vascular endothelium. However, persistent inflammation increases the demand for endoplasmic reticulum protein folding, which results in an maladaptive unfolded protein response, that leads to endoplasmic reticulum stress and further cardiomyocyte damage. This ultimately leads to oxidative stress, metabolic dysfunction and degenerative necrosis of cardiomyocytes [18, 19].

Cellular autophagy Autophagy is a process in which autophagosomes are formed by engulfing cytoplasmic components to maintain the normal structure and function of the heart. In MI/RI, autophagy alleviates cardiac damage during ischaemia-reperfusion by clearing damaged organelles and misfolded proteins; however, excessive activation of autophagy may accelerate the progression of cellular damage and ultimately lead to cell death.

As the energy centers of cells, mitochondria not only participate in energy production, but also play an important role in the regulation of vascular endothelial cell function [15]. In the development of ischemic cardiomyopathy, the damage of microvascular endothelial cells: microvascular endothelial cells further aggravatesthe injury of myocardial cells in ischemic tissue, and this endothelial damage is closely associated with mitochondrial dysfunction [20, 21]. Mitophagy, as a special form of autophagy, can remove dysfunctional mitochondria and plays a crucial role in regulating endothelial injury and metabolic dysfunction [22]. Studies have shown that the MI/RI process lead to mitophagic dysfunction, which impairs mitochondrial homeostasis and mitochondrial biosynthesis, ultimately resulting in myocardial cell death [23].

Mechanisms of injury by calcium overload in MI/RI

Calcium overload is one of the most critical interrelated mechanisms contributing to MI/RI [24]. Calcium overload mainly occurs during the reperfusion phase, and the underlying mechanisms of injury are complex andwhich are summarized as follows.

Calcium overload induced by increased reactive oxygen species Under normal physiological conditions, intracellular ROS interact with antioxidants to maintain a dynamic balance [25]. However, excessive ROS are also generated during I/R [26–30]. When myocardial ischaemia occurs, myocardial cell metabolism is impaired due to hypoxia and insufficient nutrient supply [31]. During myocardial ischaemia, Na+/H+ exchange channels are activated due to decreased intracellular pH caused by increased glycolysis, leading to an increase in Na+ influx. Meanwhile, Na+-K+-ATPase and Na+/Ca2 + exchange channels on the cell membrane are also activated. At the same time, ATP depletion results in decreased Na+-K+-ATPase activity. Consequently, to eliminate excess intracellular Na+, the Na+ /Ca2+ exchange pathway remains persistently open, ultimately leading to increased Ca2+ influx [21]. Reduced oxygen supply is associated with acidosis, energy depletion and disrupted ionic homeostasis, which leads to cardiac dysfunction and ultimately cell death [32].

Mitochondrial Dysfunction ATP is an essential substance for the survival of organisms and is involved in almost all physiological processes of the body. Mitochondria are the primary organelles responsible for ATP production, and Ca2+ is produced mainly by oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondria and Ca2+ is a key regulator of this process [25, 26]. When myocardial I/R occurs, an increase in cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration leads to enhanced mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake. After Ca2+ enters the mitochondria, a large amount of Ca2+ is converted into calcium phosphate and deposited, which ultimately results in mitochondrial structural and functional impairment and reduced energy production [27–30], which further leads to impairs the sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium pump function and reduces sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ uptake. Meanwhile, mitochondrial charge redistribution triggers the opening of the mitochondrial membrane permeability transition pore (mPTP). mPTP opening causes mitochondrial membrane depolarization, mitochondrial swelling and cytochrome C release, and ultimately resulting in irreversible cardiomyocyte damage [32].

Activation of calcium-dependent degradative enzymes Arachidonic acid (AA) is an essential fatty acid in humans and is crucial for maintaining the structural and functional integrity of cellular membranes in the body [33]. Calcium activates phospholipases and calcium-sensitive proteins that regulate AA metabolism. At the onset of ischaemia-reperfusion, elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentrations activate phospholipase A2 and protein kinase C, which promote AA generation. This not only damages the cellular membrane but also induces further Ca2 + influx. In addition, AA acts as a substrate for cyclooxygenase, and the prostaglandins and thromboxanes generated by its activation are further involved in ROS production. Phospholipase C hydrolyses phosphatidylinositol into inositol triphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol (DAG), which exacerbates calcium overload through Ca2+ and Na+ /H+ exchange, creating a vicious cycle [34].

Autophagy in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury

Autophagy and the CaMKKβ/AMPK/mTOR signalling pathway

Autophagy, first proposed by Christian de Duve in 1963, is widely found in eukaryotic cells and refers to the process by which a cell removes excess, damaged or aged organelles from its interior, which is a self-protective mechanism that plays an important role in maintaining cellular homeostasis [35]. In the current study, autophagy is usually classified into three types: macroautophagy, microautophagy and molecular chaperone-mediated autophagy. Compared with the other two types of autophagy, macroautophagy is considered to be the main type of autophagy, so we refer to macroautophagy simply as “autophagy” (Autophagy: Renovation of Cells and Tissues). More than 30 autophagy-associated genes (ATGs) have been identified as key regulators of this complex process. Among these genes, Beclin 1 is very important [36, 37], which interacts with a number of other proteins such as Vps34, Vps15, Bif1, UVRAG, and Ambra1 to produce phosphatidylinositol-3-phosphate (PI3P), which promotes the recruitment of other Atg proteins to catalyse vesicle elongation and phagophore nucleation. Thus, deficiency of Beclin 1 results in impaired autophagic activity. Recent studies have shown that autophagy is inhibited when Bcl-2 binds to BH3 of Beclin 1 and enhanced when Bcl-2 dissociates from BH3 of Beclin 1. The interaction between Beclin 1 and Bcl-2 proteins can be dynamically regulated by various mechanisms thereby modulating the level of autophagy, and the inhibitory effect of Bcl-2 on Beclin 1-mediated autophagy is primarily attributed to the localization of Bcl-2 on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [38].

Adenylate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a serine-threonine kinase that is critical for biological energy metabolism, coordinating energy-producing glucose uptake and glycolysis while down-regulating energy-consuming synthesis of proteins and lipids. Ca2+/Calmodulin-dependent protein kinase kinase kinase (CaMMKβ) is a widely distributed and multifunctional serine/threonine protein kinase involved in the regulation of a broad range of physiological and pathophysiological processes, including energy homeostasis, obesity, glucose metabolism, hematopoiesis, and various cardiac physiological processes. In the study of angiotensin II-induced autophagy and apoptosis in podocytes, it was found that autophagic induction is dependent on elevated cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels, which activate CaMKKβ; this in turn triggers AMPK activation, and AMPK then directly phosphorylates mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) to suppress its activity (Fig. 1), thereby enhancing autophagy [39, 40].

MI/RI and CaMKKβ/AMPK/mTOR-mediated autophagy

Ischemic cardiomyopathy is associated with high morbidity and mortality, and acute myocardial infarction constitutes a major cause of death worldwide. The development of reperfusion strategies such as thrombolysis and coronary artery bypass grafting has enabled the recanalization of occluded coronary vessels, thereby restoring blood flow to ischemic myocardial tissue and yielding certain therapeutic benefits. However, the I/R process still induces damage and even necrosis of cardiomyocytes, which may ultimately lead to heart failure. Therefore, MI/RI remains a major bottleneck limiting the optimal therapeutic efficacy of these reperfusion strategies. In-depth investigations have revealed that autophagy exerts a critical role in MIRI, and its cardioprotective effects during this process have become increasingly evident [42]. MIRI onset is accompanied by an energy crisis and oxidative stress activation, both of which are closely linked to the autophagic process However, accumulating evidence indicates that autophagy exerts dual roles in the ischemia-reperfusion pathophysiological process: it exerts a cardioprotective effect during the ischemic phase, yet excessive autophagy is equally detrimental to myocardial tissue [43].

Excessive autophagy or exacerbated stress and inflammatory responses can shift autophagy from a protective mechanism to a mediator of autophagic cell death or apoptosis, resulting in irreversible myocardial damage [44]. Myocardial ischemia is essentially a state of nutritional deprivation caused by inadequate myocardial blood perfusion, which is a potent inducer of autophagy [45]. In addition, the reperfusion phase further promotes autophagosome biogenesis. During the cardiac reperfusion phase, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inflammatory responses can trigger excessive autophagic activation, which results in the excessive degradation of essential cellular proteins and organelles and ultimately induces irreversible cardiomyocyte death [46]. Beclin-1 is a key gene regulating autophagy, and its expression level partially reflects autophagic activity. Microtubule-associated protein 1 LC3, a signature protein of autophagy, exists in two isoforms [47]. During autophagy, LC3-I is cleaved to generate LC3-II, which is critical for autophagosome formation. Thus, the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio is a reliable indicator of the extent of autophagic activation under stress conditions [48]. One study demonstrated that the protein levels of Beclin-1, p62, and LC3-II, as well as the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, were significantly elevated after reperfusion, indicating sustained autophagic activation. Subsequent experiments from the same study also revealed that Ca2+ can inhibit autophagic activity [49].

Autophagosome formation involves multiple autophagy-associated genes (ATGs), including those mediating the conjugation of ATG12 and the lipidation of LC3 [50]. ATG12 is activated by ATG7, transferred to ATG10, and ultimately conjugated to ATG5 to form the ATG12-ATG5 conjugate. LC3-I is generated in the cytoplasm following the processing of LC3 precursors. LC3-I is also delivered by ATG3 to ATG7 for lipidation, ultimately generating LC3-II [51, 52]. LC3-II serves as a specific marker for autophagy, and its cellular level is indicative of the autophagic status of cells [53]. In this study, Ca2+ was found to inhibit autophagy in MIRI by suppressing ATG12 expression and downregulating the gene expression of LC3 and Beclin-1. Ca2+ attenuates mitochondrial peroxidation and prevents excessive autophagy, thereby alleviating cardiomyocyte injury and enhancing myocardial resistance to MIRI. This may represent one of the underlying mechanisms responsible for the cardioprotective effects of Ca2+ .

Autophagy is often regulated by a variety of signalling pathways including Beclin-1/Class III phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K), AMPK/mTOR and PI3K/Akt/mTOR signalling pathways [54, 55]. CaMKKβ is involved in the regulation of various physiological processes in the heart, and when cardiomyocytes are damaged due to increased Ca2+ levels, CaMMKβ is continuously overexpressed and activated. CaMMKβ is one of the important mechanisms that promote myocardial hypertrophy, heart failure and arrhythmia [39]. And studies have shown that CaMKKβ-AMPK-mTOR pathway is closely related to autophagy [40, 41]. When myocardial ischemia occurs, calcium inflow increases, activating CaMKKβ, which then directly phosphorylates AMPK at Thr 172. At the same time, myocardial ischemia and reperfusion lead to a large amount of cellular ROS production, and the accumulation of reactive oxygen species induced by tertiary-butylhydroperoxide (t-BHP) leads to mitochondrial oxidative stress and dysfunction, thus leading to an energy crisis [56–59], where myocardium becomes “starved” due to the lack of energy. AMPK is a key energy sensor for the maintenance of metabolic homeostasis. AMPK is activated and autophagy is initiated via the AMPK-mTOR signalling pathway, AMPK activation may induce mTOR inactivation and phosphorylation of Ser317 and Ser777, thus leading to Ulk1 activation and further promoting autophagy [60]. Phosphorylation of Ulk1 at Ser757 inhibits Ulk1 activation and thus suppresses autophagy [61]. When cells were treated with t-BHP, p-AMPK levels were elevated, while total AMPK protein expression was downregulated. After treating cells with t-BHP and danshenin/tetramethylpyrazine derivatives, the phosphorylation level of p-AMPK was reduced, whereas the total AMPK expression level was increased; the levels of p-mTOR and p-Ulk1 (Ser757) were also decreased in t-BHP-treated cells. Rapamycin treatment attenuated the cardioprotective effect of tanshin/tetramethylpyrazine derivatives against t-BHP-induced cardiac cytotoxicity. Tanshin/tetramethylpyrazine derivatives stimulate mTOR activity, thereby inhibiting the initiation of autophagy. This also indirectly confirmed that AMPK can enhance autophagy by directly phosphorylating the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) to inhibit its activity.

TRPC channels in myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury

TRPC channel family

Since the discovery of the transient receptor potential (TRP) ion channel in 1969 [62], numerous TRP homologs have been identified. To date, 28 members of this family have been identified in mammals and classified into six subfamilies: TRPC, TRPV, TRPM, TRPA, TRPP, and TRPML [63]. Most TRP channels function as calcium-permeable non-selective cation channels, with the canonical transient receptor potential (TRPC) channel family representing a typical subgroup. Since the first report of TRPC1 in 1995, seven members of the TRPC family (TRPC1–TRPC7) have been characterized [64–72]. Based on their activation mechanisms, TRPC channels are divided into three subcategories: TRPC1/4/5, which are activated by Gαq-coupled receptors and receptor tyrosine kinases; TRPC3/6/7, which are directly activated by diacylglycerol (DAG); and TRPC2, which is a pseudogene in humans [73, 74]. Experimental studies have confirmed the expression of various TRPC subtypes in cardiac tissue. Although these channels are expressed at relatively low levels under normal physiological conditions, their expression is significantly upregulated under pathological conditions. In such conditions, TRPC channels modulate intracellular calcium signaling by mediating store-operated calcium entry (SOCE) and receptor-operated calcium entry (ROCE) [75]. Notably, most research has focused on the SOCE pathway [76–80]. By using agents that selectively deplete sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ stores—such as cyclopiazonic acid, thapsigargin, and glucosidase inhibitors—researchers have effectively isolated the SOCE pathway from ROCE interference and suppressed TRPC channel activity via multiple approaches. These investigations have demonstrated that TRPC channels exert a significant influence on SOCE in cardiomyocytes; however, the precise regulatory mechanisms underlying this interaction remain to be further elucidated.

The role of the TRPC channels in MI/RI

Research has demonstrated that TRPC channels exacerbate cardiomyocyte apoptosis after myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI) by enhancing calcium ion influx [81, 82]. Experimental studies using the inhibitor SKF96365 and TRPC gene-deficient mouse models have shown significant improvements in cardiac function after MIRI, indicating a clear association between TRPC-mediated calcium influx regulation and cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Recent evidence further suggests that the TRPC6 subtype may attenuate programmed myocardial necrosis during MIRI by modulating calcium ion influx [83].Specifically, inhibition of TRPC6 expression reduces calcium influx, thereby suppressing Ca²⁺/CaMKII-mediated programmed necrosis and ultimately mitigating myocardial injury. Moreover, multiple studies have confirmed that TRPC channels are involved in the pathogenesis of MIRI [84–85], with their inhibition reducing cardiac dysfunction. Nevertheless, these studies have largely overlooked the potential interaction between autophagy and TRPC channels.

Results and discussion

Autophagy, as a physiological process that maintains cellular homeostasis, is regulated by multiple factors, but its regulatory mechanisms have not been fully elucidated. The study of autophagy has provided new insights for the treatment of various diseases. MI/RI, a common complication of acute myocardial infarction, currently lacks an effective therapeutic strategy, and the dual role of autophagy in MIRI has become a point of contention and even a research focus. On the one hand, autophagy can help cardiomyocytes maintain homeostasis and alleviate myocardial injury caused by ischemia; on the other hand, when blood supply is restored to ischemic myocardium, excessive autophagy not only fails to protect the myocardium but also exacerbates injury, leading to secondary myocardial damage. Maintaining the balance of autophagic activity in the myocardium and enabling autophagy to act as a protective mechanism rather than a destructive one is a key research direction. TRPC channels, which function as calcium-permeable non-selective cation channels, have attracted considerable scholarly attention regarding their potential involvement in calcium ion homeostasis imbalance. Particularly in ischemic cardiomyopathy, evidence indicates that downregulation of TRPC channel expression can mitigate myocardial injury. However, whether this protective effect is associated with autophagy remains unclear.

Based on the current research progress, future research should focus on the following core directions. First, it is necessary to clarify the interaction mechanisms between autophagy and TRPC channels at different stages of MIRI, and to determine whether TRPC channels can precisely regulate autophagy by directly modulating autophagy-related signaling pathways or indirectly via calcium-dependent autophagic regulators. Second, it is essential to verify the feasibility of autophagy regulation by targeting TRPC channels, and to clarify whether different interventions can modulate autophagy to maintain a moderate level of autophagic activity during ischemia-reperfusion and prevent excessive autophagy, thereby alleviating MIRI. Finally, it is necessary to further explore the key molecular targets for the regulation of autophagic balance and elucidate the core signaling network that regulates the initiation, progression and termination of autophagy in cardiomyocytes, so as to provide theoretical support for the development of more specific therapeutic strategies.

Although some progress has been made in the study of autophagy and TRPC channels, many scientific questions remain to be addressed. The precise regulatory mechanisms underlying autophagic balance in cardiomyocytes are not fully understood, and there is a lack of effective molecular markers for defining the critical threshold between moderate and excessive autophagy. TRPC channels have multiple subtypes, and the expression and functions of different subtypes differ during MIRI. Whether the regulation of autophagy by TRPC channels is subtype-specific remains unclear. Most existing studies are based on in vitro cell or in vivo animal models. The correlation between TRPC channel activity and autophagic activity in human myocardial tissue, as well as its clinical significance, has not been confirmed, which limits the clinical translation of these research findings.

In summary, clarifying the interaction mechanisms between autophagy and TRPC channels in MIRI can not only further elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms of MIRI but also provide a new perspective for understanding the intrinsic relationship between calcium homeostasis, autophagic balance and myocardial injury. It also offers new intervention targets and therapeutic strategies for the clinical management of MIRI, which is expected to break through the current clinical dilemma in the prevention and treatment of MIRI. Meanwhile, this research direction also provides a reference for other cardiovascular diseases involving calcium homeostasis disorders and autophagic dysfunction, such as heart failure and arrhythmia, which has important theoretical value and clinical significance. Future studies should adopt multidisciplinary approaches to promote the clinical translation and application of autophagy modulation in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases.
