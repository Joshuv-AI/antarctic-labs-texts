The pharmacy graduate global competence evaluation scale (PGGCES): a nationwide study in China
Authors: Jia-Kai Li, Yi-Chang Zhao, Shi-Qi Wu, Yu-Kun Zhang, Jia-Yi Liu, Rao Fu, Bi-Kui Zhang, Miao Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch on global competence (GC) among postgraduate pharmacy students remains limited, with most studies focusing on undergraduates or general student populations. To address this gap, this study investigates the GC of Chinese pharmacy graduate students using the OECD’s 2018 PISA framework and established GC assessment tools. By analyzing personal attributes, family background, and academic environment, this study identifies key influencing factors and evaluates the effectiveness of current educational practices. The findings provide evidence-based recommendations to enhance GC education and support the development of globally competent pharmacy professionals.MethodsThis cross-sectional study, conducted in August–September 2024, surveyed 568 pharmacy graduate students across China to assess GC levels. The questionnaire, developed based on existing frameworks, included demographic information, internationalization experiences, and self-assessment of GC. Statistical analyses, including descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression, were performed using R to explore factors influencing GC.ResultsThe average GC score was 3.36, with " International Attitude " scoring highest (3.95) and " Cross-cultural Communication " lowest (2.87). Exploratory factor analysis supported a three-factor structure, explaining 48.59% of the variance (KMO = 0.852). Regression models indicated that gender, degree nature, and first-generation university student status significantly influenced GC. Local internationalization experiences had a stronger impact than cross-border activities, with publishing SCI papers emerging as a key factor. Multicollinearity tests confirmed model validity.ConclusionTo improve GC, universities should prioritize enhancing students’ global competence through reading foreign language materials and interacting with international scholars in daily teaching. Additionally, under resource-limited conditions, expanding local internationalization opportunities may be an effective strategy for fostering GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07859-4.Keywords: Global competence, Graduate education, Medical education, Internationalization cultivation, Pharmacy education

Introduction

In today’s interconnected world, global competence (GC) has emerged as a critical educational goal. International organizations, NGOs, and governments have incorporated GC into policies and frameworks to prepare learners for globalization [1, 2]. For example, Oxfam’s 2006 curriculum for global citizenship outlined essential knowledge, skills, and values young people need to engage with global issues [1]. UNESCO has likewise emphasized global citizenship education (GCED) as a means to help learners understand the world’s complexities and work together on global challenges [2]. These early frameworks set the stage for a clear definition of GC and spurred extensive research into GC education and assessment across disciplines​ [3].

Over time, scholars have refined the concept of global competence. Hunter et al. offered a widely cited definition of global competence as “having an open mind while actively seeking to understand cultural norms and expectations of others, and leveraging this gained knowledge to interact, communicate, and work effectively outside one’s environment”​ [4]. This definition highlights that beyond knowledge and skills, one’s attitude—being open-minded and respectful of cultural differences—is fundamental to GC [5–7]. In 2016, the OECD further elaborated the GC construct in preparation for its PISA assessment. PISA (Programme for International Student Assessment) is a triennial international survey conducted by the OECD to evaluate education systems by measuring 15-year-old students’ skills and knowledge. The OECD defines global competence as the capacity to (1) examine local, global and intercultural issues, (2) understand and appreciate the perspectives and world views of others, (3) engage in open, appropriate and effective interactions with people from different cultures, and (4) take action for collective well-being and sustainable development [8]. These three dimensions (often summarized as knowledge & understanding, skills, and attitudes & values) form the core of the OECD’s GC framework. Each of these conceptual developments has contributed to a sharper understanding of GC and has led to a growing body of literature on how to teach, assess, and develop global competence in various contexts [3].

Despite the global emphasis on GC, research on its development among postgraduate pharmacy students remains limited [9–14]. Most studies focus on undergraduates or general student populations, with little attention to pharmacy education at the graduate level [15, 16]. To address this gap, our study developed the Postgraduate Global Competence in Pharmacy Education Scale (PGGCES)—the first assessment tool adapted from the OECD PISA 2018 framework and existing global competence instruments and applied it to evaluate global competence among pharmacy graduate students in China [17, 18]. We assess the impact of personal attributes, family background, and academic environment on GC. The study also evaluates the effectiveness of current educational practices in fostering GC, identifying key influencing factors.

The findings will bridge a critical knowledge gap, providing evidence-based recommendations for curriculum and policy improvements. By enhancing GC education, this study aims to help higher education institutions cultivate internationally competent pharmacy professionals capable of contributing to global healthcare and sustainable development.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional, data-based study conducted from August to September 2024. A survey questionnaire (see Supplementary Material S1) was distributed to pharmacy graduate students currently enrolled across China. The survey aimed to assess the level of GC among pharmacy graduate students in China and identify the factors influencing GC development.

Study participants

A total of 1,200 surveys were distributed via institutional email and academic networks using the “WJX” platform. The target participants were currently enrolled graduate pharmacy students, including both doctoral and master’s candidates, who were studying in fields such as clinical pharmacy, pharmacology, pharmaceutics, pharmaceutical analysis, medicinal chemistry, and other pharmacy-related disciplines.

In China, graduate education consists of two types of degrees: “professional degrees” (Master’s and PhD programs with a focus on practical training and industry experience, typically known as “Master of Pharmacy” or “Doctor of Pharmacy”) and “academic degrees” (Master’s and PhD programs focused on research). The respondents were from both academic and professional tracks. Typically, Master’s programs in pharmacy span 3 years, while Ph.D. programs last 3 to 5 years. Accordingly, “Year of Study” refers to the student’s progression within their current program.The survey employed anonymous responses, and incomplete or inconsistent submissions were excluded from the study. The respondents represented 32 provincial-level administrative regions across China. Prior to participation, all respondents provided written informed consent. A Chinese-language version of the questionnaire was used. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (Approval No.: LYEC2024-0220). No personally identifiable information was collected or accessible to the authors in this study. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire was developed based on the OECD Global Competence Framework and adapted from scales developed by Cen et al., Liu et al., Hu et al. and Jiang et al. [11, 19–21]. These scales were selected for their relevance in measuring global competence in higher education contexts.

A pilot study was conducted with 50 students from the target population, selected using a convenience sampling method across five different universities. The original questionnaire contained 33 items, which were reviewed based on exploratory factor analysis (EFA). Items with factor loadings below 0.4 or those showing low item-total correlations were excluded, resulting in a final version consisting of 27 items.

The final version of the questionnaire was developed by a team of six researchers through multiple rounds of review and expert consultation to ensure its content validity and clarity. The questionnaire was translated into Chinese by three bilingual pharmacy education experts with international study experience. The translated questionnaire was then reviewed by these experts to ensure the accuracy and consistency of the translation. The final Chinese version was used for data collection with participants. To ensure transparency, the English version of the final questionnaire, which was translated using software and reviewed by the experts, is included in the Supplementary Material S1 for reference.

The final questionnaire consists of four main sections:

Demographic Information – This section collects background information on pharmacy graduate students, including gender, academic discipline, degree level, year of study, and parental education level.

Self-Assessment of Global Competence – This section evaluates three dimensions of global competence: “Knowledge and Understanding,” “Skills and Experiences,” and “Attitudes and Values.” These three dimensions are further divided into six sub-dimensions, comprising 22 items in total. Each item is assessed using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Dimension 1, “Knowledge and Understanding,” includes three factors: World Knowledge, which refers to understanding global geography, historical developments, and international political systems; Global Understanding, which involves comprehension of globalization and its impacts on national and international development; and International Academic Competence, which reflects familiarity with global academic standards and international research contributions. Dimension 2, “Skills and Experiences,” includes one factor: Cross-cultural Communication, which assesses the ability to interact effectively with individuals from diverse cultural backgrounds. Dimension 3, “Attitudes and Values,” includes two factors: International Awareness, which refers to awareness of global issues and the interconnection of the world’s problems, and International Attitude, which reflects openness, willingness to engage, and respect for other cultures.

Additionally, since the questionnaire was administered online, two quality control items were included to prevent random or invalid responses: Quality Control Item 1: What is 1 + 1? Quality Control Item 2: Please select the second option for this question. These quality control measures ensured the reliability of the collected data.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. The internal structural validity of the PGGCES items was evaluated using principal component analysis\with varimax rotation and exploratory factor analysis. The internal consistency reliability of the scale was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Descriptive statistical analysis was conducted to examine the GC levels of pharmacy graduate students. Based on previous research, multiple linear regression analysis was employed to explore the relationship between independent and dependent variables. A stepwise addition of independent variables was used to construct a series of hierarchical regression models. Specifically, four multivariate regression models were developed as follows:

Model 4: GC + demographic variables + family background variables + local internationalization variables + cross-border internationalization variables

Model 4: GC + demographic variables + family background variables + local internationalization variables + cross-border internationalization variables

The regression model was formulated as follows: PGGCi = β0 + β1 × 1 + β2 × 2+…+βiXi+.

Where GC represents the global competence score of pharmacy graduate students, X₁, X₂, … Xi represent various influencing factors, β₁, β₂, … βi are the partial regression coefficients for each factor, and ε represents the residual error. All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R (version 4.2.3) and RStudio (version 2023.03.0).

Descriptive analysis of pharmacy graduate students’ GC

In the PGGCES project, the average GC score among the 568 pharmacy graduate students was 3.36, based on self-evaluation across three dimensions: “Knowledge and Understanding,”"Skills and Experiences,” and “Attitudes and Values,” as well as six factors. The average scores for the three dimensions were as follows: “Knowledge and Understanding” at 3.36,"Skills and Experiences"at 2.87, and “Attitudes and Values” at 3.85. The highest-scoring factors were International Attitude (3.95), International Awareness (3.75), and International Academic Competence (3.57), while the lowest-scoring factor was Cross-cultural Communication (2.87) (See Table 3).

Exploratory factor analysis

In the PGGCES project, an exploratory factor analysis was conducted on all 27 items. The detailed results are presented in Table 4. Based on the initial three-dimension structure of the project, a three-factor solution was formulated. After applying quartimax rotation, five items were removed due to factor loadings below 0.5 or ambiguous factor assignment. The remaining 22 items were categorized into three factors, corresponding to the three broad dimensions of the scale: the first dimension contained 9 items, the second dimension included 7 items, and the third dimension comprised 6 items. This solution accounted for 48.59% of the total variance. The eigenvalues for the three factors were 6.24, 2.31, and 2.14, respectively. According to Kaiser’s criterion, factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 are considered meaningful, as they account for more variance than a single observed variable. Therefore, three factors were retained in this study. The overall KMO value was 0.852, indicating the adequacy of the sample for factor analysis.

Factors influencing pharmacy graduate students’ GC

As shown in Table 5, the four models demonstrated high goodness-of-fit and reliability, effectively explaining the research findings. The regression coefficients in Table 5 are unstandardized B-values, which represent the direct effect of each predictor variable on the dependent variable. These values are expressed in the original units of the variables. In Model 1, factors such as “Degree Nature” (e.g., academic or professional graduate students) and “Gender” had statistically significant effects on GC. In Model 2, after incorporating family background variables, in addition to the previously significant factors of “Degree Nature” and “Gender,” the status of being a “first-generation university student” also showed a significant impact on GC. In Model 3, local internationalization variables were further included, increasing the explanatory power of the model by 15.6%. This indicates that participation in local internationalization activities is a major factor influencing GC. Specifically, “Reading professional textbooks in foreign languages,” “Studying foreign language courses,” “Participating in international academic conferences,” and “Discussions with foreign students in their specialized fields” were significantly associated with GC. Meanwhile, the effect of “Degree Nature” was no longer significant, while “Year of Study” emerged as a significant influencing factor. In Model 4, cross-border internationalization variables were added, resulting in an overall explanatory power of 21.3%, which was only a 0.3% increase compared to Model 3. In this model, “Participating in publishing SCI papers” was newly identified as a significant factor affecting PGGC, while the effect of “Gender” was no longer statistically significant.

These results indicate a low degree of multicollinearity among variables, confirming the suitability of the model for further research.

Discussion

The PGGCES project proposed in this study evaluates the GC of Chinese pharmacy graduate students based on the global competence concepts outlined by the OECD and other researchers. It assesses GC through three primary dimensions: “Knowledge and Understanding,”"Skills and Experiences,” and “Attitudes and Values,” while also exploring its influencing factors. Our findings indicate that Chinese pharmacy graduate students exhibit a relatively high level of GC. Among the three dimensions, “Attitudes and Values” had the highest average score, followed by “Knowledge and Understanding,” while"Skills and Experiences"had the lowest score. This result suggests that respondents have a strong willingness to deepen their understanding of the world and comprehend globalization and its impact; however, they lack proficiency in globalized skills. These findings align with those of Jiang et al., who assessed the GC of Chinese clinical medicine students. Notably, the scores for communication and interaction skills with foreign peers were significantly lower [21, 22]which may be attributed to the limited opportunities for contact with foreigners in their daily academic and social environments. Consequently, their communication and interaction skills remain largely untrained, akin to a “newborn” lacking any prior exposure or practice [23–25]. Furthermore, it is important to highlight that previous studies have pointed out that China’s English education remains predominantly exam-oriented [21, 26]focusing mainly on written and reading skills. As a result, students struggle to transfer their classroom-acquired English proficiency into real-time oral communication without loss of effectiveness.

This trend is also reflected in the multiple regression model for GC, which shows that “participating in international academic conferences” and “discussing professional topics with foreign students” have a significant positive impact on GC. This suggests that engaging in academic activities characterized by communication and interaction can significantly enhance pharmacy graduate students’ GC. Similarly, German scholar Nagy et al. found that even a short-term intervention can improve communication skills and significantly boost medical students’ confidence in interacting with foreign patients [27]. These findings underscore the importance of developing communication and interaction skills among pharmacy graduate students. Chinese universities have also recognized this need and are currently implementing curriculum reforms to address it. Our previous research [28] has shown that an increasing number of universities have begun introducing doctor-patient communication courses at the undergraduate level to enhance pharmacy students’ cross-cultural communication abilities [29, 30]. Interestingly, our study found that “communicating with international students” significantly improved GC, whereas “communicating with foreign experts and scholars” had no significant effect. Similar findings [17, 31] have been reported in other studies. A possible explanation is that interacting with peers tends to be more relaxed and comfortable compared to discussions with senior experts, leading to longer and more frequent conversations. In contrast, interactions with scholars and experts may feel more formal and constrained, resulting in shorter discussions and less in-depth exchanges.

In the PGGCES project, participation in local internationalization activities significantly enhanced GC, likely due to the variety of available formats, allowing students to engage broadly. However, despite cross-border internationalization being widely recognized by experts and scholars as one of the most effective ways to enhance GC [3, 22, 32–35]our study did not observe a significant impact of such experiences on GC. Several possible reasons may explain this finding: First, insufficient sample size may have introduced bias. In this study, fewer than 10% of respondents had cross-border internationalization experiences, which may have limited the statistical power to detect an effect. Second, short duration of activities could be a contributing factor. As previously discussed, Slantcheva-Durst et al. and Yang et al. [16, 36] suggested that short-term international academic visits or expert lectures have limited effects on improving students’ GC, mainly because these experiences often lack deep interaction and practical application. Third, research by Elliott et al. indicated that sustained intercultural engagement is essential for meaningful GC development [37]. Rather than one-time visits or lectures, effective GC enhancement requires ongoing cross-cultural interactions, such as structured collaborative projects between local and international students embedded within course design.

Although the PGGCES demonstrated a stable factor structure and internal consistency, this study did not evaluate its external validity by comparing global competence scores with behavioral indicators or academic outcomes. Future research should examine the predictive and concurrent validity of PGGCES through longitudinal or multi-method studies.

Nonetheless, the PGGCES shows promise as a practical and adaptable instrument for assessing the global competence of Chinese pharmacy graduate students. Given that it does not rely heavily on pharmacy-specific content, it may be applicable to a broader population with only minor adjustments. Our research team is currently working on further validating and refining the tool through global application studies.

Conclusion

This study assessed the current state of GC among Chinese pharmacy graduate students using the PGGCES project. The findings lay a foundation for enhancing the development of GC in pharmacy graduate education. Universities should place greater emphasis on improving students’ review of foreign language papers, textbooks, and interactions with scholars in their daily teaching activities. Additionally, under resource-limited conditions, expanding opportunities for local internationalization activities whenever possible may significantly contribute to improving pharmacy graduate students’ GC.
