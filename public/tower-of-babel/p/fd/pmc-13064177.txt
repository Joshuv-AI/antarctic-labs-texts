Life expectancy gains across US states between 1941 and 2000: a cohort study
Authors: Héctor Pifarré i Arolas, José Andrade, Jason Fletcher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe use new data and robust methods to estimate mortality levels and trends for US states and Washington DC and quantify cohort life expectancy for birth cohorts between 1941 and 2000.DesignObservational retrospective cohort study.DataMortality data and population estimates for cohorts born between 1941 and 2000 at the state, regional and national levels were obtained from the US Mortality Database.Main outcome measuresWe estimated cohort life expectancy at birth by sex and geographical level. We used the coefficient of variation to assess levels and trends in convergence in cohort life expectancy by geographical level.ResultsIn contrast to recent findings, estimates suggest that all states and regions experienced cohort life expectancy gains between the birth cohorts of 1941 and 2000 for both sexes, typically more than 5 years; the range included gains of 7–8 years in Oklahoma and Arkansas to over 14 years in Arizona, New Mexico and South Carolina (men). States sharply converged in the 1940s due to the South making substantial gains in under-five mortality, and since the 1960s there has been overall stability in geographical variation that is a combination of continued convergence by the South and modest divergence in the Midwest.ConclusionsCohort life expectancy estimates across US states over the birth cohorts born between 1941 and 2000 have shown important gains in all states and regions. The lack of progress estimated in other work is not replicated; for example, recent work estimated that between 1950 and 2000, at least 15 states’ female populations experienced cohort life expectancy losses; however, the use of better data and robust methods show no losses by any state. Our results also point to early life public health and medical interventions as key explanations for regional convergence in cohort life expectancy around 1950, however, the results suggest only modest additional convergence since then.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Mortality, STATISTICS & RESEARCH METHODS

Introduction

While trends in period life expectancy can highlight fluctuations in mortality schedules by time and place, the estimates embed a lack of memory of the life course. Previous mortality experiences of individuals and cohorts are forgotten in period estimates. In contrast, cohort life expectancy retains the history of mortality exposures of a cohort but must make forecasts for mortality that is not yet experienced.1 The interpretation is thus different: while period life expectancy captures the contemporary mortality conditions experienced by a population, cohort life expectancy provides a direct measure of longevity.2 The data demands for these two measures are also quite distinct, with period measures requiring only a single year of age-specific mortality rates and cohort measures requiring age-specific mortality experienced over 100+ years, as the cohort ages through time. The two measures can complement each other by increasing our understanding of mortality processes, yet cohort measures are not widely used, due in part to the more difficult data requirements. Indeed, cohort life expectancy trends can capture histories of changes in environmental, policy and behavioural exposures that can help craft future interventions to increase gains over time. Additionally, state-specific trends in cohort life expectancy might uncover additional information not contained in period measures, as many policies, behaviours and medical interventions vary considerably by place. Differential trends could help uncover why some places have larger gains than others and whether targeted policies might reduce regional divergences in mortality in the US and elsewhere.

Recent work by Holford et al3 considerably expanded the evidence base in this area by estimating cohort life expectancies by state and sex for each birth cohort between 1900 and 2000. The authors presented several unanticipated results, including that many Southern states enjoyed very limited gains (and in many cases, losses) over the period, and that the USA outperformed all countries in cohort life expectancy around 1900. Some of the results are in contrast to official estimates from the Social Security Administration (SSA). Our paper re-examines these findings by incorporating more expansive mortality data (starting in 1941 rather than 1969) and using robust forecasting methods. Our results are inconsistent with all of the unanticipated findings of earlier work. We find that all states experienced cohort life expectancy gains between 1941 and 2000, though there is considerable variation across the country in the trajectories of gains. We quantify for the first time in the literature the variation in cohort life expectancy over this time period, showing rapid convergence during the 1940s, followed by relative stability during the period of 1960 and 2000. Age-specific patterns in these findings suggest that the convergence is explained by rapid progress in the South in reducing infant and child mortality, likely through public health and medical practices.

Methods

Data

Before outlining our methods, we briefly discuss methods and data from previous work. Holford et al leverage mortality data between 1969 and 2020 and use age-period-cohort methods to estimate mortality rates for all birth cohorts for each state/sex between 1900 and 2000. Since the data do not overlap well with the cohorts (see online supplemental materials figure 1), the models must fill in (predict) many mortality rates considerably beyond the scope of the data. For example, the models estimate infant mortality rates starting in 1900 but only leverage data on infant mortality starting in 1969. Due to the rapid and non-linear declines in infant mortality in the early part of the 20th century,4 it is likely that the models will predict infant mortality in 1900 that is much too low compared with observed (aggregate) measures, which may have led to the very high estimates of cohort life expectancy in 1900 by the authors. Indeed, the authors’ estimate of 73.8 for female cohort life expectancy in 1900 would place the USA as, by far, the best practice country in that year. Estimates from recorded data (rather than model estimates) suggest that Norway had the highest female cohort life expectancy in 1900 of 62. We do not go into further detail here but also note that age-period-cohort models face considerable challenges due to the well-known linear dependence between period=age + cohort.5

Rather than using the same data as previous work, we leverage recent releases of national, state and region-specific mortality data from the US Mortality Database (USMDB) that span 1941–2022.6 The USMDB constructs mortality rates and life tables using data from official sources, such as census records and death certificates, combined with standardised methods developed for the well-established Human Mortality Database.7 We use these mortality rates, extending to an open age interval of 110 and older, together with forecasting approaches described in the next section, to construct cohort life tables for the 1941—2000 birth cohorts, following standard demographic methods.8 Note that Alaska and Hawaii were admitted as US states in 1959, so our results largely focus on the contiguous US states and Washington DC (partial results for Alaska and Hawaii are available in online supplemental tables 1,2). The study complies with STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Statistical methods

Forecasting methods description

Calculating cohort life expectancy for currently living cohorts requires filling in the remaining mortality. The oldest cohort considered in our analysis, born in 1941, was 81 in 2022, the last observed year in our sample, meaning that calculating their life expectancy requires filling in mortality from age 82 through 110. Similarly, the youngest cohort, born in 2000, was only 22 in 2022, so estimating their full cohort life expectancy requires projecting mortality for ages 23 through 110, a total of 88 future age-specific mortality rates.

To generate these future mortality rates, we apply the Lee-Carter approach,9 separately for each geographical unit, state, region or the national level. The Lee-Carter model is a well-established methodology for mortality forecasting. Succinctly, it models the logarithm of mortality rates as a function of several components. First, it identifies a general age pattern of log-mortality that reflects baseline mortality levels across ages. Second, it estimates age-specific sensitivities to changes in overall mortality, capturing how different age groups respond to broader mortality trends. Third, it includes a time-varying mortality index that summarises the overall level of mortality at each point in time. Parameter estimation in the Lee-Carter model is typically performed using singular value decomposition, which provides a parsimonious representation of the observed mortality surface. Once the parameters are estimated, the time-varying mortality index is projected into the future using standard time series techniques, most commonly autoregressive integrated moving average models.

We use our estimated levels of cohort life expectancy from our forecasting methods to then calculate trends in cohort life expectancy between 1941 and 2000 for each state, sex and cohort combination. We embed these figures into a visualisation of the USA and estimate spatial autocorrelation in cohort life expectancy trends using a Global Moran’s I with a contiguity-based spatial weights matrix.10

Finally, we perform an out-of-sample validation exercise to assess the accuracy of our approach,11 12 fully described in the online supplemental table 1. In short, we withhold 30 years of observed mortality data (1980–2010) and forecast this period using the same modelling strategy employed throughout our analysis. We then compare cohort life expectancy estimates derived from these out-of-sample forecasts to those obtained using all available observed data.

Divergence/convergence metrics

Recent contributions have examined trends in disparities in mortality across states by tracking the coefficient of variation (CV) of period life expectancy across states.13 Following this work, we examine trends in inequality in cohort life expectancy across states using the CV for cohorts 1941 through 2000. In addition, we provide a population-weighted version of the CV that uses the population-weighted mean and the corresponding population-weighted SD (additional details provided in the online supplemental file SM1). For interpretability, both versions of the CV are normalised to 1 in the baseline year, 1941.

Patient and public involvement

Results

Table 1 summarises our main results at the regional and national levels, and online supplemental figures 2,3 visualise national and regional cohort life expectancy trends. Online supplemental tables 2,3 present disaggregated results by state, cohort and sex. Out-of-sample validation (online supplemental table 1) shows modest median cohort life expectancy deviations across all sex-geography combinations (0.04–0.12%), with maximum deviations remaining limited (highest: 3.6% for West Virginia males, cohort 1941). Our main finding is that all states, cohorts and sexes experienced cohort life expectancy gains between 1941 and 2000. Nationally, we estimate gains of 10.57 years for females and 11.57 years for men; the results also suggest similar gains for each region, between 9.69 years for females in the Midwest and 12.18 years for males in the South. Online supplemental tables 2,3 show our full range of estimated gains; the smallest gains for women occurred in Wyoming (4.81 years) and for men in Oklahoma (6.55 years), whereas the largest gains occurred in Arizona for women (12.43 years) and for men (14.73 years). Online supplemental table 4 shows cohort-level gains at the national and regional levels over time. Figure 1 maps these gains, the Moran’s I measure of spatial autocorrelation is estimated to be between 0.51 and 0.57 in levels in 1941 and 2000 and approximately 0.3 for cohort life expectancy gains. Each estimate is statistically significant at the 99% level and implies moderate-to-high levels of spatial similarity—places that experienced larger gains are neighbours of other places that also experienced large gains over this time period. These estimates are consistent with other work showing high spatial correlation in county level mortality trends in the USA (Global Moran’s I of 0.48).14 15

To further explore geographical disparities in cohort life expectancy, figure 2 displays the CV for cohorts 1941–2000, normalised to 1941 values (the dots reflect results using population weights). We find large reductions in the estimated CV of 20–25% (ie, state convergence) over the first decade, followed by nearly 50 years characterised by smaller fluctuations. The population-weighted CV shows qualitatively similar trends but indicates a relatively larger increase in disparities for males following the initial decline. Online supplemental figure 3 visualises regional cohort life expectancy, showing initial convergence between the South and the rest of the county in the 1940s followed by both divergence in the Midwest from the West and Northeast as well as continuing convergence by the South after 1950. Together, these two patterns produce overall stability in the CV between 1960 and 2000. Online supplemental tables 5,6 further decompose the sex-specific cohort life expectancy gains by age group. These estimates suggest that the rapid convergence in the early period was largely due to Southern states experiencing large gains in under-five mortality; indeed, between 1941 and 1947, we estimate that the probability of dying before age 5 fell by nearly 40% in the South (online supplemental table 7). For example, for females, estimates suggest annual cohort life expectancy gains in the 1940s of between 0.24 and 0.27 years outside of the South and 0.36 years in the South. The proportion of gains from under-five mortality was between 42% and 55% outside of the South and 65% in the South. Starting in the 1950s, all regions saw similar gains and therefore we see broad stability in the CV. For females, these later annual gains were typically half the size of the 1940s gains and ranged between 0.12 and 0.18 years in all regions, and the proportion of the gains attributed to under-five mortality fell to between 15% and 42%.

Discussion

Our results suggest universal gains in cohort life expectancy between 1941 and 2000 for all birth cohorts, sexes and states. These gains range from 7 to 16 years across the USA, showing some initial geographical convergence in the 1940s followed by 50 years of relative stability. The initial convergence was driven by important gains from lowering under-five mortality in the South and the stability has occurred during a time period of more modest gains since the 1950s, which is consistent with some period life expectancy estimates over the same time period.16 Our main results are in deep disagreement with recent results from the literature. Holford et al estimate cohort life expectancy gains between 1950 and 2000 for women (3 years) and men (5.9 years) that differ substantially from our estimates for women (7.7 years) and men (8.9 years) (see online supplemental tables 8,9 for complete estimates). The authors also report 16 states with estimated life expectancy losses for women between 1950 and 2000 including a 3.1-year loss in West Virginia, whereas we estimate universal gains, and the smallest estimate for women is a gain of 4.8 years in Wyoming; our estimate for females in West Virginia is a 6.14-year gain.

Our results and the results in Holford et al use different data and use different methods. To assess the relative validity of these two sets of results, we note several findings in Holford et al that contradict other findings and understandings. First, Holford et al estimate cohort life expectancy at birth in 1900 in the USA to be 62.8 for males and 73.8 for females. These estimates are much higher than those produced by the SSA: 51.5 for males and 58.3 for females estimated.17 Even Holford et al cohort life expectancy estimates in 1950 of 74.4 for males and 81.1 for females are higher than the 73 and 79.4 produced by the SSA; our estimates for 1950 are quite close to those from SSA: 73.2 for men and 79.9 for women. These differences in 1950 estimates, where Holford et al always estimate larger cohort life expectancy than other sources, lead to estimates of gains between 1950 and 2000 that will be systematically lower than our estimates. These lower estimates include many estimates of life expectancy losses that are not found using our data and methods.

While it is important to produce accurate measures of cohort life expectancy to track progress (or lack thereof), an added benefit in estimating state-specific cohort life expectancy is to begin explorations of potential policy-based sources for geographical variation. Indeed, Holford et al note that Kentucky has a history of weaker tobacco control policies than California. It is important to note that their claim relies on their estimate that Kentucky experienced a 1.6-year loss for women, whereas our estimate is a 6.8-year gain, which places Kentucky not as an outlier as the third-worst performing state but rather as only in the bottom 25% (ranked 11). They also contrast state experiences by noting that females in Oklahoma were estimated to have experienced a decrease in cohort life expectancy of 0.7 years between 1900 and 2000 while New York experienced an increase of 20 years. Their estimates that compare 1950 to 2000 are a loss of 1.5 years in Oklahoma and a gain of 9.6 years in New York: a 10+ years difference. Our own estimates for 1950–2000 are a gain of nearly 5 years in Oklahoma and a gain of 8.5 years in New York. The difference, though still important, is roughly one-third of the size of these prior estimates. While both estimates suggest that some states are lagging behind others, the magnitude of many of these differences, and therefore the possible policy lessons we might infer, is quite distinct between approaches. Indeed, our estimates of geographical variation in gains between 1950 and 2000 suggest stability rather than divergence, which raises the question of the extent to which the large differences in policies across US states have impacted geographical variation in cohort life expectancy.

Strengths and limitations

This study is strengthened by recently released and comprehensive historical mortality data from the USMDB. These data contain age-specific mortality data for each state and by sex between 1941 and 2022. While informative, estimates of cohort life expectancy for currently living cohorts depend heavily on mortality forecasts, which are inherently uncertain. Unanticipated events such as the opioid crisis18 can disrupt long-term mortality trends, potentially reducing the accuracy of these forecasts. This limitation is particularly relevant for younger cohorts, for whom a larger portion of the life course remains unobserved and must be forecasted. Due to data constraints, we do not analyse trends separately by race or ethnicity. However, a substantial body of research has documented persistent disparities in life expectancy across racial and ethnic groups in the USA, including long-standing gaps19 and more recent, remaining substantial differences.20 21 A further limitation is that we are not able to follow individuals’ migration patterns over time. Other work has shown that over 60% of individuals die in their birth state22 and also show evidence that period measures of life expectancy by state differ if researchers use state of birth rather than state of death in the calculations. More broadly, migration within a context of state-of-residence based cohort analyses changes the composition of cohorts over time in a way that, similar to cohort analyses of education gradients in mortality,23 poses interpretative issues that should be the subject of future work.

The USA has experienced continued gains in period and cohort life expectancy during the 20th century, although there is evidence of slower gains and reversals in more recent years.16 24 Estimating geographical variation in these gains can increase our understanding of key social and policy determinants of gains, as the USA has substantial state differences in populations and policies over this time period.25 Nearly all of the evidence of geographical differences in mortality in the USA has taken a period perspective, which can reduce our ability to understand longer term and life course determinants of some aspects of geographical disparities. Holford et al extend the literature by focusing on cohort life expectancy by state and sex across cohorts born between 1900 and 2000 using age-period-cohort methods and data capturing deaths between 1969 and 2020. In order to fully measure trends over this full-time period while using only a subset of mortality experiences, the authors must model a large amount of unmeasured mortality prior to 1969 to ‘fill in’ the experiences of birth cohorts starting in 1900. The authors find substantial state and regional variation, estimating that many Southern states experienced declines in cohort life expectancy between 1950 and 2000. In filling in unmeasured mortality rates prior to 1969, the authors produce estimates that substantially differ from estimates from the SSA cohort life tables26 and imply that the USA had the highest cohort life expectancy in the world at the turn of the 20th century.27 These overestimates of life expectancy in 1900 and 1950 then produce estimates of gains in life expectancy through 2000 that are likely substantially biased downward and suggest a lack of progress for many US states.

Conclusions and policy implications

Our analysis uses newly available data that extends the window of measured mortality from 1969 back to 1941 and uses standard demographic forecasting methods to complete mortality for cohorts born through 2000. In contrast to Holford et al, our estimates suggest universal gains in cohort life expectancy for all states by sex and birth cohort over this period. We estimate important state differences in cohort life expectancy levels and trends and quantify a large geographical convergence in mortality experiences during the 1940s, stemming from Southern states substantial decreases in infant and child mortality, followed by relative stability between 1955 and 2000. We believe our estimates can form a basis for future research to seek to uncover additional key determinants of the temporal and geographical variation in cohort life expectancy over this time period and use these discoveries to suggest future policy directions that may lead to larger, shared gains in the future.
