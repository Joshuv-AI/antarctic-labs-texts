Clinical outcomes of revision arthroplasty with a modular taper fluted titanium stem for the treatment of Vancouver B2 and B3 periprosthetic femur fractures
Authors: Jeremy Kubik, Margaret S. Juryn, Sahil Kooner, Oleg Safir, Allan Gross, Paul Kuzyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Existing literature on Vancouver B2 and B3 periprosthetic fracture management with modular tapered fluted stems (MTFS) is limited by lack of patient-reported outcomes, small samples, and unseparated inclusion of multiple implant models. This study examines the outcomes of Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures treated with a single MTFS hip system, the Zimmer Modular Revision (ZMR), with a minimum 1-year follow-up.Methods:We conducted a retrospective review of all patients who received a femoral revision with a ZMR taper stem to treat a Vancouver B2 or B3 fracture at our centre between 2006 and 2020. The primary outcome was all-cause reoperation, with secondary outcomes including the Harris Hip Score (HHS), the Oxford Hip Score (OHS), stem subsidence, and mortality.Results:We included 44 patients (mean age 71.6 yr) in the final analysis, with a mean follow-up of 50.2 months. The all-cause reoperation rate was 11.9% (n = 5). Although there were 3 cases (7.1%) of stem subsidence, there were no cases in which stem revision was required. The mean HHS was 77.2 (standard deviation [SD] 10.6, range 47 to 91) and the mean OHS was 42.5 (SD 5.2, range 33 to 48).Conclusion:Management of Vancouver B2 and B3 periprosthetic femur fractures with the ZMR MFTS option resulted in an acceptable complication profile and good functional outcome with a minimum 1-year follow-up.

As the number of primary and revision total hip arthroplasty (THA) procedures performed each year continues to increase, the incidence of periprosthetic femoral fracture — one of the most common indications for THA revision — has increased correspondingly.1–4 The Vancouver classification for periprosthetic femur fractures often guides management and is based on fracture location, implant stability, and available bone stock.1–6 Vancouver type B fractures either surround, or are just distal to, the femoral stem and are nonapophyseal. When the stem continues to be stable and well fixed, the fracture is subclassified as type B1 and is often treated with open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF).7 In cases where the femoral stem has become unstable, the quality of the surrounding bone acts as the differentiating factor between a type B2 fracture, which has good remaining bone stock surrounding the loosened stem, and a type B3 fracture, which is characterized by severe bone stock loss. Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures typically require revision arthroplasty to address the unstable femoral implant,6 as fixation of the fracture alone without revision is associated with higher failure rates.3

Diaphyseal-engaging modular tapered fluted stems (MTFS) are increasingly being used for femoral revision in the periprosthetic fracture setting because they permit reliable distal fixation in the setting of advanced proximal bone loss and because of the rotational control provided by their splined morphology, combined with the advantageous flexibility in length and offset reestablishment afforded by the usage of modular proximal components.1,3,8,9 The Zimmer Modular Revision (ZMR) hip system is a widely used modular system that offers — among other options, including a cylindrical porous-coated stem — 2 varieties of MTFS: the revision taper stem and the XL taper stem. Both stems feature a 3.5° taper geometry over the distal 105 mm of the stem and 0.75-mm splines, but the XL taper stem provides options for greater stem diameters closer to the proximal body attachment.10

Our centre has used the ZMR hip system since 2000 and has published 3 studies reporting on the survivorship of the stem options offered by this system in the revision setting generally.8,11,12 Studies examining the outcomes of MTFS for Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures specifically, however, are limited by very small samples, use of multiple different treatment methods (e.g., with or without accompanying internal fixation), or, as seen in almost all cases, use of implants from several manufacturers.1,3,9,13–21 Thus, we sought to evaluate the application of the ZMR taper stems to these specific periprosthetic fracture subtypes. The purpose of this study was to examine clinical and radiographic outcomes, and implant survival at a minimum 1-year follow-up of treatment for Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures with a single MTFS, the ZMR hip system.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a retrospective review of all patients who received a femoral revision with a ZMR stem to treat a Vancouver B2 or B3 fracture between 2006 and 2020 at our centre. We excluded patients with Vancouver B1 fractures, those with intraoperative fractures, those with junctional failure of modular femoral stem components, patients with less than 1 year of follow-up, those treated with an implant other than the ZMR modular system, and those treated with the ZMR system but with the cylindrical porous-coated stem option. Two authors (J.K. and S.K.) classified fractures by reviewing the injury radiographs and the operative report.

Surgical technique

The previous hip incision was used when possible and the surgical approach was either an anterolateral (transgluteal) approach or a modified extended trochanteric osteotomy (ETO). The modified ETO was used for all revisions of cemented implants and for revisions of cementless implants when the fracture pattern was such that conversion to an ETO was straightforward.22 After femoral stem removal, the acetabular component was evaluated and subsequently revised if deemed unstable. If the acetabular component was poorly positioned, but well fixed, a liner was cemented in a more acceptable position. The femoral diaphysis was then exposed and sequentially reamed. The length of the distal stem was selected to maximize diaphyseal fixation. After proximal reaming, trial components were used to achieve appropriate body length and size, offset and version. The definitive component was then implanted, and the proximal fracture fragments were reduced around the implant and secured with cerclage wires. In select cases, a cortical strut allograft was used to augment bone stock and construct stability. Layered closure was performed. Patients were instructed to be partial weight-bearing for 6 weeks after surgery and then progress to weight-bearing as tolerated.

Patient assessment

Patients returned for clinical and radiological assessment 6 weeks, 3 months, and 1 year after surgery, and every year thereafter. We reviewed inpatient and outpatient records for death, fracture healing, and local complications, including refracture, aseptic loosening, dislocation, infection, mechanical failure, and neurovascular injuries. We also reviewed postoperative functional outcomes at the most recent follow-up assessment, measured using a modified Harris Hip Score (HHS) and modified Oxford Hip Score (OHS).8,23

Two authors (J.K. and S.K.) performed radiographic analysis. Stem subsidence was defined as distal migration of the femoral stem greater than 5 mm over consecutive radiographs with controlled magnification.19,24 Implant loosening was defined as progressive subsidence or the presence of circumferential radiolucent lines adjacent to the stem. Any reviewer disagreement was reviewed by the senior author.

Statistical analysis

We reported descriptive statistics using means, ranges, percentages, and standard deviations (SDs) where appropriate. We used the χ2 test and Fisher exact test to test for correlation between nominal variables, and the Student t test to evaluate continuous variables. The primary outcome of failure was defined as revision for any reason. We assessed failures to determine survivorship through Kaplan–Meier analysis for all patients.

We performed Kaplan–Meier analysis to determine implant survivorship, with patient death and loss to follow-up considered to be right-censored. We calculated the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the difference between means and proportions. We considered p values less than 0.05 to be statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM).

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Mount Sinai Hospital Research Ethics Board (no. 18-0097-C).

Results

There were 163 patients admitted to our institution with periprosthetic fractures who underwent revision surgery from 2006 to 2020. After exclusions, we included 44 patients (mean age 71.6 yr) in the final analysis (Table 1). There were 24 females and 20 males, with a mean American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score of 3.1 (SD 0.6, range 2.0 to 4.0) and mean body mass index of 28.6 (SD 7.0, range 20 to 52). There were 15 cemented and 29 uncemented stems. The mean time to the operating room from injury was 3.6 (SD 3.9, range 0.3 to 19.0) days. The mean follow-up time was 50.2 (SD 38.8, range 12 to 168) months, with a median follow-up of 43 months. Of the 44 included patients, 36 had Vancouver B2 fractures and 8 had Vancouver B3 fractures. The mean ASA score was statistically different between the 2 fracture groups (3.0 v. 3.7, p = 0.001). Otherwise, there were no significant differences between the fracture groups in terms of demographic characteristics and ZMR MTFS subtype (revision or XL taper) (Table 2).

Reoperation rate

Outcome data are presented in Table 3. With 5 of 44 included patients requiring reoperation, the all-cause reoperation rate in the current study was 11.4%. Three patients (6.8%) required fixation for repeat periprosthetic fracture, which occurred at a mean of 38 (range 16 to 66) months after the index revision. All 3 of these repeat fractures were well distal to the stem (Vancouver C type) and healed with ORIF. One patient (2.3%) required a constrained liner for instability, and 1 (2.3%) required irrigation, débridement, and a liner exchange for early infection. The Kaplan–Meier survivorship, with repeat procedure as the defined outcome, was 86.0% (95% CI 72.5% to 99.9%) at 5 years and 78.2% (95% CI 59.0% to 97.4%) at 10 years (Figure 1).

Functional scores were obtained for 31 patients. The mean HHS score was 77.2 (SD 10.6, range 47 to 91) and the mean OHS was 42.5 (SD 5.2, range 33 to 48), with no significant difference between patients with Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures.

Radiographic follow-up was obtained for 42 patients. There were 3 cases (7.1%) of stem subsidence, none of which required a revision procedure. Heterotopic ossification was identified in 7 (14.9%) patients, all of which were classified as Brooker type III and required no secondary procedure.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to assess the performance of the taper stem options offered by the ZMR hip system, a widely used MTFS, in treating Vancouver B2 and B3 periprosthetic fractures at a single institution with minimum 1-year follow-up. Through a primary outcome measure of repeat procedures and secondary outcomes — including HHS, OHS, stem subsidence, and mortality — our results show that the ZMR taper stems provide an acceptable complication profile and good functional outcomes over a mean follow-up of 50.2 months.

This study represents a continuation of published work by our centre examining the ZMR hip system for treatment of periprosthetic fracture. In 2009, our group evaluated the porous-coated modular stem offered by ZMR for THA revision of any cause,12 then evaluated the ZMR taper stems in this same context a year later.11 In 2021, we published a more comprehensive study of both ZMR stem subtypes for all-cause revision.8 This study showcased the utility of the system for several pathologies in a revision setting, but noted that the option of distal fixation is particularly useful in the case of substantial proximal bone loss. It thus followed naturally to initiate a more focused examination of the performance of this MTFS in the ostensibly exemplary use case of Vancouver B2 or B3 periprosthetic fractures.

Studies have looked more broadly at MTFS for Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures1,9,14–19,21 and others that have evaluated ZMR taper for all-cause revision fractures,8,11 but none examined the ZMR taper for Vancouver B2 and B3. These distinctions create the potential to compare the ZMR Taper’s performance with other MTFS for treating Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures, as well as the ZMR taper’s performance for all-cause revision versus for Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures specifically. As such, the differentiation of this study’s design on 2 dimensions provides a valuable opportunity for comparison with existing literature in assessing the usage of this particular stem for this particular application.

ZMR taper versus other MTFS for Vancouver B2 or B3 fracture

Comparing the performance of the ZMR taper stem to other MTFS for Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures, the ZMR taper stem shows a slightly lower all-cause reoperation rate (11% v. 14% to 18% for other MTFS) at comparable follow-up periods (Table 4). The stem revision rate of 0% for ZMR taper stems in this study was similar to or less than the rate seen in other studies for other MTFS treating Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures (between 0% to 6%) (Table 4).

Although most studies included Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures treated with a mixture of MTFS options, some did provide detail on the differences in stem subsidence between included models, allowing for more specific comparisons to the ZMR taper. For example, Hannon and colleagues described an 11% stem subsidence rate (10% for Link MP stems and 12% for Stryker stems), with all of these stems subsequently becoming stable.15 Parry and colleagues reported 13% of cases had subsidence, specifically noting that subsidence occurred in 3 (9%) of 32 Link stems, 5 (18%) of 27 Stryker stems, and 0 (0%) of 2 Depuy stems.19 Both of these studies specifically noted that all stems that subsided subsequently became stable, and were not associated with increased revision rate nor poor clinical outcomes.15,19 In our ZMR taper study, radiographic analysis revealed a favourable 7% rate of subsidence, and none of these instances required revision.

Along with the outcome measures of stem revision rate and subsidence, our results for the ZMR taper stem were consistent with literature published on other MFTS stems, demonstrating parity in performance if not marginal out-performance. Hannon and colleagues reported their most common indication for reoperation as being dislocation,15 which has been reported as a complication in about 5% to 20% of cases.1,9,15,17,25 Contrastingly, in our study, only 1 patient (2%) required reoperation for dislocation, and repeat periprosthetic fracture was the most common indication for reoperation. The low threshold to revise the acetabular component to an optimal position at the time of the femoral revision for fracture (18% of patients in this cohort underwent acetabular revision) used by surgeons at our centre may have contributed to a lower subsequent dislocation rate.

In our study, HHS scores (mean 77) were comparable to those seen with the other MTFS (range 75 to 84). However, only 2 other studies have reported OHS, with a surprising disparity (27 from González-Martín and colleagues and 76 from Munro and colleagues), and no obvious reason for the very low average OHS reported by González-Martín and colleagues.14,18 As such, we were unable to make meaningful comparisons of our ZMR taper OHS scores (average 42.5) with those available for other MTFS.

ZMR taper for Vancouver B2 or B3 fracture versus broader all-cause revision

In a second dimension of comparison afforded by our study design, we can compare the performance of the ZMR hip taper stem for Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures with its performance in all-cause revision more broadly. Although comparisons along this dimension offer less direct impact to clinical decision-making as they are 2 separate treatment scenarios, they create integration with our group’s previous work examining the ZMR taper stem.8,11

McInnes and colleagues reported a 10-year stem survivorship of 87.8% for taper stems for all-cause revision, while we reported 86.0% survivorship at 5 years and 78.2% survivorship at 10 years for Vancouver B2 or B3 fractures.8 It is logical that survivorship would be lower following repair of a fracture inherently indicative of severe bone stock loss; as such, it is not prudent to deduce from this simply that the ZMR taper performs better for all-cause revision on average compared with the repair of Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures. However, the modest comparability of these figures does provide encouragement regarding the performance of the taper stems in these notoriously challenging repairs.

Both McInnes and colleagues and Lakstein and colleagues reported aseptic loosening as being the most common cause for stem revision following use of the ZMR taper for all-cause revision.8,11 It is unsurprising that the most common cause for stem revision (fracture requiring fixation) would differ, given our original index for ZMR hip usage being a Vancouver B2 or B3 fracture, which inherently suggests poor bone stock, increasing the chance of future repeat fracture of the proximal femur.

McInnes and colleagues, and Lakstein and colleagues reported an average HHS of 64.7 and 72, respectively, both slightly lower than our average HHS of 77. These studies reported slightly higher rates of dislocation (3.8% and 6%, respectively), compared with our 2%. The rates of subsidence reported by the studies (7.2% and 15%, respectively) were comparable to our rate of 7.1%.8,11 These comparisons suggest that the performance of the ZMR taper stem for repair of Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures is strong when compared with its usage for broader all-cause revision, especially based on HHS scores and rates of dislocation.

Use of modern monoblock tapered fluted stems for supraisthmic periprosthetic fractures

Several modern monoblock tapered fluted stems have been introduced since 2010, with a prominent example being the Redapt system offered by Smith & Nephew. Comparative studies of monoblock tapered fluted stems versus MTFS in Vancouver B2 and similar periprosthetic fractures have shown that these modern monoblock stems achieve equivalent restoration of leg length, comparable survivorship free of reoperation and dislocation, and similar subsidence rates as MTFS in isthmic and supraisthmic locations.26–29 Although performance appears to be equivalent, the reduced implant complexity of monoblock stems may translate to lower costs through the simplification of implant inventory and streamlined surgical workflow.30 With equivalent performance but potentially lower cost, monoblock designs may be considered as an option in isthmic and supraisthmic fractures, such as Vancouver B2 and B3 periprosthetic fractures. However, MTFS remains the preferred option infraisthmic fractures given their ability to provide adequate distal fixation through customization of stem length, offset, and version.26,30

Recent increase in comparison of ORIF to revision arthroplasty for Vancouver B2 or B3 fractures

Recently, there has been an increase in reports examining ORIF for the treatment of Vancouver B2 or B3 fractures. Three systematic reviews on this topic have been published since 2021,31–33 with mainly congruent findings. All 3 meta-analyses found that complication rates, mortality rates, and proportion of patients who achieved full weight-bearing were similar between the 2 treatment options. Further, several differences were commonly identified by the 3 reviews. For instance, ORIF had higher rates of subsidence than revision arthroplasty, but had lower rates of dislocation, less blood loss, fewer transfusion requirements, and shorter hospital stays. There was inconsistency between the reviews on comparative reoperation rates, long-term functional outcomes, and overall revision rates. For example, Lewis and colleagues found similar revision rates for ORIF and revision in B2 fractures, but Haider and colleagues and Di Martino and colleagues found higher revision rates for ORIF.31–33

As such, although ORIF is being evaluated as an alternative to revision for periprosthetic fracture and may offer benefits over revision in select populations, given its less invasive nature, stem revision is likely to remain the reliable mainstay of treatment of Vancouver B2 and B3 fractures in the near future; thus, the comparison of MTFS options for this indication remains relevant.

Limitations

This study has several limitations, most plainly its retrospective design and resulting exposure to bias. Potential bias regarding categorization of fractures would, in this classification-defined study, result in selection bias; efforts were taken to minimize risk of this effect through radiographic fracture classification by multiple authors. Additionally, the cohort describes a relatively small number of patients and a short follow-up period, although this is in keeping with other series in the literature. Most importantly, there was substantial heterogeneity in terms of the type of prostheses in situ, fracture pattern, bone quality, and time from injury to the operating room. However, this study’s use of a single hip system, leading to its differentiation and comparability to existing associated literature, supports its value.

Conclusion

With a minimum of 1-year follow-up and a mean of 4.2 years follow-up, we found that the treatment of patients with Vancouver B2 or B3 fractures with the taper stem offered by the ZMR hip system provided satisfactory results with no femoral revisions required, minimal complications, and good functional outcomes.
