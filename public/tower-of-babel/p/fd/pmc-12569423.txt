Early Prediction of Paroxysmal Atrial Fibrillation in Patients With Acute Stroke Using the BANQMR Score
Authors: Satoshi Suzuki, Eriko Sugawara, Genpei Yamaura, Mutsumi Yokoyama, Hideto Joki, Yosuke Miyaji, Yuichi Higashiyama, Takayuki Momoo, Hiroshi Doi, Fumiaki Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and PurposeParoxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF) can cause embolic stroke but is often asymptomatic and may resolve before it can be detected. We developed a simple, practical scoring system to improve the detection of PAF in stroke patients.MethodsPatients with acute ischemic stroke hospitalized in three stroke centers between 2014 and 2021 were retrospectively examined. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors for PAF in a derivation cohort. Using these factors, a scoring system was developed and validated in a separate cohort.ResultsThe derivation and validation cohorts included 649 and 583 patients, respectively. Median follow-up was 606 and 101 days, respectively. The independent risk factors for PAF were brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) ≥55 pg/mL, atrial premature contractions (APCs), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score ≥11, corrected QT interval (QTc) ≥0.46 seconds, and multiple acute cerebral infarcts (MACIs). The BANQMR score assigns points based on these factors (BNP=3, APC=3, NIHSS score=2, QTc=1, and MACIs=1) and classifies patients into three risk grades based on points: low (0–2), moderate (3–5), and high (6–10). PAF was actually detected in 53.4% of high-risk patients, 15.0% of moderate-risk patients, and 4.6% of low-risk patients. The BANQMR score outperformed other existing scoring systems in terms of PAF prediction.ConclusionsThe BANQMR score is a simple, accurate tool for predicting PAF in ischemic stroke patients shortly after hospitalization, providing a clinically applicable method for early detection.

INTRODUCTION

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is a significant risk factor for ischemic stroke.1 Appropriate medical support and anticoagulant therapy are recommended for patients who experience AF-associated embolic stroke.2 Delayed or inadequate treatment are associated with early stroke recurrence, making early detection of AF crucial.34 Paroxysmal AF (PAF) is often asymptomatic and can suddenly lead to an embolic stroke in some patients.56 Because PAF may resolve immediately after the stroke, the diagnosis of AF-associated embolic stroke can be delayed—long-term electrocardiography (ECG) monitoring may be required.78910 Although previous studies have shown that AF is detected around the fourth day of hospitalization in patients with acute ischemic stroke,11 only 17% of stroke care facilities routinely perform monitoring for more than 24 hours, primarily because of limited medical and human resources.12 Therefore, a tool to predict PAF could help clinicians plan appropriate diagnostic testing and treatment.

Several risk scores using biomarkers for PAF have been reported. However, conventional ones—such as the Score for the Targeting of Atrial Fibrillation (STAF); the identified by past history of arrhythmia or antiarrhythmic agent use, atrial dilation, and brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) elevation (iPAB) score; Fujii’s simple score; the Prediction of AF in Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source (AF-ESUS) score; and the Brown ESUS-AF score—all include left atrial (LA) size as a parameter. This requires an echocardiographic examination,1314151617 which may not be readily feasible during the early phase of hospitalization—particularly in resource-limited settings with constrained staffing or limited access to equipment. Furthermore, some existing scores incorporate medical history, but impaired consciousness can hinder their use. These limitations reduce the practical utility of conventional scores in clinical settings.

We developed a new risk score based on five known AF risk factors: BNP, atrial premature contractions (APCs), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, corrected QT interval (QTc), and multiple acute cerebral infarcts (MACIs).1819202122 These factors can be easily evaluated through physical examination, blood testing, 12-lead ECG, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Thus, combining these factors into a novel risk score could serve as a simple tool to assess PAF risk shortly after hospital admission. This study describes how we derived this new risk scoring system and compares it with other conventional scores to determine its clinical utility.

METHODS

Study population

We retrospectively analyzed patients aged 18 years or older who were admitted to three stroke centers for ischemic stroke between January 1, 2014 and December 31, 2021. Patients with all ischemic stroke subtypes were eligible, including those with strokes of undetermined etiology according to the Trials of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification.23 The study was approved by the institutional review board of each participating center. The following IRB approval numbers were obtained for each participating institution: Hiratsuka Kyousai Hospital (5-13), Fujisawa City Hospital (F2023032), and Yokohama Medical Center (2024-25). This study was conducted as an opt-out study; therefore, individual written informed consent from patients was not obtained. However, the study protocol was approved by our IRB, and patients were informed about the study through public notices, which allowed them to opt out. The conduct of the study was also communicated via the official websites of the respective institutions. All patients underwent 12-lead ECG, continuous ECG monitoring, blood testing, brain MRI, and 24-hour Holter ECG. Some patients also underwent 12-lead ECG or 24-hour Holter ECG as an outpatient after discharge. Patients with a history of PAF or AF on initial ECG and those who died during hospitalization were excluded.

Patients from one stroke center were first used as the derivation cohort to develop the PAF risk score. Then, patients from two other centers were used as the validation cohort to evaluate the score’s validity. We also compared our scoring system with the STAF; iPAB score; Fujii’s simple score; AF-ESUS score; Brown ESUS-AF score; and Age, Stroke Severity NIHSS >5 to Find AF (AS5F) score using the validation cohort’s data.131415161724 To facilitate these comparisons, data on transthoracic echocardiography, carotid ultrasonography, and carotid MR angiography were also collected in the validation cohort.

Clinical data collection and outcome variables

Demographic and clinical data

The following demographic and clinical data were collected: age, sex, medical history (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, coronary artery disease, prior ischemic stroke, arrhythmia or antiarrhythmic agent use, and heart failure), and NIHSS score at admission.

Cardiac examination

Heart rate, QTc (calculated using Bazett’s formula), and APC data were collected from 12-lead ECG performed at the time of admission.25 Transthoracic echocardiography data collected in the validation cohort included LA diameter, left ventricular hypertrophy, ejection fraction, and mitral valvular disease. LA diameter was measured at the parasternal level in M mode. Mitral valvular disease was classified as mitral regurgitation or mitral stenosis (mild, moderate, or severe).2627 Mitral regurgitation was detected using color Doppler echocardiography. Mitral stenosis was detected using two-dimensional echocardiography and pulsed Doppler recording of mitral flow velocity.

Carotid examination

Non-stenotic carotid plaques (≥3 mm in thickness and <50% arterial lumen stenosis) were evaluated using carotid ultrasonography and MR angiography in the validation cohort.28

Blood sampling

Blood sampling and plasma BNP measurements were performed within 24 hours of admission. As part of the routine laboratory workup, the D-dimer, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), and blood glucose levels were also measured during the same period.

Definition of MRI findings

MACIs and subcortical infarcts were determined based on MRI of the brain performed at admission. MACIs were defined as the presence of two or more acute ischemic cerebral lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging in at least two of the following three arterial territories: left anterior circulation, right anterior circulation, and bilateral posterior circulation.18 Subcortical infarcts were defined as acute symptomatic lesions located beneath the cerebral cortex on diffusion-weighted imaging.

Outcome variables

The primary outcome was detection of undiagnosed PAF from the time of admission to up to 2 years after discharge. Two or more neurologists performed comprehensive chart reviews to determine detection of PAF, which was documented based on 12-lead ECG, ECG monitoring, or 24-hour Holter ECG. Patients were grouped according to the presence or absence of PAF.

Scoring system

The scoring system was developed based on the beta coefficients from the logistic regression model. Point values were assigned to each risk factor by dividing its beta coefficient by the smallest beta coefficient and rounding to the nearest integer.29 In the derivation cohort, patients with a score of 3–5 had an AF detection rate of approximately 15%, which is comparable to the previously reported detection rate of 12%–15% within 1–2 years using insertable cardiac monitors in patients with cryptogenic stroke.103031 Accordingly, a score of 3–5 was defined as the moderate-risk grade, while scores of 0–2 and 6–10 were classified as low-risk and high-risk grades, respectively.

Statistical analyses

In the derivation cohort, clinical variables were compared between the PAF and no-PAF groups using the chi-square test, Mann–Whitney U test, and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to determine optimal cutoff values for age, BNP concentration, D-dimer concentration, HbA1c, NIHSS score, and QTc. Based on the results of the univariate analysis in the derivation cohort, we initially aimed to perform multivariate regression employing a full model. However, because of the limited number of patients with PAF, we additionally employed Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) to optimize variable selection, using the BNP level, APCs, NIHSS score, QTc, MACIs, D-dimer level, HbA1c level, age, and female sex as candidate variables. Multivariate logistic regression was used to calculate odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to estimate the associations between PAF and the clinical variables. The goodness of fit for the logistic regression model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. The Cochran–Armitage test for trend was used to assess the relationship between risk grades and PAF incidence. In the validation cohort, the cumulative incidence of PAF for each risk grade was analyzed and evaluated using Gray’s test. The areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) for the new scoring system and the STAF , Fujii’s simple score, iPAB score, AF-ESUS score, Brown ESUS-AF score, and AS5F score were compared using DeLong’s test. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.68 (Jichi Medical University, Shimotsuke, Japan).32 All p values of <0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

Development of the PAF prediction scoring system using the derivation cohort

The derivation cohort included 649 hospitalized patients. The median duration of hospitalization was 16 days. Of these, 508 continued follow-up in outpatient clinics after discharge, while 141 were transferred to other hospitals. The median follow-up overall after admission was 606 days. PAF was detected in 89 patients (13.7%). The median time to detection was 3 days (range, 0–413 days) (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). PAF was detected during hospitalization in 81 patients (9 using Holter ECG, 55 using ECG monitoring, and 17 using both). Detection occurred after discharge in eight patients (three using Holter ECG and five using 12-lead ECG). According to the TOAST classification,23 the derivation cohort included 234 patients with large artery atherosclerosis, 94 with cardioembolism (89 of which were PAF-related), 121 with small vessel occlusion, 37 with stroke of other determined etiology, and 163 with stroke of undetermined etiology.

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the derivation cohort according to group. Patients in the PAF group were older (p<0.001) and predominantly female (p=0.010), and they had a higher prevalence of diabetes mellitus (p=0.008), heart failure (p=0.017), and coronary artery disease (p=0.007). The NIHSS score (p<0.001), BNP concentration (p<0.001), D-dimer concentration (p<0.001), and HbA1c percentage (p=0.017) were all higher in the PAF group. In addition, the QTc interval was longer (p<0.001) and the presence of APCs (p<0.001) and MACIs (p<0.001) were higher in the PAF group. To ensure the applicability of the score to patients with unknown medical histories—such as those with impaired consciousness—diabetes mellitus, heart failure, and coronary artery disease were excluded from the multivariate logistic regression analysis. ROC curve analysis of variables that showed significant differences between the PAF and no-PAF groups identified the following optimal cutoff values: age of ≥74 years, BNP concentration of ≥56.2 pg/mL, D-dimer concentration of ≥1.17 µg/mL, HbA1c level of ≥5.9%, NIHSS score of ≥11, and QTc interval of ≥0.462 s. For practical use, the BNP cutoff value was rounded to 55.0 pg/mL, maintaining sensitivity and specificity of 92.1% and 67.5%, respectively—identical to the original values (92.1% and 68.4%, respectively). Likewise, the QTc cutoff value was adjusted from 0.462 s to 0.46 s, yielding a sensitivity of 50.6% and specificity of 74.3%, comparable to the original values at 0.462 s (50.6% and 76.9%, respectively).

These cutoff values were used in the multivariate logistic regression analysis of the derivation cohort, which identified a BNP concentration of ≥55 pg/mL, APCs, NIHSS score of ≥11, QTc of ≥0.46 s, and MACIs as significant independent risk factors for PAF. Advanced age, female sex, the D-dimer concentration, and the HbA1c level were not statistically significant (Table 2). The Hosmer–Lemeshow test confirmed adequate goodness of fit for the logistic regression model (p=0.194). Furthermore, model selection based on the AIC also identified a model consisting of the BNP concentration, APCs, NIHSS score, QTc, and MACIs as having the lowest AIC value (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement).

Development of the BANQMR score

Based on the beta coefficients of the independent risk factors, a new scoring system was developed (the BANQMR score: BNP, APCs, NIHSS score, QTc, and MACIs on MRI). The following points were assigned to each risk factor (measurements/determinations were performed at the time of admission): BNP concentration of ≥55 pg/mL=3 points; APCs=3 points; NIHSS score of ≥11=2 points; QTc of ≥0.46 s=1 point; and MACIs on diffusion-weighted imaging=1 point (Fig. 1). The total number of points determined the BANQMR score. These scores were then stratified into three risk grades based on AF detection rates in the derivation cohort: low (0–2 points), moderate (3–5 points), and high (6–10 points) (Fig. 2). The Cochran–Armitage test confirmed significant differences in risk between the three grades (p<0.001).

Validation and evaluation of the BANQMR score using the validation cohort

The validation cohort included 583 hospitalized patients. The median duration of hospitalization was 18 days. Of these patients, 383 continued follow-up in outpatient clinics after discharge, while 200 were transferred to other hospitals. The median follow-up overall after admission was 101 days. PAF was detected in 76 patients (13.0%). The median time to detection was 5 days (range, 0–720 days) (Supplementary Fig. 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). PAF was detected during hospitalization in 64 patients (15 using Holter ECG, 43 using ECG monitoring, and 6 using both). Detection after discharge occurred in 12 patients (2 using Holter ECG and 10 using 12-lead ECG). According to the TOAST classification,23 the validation cohort included 211 patients with large artery atherosclerosis, 82 with cardioembolism (76 of which were PAF-related), 69 with small vessel occlusion, 43 with stroke of other determined etiology, and 178 with stroke of undetermined etiology.

Supplementary Table 2 (in the online-only Data Supplement) shows the baseline characteristics of the validation cohort. In the validation cohort, logistic regression analysis confirmed that all five components of the BANQMR score—BNP concentration, APCs, NIHSS score, QTc, and MACIs—were independently and significantly associated with PAF (Supplementary Table 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). The proportions of patients considered low-, moderate-, and high-risk based on the BANQMR score in whom PAF was actually detected were 4.6%, 15.0%, and 53.4%, respectively (Fig. 2). The cumulative incidence function for each BANQMR risk grade showed that the risk of PAF increased across grades, with a significant result obtained by Gray’s test (p<0.001) (Fig. 3).

The AUC value of the BANQMR score in the validation cohort was 0.791 (95% CI, 0.735–0.847). According to DeLong’s test, this score outperformed all others evaluated. The AUC values for the other scores were as follows: STAF, 0.656; Fujii’s simple score, 0.693; iPAB score, 0.731; AS5F score, 0.625; AF-ESUS score, 0.598; and Brown ESUS-AF score, 0.589. The ROC curves for each scoring system are shown in Fig. 4. A comparison of the AUCs is shown in Table 3.

DISCUSSION

In this study, the BNP concentration, APCs, NIHSS score, QTc, and MACIs were retrospectively identified as independent risk factors for PAF, while advanced age, female sex, the D-dimer concentration, and the HbA1c level were not. Based on these parameters, we developed the BANQMR scoring system, which clinicians can use to identify patients with ischemic stroke at high risk of PAF shortly after admission, even in resource-limited settings where staffing and access to echocardiography may be constrained. Another significant advantage of this scoring system is that it does not require information on past medical history, making it applicable even to patients with impaired consciousness or other conditions in which medical history is unavailable. Furthermore, the BANQMR score demonstrated adequate predictive accuracy for PAF compared with several other conventional scoring systems. Risk stratification based on this score can guide subsequent monitoring and additional diagnostic testing.

Four of the five components of the BANQMR score—namely the BNP concentration, APCs, QTc, and MACIs—have been previously reported in cohort studies as being associated with AF.1819202122 Although the NIHSS score does not directly reflect the etiology of AF, AF is known to cause more severe strokes,33 which tend to present with more pronounced neurological symptoms and higher NIHSS scores.34 This suggests that the NIHSS score may serve as an indirect indicator of AF. In fact, along with the BNP concentration and APCs, the NIHSS score is widely incorporated into other scoring systems.131535 Thus, the BANQMR score, which incorporates these five parameters, can be considered a novel and valid tool that aligns with the findings of previous studies.

Unlike other studies,131724 we did not identify advanced age or female sex as independent risk factors. Although the QTc tends to be longer in older individuals and in women,36 the multivariate analysis that included these factors revealed that a QTc of ≥0.46 s was an independent risk factor. Both the HbA1c level and D-dimer concentration have been reported to be associated with AF.3738 However, because elevated HbA1c levels are associated with increased D-dimer concentrations,39 the interdependence between these two variables may have prevented their detection as independent factors in our multivariable analysis.

The BANQMR score was developed as a simple tool that could be used in a broader range of facilities by omitting the need for an echocardiographic examination. As a result, it does not include variables that reflect LA size. However, LA enlargement is a well-established factor associated with AF.40 Incorporating ECG findings that suggest LA enlargement—such as biphasic P waves in lead V1 or P mitrale in lead II41—may be a reasonable addition to the scoring system. Nevertheless, our analysis in the derivation cohort did not show significant differences in these ECG parameters between the PAF and no-PAF groups (data not shown). Given that ECG-based estimation of LA size is indirect and often inaccurate, we ultimately chose not to include these indicators in the BANQMR score.

The AS5F score, which is based solely on the NIHSS score and age, can be easily evaluated shortly after admission.24 However, its predictive value for PAF may decrease when applied >72 hours after admission.24 Not only is the BANQMR score more accurate than the AS5F score, but it also appears to be applicable out to 600 days and beyond (Fig. 3). The BANQMR score outperformed existing scores, although the performance of those scores in our validation cohort was lower than previously reported (Table 3). This discrepancy may be due to differences in validation methods and inclusion/exclusion criteria across the various scoring systems. For example, the STAF and Fujii’s simple score lack external validation, which may lead to overestimation of the AUC.42 Fujii’s simple score and the iPAB score exclude patients with dialysis or heart failure—conditions associated with elevated BNP levels and LA enlargement—whereas our study included such patients. We also excluded patients with AF on admission to avoid altering the BNP cutoff value and reducing the score’s sensitivity due to acute BNP elevation during AF,43 a step not taken in some other scoring systems. Finally, the lower proportion of patients with undetermined stroke etiology (30.5%) in our study than in the AF-ESUS or Brown-AF studies may have influenced the results.

Early placement of an insertable cardiac monitor for PAF detection in patients with cryptogenic stroke is more cost-effective than standard care.44 In our study, most cases of PAF were detected early after admission, particularly in the high-risk group (Fig. 3). Therefore, early PAF risk stratification using the BANQMR score may lead to more timely initiation of monitoring and treatment, and help allocate resources more effectively. Patients with ischemic stroke deemed to be moderate- or high-risk using the BANQMR score should undergo careful ECG monitoring during hospitalization. Even if PAF is not detected, these patients may benefit from placement of an insertable cardiac monitor or long-term Holter monitoring in an outpatient setting. Conversely, causes of stroke other than PAF should be sought in low-risk patients: transesophageal echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography, or cardiac MRI should be considered to identify other sources of emboli.4546

This study has several limitations. Those associated with its retrospective design are well known. Among other limitations, cardiac monitoring was implemented for varying durations and using various methods; some patients may not even have received adequate monitoring as an outpatient. Additionally, the N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) concentration was not measured. NT-proBNP has a longer half-life and is more stable than BNP.47 Although several scoring systems have included NT-proBNP,4849 we were unable to compare the BANQMR score with these systems. Furthermore, cases of ESUS were not specifically analyzed.50 The utility of the BANQMR score for these cases remains unknown, so comparison with the Brown ESUS-AF and AF-ESUS scores may not have been appropriate.1617 Nonetheless, this relatively large multicenter study also has several notable strengths. The BANQMR score targets a broad range of patients with ischemic stroke regardless of medical history, stroke subtype, or institutional resources, and it was externally validated, thereby supporting its strong applicability to real-world practice.

In conclusion, the BANQMR score is based on five independent risk factors and can be used to predict the risk of PAF in patients with ischemic stroke shortly after hospital admission. Further external validation studies are warranted to verify our results.
