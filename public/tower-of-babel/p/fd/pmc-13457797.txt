Point-of-care ultrasound as a frontline tool for suspected acute aortic dissection: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: André Timóteo Sapalo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute aortic dissection (AD) is a life-threatening cardiovascular emergency associated with high morbidity and mortality. Although computed tomography angiography (CTA) remains the reference diagnostic modality, rapid bedside assessment is often required in hemodynamically unstable patients. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has emerged as a potential adjunctive tool for early diagnosis and risk stratification. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the diagnostic performance and clinical utility of POCUS in patients with suspected acute AD.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines. Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for studies published between January 2005 and January 2025. Studies evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS in adult patients with suspected AD were included. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment using the QUADAS-2 tool. Pooled sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, diagnostic odds ratios (DOR), and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) analyses were calculated.ResultsEleven studies met the inclusion criteria, and ten contributed data to the quantitative synthesis. The pooled analysis demonstrated an overall sensitivity of 0.86 [99% confidence interval (CI): 0.80–0.90] and specificity of 0.84 (99% CI: 0.78–0.89) for bedside ultrasound in the detection of acute AD. Diagnostic performance was more favourable for Stanford type A disease than for distal dissections. Visualization of an intimal flap was the most specific sonographic finding, whereas pericardial effusion, aortic root enlargement, and acute aortic regurgitation provided important complementary information for clinical assessment. Evidence from recent perspective cohorts further supports the incorporation of POCUS into structured diagnostic pathways for patients with suspected acute aortic syndromes (AAS).ConclusionsBedside ultrasound represents a valuable component of the initial evaluation of suspected acute AD, particularly in emergency and critical care environments. Although confirmatory imaging remains mandatory for definitive diagnosis, POCUS can facilitate early recognition of AAS, support hemodynamic assessment, and help identify complications requiring urgent intervention.

Introduction

Acute aortic dissection (AD) is among the most serious cardiovascular emergencies encountered in clinical practice. Despite improvements in diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, delayed recognition continues to be associated with poor outcomes, especially in patients with Stanford type A dissection, where early disease progression may rapidly lead to catastrophic complications (1-4).

Acute AD predominantly affects older adults and occurs more frequently in males than in females. Data from the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection demonstrate that approximately 65–70% of cases occur in men, with a mean age at presentation ranging from 60 to 70 years (1,4). Type A dissection is more common than type B dissection and is associated with higher early mortality rates. Although AD predominantly affects elderly hypertensive patients, younger individuals may also develop the disease, particularly those with inherited connective tissue disorders such as Marfan syndrome and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (5,6).

AD results from disruption of the intimal layer of the aortic wall, allowing blood to penetrate the medial layer and create a false lumen. Several predisposing conditions contribute to structural weakening of the aortic wall and increase susceptibility to dissection. Systemic arterial hypertension remains the most common risk factor and is present in the majority of patients with AD. Chronic hypertension promotes medial degeneration, elastic fiber fragmentation, and increased mechanical stress on the aortic wall (1,6). Inherited connective tissue disorders, including Marfan syndrome and Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, are also strongly associated with AD because of abnormalities in extracellular matrix structure and vascular integrity. Additional important etiologies include bicuspid aortic valve, atherosclerotic disease, inflammatory vascular disorders, traumatic injury, and iatrogenic causes related to cardiovascular procedures or surgical interventions (5,7).

Current classification systems, including the DeBakey and Stanford classifications, play an important role in guiding clinical and surgical decision-making (6,8). Type A AD involves the ascending aorta, whereas type B AD does not involve the ascending aorta and may affect distal segments of the thoracic or abdominal aorta. In addition to anatomical classification, temporal classification is also clinically relevant, categorizing the disease into acute, subacute, and chronic phases according to symptom duration (1,5).

The clinical presentation of AD is highly variable and may range from incidental findings in oligosymptomatic patients to severe chest pain, organ malperfusion, hemodynamic instability, and cardiac arrest (9). Acute chest pain is the most common symptom and is classically described as abrupt, severe, tearing, or penetrating pain radiating to the back (10). However, atypical presentations may also occur, which frequently contributes to delayed diagnosis and increased mortality.

Although computed tomography angiography (CTA), transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide excellent accuracy for the diagnosis of AD, their use may be constrained by limited availability, logistical requirements, and the need for patient transfer (11,12). These challenges are particularly relevant in emergency settings involving hemodynamically unstable patients or healthcare facilities without immediate access to advanced cardiovascular imaging services (4,13).

Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) has emerged as an important bedside imaging tool for the early evaluation of critically ill patients in emergency departments. In suspected AD, POCUS may facilitate rapid diagnostic suspicion through the identification of direct findings such as intimal flaps and false lumen formation, as well as indirect signs including pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, and acute aortic regurgitation (11,14). Furthermore, recent prospective studies have demonstrated that integrating POCUS into diagnostic protocols may improve risk stratification and reduce time to diagnosis in acute aortic syndromes (AAS) (15-17).

Despite these advances, the diagnostic performance and clinical role of POCUS in AD remain incompletely defined, particularly regarding its sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional imaging modalities. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the available evidence regarding the diagnostic utility of POCUS in patients with suspected AD. This article is presented in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0750/rc).

Methods

Protocol registration

The review protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420261406307).

Eligibility criteria

Studies were considered eligible if they: (I) evaluated the use of POCUS for the diagnosis of AD; (II) reported diagnostic performance outcomes such as sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios, diagnostic odds ratios (DOR), or accuracy measures; and (III) involved adult patients evaluated in emergency departments, intensive care units, or critical care settings.

The exclusion criteria included case reports, editorials, letters to the editor, conference abstracts, narrative reviews, animal studies, pediatric studies, TEE-only studies, and studies lacking sufficient diagnostic accuracy data for analysis.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in the following electronic databases: Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search included studies published between January 2005 and January 2025. The last database search was performed in January 2025.

Additional records were identified through manual searches of websites (n=5), organizational resources (n=2), and citation screening (n=1). After eligibility assessment, none of these eight records met the predefined inclusion criteria and therefore were not included in either the qualitative or quantitative synthesis.

Search strategy

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords related to “point-of-care ultrasound”, “POCUS”, “ultrasonography”, and “aortic dissection” using Boolean operators (AND/OR). Search filters were applied to include studies involving human subjects and publications written in English. An example of the search strategy used in PubMed was as follows: (“point-of-care ultrasound” OR “POCUS” OR “ultrasonography”) AND (“aortic dissection” OR “acute aortic syndrome”). The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in the Appendix 1.

Selection process

Two independent reviewers (Gisele Mocelim Junkoski and Nicolas Gabriel) screened all retrieved titles and abstracts for eligibility according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Full-text articles were subsequently reviewed for final inclusion. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through consensus discussion. No automation tools or artificial intelligence-assisted screening software were used during the study selection process.

Data collection process

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers (Gisele Mocelim Junkoski and Nicolas Gabriel) using a standardized data extraction form. Extracted data were subsequently cross-checked for consistency and accuracy. Any discrepancies identified during the extraction process were resolved by consensus discussion between the reviewers.

Data items

The following variables were extracted from the included studies: study design, sample size, patient demographics, comorbidities, type of AD, POCUS findings, sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratios (PLRs), negative likelihood ratios (NLRs), DOR, and associated sonographic findings including intimal flap visualization, pericardial effusion, aortic root dilation, and aortic regurgitation. When data were incomplete or unclear, only the information explicitly reported in the original study was included in the analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality and risk of bias of the included studies were assessed using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool. Risk of bias assessment was independently conducted by two reviewers. Disagreements were resolved by consensus discussion.

Effect measures

The primary effect measures included pooled sensitivity, specificity, PLR, NLR, DOR, and summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve analysis with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Synthesis methods

Quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis were performed for studies providing sufficient diagnostic accuracy data. Statistical analyses included pooled sensitivity, specificity, positive and NLR, DOR, and SROC curve generation. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochran’s Q test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for heterogeneity analyses. Statistical analyses were conducted using Meta-DiSc software version 1.4. Studies lacking sufficient quantitative data were included only in the qualitative synthesis.

Reporting bias assessment

Publication bias and reporting bias could not be formally assessed due to the limited number of included studies and the heterogeneity of diagnostic accuracy outcomes across studies. This limitation is acknowledged in the discussion section.

Certainty assessment

The certainty of evidence was not formally assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, which is recognized as a limitation of the present systematic review and meta-analysis.

Statistical analysis

Pooled sensitivity, specificity, PLR, NLR, and DOR were calculated using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. SROC curves were generated to evaluate overall diagnostic performance. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05.

Results

Included studies

A total of 189 records were identified through database searching in Ovid MEDLINE, PubMed, EMBASE, Scopus, and Web of Science between January 2005 and January 2025. After removal of duplicate records (n=31), 158 studies underwent title and abstract screening, of which 107 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. Fifty-one full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 40 studies were excluded due to predefined exclusion criteria, including case reports, studies not performed in emergency settings, and TEE-only studies. Ultimately, 11 studies were included in the qualitative synthesis, while 10 studies providing sufficient diagnostic accuracy data were included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis), according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines (Figure 1).

Study characteristics

The included studies evaluated different applications of bedside ultrasound in patients with suspected AAS, ranging from diagnostic accuracy investigations to prospective protocol-based assessments. Collectively, these studies encompassed more than 8,000 patients evaluated in emergency and critical care settings.

One large meta-analysis synthesized evidence from sixteen studies and demonstrated that focused transthoracic ultrasound provides high specificity for the detection of acute AD, particularly Stanford type A disease. The prevalence of type A dissection was consistently higher than that of type B dissection across the included populations.

A retrospective cohort evaluating the combination of POCUS with AD risk stratification included patients with CT-confirmed Stanford type A dissection. Only examinations meeting predefined image-quality criteria were analyzed, highlighting the practical challenges associated with bedside ultrasound acquisition in emergency settings.

More recent prospective multicenter investigations evaluated integrated diagnostic pathways combining clinical risk assessment, D-dimer testing, and POCUS findings. These studies explored the role of ultrasound not only as a diagnostic tool but also as a component of structured decision-making algorithms designed to improve patient triage and risk stratification.

Similarly, the SPEED study investigated a simplified sonographic approach based on key ultrasound findings suggestive of dissection, including intimal flap visualization, pericardial effusion, and enlargement of the proximal aorta. The study demonstrated that targeted ultrasound assessment can identify most clinically relevant cases while maintaining high diagnostic performance in emergency departments.

Diagnostic accuracy findings

In the meta-analysis conducted by Mani et al. (19), focused transthoracic ultrasound demonstrated strong diagnostic performance for Stanford type A dissection, with pooled sensitivity and specificity estimates of 89% (95% CI: 82–94%) and 92% (95% CI: 88–95%), respectively. Diagnostic performance was lower for Stanford type B dissection, for which sensitivity decreased to 65% (95% CI: 45–80%), although specificity remained very high at 100% (95% CI: 69–100%).

Assessment of indirect sonographic findings also provided relevant diagnostic information. Acute aortic regurgitation showed pooled sensitivity and specificity values of 64% (95% CI: 5.2–98.2%) and 94% (95% CI: 92–96.1%), respectively. Thoracic aortic aneurysm demonstrated sensitivity of 92% (95% CI: 54–99.2%) and specificity of 87% (95% CI: 62–97%), whereas pericardial effusion yielded sensitivity and specificity estimates of 39% (95% CI: 33.8–45%) and 94% (95% CI: 92–95%), respectively. Overall, these findings indicate excellent specificity for both Stanford type A and type B dissection, while sensitivity appears substantially lower for distal aortic involvement and remains uncertain for intramural hematoma and penetrating aortic ulcer (19).

In the retrospective cohort reported by Aston et al. (20), 35 patients had ultrasound examinations of sufficient quality for analysis. Among these individuals, 30 (86%) demonstrated at least one sonographic feature compatible with AD, whereas 5 patients (14%) showed no diagnostic ultrasound abnormalities despite confirmed disease.

The PROFUNDUS study by Morello et al. (16), which enrolled 1,979 patients with suspected AAS, identified 176 confirmed cases (9%). Incorporation of POCUS into the diagnostic algorithm significantly improved risk classification compared with clinical assessment alone, resulting in a net reclassification improvement of 20% (P<0.001). Patients with positive ultrasound findings achieved a diagnosis substantially earlier than those with negative examinations (60 versus 118 minutes, P=0.042). Among 941 patients classified as low risk, the 30-day incidence of AAS was 0% (95% CI: 0–0.41%). The protocol achieved a rule-out efficiency of 48% (95% CI: 46–50%), avoided advanced imaging in 41% of patients, and increased rule-out efficiency to 54% when age-adjusted D-dimer thresholds were applied.

Similarly, the SPEED protocol evaluated by Gibbons et al. (17) demonstrated that 41 of 44 confirmed dissection cases exhibited at least one predefined sonographic abnormality. The protocol achieved a sensitivity of 93.2% (95% CI: 81.3–98.6%), specificity of 90.9% (95% CI: 89.2–92.5%), positive predictive value of 26.3% (95% CI: 19.6–33.9%), negative predictive value of 99.7% (95% CI: 99.2–100%), and overall diagnostic accuracy of 91% (95% CI: 89.3–92.5%).

Meta-analysis pooled results

The pooled analysis of these ten studies showed that POCUS had a sensitivity of 0.86 (99% CI: 0.80–0.90) and a specificity of 0.84 (99% CI: 0.78–0.89). There was no significant heterogeneity in sensitivity (P=0.68, I2=0.0%), whereas significant heterogeneity was observed in specificity (P=0.01, I2=58.86%) across these studies (Figure 2).

Echocardiographic/POCUS acoustic windows in suspected AD

POCUS evaluation of suspected AD relies on multiple acoustic windows that allow rapid bedside assessment of the thoracic aorta and associated cardiac complications. The parasternal long-axis view is particularly useful for evaluating the aortic root and identifying proximal intimal flaps, aortic root dilation, and associated aortic regurgitation. The parasternal short-axis view may provide complementary assessment of the aortic valve and ascending aorta.

The apical and subcostal windows are important for detecting pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, and global cardiac function, especially in hemodynamically unstable patients. In addition, the suprasternal notch view allows visualization of the aortic arch and proximal descending thoracic aorta, facilitating the identification of intimal flaps or luminal abnormalities involving these segments (Figure 4).

Discussion

The present systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrates that POCUS provides clinically meaningful diagnostic performance for the early bedside evaluation of acute AD, particularly in emergency and critical care settings. Rather than replacing established imaging modalities, these findings support the role of POCUS as an early triage and physiological assessment tool integrated into the diagnostic pathway of AAS.

CTA, TEE, and MRI remain the reference standards owing to their superior anatomical resolution and diagnostic accuracy (7,12). However, these modalities require patient stability and immediate access to specialized infrastructure. In contrast, POCUS allows rapid bedside imaging and immediate correlation with clinical findings, which is particularly relevant in unstable patients who cannot safely undergo transport to advanced imaging facilities.

Our analysis highlights the complementary value of combining direct and indirect sonographic findings. Direct visualization of an intimal flap or false lumen demonstrates high specificity for AD, whereas indirect signs, including pericardial effusion, cardiac tamponade, acute aortic regurgitation, and aortic root dilation, substantially contribute to early diagnostic suspicion and hemodynamic assessment (14,28). This integrated multi-view ultrasound approach aligns with the evolving concept of physiology-guided emergency imaging.

The influence of POCUS on time to diagnosis remains heterogeneous among studies. Earlier investigations suggested that bedside ultrasound could shorten diagnostic intervals and improve emergency workflow efficiency, whereas more recent analyses, including (34), did not demonstrate consistent reductions in diagnostic time. These discrepancies likely reflect differences in operator expertise, institutional imaging pathways, and patient clinical presentation. Importantly, even when diagnostic time is not significantly reduced, POCUS may still improve clinical decision-making by enabling immediate bedside risk stratification.

POCUS also appears particularly valuable in identifying life-threatening complications associated with type A dissection. Pericardial tamponade occurs in approximately 20–36% of patients and represents a major predictor of mortality (30-32). Acute aortic regurgitation, reported in up to 50–70% of cases, contributes significantly to rapid hemodynamic deterioration and may be promptly recognized during bedside evaluation (28). Early detection of these complications may directly influence resuscitation strategies, surgical prioritization, and overall patient outcomes.

Technological advances, including portable and handheld ultrasound systems, have further expanded the accessibility of cardiovascular imaging in emergency departments and intensive care settings (35). Recent reports have further demonstrated the value of POCUS for the rapid identification of critical cardiovascular complications and real-time bedside decision-making in severely ill patients, reinforcing its growing role across acute cardiovascular care pathways (36). These developments support broader implementation of POCUS as an initial screening modality in patients presenting with undifferentiated chest pain, shock, or atypical symptoms suggestive of AAS. Nevertheless, important limitations remain. Diagnostic accuracy is strongly operator-dependent, acoustic windows may be limited, and visualization of distal thoracic aortic segments, particularly in type B dissections, remains challenging.

Accordingly, negative POCUS findings should not exclude AD when clinical suspicion persists. Instead, POCUS should be interpreted as a complementary bedside modality integrated with clinical assessment and confirmatory advanced imaging techniques whenever feasible.

Several limitations of the present analysis should be acknowledged. Considerable methodological heterogeneity existed among included studies regarding ultrasound protocols, operator training, and diagnostic definitions, introducing potential spectrum bias and limiting comparability across studies. The predominance of observational single-center designs and relatively small sample sizes may contribute to publication bias and restrict external validity. Furthermore, the absence of standardized POCUS diagnostic algorithms highlights an important gap in current evidence.

Future prospective multicenter studies are required to establish standardized multi-view POCUS protocols, define competency-based operator training requirements, and evaluate outcome-based endpoints such as time to surgical intervention, mortality reduction, and healthcare resource utilization. Integration of emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence-assisted ultrasound interpretation and advanced handheld devices, may further improve diagnostic reproducibility and expand the role of bedside ultrasound in AAS management.

In summary, POCUS demonstrates acceptable diagnostic accuracy and substantial clinical utility as a rapid bedside adjunct in suspected acute AD, particularly among hemodynamically unstable patients. These findings support a shift toward integrated bedside evaluation, in which POCUS functions as an early decision-support tool guiding timely confirmatory imaging and definitive management rather than serving as a standalone diagnostic modality.

Conclusions

POCUS demonstrates acceptable diagnostic accuracy and meaningful clinical utility as a rapid bedside adjunct in the evaluation of suspected acute AD, particularly in emergency and critical care settings involving hemodynamically unstable patients. Although CTA and TEE remain the reference diagnostic modalities, POCUS may facilitate early recognition of AAS through immediate bedside assessment and identification of both direct and indirect sonographic findings.

Integration of a multi-view POCUS examination into the initial clinical evaluation may support early risk stratification, assist in detecting life-threatening complications such as pericardial tamponade and acute aortic regurgitation, and contribute to more efficient diagnostic pathways. However, due to operator dependence and limited visualization of distal aortic segments, POCUS should not be used as a standalone diagnostic tool. Negative findings do not exclude AD and must be followed by confirmatory advanced imaging when clinical suspicion persists.

Overall, current evidence supports POCUS as an important complementary modality within a multimodal diagnostic strategy for acute AD. Future prospective multicenter studies are warranted to establish standardized protocols, training requirements, and outcome-based validation of POCUS-guided diagnostic algorithms.
