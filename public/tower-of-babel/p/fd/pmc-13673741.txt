Introduction

Aplasia cutis congenita (ACC) is a rare developmental disorder with an estimated incidence of 0.5–1 per 10,000 live births [1]. The pathogenesis of scalp defects involves functional loss of the KCTD1/KCTD15 complex in neural crest cells [2], potentially associated with genetic factors, intrauterine infections, vascular abnormalities, or drug exposure. Clinically, ACC presents as congenital absence or hypoplasia of the epidermis, dermis, and even subcutaneous tissues (including muscle and bone), most commonly affecting the scalp and limbs [3]. The defect depth typically ranges from 0.5 to 1.0 cm, with severe cases potentially involving the skull or dura mater, leading to meningocele [1].

While small lesions often demonstrate spontaneous healing tendencies, extensive or deep defects are prone to complications such as infection, scarring, and functional impairment, necessitating active intervention. Currently, no standardized treatment protocol exists. Literature suggests that ACC management should focus on regular dressing changes, infection prevention, maintenance of a moist wound environment, and promotion of epithelialization [4]. Recent advances in moist wound healing theory have demonstrated significant advantages in chronic wound management. By simulating physiological conditions, this approach accelerates cell migration and tissue regeneration while avoiding the infection risks associated with traditional dry healing under scabs [5–7].

In April 2024, our department treated a male newborn at 38⁺5 weeks of gestation who was small for gestational age and had agenesis of the corpus callosum (ACC). The ACC measured 3.0 × 2.0 × 0.2 cm (length × width × depth) and presented with potential skull exposure. Adopting moist wound healing principles, we implemented a staged care protocol featuring antimicrobial moisturization and granulation promotion, combined with systemic support and pain management. Complete epithelialization without scabbing was achieved within 12 days. This case provides novel insights into neonatal ACC management, as detailed below.

Case presentation

A male newborn of Han Chinese ethnicity, aged 57 min, was admitted to neonatology department due to the presence of a scalp skin defect noted shortly after birth. The infant was G5P4 to the mother, weight was 2480 g, Apgar scores of 10 at 1, 5, and 10 min. The neonate maintained stable vital signs (T: 36.8 °C, HR: 132, RR: 40, SpO₂: 98%) and normal lab results (WBC: 11.2 × 10⁹/L, Hb: 165 g/L, Plt: 218 × 10⁹/L, CRP < 1 mg/L, glucose 4.2 mmol/L).

Immediate postnatal examination revealed two symmetrical full-thickness scalp defects measuring 3.0 × 2.0 × 0.2 cm (length × width × depth) were identified on the neonatal vertex. The lesions exhibited well-demarcated borders with a bright red appearance. The underlying skull was intact, with no active bleeding or exudate. The defects were depressed relative to the surrounding skin and devoid of hair growth (Fig. 1A). Physical exam was unremarkable. Wound cultures were negative, and neuroimaging showed no abnormalities. Following these diagnoses—aplasia cutis congenita (Type II) and full-term small-for-gestational-age status—the infant was managed with incubator isolation and a multidisciplinary approach involving the neonatology, plastic surgery, and wound care teams. Initial and inflammatory phase management

Upon initial evaluation, two symmetrical full-thickness scalp defects measuring 3.0 × 2.0 × 0.2 cm were identified on the neonatal vertex. The elliptical lesions presented with well-demarcated erythematous borders and complete absence of hair follicles, with the wound base depressed approximately 0.5 cm below the surrounding skin plane. The wound bed displayed uniform erythema, absent of active hemorrhage (Fig. 1A). According to the classification criteria [8], this case is assessed as ACC Type II.

The primary goals during this phase were wound protection and infection prevention. Hair around the wound was shaved to avoid contamination. The wound was gently irrigated with 0.9% NaCl to remove debris and exudate. After iodine disinfection and deiodination of surrounding skin, a double-layer dressing was applied: an inner lipid-colloid silver sulfate dressing (UrgoTul Silver®, Urgo Medical) was precisely fitted to the wound, and an outer contoured silicone foam (Mepilex Border®, Mölnlycke) with wedge-shaped edges for anatomical adherence, changed every 48 ± 2 h unless compromised. Pain assessment using the Neonatal Infant Pain Scale (NIPS) [9] yielded a baseline score of 6/7. Standardized procedures are available in Supplementary Material S1.

By day 2, wound characteristics remained stable with defined erythematous borders and 2 cm mild erythema/edema. Wound secretions bacterial cultures showed negative; labs showed mild inflammation: WBC 11.5 × 10⁹/L, increased by 0.3 × 10⁹/L from admission; CRP 5 mg/L, increased by 4 mg/L from baseline. By day 4, exudate resolved with 0.5 mm epithelial migration (Fig. 1B). NIPS scores improved from 6 to 3 spontaneously during dressing changes.

Proliferative phase management

During the proliferative phase, the wound showed significant improvement, reducing to 2.5 × 1.8 × 0.1 cm with complete granular, erythematous tissue coverage. Wound preparation involved gentle irrigation with 0.9% NaCl followed by daily application of recombinant bovine basic fibroblast growth factor. The “sandwich dressing” technique comprised: an inner silver lipid colloid layer (UrgoTul Silver®, Urgo Medical) for moisture regulation and antimicrobial protection; a middle foam layer (Mepilex Lite®, Mölnlycke) for exudate absorption; and an outer transparent film dressing (Tegaderm™, 3 M) for secure fixation. Standardized procedures are available in Supplementary Material S1. This phase was characterized by a 28% reduction in wound area relative to the inflammatory phase, diminished exudate, resolved granulation tissue edema, and the first signs of epithelialization. Epithelial migration progressed from 2 mm by day 7 (Fig. 1C) to 1.2 mm by day 10. Palpation confirmed complete cranial bone coverage, while cultures remained negative and inflammatory markers normalized, labs showed: WBC 9.8 × 10⁹/L, CRP 2 mg/L. NIPS scores of 2 during dressing changes and 0 at rest.

Maturation phase and follow-up management

The maturation phase protocol was implemented upon achieving complete epithelialization with 78% wound area reduction, focusing on scar prevention and functional restoration through weekly 0.9% saline irrigation followed by silicone gel sheet application (Dermatix® Ultra, 12 h/day) and protective transparent film dressing (Tegaderm™). Standardized procedures are available in Supplementary Material S1. After management, the wound fully epithelialized, reducing to 0.8 × 0.5 cm by day 12, transitioning from a 3 mm residual defect to smooth pink tissue with 2 mm translucent borders. Palpation showed soft, non-indurated tissue with minimal hyperpigmentation and mild depression (Fig. 1D). NIPS remained 0 throughout. Post-discharge care included twice-daily silicone gel massage, strict UV protection, and biweekly telemedicine assessments of skin elasticity and pigmentation, demonstrating excellent outcomes with zero score of NIPS, minimal scar formation, and 100% parental compliance.

Quality management

To ensure methodological rigor and reproducibility, we implemented a comprehensive standardized assessment protocol. First, Daily wound dimensions were precisely measured using sterile measuring tools. Second, serial photographic documentation was obtained under consistent, controlled lighting conditions. Third, all evaluations were independently performed by two trained clinicians, demonstrating excellent inter-rater reliability. Finally, all data were systematically recorded using predefined case report forms.

Systemic management

The newborn weighed 2480 g, below the 10th percentile for gestational age, and diagnosed as term SAG. The score of Neonatal Nutritional Risk Screening Tool [10] was 3 (moderate risk). To support wound healing's metabolic demands, combined nutritional therapy was initiated within 24 h after birth, delivering 120 kcal/kg/day through exclusive breastfeeding with concurrent oral immune therapy and comprehensive parenteral nutrition providing 1.5 g/kg/day amino acids, 1.0 g/kg/day lipids, and 6–8 mg/kg/min glucose, while maintaining positive nitrogen balance through urea excretion monitoring. On day 3, WBC transiently elevated to 9.2–11.8 × 10⁹/L before normalizing, while CRP peaked at 5 mg/L then decreased to < 1 mg/L. Three consecutive wound cultures were negative. Neurobehavioral assessment [11] indicated normal development.

Pain management during dressing changes for the initial NIPS score of 6 was addressed through a multimodal approach. Kangaroo care was implemented for at least 60 min pre-procedure, followed by intravenous administration of phenobarbital sodium at 2.5 mg/kg 30 min before the procedure. Post-procedure, an infant prone positioning device (Patent No. ZL202121165970) was used with maternal voice stimulation, achieving a pain-free state with NIPS score of 0. Weekly psychological education on scar prevention was provided to parents, with State-Trait Anxiety Inventory [12] monitoring showing significant reduction in anxiety levels from 52 ± 6 pre-intervention to 32 ± 4 post-intervention.

Discussion

The primary therapeutic objectives for skin defects, regardless of their morphology or etiology, focus on restoring both the mechanical barrier and immunoprotected functions of the affected area, while minimizing risks of fluid loss or organ exposure [13]. Currently, no standardized guidelines exist for managing congenital scalp defects. Conventional approaches predominantly utilize traditional agents [14], such as Vaseline dressing [15], local antibiotic creams [4, 16], epidermal growth factor and Kangfuxin [17]. While these methods facilitate wound healing, they frequently result in suboptimal eschar formation beneath the scab. Eschar-mediated healing poses multiple challenges, extended convalescence (typically two-to-three weeks), secondary trauma from eschar separation, heightened infection potential, and hypoxia-compromised reepithelialization promoting scar hypertrophy. Maintaining a moist wound environment promotes accelerated healing and enhances tissue regeneration quality [6]. The case highlights three key determinants of neonatal wound healing including phased wound evaluation, evidence-based dressing selection, and integrated systemic support.

Moist wound healing facilitates autolytic debridement, alleviates pain, minimizes scarring, activates collagen synthesis, promotes keratinocyte migration across the wound surface, and sustains the presence and function of nutrients, growth factors, and soluble mediators in the wound microenvironment. Wound dressings can be utilized to create, maintain, and control a moist environment for healing [7, 18]. Moist wound dressings can be divided into films, foams, hydrocolloids, hydrogels, and alginates [6]. Different wound dressings have varying therapeutic effects and should be selected based on the specific healing phase of the wound.

During the inflammatory phase, silver lipid-colloid dressings demonstrate effective antimicrobial properties through sustained silver sulfate ion release [19], inhibiting bacterial proliferation while maintaining optimal wound hydration via hydrogel-tissue interaction that preserves oxygen exchange. For minimal exudate, polyurethane foam layers provide balanced moisture retention. In the proliferative phase, thinner foam dressings are employed to reduce localized pressure and minimize maceration risks due to their enhanced breathability, preventing epithelial trauma during dressing changes [20]. The maturation phase prioritizes scar prevention using silicone gel dressings that hydrate the wound surface and reduce frictional forces to inhibit scar formation and hyperpigmentation [21]. Throughout all healing stages, a protective 3M transparent film serves as the outer layer to safeguard delicate epithelial tissue.

Under moist conditions, epithelial cells demonstrate enhanced migratory capacity and more efficient wound reepithelialization compared to dry environments. The presence of moisture facilitates rapid keratinocyte movement across the wound surface, while in dry conditions these cells are forced to migrate beneath a hardened scab [6]. An ideal wound dressing should fulfill multiple functions: protecting the injured area, maintaining optimal moisture levels, preserving appropriate wound temperature to support tissue repair, while ensuring easy application and removal [22]. This case's positive outcome highlights the importance of applying moisture-balancing dressings according to moist wound healing principles.

We acknowledge that our pain management protocol differs from standard neonatal analgesia approaches [23]; however, phenobarbital was chosen primarily for seizure prevention given the extensive scalp defect and potential neurological risks of ACC Type II, while kangaroo care served as an adjunctive non-pharmacological comfort measure consistent with standard guidelines.

Conclusion

Our study introduces a biologically informed, phase-adaptive management strategy incorporating scientifically optimized dressing combinations. This approach demonstrates three significant advantages: it circumvents eschar formation, enhances healing quality and efficiency, and improves the treatment experience for pediatric patients. While the advanced dressings used may be costly and less accessible in low-resource settings, affordable alternatives adhering to moist wound healing principles (e.g., petrolatum gauze) are acceptable. The key message is the phased approach, not specific brands, underscoring the protocol's potential for widespread adoption in complex wound management.
