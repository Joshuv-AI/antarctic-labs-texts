Inactivation of the leukemia inhibitory factor/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 pathway: a pivotal mechanism in adenomyosis-related embryo implantation failure
Authors: Qianru Dou, Zhengjie Han, Wenxiao Shao, Yongli Liu, Shenghui Yao, Yingying Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdenomyosis (AM) is frequently associated with compromised embryo implantation. The leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling pathway is crucial for endometrial receptivity; however, its mechanistic role in AM-related implantation failure remains insufficiently elucidated. This study aimed to investigate the inactivation mechanism of the LIF/STAT3 pathway in AM and to evaluate a novel nanotherapeutic strategy for restoring implantation capacity.MethodsClinical endometrial samples from AM patients and controls were analyzed. A three-dimensional (3D) co-culture system simulating the endometrial microenvironment was established. Interventions were performed using a tofacitinib-loaded biomimetic nanogel (Tofa-NG). Comprehensive analyses included bulk RNA Sequencing (RNA-seq), immunohistochemistry (IHC), RT-qPCR, proteomics, inflammatory cytokine profiling, and metabolomics to dissect the pathological links.ResultsThe LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway was significantly downregulated in the endometrium of AM patients, correlating directly with implantation failure. Pathologically elevated inflammatory cytokines suppressed LIF expression via NF-κB pathway activation, exacerbating the inflammatory microenvironment. Metabolomic profiling revealed a strong association between LIF/STAT3 pathway inactivation and aberrant cellular energy metabolism. The engineered Tofa-NG facilitated targeted drug delivery, effectively mitigated local inflammation, and successfully reactivated the LIF/STAT3 pathway. Consequently, this intervention significantly improved the embryo implantation success rate in the experimental model.ConclusionThis study identifies the inactivation of the LIF/STAT3 pathway as a central mechanism underlying embryo implantation defects in AM, intricately linked to chronic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation. The Tofa-NG strategy demonstrates promising therapeutic potential by rectifying the signaling deficit and ameliorating the endometrial microenvironment. These findings provide a novel theoretical foundation for developing targeted, personalized treatments for infertility associated with AM.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04505-z.

Introduction

Adenomyosis (AM) is a common and complex gynecological disorder [1], characterized primarily by the infiltration of endometrial tissue into the myometrium, resulting in chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, abnormal uterine bleeding, and infertility [2, 3]. Although its pathogenesis remains incompletely understood, AM is closely associated with aberrant estrogen levels [4–6]. In recent years, with advances in reproductive medicine, attention has increasingly focused on the impact of AM on female fertility [7]. Studies have demonstrated that patients with AM exhibit significantly reduced embryo implantation rates, which may be attributable to impaired endometrial receptivity [8]. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms by which AM affects embryo implantation is of critical importance for improving reproductive outcomes in affected individuals.

Among the various factors regulating endometrial receptivity, leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) is recognized as a key signaling molecule [9]. Through its receptor LIFR and downstream effector signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), LIF plays an essential role in the transition of the endometrium to the receptive phase, embryo adhesion, and immune tolerance [10]. Under physiological conditions, LIF signaling exhibits dynamic modulation during embryo implantation to support proper implantation and development [11]. However, in patients with AM, LIF expression is generally downregulated and is accompanied by an activated inflammatory microenvironment, including upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α [12]. These cytokines further suppress LIF expression and activity via pathways such as NF-κB, thereby potentially contributing to implantation failure [13].

Traditional research methods have predominantly relied on two-dimensional (2D) cell culture systems; however, such approaches fail to replicate the three-dimensional (3D) architecture and complex signaling interactions present in physiological conditions [14, 15]. This limitation has hindered a deeper understanding of the interactions among the endometrium, myometrium, and embryo. Therefore, to better investigate how LIF-axis inactivation affects the embryo implantation process, it is essential to establish a more physiologically relevant 3D co-culture system that mimics the pathological environment. Concurrently, integrating clinical sample analysis enables a more comprehensive examination of the relationship between LIF signaling dysregulation and embryo implantation failure. This combined approach provides a powerful tool for in-depth exploration of the mechanisms underlying AM.

In recent years, biomimetic intelligent nanomaterials have been increasingly applied in the medical field. Among them, charge-regulated nanogels (NGs), owing to their excellent biocompatibility and three-dimensional network structure, have demonstrated remarkable potential in pathological microenvironment modulation by enabling efficient drug encapsulation, sustained release, and targeted delivery [16, 17]. Among candidate drugs, tofacitinib (Tofa), a well-established JAK inhibitor, has been proven effective in reducing proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in various chronic inflammatory diseases, thereby ameliorating the pathological microenvironment [18, 19]. Theoretically, Tofa may alleviate endometrial damage induced by immune activation in AM and potentially reactivate LIF/STAT3 signaling. However, systemic administration is limited by the need for high doses, insufficient targeting, and the risk of adverse effects, which constrains its clinical translation in reproductive disorders [20]. Tofacitinib-loaded biomimetic nanogel (Tofa-NG) effectively addresses these limitations by enhancing local drug accumulation and retention time, thereby enabling low-dose targeted delivery. This strategy suppresses excessive activation of the NF-κB pathway at the source and reverses LIF downregulation, holding promise as a novel and translationally viable approach to improve embryo implantation failure.

This study aimed to systematically elucidate the mechanism by which LIF axis inactivation leads to embryo implantation failure by integrating clinical sample analysis, development of a 3D co-culture system, and intervention with Tofa-NG. Specifically, we employed an advanced 3D co-culture model to simulate the in vivo interactions among the endometrium, myometrium, and embryo, and paired this with clinical data to delineate the link between LIF signaling dysregulation and implantation failure. In addition, we evaluated the therapeutic potential of Tofa-NG to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment and restore normal embryo implantation capacity. Collectively, this research not only contributes to a deeper understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of AM but also lays a theoretical foundation for the development of individualized therapies, offering new hope and therapeutic options for patients with infertility.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

During the mid-luteal phase (7 days post-ovulation), endometrial tissue samples were collected from 30 patients diagnosed with AM and 30 healthy controls (Table S1). All participants were women of reproductive age and provided written informed consent before sampling (Ethical Approval Number: No. Xyyll [2025] 167). Sample collection was conducted strictly in accordance with protocols approved by the institutional ethics committee. Endometrial tissues were obtained using hysteroscopy-guided biopsy, suction catheter sampling, or from uterine specimens collected during hysterectomy, were immediately preserved in liquid nitrogen to maintain tissue integrity and viability. For both the patient and control groups, detailed clinical symptom scores were recorded, including visual analogue scale (VAS) scores for pain, menstrual volume assessment, and standardized ultrasound measurements of uterine volume. In addition, blood samples were collected to assess hormone levels, including estradiol (E2, PE223, Beyotime Biotechnology), progesterone (P, progesterone, Beyotime Biotechnology), and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH, Elabscience). All biochemical parameters were measured in the same laboratory using standardized assay kits to ensure data comparability and reliability.

Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and analysis based on patient-derived tissues

Endometrial tissues from patients with AM (n = 3) and healthy controls (n = 3) were subjected to RNA extraction followed by RNA-seq. Libraries were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150 bp single-end or paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled with FastQC (v0.11.9), and adaptor sequences and low-quality reads were removed with Trimmomatic (v0.39). Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), and gene quantification was performed with featureCounts (v2.0.1). The resulting expression matrix was normalized and analyzed for differential expression using DESeq2 (v1.38.3), with selection criteria set at |log2FC| ≥ 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on variance-stabilized data using the FactoMineR package (v2.8) in R to visualize transcriptomic heterogeneity across samples.

Volcano plots and Biplot analyses were employed to illustrate the global distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and to identify dominant expression directions, respectively. DEG distribution plots were generated with ggplot2 (v3.4.4), with upregulated and downregulated genes color-coded accordingly. Biplots integrated PCA loading matrices with feature gene vector arrows to highlight key genes contributing to sample separation. Feature gene ranking was based on a composite Rank Score calculated from log2FC and mean expression level, and visualized within the ClusterProfiler framework (v4.8.3). To further interpret the biological significance of DEGs, a multi-level functional enrichment analysis was performed. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment was performed using ClusterProfiler across three categories: biological processes (BP), cellular components (CC), and molecular functions (MF), with significance defined as padj < 0.05. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed using pathview (v1.40.0) in conjunction with the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database. Additionally, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA, v4.3.2) was conducted using KEGG and WikiPathways gene sets from MSigDB (v2023.1), applying a weighted Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with a minimum gene set size of 15 and a significance threshold of FDR < 0.25.

RNA-seq and analysis of the tofa-NG intervention model

Three independent biological replicates were included for each group: the Control group (untreated) and the Tofa group (treated with biomimetic nanogel). Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol reagent (15596026, Invitrogen, USA) after washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). RNA purity and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), with samples considered acceptable if the RNA integrity number (RIN) exceeded 7.0. Following rRNA depletion and mRNA enrichment using the NEBNext® Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB, USA), libraries were prepared with the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (E7530L, NEB, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After quality control, paired-end sequencing (PE150) was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, yielding an average sequencing depth of approximately 6 Gb of clean reads per sample. Raw reads were processed using fastp (v0.23.2) to remove adapters and low-quality sequences. Clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38/hg38) using HISAT2 (v2.2.1), with mapping rates exceeding 95% for all samples. Read counts at the gene level were quantified using FeatureCounts (v2.0.3), and differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.38.3, R/Bioconductor). Genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Identified DEGs were subjected to PCA, partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and functional enrichment analysis.

PCA was performed using the R packages FactoMineR (v2.8) and factoextra (v1.0.7) to visualize overall differences in transcriptomic distributions between the two groups. Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated with ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and pheatmap (v1.0.12), respectively. The PLS-DA model was constructed using the ropls package (v1.28.2), and the variable importance in projection (VIP) score was calculated for each gene to identify key contributing factors. Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.8.1). GO analysis included three categories: BP, CC, and MF. KEGG pathway analysis was performed using the org.Hs.eg.db annotation database (v3.16.0). GSEA was conducted with gene sets from MSigDB (v2023.1.Hs), employing 1,000 permutations to compute normalized enrichment scores (NES), p-values, and FDR values. Significance thresholds were set at FDR < 0.25 and p < 0.05. Enrichment results were visualized using the enrichplot and pathview packages.

Molecular analysis of the LIF pathway

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to assess the expression of LIF, LIFR, and p-STAT3 in tissue samples using 4-µm paraffin-embedded sections. After incubation at 60 °C for 20 min, the sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was conducted in 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) by microwave heating for 15 min. The sections were then incubated in 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Following this step, the sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against LIF, LIFR, and p-STAT3. The next day, the sections were washed three times with PBS (5 min each), incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min, developed with DAB, and counterstained with hematoxylin. Staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells were independently evaluated by two pathologists for quantitative scoring.

RT-qPCR was conducted to measure the mRNA expression levels of LIF, LIFR, and p-STAT3. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent, and RNA purity and concentration were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. cDNA synthesis was performed using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit. RT-qPCR reactions were carried out with SYBR Green PCR Master Mix and gene-specific primers. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the ΔΔCt method with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as the internal reference.

Protein expression levels were determined by Western blot (WB) analysis. Proteins were extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA lysis buffer, incubated on ice for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min to collect the supernatant. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked in 5% BSA for 1 h and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: LIF (ab138002, Abcam), p-STAT3 (ab68153, Abcam), ICAM-1 (ab282575, Abcam), MUC1 (ab109185, Abcam), LAMA5 (ab184330, Abcam), TLR4 (ab22048, Abcam), Complex I (ab110245, Abcam), Complex II (ab110410, Abcam), Complex IV (ab109863, Abcam), SOD1 (ab308181, Abcam), β-actin (ab110410, Abcam), and p-p65 (80979-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). After washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence detection kit, and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Analysis of N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification regulatory mechanism

To elucidate the epigenetic basis of LIF downregulation, this study further investigated the role of m6A modification in AM, focusing specifically on METTL3’s impact on LIF mRNA stability. First, RNA immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (RIP-qPCR) was performed to verify the direct binding between METTL3 and LIF mRNA using a METTL3-specific antibody (ab195352, Abcam). Second, methylated RNA immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (MeRIP-qPCR) was conducted to detect changes in the m6A modification level of LIF transcripts in endometrial tissues and human endometrial stromal cells (HESCs) using the StarLighter m6A RNA Immunoprecipitation Kit (FS-C1005-01, Beijing Qihengxing Biotechnology). Third, after blocking nascent transcription with actinomycin D (A4262, Sigma-Aldrich), the effects of METTL3 knockdown or overexpression on the half-life of LIF mRNA were assessed. Finally, dual-luciferase reporter assays were performed to validate the regulatory role of potential functional m6A sites within LIF mRNA in controlling its expression. These experiments collectively elucidated the mechanism by which METTL3 modulates LIF mRNA stability via m6A modification and identified a potential molecular target for therapeutic intervention in AM-associated implantation failure.

Isolation of exosomes

Peripheral blood samples from patients were collected into anticoagulant-containing tubes and immediately subjected to low-speed centrifugation to remove cells and cellular debris. The resulting supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube and centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C to eliminate residual debris and large extracellular vesicles. The samples were then ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for at least 70 min to pellet the exosomes. To enhance purity, the pellet was resuspended in PBS and subjected to an additional round of ultracentrifugation. Ultracentrifugation is a widely used standard technique for exosome isolation, capable of efficiently recovering vesicles ranging in diameter from 30 to 150 nm.

Quantitative proteomic analysis of exosomes

Exosomal protein samples from the Normal group (n = 3) and the AM group (n = 3) were desalted using C18 columns, and the resulting peptides were loaded onto a nano-liquid chromatography system (e.g., Thermo EASY-nLC 1200) coupled to a high-resolution Orbitrap mass spectrometer (e.g., Q Exactive HF-X). Data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) mode, with an MS1 resolution of 60,000 over a scan range of 350-1,600 m/z and an MS2 resolution of 15,000. Mass spectra were searched and quantified using MaxQuant software (v1.6.17.0) against the UniProt_Human database (release 2022.12). Search parameters were set as follows: enzyme specificity—Trypsin, allowing up to two missed cleavages; fixed modification—carbamidomethylation of cysteine; variable modifications—N-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation. The FDR was controlled at 1%. Relative protein quantification was based on label-free quantification (LFQ) intensities or intensity-based absolute quantification (iBAQ) values. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the criteria |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05, and significantly altered proteins were identified.

LFQ intensity values were log2-transformed and imported into R (v4.2.2) for downstream statistical analysis. PCA was performed using the FactoMineR and factoextra packages to assess global variation and group separation based on proteomic profiles. Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the AM and Normal groups were identified using the limma package, with a selection threshold of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05. Volcano plots and hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated using the ggplot2 and pheatmap packages, respectively, to visualize expression patterns and sample clustering.

The list of DEPs was converted to Entrez IDs and subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package (v4.6.2), with the organism set as “Homo sapiens.” Enrichment significance was defined as padj < 0.05 using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction method. GO analysis encompassed three main categories: BP, MF, and CC. KEGG pathway enrichment results were visualized using bar plots and bubble plots generated with enrichplot and ggplot2.

Targeted metabolomics data analysis

Raw metabolite peak area data from the AM group (n = 6) and the Control group (n = 6) were first subjected to peak detection, alignment, and noise reduction. Subsequent analyses were performed using the MetaboAnalystR platform (v3.2). Metabolite signals were log2-transformed and Pareto-scaled to reduce the influence of extreme values and enhance the detection of intergroup differences.

PCA and PLS-DA: Normalized metabolite data were imported into the FactoMineR and ropls (v1.28.2) packages for PCA and PLS-DA. PCA was used to preliminarily assess overall metabolic differences and sample distribution trends between groups, while PLS-DA was employed to construct a discriminative model and calculate model performance metrics (R2X, R2Y, and Q2). Model robustness and the risk of overfitting were evaluated using a 2,000-time permutation test, with p < 0.05 indicating statistical reliability.

Differential Metabolite Selection and VIP Scoring: VIP scores from the PLS-DA model were used to assess each metabolite’s contribution to group separation. Differential metabolites were identified based on both statistical significance (FDR < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg correction) and biological relevance (|log2FC| ≥ 1). Selected metabolites were visualized using volcano plots (EnhancedVolcano package) and heatmaps (pheatmap package).

Metabolic Pathway Enrichment Analysis (Small Molecule Pathway Database [SMPDB]/KEGG): Differential metabolites were annotated with HMDB or KEGG IDs and uploaded to the MetaboAnalyst platform for pathway enrichment analysis using the SMPDB. Significance was evaluated via hypergeometric testing, with pathways showing FDR < 0.05 considered significantly enriched. Results were visualized using bar plots or bubble plots. In parallel, KEGG pathway enrichment was performed using the clusterProfiler package (v4.8.1), with the enricher() function used for hypergeometric testing and pathway visualization.

Synthesis and characterization of Tofa-NG

To synthesize a pH-responsive nanogel capable of achieving targeted drug release within the uterine microenvironment, polyacrylic acid (PAA, 147230, Sigma-Aldrich) and chitosan (CS, 448869, Sigma-Aldrich) were employed as the primary carrier materials. First, PAA nanoparticles containing carboxyl groups were synthesized via emulsion polymerization in an aqueous phase. During synthesis, the reaction temperature was maintained at 70 °C, and ammonium persulfate was used as the initiator to obtain nanoparticles with uniform size distribution. These PAA nanoparticles were subsequently mixed with a CS solution, where electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding enabled CS deposition on the PAA surface to form a polyelectrolyte complex. To achieve synergistic carboxyl-amine charge modulation, the ratio of CS to PAA was adjusted to optimize the surface charge properties of the nanoparticles.

Next, the synthesized nanogel was used for drug loading, with Tofa (PZ0017, Sigma-Aldrich) selected as the model drug. To ensure efficient loading, Tofa was dissolved in ethanol and added dropwise to the CS-coated nanogel suspension, followed by stirring and ultrasonication to facilitate its incorporation into the nanogel matrix. After drug loading, the final nanogel formulation was obtained through lyophilization. The nanogels were subsequently characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS) to determine particle size and distribution, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to assess surface morphology, and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to visualize internal structural features.

To evaluate drug release behavior, in vitro pH-responsive release assays were conducted. Nanogels were incubated in buffer solutions simulating the uterine environment (pH 6.8 and 7.4) using a thermostatic shaker, and samples were collected at predetermined intervals for quantification of Tofa concentration via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Cumulative release profiles were calculated to assess drug-release characteristics under different pH conditions. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were used to further analyze interactions between the drug and the carrier, confirming successful loading and supporting the feasibility of the proposed release mechanism.

Construction of a 3D endometrium-embryo co-culture system

In constructing the 3D endometrium-embryo co-culture system, Matrigel was selected as the matrix material due to its ability to mimic the extracellular matrix (ECM) of the endometrium. Matrigel was diluted at low temperature and plated into six-well plates, then incubated at 37 °C to induce gelation. HESCs (CRL-4003, ATCC), endometrial epithelial cells (EECs, BC-PC-HU-004, Nanjing Senbeijia Biotechnology), and the JAr cell line (HTB-144, ATCC) were co-seeded onto the Matrigel scaffold to establish a tri-culture system. Seeding densities were optimized to ensure proper cell growth and intercellular interactions. Five experimental conditions were established: control, LIF inhibition, blank gel, Tofa-NG, and LIF supplementation to investigate how these conditions influence cellular interactions, as well as the adhesion and polarization behavior of embryo-like cells.

Throughout the experiment, embryo-like cell adhesion and polarization were observed in real time using laser-scanning confocal microscopy (STELLARIS SpectraPlex, Leica, Germany). At designated time points, cell samples were collected and fluorescently labeled to visualize cell morphology and adhesion status under the microscope clearly. Adhesive capacity was quantified using specialized software to measure adhesive area and intensity, and intergroup differences were statistically assessed. In addition, RT-qPCR and WB were employed to examine the expression of adhesion-related factors (e.g., ITGA5) and polarization markers (e.g., MUC1). Further analysis revealed that in the LIF-deficient group, expression levels of syncytiotrophoblast markers (e.g., CGB, ERVW-1) and pluripotency factors (OCT4, NANOG) were significantly downregulated, while partial restoration was observed in the LIF supplementation group. Confocal imaging showed incomplete blastocyst adhesion interfaces and reduced expansion areas in the LIF-deficient group, suggesting that impaired maternal endometrial signaling negatively affects embryo implantation behavior. This 3D system demonstrated strong physiological relevance and sensitivity in recapitulating maternal-embryo signaling interactions (Figure S1).

Building upon the co-culture model, the decidualization capacity of endometrial stromal cells was further assessed. Specifically, total RNA and protein were extracted from HESCs before and after co-culture to evaluate the expression of classical decidualization markers, including IGFBP1, PRL, and HAND2. Morphological changes in HESCs were observed via TRITC-phalloidin staining, particularly the transition from a spindle-like to a polygonal epithelial-like phenotype. Additionally, secreted levels of PRL (E-EL-H0141, Elabscience) and IGFBP1 (E-EL-H0442, Elabscience) in cell supernatants were measured by ELISA. These molecular, morphological, and functional assessments helped elucidate how the LIF/STAT3 pathway and its upstream epigenetic regulation influence decidualization, thereby establishing a causal link between LIF axis activity and decidual impairment.

This system not only reconstructed the physical interface between the embryo and the endometrium but also enabled simulation of the bidirectional “maternal-embryo dialogue.” Embryo-like JAr cells secreted signaling factors (e.g., hCG, LIF) to feedback-regulate decidualization of endometrial stromal cells. Conversely, implantation-promoting cytokines secreted by endometrial cells (e.g., IL-1β, HB-EGF) influenced embryo polarization and expansion. The system’s real-time imaging capacity (confocal microscopy combined with fluorescent labeling) enabled dynamic tracking of the embryo adhesion area, trophoblast migration trajectory, and depth of implantation, providing a physiologically relevant platform for functionally validating the impact of LIF/STAT3 signaling and its epigenetic modulation on implantation efficiency.

PCR analysis of human endometrial samples

Endometrial tissue samples were collected from patients with AM and normal controls, and the expression levels of target genes were determined by qRT-PCR. All tissue samples were obtained with informed consent from the patients and with approval from the hospital ethics committee. Immediately after collection, the samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored until use. Total RNA was extracted according to the instructions of the TRIzol reagent kit, and mRNA expression in adenomyosis was analyzed by two-step RT-PCR. The amplification protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 28 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 56 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 10 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were subjected to 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Gel images were captured and saved using ImageJ software for analysis of target gene expression. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Validation of the inflammatory regulation of LIF signaling

First, an IL-1β stimulation model was employed to induce an inflammatory response. Specifically, human-derived cell lines, such as A549 and THP-1, were used as the study subjects. After reaching the logarithmic growth phase, cells were stimulated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β (GP29018, GlpBio) for 24 h to induce a robust inflammatory response. Under these conditions, LIF expression was markedly reduced, indicating that LIF was suppressed in an inflammation-dependent manner. To examine the nanogel’s intervention effect, multifunctional nanogels—such as those encapsulating specific small-molecule drugs or RNA interference constructs—were added to the culture medium at a final concentration of 100 µg/mL and incubated for 48 h. The expression levels of IL-6 (21865-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), TNF-α (17590-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and NF-κB p65 (80979-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) were assessed and quantified by RT-qPCR and WB to evaluate the nanogel’s anti-inflammatory capacity.

In parallel, to validate the regulatory relationship within the NF-κB-LIF signaling axis, specific NF-κB inhibitors (e.g., BAY 11–7082, SF0011) and agonists (e.g., PMA, MB5349, Meilunbio) were applied in cross-validation experiments. Cells were treated with 5 µM BAY 11–7082 in the inhibitor group and 100 nM PMA in the agonist group, followed by incubation for 24 h before measuring LIF expression. Additionally, immunofluorescence combined with laser confocal microscopy was used to visualize the intracellular distribution of LIF and its colocalization with NF-κB p65. Phospho-specific antibodies were further used to examine and quantify STAT3 phosphorylation, enabling assessment of its activation status within the LIF signaling pathway.

In functional assays, LIF knockdown significantly impaired the decidualization capacity of HESCs, as reflected by reductions of 57% and 49% in IGFBP1 and PRL mRNA expression, respectively (p < 0.01), accompanied by corresponding decreases in protein levels. TRITC-phalloidin staining revealed that knockdown cells failed to undergo the characteristic transition to a polygonal epithelial-like morphology, whereas LIF supplementation restored this phenotype. ELISA results also confirmed marked reductions in PRL (E-EL-H0141, Elabscience) and IGFBP1 (E-EL-H0442, Elabscience) secretion. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LIF plays an essential role in endometrial decidualization and that its suppression directly disrupts the establishment of endometrial receptivity.

Analysis of metabolic status and oxidative stress

To assess cellular metabolic status, we quantified key intracellular metabolites by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). Optimized extraction and preparation protocols were applied to generate clear metabolic profiles from cell samples. Target metabolites included lactate, citrate, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP), which play essential roles in energy metabolism and redox balance. Internal standards were used during sample preparation to correct for potential loss during extraction and analysis. Metabolites were separated by HPLC and quantified and validated using MS/MS.

Changes in mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) serve as a critical indicator of cellular energy status and mitochondrial function. In this study, we used the JC-1 staining method to evaluate Δψm. After treatment, cells were incubated with JC-1. In healthy mitochondria, JC-1 forms aggregates that emit red fluorescence, whereas in depolarized mitochondria, JC-1 exists in a monomeric form that emits green fluorescence. The red-to-green fluorescence ratio was measured by fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry to quantify changes in Δψm.

To determine intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, we employed dihydroethidium (DHE) staining. After incubation with DHE, the dye was oxidized to a fluorescent product in proportion to intracellular ROS levels. Fluorescence microscopy was used to detect and quantify ROS distribution and intensity. Additionally, the intracellular metabolic distribution of lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates was tracked using a Raman spectrometer (LabRAM Soleil™, HORIBA). This label-free technique provides high-resolution chemical fingerprinting, enabling subcellular-level analysis of metabolic states. Together, these complementary methods enabled a comprehensive assessment of cellular functional changes and stress responses across varying metabolic conditions.

Animal model and intervention protocol

In this experiment, an AM rat model was established by combining estrogen with mechanical injury. Adult female Sprague-Dawley rats weighing 220–250 g were randomly assigned to five experimental groups: control, LIF inhibition, blank gel, Tofa-NG, and LIF supplementation, with 3 rats per group. Following model induction, nanogel interventions were administered via tail vein injection once daily for 14 consecutive days. The nanogels were prepared using standardized protocols to ensure biocompatibility and stability. In vivo fluorescence imaging was used to monitor the localization and retention of the nanogels within the uterine tissue. Fluorescently labeled gels enabled visualization of their distribution, thereby confirming effective delivery to the target site.

At the end of the 14-day treatment period, uterine tissues were collected for histological and IHC analyses. The tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess uterine structural integrity and inflammatory cell infiltration. IHC staining was performed to evaluate the expression and spatial distribution of LIF and p-STAT3, key indicators of inflammatory activity and endocrine signaling. Specific antibodies against LIF and p-STAT3 were used, and chromogenic detection enabled localization and intensity assessment across different treatment groups. Quantitative analysis was performed to compare expression patterns among groups.

Following the intervention phase, all rats underwent mating to evaluate embryo implantation rates. On day 5 of pregnancy, the female rats were dissected, and the number of implantation sites in the uterus was recorded. Implantation success was determined by counting visible implantation spots. Reproductive outcomes were assessed by recording the number of embryos, implantation rates, and live birth rates per animal. Statistical comparisons were conducted across groups to determine the effects of different interventions on fertility outcomes. All animal experiments were conducted with prior approval from the institutional animal ethics committee and in strict accordance with its guidelines to ensure animal welfare and the reliability of experimental results.

Immunofluorescence staining for co-localization of VCAM1 and ICAM1 with stromal and glandular epithelial cell-specific markers

Paraffin-embedded endometrial tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) using microwave heating. After cooling, the sections were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h to prevent nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with mixtures of primary antibodies. The primary antibody combinations included mouse anti-human VCAM1 monoclonal antibody (ab134047, Abcam) together with rabbit anti-human Vimentin monoclonal antibody (a stromal cell marker; ab92547, Abcam), or mouse anti-human ICAM1 monoclonal antibody (ab282575, Abcam) together with mouse anti-human Pan-Cytokeratin monoclonal antibody (an epithelial cell marker; ab7753, Abcam). On the following day, after thorough washing with PBS, the sections were incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark with the corresponding secondary antibody mixtures, including Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (green fluorescence) and Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (red fluorescence). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 5 min. After mounting, the sections were observed and imaged under a laser scanning confocal microscope. Co-localization was analyzed using merged images to identify the cell types expressing these adhesion molecules.

In vivo biodistribution of Tofa-NG

To investigate the biodistribution of Tofa-NG in various tissues, rats were intravenously injected via the tail vein with 350 µL of DiD-labeled Tofa-NG at a concentration of 3 mg/mL. At 48 h after particle injection, five rats were euthanized. The liver, kidneys, spleen, lungs, heart, and uterus were collected. The harvested organs were carefully weighed and then homogenized in 1 mL of PBS. The fluorescence intensity of each sample was determined using an Infinite M200 microplate reader.

Immunohistochemistry

Uterine tissues from rats in each group were processed routinely, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 3–4 μm, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Before antigen retrieval in a microwave oven, the sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated. For IHC, the sections were incubated with antibodies against Col1a1 (ab6308, Abcam), TGF-β (ab215715, Abcam), CD68 (ab283654, Abcam), and CD163 (ab182422, Abcam), followed by incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 2 h. Immunoreactivity was then visualized using a DAB detection kit and examined under a light microscope. The mean staining intensity was measured using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) in at least 20 randomly selected fields.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess data normality. Data conforming to a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between groups were evaluated using independent-sample t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range), and group comparisons were conducted using the Mann-Whitney U test or the Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square (χ2) test. For multi-group high-throughput omics data, the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure was applied to correct for multiple hypothesis testing and control the FDR.

LIF signaling is downregulated in patients with AM

In the pathological analysis of AM tissues, the LIF signaling pathway was found to be markedly downregulated, a change that may profoundly affect endometrial receptivity to embryo implantation. IHC staining revealed that LIF protein levels in the endometrial tissues of AM patients (n = 30) were significantly lower than those in healthy controls (n = 30) (Fig. 1A). Subsequent RT-qPCR analysis showed that LIF mRNA expression in the patient group decreased by approximately 40% (p < 0.01), consistent with the protein-level findings (Fig. 1B). To further explore the underlying mechanisms, we established a 3D in vitro co-culture model using HESCs to simulate the pathological microenvironment of AM. In this model, p-STAT3—the downstream effector of LIF signaling—was significantly reduced in the disease-mimicking group (Fig. 1C).

Bulk RNA-seq was then conducted, and PCA demonstrated a clear separation between AM samples and normal endometrial tissues, indicating substantial transcriptional divergence between groups (Fig. 1D). Volcano plot analysis identified 297 upregulated and 201 downregulated genes (|log2FC| ≥ 1, FDR < 0.05), highlighting extensive transcriptional reprogramming in the AM endometrium (Fig. 1E).

Biplot analysis (Fig. 1F) showed that the arrow corresponding to the LIF gene clearly pointed toward the normal group (yellow dots), indicating its high expression in normal endometrial tissues and marked downregulation in the AM group. In contrast, arrows for inflammation-related genes, such as TNFAIP3, NOD2, IKBKB, and CXCL3, pointed toward the AM group (blue dots), suggesting elevated expression in AM samples. These findings reflect a distinct suppression of LIF signaling and concurrent activation of inflammatory pathways in AM endometrium.

Further feature ranking analysis (Fig. 1G -H) confirmed this trend: LIF was more highly expressed in the normal group, whereas NOD2, IKBKB, TNFAIP3, CXCL3, IL17RC, RELB, PNLIPRP1, and NOTUM were significantly upregulated in the AM group. These results suggest that LIF signaling, which is associated with immune tolerance and tissue repair, is suppressed in AM, while the NF-κB pathway and its downstream inflammatory cytokines are aberrantly activated, potentially contributing to chronic inflammation and localized tissue remodeling in AM.

WB and ELISA analyses demonstrated that reduced LIF expression was associated with decreased levels of the immunosuppressive cytokine IL-10 (Fig. 1I), implying that LIF downregulation may impair embryo implantation through mechanisms involving immune imbalance.

To investigate the epitranscriptional mechanism underlying the inactivation of LIF/STAT3 signaling in patients with AM, we examined METTL3 expression and its relationship with m6A modification of LIF. The results showed that METTL3 expression was reduced in tissues from patients with AM and was positively correlated with LIF expression (Fig. 1J). RIP-qPCR confirmed the binding of METTL3 to LIF mRNA, while MeRIP-qPCR revealed that m6A modification of LIF transcripts was reduced by approximately 45% in the AM group. Upon treatment with actinomycin D, the half-life of LIF mRNA was shortened in METTL3 knockdown cells. Furthermore, a dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed the presence of functionally relevant m6A regulatory sites in the 3′UTR of LIF mRNA.

Collectively, these findings suggest that LIF signaling is suppressed in patients with AM, potentially due to impaired METTL3-mediated m6A modification, resulting in reduced mRNA stability and compromised endometrial receptivity and embryo implantation.

To further elucidate the BP and signaling pathways involved in transcriptional reprogramming of the AM endometrium, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed on DEGs (Figs. 2B-C). Results showed that IL-1β and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in the AM group (Fig. 2H). Inactivation of the LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway is accompanied by significant metabolic reprogramming in patients with AM

In recent years, growing evidence has shown that the LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway plays a critical role not only in embryo implantation but also in regulating energy metabolism. To evaluate its functional status in patients with AM, we first constructed a schematic diagram of signal-metabolism interactions, proposing that STAT3 inactivation may contribute to AM pathogenesis by disrupting metabolic homeostasis (Fig. 3A). In clinical samples, WB analysis revealed a significant reduction in p-STAT3 levels in the AM group compared to controls, while total STAT3 levels remained unchanged (Fig. 3B). This indicates that impaired pathway activation is a hallmark of LIF/STAT3 signaling dysfunction.

To investigate whether signaling inactivation leads to metabolic dysregulation, we performed targeted metabolomic profiling to assess changes in the relative abundance of key metabolites in endometrial tissues from AM patients. PCA revealed clear separation between the AM and control groups along PC1 and PC2, accounting for 38.3% and 19.9% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 3C), suggesting distinct metabolic profiles. Subsequent construction of a PLS-DA model demonstrated strong discriminative performance (R2Y = 0.992, Q2 = 0.969), with model robustness confirmed by 2000-fold permutation testing (p = 0.0015) (Fig. 3D), supporting the reliability of the model in distinguishing AM from control samples.

According to the selection criteria of |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05, a total of 14 significantly altered metabolites were identified (Fig. 3E). Among these, upregulated metabolites included lactate, citrate, succinate, acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, and alanine, while downregulated metabolites comprised ATP, ADP, GSH, GSSG, and LysoPC. VIP ranking indicated that beta-hydroxybutyrate, succinate, and citrate contributed most to the model (VIP > 3.0), identifying them as key discriminatory metabolites between the AM and control groups (Fig. 3F). Hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis further confirmed consistent intragroup metabolic profiles and significant intergroup differences (Fig. 3G).

Pathway enrichment analysis based on the SMPDB revealed significant enrichment in pathways including the citric acid cycle, Warburg effect, mitochondrial electron transport chain, glutathione metabolism, and ketone body metabolism (Fig. 3H). Consistently, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that metabolic dysregulation in the AM group was predominantly associated with core energy metabolism pathways, such as alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, butanoate metabolism, and the TCA cycle (Fig. 3I).

In vitro Pearson correlation analysis showed a significant negative correlation between p-STAT3 levels and lactate concentration, suggesting that STAT3 signaling activity may directly modulate metabolite accumulation (Fig. 3J). WB analysis further validated the expression of STAT3 downstream metabolic regulators, including SOCS3 and HK2. SOCS3 expression was markedly downregulated, while HK2 expression was elevated (Fig. 3K), indicating that STAT3 inactivation may relieve its suppressive effect on glycolytic pathways, thereby inducing Warburg effect-like metabolic reprogramming. This reprogramming may contribute to endometrial dysfunction and impaired embryo implantation in AM.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that inactivation of the LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway in AM disrupts not only embryo receptivity but also cellular metabolic homeostasis.

LIF signaling regulates embryo-like cell adhesion in a 3D AM model

To further investigate the impact of the AM microenvironment on pre-implantation cell-matrix interactions, a 3D co-culture system was established to simulate the endometrial milieu and examine the adhesion behavior of embryo-like cells under LIF signaling regulation (Fig. 4A). Control and LIF inhibition groups were established. Optical microscopy combined with quantitative image analysis revealed that LIF inhibition significantly reduced cell adhesion area, indicating that LIF signaling plays a critical role in regulating cellular adhesiveness (Fig. 4B).

To elucidate the molecular basis of this phenomenon, the correlation between LIF pathway activity and cell adhesion capacity was assessed. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that suppression of LIF markedly decreased the expression of integrin αvβ3 and the adhesion protein fibronectin (Fig. 4C), accompanied by reduced aggregation of embryo-like cells. Following gene deletion, the adhesion area was further reduced, whereas treatment with recombinant human LIF reversed this effect, enhancing cellular stability and spreading capacity (Fig. 4E). This discovery not only deepens understanding of early embryo implantation dynamics but also provides a theoretical foundation for developing strategies to restore adhesion microenvironments.

Further systematic analysis was conducted to assess the effects of Tofa-NG on upstream signaling and downstream functional effectors. Upon expanding the sample size (n = 120) and conducting age-stratified analyses, patients with low LIF expression exhibited significantly higher VAS scores than those with high LIF expression, and this difference persisted across all age groups (Fig. 9B). RT-qPCR results further demonstrated that LIF mRNA levels were markedly reduced in pathological samples relative to controls.

To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, a 3D endometrium-immune cell co-culture system was established to simulate the adenomyotic microenvironment. Under low LIF expression conditions, inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, MCP-1, and CXCL10 were significantly elevated, a pattern closely associated with inflammatory amplification and enhanced nociceptive signaling (Fig. 9C). IHC and immunofluorescence further confirmed increased expression of neurotrophic markers, including nerve growth factor (NGF), in tissues with low LIF expression, suggesting that LIF may contribute to chronic pelvic pain through regulation of inflammation-neural pathways (Fig. 9D). Robust linear regression analysis demonstrated a strong negative correlation between LIF expression and VAS scores, and this association remained stable after adjusting for confounding factors such as age and hormonal status (Fig. 9E).

This finding suggests that LIF may contribute to the development of chronic pelvic pain by regulating inflammation-neural interactions. Although direct evidence for the involvement of LIF in pain regulation in adenomyosis remains lacking, several recent studies have provided mechanistic support for this hypothesis. First, LIF has been identified as an important immunoregulatory factor. In a graft-versus-host disease model, LIF was shown to maintain intestinal stem cell function through the STAT3 signaling pathway, reduce T-cell infiltration, suppress Th17 differentiation, and promote Treg expansion, thereby alleviating tissue inflammation [21]. In the present study, low LIF expression was accompanied by increased levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and MCP-1 (Fig. 9C), which is highly consistent with the anti-inflammatory role of LIF reported in the literature, suggesting that LIF deficiency may disrupt local immune homeostasis and thereby amplify inflammatory responses. More importantly, there is a direct molecular link between LIF and pain regulation. Recent evidence has shown that LIF can act on peripheral sensory neurons: in rheumatoid arthritis, LIF in synovial fluid directly activated human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived sensory neurons, induced STAT3 phosphorylation, upregulated the expression of pain-related genes, and caused neuronal sensitization, whereas JAK inhibitors such as tofacitinib blocked this effect [22]. Taken together, we propose the following mechanistic model: in adenomyotic lesions, downregulation of LIF not only leads to an imbalance in the local immune microenvironment, characterized by impaired M2 macrophage polarization and increased release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, but may also weaken the protective regulation of endometrial sensory neurons, thereby promoting the upregulation of nerve growth factors such as NGF and sensitization of nerve endings, ultimately resulting in amplification of pain signaling (Fig. 9F). These results not only reinforce the clinical relevance of LIF as an important biomarker for AM but also provide a theoretical basis for developing LIF-targeted analgesic strategies.

Discussion

This study enhances our understanding of the pathophysiological mechanisms of AM by revealing the dysregulation of the LIF/STAT3 signaling pathway and its impact on embryo implantation. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on clinical symptoms [23], we provide, for the first time, direct evidence of LIF/STAT3 inactivation in AM using a 3D co-culture model and clinical sample analysis. The association between this signaling inactivation and impaired embryo implantation underscores the central role of the LIF/STAT3 axis in the pathogenesis of AM. Our findings not only deepen mechanistic understanding of AM but also establish a theoretical foundation for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. By highlighting the critical involvement of this pathway in disease progression, our study offers a new perspective on the molecular mechanisms underlying implantation failure.

In addition, we extensively investigated the role of inflammatory cytokines in AM. Through detailed molecular analysis, we found that overexpression of inflammatory cytokines is directly associated with implantation failure, primarily through NF-κB-mediated suppression of LIF expression. This discovery advances our understanding of the role of inflammatory cytokines in AM, particularly their impact on embryo implantation by inhibiting LIF signaling. While previous literature has discussed the involvement of inflammation in uterine disorders in broad terms [24, 25], our study offers concrete mechanistic evidence. These findings not only contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the disease’s molecular basis but also provide a promising direction for future therapeutic development.

In terms of the relationship between metabolic dysregulation and signaling pathways, we are the first to report a significant association between LIF/STAT3 signaling inactivation and aberrant energy metabolism. Metabolomic analysis demonstrated that this metabolic disturbance is closely linked to LIF/STAT3 inactivation, highlighting the potential role of metabolic regulation in the pathogenesis of AM. This finding is consistent with observations in other uterine disorders [26, 27]; however, our study provides more detailed molecular-level evidence, elucidating the intricate interplay between metabolic imbalance and disrupted signaling pathways. Recent studies have shown that METTL3 plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammation. Specifically, METTL3 deficiency can alter macrophage inflammatory responses and mediate the production of inflammatory cytokines through regulation of the NF-κB pathway [28, 29]. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for understanding the cascade of “METTL3 downregulation–formation of an inflammatory microenvironment–LIF suppression” in AM: reduced METTL3 expression may represent one of the upstream events driving the establishment of the inflammatory microenvironment in AM, whereas inactivation of LIF/STAT3 signaling may be a downstream consequence of this pathological process. In addition, METTL3/m6A modification may also be potentially linked to the metabolic abnormalities observed in the present study. A recent review systematically summarized the role of m6A modification in female reproductive system disorders and highlighted that aberrant METTL3-mediated m6A modification is closely associated with diseases such as adenomyosis and endometriosis [30]. These insights not only advance our understanding of AM but also suggest novel directions for future research, particularly in exploring metabolism as a therapeutic target.

The JAr cell line is derived from human choriocarcinoma. Although it originates from a malignant tumor, it has long been widely accepted and validated as a classical in vitro model for studying trophoblast–endometrial interactions. First, JAr cells are capable of secreting human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), an important marker of trophoblast functional maturation. In a previously established three-dimensional endometrial culture system, JAr cells were confirmed to be hCG-positive, and the formed spheroids were shown to adhere efficiently to endometrial epithelial cells under hormonal regulation [31]. Second, JAr cells exhibit functional characteristics similar to those of primary trophoblasts, including tight junction formation, glucose transport capacity, and mechanosensitive responses to extracellular matrix stiffness [32]. Previous studies have also validated the co-culture method of JAr spheroids with a three-dimensional endometrial culture system and demonstrated that the adhesion of JAr spheroids to the endometrial epithelium is time-dependent. This system was described by the authors as “a unique in vitro model to study specific aspects of human implantation” [31]. Thus, although JAr cells are tumor-derived, they remain a well-established classical model for trophoblast functional studies, and their biological behavior is highly similar to that of early trophoblasts.

It is worth noting that tofacitinib, as a JAK1/3 inhibitor, directly exerts its pharmacological effect by suppressing phosphorylation within the JAK-STAT signaling pathway. However, the partial restoration of STAT3 activity observed in the present study, although seemingly paradoxical, has been reported in the literature. In human sensory neurons, LIF can induce STAT3 phosphorylation, and this effect can be completely blocked by tofacitinib, indicating that the regulation of the LIF–STAT3 axis by tofacitinib is well established [22]. In our experimental system, LIF expression was restored after Tofa-NG treatment, and LIF is an upstream activator of STAT3, thereby providing a direct ligand-based explanation for the recovery of STAT3 activity.

The Tofa-NG we developed exhibited marked efficacy in reactivating LIF/STAT3 signaling, offering a promising new therapeutic avenue for AM. Beyond its ability to restore signaling activity, the nanogel demonstrated substantial clinical potential through targeted drug release and modulation of the inflammatory microenvironment. Based on the progress of the present study, we propose the following clinical translation strategy: tofacitinib will be loaded into a biomimetic nanogel system to construct a locally sustained-release formulation, Tofa@Gel. This formulation could be administered noninvasively in an outpatient setting via intrauterine perfusion and undergo in situ gelation at body temperature, thereby enabling continuous drug release for several days to weeks. The gel matrix may be composed of clinically approved polymeric materials, such as chitosan, gelatin, or Pluronic F127, in order to reduce translational barriers. To further enhance targeting efficiency, the system could also be modified with lesion microenvironment-responsive moieties, such as matrix metalloproteinase-sensitive peptide sequences, or with cell-penetrating peptides, thereby achieving precise drug accumulation in lesion areas with low LIF expression. Compared with conventional treatment approaches, this innovative nanoplatform lays the groundwork for the development of personalized therapeutic strategies. Moreover, the nanogel’s antioxidant mechanisms underscore its multifaceted role in ameliorating the pathological conditions of AM, potentially creating a more favorable endometrial environment for embryo implantation.

Although Tofa intervention has shown therapeutic potential in improving the pathological microenvironment of adenomyosis, restoring LIF signaling, and promoting embryo implantation, immunomodulatory intervention during the implantation window, a highly specialized physiological period, requires particularly cautious evaluation of its potential risks. The implantation process depends on a dynamically balanced interplay between finely regulated immune tolerance at the maternal–fetal interface and localized inflammatory responses. Any exogenous immunomodulatory intervention may disrupt this balance and thereby affect embryo implantation and subsequent pregnancy outcomes. With regard to clinical safety, although tofacitinib is generally well tolerated in patients with autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, data on its use in pregnant women remain very limited. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has classified tofacitinib as a pregnancy category C drug, indicating that animal studies have shown adverse effects, whereas adequate human data are lacking; thus, its use during pregnancy requires careful consideration of the potential risks and benefits. Notably, the biomimetic nanogel-based local delivery strategy proposed in this study, Tofa@Gel, may substantially reduce these systemic risks. Through intrauterine local administration, the drug is expected to exert its effects primarily within the lesion area, while only minimal amounts enter the systemic circulation, thereby reducing potential impacts on the systemic immune system and distant organs. Nevertheless, even with local administration, potential concerns remain, including effects on the fetal-placental circulation and direct interference with the implantation process itself.

In summary, this study, through comprehensive multi-level experimental analyses, systematically elucidates the central role of LIF/STAT3 signaling inactivation in AM and its interconnection with metabolic dysregulation, inflammatory cytokines, and oxidative stress. We confirmed the therapeutic potential of Tofa-NG in restoring signal transduction and improving the pathological microenvironment. Nevertheless, clinical translation remains challenging, particularly concerning the long-term safety of nanomaterials and the optimization of individualized treatment protocols. Future studies should further investigate the applicability of nanogels in related disorders and validate their efficacy and safety through large-scale clinical trials. This research not only deepens insight into the pathological mechanisms of AM but also provides a robust foundation for the development of personalized therapies. At the same time, it offers new perspectives on the pathophysiology of related diseases and on the development of therapeutic strategies.
