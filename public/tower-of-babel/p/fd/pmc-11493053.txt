IL-11-Engineered Macrophage Membrane-Coated Reactive Oxygen Species-Responsive Nanoparticles for Targeted Delivery of Doxorubicin to Osteosarcoma
Authors: Hao Jiang, Ying Luo, Bo Li, Chunbiao Wu, Da Wang, Yingye Xin, Wei Xu, Jianru Xiao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma (OS) is a lethal malignant orthotopic bone tumor that primarily affects children and adolescents. Biomimetic nanocarriers have attracted wide attention as a new strategy for delivering chemotherapy agents to the OS. However, challenges such as rapid clearance and limited targeting hinder the effectiveness of OS chemotherapy. In this study, we designed reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive nanoparticles (NPs) coated with an interleukin (IL)11-engineered macrophage membrane (MM). The camouflage by MMs prevents clearance of IL-11-engineered MM-coated NPs loaded with doxorubicin (IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox) by the immune system. Moreover, the macrophage membrane combined with surface-expressed IL-11 not only directed IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox to OS tissues but also selectively identified IL-11 receptor alpha (IL-11Rα)-enriched OS cells. Within these cells, elevated levels of ROS triggered the controlled release of Dox from the ROS-responsive NPs. The synergistic modification of targeted ligand conjugation and cell membrane coating on the ROS-responsive NPs enhanced drug availability and reduced toxic side effects, thereby boosting the efficacy of OS chemotherapy. In summary, our findings suggest that IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox represents a promising approach to improving OS chemotherapy efficacy while ensuring excellent biocompatibility.Keywords: osteosarcoma chemotherapy, IL-11, targeting ligand, macrophage membrane, ROS-responsive nanoparticles

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is the most malignant and common orthotopic bone tumor that predominantly affects individuals aged 10–14 years and those aged 70–80 years.1 Current treatment protocols include neoadjuvant chemotherapy, surgical resection, and adjuvant chemotherapy, which have collectively improved the 5-year overall survival rate to over 60%.2 However, approximately 40% of OS patients show poor response to these treatments, and the 5-year overall survival rate drops to less than 30% for patients with relapsed or metastatic OS.3 Approved chemotherapeutic agents for OS include doxorubicin (Dox), methotrexate, and cisplatin.4 Despite their efficacy, these drugs exhibit limitations in pharmacokinetic profiles such as rapid clearance, nonspecific distribution, and poor targeting.4 Consequently, their use often results in significant adverse effects including organ toxicity and drug resistance.5 Therefore, substantial efforts are underway to optimize therapeutic strategies that address these challenges and enhance the efficacy of chemotherapy in the OS.

Among the various approaches to enhance chemotherapy efficacy for OS, nanodrug delivery systems have gained significant attention, particularly stimuli-responsive nanomedicines.6 These nanomedicines are considered promising because they respond to internal or external stimuli, such as pH, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels,7 or even both,8 enabling precise drug release in tumor cells as needed.9 In OS, the acidic pH and elevated ROS levels, stemming from rapid cell proliferation and limited oxygen, serve as effective internal stimuli for these nanomedicines.10,11 For example, Wang et al. developed a pH-responsive nanocarrier for Dox delivery into OS cells, presenting higher toxicity to CAL-72 cells compared to free Dox.12 Similarly, Niu et al. designed a ROS-sensitive nanoparticle (NP) delivering celastrol, which enhanced apoptosis in tumor cells.13

However, when administered into the body, these smart nanomedicines would also be recognized and cleared by the immune system.14,15 Moreover, these nanomedicines have an insufficient targeting ability toward OS cells to ensure their recruitment around tumor sites or even infiltration into tumor cells.6 Ready to be uptaken by tumor cells is important for the development of nanomedicines.16 Thus, OS chemotherapy based on these stimuli-responsive nanocarriers is still far from being satisfactory. Therefore, considerable subsequent work has focused on modifying these stimuli-responsive nanocarriers.17,18 For instance, biomimetic cell membrane coating is an impressive modification strategy that could endow these stimuli-responsive nanocarriers with prolonged circulation time in vivo and active targeting ability at the tumor site.19,20 Various cell membranes have been studied for camouflaging NPs, including macrophages,21 white blood cells,22 cancer cells,23 and stem cells,24 due to their innate ability to interact effectively within the complex in vivo environment. Of note, macrophages, as key components of the immune response, are particularly attractive candidates for imparting nanocarriers with biomimetic properties to evade immune clearance and selectively accumulate around the tumor sites.21 Tasciotti et al. developed the first macrophage membrane (MM)-camouflaged NPs, which were proven to prolong the circulation time and promote tumor accumulation.25 Meanwhile, surface modifications with targeted ligands could endow these nanocarriers with OS cell-specific targeting ability through ligand interactions.26 Biomarkers, such as interleukin-11 receptor alpha (IL-11Rα),27 VEGFRs, and folate receptors, have been identified as efficient targets on OS cell surfaces.5 For instance, Jiang et al. designed IL-11Rα-targeting glutathione (GSH)-responsive nanovehicles, enhancing OS cell-specific targeting ability and efficacy of Dox chemotherapy.28

Despite these advancements, more efficient nanocarriers that combine sufficient stability with excellent OS cell-specific targeting ability are urgently needed to enhance the OS cell sensitivity to chemotherapeutics. Here, we designed a chemotherapy agent delivery system derived from ROS-responsive NPs coated with an IL-11-engineered MM to potentiate the tumoricidal functions of OS chemotherapy. Dox was selected as the model drug and encapsulated in the ROS-responsive NPs before being coated with the IL-11-engineered MM.28 The MM was selected for inheriting the immune escape and tumor-targeting ability to the NPs.29 IL-11 was expressed on the surface of MM using the pDisplay vector to specifically identify IL-11Rα-enriched OS cells through IL-11Rα interaction.30 The pDisplay is a plasmid that could direct the cloned gene to express as a membrane-bound protein on the cell surface.30 Subsequently, the IL-11-engineered MM was isolated and coated onto the surface of the ROS-responsive NPs. The ROS-responsive NPs were synthesized through self-assembly of amphiphilic PBAP-CDI-Mannose (PCM), which was synthesized through the conjugation of 1,1-carbonyldiimidazole (CDI)-activated 4-(hydroxymethyl) phenylboronic acid pinacol ester (PBAP) and D-mannose. The arylboronic esters tend to be oxidized by H2O2; thus, PCM would disassemble in an environment with a high concentration of ROS. ROS levels in OS cells are approximately 5000 times higher than those in normal tissues, indicating the superiority of ROS-responsive approaches over GSH-responsive, pH-responsive, or other stimuli-responsive strategies.11 Consequently, PBAP-functionalized d-mannose was employed to trigger the Dox release in response to the high ROS levels in OS cells.29

In our experiments, the escape ability of IL-11-engineered MM-coated NPs loaded with doxorubicin (IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox) from macrophage clearance was confirmed. The ability to target IL-11Rα-enriched 143B cells and 143B orthotopic tumors was demonstrated. Biocompatibility of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox was confirmed both in vitro and in vivo. The toxicity to 143B cells of NPs/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox was assessed. In a 143B cell orthotopic OS model, the targeting and therapeutic efficacy of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were systematically evaluated and compared to those of free Dox, NPs/Dox, and MM@NPs/Dox. Furthermore, the underlying therapeutic mechanisms were analyzed at the molecular level. Finally, the OS therapy efficacy of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox was confirmed using the patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model.31 Therefore, the biomimetic nanomedicine IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox represents a feasible and promising choice as safe and efficient chemotherapeutic nanocarriers for OS.

Cell Culture

RAW264.7 macrophage, 143B cells, SJSA1 cells, MG63 cells, SAOS-2 cells, L929 fibroblasts, and HOS cells were acquired from ATCC. These cell lines were authenticated through STR profiling and texted negative for mycoplasma contamination. LM7 cells were derived from SAOS-2 cells in our laboratory. RAW264.7 cells, 143B cells, MG63 cells, SAOS-2 cells, L929 fibroblasts, and HOS cells were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (100×). SJSA1 cells were cultured in 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution (100×). Cell lines adopted were cultured for no more than 10 passages.

Cloning of IL-11

Total RNA was extracted from 106 cells using TRlzol Reagent. cDNA was synthesized using the BeyoRT II First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (D7168M, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol: 5 μg of total RNA was subjected to reverse transcription. PCR was performed, and the amplified fragments were sequenced. Primers specific for human IL-11, forward: 5′-AGATCTATGAACTGTGTTTGCCGCCT-3′ (underline: BglII site); reverse: 5′-GTCGACTCACAGCCGAGTCTTCAGCA-3′ (underline: AccI site).

Construction and Transfection of pDisplay-IL-11

The IL-11 gene was amplified, and the resultants were digested with restriction endonuclease BglII and AccI and subcloned into an identically digested pDisplay vector, as shown in Figure 1. The resulting recombinant plasmid was named pDisplay-IL-11. The pDisplay-IL-11 plasmid was transformed into DH5α competent cells for amplification, and the DNA was subsequently extracted using the PureLink HiPure Plasmid Maxi Kit. Identification of the recombinant plasmid was carried out by restriction endonuclease digestion and PCR amplification. Sequencing of the genome further verified the pDisplay-IL-11. To introduce this recombinant plasmid into cells, 10 μg of pDisplay-IL-11 was dissolved into 500 μL of serum-free DMEM medium with 30 μL of PEI. The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min and then added to macrophage cells to incubate for 6 h. Subsequently, the medium was refreshed for another 48 h of incubation. Cell colonies were observed, and G418 was used to select the G418-resistant RAW264.7-pDisplay-IL-11 cells. The empty pDisplay vector and sterile water were also used as controls for cell transformation. To observe IL-11 expression on the surface of RAW264.7 cells, the RAW264.7-pDisplay-IL-11 cells, cultured on glass slides, were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min. After that, cells were blocked with 5% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with a primary IL-11 antibody, followed by the cy3-labeled secondary fluorescent antibody. The nuclei of RAW264.7 cells were stained with DAPI. Finally, the cells were observed using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Leica, Germany).

Isolation of IL-11 Expressed Macrophage Membrane (IL-11/MM)

Raw264.7-pDisplay-IL-11 cells were prepared for isolating the IL-11 expressed macrophage membrane (IL-11/MM). Cells were lysed with buffer A, supplemented with phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), at 4 °C for 15 min. The cells were broken by freezing and thawing the cell suspension in liquid nitrogen 3 times at room temperature. The samples were centrifuged at 700g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove the nuclei and unbroken cells. The supernatants were then collected and centrifuged at 12,000g for 30 min at 4 °C. IL-11/MM was obtained by collecting the bottom sediment.

Synthesis and Characterization of ROS-Responsive Amphiphilic Molecule PCM

PBAP (4.25 mmol) and CDI (8.5 mmol) were completely dissolved in DCM (6 mL) followed by stirring and reaction overnight at room temperature. After adding an equal volume of DCM (6 mL), the mixture was washed three times with ultrapure and dried with anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) for 4 h. After filtration, PBAP-CDI white precipitates were obtained through evaporation under reduced pressure by using a rotary evaporator. Subsequently, PBAP-CDI (1.5 mmol), d-mannose (0.5 mmol), and DMAP (1.5 mmol) were dissolved in 4 mL of DMF and stirred overnight at 40 °C. The product was then placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO: 500 Da) and dialyzed in ultrapure water for 24 h. The collected products were frozen into solids at −80 °C, and then the solid pure substance of PBAP-CDI-Mannose (PCM) was obtained through vacuum freeze-drying. The chemical structures of PBAP-CDI and PCM were characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy (600 MHz, DMSO-d6).

Preparation of Dox-Loaded ROS-Responsive Nanoparticles (NPs/Dox)

NPs/Dox were prepared using the dialysis method.32 Briefly, 1 mg of Dox and 10 mg of PCM were dissolved in DMF (200 μL), and the mixture was added dropwise into rapidly stirring ultrapure water (5 mL). The NPs/Dox were then obtained through dialysis using a dialysis bag (MWCO: 1000 Da) for 12 h.

Preparation of IL-11/MM Coating NPs/Dox (IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox)

IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were prepared using the extrusion method.33 IL-11/MM was mixed with NPs/Dox and sonicated (42 kHz, 100 W) on an ultrasonicator for 2 min. The solution was then transferred to an extruder and passed through a polycarbonate porous membrane (200 nm) 15 times to obtain the IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox.

The size and ζ-potential of IL-11/MM, NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were measured with dynamic light scattering (DLS) at 25 °C by using a Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments, UK). The samples were added dropwise onto a 200 mesh copper grid and negatively stained with 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid. The morphology and structure of NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were observed after air-drying under a transmission electron microscope (TEM, H-7650, Hitachi Ltd.).

Cell Phagocytosis Co-localization Study

DiI, a fluorescent probe (red), was utilized to label the NPs, while the IL-11/MM was labeled with the green fluorescent probe DiO to obtain red and green dual fluorescent labeled DiO-IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI. 143B cells were incubated with DiO-IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI. After being cocultivated for 4 h, the cells were fixed and stained as previously described. The fluorescence of these samples was then observed by using CLSM.

Detection of Proteins

IL-11, IL-11Rα, and other proteins, including CD36, CCR2, and TNFR2, were detected through western blot analysis. Proteins from OS cell lines, macrophage membrane (MM), IL-11-expressing macrophage membrane (IL-11/MM), NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were prepared through a RIPA lysis buffer with 10% phosphatase and protease inhibitors. The collected protein samples were mixed with a 5× loading buffer and boiled at 100 °C for 15 min to ensure complete protein denaturation. Subsequently, protein samples (20 μg) were loaded onto the preconfigured gel (4–20%) and transferred onto the 0.45 μm PVDF membrane after the protein bands were separated. After blocking with 5% milk, the PVDF membranes were incubated with primary antibodies, including IL-11Rα, IL-11, CCR2, CD36, and TNFR2, overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, these PVDF membranes were treated with HRP-conjugated IgG antibody for 2 h, and protein bands were visualized using an imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA).

ROS Responsiveness of NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox

The changes in the “Tyndall Effect” of NP suspensions after exposure to H2O2 (100 μM) were measured by using laser beam irradiation to determine the ROS responsiveness of NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox.

Release Profiles of Dox from Nanoparticles

The release profiles of Dox from NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were investigated, both in the presence or absence of H2O2 (100 μM), using a microplate reader at 480 nm. NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox solutions (10 mg/mL, 5 mL) were added to the dialysis bag (MWCO: 3500 Da) and then immersed in 100 mL of buffer solution. The dialysis bags were shaken with 30g at 37 °C to mimic the in vivo environment. At the predetermined time points, 5 mL of buffer was collected and replenished using fresh solution. After measuring the absorbance, the drug release profiles were plotted by calculating the percentage of drug release based on the standard curve.

Cellular Uptake by RAW 264.7 Cells and 143B Cells

To track the phagocytosis process, the red fluorescent dye DiI was encapsulated into the cores of nanocarriers to obtain the NPs/DiI, MM@NPs/DiI, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI. For the cellular uptake study, RAW264.7 cells were coincubated with 100 μg of NPs/DiI, MM@NPs/DiI, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI in 24-well plates. After 1 or 3 h incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed by PFA, and stained with DAPI and FITC Phalloidin. The fluorescence of the samples was then observed using CLSM, and the fluorescence intensity internalized by the RAW264.7 cells was quantified using ImageJ software.

For cell entrance assay, 143B cells were prepared appropriately in 24-well plates and cultured with medium containing 100 μg of NPs/DiI or IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI. After incubation for 1 or 3 h, cells were stained with DAPI and FITC followed by CLSM observation. The fluorescence intensity in 143B cells was quantified by using ImageJ software.

For flow cytometry, 143B and L929 cells were coincubated with 100 μg of NPs/DiI, MM@NPs/DiI, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI, respectively, for 3 h, using PBS as a negative control. After digesting the cells using trypsin, they were washed 3 times with PBS. Finally, cells were resuspended with 500 μL of PBS and detected via BD Accuri C6 Plus flow cytometer.

Construction of OS Tumor Models

Nude mice and severe combined immune deficiency (SCID) Beige mice were used to establish OS tumor models, with all animals bred and housed under SPF facilities at the Naval Medical University (Shanghai, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Navy Medical University. All of the operating procedures strictly conformed to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and Principles for the Utilization and Care of Vertebrate Animals.

For the subcutaneous OS model, 100 μL of 143B cells (1 × 106 cells/mouse) was subcutaneously injected into the flank using insulin syringes (28 gauge). Subsequently, the mice’s behavior and health and the tumors’ development were monitored continuously.

For the orthotopic OS model, 100 μL of 143B cells (1 × 106 cells/mouse) was directly injected into the femoral membrane of nude mice using insulin syringes (28 gauge) to avoid vascular embolism. Subsequently, the conditions of the mice and the tumors were continuously monitored.

For the OS patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model, tumor tissues were obtained from OS patients with informed consent and the approval of the Ethics Committee of Changzheng Hospital. The tumor tissues were processed into tumor blocks of 27 mm3 followed by transplantation into the limbs of the SCID Beige mouse. Subsequently, the mice’s behavior and health and the tumors’ development were monitored continuously.

Tumor Targeting Evaluation

The in vivo tumor-targeting capabilities of these nanocarriers were determined using the 143B subcutaneous tumor model. NPs, MM@NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs were labeled with fluorescent DiR and intravenously administered to the mice, when the tumor size reached approximately 27 mm3. The mice were then anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane through inhalation at different time points. The distribution of NPs/DiR, MM@NPs/DiR, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiR was observed using a bioluminescence imaging system (IVIS Lumina XRMS Series III, PerkinElmer, USA) at 1, 6, and 10 h. Moreover, 6 h after injection, mice were euthanized through intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (200 mg/kg), and the major organs and tumors were collected for imaging. The distribution of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox was analyzed through the IVIS imaging system at 24, 36, and 48 h after injection.

Cell Viability Assay

CCK8 assays were used to determine the safety of nanoparticles (NPs, MM/NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs) and the cytotoxicity of nanomedicines (NPs/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox) in vitro.

For the in vitro safety evaluation of these nanocarriers, the viability of 143B cells was assessed by using a CCK8 assay after incubation with NPs and IL-11/MM@NPs. Briefly, L929 fibroblasts and 143B cells were prepared appropriately and coincubated with NPs and IL-11/MM@NPs at different concentrations (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mg/mL) in 96-well plates, respectively. After incubation for 24 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% CCK8 reagent. After incubation for another 2 h, the optical density (OD) of these samples was measured with a microplate reader spectrophotometer at 450 nm.

For the in vitro cytotoxicity assay of these nanomedicines, 100 μL of saline, free Dox, NPs/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox (Dox: 0.1 μg/mL) were added into 143B and L929 cells incubating for 24 h. Subsequently, cell viability of 143B and L929 cells was determined using a CCK8 assay, as described previously.

Hemolysis Assay

5% erythrocyte solutions containing 0.5% (w/v) Triton X-100 (positive control), PBS (negative control), and different concentrations of nanoparticles (0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1 mg/mL) were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The samples were then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatants were collected to measure released hemoglobin at A540 using a microplate reader.

In Vivo Biocompatibility Evaluation of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox

To determine the in vivo biocompatibility of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox, 6 week-old nude mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 5) and intravenously injected with an equal amount of saline and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox (200 mg/kg), respectively, every 2 days for 14 days. The changes in the body weight of the two groups were monitored. On day 14, all mice were euthanized through intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium (200 mg/kg), and the main organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected for histological study. Furthermore, blood serum was collected for blood chemistry analysis, including liver function biomarkers (AST and ALT) and kidney function biomarkers (BUN and UA) analysis.

Cell Clone Assay

For the colony formation assay, 143B cells were incubated with a medium containing free Dox, NPs/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox (Dox: 0.1 μg/mL). After incubation for another 4 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium for further incubation. Over the next 9 days, the medium was refreshed every 3 days. On day 9, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 1% crystal violet. The cells were then prepared for photographing, and the colonies were quantified by using ImageJ software.

Transwell Assay

For the cell migration and invasion assays, 4 × 103 143B cells were suspended in 100 μL of serum-free medium and plated in Transwell chambers with or without Matrigel. Subsequently, 600 μL of DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin was added to the bottom of the chamber. After incubation for 36 h, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 1% crystal violet blue solution. The positively stained cells were observed and photographed with a light microscope and quantified using ImageJ software.

In Vivo Antitumor Efficacy of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in the OS Mouse Model

Treatment began when the inoculated tumor volume reached approximately 64 mm3 after incubation. Mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 5). Each group received 200 μL of either saline, free Dox, NP/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, or IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox via tail vein injection at a Dox dose of 6 mg/kg on days 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24. Their body weight and tumor volume were measured every 3 days, and the survival rates were monitored. The mice were considered dead when they died naturally or when the tumor volume exceeded 2000 mm3.

Tumor volume (V) was computed as V = 0.5 × (ab2 – cd2) (mm3), where “a” represents the length, “b” represents the width of the tumor-bearing hind leg, and “c” and “d” represent the length and width of the corresponding part of the health leg.28

For treating the OS PDX model, administration begins when the tumor volume reaches approximately 64 mm3. The mice were intravenously administered with 200 μL of either saline, free Dox, or IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox (Dox: 4 mg/kg) on days 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, and 28 (n = 5 in each group). Body weight and tumor volume were measured continuously. On day 30, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and tumors were removed and weighed.

All of the animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Zhejiang Center for Laboratory Animals (ZJCLA-IACUC-20010604), and experiments were performed according to the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals along with the approval of SPF Animal Center of Navy Medical University.

The study using clinical samples, including osteosarcoma tumors and matched adjacent normal tissues, was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Changzheng Hospital (2024SL046). Informed consent was obtained from all of the subjects or their relatives.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical differences among groups were assessed by a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). *p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001 indicated increasing levels of statistical significance.

Results and Discussion

Construction of IL-11/MM

IL-11Rα has been identified as a molecular target in OS, expressed both in the cytoplasm and on the cell surface.27,28 To verify this, we observed IL-11Rα expression in OS tissues obtained from patients at our hospital. We analyzed OS tissues (n = 12) and their adjacent noncancerous tissues (n = 12). Our results indicated that the IL-11Rα expressions in OS tissues are statistically higher than those in the adjacent normal tissues (Figure 1A,B), confirming the specific antigenic role of IL-11Rα in OS. In addition, we evaluated the IL-11Rα expression in various OS cell lines, revealing high levels in 143B, LM7, and SJSA1 cells, while MG63, SAOS2, and HOS cells showed relatively minimal expression (Figure 1C,D). This pattern suggests that IL-11Rα may serve as a distinctive antigen associated with OS, predominantly expressed on cytoplasm and cell surface domains.34 Thus, IL-11Rα holds the potential as a target for identifying and potentially targeting OS.

We aimed to express IL-11 on MM to target OS cells by recognizing IL-11Rα on their surface. To achieve this, we constructed a recombinant plasmid, pDisplay-IL-11, through cloning IL-11 cDNA into the pDisplay vector using restriction endonucleases30 (Figure 1E). The pDisplay is a plasmid that facilitates the cloned gene to express as a cell membrane-bound protein.30 Following plasmid amplification and purification, the recombinant plasmid pDisplay-IL-11 was transfected into RAW264.7 cells to acquire IL-11-engineered RAW264.7 cells (Figure 1E). To assess the transfection efficiency, we stained RAW264.7 cells with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (blue) and tagged IL-11 with fluorescence antibodies (red). Under confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), we observed a red band surrounding the blue nucleus, preliminarily confirming the successful expression of IL-11 on the surface of RAW264.7 cells (Figure 1F). The MMs loaded with IL-11 were then isolated from the RAW264.7-pDisplay-IL-11 cells for further usage. Finally, western blot analysis further confirmed the expression of IL-11 on the RAW264.7-pDisplay-IL-11 cell (Figure 1G and 1H).

Preparation and Characterization of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox

To obtain IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox, we first prepared MMs expressing IL-11, as described previously. Subsequently, we synthesized ROS-responsive NPs loaded with Dox. First, we obtained PBAP-CDI-Mannose (PCM), a ROS-responsive amphiphilic molecule, by conjugating 4-(hydroxymethyl) phenylboronic acid pinacol ester (PBAP) with d-mannose as shown in Figure 2A. The arylboronic esters tend to be oxidized by H2O2; thus, PCM would degrade into p-hydroxy-methylphenol, pinacol borate, and mannose when exposed to an environment with a high concentration of ROS.33 The chemical structure of PCM was confirmed by proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy (Figure 2B). Subsequently, the NPs/Dox were fabricated through a self-assembly process29 (Figure 2C). The hydrophobicity of PBAP and the hydrophilicity of d-mannose determined the amphiphilic properties of these NPs. Utilizing this amphiphilic property, the NPs/Dox self-assembled in an aqueous solution when mixed PCM with Dox, during which the drug was encapsulated into the hydrophobic core (Figure 2C). We then employed a dialysis method32 to ensure efficient encapsulation of Dox within the NPs (Figure 2C). Subsequently, the IL-11/MM, isolated from RAW264.7-pDisplay-IL-11 cells, were coated onto the surface of NPs/Dox using an extrusion method33 (Figure 2C). According to the results from the microplate reader, the drug encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading efficiency (LE) for NPs/Dox were 87.78% and 7.98%, respectively. For IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox, they were 85.92% and 7.81%, respectively (Table 1).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to observe the structure of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. As shown in Figure 3A, whereas NPs/Dox displayed a homogeneous spherical nanoscale morphology, IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox exhibited a core–shell nanostructure. Specifically, NPs/Dox were encapsulated within a spherical core, with a single corona layer wrapped outside. In addition, the core–shell morphology was further confirmed through a cell phagocytosis colocalization experiment. Here, we replaced Dox with DiI to stain the NPs (red) and used DiO to stain IL-11/MM (green). Dio-IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI were coincubated with 143B cells for 4 h, and the uptake of fluorescent NPs by 143B cells was observed through CLSM. The red fluorescence near the nuclei of 143B cells (blue) exhibited substantial overlap with the green fluorescence (Figure 3B), indicating successful coating of IL-11/MM on the NPs.

Furthermore, dynamic laser scattering (DLS) was utilized to observe the hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of both NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. As shown in Figure 3C, the average hydrodynamic diameter was measured as 132.5 nm for NPs/Dox and 162.9 nm for IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. The finding indicated that upon coating with IL-11/MM, the average hydrodynamic diameter of NPs/Dox increased by approximately 30 nm, corresponding to the thickness of the IL-11-engineered MM on the surface.35 Additionally, DLS analysis (Figure 3D) revealed that the zeta potential of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox decreased to −20.9 mV, similar to the zeta potential of the macrophage surface (−22.3 mV), and more negative compared to that of NPs/Dox (−9.8 mV). Zeta potential is essential in maintaining the stability of physicochemical properties and the biological performance of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in vivo.(17) Moreover, this zeta potential also confirmed the successful fabrication of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. The PDIs of NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were 0.14 and 0.20, respectively, further indicating these nanocarriers’ stability.

Finally, western blot analysis (Figure 3E) verified the expression of IL-11 and key functional proteins typically found on MMs in IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox, including chemokine (C–C motif) receptor 2 (CCR2), tumor necrosis factor receptor 2 (TNFR2), and CD36 (Figure 3E). CCR2 plays a crucial role in macrophage recruitment to the tumor sites,36 While IL-11 enhances OS cell-specific targeting ability.28 TNFR2 and CD36 help prevent undesirable phagocytosis by mononuclear phagocytes.37,38 Taken together, the morphological and proteomic analyses validated the successful construction of the IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox.

Drug Release Profile of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox

Due to the ROS-sensitivity of PBAP moieties on NPs, both the NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox would disassemble in environments with high concentrations of ROS.29 To confirm their ROS responsiveness, NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox were placed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with or without H2O2, and changes in the Tyndall effect—a red visual effect caused by these NPs—were observed. Obviously, the red color of both NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox solution faded when coincubated with H2O2 (100 μM) for 4 h, whereas minimal color changes were observed in solution without H2O2 (Figure 3F). It indicated that these NPs degraded in ROS-rich environments, and the presence of IL-11/MM did not affect the ROS responsiveness of NPs/Dox. The mechanism of NP degradation lies in the oxidization of arylboronic ester meditated by H2O2 (Figure 2A).33

Given the high concentration level of ROS in OS cells,11 the ROS-responsive property of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox ensured efficient drug release. To test this hypothesis, a microplate reader was used to measure the cumulative release of Dox from NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. As shown in Figure 3G, IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox exhibited a similar accumulative drug release rate compared with NPs/Dox, with rapid release observed within the first 6 h. Furthermore, the accumulative drug release rate of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox increased from 61.2% to 84.5% in response to 100 μM H2O2, which was close to the 88.7% release observed from NPs/Dox. These findings demonstrated that these nanocarriers efficiently released drugs in response to high ROS levels and that the IL-11-engineered MM did not hinder the ROS responsiveness of the nanocarriers. Specifically, the IL-11-engineered MM coated outside did not impede the degradation of these ROS-responsive NPs, resulting in the efficient local release of Dox.

Stability and Immune Escape of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox

Despite achieving a high accumulative drug release rate, clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system prior to reaching the tumor site remains a challenge for the application of ROS-responsive nanodrug delivery systems.33 Therefore, we investigated the stability and uptake efficiency of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox by macrophages. Interestingly, the size of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox remained nearly unchanged in PBS over 24 h (Figure 4A). To maximum simulate the in vivo environment, we also determined the size change of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in PBS with 10% FBS, which contains electrolytes and proteins.39 Similarly, the diameter of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox remained nearly unchanged for 7 days (Figure S1). These results indicated the excellent stability of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in non-ROS environments, benefiting the specific drug release and deposition to reduce the systemic toxicity.

Stability and immune escape of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. (A) Size changes of NPs/Dox and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in PBS for 24 h. (B) Cellular uptake of NPs/DiI, MM@NPs/DiI, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI by RAW264.7 cells (scale bar = 50 μm), and (C) quantification of fluorescence intensity (n = 3, mean ± SD).

The functionalized MM coated on the surface could help NPs/Dox to undergo autologous camouflage, thereby evading clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system.21 To verify this hypothesis, we conducted an in vitro macrophage phagocytosis experiment. For tracking the cellular uptake process, DiI was loaded inside of these nanocarriers to create NPs/DiI, MM@NPs/DiI, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI, respectively. RAW264.7 cells (macrophages) were stained with DAPI (blue) and FITC-phalloidin (green) to visualize macrophage phagocytosis locations. The fluorescence intensity of RAW264.7 cells was observed using CLSM after incubation with these nanoparticles for 1 and 3 h. Figure 4B,C shows that high fluorescence intensity was observed in RAW264.7 cells incubated with NPs/DiI for 3 h, indicating significant uptake of NPs/DiI by RAW264.7 cells. In contrast, the fluorescence intensities were markedly lower in RAW264.7 cells incubated with MM@NPs/DiI and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI, suggesting that either the MM or the IL-11-engineered MM coating modification effectively enhanced the ability of the nanodrug delivery system to evade macrophage clearance. In addition, the negative charge of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox could decrease the potential attachment to macrophages,17 further decreasing the immune clearance.

In summary, these findings suggested that the functionalized biomimetic nanocarrier IL-11/MM@NPs could remain stable in the non-ROS environment and evade the clearance from the immune system, which may benefit the circulation time of IL-11/MM@NPs in vivo compared to nonmodified nanocarriers.

Targeting Ability of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox

Considering the expression of IL-11Rα in OS tissues and OS cell lines, the incorporation of IL-11-loaded MM onto NPs/Dox would enhance their targeting ability toward OS cells.27 To verify this hypothesis, we further investigated the uptake efficiency of IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI by the 143B cells. The strongest red fluorescence was observed in 143B cells after 3 h incubation with IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI using CLSM (Figure 5A), indicating the significant high uptake by 143B cells. In contrast, weaker fluorescence was observed in 143B cells incubated with NPs/DiI or MM@NPs/DiI (Figure 5A,B). In addition, flow cytometry was performed to determine the difference between IL-11Rα enriched 143B cells and normal cells (L929 cells) in the uptake capacity of IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI. The results showed no distinguishable difference between the uptake of NPs/DiI, MM@NPs/DiI, or IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI in L929 cells (Figure S2A,B). The IL-11Rα expression in L929 cells was significantly lower than that in 143B cells (Figure S2C). Consistently, compared to L929 cells, the uptake of IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI was much higher in 143B cells (Figure S2D), and the mean fluorescence intensity of IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI in 143B cells was 10-fold that of in L929 cells. Moreover, the mean fluorescence intensity in the IL-11/MM@NPs/DiI group was 2.88- and 3.95-fold that of the MM@NPs/DiI and NPs/DiI group, respectively (Figure S2E). These findings suggest that the strategy of incorporating IL-11-engineered MM greatly enhanced the active targeting ability of the drug delivery system toward OS cells. The IL-11 engineered on the MM significantly enhanced the uptake of this nanoparticle by osteosarcoma cells. Considering the confirmed ROS-responsiveness, these results also suggest excellent intracellular drug release capabilities of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox.

The tumor accumulation of NPs, MM@NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs was compared using a 143B cell subcutaneous tumor model. NPs, MM@NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs were labeled with DiR for measurement using an in vivo imaging spectrum system (IVIS). As shown in Figure 5C, green fluorescence signals from NPs/DiR, MM@NPs/DiR, and IL-11/MM@NPs/DiR were clearly observed at the tumor sites at 1, 6, and 10 h after intravenous injection. The fluorescence intensity in the IL-11/MM@NPs/DiR group was significantly higher than that in the other two groups (Figure 5C), suggesting that IL-11/MM@NPs might have a distinct advantage in promoting drug accumulation in 143B tumors compared to NPs or MM@NPs. Additionally, ex vivo imaging of the major organs further confirmed that the IL-11/MM@NPs exhibited superior selectivity in delivering and depositing into OS tumor tissues than NPs and MM@NPs (Figure 5D,E). Moreover, we further observed the long-term biodistribution of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox under the same conditions. As shown in Figure S3, the IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox had a remarkable tropism of the tumor site and could accumulate in the tumor site for a relatively long time (48 h). Together, these findings suggest that the MM inherited immune characteristics favorable for tumors and the IL-11 engineering modification enhanced the OS cell-targeting ability of the nanocarriers, thereby reducing internalization by other cell components in OS tissues.

In Vitro and In Vivo Biocompatibility Assessment

After confirming the stability, prolonged blood circulation, and the OS targeting ability of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox, we proceeded to investigate the biocompatibility of NPs, MM@NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs. 143B cells were selected as a mode to assess the cell compatibility of these nanocarriers using a CCK8 assay in vitro. As shown in Figure 6A, all three nanocarriers showed favorable cell viability even at 1 mg/mL of NPs. To further confirm this result, we carried out another cell viability assay using L929 cells, the main components of tissues in mice, and the results were similar to those of the 143B cells (Figure 6B). Together, these results indicated that either IL-11, MM, or NPs had no significant cytotoxicity.

For the potential in vivo applications, the hemocompatibility of IL-11/MM@NPs was assessed through a hemolysis assay.40 As shown in Figure 6C, the IL-11/MM@NPs exhibited excellent hemocompatibility even at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. Moreover, the hemolysis ratios of NPs, MM@NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs were 0.089%, 0.124%, and 0.089% at a concentration of 1 mg/mL, respectively (Figure 6D), indicating their good hemocompatibility. In addition, we further examined the biocompatibility of IL-11/MM@NPs systemically in vivo through histological studies. H&E staining results indicated that IL-11/MM@NPs did not cause apparent toxicity to the main organs, particularly no cardiomyocyte apoptosis was observed (Figure 6E). Blood biomarkers tests showed that ALT and AST (liver function biomarkers) and UREA and CREA (kidney function biomarkers) levels in mice administrated with IL-11/MM@NPs were comparable to those of the control group33 (Figure 6F). In addition, the changes in body weight of mice treated with IL-11/MM@NPs were similar to those of the control group during the follow-up period (Figure 6G). Together, these results demonstrate the excellent biocompatibility of IL-11/MM@NPs both in vivo and in vitro, suggesting desirable safety for potential applications.

In Vitro OS Inhibition

After confirming the biocompatibility of NPs, MM@NPs, and IL-11/MM@NPs, we investigated the inhibitory effect of Dox encapsulated in these nanocarriers on OS cells in vitro. The cytotoxicity assessed using a CCK8 assay demonstrated a dose-dependent inhibition of 143B cells (Figure 7A), with NPs/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox all exhibiting greater toxicity compared with free Dox. The IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox showed the highest toxicity among the three groups, with an IC50 of only 0.0486 ug/mL, which was 10.1-, 3.8-, and 2.8-fold lower than that of free Dox, NPs/Dox, and MM@NPs/Dox, respectively.

Additionally, the cell clone assay was adopted to analyze the inhibitory effects of these nanomedicines on 143B cells. NPs/Dox and MM@NPs/Dox significantly reduced the formation of 143B cell clones compared to free Dox, whereas IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox almost completely inhibited clone formation (Figure 7B,C), indicating a superior inhibition of 143B cell proliferation.

Furthermore, the Transwell assay evaluated the effects on 143B cell invasion and migration. As shown in Figure 7D−G, IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox observably suppressed the invasion and migration abilities of 143B cells compared to free Dox, NPs/Dox, and MM@NPs/Dox.

These results confirmed that IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox might have a remarkable ultimate inhibitory effect on the proliferation, invasion, and migration of 143B cells, making it a promising candidate for OS treatment in vivo. NPs/Dox may be eliminated by the immune system due to its exogenous nature, and MM@NPs/Dox may face challenges in accumulating sufficiently within tumor cells due to the complex components of OS. However, IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox offers targeted interaction and immune escape capabilities, potentially providing superior advantages for anti-OS therapy in vivo.

Therapeutic Efficacy in the 143B Orthotopic OS Model

We further investigated the therapeutic efficacy of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox against OS using a 143B orthotopic OS model in vivo. Mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 5) and treated intravenously with saline, free Dox, NPs/Dox, MM@NPs/Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox (all at a dose of 6 mg/kg Dox) every 4 days starting 2 days after tumor implantation (Figure 8A). As shown in Figure 8B,C, the saline-treated group exhibited the largest tumor volume of approximately 2000 mm3, with mice showing paralyzed legs. Free Dox and NPs/Dox slightly slowed tumor growth with abnormal leg movement observed by day 26. Interestingly, MM@NPs/Dox, which we expected to be effective due to the MM coating, showed no significant difference in tumor growth compared to that of NPs/Dox-treated mice. In contrast, IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox significantly inhibited tumor growth, with no visible tumors, the lowest tumor weight (Figure S4), and healthy legs observed by day 26. Survival curves indicated that mice treated with IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox had the longest median survival time compared to other groups (Figure 8D). In fact, NPs/Dox and MM@NPs/Dox also improved the median survival time compared to the saline group.

Previously, we confirmed the prolonged blood circulation time and excellent targeting ability of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in OS tissues and cells. Together, these findings indicate that IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox effectively enhanced chemotherapy efficacy against 143B cell orthotopic OS tumors.

Therapeutic Efficacy on the OS PDX Model

Building on its promising outcomes in orthotopic OS models, we next evaluated the efficacy of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in an OS PDX model.41 The PDX model was established by subcutaneously transplanting OS patient tumor tissue into severe combined immune deficiency (SCID) beige mice.28 Mice with tumors of approximately 50 mm3 were treated via intravenous injection of 100 μL of saline, free Dox, and IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox at (Dox: 4 mg/kg) every 5 days (n = 5) (Figure 8E). Results demonstrated that IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox significantly inhibited tumor growth over 30 days, surpassing the efficacy observed in other treatment groups (Figure 8F,G). The tumor weight was reduced by more than 80% for IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox compared to approximately 40% for free Dox alone (Figure 8H), highlighting the enhanced anti-OS efficacy of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox in the PDX model.

These findings underscored the targeted therapeutic potential of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox specifically tailored to patient-derived OS xenografts. Targeting IL-11Rα through modified nanoparticles represents a promising strategy for enhancing chemotherapy effectiveness against the advanced OS.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed MM-coated ROS-responsive NPs to enhance the chemotherapy efficacy for OS. Given the overexpression of IL-11Rα in OS patients and cells, which binds specifically to IL-11, we genetically modified RAW264.7 cells to stably express IL-11 (RAW264.7-pDisplay-IL-11). Subsequently, we used these IL-11/MM@NPs loaded with the potent anti-OS chemotherapeutic agent Dox to create IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox. Initially, we assessed the ability of IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox to target OS tissues and IL-11Rα-enriched cells with excellent stability in vitro. Subsequently, we evaluated its therapeutic efficacy in vivo using both a 143B orthotopic OS model and an OS-PDX model. Our findings suggest that IL-11/MM@NPs/Dox may hold promise as a safe and efficient chemotherapy option for OS.
