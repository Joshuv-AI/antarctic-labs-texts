Magnitude of short birth intervals and associated factors among reproductive-age women in sub-Saharan Africa: a multilevel modified Poisson regression analysis of recent DHS data (2021–2024)
Authors: Temesgen Gebeyehu Wondmeneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundShort birth intervals remain a significant public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), adversely affecting maternal and child health. Although socioeconomic, reproductive and healthcare factors influence birth spacing, evidence from recent demography and health survey (DHS) data is limited. This study aims to determine the magnitude of short birth intervals and identify associated factors among reproductive-age women in SSA using multilevel modified Poisson regression, providing easily interpretable evidence to inform policy and intervention programs.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed recent DHS data from SSA countries to examine short birth intervals and associated factors among women aged 15–49 years. A total weighted sample of 345,958 women was included. Multilevel mixed-effects modified Poisson regression was used to account for clustering and estimate adjusted prevalence ratios (APR), with the significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe median birth interval was 32 months. The prevalence of short birth intervals among reproductive-age women in SSA was 50.4% (95% CI: 50.2–50.6). Women aged 25–34 years (APR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.74–0.77) and 35–49 years (APR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.63–0.66), literate women (APR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.91–0.94), women with media exposure (APR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98), those from middle-wealth (APR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.98) and rich households (APR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.88–0.92), women with one under-five child (APR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.86–0.90), women with a history of abortion (APR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.98), current family planning users (APR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99), currently breastfeeding women (APR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.88–0.90), and women with health insurance (APR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.89–0.93) had a lower prevalence of short birth intervals. Conversely, a higher prevalence of short birth intervals was observed among rural residents (APR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04–1.09), women with primary (APR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05) or secondary and higher education (APR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07), women whose husbands had primary (APR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04) or secondary and higher education (APR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06), women living in households with ≥ 5 members (APR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.07–1.12), those with ≥ 5 living children (APR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.34–1.40), those with ≥ 3 under-five children (APR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.08–1.12), women whose husbands alone (APR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05) or jointly with them (APR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03) made family planning decisions, and women who reported distance to a health facility as a big problem (APR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04).ConclusionShort birth intervals remain highly prevalent among reproductive-age women in SSA. Socioeconomic status, reproductive history, family planning use, and access to health services significantly influence birth intervals. Strengthening women’s empowerment, improving access to family planning and maternal health services, and addressing rural and household-level disadvantages are essential to promote optimal birth spacing and improve maternal and child health outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Short birth intervals, commonly defined as the time between two successive live births of less than 33 months [1, 2], remain a pervasive public health problem affecting maternal, neonatal, and child health outcomes in many low and middle income countries, particularly in SSA [3, 4]. Evidence indicates that shorter interval between births is associated with increased risks of preterm birth, low birth weight, maternal anemia, and higher rates of infant and under five mortality [5, 6], which collectively hinder progress toward global health targets such as Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). From a physiological perspective, insufficient recovery time between pregnancies can deplete maternal nutrient stores and impair optimal reproductive health, thereby increasing risk of adverse outcomes for both mothers and infants [7]. Additionally, short birth interval have been associated with child growth faltering and stunting, mediated by maternal anemia and low birth weight [8].

Evidence from a systematic review conducted in the Asia–Pacific region indicated that the prevalence of short birth intervals was 33.8% [9]. Multicountry analyses of DHS data indicated substantial variation in the prevalence of short birth intervals across SSA, ranging from 11.27% in Zimbabwe to 30.2% in Chad [10]. In high-fertility SSA countries, nearly 59% of reproductive-age women were found to experience short birth intervals, with significant differences observed according to socioeconomic and reproductive characteristics [11]. Women with low education, who are employed, have limited access to contraception, live in poverty, have high parity, have illiterate husband, who do not breastfeed, or reside in rural areas are at higher risk of experiencing short birth intervals [11, 12]. Tanzanian DHS data showed that younger, high-parity, married, and rich women were more likely to experience short birth interval [13]. Bangladesh DHS data showed that women’s empowerment, education, and economic status increase birth intervals, helping to reduce fertility rates [14]. Studies have also reported that proximity to health facilities, female household heads, having living children, access to family planning, antenatal care (ANC), and postnatal care are associated with a lower risk of short birth intervals [15–17].

Despite recognition of these adverse consequences, evidence on the magnitude and associated factors of short birth intervals in SSA using recent DHS datasets remains limited. Most existing studies focus on individual countries or small country clusters and often use varied analytic methods with inconsistent definitions of short birth intervals. This highlights the need for a multilevel analysis of recent DHS data to estimate prevalence and identify associated factors in SSA, using modified Poisson regression to produce prevalence ratios that are easily interpretable and actionable for policymakers. Findings from such analyses can inform reproductive health policies and family planning programs across diverse demographic settings, enabling targeted interventions to promote optimal birth spacing and improve maternal and child health outcomes.

Methods

Study design, setting, and data sources

This study employed a cross-sectional design using secondary data obtained from DHS conducted between 2021 and 2024. The analysis focused on reproductive-age women in selected countries in SSA. A total of 11 countries were included: Tanzania, Senegal, Mozambique, Mali, Madagascar, Lesotho, Kenya, Ghana, Côte d’Ivoire, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, and Burkina Faso. Data were drawn from the most recent publicly available DHS datasets for each country during the study period. The DHS program collects nationally representative household survey data that provide detailed information on fertility, maternal and child health, family planning, and socio-demographic characteristics. The datasets used in this study were accessed from the DHS Program website at https://dhsprogram.com/data/ [18].

Study population

The study included women aged 15–49 years from SSA countries who participated in the most recent DHS conducted between 2021 and 2024. The analysis considered births that occurred within the five years preceding each survey (0–59 months before the interview). In accordance with DHS analytical guidelines for preceding birth interval analysis, only non-first births were eligible because a preceding birth interval cannot be defined for first-order births. Therefore, first-order births and their twins were excluded from the analysis. Births occurring more than 59 months before the survey were also excluded. After applying these DHS eligibility criteria, births with a defined preceding birth interval were included in the final analysis.

Sampling design and data collection procedures

The surveys employed a stratified two stage cluster sampling design to ensure national and subnational representativeness, including both urban and rural populations. The sampling frame in each country was based on the most recent national population census. Stratification was performed by place of residence, urban and rural, and, where applicable, by administrative regions or provinces. In the first stage, enumeration areas or clusters were selected using probability proportional to size sampling. In the second stage, households within each selected cluster were chosen through systematic sampling. All eligible women aged 15 to 49 years who were usual residents or present in the selected households at the time of the survey were interviewed. Data were collected through face to face interviews using standardized and pretested questionnaires administered by trained personnel. The women’s questionnaire captured detailed information on birth histories, reproductive health indicators, and socio demographic characteristics. The use of uniform procedures across countries ensured data quality and comparability and allowed for the generation of reliable national and subnational estimates, as well as pooled analyses [18].

Sample size determination

From the initial sample of 507,020 reproductive-age women in the pooled DHS datasets, births that did not meet the DHS definition for preceding birth interval analysis were excluded (n = 141,771). Specifically, first-order births and their multiples (e.g., twins), as well as births occurring more than 59 months before the survey, were excluded because preceding birth intervals cannot be calculated for these cases according to DHS guidelines. After applying these eligibility criteria, a total of 365,249 births remained for analysis. Therefore, the excluded observations were due to analytical restrictions rather than missing data. The combined weighted sample comprised 345,958 women across the 11 countries. Sample sizes varied by country according to population size and survey implementation (Table 1).

Variables

Outcome variable

The primary outcome was short birth interval, defined as the duration between two consecutive live births of less than 33 months, in accordance with World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations. Birth interval was measured using the DHS birth history variable b11, which records the number of months since the preceding live birth. Only women with at least two live births were included in the analysis; first-order births were excluded because there was no preceding birth interval. The outcome variable was coded as binary (short birth interval: <33 months; not short: ≥ 33 months) [1, 19].

Independent variables

Independent variables in this analysis were selected based on evidence from previous studies on factors associated with birth interval. The study included a range of socio-demographic, maternal, household, reproductive, and community-level characteristics. All variables were derived from DHS dataset. Several variables were recoded or categorized to improve interpretability and ensure consistency with prior research (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Stata version 17. Sampling weights were applied to account for the complex survey design and to ensure national representativeness. Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the characteristics of the study participants. Due to the hierarchical structure of the DHS data, a three-level multilevel modified Poisson regression model was fitted to identify factors associated with short birth intervals. In this model, individual women (level 1) were nested within clusters or enumeration areas (level 2), which were further nested within countries (level 3). This approach accounted for potential clustering and country-level variations across the pooled Sub-Saharan Africa. The model was specified using a log link function with a Poisson distribution and robust variance estimation to obtain adjusted prevalence ratios. The general form of the model can be expressed as: log(µ_ijk) = β0 + β1 × 1_ijk + β2 × 2_ijk + … + βpXp_ijk + u0k + v0jk.

Where: µ_ijk = expected probability of a short birth interval for woman i in cluster j within country k; β0 = overall intercept; β1…βp = regression coefficients for explanatory variables; X1_ijk … Xp_ijk = individual, household, and community-level explanatory variables; u0k = random effect for country k; v0jk = random effect for cluster j within country k.

Assumptions for random effects: u0k ~ N(0, σ²u), and v0jk ~ N(0, σ²v).

Exponentiating the regression coefficients (exp(β)) gives the adjusted prevalence ratios (APR) with 95% confidence intervals. This approach directly estimates APRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), which are more interpretable than odds ratios in cross sectional studies with common outcomes. When the outcome is common, logistic regression may substantially overestimate the magnitude of association because the odds ratio diverges from the prevalence ratio. The modified Poisson approach for binary outcomes, as described by Zou [20], uses a Poisson regression model with a robust sandwich variance estimator to provide consistent estimates of prevalence ratios and correct standard errors. This method has been widely recommended for cross sectional and cohort studies when the outcome is common [21]. To account for clustering, a random intercept was specified at the cluster level, allowing the baseline risk of short birth interval to vary across enumeration areas and capturing unobserved contextual heterogeneity. This approach adjusts for intra cluster correlation and produces more accurate standard errors and effect estimates in hierarchical data structures. Both bivariable and multivariable models were fitted. Variables with a p value less than 0.2 in the bivariable analysis were entered into the multivariable model to control for potential confounding. Statistical significance in the final model was declared at a p value less than 0.05. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs).

Model fitness

Model fitness was assessed using log-likelihood, deviance, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). The log-likelihood reflects how well the model predicts the observed data, with higher values indicating better fit. Deviance, calculated as − 2 × log-likelihood, quantifies model misfit and allows formal comparison of nested models using chi-square tests. Both AIC and BIC provide criteria for model selection by balancing goodness of fit with model complexity; AIC applies a moderate penalty for additional parameters, while BIC applies a stronger penalty in larger samples. In the model selection process, nested models were first compared using deviance, while AIC and BIC were used to compare both nested and non-nested models. The model with the lowest deviance, AIC, and BIC values, indicating the best balance between fit and parsimony, was selected as the final model for inference.

Measurement of variation (Random effects)

Since women were nested within enumeration areas (clusters), between-cluster variability was quantified using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), Proportional Change in Variance (PCV), and Median Prevalence Rate Ratio (MPRR).

Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC): The ICC measures the proportion of total variance attributable to between-cluster variability. For a two-level model: ICC = σ²u / (σ²u + σ²e), where σ²u = between-cluster variance and σ²e = within-cluster variance. For Poisson models, the individual-level variance is assumed to follow a log distribution. When approximated using the latent variable method: ICC = σ²u / (σ²u + π² / 3).

Proportional Change in Variance (PCV): PCV quantifies the percentage reduction in cluster-level variance after adding explanatory variables to the model: PCV = (σ²u (null) − σ²u (model)) / σ²u (null), where σ²u (null) = variance in the empty model, and σ²u (model) = variance in the adjusted model.

Median Prevalence Rate Ratio (MPRR): The MPRR translates cluster-level variance into an interpretable prevalence rate ratio scale: MPRR = exp(0.6745 × √(2 × σ²u), Where σ²u = cluster-level variance, and 0.6745 corresponds to the 75th percentile of the standard normal distribution. The MPRR represents the median increase in the prevalence of short birth interval when comparing two women from clusters with higher versus lower risk.

Assessment of missing data patterns and mechanisms

The extent and pattern of missing data were assessed for all explanatory variables prior to multivariable analysis. Important maternal healthcare variables, including ANC, postnatal care, place of delivery, and fertility preference for waiting time until the next birth, were excluded from the multivariable analysis due to substantial missing data (> 80%). In the DHS, these variables are collected only for the most recent birth within the 3 to 5 years preceding the survey, resulting in structural missingness for women with longer reproductive histories. For variables with moderate levels of missingness, the proportion of incomplete observations and the overall missing data pattern were examined. The structure of missingness was evaluated to determine whether it followed a monotone or non-monotone pattern. To explore the mechanism of missingness, binary indicators were created for each variable with missing data. Logistic regression models were fitted to assess whether missingness was associated with observed covariates. This approach was used to evaluate whether the data were consistent with missing completely at random or missing at random assumptions. In addition, missingness indicators were incorporated into the outcome model to assess whether missingness was associated with the dependent variable, birth interval. The strength and statistical significance of these associations were examined to further inform assumptions regarding the missing data mechanism. Based on the observed patterns and associations, appropriate methods for handling missing data were determined. Multiple imputation was considered for variables with moderate levels of missingness and evidence supporting the missing at random assumption.

Multiple imputation procedure

To address missing data, multiple imputations by chained equations was applied. Twenty imputed datasets were generated. An iterative chained equations approach was used, specifying appropriate regression models according to the measurement level of each variable. Binary logistic regression models were fitted for dichotomous variables, and multinomial logistic regression models were used for categorical variables with more than two categories. All variables included in the main analysis model, along with relevant covariates associated with missingness and the outcome, were incorporated into the imputation model to satisfy the missing at random assumption. The imputation procedure was run for a sufficient number of iterations to ensure convergence and stability of parameter estimates. After imputation, the datasets were combined, and parameter estimates were pooled using Rubin’s rules to obtain overall estimates and standard errors that account for between and within imputation variability.

Ethical approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for each country’s DHS cycle was obtained by the respective national implementing agencies in collaboration with the Ministries of Health, Institutional Review Boards, and National Statistical Authorities. All participating countries adhered to established ethical standards for human subjects research, including voluntary participation, informed consent, and the protection of confidentiality. Trained fieldworkers obtained informed consent from all respondents at the time of data collection, and assent procedures were followed for minors in accordance with country-specific protocols. The present study used de-identified DHS data obtained through formal approval from the DHS Program (ICF International) after submission of a research proposal via the official data access portal. The data were accessed and used in accordance with the DHS Program’s terms and conditions, which require the protection of respondents’ confidentiality. As this study involved the secondary analysis of publicly available, anonymized data, additional ethical approval was not required. The research was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines for secondary data analysis.

Results

Demographic characteristics of reproductive-age women in sub-Saharan Africa

A total weighted sample of 345,958 reproductive-age women (unweighted = 365,249) was included in the study. The majority of women were aged 35–49 years (63.5%). Most women lived in large households with more than four members (82.9%) and had more than four living children (55.1%). More than three-quarters (77.7%) of households had at least one child under five years of age. Most women were married (87.2%) and employed (65.7%), while 90% of husbands were employed. A large proportion resided in rural areas (71.9%) and in male-headed households (77.4%). About 43.7% of women had no formal education, and 57.8% were illiterate. Similarly, 40.9% of husbands had no formal education. Nearly two-thirds (65.3%) of women had media exposure, and nearly half (46.6%) belonged to poor households. More than one-third (37.3%) of women reported that distance to a health facility was a major problem, and only 10.2% utilized health insurance. Regarding family planning utilization, 66% of women were not currently using any method, while decision-making on family planning was mainly joint (38.3%). Among participants, 40.5% preferred having another child, and 55.5% favored a waiting time of less than two years before the next birth. Approximately one-third of women (31.1%) were currently breastfeeding. Most women had no history of abortion (78%). While 59.8% of women had four or more ANC visits and 75.6% gave birth in health facilities, only 25.9% utilized postnatal care services. Nearly half of the women lived in communities with low literacy (49.6%), low wealth (51.1%), and low media exposure (45.9%) (Table 3).

Demographic characteristic of reproductive-age women in Sub-Saharan Africa

Short birth interval among reproductive age women in Sub-Saharan Africa

In SSA countries, the average birth interval was 38.2 months (SD = 23.6), with a median of 32 months and an interquartile range of 23 months. This indicates that half of the women have birth intervals between two years (first quartile = 23 months) and four years (third quartile = 46 months). The lower median compared with the mean reflects a right-skewed distribution, in which most women have relatively shorter intervals, while a smaller proportion have much longer spacing. The magnitude of short birth intervals among reproductive-age women in SSA was 50.4% (95% CI: 50.2–50.6), indicating that approximately one in every two women experienced a short birth interval. The very narrow confidence interval demonstrates a high level of precision in the estimate, likely due to the large sample size. The highest prevalence of short birth intervals is observed in the Democratic Republic of Congo (61.2%) and Mali (60.0%), indicating that more than half of women in these countries have births spaced shorter than the recommended interval. In Madagascar (53.7%), Senegal (51.7%), and Kenya (51.2%), roughly half of women experience short birth intervals. Tanzania (49.7%), Côte d’Ivoire (48.5%), and Mozambique (45.7%) show slightly lower prevalence, while Ghana (41.2%), Burkina Faso (42.3%), and Lesotho (30.9%) have the lowest prevalence, suggesting comparatively better birth spacing practices. Overall, short birth intervals are common across these countries, but there is substantial variation (Fig. 1).

Evaluation of missing data and underlying mechanisms

Several explanatory variables had substantial missing data, including ANC at 84.44%, postnatal care at 84.32%, place of delivery at 81.94%, and fertility preference for waiting time until the next birth at 62.5%. Because these variables exceeded 50% missingness, they were excluded from the multivariable analysis to prevent biased estimates and loss of statistical power.

For the remaining six variables, namely distance to health facility, husband’s occupation, health insurance, husband’s education, fertility preference, and family planning decision making, 69% of observations were complete, while 31% had at least one missing value. Missingness ranged from 7% for distance to a health facility and fertility preference to 22% for family planning decision-making. It was 14.7% for health insurance, 15.3% for husband’s education, and 12.4% for husband’s occupation. The pattern of missingness was non monotone, which supports the use of multiple imputations. For these six variables, logistic regression showed no variation in missingness for distance to the health facility; therefore, it could not be modeled. In contrast, missingness in health insurance was significantly associated with the observed covariates (LR χ² = 4675.12, p < 0.001). Missingness in health insurance was negatively associated with distance to the health facility, family planning decision-making, fertility preference, and husband’s occupation, and positively associated with husband’s education. When missingness indicators were included in the birth interval model, missingness in health insurance significantly predicted the outcome (p < 0.001). These results indicate that the data are unlikely to be missing completely at Random and are more consistent with missing at Random. Therefore, multiple imputations are preferred over complete-case analysis to reduce potential bias for these variables.

Results of multiple imputation

For the Six variables, namely distance to the health facility, husband’s occupation, health insurance, husband’s education, fertility preference, and family planning decision making, the multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) procedure was successfully performed using 20 imputations (m = 20). A total of 20 imputed datasets were generated, and no previous imputations were updated. The algorithm ran for 200 iterations with a burn-in period of 10 iterations, indicating adequate convergence and stabilization of the imputation process. Binary logistic regression models were used to impute distance to the health facility, husband’s occupation, and health insurance status, whereas multinomial logistic regression models were applied for family planning decision-making, fertility preference, and husband’s education. The total analytic sample ranged from 364,805 to 365,249 observations across variables. For distance to the health facility, 339,766 observations were complete and 25,483 were incomplete; 25,252 values were imputed out of 365,249 total observations. For family planning decision-making, 284,907 observations were complete and 80,325 were incomplete, with 80,094 values imputed out of 365,232 total observations. For husband’s education, 309,253 observations were complete and 55,552 were incomplete, of which 55,517 were imputed from a total of 364,805 observations. For husband’s occupation, 320,054 observations were complete and 44,979 were incomplete; all 44,979 missing values were imputed out of 365,033 total observations. For health insurance, 311,444 observations were complete and 53,805 were incomplete, with 53,279 values imputed out of 365,249 total observations. Of the total 365,249 observations, 339,766 had complete data on fertility preference, while 25,483 had missing values. All 25,483 missing observations, representing approximately 7.0% of the total sample, were imputed. The slight differences between the number of incomplete and imputed observations arise because the “Imputed” column reflects the minimum number of filled-in values across the 20 imputed datasets. Overall, the successful completion of the imputation process and the large proportion of missing values addressed support the robustness of the subsequent multivariable analyses.

Measure of variations

Cluster-Level Variation (v021)

The cluster-level (v021) variation in birth interval was minimal across all models. In the null model, the ICC was 0%, and the MRR was 1.0, indicating that virtually no variation in birth interval existed between clusters before adjusting for covariates. When only individual-level factors were included, the PCV was 82%, showing that most of the small residual cluster-level variation was explained by differences in individual characteristics. Including only community-level covariates accounted for 0% of the cluster-level variance, whereas the full model, incorporating both individual- and community-level covariates, explained 86.7% of the cluster-level variance. Overall, these results indicate that differences between clusters contributed negligible variability in birth interval, and that the observed cluster-level variation was largely explained by individual-level factors.

Country-Level Variation (v021 > v000)

In contrast, country-level variation was more substantial. The null model showed an ICC of 4.6% and an MRR of 1.23, suggesting that women with identical characteristics in higher-risk countries had approximately 23% higher rates of short birth interval compared with women in lower-risk countries. Adjusting for individual-level covariates reduced the ICC to 1.64% and the MRR to 1.15, indicating that individual characteristics explained a large portion of country-level differences, as reflected in a PCV of 62.1%. Including only community-level covariates reduced the ICC to 3.6% and the MRR to 1.2, with a PCV of 21.5%, showing that community context explains some, but less, of the country-level variation. The full model including both individual and community-level factors produced an ICC of 1.5% and an MRR of 1.13, with a PCV of 65.2%, confirming that most country-level variation is accounted for by individual-level covariates, with community-level factors contributing modestly (Table 4).

Results of model fitness statistics

The fitness of the multilevel models was evaluated using multiple criteria, including the log-likelihood ratio (LLR), deviance (-2LLR), AIC, and BIC. In general, higher log-likelihood values (closer to zero) and lower deviance, AIC, and BIC values indicate better model fit. Comparing the models, the final full model including both individual- and community-level covariates showed the best fit. Specifically, the log-likelihood improved from − 291,934.8 in the null model to -200,235 in the full model, indicating a substantial increase in explanatory power. The corresponding deviance decreased from 583,869.6 to 400,470, and both AIC (400,532.1) and BIC (400,854.4) were lower in the full model than in the null or intermediate models. These improvements suggest that the inclusion of both individual- and community-level variables significantly enhanced the model’s ability to explain variation in birth interval, providing the best balance of fit and parsimony among the compared models (Table 4).

Associated factors of short birth interval

Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using VIF. The VIF values ranged from 1.01 to 2.49, with a mean of 1.38, indicating no evidence of problematic multicollinearity, as all values were well below the commonly used threshold of 5. However, despite acceptable multicollinearity diagnostics, fertility preference and marital status were excluded from the final multilevel modified Poisson regression model due to limited within-cluster variation and estimation instability when accounting for cluster-level random effects. Consequently, their independent effects could not be reliably estimated in the full model, while all other covariates were successfully retained.

In the full model, several individual- and household-level factors were significantly associated with short birth intervals. Women aged 25–34 years had a 24% lower prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.74–0.77), and those aged 35–49 years had a 35% lower prevalence (APR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.63–0.66) compared with women aged 15–24 years. Rural residence was associated with a slightly higher prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 1.06, 95% CI: 1.04–1.09) compared with urban residence. Women with primary education (APR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05) and those with secondary or higher education (APR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.07) had a higher prevalence of short birth intervals compared with women with no formal education. Similarly, women whose husbands had primary education (APR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.04) or secondary and higher education (APR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.02–1.06) had a slightly higher prevalence compared with those whose husbands had no formal education. Women living in households with five or more members had a 9% higher prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.07–1.12) compared with those in smaller households. Literate women had a 7% lower prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.91–0.94). Media exposure was also associated with a reduction in the prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.95–0.98). Women from middle-wealth households had a 3% lower prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.98), and those from rich households had a 10% lower prevalence (APR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.88–0.92) compared with women from poor households. Women with five or more living children had a 36% higher prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.34–1.40) compared with those with a smaller number of living children. Having one under-five child was associated with a decreased prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.86–0.90), whereas having three or more under-five children was associated with a 10% higher prevalence (APR = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.08–1.12). Women with a history of abortion (APR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.96–0.98), current family planning use (APR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.97–0.99), and current breastfeeding (APR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.88–0.90) were associated with a lower prevalence of short birth intervals. Women whose husbands or partners made decisions regarding family planning use (APR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.05), or who made joint decisions with their husbands or partners (APR = 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.03), had a slightly higher prevalence of short birth intervals compared with women who made family planning decisions independently. Women reporting distance to a health facility as a big problem was associated with an increase in the prevalence of short birth intervals (APR = 1.03, 95% CI: 1.02–1.04), while having health insurance was associated with a lower prevalence (APR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.89–0.93) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study examined the prevalence and associated factors of short birth intervals among reproductive age women in SSA using recent DHS data. The findings showed that the median birth interval was 32 months, and half (50.4%) of women experienced short birth intervals of less than 33 months. The observed median and magnitude of short birth interval suggested that substantial proportion of women in SSA give birth within a timeframe considered suboptimal for maternal and child health. The high prevalence underscores the persistence of short birth interval as a major public health concern in the region, consistent with evidence highlighting its contribution to adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes [3–6]. This prevalence is substantially higher than estimates reported in Asia-Pacific regions that was 33.8% [9] and earlier multicountry DHS analyses, which ranged from 11.27% in Zimbabwe to 30.2% in Chad [10], but slightly lower than findings from high fertility SSA countries, where nearly 59% of women experienced short birth intervals [11]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study populations, particularly the inclusion of high-fertility reproductive-age women in the previous study [11], as well as variations in time periods, methodological approaches, sociocultural factors, and participant characteristics. The high prevalence observed in the current study reinforces concerns that short birth spacing continues to undermine progress toward improved maternal and child health outcomes and SDGs [3, 4]. This study demonstrates considerable cross-country variation in the prevalence of short birth intervals across SSA, although the practice remains widespread in all countries examined. The highest prevalence was observed in the Democratic Republic of Congo and Mali, where more than six in ten women experienced short birth intervals. Approximately half of women in Madagascar, Senegal, and Kenya also had closely spaced births. In contrast, Ghana, Burkina Faso, and particularly Lesotho had the lowest prevalence, suggesting relatively better birth spacing practices. The markedly high prevalence in countries such as the Democratic Republic of Congo and Mali may reflect persistent structural and contextual barriers, including high fertility norms, limited contraceptive uptake, weak health system coverage, sociocultural expectations favoring large family size, and gender norms that constrain women’s reproductive autonomy. In fragile or resource-limited settings, disruptions in health service delivery and limited access to postpartum family planning may further contribute to closely spaced births [11–17]. These contexts often combine high desired fertility with unmet need for contraception, accelerating successive childbearing.

Women aged 25 to 34 and 35 to 49 years were less likely to experience short birth intervals compared to younger women. This finding is consistent with evidence from Tanzania showing that younger women are more likely to have short birth spacing [13]. The higher likelihood of short birth intervals among younger women may be explained by life course and reproductive factors. Women in the early stages of marriage often have stronger fertility intentions and aim to reach their desired family size quickly. Consistent with this, a study in Lesotho found that younger women were less likely to use modern contraceptives than older women, suggesting a preference for early and closely spaced childbearing [22]. They may also have limited autonomy in reproductive decision-making and reduced ability to negotiate contraceptive use, which increases the probability of closely spaced births. In contrast, older women are more likely to have reached or approached their desired family size and may therefore adopt contraception more consistently, resulting in longer birth intervals. Furthermore, closely spaced pregnancies can worsen maternal nutrient depletion [7], a risk that is particularly important among adolescents whose biological growth may still be ongoing, thereby increasing their vulnerability to adverse maternal and child health outcomes [3–6, 8]. Women from middle- and high-wealth households were less likely to experience short birth intervals, consistent with evidence that poverty increases the risk of closely spaced births [11]. Media exposure was identified as a protective factor against short birth intervals, likely by enhancing women’s knowledge of family planning, reproductive health, and appropriate birth spacing practices. This finding is consistent with evidence from the Bangladesh DHS, which reported that women’s empowerment, education, and economic status were associated with longer birth intervals [14]. Altogether, these results highlight the critical role of informational and educational interventions in promoting optimal birth spacing and improving reproductive health outcomes. Health insurance coverage similarly reduced the likelihood of short birth intervals. Health insurance improves access to health services by reducing financial barriers, facilitating use of maternal, child, and family health services [15–17], supporting continuity of care, and promoting health-seeking behavior, ultimately reducing catastrophic expenditures and improving health outcomes, especially in low- and middle-income settings [3–8]. The findings revealed a notable contrast between literacy and formal education. Literacy was associated with a reduced risk of short birth intervals, likely reflecting its role as a proxy for functional skills, including the ability to access, understand, and apply health-related information. Literate women are more likely to comprehend family planning messages, engage with health services, and adopt optimal birth spacing practices. This interpretation is supported by DHS evidence linking women’s empowerment and access to information with longer birth intervals [14]. In contrast, primary and secondary/higher educational attainment was positively associated with short birth intervals, diverging from prior studies suggesting that lower education increases the likelihood of short spacing [11, 14]. This discrepancy may reflect contextual and behavioral factors in SSA. More educated women often marry later due to prolonged schooling, which compresses the reproductive period and may lead them to intentionally shorten birth intervals to achieve their desired family size [23]. However, formal schooling alone may not ensure adequate reproductive health knowledge or behavior change, particularly in settings where access to quality family planning services remains uneven. The observed positive association between maternal education and short birth intervals should therefore be interpreted with caution, as it may be influenced by residual confounding and contextual factors. Maternal age, household wealth, and place of residence are closely linked to educational attainment and may partially explain this relationship. Educated women are more likely to delay childbearing, resulting in older maternal age at subsequent births, and to live in urban areas and participate in the workforce, which may contribute to shorter breastfeeding durations. Reduced breastfeeding can shorten the period of lactational amenorrhea, leading to earlier resumption of ovulation and an increased likelihood of shorter birth intervals. Overall, the association likely reflects a combination of socioeconomic, behavioral, and biological mechanisms rather than a direct effect of education alone, and should be interpreted in light of potential residual confounding and contextual heterogeneity. Women with five or more living children or households with more than five members, and those with three or more under-five children, were significantly more likely to experience short birth intervals, consistent with evidence that high parity increases the risk [13, 15–17]. In high-fertility contexts, closely spaced births may reflect continued childbearing to achieve desired family size or limited access to contraception [24]. Conversely, having one under-five child was protective, possibly due to greater caregiving demands and breastfeeding practices that naturally extend interbirth intervals through lactational amenorrhea. Current breastfeeding was significantly associated with reduced short birth intervals, supporting physiological evidence that lactational amenorrhea suppresses ovulation and prolongs postpartum infertility [25, 26], thereby increasing spacing [7, 11]. Women who were currently using family planning were less likely to have short birth intervals, reinforcing well-established role of contraception in promoting optimal birth spacing [15–17]. Husband-only or joint decision-making regarding family planning was positively associated with short birth intervals. This may reflect limited female autonomy, whereby male-dominated decision-making could reduce effective contraceptive uptake despite nominal involvement. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of women’s empowerment and access to reproductive health services in extending birth intervals [14]. Rural residence and reporting distance to a health facility as a big problem were associated with a higher prevalence of short birth intervals, consistent with earlier reports [11, 12]. The disadvantage of rural residence remained even after controlling for distance to a health facility, indicating that factors beyond physical accessibility contribute to shorter birth intervals. These may include inadequate health infrastructure, limited availability of reproductive health services, and lower exposure to reproductive health information. In addition, restricted access to contraceptives and postpartum care can further increase the likelihood of closely spaced pregnancies, highlighting the need for comprehensive interventions that address both service delivery and informational barriers [15–17].

Limitations and strengths of study

Strengths

This study used recent (2021–2024), nationally representative DHS data from SSA countries, enhancing external validity and regional generalizability to the included countries. The very large pooled weighted sample provided substantial statistical power and produced highly precise estimates. Appropriate statistical modeling further strengthened the validity of the findings. The use of multilevel modified Poisson regression allowed direct estimation of adjusted prevalence ratios, avoiding the overestimation that can occur with odds ratios when outcomes are common. Model diagnostics demonstrated strong improvement in fit from the null to the full model, supporting the robustness of the final model. Although the magnitude of between-cluster variation was small, multilevel modeling was statistically justified. Missing data were systematically assessed rather than ignored. The study evaluated missing data mechanisms and found patterns consistent with missing at random (MAR). Multiple imputation by chained equations was successfully implemented, imputing a substantial number of missing observations. This approach reduced bias compared with complete-case analysis and strengthened internal validity. Multicollinearity was carefully assessed, confirming the stability and independence of predictors. The study also transparently excluded fertility preference from the final multilevel model due to estimation instability and limited within-cluster variation, demonstrating methodological rigor. Finally, the study identified several statistically significant and policy-relevant associated factors of short birth intervals. The consistency and precision of these adjusted prevalence ratios across models further support the robustness of the findings.

Limitations

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although associations were identified, temporal relationships cannot be definitively established, and reverse causality cannot be ruled out. Substantial missingness was observed for key maternal healthcare variables, including antenatal care, postnatal care, and place of delivery. In the DHS, these variables are collected only for the most recent birth within the 3–5 years preceding the survey. As this study included women with longer reproductive histories, these variables were structurally missing for a large proportion of observations (> 50%). Consequently, they were excluded from the multivariable analysis, limiting the assessment of important healthcare-related factors influencing birth spacing. Their exclusion may have affected the estimated effects of access-related variables, such as distance to a health facility and health insurance coverage; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. A substantial number of women were excluded from the analysis due to missing information on short birth intervals. This exclusion limits generalizability to all reproductive-age women and may introduce selection bias. Although multilevel modeling was applied, the magnitude of clustering was small, indicating that community-level factors contributed relatively little to the overall variability. Important higher-level contextual factors (e.g., national policies and cultural norms) were not explicitly modeled, and pooling heterogeneous countries may mask country-specific differences. Some exposure variables (e.g., current family planning use and breastfeeding status) were measured at the time of the survey rather than across the entire interbirth interval. This temporal misalignment may introduce measurement bias. Birth interval data relied on retrospective self-reported birth histories, which may be subject to recall bias, particularly for events occurring many years before the survey.

Policy implications

The high prevalence of short birth intervals in SSA underscores the need to prioritize birth spacing as a core strategy within reproductive, maternal, newborn, and child health programs. Governments should strengthen national family planning policies by ensuring universal access to affordable, high-quality modern contraceptive services, particularly for young women, rural populations, and high-parity mothers. Expanding health insurance coverage and financial protection schemes is essential to reduce cost-related barriers to maternal and reproductive health services. Education policies that promote female literacy and the retention of girls in secondary school can have long-term benefits for optimal birth spacing. Additionally, policies should address rural–urban inequities through investments in primary healthcare infrastructure, transportation, and community-based service delivery. Gender-responsive policies that strengthen women’s decision-making autonomy while constructively involving men in reproductive health are essential. The current findings that joint and husband-only decision-making increase the risk of short birth intervals suggest that even joint decisions may reflect the husband’s fertility preferences, highlighting the need to empower women in reproductive choices. In addition, targeted interventions such as postpartum family planning programs are recommended to bridge the gap between delivery and the next pregnancy, particularly for high-risk groups such as women in rural areas and those with high parity. Finally, behavior change communication strategies leveraging mass media and community platforms should raise awareness of recommended birth spacing and its associated health benefits. This approach is supported by our finding that women with media exposure had a 3% lower likelihood of experiencing short birth intervals, highlighting the important role of media in promoting optimal reproductive health behaviors.

Conclusion

This study highlights that short birth intervals remain a substantial public health concern in SSA, with half of reproductive-age women experiencing intervals below the recommended duration. Birth spacing is shaped by a multifaceted interaction of demographic, socioeconomic, reproductive, and healthcare access factors, including maternal age, parity, breastfeeding status, contraceptive use, household wealth, and access to health services. The unexpected positive association with educational attainment suggests the need for further context-specific research to clarify underlying mechanisms. Strengthening family planning services, promoting optimal breastfeeding practices, enhancing women’s empowerment, expanding equitable access to maternal and reproductive health services, and improving rural healthcare infrastructure are essential strategies to reduce short birth intervals and improve maternal and child health outcomes in the region. Addressing socioeconomic disparities and implementing integrated interventions that combine education, service accessibility, and community-level development may be particularly effective in promoting optimal birth spacing and reducing the burden of short birth intervals across sub-Saharan Africa.
