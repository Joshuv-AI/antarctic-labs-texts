A spontaneous rupture of a simple renal cyst complicated by hemorrhagic shock: a case report
Authors: S Taouihar, H Dergamoun, A Bouabdallaoui, Y Hadzin, A El Gdaouni, FZ Haddari, A El Mekkaoui, O Benlenda, I Ziouziou, H Nassik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction and importance:The spontaneous and non-traumatic rupture of renal cysts is a rare clinical condition, which can lead to hemorrhagic shock. In this paper, the authors report a case of spontaneous rupture of a renal cyst and discuss the main features of this condition.Case report:A 21-year-old patient presented urgently with hemorrhagic shock with no history or evidence of trauma. Physical examination revealed a swollen left lumbar fossa on palpation. Abdominal CT revealed an abundant retroperitoneal hematoma. Resuscitation measures were urgently initiated to ensure hemodynamic stabilization, followed by a hemostasis nephrectomy. The patient was stabilized, and subsequent postoperative recovery proceeded without complications. He was sent home on the seventh day following his admission.Discussion:Spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhages may stem from various pathologies, including simple renal cysts, and frequently manifest with Lenk’s triad. Wunderlich syndrome, an infrequent manifestation of such hemorrhages, necessitates prompt recognition and hemodynamic stabilization, with potential treatment including embolization or surgical intervention.Conclusion:Although simple renal cysts are generally asymptomatic, they can lead to serious complications such as hemorrhage that could be life-threatening without appropriate medical intervention. The treatment involves performing an emergency nephrectomy for hemostasis, along with implementing resuscitation measures.Keywords: hemorrhagic shock, kidney, nephrectomy, renal cyst, retroperitoneal hematoma, spontaneous rupture

Abdominal pain constitutes a common motive for visits to the emergency department. This symptom can lead to various diagnoses, ranging from benign to more serious conditions, sometimes with atypical presentations1. The patient’s age, along with their risk factors and comorbidities, serve as pivotal criterion for directing diagnostic evaluations, formulating differential diagnoses, evaluating risks, and devising management strategies for abdominal pain1.

This condition, characterized by the sudden rupture of a cyst within the kidney resulting in retroperitoneal and intraperitoneal hemorrhage, presents a diagnostic challenge due to its nonspecific symptoms and potential for rapid deterioration. While uncommon, the severity of this condition is underscored by the potential for life-threatening complications, especially when complicated by hemorrhagic shock. Urgent intervention is imperative to mitigate adverse outcomes2,3.

In this paper, we report a case of spontaneous rupture of a renal cyst and discuss the main features of this condition. This work was reported according to the SCARE 2023 criteria4.

Case

A 21-year-old man arrived at the emergency department reporting diffuse abdominal pain and vomiting that had started the previous day. He has no notable medical or traumatic history aside from a history of smoking and cannabis use.

The clinical evaluation showed an conscious but restless individual with a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 15. Initial blood pressure was 90/42 mm Hg, heart rate was 150 beats per min, respiratory rate of 25 cycles per min and oxygen saturation was 97% while receiving oxygen via a 5-liter-per-min mask. The patient exhibited pallor and discolored conjunctivae, along with prolonged capillary refill time. Abdominal examination revealed diffuse abdominal pain with generalized tenderness, and percussion indicated a dullness shifting with changes. The remainder of the clinical examination did not identify any significant abnormalities.

Laboratory tests showed a significant drop in hemoglobin from 7.5 to 5.5 g/dl, thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 117 000/mm3, prothrombin time of 58%, and an activated cephalin time was 1.5. renal function was altered with creatinine levels at 15.11 mg/l and urea at 0.34 g/l; liver function was unremarkable.

Hemorrhagic shock management included the placement of a right central jugular catheter for initial fluid resuscitation with a crystalloid solution at 500 ml/h. Tranexamic acid was administered intravenously at a dose of 1 g concurrently with transfusion of 2 units of packed red blood cells and platelets. Additionally, a low-dose catecholamine infusion at 0.5 mg/h was initiated to stabilize the patient’s hemodynamic status.

The abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan showed a large retroperitoneal hematoma around and near the kidney, measuring 270×137×100 mm, with active bleeding evident from arterial extravasation of contrast medium originating from the left medio-lobar renal artery. There was also significant intraperitoneal fluid accumulation noted in the left parietocolic gutter and Douglas’ cul-de-sac (Fig. 1).

Computed tomography abdomen demonstrated voluminous retroperitoneal peri- and para-renal hematoma t of the left kidney.

For emergent intervention, the patient was immediately transferred to the operating room. The hematoma was evacuated, and a left nephrectomy was conducted via a median laparotomy approach to achieve hemostasis, control bleeding and stabilize the patient’s hemodynamic condition (Fig. 2).

Image showing the nephrectomy section with rupture of the renal cortical cyst and the evacuated hematoma.

The left colon was mobilized medially to expose the renal fascia. Large clots were extracted, followed by ligation of the renal pedicle using Hem-o-Lock clips.

The pathological anatomy analysis reported a simple renal cyst with no malignancy cells (Fig. 3).

(A) Histological section showing a cystic wall with hemorrhagic and congestive changes. (B) Histological section showing the cyst lined with a regular, single-layered cuboidal epithelium.

Following surgery, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit, where he was gradually weaned off catecholamine and extubated. He remained in the intensive care unit for 4 days, during which he showed significant clinical improvement and regained normal renal function. The patient was discharged on 12th day following the surgical operation.

Discussion

Spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage can be caused by various renal and non-renal pathologies, including tumors, vascular changes, infections, polycystic kidney disease, and the presence of simple renal cysts5. Non-renal origins of spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage include rupture of an abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), as well as pancreatic and adrenal pathologies1,6.

Non-traumatic spontaneous renal hemorrhage a rare condition known as Wunderlich syndrome, was initially described in 18561. Patients may present with classic manifestations, such as the Lenk triad characterized by lumbar pain in 67% of cases, followed by hematuria in 40%, or signs of shock or internal bleeding in 26.5%. Other atypical or attenuated signs may include lumbar swelling, fever, oligoanuria, and anemia1,7.

In our patient, two symptoms were observed: abdominal pain and signs of hemorrhagic shock.

Spontaneous kidney rupture remains a rare complication occurring in patients with chronic kidney failure, angiomyolipoma, arteriovenous malformation, vascular conditions such as polyarteritis nodosa, or renal cysts, as seen in our clinical case6,8–12.

Moreover, in a retrospective study conducted by Daskalopoulos et al. 13 over a period of 10 years, spontaneous retroperitoneal hemorrhage was primarily caused by angiomyolipoma and renal cell carcinoma, this was followed by occurrences related to coagulation abnormalities, polyarteritis nodosa, Wegener’s granulomatosis, and cystic kidney diseases.

The simple renal cyst is defined as a non-tumoral parenchymal lesion of epithelial origin, consisting of a thin envelope and purely liquid content, and does not require further follow-up unless the patient is symptomatic2. This definition excludes many cystic conditions of renal parenchyma, whether dysplastic, dystrophic, hereditary, or acquired in chronic hemodialysis5,14.

It is important to highlight that while simple renal cysts are highly prevalent, their frequency escalates with age. These cysts are common as they range from 6% in adults aged 21–40 years to 35% in individuals over 70 years old. However, their spontaneous and unprovoked rupture is deemed a rare occurrence, with only a few cases documented in the literature7,9,14,15.

Diagnosing an uncomplicated simple renal cyst is incidental and presents minimal challenge for imaging techniques. They are usually discovered during a renal or abdominal ultrasound, which confirms the diagnostic criteria. A definitive diagnosis is often achieved through CT scan or MRI with intravenous administration of iodine or gadolinium-based contrast agents. The simple cyst belongs to the Bosniak category, which has been the indispensable tool for diagnosing renal cystic masses on computed tomography scans for the past three decades5.

The underlying mechanisms behind the spontaneous rupture of a simple renal cyst are not fully understood. One hypothesis suggests that an elevated intracystic pressure leads to excessive volume expansion, thereby causing damage to blood vessels9. On the other hand, intracystic hemorrhage may serve as the initial event, triggering subsequent cyst enlargement and eventual rupture9. This latter hypothesis is supported by the observation that this occurrence is more common among patients with coagulation abnormalities or those undergoing anticoagulant therapy, such as warfarin or heparin15,16.

The initial management of Wunderlich syndrome relies on a dual approach: early identification of the syndrome and hemodynamic stabilization of the patient in hemorrhagic shock. To establish an accurate etiological diagnosis, a CT scan must be performed to identify the cause of renal hemorrhage. Once the renal etiology is confirmed, hemostatic treatment may involve embolization or surgical intervention, which could involve total or partial nephrectomy2,5,17.

In our specific case, given the active bleeding and hemodynamic instability, the decision was made to proceed with an open surgical intervention involving left hemostatic nephrectomy.

Although rare, this case highlights a serious condition that requires prompt differentiation from more common causes of abdominal and flank pain. It emphasizes the importance of raising awareness among emergency physicians about Wunderlich syndrome to enhance therapeutic management and improve outcomes for patients.

Conclusion

The spontaneous rupture of a renal cyst remains a rare but significant event that requires careful attention from clinicians, necessitating prompt and appropriate management. Clinicians should keep this possibility in mind when evaluating patients with unexplained flank pain and hemorrhagic symptoms.
