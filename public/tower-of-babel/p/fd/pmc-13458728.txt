Combined application of the modified early warning score and the modified hematoma expansion prediction score for predicting hematoma expansion in intracerebral hemorrhage
Authors: Tao Luo, Jie Peng, Ai Chen, Wei Xiong, Jianchao Xu, Caiyong Jiang, Kehan Li, Yuanshu Yang, Chunhong Li, Jun Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate the predictive value of the combined Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS) and Modified Hematoma Expansion Prediction (MHEP) score for hematoma expansion(HE)in patients with intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). The goal was to provide an accurate and practical tool for risk assessment in this patient population. Clinical data from patients with ICH admitted to Nanchuan Hospital Affiliated to Chongqing Medical University between January 2024 and June 2025 were prospectively collected. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with HE. Subsequently, the predictive performance of the combined MEWS and MHEP scores (termed MEWS-MHEP) was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. A total of 421 patients were enrolled and categorized into the hematoma expansion group (n = 58) and the non-hematoma expansion group (n = 363). Significant differences (P < 0.05) were observed in smoking history, alcohol consumption, hematoma location, hematoma volume, island sign, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, indicating their association with HE. The MEWS showed a moderate correlation with HE (P = 0.004). Both the MHEP score and the combined MEWS-MHEP score were significantly higher in the HE group (P < 0.001). The MEWS-MHEP score yielded an AUC of 0.813, which was significantly higher than that of the MEWS alone (0.616) and the MHEP score alone (0.699) (P < 0.001), with an overall prediction accuracy of 80.76%.The MEWS-MHEP score outperforms either the MEWS or MHEP score alone in predicting HE in patients with ICH. Thus, it represents a reliable tool for emergency risk stratification and may aid in optimizing medical resource allocation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55173-w.Keywords: Intracerebral hemorrhage, Hematoma expansion, Modified Early Warning Score, Modified Hematoma Expansion Prediction Score, Predictive modelSubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience, Risk factors

Introduction

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a frequent neurosurgical emergency and a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide1.Hematoma expansion(HE)is a critical determinant of neurological deterioration and poor outcome, contributing to the high acute-phase mortality observed in approximately 50% of ICH patients2. As hematoma management—be it conservative or surgical—constitutes the primary modifiable treatment target, accurate prediction of HE is paramount for guiding therapeutic decisions and improving prognosis3,4. Existing prediction tools, however, are often limited by suboptimal clinical applicability, inconsistent performance, or a narrow assessment scope. There is, therefore, a clear clinical need for a practical, comprehensive, and user-friendly model to predict HE in ICH.Such a model would enable the early identification of high-risk patients, facilitate timely intervention, and ultimately help reduce disability and mortality rates, alleviate the associated socioeconomic burden, and optimize resource allocation5.To address this gap, we developed and validated a novel multi-dimensional scoring system. By integrating the Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS), which reflects systemic physiological derangement, with the Modified Hematoma Expansion Prediction (MHEP) score, which incorporates local hematoma features, medical history, and key clinical data, we constructed the MEWS-MHEP score. This integrated approach leverages complementary information from different pathophysiological domains, aiming to enhance predictive accuracy while maintaining practicality for use in fast-paced emergency settings.

Methods

Study population

This prospective study enrolled adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) who were admitted to the stroke unit within 24 h of symptom onset. Participants were recruited from Nanchuan District People’s Hospital of Chongqing between January 1, 2024, and June 1, 2025.All patients underwent both baseline and follow-up non-contrast computed tomography (NCCT). Key exclusion criteria were: (a) ICH requiring immediate surgical intervention (e.g., due to massive hematoma, intraventricular casting, acute obstructive hydrocephalus, or brain herniation); (b) hemorrhage secondary to structural lesions (intracranial aneurysm, tumor, or arteriovenous malformation); (c) active systemic or intracranial infection, or coagulopathy; (d) long-term anticoagulation or hemodialysis; (e) known severe bleeding tendency; (f) severe dysfunction of major organs (liver, kidney, heart, or lungs); (g) history of prior stroke or cerebral amyloid angiopathy.Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal surrogates. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nanchuan District People’s Hospital of Chongqing (Approval No. YXYJ-2023-024-02; Date of approval: January 3, 2023).

Clinical data and outcome assessment

Hematoma expansion(HE)was defined as an absolute growth > 6 mL or a relative growth > 33% on follow-up NCCT compared to the baseline scan6.Based on this definition, patients were dichotomized into HE and non-HE groups for comparative analysis.Demographic and clinical data were prospectively collected. These included age, sex, history of hypertension, diabetes, dementia, smoking, alcohol use, and antiplatelet therapy. Clinical and radiographic parameters recorded at admission comprised vital signs (body temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressure), GCS, time from onset to first CT, presence of island sign, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), hematoma location (supratentorial or infratentorial), and hematoma volume. Laboratory data obtained from emergency blood tests included point-of-care blood glucose, activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), and platelet count (PLT).

Definition of key variables

Smoking history was defined as continuous or cumulative smoking for ≥ 6 months. Dementia was diagnosed according to the 2018 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Dementia and Cognitive Disorders.Antiplatelet therapy was defined as the regular use of any antiplatelet agent (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel, or ticagrelor) for thromboembolism prophylaxis prior to hospital admission. MEWS score is a rapid assessment tool based on five physiological parameters: level of consciousness, body temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, and systolic blood pressure, each assigned a specific weight (Table 1)7,8. MHEP score is a revised version of the traditional HEP score. The non-specific item “baseline SAH” was removed, and the “island sign”—a strong predictor of HE—was incorporated. The MHEP score comprises six weighted variables: time from onset to first CT, history of dementia, smoking, antiplatelet therapy, GCS score, and island sign (Table 2)9,10. MEWS-MHEP score was derived by combining the weighted items from the original MEWS and MHEP scales. The integrated scoring system is presented in Table 3.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0). Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). For between-group comparisons, the choice of test was guided by the expected cell frequencies: when all expected cell counts were ≥ 5, the Pearson chi-square test was used, and the P-value was derived from the chi-square distribution with the appropriate degrees of freedom. When any expected cell count was < 5, Fisher’s exact test (two-sided) was applied, with the P-value obtained directly from the hypergeometric distribution.

Continuous variables were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation [SD]), and between-group comparisons were performed using the independent two-sample t-test; the P-value was derived from the t-distribution with (n₁ + n₂ − 2) degrees of freedom. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median (interquartile range [IQR]), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for between-group comparisons; the P-value was obtained from the Z-value corresponding to the approximate normal distribution of the U statistic.

Binary logistic regression was employed to explore factors associated with hematoma expansion and to identify independent predictors. First, univariate regression analyses were performed to calculate the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) for each candidate variable; the P-value was based on the Wald test. Because the combined MEWS-MHEP score already encompassed the MEWS score, the MHEP score, and all their constituent variables (see Table 4, 5 footnote), to avoid multicollinearity, the multivariate logistic regression model only included the MEWS-MHEP score together with other variables that were significant (P < 0.05) in univariate analysis and were not part of these constituent variables. The Enter method (forced entry) was used to select independent predictors, and the adjusted odds ratio (aOR) with 95% CI was reported. The P-values in the multivariate analysis were also based on the Wald test.

The goodness-of-fit and predictive performance of the final multivariate logistic regression model were evaluated as follows: Nagelkerke pseudo-R² was used to estimate the proportion of variance in the outcome explained by the model; the Hosmer-Lemeshow test was applied to assess calibration; and the overall classification accuracy was calculated using a probability cutoff of 0.5.

According to the scoring rules presented in Tables 1, 2 and 3, the MEWS score, MHEP score, and the combined MEWS-MHEP score were calculated by assigning corresponding points to each component variable and summing them. The optimal cut-off values of the three scoring systems for predicting hematoma expansion were determined using Youden’s index (sensitivity + specificity − 1). Based on these cut-off values, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy of each score were calculated. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive ability of each score for hematoma expansion, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to assess diagnostic accuracy. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 537 patients with ICH were initially screened, of whom 421 met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1). Based on the occurrence of hematoma expansion (HE), patients were categorized into HE (n = 58) and non-HE (n = 363) groups. The baseline characteristics of both groups are presented in Table 4.Most baseline variables, including age, sex, history of hypertension or diabetes, antiplatelet therapy, dementia, and vital signs, were comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05). Although not statistically significant, trends toward differences were observed for systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and platelet count (0.05 < P < 0.1). Significant differences were found in smoking history, alcohol use, and hematoma location (P < 0.05). Notably, GCS score, time to baseline NCCT, island sign, and hematoma volume showed strong associations with HE (P < 0.001). The MEWS score was also significantly associated with HE (P = 0.004), and was higher in the HE group. Both the MHEP score and the MEWS-MHEP score were significantly elevated in patients with HE (P < 0.001).

Univariate analysis

As shown in Table 5, multiple factors were significantly associated with HE (all P < 0.05), including smoking history, alcohol use, dementia, diastolic blood pressure (DBP), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, time to baseline NCCT, hematoma location, baseline hematoma volume, island sign, point-of-care glucose, MEWS, MHEP score, and the MEWS-MHEP score.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis

To avoid multicollinearity, the MEWS-MHEP score—a composite variable integrating its component variables (highlighted in bold in Table 5)—was entered into the multivariate logistic regression model as a single entity alongside other significant univariate predictors. The analysis identified the MEWS-MHEP score as an independent predictor of HE (adjusted OR 1.57, 95% CI 1.35–1.81; P < 0.001). Supratentorial hematoma location was another independent predictor (adjusted OR 4.30, 95% CI 1.30–14.26; P = 0.017). Other variables, did not retain independent significance (Table 5). The final multivariable logistic regression model showed good fit (Nagelkerke pseudo-R²=0.290; Hosmer-Lemeshow test: χ²=10.42, df = 8, P = 0.237) and adequate predictive performance, with an overall classification accuracy of 86.7%.

Predictive performance

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis demonstrated that the MEWS-MHEP score had an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.813 (95% CI 0.762–0.863, P < 0.001) for predicting HE (Figs. 3 and 4). Discussion

Intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) is a major cerebrovascular disease with a high incidence in Asia, characterized by mortality and disability rates substantially exceeding those of ischemic stroke. Against the dual backdrop of a rapidly aging population and a rising incidence among young and middle-aged adults, the overall challenge of ICH prevention and management is becoming increasingly severe11,12.Early identification of patients at high risk for hematoma expansion(HE) and optimization of treatment decisions are therefore critical for improving outcomes in ICH13,14.Addressing this clinical need, the present study developed and validated the MEWS-MHEP score and systematically evaluated its predictive value for hematoma expansion in ICH patients. The results showed that at an optimal cut-off value of 7.5, the MEWS-MHEP score achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.813 (95% CI: 0.762–0.863) and a predictive accuracy of 80.76%. Its performance was significantly superior to that of the MEWS alone (AUC = 0.616, accuracy = 42.52%) or the MHEP score alone (AUC = 0.699, accuracy = 62.95%). Notably, all variables incorporated into the score are derived from routine assessments performed at admission, requiring no additional specialized tests. This design aims to balance predictive accuracy with clinical feasibility, offering a new and practical strategy for the rapid assessment of hematoma expansion risk in emergency settings.

Current research on predicting HE primarily follows three directions: clinical scoring models, laboratory parameters, and imaging markers. However, each approach has significant limitations. Clinical scores (e.g., the Brain Score, the 9-point score) are often limited in their adoption in primary care or emergency settings due to complex workflows, incomplete variable systems, or dependence on specialized imaging like CT angiography15,16. Laboratory biomarkers (e.g., inflammatory factors, metabolic markers, coagulation parameters), while pathophysiologically grounded, suffer from high heterogeneity in cut-off values, susceptibility to confounding by systemic conditions (e.g., infection, hepatic or renal dysfunction), and a lack of direct evidence showing that correcting these parameters improves HE outcomes17–20. Imaging signs (e.g., black hole sign, spot sign) can visually indicate hematoma instability but their assessment requires specialized expertise, introduces subjectivity, and fails to capture the patient’s systemic pathophysiological state, thus providing an incomplete risk quantification21–24. Consequently, developing an integrated prediction model that combines easily obtainable clinical parameters with potent imaging features represents a necessary step to overcome the current bottlenecks in HE prediction.

The MEWS-MHEP score was designed to fulfill this integrative need, providing a complementary “systemic-local” assessment. The MEWS component, focusing on vital signs and level of consciousness, can be performed at the bedside within minutes without specialized equipment, offering a rapid quantification of the systemic physiological response to ICH. However, it does not account for local hematoma features or key medical history. To address this gap, we employed the MHEP score, an ICH-specific modification of the traditional HEP score25. To enhance predictive specificity for ICH, we omitted the “baseline CT subarachnoid hemorrhage” item, which is more strongly associated with cerebral amyloid angiopathy or intracranial aneurysm, and incorporated the “island sign”—a potent imaging marker of hematoma expansion26,27. The MHEP score thereby assesses local hematoma stability and the temporal profile of bleeding by integrating key imaging features, time variables (time to first CT), and clinical indicators (GCS score, medical history). The weights assigned within each score reflect pathophysiological relevance. Their combination establishes a dual-dimensional framework that simultaneously evaluates systemic physiological homeostasis and local lesion characteristics, effectively overcoming the coverage limitations of either score used in isolation.While previous studies have combined clinical and imaging markers for HE prediction (e.g., the 9-point score by Brouwers et al., reporting AUCs between 0.72 and 0.77)28, our MEWS-MHEP score achieved a superior AUC of 0.813.Moreover, a key innovation of our model is its reliance solely on routinely available clinical data and standard non-contrast CT, eliminating the need for advanced imaging like CTA. This significantly enhances its operational simplicity and feasibility for widespread clinical adoption.

Previous observational studies have established that a lower GCS score, shorter time from symptom onset to baseline NCCT, presence of the island sign, larger initial hematoma volume, and history of smoking or alcohol use may increase the risk of HE29,30. Our findings are consistent with and reinforce these associations.Both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses confirmed significant associations between HE and the MEWS score (P = 0.015), the MHEP score (P < 0.001), and the combined MEWS-MHEP score (P < 0.001). Importantly, the MEWS-MHEP score remained an independent predictor of HE (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.60, 95% CI 1.40–1.83; P < 0.001) even after adjusting for potential confounders including alcohol use, diastolic blood pressure, hematoma location, baseline hematoma volume, and point-of-care glucose, underscoring the robustness of its predictive value.The three scores exhibited distinct diagnostic profiles. The MEWS score, with its high sensitivity (82.76%), is suitable for initial screening to rapidly identify a broad pool of patients at potential risk. The MHEP score, with moderate sensitivity (68.97%) and specificity (61.98%), provides a more focused assessment of local hematoma stability.A notable finding is that while the combined MEWS-MHEP score demonstrated high specificity (83.20%), its positive predictive value (PPV) was relatively low (38.38%). This phenomenon is primarily attributable to the low baseline prevalence of HE (13.8%, 58/421) in our ICH cohort, a well-recognized statistical characteristic of predictive models operating in low-prevalence settings, rather than a flaw in the score itself31.This finding clarifies the core clinical utility of our score. Its greatest value lies in its high negative predictive value (NPV, 93.79%) consequent to high specificity, enabling the reliable identification of low-risk patients in whom management can be safely streamlined. For patients flagged as high-risk, the score serves as a powerful alert mandating intensified monitoring (e.g., shortened CT intervals) and comprehensive evaluation integrating other clinical data, not as a sole determinant for a specific therapy. This “stratified alert and differentiated management” strategy is key to the score’s potential for optimizing clinical resource allocation.

To translate this “stratified alert” strategy into concrete and practical clinical steps, the MEWS-MHEP score is designed with a focus on practicality. Its strength lies in the effective balance between accuracy and practicality, enabling direct guidance for stratified clinical management. All component variables—vital signs, GCS score, baseline NCCT imaging, and basic medical history—are routinely collected during initial admission assessment, requiring no additional tests. The MEWS can be calculated at the bedside within minutes, the MHEP relies on the interpretation of standard NCCT scans, and the combined score is derived from simple arithmetic summation. This operational simplicity, free from specialized hardware requirements, facilitates its potential adoption even in primary care hospitals or busy emergency departments.Based on the Youden index maximization principle from the ROC analysis, we propose a triage system with three distinct risk strata, each linked to a specific management protocol: (1) High-risk (score ≥ 7.5): Prioritize admission to a neuro-intensive care unit (NICU), initiate close monitoring (e.g., CT every 6 h), and formulate individualized interventions (e.g., intensive blood pressure control, consideration of hemostatic agents) based on a comprehensive assessment including coagulation profile and blood pressure variability. (2) Intermediate-risk (score 4.5–7.4): Manage in a general stroke unit with standard vital signs and neurological monitoring, performing CT scans every 12 h to assess hematoma status. (3) Low-risk (score < 4.5): Observation in a general ward is sufficient. The CT follow-up interval can be safely extended to 24 h (compared to the traditional 6–12 h window), and prophylactic hemostatic agents (e.g., tranexamic acid) may be reasonably withheld.This strategy allows for precision allocation of NICU resources while reducing healthcare costs and the risk of drug-related adverse events in low-risk patients. It aligns closely with the central principle of “avoiding over-treatment” emphasized in the 2022 guidelines for the management of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage32. The absence of adverse outcomes attributable to streamlined management in the low-risk group within our study provides preliminary validation for the safety and reasonableness of this stratified approach.

The practical utility outlined above is grounded in the score’s robust pathophysiological rationale. The superior predictive performance of the combined score stems from its systematic integration and quantification of the multifaceted pathophysiology underlying HE. The “island sign” included in the MHEP component is a decisive predictor, with its presence indicating a substantially higher risk of HE, primarily by reflecting active bleeding from an unstable hematoma wall. Variables such as the GCS score and time to first CT are directly linked to the severity of neurological injury and the temporal profile of bleeding, serving as key drivers related to the timing and seriousness of expansion. Conversely, the MEWS component captures the impact of systemic circulatory derangement on intracranial hemorrhage by quantifying core physiological parameters like blood pressure and heart rate. The underlying mechanism may involve elevated blood pressure increasing perfusion pressure in cerebral small arteries, thereby destabilizing the hematoma wall and promoting continued bleeding. Furthermore, by incorporating medical history factors such as smoking (which showed a significant association, P < 0.05), the MHEP score adds another dimension to risk assessment. This design demonstrates that the MEWS-MHEP score is not a simple aggregation of indicators but a precise mapping of the multi-step pathological cascade in HE, which constitutes the fundamental reason for its predictive advantage over individual scores.

Our findings not only reinforce existing knowledge but also offer new insights. Unlike prior studies that have predominantly focused on systolic blood pressure33, our analysis suggests a potential association between diastolic blood pressure and hematoma expansion. This observation adds a nuanced perspective to potential targets for blood pressure management, although the independent prognostic value of diastolic pressure requires confirmation in future studies. More importantly, multivariate analysis established supratentorial hematoma location as an independent predictor, which aligns with the mechanistic understanding of stability related to local anatomy34. Furthermore, it should be noted that due to the limited sample size, factors such as antiplatelet therapy did not reach statistical significance in our cohort.

The overall implications of this study should be interpreted within its specific design context. By focusing on a well-defined, typical ICH population, we prioritized the evaluation of the MEWS-MHEP score’s utility for the most common clinical decision-making scenarios in the emergency setting. Several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the single-center design may limit the generalizability of our sample. The score’s performance requires external validation in broader, more diverse populations, particularly those with complex comorbidities that were excluded from the present study. Second, the optimal cut-off value and weighting parameters are derived from this specific cohort; thus, their general applicability needs to be further calibrated in independent, prospective cohorts. Finally, this study primarily validated the score’s predictive value for the early event of hematoma expansion; its ability to predict long-term neurological functional outcomes remains unassessed and represents a critical direction for future follow-up research. These limitations do not diminish the preliminary value of our findings but rather outline a clear pathway for the necessary validation steps required prior to potential clinical translation.

Conclusion

The MEWS-MHEP score provides an accurate and practical tool for predicting hematoma expansion in intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) by integrating both systemic physiological status and local hematoma characteristics. Its high specificity and ease of use render it a reliable instrument for rapid risk stratification in the emergency setting. While limitations inherent to its single-center design and potential selection bias are acknowledged, this study offers robust preliminary evidence for its validity. Future multi-center, prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate its generalizability and ability to predict long-term outcomes. Further refinement through the incorporation of dynamic data and advanced analytics may ultimately transform this scoring system into a standardized decision-support tool for ICH emergency management, with the potential to improve patient care.
