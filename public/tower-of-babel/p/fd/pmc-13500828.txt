A precision-constrained framework for evaluating noninvasive biomarkers in MASLD and beyond
Authors: Guangyi Zhang, Xiaohong Wang, Arinc Ozturk, Eugene Cheah, Peng Guo, Marian Martin, Angela Shih, Atul K. Bhan, Nancy Obuchowski, Amon Asgharpour, Arun J. Sanyal, Brian A. Telfer, Theodore T. Pierce, Anthony E. Samir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In patients with Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), accurate and low-cost non-invasive risk stratification remains a major unmet need. We developed a clinical records-based neural network integrating patient history, routine laboratory tests, and ultrasound imaging features. Here we show that in an internal test set (n = 209), the model achieved receiver operating characteristic area under the curve (ROC-AUC) values of 0.85 vs 0.82 (F ≥2), 0.90 vs 0.86 (F ≥3), 0.96 vs 0.89 (F = 4) compared with Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4). To address limitations of ROC-AUC, we applied RP-AUC0.5−0.7, a recall-precision metric focused on clinically relevant precision range, showing improved performance over FIB-4. External validation (n = 194) shows reduced liver biopsy failure rate from 86.6% to 50.0% for at-risk metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) prediction. Our work presents a low-cost neural network improving FIB-4, introduces RP-AUC0.5−0.7 for biomarker comparison, and provides a generalizable framework for evaluating screening biomarkers in clinical care and drug trials.

Introduction

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), affects about 30% of the global population1, with prevalence projected to reach 55.7% by 20402.

Liver fibrosis, characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix proteins, represents a key consequence of disease progression. Patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), previously known as nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), are at higher risk of developing fibrosis than in those with simple steatosis, the early stage of MASLD3,4. Fibrosis severity is not only strongly associated with liver-specific complications but also serves as an overall predictor of mortality, with established links to liver-related and cardiovascular adverse outcomes5,6. In adults with biopsy-confirmed MASLD, advanced fibrosis (F ≥ 3) and significant fibrosis (F ≥ 2), as defined by the NASH Clinical Research Network (CRN) staging system7,8, are independent predictors of cardiovascular disease risk, even after considering traditional cardiovascular risk scores and factors, highlighting the importance of fibrosis severity in primary cardiovascular prevention9.

Patients with significant or advanced fibrosis face the highest risk of severe liver-related complications, including cirrhosis, portal hypertension, hepatic failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma, highlighting the importance of liver fibrosis as a key marker for identifying patients who would benefit most from early therapeutic interventions10,11. A high-risk subgroup within this population, referred to as at-risk MASH, is defined as MASLD activity score (MAS, formerly known as NAS [NAFLD activity score]) ≥4 with F2-F3 fibrosis12,13. This group is associated with a greater likelihood of progression to cirrhosis and serves as the target study population for most Phase IIb/III clinical trials for MASH14. Thus, it is essential to make accurate staging of liver fibrosis in MASLD patients to guide clinical decisions and select appropriate candidates for emerging therapies3. The development of reliable biomarkers to accurately predict significant or advanced fibrosis is crucial for enhancing patient stratification and optimizing therapeutic strategies15–18. Recently, researchers have shown growing interest in stratifying patients with more advanced fibrosis19, recognizing that cirrhosis (F = 4 by NASH CRN) is a dynamic and a potentially reversible condition20,21, especially in its compensated stage. Identifying high-risk individuals within this group may enable earlier interventions to prevent decompensation22.

Liver biopsy remains the reference standard for assessing liver fibrosis, offering a direct histological evaluation of liver tissue, including fibrosis staging23. However, due to its invasive nature, high cost, susceptibility to intra- and inter-observer variability, sample variability, and associated risks, including bleeding and infection, there is a growing demand for non-invasive alternatives to diagnose liver fibrosis24–26.

Several non-invasive methods have been developed to address the limitations of liver biopsy, including blood-based biomarkers and imaging techniques. Routine and low-cost laboratory tests, for example, the AST to platelet ratio index (APRI)27 and Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) Index19 are attractive as they add minimal cost to standard care. FIB-4 was initially developed to rule out advanced fibrosis in HIV-HCV co-infection19,28 and later applied to MASH, guiding monitoring and risk assessment when no therapies existed. Updated thresholds of ≤1.30 and ≥2.67 have been proposed for F3-F4 in MASLD19, offering improved predictive values. However, specificity and predictive performance remain dependent on disease prevalence, with lower prevalence populations showing reduced positive predictive value (PPV) and high rates of indeterminate results19. With antifibrotic treatments, its low PPV in the general population29 and limited benefit from secondary tests highlight the need to reassess screening strategies to better identify patients with MASH and significant fibrosis or cirrhosis who may benefit from treatment. Proprietary blood-based tests, including Enhanced Liver Fibrosis (ELFTM) and NIS4TM, offer improved diagnostic performance but are limited by higher cost and accessibility30,31.

Imaging techniques provide a complementary approach. Ultrasound (US)-based methods include transient elastography (TE), shear wave elastography (SWE)32, while magnetic resonance imaging based methods include magnetic resonance elastography. Machine learning and deep learning models have also been applied to imaging or routine clinical data for fibrosis staging33–37. Although these methods have shown reasonable diagnostic performance, their clinical application is often constrained by cost, accessibility, or variability in measurement38–41. Most prior studies report performance primarily using conventional metrics such as area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC).

A key challenge in liver fibrosis research is the lack of accuracy in non-invasive assessment of fibrosis severity. For example, patients with FIB-4 score below 1.3 may still have advanced fibrosis42, while many with scores above 2.67 do not43, indicating misclassification at clinically relevant thresholds44. Similar limitations have been observed in other widely used tools, such as vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE)45. These inaccuracies pose barriers to effective risk stratification of liver fibrosis. Meanwhile, both APRI and FIB-4 rely on formula-derived fixed cutoffs for risk stratification and do not generate probabilistic scores (0–1), which limits their ability to support dynamic risk stratification for varied clinical needs, such as those in MASLD/MASH drug trials.

Another challenge involves the limitations of commonly used performance metrics such as ROC-AUC. While ROC-AUC summarizes overall test performance, it does not reflect the trade-off between sensitivity and specificity at clinically actionable thresholds. In practice, increasing sensitivity typically reduces specificity and vice versa, leading to false positives or negatives that may affect clinical decision-making. Clinical applications often require a balanced and acceptable range of sensitivity and specificity, rather than extreme values of either metric, though certain clinical settings may prioritize high sensitivity or specificity depending on the consequences of misdiagnosis. Furthermore, the prevalence of liver fibrosis varies across clinical settings, low in the general population and primary care, but higher in hepatology clinics. ROC-AUC may overestimate performance in low-prevalence settings as it reflects the overall discrimination without accounting for disease prevalence. The area under the recall-precision curve (RP-AUC) focuses on the relationship between recall (sensitivity) and precision (PPV). RP-AUC is particularly informative when true positives are rare46–48, such as identifying advanced fibrosis in large populations. By emphasizing the accuracy of positive predictions, RP-AUC better aligns with clinical goals, where minimizing false positives is crucial for decision-making and resource allocation.

In this work, we develop and validate a generalizable and robust model using routinely available clinical data, including patient demographics and standard laboratory results, across multiple cohorts, to support screening for liver fibrosis staging and at-risk MASH. We evaluate model performance using both ROC-AUC and RP-AUC0.5−0.7 to assess overall discriminative ability as well as the accuracy of positive predictions in clinically relevant risk ranges. We show that the model achieves strong performance in predicting liver fibrosis staging and at-risk MASH, supporting a framework for improved biomarker evaluation in screening settings.

Results

Study cohorts

This study included 715 patients with 715 liver elastography exams (including SWE or VCTE) from Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH) and Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) (Table 1). We retrospectively collected data from 521 clinically diagnosed MASLD adult patients who were scheduled for liver biopsy at MGH between 2013 and 2021 (Supplementary Fig. S1) and had SWE US examinations within one year of liver biopsy. The cohort was originally classified as NAFLD. For consistency with current adoption of the MASLD nomenclature49, we refer to the population as MASLD throughout, noting that recent data indicate over 99% overlap between NAFLD and MASLD classifications50. As shown in Fig. 1, the cohort was randomly divided into two subsets: the model development set (n = 312) and the internal test set (n = 209) with a 60:40 split. The model development subset was further randomly split into training (n = 249) and hold-out validation (n = 63) sets with 80:20 ratio. Performance comparison on the internal test set

An operating point refers to a decision threshold, typically a probability or biomarker cutoff, used to convert continuous model outputs into binary clinical outcomes, with each threshold corresponding to a specific trade-off between sensitivity, specificity, and other metrics as visualized on performance curves such as the ROC or RP curve. While both ROC-AUC and RP-AUC assess performance across all operating points, ROC-AUC is not affected by disease prevalence, whereas RP-AUC better reflects clinical relevance in low-prevalence settings (see detailed descriptions in “Methods”). We report ROC-AUC and precision-constrained RP-AUC where precision is within [0.5, 0.7] (RP-AUC0.5−0.7) to compare biomarker performance across four subgroups: significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and at-risk MASH. Precision (PPV) and recall (sensitivity) define the RP curve, which is particularly informative in low-prevalence settings. The selected precision (PPV) range 0.5–0.7 in this study was based on expert opinion, and before the computation of results. The rationale for selecting this range is straightforward: it represents a screen failure rate (1–PPV) of 30–50%, comprising a desirable and potentially achievable improvement compared to the very high screen failure rate of up to 80% commonly reported in MASH trials51. Since RP curves are driven primarily by the positive cases, small changes in predicted fibrosis stages can produce larger fluctuation in precision and recall. Figure 2a–d compare the performance of our final models, with and without LS characteristics, against traditional clinical biomarkers (APRI and FIB-4) for the fibrosis prediction (F ≥2, ≥3, =4) and at-risk MASH on the internal test set (n = 209) from the MGH cohort.

In terms of ROC-AUC, for prediction tasks in the subgroups of significant fibrosis, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis, our model with LS features had higher ROC-AUCs than FIB-4 (0.85 [95%CI: 0.79, 0.91] vs. 0.82 [95%CI: 0.74, 0.88, p = 0.17]; 0.90 [95%CI: 0.85, 0.95] vs. 0.86 [95%CI: 0.79, 0.92], p = 0.04; 0.96 [95%CI: 0.92, 0.99] vs. 0.89 [95%CI:0.82, 0.94], p = 0.03). Of note, in the study population of at-risk MASH, our model without LS features achieved the highest ROC-AUC of 0.76 (95%CI: 0.67, 0.83), compared to 0.68 (95%CI: 0.57, 0.77) for APRI (p = 0.13), 0.72 (95%CI: 0.63, 0.81) for FIB-4 (p = 0.48), and 0.73 (95%CI: 0.64, 0.81) (p = 0.41) for our model incorporating LS features.

Regarding the precision-constrained recall-precision (RP) curves, our models showed higher RP–AUCs0.5−0.7 compared to APRI and FIB-4. Specifically, our model with LS features achieved the highest RP–AUCs0.5−0.7 in the significant and advanced fibrosis subgroups (0.16 [95%CI: 0.09, 0.17] and 0.16 [95%CI: 0.09, 0.17]), respectively) while maintaining the recall (sensitivity) range from 60 to 80%. For the cirrhosis and at-risk MASH subgroups, operating points were not available for FIB-4.

Despite achieving a ROC-AUC of 0.89 for cirrhosis prediction, ROC-AUC does not account for prevalence; FIB-4 failed to achieve a precision higher than 0.5, limiting its clinical applicability in this population. For at-risk MASH prediction, our models, either with or without LS features, provided multiple operating points, where FIB-4 failed to produce threshold-achievable operating points within the precision band. Of note, if a biomarker lacks any operating point within the precision range of [0.5, 0.7], as observed for FIB-4 in both subgroups of cirrhosis and at-risk MASH (Fig. 2c, d), then the corresponding RP-AUC0.5−0.7 is set to zero by definition. Similarly, if only a single operating point falls within this range, as observed for APRI in at-risk MASH prediction (Fig. 2d), RP-AUC0.5−0.7 is also defined as zero. Additional methodological and diagnostic analysis supporting the RP-AUC framework, including model convergence, likelihood-ratio requirements, simulation analyses, and probability calibration, are provided in Supplementary Figs. S3–S6.

To evaluate whether the findings were sensitive to the pre-specified primary precision range of [0.5, 0.7], we performed sensitivity analyses across two additional clinically relevant precision bands, [0.3, 0.5] and [0.7, 0.9], corresponding to lower- and higher-precision screening settings, respectively. Performance comparison on the external test set

Figure 2e–h compare the performance of our final models, with and without liver stiffness (LS) features, against FIB-4 for predicting fibrosis (F ≥ 2, ≥ 3, = 4) and at-risk MASH on the external test set (VCU cohort, n = 194). APRI was not evaluated as its values can vary between patients due to differences in the laboratories that measured AST. The same final models (W/ and W/O LS) were used for evaluation on both internal and external test sets, without any additional fine-tuning.

As shown in Fig. 2, our proposed models, particularly the one incorporating LS, achieved the highest performance in terms of ROC-AUC. The model incorporating liver stiffness measurement (W/LS) consistently demonstrated higher ROC-AUCs compared to FIB-4 across all subgroups: significant fibrosis (0.77 vs. 0.67, p < 0.001), advanced fibrosis (0.79 vs. 0.68, p < 0.001), and cirrhosis (0.84 vs. 0.67, p < 0.001). Even without LS, our model (W/O LS) still showed higher ROC-AUCs than FIB-4, with increases of 10, 7, and 7% for F ≥ 2, ≥ 3, = 4 predictions, respectively. Notably, it also achieved a 29% higher ROC-AUC for identifying at-risk MASH patients.

Findings from the precision-constrained RP curves aligned with the ROC-AUC results in predicting significant and advanced fibrosis. As shown in Fig. 2e, within the precision range of [0.5, 0.675], both of our models achieved consistently higher recall than FIB-4. In the higher precision range of [0.675, 0.7], the performance of our model without LS becomes less stable for significant fibrosis prediction; however, our model with LS provided multiple operating points with recall of approximately 80–90%, compared to 25–60% for FIB-4.

For advanced fibrosis, as shown in Fig. 2f, our model with LS features demonstrated consistently better performance than FIB-4 across the clinically relevant precision range. In particular, our model (W/ LS) maintained a stable recall range of 50–70% within the precision range of [0.5, 0.6], whereas FIB-4’s recall declined sharply from 45 to 15%. At higher precision levels, FIB-4 offered only two operating points, both with low recall around 10%. In contrast, our model with LS provided multiple operating points within the same precision range, maintaining recall between 45 and 60%. These findings highlight the robustness and generalizability of our models in predicting both significant and advanced fibrosis.

FIB-4 exhibited limited recall of approximately 15% or less across the constrained precision range (0.5, 0.7) for detecting cirrhosis (Fig. 2g). Our model without liver stiffness showed a comparable trend, with very few operating points within this range, achieving recall between 10 and 21%. In contrast, incorporating LS features substantially improved our model’s performance: recall exceeded 50% at a precision of 0.5 and remained above 40% at a precision of 0.57. While some fluctuations and a general decline in recall were observed at higher precision levels, our model (W/ LS) remained a higher recall range than FIB-4 and the model without LS. Notably, the number of cirrhosis cases in this cohort set was limited, which contributed to the sparse distribution of operating points in Fig. 2g.

For at-risk MASH, our model with LS features demonstrated a higher ROC-AUC of 0.69 (95%CI:0.56, 0.8) compared to both FIB-4 and the model without LS features. The number of operating points in this population was limited, with neither FIB-4 nor the model without LS yielding any valid operating points.

Biomarker screening pathways on the external test set

We evaluated three screening pathways for enrollment of non-cirrhotic patients with at-risk MASH: (1) FIB-4 alone, (2) our model without LS features, and (3) our model incorporating LS features. These screening pathways were then compared against the current American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD) guideline-recommended pathway44.

Two screening performance metrics were evaluated: liver biopsy failure rate (LBFR) and number needed to screen (NNS). As defined in a prior study13, LBFR is calculated as the proportion of negative liver biopsies among all biopsies performed. It reflects the rate of liver biopsy failure (i.e., patients without at-risk MASH) and is particularly relevant for clinical trials, where reducing unnecessary biopsies improves screening efficiency and minimizes procedural burden. NNS is defined as the number of patients who must be screened to successfully enroll 1000 participants under a given screening pathway. This metric directly reflects screening efficiency and has important cost implications, as higher NNS values correspond to greater recruitment efforts and increased trial expenses.

As shown in Fig. 3, the LBFR fluctuates within the FIB-4 cut-off range of 0.75 to 1.5. It reaches the lowest LBFR of 84.7% at a cut-off of 0.97, corresponding to a NNS of 8818 to achieve 1000 inclusions. Regardless of the selected cutoff threshold or the number of patients recruited, FIB-4 consistently fails to reduce the LBFR below 84.7%. Notably, as the NNS increases, starting from 8818, LBFR exhibits a worsening trend, indicating the limitation of FIB-4 as a predictive screening tool for at-risk MASH in high-throughput clinical trial settings. Discussion

Accessibility, affordability, scalability, and excellent diagnostic performance are crucial elements of a suitable screening test for liver fibrosis in large populations. Such a test will substantially benefit MASLD or MASH drug trials by reducing the liver biopsy failure rate (LBFR). Widely available low-cost synthetic biomarkers derived from common blood tests, such as APRI and FIB-4, may not be cost-effective due to their high LBFRs53. We developed a generalized, clinical record-based synthetic biomarker for the detection of patients with significant, advanced liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and at-risk MASH using pathology as the reference standard. Based on the evaluation metrics of receiver operating curve (ROC) and recall-precision (RP) curves, our model, which incorporates widely adopted blood and imaging data, achieved excellent results in internal test and external test datasets. These results were superior to two widely used non-invasive screening tests (APRI and FIB-4 scores). Notably, we employed the RP curve, a performance evaluation tool for testing chain optimization, and we introduced precision-constrained RP-AUC to comparatively evaluate the real-world performance of biomarkers across a clinically acceptable range of LBFRs. For model comparisons, we use the AUC within the precision range of [0.5, 0.7] (RP-AUC0.5−0.7). This desired precision range, considered reasonable on clinical grounds for MASLD risk stratification, can be modified for screening biomarker evaluation across many disease processes and therefore provides a useful tool for the comparative evaluation of screening biomarkers.

Additionally, our study found that poor SWE measurement quality (IQR/Mdn > 0.3) had a considerable impact on the performance of our models, especially when liver stiffness measurement was used. Finally, our model demonstrates clear advantages as a screening enrichment tool for patients with liver fibrosis in drug trials, where it can optimize screening costs within a predefined desired LBFR of 30–50%. This advantage persists in the key at-risk MASH patient subgroup, where our model’s LBFR was below or equal to 50% in both the internal and external data sets.

Liver biopsy referrals are typically made when non-invasive diagnostic tests, such as liver function tests, indicate the need for further evaluation of liver injury. However, the low prevalence54 of liver fibrosis in the general population and the varied prevalence of liver fibrosis across different settings54 lead to a high rate of unnecessary hepatology referrals and discordant liver biopsies. In our model, we selected widely available clinical and imaging features known to be associated with significant fibrosis, enhancing the generalizability of our machine learning models to primary care settings, where specialized liver fibrosis testing is typically unavailable. The improved performance, accomplished at minimal incremental testing cost, has the potential to accelerate recruitment for MASLD/MASH drug trials and improve the utilization of limited sub-specialized hepatology care.

The performance of our clinical records-based biomarker, particularly with US-based liver stiffness, is comparable to other studies18,55 in predicting pathologically significant, advanced fibrosis, or cirrhosis, with all ROC-AUCs exceeding 0.8. However, as discussed earlier, the use of the ROC-AUC to summarize biomarker performance is a simplification that can lead to incorrect conclusions. To address this, we computed the area under the precision-constrained recall-precision curve (RP-AUC0.5−0.7) to assess model performance across a clinically important precision range based on the rationale described above. This method resembles the consideration of tradeoffs that form the basis of conventional pragmatic clinical practice. The results, which demonstrate the superior performance of our model across the desired precision range, reflect a substantial improvement in biomarker performance when it matters most, underscoring the value of the metric.

In the setting of subject screening for MASLD/MASH trials, ensuring that patients predicted as positive by a diagnostic test are truly positive is of great importance. In imbalanced datasets, such as those with a low prevalence of liver fibrosis, RP-AUC better reflects model performance for the key task of detecting the positive class, and is less affected by the high prevalence of negative cases. Although the use of precision-recall curves and area under the precision recall curve is established in the statistical literature56 and commercial sector57, it has only recently gained recognition within the medical machine learning community58, including MASLD studies59,60. In this work, we extend these concepts and introduce an alternative approach: targeting a precision (equivalent to PPV) range of interest a priori. Clinically-informed selection of a desired precision range permits model design to maximize recall across this range. For example, if a clinical trialist decides that the current LBFR rate of 50% 61 is unacceptable, the trialist could then decide, on clinical or ethical grounds, that a LBFR of 30–50% is acceptable, and comparative assessments of biomarker performance can be made within clinically-expected prevalence ranges. In this manner, biomarker cutoffs can be optimized for the clinical construct regarded as most important by the hepatologist and clinical trialist and not for the artificial construct of the ROC-AUC, which while prevalence-independent, has limited real-world utility. Moreover, model design can be targeted at maximizing the area under the R–P Curve across the clinically relevant range. This concept is particularly important in the coming era of machine-learning-derived synthetic biomarkers, which are presently almost invariably targeted at maximization of the typically clinically irrelevant ROC-AUC.

It is important to note that the AASLD guideline employs a sequential screening strategy44: FIB-4 first, followed by VCTE for patients at intermediate risk. In comparison, our model integrates patient history information, laboratory tests, either with or without liver stiffness, simultaneously. By modeling these interactions directly, our approach may capture patterns not accessible through sequential rules, potentially improving risk stratification. This distinction should be considered when interpreting comparative performance results.

Previous studies have explored machine learning or deep learning-based approaches for liver fibrosis prediction using a variety of data sources, including circulating biomarkers18,36, routinely collected laboratory, clinical, and demographic data35–37, US images33,55 as well as prescription and medical claims57. Across these studies, reported ROC-AUCs for fibrosis prediction typically range from 0.70 to 0.94. Although several models were developed and validated in large cohorts35–37,62, liver biopsy was not consistently used as the reference standard. For example, biopsy data were available only for a subset of the overall cohort36. In addition, the study populations may include other etiologies such as viral hepatitis62, rather than MASLD alone. Our model demonstrated a high ROC-AUC of 0.90 (95% CI: 0.85, 0.95) and 0.96 (95%CI: 0.92, 0.99) for predicting advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis, and an intermediate ROC-AUC of 0.76 (95%CI: 0.67, 0.83]) for predicting at-risk MASH, using the internal test protocol. In the external test set, our model remains advantageous for predicting liver fibrosis stagings, with ROC-AUCs of 0.72–0.84, compared to those of FIB-4 and APRI models. In general, our proposed model with liver stiffness achieved a considerably better performance than the one without it, indicating the additional value of SWE. While ROC-AUC provides a conventional measure of discriminative performance, it does not capture the ability to identify patients within clinically meaningful precision ranges. In comparison, our metric of RP-AUC0.5−0.7 explicitly evaluates model performance in the precision range most relevant for clinical trial enrollment and screening, representing a conceptual advance over prior machine learning studies that reported only ROC-AUCs.

Our proposed neural network-based screening tool has the potential to enhance clinical decision-making and improve the efficiency of patient selection for MASH drug trials. By facilitating cost-effective MASLD risk stratification, the model may help optimize clinical care. When integrated into routine clinical workflows as a diagnostic enrichment screening tool, it could enable clinicians to identify liver fibrosis with optimal sensitivity in a desired PPV range. This approach has the potential to support early intervention strategies and contribute to improved patient outcomes.

In the context of drug trials, our proposed model could serve as a screening tool to optimize patient recruitment by increasing the likelihood of selecting subjects with advanced disease and further reducing the screen failure rate.

Although our model demonstrated promising performance in predicting significant and advanced liver fibrosis as well as cirrhosis, several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, our cohort was drawn from patients referred for liver biopsy or hepatology clinics, resulting in relatively higher disease prevalence than would be expected in primary care populations. This may influence the absolute values of performance metrics in low-prevalence screening settings. Second, our clinical and imaging database was primarily from a single academic institution, which may introduce subject selection bias. Third, the liver stiffness measurement method differed between the internal and external cohorts (2-D SWE vs. VCTE), which may affect the absolute values. Despite this, the model performed well in the external cohort, and future work incorporating additional data and harmonized or simplified stiffness features will further assess generalizability across elastography techniques. Fourth, the sample size of key subgroups, such as cirrhosis and at-risk MASH, is limited. This may constrain the robustness of subgroup-level analyses. As with other predictive models, some degree of optimism in estimated performance may exist, and this should be considered when interpreting the results. In future work, we plan to conduct prospective evaluations in real-world clinical workflows to further confirm its generalizability and clinical impact. Finally, a small proportion of participants had self-reported alcohol intake at the visit closest to the liver biopsy that exceeded current MASLD thresholds44. These individuals were clinically diagnosed with NAFLD at the time of evaluation, before the introduction of MASLD, and were retained to reflect real-world clinical practice63,64. Retrospective alcohol consumption data are inherently limited, and future studies will adhere to contemporary MASLD definitions to better capture disease etiology.

In conclusion, our redefined framework for biomarker evaluation offers an alternative approach to optimizing the trade-off between screen failure rates and screening efficiency in clinical care and drug trials. This approach is illustrated in a unique clinical records-based biomarker that integrates patient history information, routine laboratory tests, and imaging features, showing robust performance in fibrosis (F ≥ 2, ≥ 3, = 4) prediction using the clinically-relevant evaluation metric of RP-AUC0.5−0.7. Ongoing work will focus on expanding the model with additional datasets and evaluating its performance within clinical workflows to establish its practical applicability and robustness.

Methods

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. This study protocol was approved by the Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (IRB) (protocol no. 2021P003339) for model development and internal testing, and by VCU (protocol no. HM20001118) for the external test set. An additional IRB approval by the Mass General Brigham IRB (protocol no. 2024P001544) covers the external test set analysis. Informed consent was waived for the internal cohort by the Mass General Brigham IRB. Written informed consent was obtained from participants in the external cohort. No participant compensation was provided in either cohort.

Study population

We enrolled adult participants who were scheduled for liver biopsy at our institution from November 2013 to September 2021 and had SWE US examinations within one year of liver biopsy.

Internal cohort cases were identified through institutional electronic medical record and radiology searches, an internal laboratory imaging archive, and manual pathologist review. We excluded patients based on the following criteria: (a) biopsy-SWE pairs not obtained within a one-year time frame; (b) absence of pathology reports and/or pathology reports with insufficient information to derive the fibrosis stage; (c) liver disease other than clinically diagnosed MASLD/MASH; (d) SWE exams yielding liver stiffness > 50 kPa, as these very high values are likely to be artifactual based on our prior internal analyses.

Following exclusions, the dataset comprised 543 examinations from 521 subjects, with 20 subjects undergoing two examinations and 1 subject with three examinations. Of these, 472 examinations were performed using SuperSonic Imagine devices (SSI Aixplorer), 70 with General Electric devices (GE Logiq E9 and E10), and one with a Siemens device (Siemens ACUSON S2000).

Before finalizing the subsets, we tackled cases where patients underwent multiple examinations, especially when those exams reflecting different stages of fibrosis. To maintain the assumption of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) samples and to avoid introducing conflicting data points from the same individual, only the most recent US exam was kept for each patient.

For external validation, we used an independent VCU retrospective cohort of adult MASLD/MASH patients enrolled between 2015 and 2025 with available age at diagnosis, fibrosis staging, and paired liver biopsy-VCTE within one year, retaining the VCTE examination closest to biopsy when multiple examinations were available. After excluding patients with missing paired biopsy/VCTE data, missing age or fibrosis stage, biopsy-VCTE interval > 1 year, or liver diseases other than MASLD/MASH, the final external cohort included 194 patients with 194 examinations; the curation flowchart is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Among the included subjects, 24 participants (3%, 24/715) across the internal and external cohorts had documented alcohol abuse based on clinical records (> 7 drinks/week for women or > 14 drinks/week for men). All were clinically diagnosed with NAFLD by the treating physician at the time of evaluation. Of these, eight participants (33%, 8/24) were prior alcohol users who had discontinued alcohol consumption more than one year before liver biopsy, leaving 7 (3%, 7/249) current users in the training set, 1 (2%, 1/63) current user in the hold-out validation set, 8 (4%, 8/209) current users in the internal set. These cases were retained in the analysis to preserve the real-world composition of the cohort63,64.

Sex was recorded for all participants in both cohorts based on information documented in the electronic medical record.

Additional details regarding database development, case identification, data curation, imaging repository curation, and data quality assurance procedures are provided in Supplementary Section S1.

US procedure

SWE was performed with one of the three different US scanners (GE Logiq E9 and E10, GE Healthcare; Siemens ACUSON S2000, Siemens Healthineers; Aixplorer, SuperSonic Imagine) equipped with convex probes (frequency range: 1–6 MHz). Multiple sonographers with varying US experience and trained in SWE image acquisition performed clinical US SWE. Participants fasted for 4 h and were positioned supine or in the left decubitus semi-oblique position with the right arm elevated. SWE images were acquired via an intercostal space approach during a neutral breath hold. A region of interest box was placed in the liver parenchyma, avoiding major vessels, rib shadows, and lung shadows. Liver tissue Young’s modulus was expressed in kilopascal (kPa). All biopsies were performed under US guidance as per the standard protocol at our institution65.

Pathology reference standard

We histopathologically re-evaluated clinically indicated non-focal liver biopsy tissue samples to ensure completeness of histopathologic interpretation, reduce interobserver variability, and maintain a centralized reference standard method. We retrieved approximately 2605 histopathology slides from 521 subjects, which comprised a variety of stains, including trichrome, hematoxylin & eosin (H&E). The trichrome stain was used to evaluate established fibrosis. The H&E stain was used to evaluate a variety of morphologic features, including steatosis, steatohepatitis, and inflammation. 19 different data points were reviewed by pathologists and manually recorded for each subject. In total, approximately 10,000 data points were collected. Three subspecialist board-certified pathologists with liver pathology experience reviewed the biopsy samples while blinded to clinical history and imaging results. Liver fibrosis was scored using the NASH CRN staging system8. NASH CRN staging was performed for fibrosis staging (F Stage, F0 − 4) by the same pathologist using the following criteria: (0): No fibrosis; (1): 1a, mild, zone 3 perisinusoidal fibrosis, (1b), moderate, zone 3 perisinusoidal fibrosis, (1c), portal/periportal fibrosis only; (2): Zone 3 perisinusoidal and portal/periportal fibrosis; (3): Bridging fibrosis; (4): Cirrhosis. MASLD Activity Score (NAS) was evaluated using sum of steatosis, lobular inflammation, and hepatocellular ballooning (NAS Score 0–8). Steatosis was scored using the following criteria: (0), < 5%; (1): 5–33%, (2):33–66%, (3): > 66% amount of surface area. Lobular inflammation was scored using the following criteria: (0) No foci; (1) < 2 foci; (2) 2–4 foci; (3) > 4 foci. Hepatocellular ballooning was scored using the following criteria: (0) None; (1) Few balloon cells, (2) Many balloon cells8.

Clinical records description

The clinical records used in this study include five major categories of patient characteristics: patient history, laboratory tests, imaging variables, histology, and fibrosis scores. As detailed in Table 1, patient history information includes demographic information (e.g., age, sex, BMI), and relevant medical history (e.g., DM). Laboratory tests include liver function tests (e.g., ALT, AST, ALB), renal function tests (e.g., Cr, ALB), diabetes related tests (e.g., Glu, HbA1C), lipid profiles (e.g., Chol., LDL, TGs), hematology tests (e.g., WBC, PLT, HGB), coagulation profile (e.g., INR, APTT), and iron studies (e.g., Ferr., Fe, TIBC).

Table 1 summarizes the imaging variables used in this study. 2D-SWE was used as the imaging modality for the MGH cohort, which includes both the model development dataset and the internal test set. Among the imaging features, SCD represents the thickness of the tissue layer between the skin surface and the liver capsule, manually measured by the operator on B-mode US images. LS was obtained using SWE, with ten measurements acquired per examination; the mean, median, maximum, and standard deviation were subsequently calculated. In contrast, the VCU cohort (external test set) employed FibroScan, a VCTE-based technique, which provides a single liver stiffness value per examination along with the IQR/Mdn to report data variability.

To ensure appropriate statistical analysis, we conducted a normality check on all continuous variables to assess the distribution of continuous variables in both MGH and VCU cohorts. Data were considered normally distributed if the skewness values ranged between −3 and 3, and the kurtosis was below 4. Following this assessment, we used specific reporting conventions: continuous variables with a normal distribution are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas those with a non-normal distribution are presented as the median and interquartile range (IQR). For the consistency in data presentation, values less than 10 are reported to two decimal places, while values equal to or greater than 10 are reported without decimal places. An exception is made for multi-level categorical variables, which are reported to one decimal place. Additional clinical variables were collected but only included in the analysis if fewer than 50% of values were missing.

To address missing data, we applied multivariate imputation by chained equations implemented using the IterativeImputer algorithm69. Imputation was performed separately within each of the three primary feature groups, patient history, laboratory tests, and imaging, to leverage within-group feature relationships. For categorical variables, missing values were initialized using the most frequent category, whereas continuous variables were initialized using the median before iterative imputation. The iterative imputation procedure was run for up to 100 iterations. For continuous features, negative imputed values were rare; however, as a safeguard, any such values were replaced with the minimum observed value of the corresponding feature to ensure physiologically plausible ranges. We did not use K-nearest neighbors imputation because its performance is sensitive to hyperparameter choices (e.g., number of neighbors) and typically requires extensive tuning.

Training details

We used a compact shallow feed-forward neural network architecture suited to the size and feature dimensionality of the dataset to reduce the risk of overfitting. The network comprised a single hidden layer with 32 units and ReLU activation, followed by a two-dimensional linear output layer producing class logits for binary classification (Fig. 1c). Categorical variables (e.g., race, five categories) were one-hot encoded to avoid implicit ordinal assumptions. Continuous variables were standardized using z-score normalization (parameters estimated from the training set). The final feature set is summarized in Table 4. To address class imbalance and varying data completeness, we used a weighted cross-entropy loss incorporating both class-level inverse-frequency weights and sample-level weights proportional to the fraction of non-imputed features.

The clinical records-based model was trained for 10 epochs using a batch size of 8. Class weights were computed from the training labels as the inverse of class frequencies. In addition, the loss contribution of each sample was scaled by the proportion of non-imputed features, thereby down-weighting samples with higher missingness to mitigate potential bias introduced by imputation. The model was optimized using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and default momentum parameters. No dropout or weight decay was applied. Model performance was evaluated using the model weights corresponding to the minimum validation loss within the 10 training epochs. Training was performed with a fixed random seed. No fine-tuning was conducted. Training and validation loss curves demonstrated stable convergence without evidence of divergence. Representative convergence curves are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. Convergence behavior was consistent across all the binary prediction tasks (F ≥ 2, F ≥ 3, F = 4, and at-risk MASH).

All experiments were implemented in PyTorch (version: 2.1.0 + cu121) and executed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPU.

Evaluation metric

Liver biopsy screen failure rates in MASLD clinical trials have been reported to be as high as 80% 70,71. The high biopsy screen failure rate increases drug development trial cost and complexity, creating an impediment to the development of effective MASLD therapeutics. The high biopsy screen failure rate reflects a conundrum familiar to MASLD drug trialists. In MASLD clinical trials, subjects typically undergo a non-invasive diagnostic enrichment screen followed by a biopsy screen before randomization. The more stringent the diagnostic enrichment screen, the more subjects with advanced fibrosis will be removed from the study population. A reduced biopsy screen fail rate counterbalances this loss of desirable subjects. Optimizing the trade-off between false negative diagnosis at the diagnostic enrichment stage and PPV at the biopsy stage is a central goal of clinical trial optimization in MASLD. Unfortunately, the commonly used ROC-AUC metric of test performance provides little insight into this trade-off. The ROC-AUC metric provides a helpful prevalence-independent metric of test performance that can be used to compare the performance of different biomarkers in a prevalence-independent manner. However, biomarkers are used in clinical trials and therapeutic settings where prevalence estimates are typically available, and accurate estimation of testing chain diagnostic performance is essential for effective clinical trial design. Estimating pre-randomization testing objectives is particularly important when developing machine learning-based biomarkers, as model training is optimally performed relative to a specific goal. The widely used approach of using ROC-AUC maximization as a default goal is illogical and unlikely to produce optimal results.

To address this challenge in the setting of MASLD clinical trials, we propose focusing on the trade-off between enrichment screen false negative rate (FNR = FN/(FN + TP)) and biopsy positive predictive value (PPV = TP/(TP + FP)) at a given prevalence. Optimization of this trade-off will improve the screen failure rate while minimizing subject loss due to false negative diagnoses at the diagnostic enrichment stage. Algebraically, the false negative rate is equivalent to 1–sensitivity, and the screen failure rate is equivalent to 1–PPV. Therefore, this metric of test performance can be depicted as a recall-precision curve. The area under the recall-precision curve summarizes testing chain performance across recall (sensitivity)—precision (PPV) pairs. A range of pairs can be selected for focused testing chain optimization. For example, a drug trial designer could pre-specify a desired screen failure rate of 0.5 or less, which would correspond to a PPV of 0.5. In such cases, it is desirable to maximize the area under the recall-precision curve within that precision range. This idea is analogous to the concept of partial ROC-AUC, which summarizes model performance over a pre-specified range of false negative or false positive rates72. Both approaches support focused optimization and model comparison in clinically meaningful operating regions, beyond what global ROC-AUC or RP-AUC alone can provide. However, unlike partial ROC-AUC, the recall-precision curve directly emphasizes sensitivity and PPV, making it more suitable for evaluating diagnostic performance in imbalanced settings like MASH drug trials, where actionable trade-offs such as controlling screen failure rate are critical. The mathematical formulation and computation of precision-constrained RP-AUC are defined below.

To support interpretation when threshold-attainable operating points within the prespecified precision band are sparse or absent, we additionally report two complementary operating-point summaries: the probability that at least one operating point lies within the target precision range, Pr(I0.5−0.7), and the maximum recall achievable while maintaining precision ≥0.5, Recp ≥ 0.5. Because the proposed models output predicted probabilities, we additionally assessed probability calibration in the internal and external cohorts to support interpretation of the absolute probability scale under cross-site validation. Reliability diagrams, the Brier score, and calibration intercept and slope are provided for the proposed models in the Supplementary Materials (Section S3. All analyses were performed using Python version 3.10.12. ROC curves were generated by plotting the true positive rate against the false positive rate, and ROC-AUCs were calculated using scikit-learn (version 1.7.2). RP curves were generated by plotting recall against precision, with the area under the precision-constrained RP curve was calculated using the trapezoidal rule (NumPy 1.26.4). Simulation study: separability and prevalence

To clarify the distinct roles of class separability and prevalence in recall-precision behavior, we conducted a controlled simulation study under a parametric score-generating model.

Scores were sampled from equal-variance Gaussian class-conditional distributions: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S| Y=1 \sim {{\mathcal{N}}}({\mu }_{1},{\sigma }^{2}),\,S| Y=0 \sim {{\mathcal{N}}}({\mu }_{0},{\sigma }^{2}),$$\end{document}S∣Y=1~N(μ1,σ2),S∣Y=0~N(μ0,σ2),with σ = 1. Prevalence settings

Evaluation cohorts were simulated with prevalence π ∈ {5, 10, 30, 50%}. For each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(\uppi,{d}^{{\prime} })$$\end{document}(π,d′) configuration, n = 1000 samples were independently generated.

Evaluation protocol

For each simulated dataset, we computed: (i) ROC curve and ROC-AUC, (ii) the upper boundary of threshold-achievable recall-precision operating points, represented by R⋆(p) in Eq. (4) and evaluated using strict thresholding, and (iii) the distribution of operating points within three precision bands: [0.3, 0.5], [0.5, 0.7], and [0.7, 0.9].

Simulation findings

Consistent with theory, ROC curves were governed primarily by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}d′ and were prevalence-independent (with minor finite-sample variability at n = 1000). In contrast, recall-precision behavior exhibited strong and systematic prevalence dependence, as implied by Eq. (1). In particular, Eq. (3) shows that achieving high precision at low prevalence requires extremely large LR+ and therefore extremely low false-positive rates, which typically occurs only at thresholds that yield low recall. Consequently, at low prevalence (e.g., π = 5%), the attainable high-precision region may contain few or no operating points for moderate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${d}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}d′, whereas at higher prevalence (e.g., π = 30% or 50%), a larger portion of the precision axis lies above the baseline prevalence even for moderately discriminative models. These observations motivate precision-banded evaluation and the use of RP − AUCp∈[α, β] to assess robustness of method comparisons across clinically relevant precision intervals.

Sensitivity and prevalence scenario analysis

To assess whether biomarker comparisons were robust to the prespecified primary precision band [0.5, 0.7], we additionally computed precision-constrained RP-AUC across alternative precision ranges [0.3, 0.5] and [0.7, 0.9]. To assess the transportability of screening pathways across populations with different disease burdens, we conducted prevalence scenario analyses assuming at-risk MASH prevalence of 5, 10, and 20%. Sensitivity and specificity estimated from the external test cohort were used to analytically recalculate expected true-positive and false-positive counts under each prevalence assumption while preserving threshold-specific operating characteristics. LBFR and NNS were then computed accordingly. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Sections S4 and S5 and Supplementary Figs. S7–S9. Additional screening pathway analyses for fibrosis staging endpoints and at-risk MASH in the internal and external cohorts are provided in Supplementary Figs. S10 and S11.

Subgroup analysis

To assess the effect of obesity, we stratified clinical records into two subgroups: BMI ≤ 35 and BMI > 35, with missing BMI values imputed. For liver stiffness measurement quality, we followed established reliability criteria. As recommended by ref. 52, liver stiffness measurements (kPa) were considered reliable when at least 10 measurements were obtained with interquartile range to median (IQR/Mdn) ratio lower than 30%.

In our study, imputing missing values for each LS measurement was challenging due to the variations in acquisition conditions. Therefore, we calculated the IQR/Mdn ratio using only the available measurements, excluding missing data. The clinical records were then stratified into two subgroups based on LS measurement quality: IQR/Mdn ≤ 0.3 and IQR/Mdn > 0.3, regardless of the total number of valid measurements.
