Complete thoracoscopic retrieval of the left internal mammary artery using ultrasonic scalpel in High-Risk surgical patients: a case series
Authors: Xiuxiu Zhang, Yan Zhang, Peisong Wang, Zhenqi Fu, Tiantong Zhao, Yichang Song, Dapeng Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the feasibility and safety of performing a complete thoracoscopic ultrasonic scalpel procedure to harvest the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) during minimally invasive coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) in high-risk patients.MethodsThis retrospective study compiled data from patients who underwent minimally invasive CABG with LIMA harvesting via thoracoscopic ultrasonic scalpel at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery, D’E Hospital, from January 2022 to July 2023.ResultsThe study comprised eight patients with STS scores ≥ 7, among whom three were over 80 years old and five had low left ventricular ejection fractions (LVEF). The median operation duration was 220 min (range: 175–270), and all patients underwent single-vessel grafting. Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a common disease, with a global incidence of 898 per 100,000 person-years and a mortality of 264.3 per 100,000 person-years in 2017 [1]. Individuals over the age of 65 are vulnerable to CVD, accounting for 81% of all cardiovascular-related deaths [2]. Patients afflicted with CVD often exhibit a notably reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), which serves as a crucial guideline for treatment decisions [3].

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is the procedure of choice for revascularizing infarcted areas, improving LVEF, and enhancing patient prognoses [4]. However, CABG is an invasive surgical intervention that resulting in considerable trauma, extended recovery periods, and an increased risk of postoperative complications [5–7]. Older adults face heightened risks of complications and adverse outcomes following CABG surgery [5–7].

In response to these challenges, efforts have been made to mitigate the toll of the procedure through the development of minimally invasive CABG approaches [8–10]. Minimally invasive cardiac surgery, including thoracoscopic techniques, has gained prominence in the field of cardiac surgery due to its ability to minimize trauma and expedite postoperative recovery [8–10]. The ultrasonic scalpel stands out for its precision, complete hemostasis, and minimal vascular injury [11]. It is well-suited for delicate vascular procedures, including the harvesting of the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) [12]. Its application in minimally invasive CABG surgery is expected to improve feasibility and safety [12]. Utilizing the ultrasonic scalpel in minimally invasive CABG surgery has the potential to improve surgical outcomes and reduce postoperative complications.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore the feasibility and safety of performing a complete thoracoscopic ultrasonic scalpel procedure to obtain the LIMA during minimally invasive CABG in high-risk patients, especially older adults and those with low LVEF.

Methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective study collected data from patients who underwent minimally invasive CABG between January 2022 and July 2023 at the Department of Cardiothoracic Surgery of D’E Hospital. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of D’E Hospital. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

The inclusion criteria were (1) patients who underwent minimally invasive single-vessel CABG, (2) STS>5 points [13], and (3) older patients (age ≥ 80 years) or those with low LVEF (≤ 30%). The exclusion criteria were (1) previous chest surgery of any form or severe pleural adhesions, (2) planned concurrent cardiac surgeries, (3) emergent surgery, (4) severe pulmonary diseases leading to intolerance of single-lung ventilation, or (5) incomplete clinical data.

Surgical procedure

The patients were placed in a 30° right lateral decubitus position on the operating table and underwent double-lumen endotracheal intubation with right single-lung ventilation. A 10-mm trocar was inserted in the third intercostal space along the left midaxillary line for camera access. A 10-mm trocar was inserted in the fourth intercostal space along the left midaxillary line for the main operating port. A 5-mm trocar was inserted in the fifth intercostal space along the left anterior axillary line for the auxiliary operating port (Fig. 1A). The camera port facilitated thoracoscopic visualization, the main port accommodated the ultrasonic scalpel, and the auxiliary port housed vascular forceps. Generally, the thoracoscope is placed in the third intercostal space. If the visual field is unclear or the operation of the ultrasonic scalpel is inconvenient during the procedure, the thoracoscope should be moved to the fourth intercostal space, while the ultrasonic scalpel should be placed in the third intercostal space. Carbon dioxide was used for during LIMA harvesting. Generally, the pressure is approximately 3 mmHg and the flow rate is 5 L per minute. The course of the left internal mammary artery (LIMA) was initially identified. Any obstructing excessive fatty tissue on the pleural surface was excised if it hindered visibility (Fig. 1B). The pleural and fascial tissues were incised approximately 0.5 cm to the left side of the LIMA, revealing the gap between the LIMA and the chest wall. The LIMA was cautiously separated both cranially and caudally, keeping the ultrasonic scalpel head away from the LIMA to prevent thermal injury (Fig. 1C). In cases where the vessel adhered tightly to the rib, the vessel was first detached from the upper and lower intercostal spaces and then gently separated using the ultrasonic scalpel or scissors, if needed. Branch vessels were cut with the ultrasonic scalpel approximately 1–2 mm from the main trunk. If the ultrasonic scalpel came close to the LIMA, the scalpel head was gently rotated to create distance. The internal tissue layers around the LIMA were incised to join it with the accompanying vein, creating a vascular graft extending from the first intercostal space to the fifth or sixth intercostal space on the cranial and caudal sides (Fig. 1D). The distal end was ligated after administering heparin at 200 mg/kg (Fig. 1E). Venous bleeding could be managed by light compression or the ultrasonic scalpel, while arterial bleeding was initially addressed through compression, and if ineffective, heparin was administered, followed by suturing the upper and lower ends of the vessel with a 7 − 0 vascular anastomosis suture line to close the vessel outer membrane. Because the ultrasonic scalpel has high-frequency vibration cutting and low-temperature coagulation hemostasis effects, it can achieve thorough hemostasis, making the use of ligaclips basically unnecessary when harvesting the LIMA.

Surgical procedure. (A) Position of the three ports. (B) The course of the left internal mammary artery (LIMA). (C) Exposure of the gap between the LIMA and the chest wall. (D) Complete acquisition of the LIMA. (E) Clipping the distal end of the LIMA. (F) Blood flow of LIMA-LAD

A small incision, 3–5 cm in length, was made below the left nipple in the fourth or fifth intercostal space to access the pleural cavity. The pericardium was opened, approximately 10 cm in length, and secured for stabilization. During grafting, a cardiac stabilizer device was employed to secure the target vessel on both sides. The trimmed LIMA was anastomosed with the target vessel, and the LIMA stump was secured to the epicardium. Blood flow in the graft vessel was assessed (Fig. 1F). All surgeries were conducted by the same surgeon. And all the surgeries were completed while the heart was beating. (Please refer to the supplementary materials for the surgical video.)

Data collection

Patient baseline characteristics, surgical duration, blood loss, LIMA flow, postoperative complications, and length of hospital stay were extracted from the patients’ medical records. Descriptive statistics were employed for analysis.

Results

The study included eight patients with STS scores >5 (median: 11.3; range: 7.9–15). The group comprised four (50%) men and four (50%) women, with a median age of 72 years (range: 65–87), including three patients aged over 85 years. The median BMI was 25.0 kg/m² (range: 19.7–28.3). The median LVEF was 28% (range: 23%−64%), with five patients presenting low LVEF. Angiography results indicated that three patients had two lesions, four patients had three lesions, and one patient had lesions in the left main artery and three other sites. The LIMA was grafted to the LAD in all patients, and 5 patients underwent hybrid procedure (Table 1).

Discussion

The results from the eight included patients suggest that using a complete thoracoscopic ultrasonic scalpel procedure to obtain the LIMA during minimally invasive single-vessel CABG surgery in high-risk patients may be feasible and safe. By demonstrating the feasibility and safety of this technique, it offers a potential avenue to reduce surgical trauma and expedite postoperative recovery in a specific subset of patients, ultimately enhancing their overall quality of life.

Minimally invasive CABG is being developed to improve postoperative patient outcomes (smaller trauma and shortest recovery, which can improve survival). CABG involves the harvesting of an autologous vessel graft that contributes to the morbidity of CABG and the risks of complications. Using the LIMA is routine in CABG [14, 15], but it also has to be harvested in a minimally invasive way to achieve the goal of minimally invasive CABG. Besides the classical sternotomy LIMA harvesting methods [16], the LIMA can be harvested using a mini-thoracotomy under direct vision [17], thoracoscopy [18], and robotic-assisted methods [19]. Mini thoracotomy remains an open surgery technique, and there are challenges to harvesting the distal part of the LIMA. Robotic-assisted surgery is expensive, not widely available, and has its own challenges [20]. Hence, thoracoscopy is an appropriate method to achieve minimal invasiveness for CABG.

Irrespective of the method used for harvesting, the LIMA graft must be of high quality to achieve the best patient outcomes. The LIMA quality has been shown to be comparable between minimally invasive and conventional CABG [21, 22]. A study suggested that the learning curve has a minimal impact on the quality of LIMA harvested using minimally invasive methods [20]. The ultrasonic scalpel can be used to harvest the LIMA [12] due to its precision, hemostatic effect, and minimal injury to the blood vessels [11]. A meta-analysis of eight studies showed that compared with electrocautery, using the harmonic scalpel to harvest the LIMA led to smaller thermal injury, better preservation of the endothelial cells, satisfactory blood flow, promising patency, and decreased morbidity and mortality [12]. On the other hand, using the harmonic scalpel led to longer harvest times and operations and higher expenses [12]. Similar results were reported by another meta-analysis of 12 papers [23]. Such favorable outcomes were also observed when harvesting other thoracic arteries [24].

In the present study, the LIMA graft was patent in all patients. Ruel et al. [25] reported a 6-month 100% patency rate after minimally invasive LIMA harvesting. Other studies reported patency rates of 90.7%−100% before discharge [16, 26–30]. These results support the feasible of minimally invasive LIMA harvesting.

In the present study, the median operation duration was 220 (range: 175–270) min, the median blood loss was 26 (range: 7–300) ml, the median endotracheal intubation duration was 69 (range: 12–144) h, the chest drainage duration was 25 (range: 16–36) h, the median ICU stay was 132.5 (range: 71–212) h, the median hospitalization was 7 (range: 6–16) days. This is likely related to the patient’s advanced age, low left ventricular ejection fraction, multiple comorbidities, and high surgical risk. Stanilawski et al. [31] reported a median drainage of 440 ± 189 ml and a ventilation time of 8.75 ± 5.23 h after minimally invasive CABG; reoperation for bleeding was performed in 5.8%. Raja et al. [32] reported an operation time of 177 ± 32 min after minimally invasive CABG; conversion was 0%, re-exploration for bleeding was 3.1%, and 30-day mortality was 2.0%. Zhang et al. [33] observed a total surgery time of 152 ± 43.5 min, ventilation of 9.27 ± 5.14 h, and ICU stay of 24.27 ± 17.25 h; the 1-month mortality was 0.33%. In the present study, two patients had complications: atrial fibrillation in one and pneumonia and new-onset heart failure in another. No patient died. Of note, the patients included in the present study were either old (≥ 80 years old) or had an LVEF ≤ 30%, placing them in the high-risk category of patients for surgery.

This study had some limitations. The patients were from a single center, resulting in a small sample size. Minimally invasive CABG is a complicated procedure requiring skill, experience, and a steep learning curve. Hence, it is offered to a limited number of highly selected patients, leading to a selection bias. The study was retrospective, limiting the examinations and data to those found in the patient charts. There was no control group, preventing the comparison with a conventional procedure. Multicenter studies are necessary to determine the exact usefulness of the procedure examined here.

Conclusions

In conclusion, using a complete thoracoscopic ultrasonic scalpel procedure to obtain the LIMA during minimally invasive single-vessel CABG surgery in high-risk surgical patients may be feasible and safe. The procedure could benefit a broader range of high-risk surgical patients, especially older adults or those with reduced LVEF, providing the most effective treatment.
