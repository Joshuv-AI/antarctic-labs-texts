Seed inoculation with Rhizobium and integrated nutrient management influences the productivity of groundnut and their residual impact on finger millet
Authors: Jnana Bharati Palai, Ganesh Chandra Malik, Sagar Maitra, Masina Sairam, Mahua Banerjee, Joy Kumar Dey, Sumit Ray, Lamya Ahmed Alkeridis, Laila A Al Shuraym, Samy Sayed, Ahmed Gaber, Akbar Hossain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSole application of chemical fertilizers for crop raising has shown a harmful effect on the environment. Integrated nutrient management (INM) is a better option that meets the crop demand as well as improves the soil quality and the residual soil nutrient for the succeeding crop. Based on the preceding rationale, a two-year factorial randomized block design (FRBD with three replications was conducted to evaluate the impact of integrated nutrient management (INM) on the growth, yield, and nutrient acquisition of groundnut (Arachis hypogaea L.), as well as its subsequent residual effects on finger millet (Eleusine coracana L. Gaertn.). The experimental design incorporated two factors: seed inoculation with biofertilizers and nutrient management practices. The seed inoculation factor comprised two levels: S1 (solid carrier-based Rhizobium) and S2 (liquid carrier-based Rhizobium). The nutrient management factor consisted of five levels: N1 (100% recommended dose of nitrogen (RDN) supplied via urea), N2 (75% RDN from urea + 25% RDN from farmyard manure (FYM)), N3 (50% RDN from urea + 50% RDN from FYM), N4 (25% RDN from urea + 75% RDN from FYM), and N5 (100% RDN supplied via FYM). The recommended dose of fertilizer (RDF) for groundnut was 20:40:20 kg ha− 1 of nitrogen, phosphorus (P2O5) and potassium (K2O). The results showed that seed inoculation did not influence significantly a few growth characters and yield parameters of both groundnut and finger millet, however, seed and grain yield of respective crops and nutrient removal were influenced significantly, and, S1: solid carrier-based Rhizobium noted the highest value. All the growth, yield parameters, yield and nutrient removal by groundnut and finger millet were significantly influence by treatments of nutrient management. The treatment N1: 100% RDN (urea) in groundnut and N5: 100% RDN (FYM) in finger millet indicated significantly the highest growth, productivity and nutrient removal in both years of the experimentation.Keywords: Finger millet, Groundnut, Nutrient management, Nutrient removal, Rhizobium, Seed inoculation, YieldSubject terms: Plant sciences, Plant symbiosis

Introduction

Appropriate nutrient management is essential for obtaining the desired productivity and agricultural sustainability of crops1,2. Groundnut (Arachis hypogaea L.) is an important oilseed crop that is cultivated worldwide within the latitudinal belt of 400N and 400S3,4. Groundnut is grown on 30.92 million ha worldwide, with yields of 54.27 million tonnes and productivity 1755 kg ha− 1, respectively, in 20235. India, a major contributor to global groundnut production, harvested 10.29 million tons from 4.9 million hectares in 2023, achieving an average productivity of 2075 kg ha− 15. Ranking as the world’s second-largest groundnut producer after China6,7. Groundnut kernels are a significant source of nutritional components, containing 45–51% oil and 25% protein8, alongside essential vitamin B complexes, vitamin E, and amino acids, notably a high concentration of arginine9. The management of nutrients through chemical fertilizer alone has adverse effects on the environment, agroecosystems, and soil health. The application of chemical fertilizer to supply plant nutrients for sustainable crop production is not a wise option, and the overuse of chemicals increases the total expenditure of crop production with a reduction in nutrient recovery and soil health10–13. Moreover, simple or complex chemical fertilizers cannot fulfil the demand for all essential nutrients of a crop14, whereas adding organic nutrient inputs to the soil is a holistic approach that adds almost all essential nutrients and supports improved soil properties15, crop quality and agricultural sustainability16. Nutrient availability to crops can be increased by the incorporation of organic manures such as farmyard manure (FYM) into the soil because it improves soil microbial activity17; however, organic manures have low analytical value18. The increase in yield of legume crops caused by bioinoculants has been recorded19,20. Groundnut respond well to microbial inoculants20,21. The productivity enhancement of groundnut was recorded by the integrated application of FYM and microbial inoculants22. Among the different microbial inoculants used, Rhizobium, which is a gram-negative nitrogen-fixing soil bacterium, positively interacts with legumes such as groundnut23, and the use of Rhizobium as a biofertilizer fixes nitrogen from the atmosphere in the soil and provides it to plants as ammonia24.

The medium used for the multiplication of Rhizobium is a vital factor for microbial culture preparation25. The medium that carries live microorganisms is referred to as the carrier. In India, different solid carrier materials, such as rice husk, charcoal, peat, press mud, vermiculite, lignite, soil, and coir dust, are used for Rhizobium inoculation. Currently, liquid carrier-based Rhizobium strains are prepared for maintaining the viability of organisms for a long period of time in yeast extract media supplemented with additives such as polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP), alginate, arabinose, glycerol, etc26. An efficient way to achieve sustainable crop production with high yield is INM, which involves the collective application of chemical fertilizer, organics, and biological agents14. Judicious integration of organic and chemical nutrient inputs can decrease the cost of cultivation because of the increase in the price of chemical inputs such as fertilizer and increase the efficiency of added chemical fertilizer15,27. Furthermore, for the efficient production of crops on farmland, a cropping system-based approach should be considered rather than nourishing the crops individually28. The groundnut-based cropping system is commonly followed by finger millet (Eleusine coracana L. Gartn) among the farmers in the current study area. Finger millet is cultivated mainly in the low-fertility soils of the tropics and subtropics by Indian farmers29. Although it is a less nutrient-requiring crop, its cultivation in less fertile soil and input reduces the yield potential of the crop30–32. Finger millet is a low nutrient-demanding coarse cereal; however, with the organic source of nitrogen, namely FYM, vermicompost and poultry manure etc. registered higher yield as compared to unfertilized plot29. The combination of cereal and legume in sequential cropping provides holistic benefits29. Both groundnut and finger millet are widely cultivated in resource-poor conditions in Odisha, India. Under such conditions, the productivity of both crops is affected by deterioration of the soil quality. On the basis of the popularity of the groundnut-finger millet cropping system and production constraints, a field experiment was conducted to evaluate the impact of INM on the growth, yield, and nutrient uptake of kharif groundnut and its residual effect on rabi finger millet.

The experiment was established at the P.G. Research Farm of M.S. The soil at the experimental site was classified as sandy clay loam.

Experimental design and treatments

The investigation spanned two consecutive agricultural years, encompassing the Kharif and Rabi seasons of 2018–2019 and 2019–2020. The groundnut was cultivated during the Kharif season, followed by the transplantation of finger millet in the subsequent Rabi season. A factorial randomized block design (FRBD) was employed in both years, with treatment combinations replicated three times. Identical plot dimensions of 5.5 m × 3.6 m were maintained for both sequentially growing crops across the two-year period in the same experimental field. The first experimental factor consisted of two levels of Rhizobium inoculation: S1 (solid carrier-based) and S2 (liquid carrier-based). The second factor comprised five nutrient management regimes: N1 (100% recommended dose of nitrogen (RDN) as urea), N2 (75% RDN as urea + 25% RDN as farmyard manure (FYM)), N3 (50% RDN as urea + 50% RDN as FYM), N4 (25% RDN as urea + 75% RDN as FYM), and N5 (100% RDN as FYM). The recommended dose of fertilizer (RDF) for groundnut is 20:40:20 kg ha− 1 N: P₂O₅:K₂O. The groundnut seeds were inoculated with solid carrier-based Rhizobium @ 35 g kg− 1 seeds and liquid carrier-based Rhizobium @ 20 ml kg− 1. This nutrient regime was exclusively applied to the groundnut crop, enabling evaluation of residual fertility effects on the subsequent finger millet crop under zero-input conditions. The experimental design facilitated a comprehensive assessment of both the immediate treatment effects on groundnut productivity, and the carry-over impacts on the succeeding finger millet crop within the rotation system.

Treatment descriptions

Charcoal is used as a carrier in solid carrier-based Rhizobium. Initially, during seed inoculation, the Rhizobium population was 108 CFU ml− 1. The recommended dose of fertilizer was 20 kg N, 40 kg P2O5 and 40 kg K2O ha− 1. Inorganically, N is applied through urea; however, P and K are supplied through single super phosphate (SSP) and muriate of potash (MOP), respectively. The proportion of nutrient required through the FYM was calculated separately according to the treatment and was applied to the respective plots one week before the sowing of the groundnut, and it was mixed with the soil properly and spread evenly throughout the whole field. The nutrient contents of the FYM were 0.5% N, 0.2% P2O5 and 0.5% K2O for first year and 0.48% N, 0.23% P2O5 and 0.51% K2O for second year. (N, P2O5 and K2O content of FYM was analysed in the laboratory on dry weight basis and the mentioned nutrient content was used to quantify FYM requirement for field application). The P2O5 and K2O contents in the FYM were considered for the treatments containing FYM and subtracted from the required quantities of SSP and MOP.

Crop management

Agronomic management adopted for groundnut

The experiment was carried out in the FRBD for each treatment combination while maintaining the provision of irrigation and drainage. The seeds of the groundnut variety Kadiri-6 (K-6) were chosen from the Kharif season, with row × plant spacing of 30 cm × 10 cm. The groundnut seeds were inoculated with biofertilizers such as solid carrier-based Rhizobium at 35 g kg− 1 seeds and liquid carrier-based Rhizobium at 20 ml kg− 1. The whole amount of FYM, urea, SSP or MOP was applied as the basal dose40 The crop was hand weeded once with simultaneous hoeing at 25 DAS (Days after sowing) in groundnut in both years. Irrigation was given once after sowing groundnut during the first year for a uniform crop stand, as there was insufficient soil moisture. The crop was irrigated via the border strip method. However, no irrigation was given during the second year to the groundnut crop because of the well-distributed rainfall during the entire crop period. To reduce crop weed competition, imazethapyr@100 ml ha− 1 was applied as an early postemergence herbicide in groundnut. During the crop growing period, groundnut suffer from late leaf spot disease (Phaeoisariopsis personata), which is controlled by the application of a mixed formulation of carbendazim and mancozeb in 2.5 g L− 1 water. Insects such as tobacco caterpillar (Spodoptera litura) and white grub (Holotrichia serrata) were observed during both years of the crop growing season, and these insects were controlled by the application of chlorpyrifos @2.5 ml L− 1 of water.

Agronomic practices of finger millet

Finger millet was transplanted into the same field where the layout was designed for groundnut, and the land was prepared for finger millet without breaking the layout to study the residual effect of Rhizobium and INM used for groundnut crop on succeeding finger millet. Finger millet is cultivated in the residual soil of previous groundnut crops without any externally added nutrients, such as organic or chemical fertilizers. The same method was followed in both years. The finger millet variety Godavari was used for the study and was sown in the nursery for transplanting at a rate of 2 kg ha− 1. The crop was transplanted in the Rabi season with a row spacing of 20 cm × 10 cm and one seedling per hill. Once, hand weeding was performed on the finger millet at 21 days after transplanting (DAT) in 2018-19 and 23 DAT in 2019-20. Irrigation was provided to the finger millet three times: once before the seedlings were transplanted and once at three-week intervals in both years. Finger millet was attacked by blast disease (Pyricularia setariae) and a pink stem borer (Sesamia inferens), and both were managed by the application of separate sprays of carbendazim@ 2 g L− 1 and chlorpyrifos@ 2.5 ml L− 1, respectively, for both years. The net plot area of finger millet was harvested manually, sun-dried, and threshed separately for each treatment combination. The weight of the grain was recorded for each plot at 13% moisture content and converted to kg ha− 1.

Measurements and analytical procedure

Growth and yield attributes and yields of groundnut

During the experiment, measurements of plant height, dry matter accumulation, number of branches, and leaf area were taken at different developmental phases to assess growth. For dry matter determination, the aerial plant parts were separated and subjected to oven drying at approximately 60 °C until a stable weight was reached. The total dry matter accumulation was then computed by aggregating the dry weights of all the plant fractions, with the results presented as g m− 2. At the time of harvest, key yield components, specifically the number of filled pods, the weight of 100 seeds, and the pod weight per individual plant, were evaluated.

Growth, yield attributes and yields of finger millet

Like those of groundnut, the growth parameters of finger millet, namely, plant height, dry matter accumulation, tiller number and leaf area, were measured periodically. The yield attributes, such as effective tillers, finger earhead− 1 weight and 1000-grain weight, were noted at harvest. After being harvested from the net plots, the millet ears/heads and straw were dried according to the treatment combinations. After sun drying, with a moisture level of 13%, the grain weight was recorded and converted to kg ha− 1. The straw yield was also quantified according to the treatment combination.

Leaf area index, harvest index and nutrient uptake of crops and shelling percentage

The leaf area index (LAI) of the crops was determined via the methodology outlined by Watson41.

The harvest index (HI), expressed as a percentage, for both groundnut and finger millet was calculated according to the formula provided by Donald42.

Nutrient uptake by various components of groundnut and finger millet plants was quantified by multiplying the nutrient concentration in each plant part by its corresponding dry matter yield, following the method described by Sharma43.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analysed via a mixed-model approach implemented in Microsoft Excel 2019 (Gomez and Gomez, 1984)44. Fixed treatment effects were evaluated through F-tests in the ANOVA framework, whereas random effects were accounted for in the error structure. Treatment means were compared via least significant differences derived from the standard error of means at α = 0.05. Multiple comparisons were subsequently performed via DMRT in OPSTAT (http://opstat.somee.com/mulcomp/data1.asp; accessed on 20 Nov. 25) to control the familywise error rate across all possible treatment contrasts.

Effects on nutrient removal

Seed inoculation with S1 (solid carrier-based Rhizobium) resulted in significantly greater total nitrogen removal in groundnut than did S2 (liquid carrier-based Rhizobium) in both 2018-19 and 2019-20 (Table 5). Discussion

The substrate that holds the microbes improves their shelf-life and provides nutrients for their survival, which is used for successful inoculation45,46. From the above experiments, we found that both solid and liquid Rhizobium presented similar results for most of the parameters, this might be due to equal CFU count maintained initially in both the treatments. In the present study, treatment with solid carrier-based Rhizobium was significantly superior to treatment with liquid Rhizobium in terms of the expression of a few attributes of groundnut and finger millet. In support of the above results, one study revealed that the effects of solid carrier-based Rhizobium and liquid-based Rhizobium on the plant height and dry matter accumulation of lentils remained statistically significant; however, the solid carrier-based Rhizobium had a marginally greater value47. The liquid bacterial inoculation is less efficient in enhancing crop growth due to environmental constrains. The limitations include short self-life and susceptibility to microbial dispersion onto atmosphere and groundwater due to improper handling. In contrast, solid carrier-based inoculants demonstrated better potential for microbial viability, facilitates targeted delivery in the rhizosphere, better self-life and better handling. The solid carriers have enhanced physical and chemical stability coupled with favourable buffering capacity and enhanced soil water retention. Such synergistic crosslinks create a conducive microenvironment for microbial growth and development. Hence, such inherent capabilities of solid carrier may have accounted for the superior performance of solid carrier treatment (S1) as compared to liquid counterpart (S2)48.An earlier study reported that 75% of the recommended NPK + Rhizobium liquid formulation for seed inoculation resulted in marginally greater fenugreek seed yield (1394 kg ha− 1) than did the 75% RDF + Rhizobium solid carrier-based formulation for seed inoculation (1379 kg ha− 1), but both were statistically significant49. However, in contrast to the above results, one researcher50 mentioned that liquid Rhizobium performed better than the solid carrier Rhizobium might be due to a higher population density of microbes in the liquid formulation. However, inoculation with Rhizobium biofertilizer and integrated nutrient management practices promoted the growth and yield of groundnut, likely because of an increase in root nodulation and nitrogen fixation51.

Compared with treatments involving combinations of chemical fertilizers and farmyard manure (FYM), the application of 100% of the recommended dose of nitrogen (RDN) through chemical fertilizers resulted in the greatest growth and yield of groundnut at the same total RDN. This finding aligns with previous research52, which reported that the maximum plant height, number of branches per plant, and pod yield in groundnut were achieved with 100% of the recommended fertilizer dose, followed by treatments combining 75% RDN (urea) with 25% nitrogen from the FYM. Furthermore, a slightly greater groundnut pod yield and nutrient uptake were observed with a combination of 75% RDN (urea) and 25% RDN (FYM) than with the application of 100% RDN solely through FYM51. This might be due to the acceleration of the assimilation rate, plant metabolism and cell division process, with enhanced mineralization as chemical nutrients are provided. The increase in root and shoot length, number of leaves m− 2 and plant dry matter accumulation of Brahmi under the N: P:K:100:50:50 kg ha− 1 treatment compared with the N:25 kg ha− 1 treatment, along with FYM@15 t ha− 1 (75 kg N), might be associated with readily available nitrogen from urea during the vegetative stages of crop growth53. The highest dry matter accumulation with the mentioned treatment might be due to the greater shoot length and number of leaves m− 2 obtained because of greater nutrient availability. The above experiment revealed that the substitution of the 25% N dose with FYM and the application of the remaining 75% N dose through urea resulted in the highest shoot length, LAI and pod yield in the second year compared with 100% N through urea, which was also similar to the findings of other authors54. A marginally greater plant height and tuber yield were recorded for potato due to the application of 75% RDF + 25% N-FYM, followed by 100% RDF, and both treatments were statistically similar55. Similar results were also reported in the present study. The number of Pods in plant-1 steadily decreased with increasing supplementation of the recommended doses of N through the FYM, which might be due to the slow mineralization of nutrients, which reduced nutrient availability at the time of crop requirements56.

A greater dry matter accumulation, LAI, number of effective tillers m− 2, and grain and straw yield of rice were found in the 75% RDN (urea) + 25% N (FYM) treatment, followed by the 100% RDN (urea) treatment, and both remained statistically significant. The results of the present study are in accordance with the findings of previous studies, which clearly mentioned that the readily available nutrients from inorganic fertilizer might be responsible for the enhancement of growth characteristics at the vegetative stage and yield attributes and yield at the reproductive phase57. A significantly greater green fodder yield and nutrient uptake were recorded in oat with the application of 100% N (urea) than in those with 50% N (urea) + 50% N (FYM), which is also similar to the current experimental results58. Most crop nutrient uptake depends on the available soil nutrient status, plant dry matter accumulation and nutrient content of the plant. The relatively high plant nutrient content and biological yield in the aforementioned treatments were responsible for relatively high nutrient removal by oats58. The highest available nutrient content in postharvest groundnut soil was recorded with 100% RDN (FYM), and the lowest was recorded with 100% RDN (urea). This might be the reason for the highest recorded plant height in finger millet from 100% RDN (FYM). The above results are related to the findings of59. Both the crops received a well distributed rainfall during second year cropping period than the first year. This might be the reason for overall growth and yield of both the crops were higher in second year as compared to the first year,

The increase in available N and P2O5 contents might be due to the decomposition of FYM, which adds N and P directly into the soil60. The residual soil fertility status was good, as the previous crop was groundnut (a leguminous crop), and furthermore, it was greater in the treatment consisting of 100% RDN (FYM) than in that consisting of 100% RDN (urea). For this reason, there might be a greater LAI of finger millet with 100% RDN (FYM), and the lowest LAI was recorded with 100% RDN (urea). Earlier, a report revealed that there was a residual effect on succeeding crop wheat from FYM-treated preceding rice61. Moreover, they mentioned that FYM application could help sustain a long-term rice‒wheat cropping system. There are many advantages of the application of FYM to soil, as it releases organic acids into the soil, which increases nutrient availability; in particular, P fixation in the soil is minimal, which helps improve root development. The WHC (water holding capacity) increased due to the addition of FYM. The probable reason for the greater dry matter accumulation was that the residual effect of FYM in groundnut and residual soil fertility with 100% RDN (FYM) enhanced the second crop to flourish, followed by the amalgamation of both FYM and chemical fertilizer. The lowest dry matter production in finger millet was recorded with the treatment comprising 100% RDN (urea) in the preceding groundnut. The application of organic nutrients to preceding crops might result in better performance of succeeding crops due to pronounced residual effects62. The number of tillers plant− 1 of finger millet might have impacted dry matter accumulation, as noted with the respective treatments, and this effect was due to the residual nutrients in the soil after groundnut. Furthermore, the treatments comprising a relatively high proportion of organic inputs facilitated enhanced growth attributes, including dry matter accumulation and the LAI of finger millet, because more residual soil nutrients were available from the aforesaid treatments.

The application of inorganic fertilizer resulted in a greater yield in the applied season; however, it did not have a large effect on the subsequent crop in terms of the nutrients applied to the preceding crop. Although organic manures are less efficient at nutrient supplementation than inorganic fertilizers are, in the long run, they improve the residual soil nutrient status and crop yield as well as increase nutrient uptake by the second crop63,64. This might be the cause of the greater productivity of finger millet in the residual soil of the groundnut plot treated with 100% RDN (FYM). Higher seed and haulm yield of black grams have been recorded from the 75% RDN + 25% (vermicompost) treatment than from the 100% (RDN through urea) treatment when cultivated on residual soil fertility from the preceding rice crop65. Although urea hydrolyses rapidly, losses of nitrogen is significant through volatilization, leaching and denitrification. Further, the continuous use leads to soil acidification, curtailing P availability, decreases soil microbial activity which directly hampers the nutrient cycling for the subsequent crops. Therefore, the aforementioned reasons could have resulted in decline of succeeding finger millet yield in 100% N applied through urea66–69.The available N status of the postharvest soil after groundnut harvesting was greater in the 100% RDN (FYM)-treated plot than in the control plot, which could be due to reduced N removal, as a lower biological yield of groundnut was noted. The slow release of nitrogen and better root nodulation and N fixation in the 100% RDN (FYM)-treated plot might be the other reasons for the greater amount of available N in the postharvest soil after groundnut70. The interaction of organic matter and chemical fertilizers improved the available P and K content in the soil71,72. This might be due to the reduced K fixation by the organic acids released in the decomposition process of organic manures73.

Conclusion

After two years of cultivation in groundnut-finger millet cropping systems, the results revealed that, compared with the liquid carrier-based Rhizobium system, the solid carrier-based Rhizobium system improved the growth, productivity and nutrient removal of groundnut and finger millet. However, some growth and yield attributes of both crops remained unaffected by seed inoculation. This is a consequence of the equal Rhizobium population (CFU) initially maintained in both treatments. Similarly, among the nutrient management practices, 100% RDN (urea) resulted in significantly greater growth, yield and nutrient removal of groundnut due to readily available nutrients. However, the 100% RDN (FYM) treatment resulted in the greatest growth, yield and nutrient uptake of finger millet because more residual nutrients were present in the soil obtained from the abovementioned treatments. We concluded that different solid and liquid carrier-based Rhizobium strains did not have greater effects if an equal Rhizobium population (CFU) was maintained. In the case of nutrient management, the first crop performed better with chemical nitrogen; however, the FYM-treated plot presented greater residual soil fertility, which improved the yield of the succeeding finger millet. On the basis of the results of the present study, INM in groundnut may facilitate proper growth and productivity in the groundnut-finger millet cropping system. Hence, integrated nutrient management in groundnut-finger millet cropping systems can be considered a suitable option to ensure agricultural sustainability in southern Odisha, India. This study paved the way to replicate this experiment in various agroclimatic zones of India. As the Rhizobium species is host plant specific, seed inoculation should be done with appropriate Rhizobium species in groundnut.
