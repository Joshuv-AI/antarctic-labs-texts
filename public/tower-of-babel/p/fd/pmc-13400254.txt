Mannose-glycosylated BSA and metal-phenolic network nanocoatings enhance gastrointestinal transit survival and intestinal adhesion of Lactobacillus plantarum B2
Authors: Xiu-Xiu Zhang, Wang-Wei Zhang, Yuan Song, Run-Hui Ma, Zhi-Jing Ni, Kiran Thakur, Jian-Guo Zhang, Zhao-Jun Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMannose-glycosylated bovine serum albumin (GBSA) offers enhanced intestinal adhesion capabilities through improved surface physicochemical properties and potential interactions with the intestinal mucosa. This study prepared the GBSA with a grafting degree of up to 91.07% in a natural deep eutectic solvent (NADES). BSA or GBSA was combined with EGCG/Ca2+ metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) to develop nanocoatings. Through layer-by-layer self-assembly, these coatings encapsulated the high-riboflavin-producing Lactobacillus plantarum B2 to enhance gastrointestinal survival and probiotic functionality. The results demonstrated that this encapsulation strategy achieved efficient probiotic loading and markedly improved storage stability. The coatings also fortified gastrointestinal transit tolerance and ROS defense of the encapsulated strain. Notably, the GBSA-based nanocoating enhanced adhesion to Caco-2 cells and prolonged intestinal fluorescence retention, while highly preserving the riboflavin-producing capacity of the probiotic. This single-cell encapsulation technology enables high survival rates and enhanced intestinal retention of a riboflavin-producing probiotic, showing considerable promise for functional food applications and dietary riboflavin supplementation.Keywords: Glycosylated bovine serum albumin, Intestinal adhesion, Metal-phenolic networks, Probiotic encapsulation, Lactobacillus plantarum B2, Riboflavin

Introduction

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is widely recognized as a safe carrier in food and drug delivery systems, offering high biocompatibility, biodegradability, and mucosal adhesion (Pinto et al., 2022). BSA modification methods include phosphorylation, reductive amination, and glycosylation (Wang & Zhang, 2018). Among these, Maillard glycosylation mediated by natural deep eutectic solvents (NADES) demonstrates superior controllability and efficiency. NADES typically form through specific ratios of hydrogen bond acceptors (e.g., choline chloride) and hydrogen bond donors (e.g., reducing sugars) (Ma et al., 2023). As the C2 isomer of glucose, mannose displays heightened glycation propensity attributable to its unique stereochemical structure, achieving higher modification levels. Prior studies have demonstrated the efficacy of mannose-modified proteins for mucosal targeting; for instance, mannosylated bovine serum albumin (Man-BSA) has been shown to specifically bind to mannose receptors on macrophages and dendritic cells, facilitating receptor-mediated endocytosis (Higuchi et al., 2004), and mannose-modified Prunus persica kernel protein nanoparticles have been developed for oral delivery to target inflammatory macrophages in the colon via mannose receptor-mediated cellular internalization (Tao et al., 2026). Glycated BSA retains advantages such as low toxicity, degradability, and mucosal adhesion (Yang et al., 2025). Furthermore, mannose residues have been reported to interact with mannose receptors on M cells and macrophages in intestinal Peyer's patches, suggesting their potential to enhance intestinal interactions in vivo (Andriana et al., 2024). These properties make glycosylated BSA a promising material for improving probiotic adhesion and retention at the intestinal mucosa.

Probiotics have emerged as promising therapeutic agents for various health conditions, yet their clinical application faces significant challenges. Probiotics enhance gut health by promoting digestion and absorption, antagonizing pathogens, and balancing microbiota. However, their extreme sensitivity to high temperatures, gastric acid, and bile results in drastically reduced viability during gastrointestinal transit (Zhu et al., 2025). Weak adhesion and short retention time further diminish their therapeutic potential (Zhu et al., 2025). Therefore, developing an oral delivery technology that shields environmental stressors, enhances intestinal adhesion and retention, maintains biological functionality, and prolongs retention time has become a critical research focus. Microencapsulation and single-cell encapsulation strategies—such as biomimetic mineralization, lipid self-assembly, silkworm cocoon-inspired nanocoatings (Zhang et al., 2025), and metal-phenolic network (MPN) coatings—have been widely applied for oral probiotic delivery (Zhu et al., 2023). Among these, MPN coatings have gained prominence due to their operational simplicity and efficiency. By forming a nano-armor on each probiotic cell surface, MPN coatings not only shield against gastrointestinal stress but also enhance mucosal adhesion without compromising probiotic functionality, making them a current hotspot in probiotic delivery (Fan et al., 2022).

The success of MPN nano-armor hinges on the coordination strength between polyphenols and metal ions (Geng et al., 2022). Recent studies have further demonstrated that metal ion-mediated dual-polysaccharide coatings can enhance the microenvironmental homeostasis and adhesion capacity of probiotics, highlighting the critical role of metal coordination in stabilizing protective coatings and promoting mucosal interaction (Si et al., 2026). Polyphenolic compounds naturally occur in plants like tea leaves, fruits, and vegetables, exhibiting diverse biological activities including antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties (Su et al., 2023). On microbial surfaces, their catechin or gallocatechin groups can coordinate with transition metal ions, rapidly assembling into a nanocoating. This coating retains multiple biological activities, such as antioxidant properties, after film formation (Gao et al., 2025). Among these, tea polyphenols like epigallocatechin (EGC) and (−)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) exhibit excellent biocompatibility and degradability, making them ideal materials for constructing food-grade single-cell armor (Ge et al., 2024). EGCG has garnered significant attention due to its multiple chelation sites, pH responsiveness, and potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (Rosenthal et al., 2025). Upon chelation with food-grade metal ions (Ca2+, Zn2+, or Fe3+), it rapidly assembles into a pH-sensitive, reversible, antioxidant, and antibacterial “polyphenol-metal” nanocoating on probiotic surfaces (Zhang et al., 2025). However, the monolayer shell exhibits limited mechanical strength and lacks adhesion-promoting surface ligands, making it vulnerable to gastric emptying, bile dissolution, and mucus renewal, thereby significantly limiting intestinal colonization rates. The combination of MPN coatings with glycosylated BSA offers a promising strategy to overcome these limitations by providing both physical protection and enhanced intestinal adhesion and retention.

Among the various probiotic strains that could benefit from such advanced delivery systems, riboflavin-producing strains hold therapeutic significance. Riboflavin, a water-soluble essential micronutrient, participates in human redox balance, oxidative phosphorylation, and other enzymatic reactions (Wang et al., 2022). Enzymatic conversion of riboflavin yields flavin mononucleotide (FMN) and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), acting as redox-active cofactors for flavoproteins (Feng et al., 2021). Flavoproteins play crucial roles in carbohydrate, protein, and lipid metabolism, as well as numerous biological redox reactions (Ripa et al., 2022). Humans cannot synthesize riboflavin and must obtain it from dietary sources such as dairy products, meat, eggs, or green leafy vegetables (Lu et al., 2022). Deficiency disrupts metabolic processes and disturbs the gut microbiota, leading to symptoms such as growth retardation, gastrointestinal disorders, and angular cheilitis (Bedani et al., 2024). Although over half of gut microorganisms possess riboflavin synthesis pathways, their in situ production contributes minimally to the human requirement of 1.1-1.3 mg/day, with clinical deficiency manifesting at intakes below 0.5-0.6 mg/day, highlighting the urgent need to develop high-yielding vitamin B2 strains and efficient delivery methods (Diez-Ozaeta et al., 2025). Among them, Lactobacillus plantarum B2 (L. plantarum B2, CECT 8328), a riboflavin-overproducing mutant isolated in Valencia, Spain, retains classic probiotic benefits and synthesizes large amounts of riboflavin in situ. Applied in non-dairy B2-fortified matrices, this strain mitigates hyporiboflavinemia, oxidative damage, enteritis, and dysbiosis in vivo, offering a convenient microbial solution for riboflavin insufficiency (Zhu et al., 2022). However, like other probiotics, L. plantarum B2 faces challenges in surviving gastrointestinal transit and achieving prolonged intestinal retention, necessitating the development of protective delivery strategies.

Accordingly, the present study was designed not merely to improve probiotic survival, but to integrate three complementary components within a single delivery system: an EGCG/Ca2+ MPN layer for gastrointestinal and oxidative protection, a mannose-glycosylated BSA outer layer for coating stabilization and enhanced intestinal interaction, and the riboflavin-overproducing strain L. plantarum B2 as the functional core. EGCG/Ca2+ was first assembled on the bacterial surface, followed by deposition of BSA or GBSA to form a bilayer single-cell coating. The effects of the coating on cell viability, storage stability, oxidative-stress resistance, gastrointestinal survival, adhesion, intestinal retention, and metabolic activity were then evaluated. In contrast to conventional single-layer MPN encapsulation, protein-only coatings, or nonspecific mucoadhesive carriers, this strategy combines protection, adhesion-related surface functionalization, and in situ riboflavin production in one food-grade probiotic delivery platform.

Materials and methods

Materials

Riboflavin, daidzin, daidzein, genistin, and genistein (≥98% purity) were procured from Chengdu Mansite Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). FITC and Hoechst 33342 were procured from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All other analytical-grade reagents such as bovine serum albumin (BSA), (−)-epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), choline chloride, and D-mannose were purchased from Yuanye (Shanghai, China).

Preparation of mannose-glycosylated BSA (GBSA)

NADES were generated as described by Ma et al. (2023) via blending choline chloride and D-mannose (equimolar, 1:1), followed by 90 °C agitation for 3 h until the mixture became clear and homogeneous. Maillard reaction conditions were optimized using Design Expert 13.0 (Stat-Ease Inc., USA) using grafting degree (DG) as the response (experimental design detailed in Table S1). After glycosylation, the reaction mixture was transferred into a dialysis membrane with a molecular-weight cutoff of 3500 Da and dialyzed against deionized water at 4 °C for 48 h. The external water was replaced every 2 h to remove residual choline chloride, unreacted mannose, and soluble low-molecular-weight Maillard reaction products. OPA assay protocol: The OPA reagent was freshly prepared by dissolving 40 mg o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) in 1 mL methanol, followed by addition of 2.5 mL sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, 20%, w/v), 25 mL sodium tetraborate solution (100 mmol/L, pH 9.7), and 100 μL β-mercaptoethanol, with deionized water added to a final volume of 50 mL. For glycosylation degree determination, BSA or GBSA samples were dissolved in PBS (0.1 mol/L, pH 7.4) to a final concentration of 2 mg/mL. The sample solution (200 μL) was mixed with 4 mL OPA reagent and incubated at 35 °C for exactly 2 min. The absorbance was measured at 340 nm against a blank containing PBS and OPA reagent without protein. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

The vibrational states of D-mannose, BSA, and GBSA were recorded using an FTIR spectrometer (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in the range of 4000 – 650 cm−1. Samples were prepared by mixing with KBr (1:100, w/w) and pressing into transparent pellets. Spectra were collected at a resolution of 4 cm−1 with 32 scans per sample. Background correction was performed using a pure KBr pellet (Ma et al., 2023).

Preparation of nanocoated encapsulated L. plantarum B2

L. plantarum B2 (CECT 8328, Valencia, Spain) was activated in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h. Following Wang et al. (2022), the nano-encapsulation procedure was illustrated in Fig. 1A. Cells from 50 mL culture (OD600 = 0.2) were pelleted by centrifugation (4 °C, 10,000 × g, 5 min), rinsed with 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4), and re-dispersed in the identical buffer to the initial volume. The suspension was added dropwise to a freshly prepared mixture of EGCG (1 mg/mL, 150 mL) and CaCl2 (1 mg/mL, 50 mL), resulting in a coating mixture with final concentrations of 0.75 mg/mL EGCG and 0.25 mg/mL CaCl2 (total volume 200 mL; EGCG/Ca2+ mass ratio = 3:1), and stirred at 25 °C, 100 rpm for 30 min to form EGCG@L. These concentrations and the 3:1 mass ratio were adopted from Wang et al. (2025), who previously demonstrated that this formulation achieves optimal MPN coating uniformity, stability, and biocompatibility for L. plantarum B2 encapsulation. After centrifugation, the pellet was re-dispersed in 50 mL BSA or GBSA solution (1 mg/mL) and stirred under the same conditions for 1 h to yield BSA-EGCG@L or GBSA-EGCG@L, respectively. The BSA/GBSA concentration of 1 mg/mL was selected to ensure complete surface coverage of the EGCG@L intermediate through protein-polyphenol interactions, based on established layer-by-layer encapsulation protocols (Zhang et al., 2025; Gao et al., 2025). The double-encapsulated bacteria were collected by centrifugation, washed and stored until use.

Characterizations

The morphology and structure of L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L suspensions were visualized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM-1400Flash, JEOL, Japan). Samples (5 μL) were applied to carbon-coated copper grids, rinsed with sterile water, air-dried and imaged at 120 kV and 15,000× magnification. For scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Regulus 8230, Hitachi, Japan), equivalent aliquots were deposited on metal foils, air-dried, mounted on stubs and sputter-coated with gold prior to observation. Apparent morphology and height distribution were obtained by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Dimension Icon, Bruker, USA) using freshly cleaved mica substrates air dried and scanned at 4000× magnification. Particle size and zeta potential were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern NanoZS90, UK) at 25 °C (Zhu et al., 2025).

Encapsulation efficiency

We evaluated encapsulation efficacy using laser scanning microscopy (LSM980, Carl Zeiss, Germany). L. plantarum B2 cells were stained with Hoechst 33342, encapsulated to form EGCG@L, and then labeled with BSA-FITC or GBSA-FITC for 30 min. After washing, samples were imaged at 1000× and 4000× magnification (Gao et al., 2025).

Survival throughout the coating process

Cell suspensions (L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L) at OD600 = 0.2 were decimal-diluted (10−1 to −6), inoculated onto MRS agar, and cultivated at 37 °C for 24 h. Survival rates were calculated for each group. Separate aliquots underwent viability staining (live/dead bacterial kit) and examined by LSM980 to determine the live/dead ratio (Zhu et al., 2023).

Long-term storage viability

Probiotic suspensions (L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L) were stored at 4 and 25 °C. Viable cells were quantified at 7-day intervals over a 21-day period according to Section 2.6. The live/dead ratio was simultaneously assessed by LSM980 (Wang et al., 2022).

H2O2 tolerance

For oxidative-stress assays, 1 mL aliquots of each suspension were challenged with 9 mL of 0.5 mM H2O2 and maintained at 37 °C. Cell viability was determined at 30-min intervals up to 120 min following the procedure outlined in Section 2.6. Concurrently, cell suspensions underwent LSM980 imaging to quantify survival rates via live/dead discrimination (Gao et al., 2025).

To quantify intracellular ROS levels, a DCFH-DA assay was performed following H2O2 challenge. Bacterial suspensions (Free L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L, and GBSA-EGCG@L) were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing with PBS to remove extracellular probe, cells were challenged with 0.5 mM H2O2 for 2 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence microplate reader (Synergy H1, BioTek, USA; excitation 488 nm, emission 525 nm). Survival throughout simulated digestion

Simulated oral (SOF), gastric (SGF), and intestinal (SIF) digestive fluids were prepared as described by Zhu et al. (2025). Bacterial suspensions (four groups) underwent successive challenge with SOF (5 min), SGF (1 h), and SIF (2 h) at 37 °C with mild shaking (100 rpm). Viable cells were quantified at each stage as described in Section 2.6. Parallel samples were stained and immediately examined by LSM980 to determine the live/dead ratio.

Adhesion capacity to Caco-2 cells

Caco-2 monolayers were prepared as described by Zhu et al. (2023). Cells were maintained in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 20% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin under 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells (1.0 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded at 1 mL per well into a 24-well plate and cultured for 15 days with daily medium changes to establish a complete monolayer. The monolayer was stained with Hoechst 33342 for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by gentle washing with PBS to remove excess dye. mCherry-labeled L. plantarum B2 was encapsulated and diluted with 4× sterile PBS. Diluted bacterial suspensions (500 μL) were inoculated into individual wells of 24-well plates and maintained at 37 °C for 1 h. Non-adherent bacteria were gently washed away with PBS. Adherent bacteria were observed using LSM980, mCherry-labeled bacteria appear red, while Hoechst-stained Caco-2 cells appear blue.

In vivo adhesion

mCherry-labeled L. plantarum B2 encapsulated in nanocoatings was evaluated for in vivo adhesion using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS, In-Vivo Fx Pro, Consul, Canada). Twenty-four two-month-old male BALB/c mice (Animal Ethics Approval No. HFUT20260114002) were randomly divided into four groups (n = 6 per group): free L. plantarum B2 (control), EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L, and GBSA-EGCG@L. Mice underwent 18-h food deprivation with free drinking water containing IPTG (5 g/L) and ampicillin (1 g/L). The 18-h fasting period was employed to standardize gastrointestinal transit conditions and minimize residual food interference with bacterial retention and fluorescence imaging. IPTG (5 g/L) was administered to induce mCherry fluorescence expression in the recombinant L. plantarum B2 strain, which carries an IPTG-inducible promoter upstream of the mCherry gene. Ampicillin (1 g/L) was administered primarily to maintain selective pressure for retention of the mCherry-expression plasmid and thereby improve the stability of fluorescence tracing during the experiment. Following the fasting period, mice received 200 μL oral gavage of bacterial suspension (OD600 = 0.2, approximately 1.0 × 108 CFU/mL) containing L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L, or GBSA-EGCG@L. The gavage dosage (200 μL) and bacterial concentration (OD600 = 0.2) were selected based on established protocols for probiotic colonization studies in murine models, ensuring a standardized bacterial load of approximately 2.0 × 107 CFU per mouse while minimizing gastrointestinal distension. In vivo fluorescence imaging was performed at 4, 8, 16, 24, and 48 h post-gavage under anesthesia. At 48 h, mice underwent euthanasia for whole-gut tissue extraction and subsequent imaging (Zhu et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025).

Physiological activity

Growth curve

To generate growth curves, 100 μL aliquots of each bacterial suspension (OD600 = 0.2) were inoculated into 200 mL of MRS broth and incubated at 37 °C. OD600 was monitored at 3-h intervals throughout a 24-h period to generate growth curves (Wang et al., 2022).

Acid production capacity

Per Zhu et al. (2022), fresh soy milk was produced. Bacterial suspensions were dispensed into MRS and soymilk matrices (1:100, v/v). Temporal pH changes were assessed at 3-h time points over 24 h at 37 °C.

Riboflavin production capacity

Following pH adjustment (to 4.0) and centrifugation (13,000 × g, 10 min), supernatants from fresh or fermented soy milk (37 °C, 8 h) were filtered (0.22 μm) and subjected to HPLC-UV analysis (WondaSil C18, 4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) to determine riboflavin (25 °C; water/methanol 70:30, v/v; 0.2 mL/min; λ = 267 nm; 20 μL injections) (Zhu et al., 2025).

Isoflavone conversion capacity

Isoflavones were extracted from freeze-dried powders of fresh and fermented soy milk (prepared in 2.12.3) using methanol, and the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. UPLC-MS/MS analysis (Waters Acquity Premier XE) of isoflavones utilized a WondaSil C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm) with gradient elution (A: water, B: methanol): 0 - 8 min (90 - 10% A), 8 - 8.2 min (10 - 90% A), 8.2 - 10 min (10% A) at 0.1 mL/min, λ = 260 nm, 20 μL injection (Zhu et al., 2023).

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test (SPSS Statistics 22.0, SPSS Inc., USA) at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Optimized preparation of GBSA

Response surface methodology (RSM) optimization of BSA Maillard reactions in choline chloride-mannose NADES yielded DG values and variance metrics listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. The model showed satisfactory agreement between the predicted and experimentally determined DG values. Response surface plots (Fig. S1) elucidated factor impacts on DG. Under optimized conditions (79.35 °C, 2.81 h, 24.80% moisture, NADES:BSA 22.03, w/w), GBSA synthesis in choline chloride-mannose NADES yielded a predicted DG of 93.92%. Experimental verification revealed 91.07% DG, with a deviation of approximately 3% from the predicted value, thereby validating model robustness. FTIR spectroscopy confirmed successful mannose grafting, as evidenced by the pronounced absorption band at ∼1075 cm−1 corresponding to glycosidic C–O–C stretching vibrations in GBSA, characteristic of the mannose moiety (Fig. S2). These findings corroborate the elevated glycosylation yields previously documented for BSA in choline chloride/glucose NADES (Ma et al., 2023) and significantly exceed the DG value (21.10%) achieved in conventional aqueous systems under pulsed electric fields for glucose glycosylation of BSA (Xu et al., 2024). NADES significantly increased the effective collision frequency between proteins and sugar molecules by reducing mass transfer resistance and enhancing local substrate concentration, thereby boosting DG (Fu et al., 2020). This further establishes NADES as a suitable medium for efficient glycosylation.

After the Maillard reaction, GBSA was dialyzed against deionized water using a 3500 Da molecular-weight-cutoff membrane at 4 °C for 48 h, with water replacement every 2 h, to remove residual choline chloride, free mannose, and soluble low-molecular-weight reaction products. However, because these residual components were not quantitatively measured, their complete removal cannot be confirmed. Moreover, the digestibility, immunogenicity, and long-term ingestion safety of highly glycosylated BSA remain to be evaluated before its broader food-related application.

Morphology and encapsulation efficiency

The coating procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1A. EGCG, a safe and edible polyphenol, acts as a bio-crosslinker and has been widely used for probiotic nano-encapsulation (Chen et al., 2025). Recent advances in silkworm cocoon-inspired nanocoatings have demonstrated that hierarchical, multi-layered architectures can provide robust mechanical protection while maintaining cell viability (Zhang et al., 2025), supporting the rationale for our double-layer MPN/protein coating design. BSA exhibits excellent biocompatibility and polyphenol-binding capacity, while mannose-glycosylated BSA (GBSA) improves dispersion stability and may enhance intestinal adhesion and retention (Tao et al., 2026). Briefly, EGCG first adsorbs onto the bacterial surface via its catechol/gallocatechol moieties and subsequently coordinates with Ca2+ to form a cross-linked monolayer (EGCG@L) as illustrated in Fig. 1A. Leveraging the affinity between BSA/GBSA and EGCG, a protein-polyphenol bilayer nanocoating (BSA-EGCG@L or GBSA-EGCG@L) was then deposited, yielding the final double-layer architecture.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) visualized a conspicuous outer shell encapsulating L. plantarum B2, unlike the smooth native morphology of unencapsulated controls (Fig. 1B). EGCG@L exhibited a pale gray halo, confirming Ca2+-mediated MPN coating adsorption onto the bacterial surface (Liu et al., 2021). Following secondary encapsulation with BSA or GBSA, a translucent membrane appeared around the cells, suggesting the formation of a protein-polyphenol composite network. Notably, the edges of the GBSA-EGCG@L coating appeared fluffy and flocculent, indicating that glycosylation modification significantly increased the coating's spatial extensibility (Duan et al., 2024).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further revealed that cells in the EGCG@L group were well dispersed with a slightly rough surface. In contrast, severe aggregation occurred in the BSA-EGCG@L group, while the aggregation level was significantly improved in the GBSA-EGCG@L group (Fig. 1C). This difference may be primarily attributed to the physicochemical effects of the introduced mannose chains, which increase surface hydration and steric hindrance and thereby reduce intercellular hydrophobic attraction and hydrogen bonding (Li et al., 2021).

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) results complemented those from TEM. The average heights of L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L, and GBSA-EGCG@L were 437.93 nm, 474.38 nm, 486.01 nm, and 517.83 nm, respectively, significantly higher than that of unencapsulated bacteria (Fig. 1D). Combined TEM, SEM, and AFM results demonstrate that the nanocoating achieves surface functionalization while preserving the fundamental morphology of the cells.

Particle size and zeta potential analyses corroborated the effective coating of probiotics (Fig. 1E and F). The average particle size of native L. plantarum B2 was 1184.67 nm, with a zeta potential of −24.27 mV. EGCG@L exhibited an increased particle size of 1305.33 nm, but the zeta potential decreased to −12.83 mV, indicating that EGCG adsorption increased the hydration layer thickness while partially shielding the surface negative charge (Zhang et al., 2025). BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L showed further increases in particle size to 1369.67 nm and 1404.00 nm, with zeta potentials restored to −25.17 mV and −27.60 mV, respectively. This restoration is attributed to the negative contribution from BSA/GBSA (Ma et al., 2023). Notably, the more negative zeta potential of GBSA-EGCG@L (−27.60 mV) compared to BSA-EGCG@L (−25.17 mV) suggests enhanced electrostatic repulsion between particles, which, together with the steric hindrance provided by surface-grafted mannose moieties, favors colloidal stability and mitigates aggregation during storage. These morphology and surface-charge results support a physicochemical mechanism for the improved dispersion of GBSA-EGCG@L that is independent of receptor-mediated recognition. This is consistent with the sustained viability observed for GBSA-EGCG@L throughout the 21-day storage period (Fig. 4B and C).

Survival throughout the coating process

During probiotic encapsulation, shear stress, centrifugal acceleration, and the chemical microenvironment of the encapsulation material may induce cell membrane damage and metabolic inhibition, leading to significant activity decline (Han et al., 2024). This study systematically evaluated the impact of layer-by-layer self-assembly encapsulation on the viability of L. plantarum B2 using a dual-fluorescence live/dead staining assay combined with LSM and plate counting. Comparable live/dead ratios across all coated groups and the free strain, as visualized by LSM (Fig. 3A), attributing to robust viability and negligible encapsulation-induced injury. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3B) showed that EGCG@L exhibited a survival rate of 92.90%, which was not significantly different from the control group (96.56%) (p > 0.05), validating the mild nature of the EGCG/Ca2+ coating process. BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L showed slightly decreased survival rates of 89.39% and 89.23%, respectively, compared to EGCG@L, but still maintained high viability. This reduction may be attributed to the increased viscosity of the BSA/GBSA solution, leading to greater mechanical shear stress during centrifugation (Pinto et al., 2022). The multiple wash-centrifugation cycles required for secondary encapsulation may also contribute to partial cell loss (Zhu et al., 2023, 2025). Notably, no significant difference was observed between the BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L groups (p > 0.05), indicating that mannose glycosylation modification did not introduce additional cytotoxicity. GBSA, as a food-grade modified protein, exhibits good biocompatibility (Gao et al., 2022). These results demonstrate that the nanoencapsulation process is a mild, biocompatible procedure capable of effectively preserving probiotic viability.

Long-term storage viability

Probiotics exhibit significant sensitivity to ambient stressors (oxygen, moisture, pH, temperature), leading to cell inactivation and limiting their widespread application (Zhu et al., 2025). The final viable cell count in functional probiotic foods must exceed 106 CFU/mL (Zhu et al., 2023). Fig. 4B - C illustrated the relationship between probiotic survival and storage duration at 4 and 25 °C for free versus nano-encapsulated probiotics, respectively. Nano-coated groups exhibited superior survival relative to uncoated cells at both temperatures. Specifically, unencapsulated populations dropped from 8.19 to 4.75 log CFU/mL following 21-day refrigeration (4 °C). In contrast, EGCG@L maintained higher viability at 5.79 log CFU/mL, while BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L achieved approximately 6.0 log CFU/mL. At 25 °C, viable cell counts in the free, EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L, and GBSA-EGCG@L groups decreased to 2.17, 3.63, 3.92, and 3.91 log CFU/mL, respectively. LSM images (Fig. 4A) correlated with plate counting results: after 21 days at both temperatures, free samples exhibited predominantly red fluorescent (dead) cells, whereas the three encapsulated groups remained primarily green fluorescent (viable). These findings demonstrate that both single-layer (EGCG@L) and double-layer (BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L) nanocoatings significantly enhance the storage viability of L. plantarum B2 relative to unencapsulated cells at both temperatures. Unlike conventional alginate or chitosan-coated alginate microcapsules that mainly provide bulk hydrogel protection, the GBSA-EGCG coating retains a compact single-cell architecture and reduces the diffusion limitation associated with micron-sized matrices (Chávarri et al., 2010; Ji et al., 2019). Notably, at 4 °C, all encapsulated groups maintained viability above the 106 CFU/mL threshold for functional probiotic products, underscoring their practical utility under refrigerated storage. However, at 25 °C, although nanocoatings provided substantial relative protection (67 – 81% higher viability than free cells), the absolute viable counts fell below this threshold, indicating limited applicability for room-temperature storage without additional stabilization strategies. The similar viable counts of BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L indicate that the principal storage-related benefit of GBSA lies in improved dispersion stability rather than a further increase in cell survival. This improvement is more directly explained by increased surface hydration, steric stabilization, and altered surface charge than by mannose receptor-mediated recognition.

H2O2 tolerance

Riboflavin, as a cofactor for multiple redox enzymes, participates in various redox reactions. Its deficiency reduces host antioxidant capacity, exacerbates oxidative stress, and elevates local ROS levels (Darenskaya et al., 2025). In vitro studies indicate that excessive ROS (e.g., •O2−, •OH, and H2O2) not only damages intestinal epithelial cells but also induces probiotic death through oxidation of membrane proteins and lipids, leading to cellular content leakage (Zhang et al., 2025). Therefore, the impact of ROS must be considered when utilizing high-riboflavin-producing lactic acid bacteria for riboflavin deficiency treatment. This study determined the survival rate of coated L. plantarum B2 after H2O2 treatment using LSM, plate counting, and intracellular ROS quantification (DCFH-DA assay), with results shown in Fig. 4D – E and Table S3. Prior to treatment, minimal red fluorescent (dead) cells were observed across all groups. After 1 h of exposure to 0.5 mM H2O2, the control group exhibited extensive red fluorescence, whereas encapsulated groups showed predominantly green fluorescence, demonstrating the protective effect of nanocoatings against ROS damage. Plate counting corroborated these findings: after 2 h of H2O2 treatment, viable counts in the control group decreased from 8.26 to 4.72 log CFU/mL, whereas EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L, and GBSA-EGCG@L maintained significantly higher viability at 7.62, 7.68, and 7.85 log CFU/mL, respectively. This protective effect can be attributed to EGCG's free radical scavenging capacity, the physical barrier and reducing effects of the BSA/GBSA layer, and GBSA's enhanced ROS quenching and mucosal retention capabilities conferred by its mannose chains (Tang et al., 2021; Oettl and Marsche, 2010; Duan et al., 2025; Salman et al., 2006).

DCFH-DA intracellular ROS detection provides quantitative supporting evidence that the nanocoatings reduced DCF-detectable intracellular oxidative burden under H2O2 challenge (Table S3). After 2 h of H2O2 challenge, free L. plantarum B2 exhibited intense fluorescence (32,300 ± 2014 RFU), indicating severe intracellular ROS accumulation. In contrast, all encapsulated groups demonstrated significantly attenuated ROS levels (p < 0.01), with ROS scavenging efficacy following the order: GBSA-EGCG@L (67.27 ± 3.60%) > BSA-EGCG@L (62.98 ± 4.35%) > EGCG@L (53.25 ± 5.30%). Compared with previously reported MPN coatings mainly designed to resist oxidative or environmental stress, GBSA-EGCG@L further integrates the EGCG antioxidant interface, the protein barrier, and mannose-associated surface hydration (Wasuwanich et al., 2022; Gao et al., 2025). These data confirm that nanocoatings protect probiotics by reducing intracellular ROS accumulation through the combined antioxidant activity of EGCG and the barrier effects of the protein-containing coating. Although GBSA-EGCG@L showed the lowest numerical DCF fluorescence, it did not differ significantly from BSA-EGCG@L; therefore, the specific contribution of mannose glycosylation to ROS reduction requires further investigation.

Survival throughout simulated digestion

After oral administration, probiotics must traverse multiple barriers - including gastric acid and bile - to colonize and exert their effects, yet they remain highly vulnerable to these extreme environments (Valle-Vargas et al., 2025). Metal ion-mediated coatings, including dual-polysaccharide systems, have been shown to enhance microenvironmental homeostasis and protect probiotics during gastrointestinal transit (Si et al., 2026), consistent with our observation that EGCG/Ca2+ MPN coatings significantly improve survival throughout simulated digestion. Strong acids (pH 1.5 - 3.0) disrupt membrane potential and inhibit transmembrane transport; bile salts damage cell walls, eliciting cytoplasmic leakage and eventual bacterial lethality (Zhu et al., 2025). To simulate human digestion, continuous digestion with simulated gastrointestinal fluids was performed. Viability assessment of L. plantarum B2 across digestion stages employed LSM (live/dead kit) (Fig. 5A). Prior to digestion, nearly all cells in each group appeared green (live). Post-oral incubation (5 min), viability remained comparable across all samples. Conversely, gastric challenge triggered a pronounced accumulation of dead (red-stained) cells among uncoated controls. Intestinal exposure subsequently rendered free bacteria near-completely non-viable. Throughout gastrointestinal transit, substantial numbers of green (live) bacteria persisted in other groups, confirming the strong protective effect of the nanocoating on probiotics. Plate counts were performed to verify LSM results (Fig. 5B). Oral digestive fluids had negligible impact on probiotic survival rates. Post-gastric challenge (1 h), uncoated populations dropped to 4.18 log CFU/mL, while EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L maintained 7.75, 7.95 and 7.98 log CFU/mL, respectively. Intestinal exposure (2 h) further reduced free bacteria to 3.66 log CFU/mL, whereas EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L remained at 7.02, 7.21 and 7.35 log CFU/mL, respectively. Whereas alginate and chitosan systems mainly protect probiotics through passive hydrogel shielding and require subsequent cell release, GBSA-EGCG@L directly coats individual cells and simultaneously provides gastrointestinal protection and antioxidant functionality (Abbaszadeh et al., 2014; Ji et al., 2019). These results indicate that BSA/GBSA-EGCG nanocoatings effectively enhance probiotic resistance to digestion, improving the survival rate of L. plantarum B2 during simulated digestive processes.

Adhesion in vitro and in vivo

The survival rate of probiotics in the gastrointestinal tract, their intestinal adhesion capacity, and controlled-release characteristics are key determinants of colonization efficiency and functional performance (Guo et al., 2024). Metal ion-mediated dual-polysaccharide coatings have been reported to enhance adhesion capacity of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum to intestinal epithelium (Si et al., 2026), corroborating our finding that the EGCG/Ca2+/GBSA coating system significantly improves both in vitro Caco-2 adhesion and in vivo intestinal retention. We therefore evaluated the adhesion of coated L. plantarum B2 to Caco-2 cells and its subsequent retention in vivo (Zhu et al., 2023, 2025). mCherry-labeled nano-encapsulated L. plantarum B2 was incubated with Hoechst-labeled Caco-2 cells; after gentle washing, adhesion was imaged by LSM (Fig. 6). Only scattered bacteria were visible in the control, whereas EGCG@L showed a modest increase and both BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L exhibited significantly higher adhesion, demonstrating that the secondary protein layer enhances association with the enterocyte monolayer and may improve mucosal attachment in vivo. Unlike chitosan coatings that mainly rely on nonspecific electrostatic mucoadhesion and alginate coatings lacking specific recognition motifs, GBSA introduces surface-exposed mannose residues that provide additional biointerfacial functionality (Chávarri et al., 2010; Ji et al., 2019).

Adhesion of free and nano-coated L. plantarum B2 to Caco-2 cell monolayers. LSM micrographs showing mCherry-labeled L. plantarum B2 (red channel) adhered to Hoechst 33342-stained Caco-2 cells (blue channel).

Enhanced in vitro adhesion prompted an in vivo retention study. mCherry-labeled formulations were gavage fed to 2-month-old BALB/c mice, and whole-body fluorescence was tracked by an in vivo imaging system (Fig. 7B). Signals in the free L. plantarum B2 group declined rapidly and were almost undetectable by 48 h. In contrast, GBSA-EGCG@L displayed the slowest decay, maintaining significantly higher fluorescence than L. plantarum B2, EGCG@L and BSA-EGCG@L at all time points (Fig. 7A). Because BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L shared the same EGCG/Ca2+ inner layer, the enhanced retention of GBSA-EGCG@L suggests that mannose glycosylation provides additional adhesion-related benefits beyond the stabilizing effect of native BSA. However, the prolonged fluorescence signal should be interpreted as enhanced intestinal retention rather than definitive evidence of viable bacterial colonization. The detected signal may originate from viable bacteria, non-viable bacteria, bacterial fragments, or labeled material that had not yet been excreted.

In vivo viability of free and nano-coated L. plantarum B2 in BALB/c mice. (A) Fluorescence images at different time points post-administration. (B) Ex vivo fluorescence images of the gastrointestinal tract at 48 h.

Ampicillin was required in this fluorescence-tracing model to maintain selective pressure for the mCherry-expression plasmid and improve labeling stability. However, antibiotic exposure may alter the indigenous gut microbiota, reduce colonization competition, and modify the intestinal mucus environment, thereby influencing the apparent retention of the administered bacteria. Because all groups received the same ampicillin treatment, the results support relative comparisons among formulations, but may not fully represent intestinal retention under an intact microbiota.

In the present study, “enhanced intestinal interaction” refers specifically to the increased Caco-2 adhesion and prolonged intestinal fluorescence retention observed after mannose modification. Two non-exclusive mechanisms may account for these improvements. First, the introduced mannose chains may increase surface hydrophilicity and steric hindrance, alter surface charge, and improve dispersion, thereby enhancing nonspecific interactions with the intestinal mucosal interface. This physicochemical mechanism is consistent with the enhanced adhesion observed in Caco-2 cells, as these cells do not express the mannose receptor CD206 under standard culture conditions. Second, in the complex in vivo environment, mannose residues may additionally interact with CD206-expressing M cells or macrophages in Peyer's patches, potentially contributing to the prolonged retention observed in vivo (Tao et al., 2026; Higuchi et al., 2004). However, this receptor-mediated mechanism remains unconfirmed because free-mannose competition, receptor-blocking, and M-cell model experiments were not performed. Future studies comparing mannose-, glucose-, and galactose-glycosylated BSA coatings, together with free-mannose competition, receptor-blocking, and M-cell model experiments, will help distinguish general sugar-chain-induced physicochemical effects from mannose-specific receptor recognition. Thus, the GBSA-EGCG nanocoating enhances adhesion to the intestinal mucosal interface and prolongs intestinal fluorescence retention; however, the present imaging results alone do not establish persistent colonization by viable bacteria. The phenolic hydroxyl groups of the EGCG/Ca2+ network form strong non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces) with mucins, while mannose residues on GBSA may enhance surface hydration and mucosal interactions and potentially contribute to receptor-related recognition, collectively extending the intestinal fluorescence retention of the administered formulation (Tao et al., 2026; Wang et al., 2025). Future studies should combine viable bacterial counts from different intestinal segments, strain-specific qPCR, dynamic fecal shedding analysis, and bacterial recovery from washed intestinal tissues to determine whether prolonged fluorescence retention translates into sustained colonization by viable L. plantarum B2. These experiments should preferably be performed without antibiotic treatment, or in microbiota-controlled models, to verify the retention and colonization capacity under a physiologically representative intestinal environment.

Physiological activity

An ideal nanocoating must preserve the intrinsic proliferation and metabolic activity of probiotics while ensuring high gastrointestinal survival (Wu and Liu, 2022). As shown in Fig. 8A, the growth curves of BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L almost overlapped that of free L. plantarum B2, whereas EGCG@L exhibited a slight but transient lag, attributed to the rigid single-layer barrier that transiently restricts nutrient exchange (Gao et al., 2025). Notably, by 24 h, all groups reached comparable OD600 values, indicating that the delay in proliferation was temporary and did not compromise final growth yield. After 24 h fermentation, the pH of MRS and soymilk decreased from 6.47 to 7.26 to 4.40 and 4.73, respectively, for the control; nano-encapsulated cells produced an identical pH in MRS and only a marginal delay in soymilk (Fig. 8B and C). Riboflavin production in soymilk is illustrated in Fig. 8D: initial concentration 0.12 μg/mL increased to 1.52 μg/mL in the free-cell control, while EGCG@L yielded 1.13 μg/mL (p < 0.05) and BSA/GBSA-EGCG@L reached 1.34 μg/mL, values not significantly different from the control, indicating that the double-layer coating largely preserved the riboflavin-producing capacity of L. plantarum B2 under the tested fermentation conditions. However, this result reflects preservation of bacterial metabolic potential and does not directly demonstrate improvement or treatment of host riboflavin deficiency, which would require validation in an appropriate nutritional-deficiency model. As shown in Fig. 8E, fresh soymilk contained 69.26 μg/mL glycosides and 6.99 μg/mL aglycones; after 8 h the control contained 29.05 μg/mL glycosides and 41.72 μg/mL aglycones, whereas EGCG@L, BSA-EGCG@L and GBSA-EGCG@L reduced glycosides to 34.13, 31.79 and 29.16 μg/mL and elevated aglycones to 39.33, 40.99 and 42.15 μg/mL, respectively. Compared with dense alginate or chitosan hydrogel matrices that may hinder nutrient and metabolite diffusion, the GBSA-EGCG coating preserved bacterial growth, riboflavin production, and isoflavone conversion, indicating a better balance between barrier protection and metabolic permeability. The physiological activity of EGCG@L was slightly lower, possibly due to the isolation of probiotics from external environmental nutrients, which slowed down their growth rate (Yun et al., 2019). BSA/GBSA-EGCG double-layer nanocoatings protected the cells during digestion and oxidative stress while largely preserving the physiological activity of L. plantarum B2, including its riboflavin-producing potential. This advantage may be associated with the antioxidant capacity and nutrient permeability of the outer protein layer (Pinto et al., 2022; Zhu et al., 2022).

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that mannose-glycosylated bovine serum albumin (GBSA), prepared in choline chloride/mannose NADES with a grafting degree of 91.07%, served as a functional outer layer that improved coating stability and intestinal interaction. The combination of GBSA with EGCG/Ca2+ metal-phenolic networks (MPNs) enabled successful layer-by-layer nanocoating of Lactobacillus plantarum B2, achieving efficient probiotic encapsulation and improved storage stability. The nanocoated probiotics exhibited enhanced tolerance to simulated gastrointestinal fluids and reactive oxygen species, addressing critical challenges in oral probiotic delivery. Notably, the GBSA nanocoating significantly improved intestinal adhesion to Caco-2 cells and prolonged intestinal fluorescence retention in vivo. Furthermore, the encapsulation approach preserved the viability and riboflavin-producing capacity of L. plantarum B2, supporting its potential as a delivery system for riboflavin-producing probiotics. Compared with the EGCG/Ca2+ single coating and native BSA-containing coating, GBSA-EGCG@L showed improved dispersion, adhesion, and intestinal retention. These advantages may primarily arise from mannose-induced changes in surface hydration, steric hindrance, surface charge. The contribution of mannose-specific receptor-mediated recognition (e.g., via CD206 on M cells or macrophages) remains to be verified in appropriate cell models, as the Caco-2 cells used in this study do not express CD206. Its main advantage over conventional alginate, chitosan, or native BSA single-coating systems lies in integrating physical protection, antioxidant activity, colloidal stabilization, and intestinal interaction within a compact single-cell coating. In summary, this single-cell encapsulation technology, integrating GBSA-mediated enhancement of intestinal adhesion and retention with MPN-based protection, offers a promising platform for functional foods and the delivery of riboflavin-producing probiotics, with potential application in dietary riboflavin supplementation.
