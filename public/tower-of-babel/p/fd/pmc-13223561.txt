An innovative concept of using community information for surveys and developing a map of Simulium breeding sites
Authors: Daniel A Boakye, Kareen Atekem, Franklin Ayisi, Olabanji Surakat, Adoum M Oumar, Lassane Koala, Arwa Elaagip, Dziedzom K de Souza, Jamie Tallant, Sashi Leff, Eve Gatawa, Anne Heggen, Charles D Mackenzie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHaving a Simulium vector distribution map is important for all entomological and epidemiological aspects for the elimination of onchocerciasis. Developing these maps using topographic maps has some limitations such as identifying the exact locations of rapids, especially with low-scale maps. This leads to breeding sites being missed during prospecting. Recent efforts to map breeding sites involve the development of technologies such as satellite images, remote sensing modelling and drones. The limitations of these technologies include cost, security concerns and tree cover preventing the observation of rapids from the sky. Although community support has traditionally been used to guide entomological teams during prospecting for breeding sites, community knowledge has not been an integral part in planning breeding site surveys.MethodsThis article proposes a concept of using community knowledge and health system structure for planning and implementation of breeding sites surveys. The approach uses questionnaires to gather information on fast-flowing stretches of rivers within communities. Using this information, a breeding site survey plan is developed at the national level and reviewed and implemented at the district and subdistrict levels using discussions, photos and videos of fast flowing rivers, as well as topographic maps. A working timetable is then developed that indicates areas to visit each day, maximizing time in the field and other logistics.ConclusionsUsing health system structures and community members not only provides a rapid approach for entomological surveys but also strengthens entomological fieldwork and fosters community engagement in vector surveillance.Keywords: breeding site surveys, community knowledge, health system, onchocerciasis

Introduction

Species of the genus Simulium are vectors of the filarial nematode Onchocerca volvulus, which causes human onchocerciasis. While there are various species of Simulium involved in the transmission of human onchocerciasis, in Africa, the major vectors are sibling species of the Simulium damnosum complex and members of the Simulium neavei group. The S. damnosum complex is the most widely distributed, being found in all endemic countries, while the members of the S. neavei group are found in East, Central and southern Africa, mostly in isolated foci. Members of the S. damnosum complex breed in fast-flowing sections of rivers of various sizes and vegetation cover from forest to savanna zones. Developing stages of the S. neavei group are found on crabs in a phoretic association in smaller fast-flowing forested rivers.

The transmission and distribution of human onchocerciasis are intrinsically linked to the distribution of the vector Simulium species. Thus, areas of highest risk of transmission and infection with the parasite O. volvulus are found closest to breeding sites of the vector.1 Because of this, knowledge of the distribution of breeding sites is fundamental to any onchocerciasis control/elimination activity. This includes mapping, delimiting communities that are endemic or at risk of infection for onchocerciasis, monitoring and evaluation of elimination interventions, impact and stopping mass drug administration assessments and declaring elimination of transmission.

Mapping the distribution of vector breeding sites is not easily done, as it necessitates looking for fast-flowing stretches of rivers and prospecting for aquatic stages of the vectors. This demands a good understanding and use of topographic maps, identifying river sections with rapids and waterfalls and man-made structures such as bridges and dams that lead to the creation of rapids. Unfortunately, not all of these are easily determined on topographic maps. Furthermore, the physical search for breeding sites is hampered by challenges of knowing exactly where the fast-flowing stretches are located on rivers even when they have been identified on the maps. The consequences of this are that the majority of the breeding sites are missed and many times mostly only those close to roads are identified.

Due to the importance of knowing where breeding sites are located for onchocerciasis elimination and the challenges associated with the use of topographic maps to search for them (breeding sites), various methodologies including the use of satellite images, remote sensing modelling2 and drone technology have been or are being developed.3 While each of these provides information that helps in determining where to visit and search for aquatic stages of vectors, there are limitations on their use. Important among these are tree cover preventing the observation of rapids from the air, cost associated with obtaining satellite images, security concerns, especially in the use of drones, and the identification of artificial breeding sites.

Generally, in vector-borne diseases research and control, most entomological field activities are performed by skilled technicians from research institutions or control programs. Thus entomological tasks have remained on the fringes of public health. Recently, this has begun to change, by incorporating community collaboration for vector control activities. For example, in Cuba, areas that reported greater improvements in entomological indices in the control of dengue had integrated community working groups into the mainstream health system.4 Strengthening community participation and networks improved malaria control, which subsequently led to decreased malaria incidence in some areas of Columbia.5 In Africa, it has been shown that communities can be used in various malaria control activities, including larval control,6 adult vector collections7 and environmental modification.8

An aspect of community involvement that has been used during prospecting for breeding sites is community support to locate where fast-flowing stretches are located and dangers such as the presence of hippopotami or crocodiles in different rivers. Unfortunately, community knowledge has not been harnessed in planning for breeding site surveys. We present a planning tool developed to complement the use of topographic maps for rapid determination of breeding sites. This involves using the country’s Ministry of Health (MoH) system to obtain information from communities on where to find rapids on rivers, the presence of biting blackflies and the period of high biting.

Information flow through the MoH structure

In most countries, the MoH is structured from a national to a community level (Figure 1). The neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) program is located at the national level within which is the onchocerciasis unit. Depending on the country, the NTD program has focal persons at each level of the health system. These focal persons could be disease control officers, environmental health officers, community health officers etc. For the implementation of NTD activities, information flows from the NTD program through the various structures of the MoH to the community level. The reverse happens when reports or other information have to move from the community to the NTD program at the national level. This structure and information flow can be harnessed for rapid ground truthing for Simulium breeding sites.

Current approach for breeding site surveys: topographic maps

The use of detailed topographic maps has been fundamental in planning for breeding site surveys. Usually a map with a scale of 1:50 000 is needed. Rivers, rapids on rivers, marshy areas, roads, bridges etc. are identified on these maps. Once the rivers are identified, the team visits the communities around the rivers and a community guide is selected to accompany the team.

Proposed approach: questionnaire to gather information from the community

In this approach, a simple open-ended questionnaire is developed at the national level, pretested in selected communities and sent through the health system beginning with the regional, district and health centre to the community level to gather information on rivers from the communities. Officers from both the district and health centre levels, especially the community health officers in charge of the catchment area, oversee distribution of the questionnaires and collection of the information at the community level. Information is gathered from all communities in the survey area. The questionnaire must be sent to the communities in enough time before the fieldwork is undertaken.

Using the information from the questionnaires for planning breeding site surveys

With the information gathered from the communities, a list of rivers within the different communities to be prospected for aquatic stages of vector S. damnosum s.l. or S. neavei is compiled by the NTD focal person at each district health facility/system and sent to the NTD program. Based on the information provided, a breeding site survey plan is developed. This includes which areas to visit, the number of teams to be formed, the number of days needed, logistics and budget.

Implementation of the approach

During the field implementation, a review of the list of rivers from the communities is done at the district or subdistrict level through discussions, the use of sample photos and videos of fast-flowing rivers and topographic maps. A working timetable is then developed that indicates areas to visit each day to maximize time in the field, and other logistic aspects. Similar to the traditional method, community members should still be selected as guides and to corroborate the information received from the questionnaires.

Conclusions

Mapping Simulium vector breeding sites is essential for the successful elimination of onchocerciasis. Our proposed approach—integrating community knowledge into the planning and execution of breeding site surveys—offers a cost-effective and practical solution to enhance survey accuracy and efficiency. By leveraging existing health system structures and community members to understand the situation on the ground, this method strengthens entomological fieldwork and fosters community engagement in vector surveillance.

The incorporation of community knowledge in breeding site survey planning not only improves the ability to identify breeding sites but also improves field coordination and resource allocation. As onchocerciasis elimination programs begin monitoring and evaluation of interventions, incorporating community-driven data collection with vector surveillance can contribute significantly to more effective monitoring and evaluation strategies. Future studies should evaluate the impact of this approach on survey efficiency and its potential scalability across different settings.
