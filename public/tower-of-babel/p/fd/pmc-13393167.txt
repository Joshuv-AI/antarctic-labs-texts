Sulfated galactans from Gracilaria corticata reprogram metabolism, activate Immunity, promote regeneration, and exhibit anti-melanoma activity
Authors: Amal D Premarathna, Katarzyna M Dziubinska-Kuehn, Anti Sooäär, Nambukara Palliyaguruge Pushpitha, Clarisa Naa Shormeh Darko, Renu Geetha Bai, Indrek Reile, Rando Tuvikene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySulfated polysaccharides from marine algae hold therapeutic potential, but their mechanisms remain underexplored. This study investigates how distinct sulfated galactans from the red seaweed Gracilaria corticata reprogram cellular metabolism to drive specific biological responses. Six fractions (GC-1A/B, GC-2A/B, GC-3A/B) obtained by sequential cold and hot extraction were structurally characterized using NMR and FTIR. Cold-extracted fractions (GC-1A/B) enhanced macrophage phagocytosis and nitric oxide production, indicating immunostimulation. Hot-extracted fractions (GC-2A/3A) promoted human skin cell proliferation and migration while inhibiting B16-F10 melanoma cell growth. Targeted metabolomics revealed that these effects arise from cell-type-specific metabolic reprogramming, including changes in amino acid and lipid profiles. In skin cells, the active fractions also increased antioxidant enzyme activity and modulated wound healing genes. These findings establish sulfated galactans as metabolic modulators whose bioactivity depends on the extraction method. This work provides a framework for developing marine polysaccharides into targeted therapies for immune disorders, tissue regeneration, and cancer.Keywords: Gracilaria corticata, agar-type sulfated galactans, immunomodulation, wound healing, antioxidant activity, melanoma, structure-activity relationship

Introduction

Sulfated polysaccharides from red algae (Rhodophyta) are biopolymers of significant industrial and pharmacological interest. Among these, galactans, such as agar and carrageenan, are distinguished by a linear backbone of alternating 3-linked β-d-galactopyranose and 4-linked α-galactopyranose units.1 Their structural complexity, resulting from 3,6-anhydro bridges and substitutions with sulfate, methyl, or pyruvic acid groups, presents diverse physicochemical and biological properties.2,3 Within this context, the genus Gracilaria is a cornerstone of the global phycocolloid industry, serving as the source for over 65% of the world’s agar production.4 This economic importance is largely due to the decline in wild stocks of other agarophytes such as Gelidium and the genus’s high abundance in temperate and subtropical zones. The functional quality of agar, defined by its gel strength, gelling temperature, and yield, is heavily influenced by the specific pattern of substitutions on its polysaccharide chain, which varies between species and is affected by environmental and extraction factors.5,6

Gracilaria corticata is a widely distributed species, found along the coasts of China, India, Iran, and Sri Lanka.7 It is particularly valued for its high-quality agar and resilience to environmental fluctuations. Structural analyses have revealed that its sulfated galactans feature a complex and specific substitution pattern, including sulfation at positions such as C-4 of the 1,3-linked d-galactose (d-Gal) and C-6 of the 1,4-linked l-galactose units.8,9 Beyond its industrial use, G. corticata is a rich source of bioactive compounds. Sulfated polysaccharides from this and other Gracilaria species have demonstrated potent antioxidant, antimicrobial, immunomodulatory, and anti-inflammatory activities, highlighting their potential as natural alternatives to synthetic drugs and as promising therapeutic agents.10,11

Despite these promising bioactivities, a direct and mechanistic understanding of how specific structural features of G. corticata polysaccharides translate into distinct biological outcomes remains limited. Traditional structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies have primarily focused on physicochemical parameters such as molecular weight (Mw), sulfate content, and monosaccharide composition, typically assessed using techniques such as Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR), nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), and chromatographic analyses.12 While these approaches are irreplaceable for defining polymer architecture, they provide limited insight into how polysaccharides reprogram intracellular metabolism following cell interaction. Recent advances in polysaccharide biology increasingly recognize that bioactivity is not governed solely by receptor binding, but also by downstream metabolic remodeling within target cells, particularly involving amino acid and lipid metabolism.

In this context, mass-spectrometry-based metabolomics has emerged as a powerful complementary approach to conventional structural analyses. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) enables sensitive profiling of intracellular amino acid pools, offering direct insight into immunometabolic activation, proliferative capacity, and redox regulation, processes central to immune responses, tissue regeneration, and cancer metabolism.13 Similarly, gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) based fatty acid profiling provides critical information on membrane remodeling, lipid signaling, and inflammatory mediator synthesis,14 which are increasingly recognized as key determinants of polysaccharide-induced bioactivity. Recent studies have demonstrated that sulfated polysaccharides can selectively reprogram amino acid utilization and lipid composition in immune and cancer cells, thereby shaping functional outcomes such as macrophage activation, wound healing, and selective tumor suppression.15,16 However, such metabolomics-oriented SAR investigations remain scarce for Gracilaria-derived galactans.

Therefore, this study aims to provide a comprehensive and mechanistically informed evaluation of sulfated polysaccharides isolated from G. corticata collected from the Sri Lankan coast. This work represents the first comprehensive study on Gracilaria-derived galactans, integrating extraction-dependent fractionation, detailed structural characterization, functional bioactivity assessment, and targeted LC-MS based amino acid profiling together with GC-MS based fatty acid analysis. To explore potential anticancer properties, the murine melanoma cell line B16-F10 was used as a highly metastatic and immunocompetent model for assessing tumor cell viability, migration, and metabolic vulnerability. In parallel, wound-healing potential was evaluated through proliferation and migration assays in human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) and human keratinocytes (HaCaT), while immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory activities were assessed using murine RAW264.7 macrophages through cytokine expression and functional responses. By integrating structural, biological, and metabolomic data, this work seeks to bridge the critical gap between polysaccharide architecture and cellular function. The findings provide new insight into how extraction-driven structural variation in G. corticata galactans governs amino acid and lipid metabolic reprogramming, ultimately determining their immunomodulatory, regenerative, and selective anticancer effects. Consequently, the presented metabolomics-informed framework highlights the potential of marine sulfated polysaccharides as multifunctional biomaterials for nutraceutical, pharmaceutical, and regenerative medicine applications.

Results

Physicochemical properties of the extracted GC polysaccharide

Extraction yield, molecular weights

The polysaccharide fractions isolated from GC exhibited varying extraction yields and Mw, highlighting the impact of the sequential extraction process. The yields for each fraction were as follows: GC-1A (2.16%), GC-1B (4.94%), GC-2A (2.61%), GC-2B (3.97%), GC-3A (1.25%), and GC-3B (3.97%) (Figure 1A). Analysis of the weight-average Mw revealed a clear distinction between fractions. The initial cold-extracted fractions, GC-1A and GC-1B, had the lowest Mw values of 100 kDa and 900 kDa, respectively. In contrast, the subsequent hot-extracted fractions yielded significantly larger polymers: GC-2A (1180 kDa), GC-2B (1280 kDa), and GC-3B (1160 kDa). Interestingly, fraction GC-3A was an outlier with a relatively low Mw (210 kDa) despite being a hot extract. The latter fractions (GC-2A, GC-2B, GC-3B) are characterized by their high Mws, a key structural property often associated with enhanced bioactivity.

FT-IR

All fractions exhibited a broad, intense absorption band in the range of 3365–3393 cm−1 (see Figure S1), corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations of hydroxyl groups, which is a definitive hallmark of polysaccharide structures.17,18 In the region of 2919–2920 cm−1, weak signals representing the C-H stretching vibrations of methylene groups19 were found in all fractions except GC-2A. The presence of bound water and carbonyl groups was represented by bands in the 1615–1644 cm−1 range, corresponding to O–H stretch of water and/or weak C=O stretching.20 A distinct band at 1541 cm−1, corresponding to N–H bending and C–N stretching vibrations characteristic of the amide II band,21 was observed exclusively in fraction GC-3B, suggesting the presence of residual proteinaceous material in this fraction. A critical diagnostic region for sulfated polysaccharides is between 1275 and 1200 cm−1, where a strong absorption band from 1224 to 1230 cm−1 was consistently present across all fractions. This band is assigned to the asymmetric S=O stretching vibration of sulfate esters (O-SO3-),22 providing direct evidence for the sulfated nature of these galactans. Furthermore, the complex region between 1150 and 1030 cm−1, which includes contributions from C–O–C glycosidic bond stretching and C–O–H vibrations, exhibited variability in peak intensity and shape.23 Fractions GC-1B and GC-2A displayed resolved peaks at 1069 and 1036 cm−1 (Figure 1C), respectively, while others showed broader features in this area.

1H and 13C NMR analysis

NMR spectroscopy provided structural insights into the extracted GC polysaccharides. Both 1H and 13C 1D spectra of fractions GC-2A and GC-3A (Figures 1F and 1H) showed signals characteristic of agar-type galactans, which were identified based on 2D COSY (Figure 1G) and HSQC spectra (Figure 1I; Figure S2) of fractions GC-2A and GC-3A, respectively. Characteristic anomeric resonances appeared at 1H/13C 4.59/104.87 ppm for d-Gal and 5.08/96.89 ppm for 3,6-anhydro-α-l-galactose (LA).24 Furthermore, two additional anomeric peaks at 5.14/98.56 ppm and 5.23/101.09 ppm represent the H1/C1 in LA linked (1→4) to pyruvated d-Gal (LA’) and l-anhydrogalactose-6-sulfate (LA6S), respectively.25 In the case of d-Gal and LA, the correlations between the carbohydrate ring protons were clearly shown in COSY spectra. For d-Gal, the following peaks were assigned: H2/C2 at 3.62/72.04; H4/C4 at 4.12/72.17; H5/C5 at 3.75/77.60; and H6/C6 at 3.78/63.65, and similarly for LA, H2/C2 at 4.11/68.59; H3/C3 at 4.52/81.70; H4/C4 at 4.65/79.20; H5/C5 at 4.56/78.04; and H6/C6 at 4.05/71.80 (Table 1). The H6/C6 assignment was additionally confirmed by HSQC experiments. In both fractions, the peak at 3.42/61.34 ppm was assigned to protons of 6-O-methyl-β-d-Gal (G6M), while the peak at 3.56/65.35 ppm corresponds to H6/C6 in pyruvated d-Gal.26 The latter was present in greater quantity in the GC-2A fraction. The resonance observed at 4.86/76.0 ppm was assigned to the characteristic sulfated H4/C4-SO3 in the 3-linked β-d-galactopyranose fragment of the sulfated agar (agaran) residue. A sharp singlet at 1.45/27.81 ppm was identified as the methyl group of pyruvic acid ketal bound in positions C4 and C6 of d-Gal, a modification common in Gracilaria agars.27

Total sugar, sulfate, protein, phenolic content, and anticoagulant activity

Total sugar content varied substantially across fractions. Fraction GC-2A exhibited the highest mean sugar content (84.65 ± 1.14%), which was significantly greater than that of GC-1A (68.14 ± 0.61%; p < 0.0001) and GC-3A (56.63 ± 0.96%; p < 0.0001). No significant difference was observed between GC-2A and GC-1B (83.48 ± 1.12%; p = 0.7442), indicating similar carbohydrate purity in these two fractions. Sulfate content was also analyzed and was found to differ significantly among the fractions (Figure 1D). GC-1B displayed the highest sulfate level (12.04 ± 0.09%), which was significantly greater than that of GC-2A (9.00 ± 0.18%; p < 0.0001). Conversely, sulfate content in GC-2A was significantly higher than in GC-1A (6.98 ± 0.18%; p < 0.0001) and significantly lower than in GC-3A (11.16 ± 0.09%; p < 0.0001).

Protein content was considered to be relatively low in all fractions. GC-2A (1.76 ± 0.23%) contained significantly more protein than GC-1B (0.96 ± 0.17%; p = 0.0005) and GC-3A (0.76 ± 0.11%; p < 0.0001). The difference between GC-2A and GC-1A (1.41 ± 0.30%) was not statistically significant (p = 0.0915). Total phenolic content (TPC) was lowest in GC-3A (0.01 ± 0.02%) and highest in GC-2A (0.37 ± 0.01%). The TPC in GC-2A was significantly greater than in all other fractions: GC-1A (0.14 ± 0.00%; p < 0.0001), GC-1B (0.09 ± 0.05%; p < 0.0001), and GC-3A (p < 0.0001) (Table S2). Anticoagulant activity did not differ significantly among all fractions (p > 0.05 for all fractions), with mean values ranging from 1.75 to 1.83%. The compositional profiles confirm distinct biochemical characteristics among fractions (Figure 1E).

Evaluation of biological activity in vitro

MTT assay

The cytotoxicity and proliferative effects of GC polysaccharide fractions (GC-1A, GC-1B, GC-2A, GC-3A) were evaluated across four cell lines using the MTT assay. The results revealed distinct, fraction-dependent biological activities. On RAW264.7 macrophages (tested from 0.01 to 0.50 μg/μL), the fractions exhibited varying effects on cytotoxicity and proliferation. In contrast, the fractions were largely non-cytotoxic to HDF, showing no significant reduction in cell viability across a higher concentration range (0.02–1.00 μg/μL). A divergent response was observed in HaCaT keratinocytes (0.01–0.50 μg/μL), where most fractions induced a concentration-dependent cytotoxic effect, with significant reductions in viability, notably at lower concentrations. Conversely, when tested on B16-F10 murine melanoma cells across an extensive concentration range (0.0002–0.50 μg/μL), the fractions displayed distinct anti-proliferative potential (reduced cell viability), with the GC-3A fraction in particular demonstrating significant anti-cancer activity.

RAW264.7 macrophage cell viability

GC-1A exhibited a significant proliferative effect at the highest concentration tested (0.50 μg/μL), increasing RAW264.7 macrophage cell viability to 120.40 ± 6.38% compared to the untreated control (NT = 100%, p = 0.0128). No significant cytotoxicity was observed at any other concentration (0.01–0.25 μg/μL, p > 0.05), indicating a safe profile at lower and mid-range doses. GC-1B also demonstrated a strong and significant proliferative response at 0.50 μg/μL, with viability reaching 133.10 ± 8.66% (p = 0.0094). Similar to GC-1A, all other concentrations showed no significant difference from the control (p > 0.05), confirming a non-cytotoxic profile across the dilution series. In contrast, GC-2A induced a powerful and highly significant (p < 0.0001) proliferative response across its entire tested concentration range (0.01–0.50 μg/μL). Viability values were substantially elevated, ranging from 150.90 ± 2.67% at 0.03 μg/μL to 168.0 ± 7.36% at 0.50 μg/μL. GC-3A similarly promoted highly significant (p < 0.0001) macrophage proliferation at all concentrations.

The cell viability effects were dose-dependent, most pronounced at higher concentrations (169.20 ± 6.07% at 0.50 μg/μL and 152.8 ± 6.82% at 0.25 μg/μL), but remaining significant even at the lowest tested dose of 0.01 μg/μL (135.1 ± 5.28%, p = 0.0009). All four tested fractions (GC-1A, GC-1B, GC-2A, GC-3A) are non-cytotoxic to RAW264.7 macrophages across a wide dose range (Figure 2A). Notably, fractions GC-2A and GC-3A function as potent mitogens, stimulating significant dose-dependent macrophage proliferation at every concentration tested.

Human dermal fibroblast cell viability

GC-1A demonstrated no significant effects on HDF cell viability at any tested concentration (p > 0.05). Mean viability values ranged from 94.70 ± 3.10% at 0.02 μg/μL to 99.62 ± 3.51% at 0.13 μg/μL, indicating a neutral to slightly supportive effect on cell metabolism compared to the untreated control (NT = 100%). Similarly, GC-1B showed no significant cytotoxicity across the entire dose range (p > 0.05). Viability values were consistent, ranging from 93.20 ± 3.05% at 0.50 μg/μL to 97.55 ± 3.46% at 0.06 μg/μL, confirming a safe and non-detrimental profile for HDFs. GC-2A also exhibited an excellent safety profile, with no significant reduction in cell viability at any concentration (p > 0.05). Notably, this fraction showed a trend toward enhancing cell metabolism, with mean viability values reaching 101.7 ± 4.69% at both 0.13 and 0.03 μg/μL. In contrast, GC-3A was the only fraction to show a statistically significant effect, inducing a mild but significant reduction in viability to 87.98 ± 3.06% specifically at the 0.50 μg/μL concentration (representing a ∼13% decrease compared to NT (100%), p = 0.0309). Cell viability remained over 85%, a level generally not considered severely cytotoxic.28 All other concentrations for GC-3A showed no significant difference from the control (p > 0.05). All four fractions (GC-1A, GC-1B, GC-2A, GC-3A) are completely non-cytotoxic to HDF across a wide range of concentrations (0.02–1.00 μg/μL) (Figure 2F).

HaCaT keratinocyte cell viability

GC-1A exhibited a surprising inverse concentration-effect relationship on HaCaT keratinocyte cells. While the highest concentration (0.50 μg/μL) showed no significant cytotoxicity (110.20 ± 5.64%, p > 0.05), significant cytotoxicity emerged at lower doses. Viability was significantly reduced to 79.79 ± 4.51% at 0.02 μg/μL (p = 0.0088) and 78.61 ± 3.93% at 0.01 μg/μL (p = 0.0053), indicating that lower concentrations of this fraction were more detrimental to keratinocyte metabolism. GC-1B demonstrated a similar pattern of increased cytotoxicity at lower concentrations. While the fraction was well-tolerated at 0.50 μg/μL (98.75 ± 2.80%, p > 0.05), significant cytotoxicity was observed at concentrations of 0.06 μg/μL and below. The most pronounced effects were seen at the lowest concentrations, with viability reduced to 78.19 ± 1.50% at 0.02 μg/μL (p = 0.0002) and 77.61 ± 1.14% at 0.01 μg/μL (p = 0.0002). GC-2A presented a safer profile overall. A mild but significant reduction in viability was observed only at 0.03 μg/μL (86.98 ± 4.41%; p = 0.0369). At all other concentrations tested, including both higher and lower doses, no significant cytotoxicity was detected, with viability at the highest dose (0.50 μg/μL) remaining at 103.6 ± 1.91% (p > 0.05).

GC-3A demonstrated the most consistently cytotoxic profile among the fractions tested. Higher reductions in cell viability were observed at multiple concentrations: 0.50 μg/μL (78.56 ± 4.65%, p = 0.0393), 0.06 μg/μL (74.85 ± 2.67%, p = 0.0118), and 0.02 μg/μL (73.82 ± 2.36%, p = 0.0084) (see Table 2). This suggests a broader cytotoxic effect across the concentration range for this fraction (see Figure 3A).

B16-F10 murine melanoma cell viability

GC-1A exhibited no cytotoxic or anti-proliferative effects on B16-F10 cells at any tested concentration (p > 0.05 for all comparisons). Viability values consistently remained near or above the untreated control (NT = 100%), ranging from 96.59 ± 2.21% at 0.02 μg/μL to 105.6 ± 1.33% at 0.50 μg/μL. This suggests that GC-1A is non-toxic to this cancer cell line and does not inhibit its metabolic activity. GC-1B showed a mild but significant anti-proliferative effect at specific mid-range concentrations. A significant reduction in viability was observed at 0.03 μg/μL (91.96 ± 2.38%, p = 0.0027) and 0.02 μg/μL (92.34 ± 2.16%, p = 0.0047). However, this effect was absent at both higher and lower concentrations, indicating a narrow window of activity for this fraction. GC-2A demonstrated the most potent and consistent anti-proliferative effects against B16-F10 cells. Significant cytotoxicity was observed across multiple concentrations, with the strongest effects seen at 0.02 μg/μL (88.74 ± 1.32%, p < 0.0001), 0.03 μg/μL (91.10 ± 0.82%, p < 0.0001), and 0.50 μg/μL (110.2 ± 1.12%, p < 0.0001).

Interestingly, the highest concentration showed a stimulatory effect, while lower doses were inhibitory, revealing a biphasic or inverse dose-response relationship. GC-3A displayed the most pronounced and broad-spectrum anti-cancer activity. Highly significant cytotoxicity was observed across five concentrations: 0.13 μg/μL (80.70 ± 4.05%, p = 0.0238), 0.06 μg/μL (78.31 ± 4.99%, p = 0.0081), 0.03 μg/μL (78.59 ± 5.85%, p = 0.0092), and most notably at 0.02 μg/μL (72.94 ± 5.88%, p = 0.0005). This represents a greater than 25% reduction in cell viability at the most effective dose (Figure 4A).

Cell proliferation

RAW264.7 macrophage cell proliferation

The alamarBlue assay revealed time-dependent effects of GC fractions on RAW264.7 macrophage proliferation at 0.5 μg/μL. At 24 h, hot-extracted fractions GC-2A (168.0 ± 7.36%, p < 0.0001) and GC-3A (169.20 ± 6.07%, p < 0.0001) showed highly significant proliferative effects compared to NT (100%). Cold-extracted GC-1B (133.10 ± 8.66%, p = 0.0020) also significantly enhanced proliferation, while GC-1A (120.4 ± 6.38%) showed a non-significant increase (p = 0.0888). By 48 h, all fractions exhibited significant differences compared to the NT group. GC-1A (138.20 ± 7.37%, p < 0.0001) and GC-1B (169.3 ± 3.09%, p < 0.0001) maintained strong proliferative activity, while GC-2A (37.91 ± 0.73%, p < 0.0001) and GC-3A (38.04 ± 1.19%, p < 0.0001) showed dramatic cytotoxic effects, reducing viability by approximately 60%. At 72 h, GC-1B (158.4 ± 7.99%, p < 0.0001) continued to show strong proliferative effects, while GC-1A (106.6 ± 5.06%) was not significantly different from NT (p = 0.8339). Both GC-2A (31.15 ± 1.28%, p < 0.0001) and GC-3A (31.26 ± 1.24%, p < 0.0001) maintained severe cytotoxicity.

Human dermal fibroblasts cell proliferation

In HDF cells, all fractions showed time-dependent proliferative effects. At 24 h, only GC-3A (87.98 ± 3.06%, p = 0.0495) showed a significant effect (reduction), while other fractions maintained cell viability similar to NT. By 48 h, all fractions demonstrated highly significant differences in proliferative enhancement: GC-1A (168.3 ± 4.12%, p < 0.0001), GC-1B (140.3 ± 2.15%, p < 0.0001), GC-2A (132.4 ± 3.15%, p < 0.0001), and GC-3A (121.5 ± 2.94%, p < 0.0001). At 72 h, significant proliferation continued: GC-1A (129.6 ± 8.32%, p < 0.0001), GC-1B (123.0 ± 1.88%, p < 0.0001), GC-2A (117.3 ± 1.64%, p = 0.0035), and GC-3A (116.1 ± 2.05%, p = 0.0047).

HaCaT keratinocyte cell proliferation

HaCaT cells exhibited progressive proliferative responses when treated with GC-1B and the hot-extracted fractions GC-2A and GC-3A, with the highest cell proliferation observed at 72 h. At 24 h, no significant effects were observed for any fraction compared to NT. By 48 h, significant enhancement was seen in GC-1B (116.7 ± 3.06%, p = 0.0299), GC-2A (134.1 ± 5.92%, p < 0.0001), and GC-3A (120.0 ± 4.22%, p = 0.0079). Most dramatically, at 72 h, all fractions showed highly significant proliferative effects: GC-1B (179.7 ± 17.28%, p < 0.0001), GC-2A (202.0 ± 3.68%, p < 0.0001), GC-3A (178.2 ± 9.80%, p < 0.0001), and GC-1A (122.5 ± 4.87%, p = 0.3335, ns). The effects observed on HaCaT keratinocytes demonstrate a particularly interesting time-dependent amplification of proliferative responses across all GC fractions. The increasingly strong effects over time (peaking at 72 h with 122.5–202.0% viability) suggest these compounds may activate gradual activation of proliferative pathways or initiate autocrine signaling loops that enhance their effects over time. This pattern is especially pronounced for GC-2A, which showed the strongest effect at 72 h (202.0 ± 3.68%, p < 0.0001).

B16-F10 melanoma cell proliferation

The B16-F10 melanoma cells showed a distinct pattern from normal cells. At 24 h, no significant effects were observed for any fraction compared to NT. However, by 48 h, all fractions showed significant higher anti-proliferative effects: GC-1A (77.64 ± 2.67%, p = 0.0003), GC-1B (73.92 ± 3.33%, p < 0.0001), GC-2A (74.34 ± 2.47%, p < 0.0001), and GC-3A (77.24 ± 5.59%, p = 0.0003). At 72 h, GC-2A (75.67 ± 5.04%, p = 0.0173) maintained significant anti-proliferative activity, while other fractions showed non-significant effects: GC-1A (105.1 ± 8.19%), GC-1B (85.02 ± 5.73%), and GC-3A (81.88 ± 5.60%). The effects on B16-F10 melanoma cells reveal a distinct pattern of selective anti-proliferative activity that differs from the stimulatory effects observed on normal cells. The most significant inhibition was observed with GC-2A at 72 h (75.67 ± 5.04%, p = 0.0173), demonstrating a gradually increasing inhibitory effect that peaked later in the treatment period. This delayed response suggests that the anti-melanoma mechanisms may involve alterations in gene expression, cell cycle regulation, or induction of differentiation rather than direct cytotoxicity.

Cell migration

The capacity of GC polysaccharide fractions to promote cell migration, a critical process in the proliferative phase of wound healing,29 was evaluated using a standardized scratch-wound and exclusion assay on confluent monolayers of HDF and HaCaT. Treatments were applied at a concentration of 0.5 μg/μL, and wound closure was monitored over 24, 48, and 72 h using live-cell imaging. The results demonstrated a pronounced, fraction-dependent enhancement of cell migration that strongly complemented the proliferative bioactivities observed in viability assays. Specifically, the cold-water-extracted fraction GC-1A emerged as the most efficacious candidate for promoting migration in HDF cells, inducing rapid and complete wound closure. The GC-2A fraction was the most potent stimulator of HaCaT keratinocyte migration, a key process in re-epithelialization. The high Mw (GC-2A: 1180 kDa) and specific structural features of the active fractions, particularly their high 3,6-anhydrogalactose content, are proposed to facilitate interactions with cell surface receptors.30 This interaction may trigger the cytoskeletal rearrangements and expression of matrix metalloproteinases necessary for cellular migration.31 Interestingly, the same polysaccharide fractions (GC-1A and GC-2A) that stimulated normal cell migration exhibited a clearly inhibitory effect on the migration (spreading rate) of B16-F10 murine melanoma cells (see Figure 4C).

Phagocytosis

Phagocytosis is a fundamental mechanism of the innate immune response, enabling macrophages to engulf and clear pathogens, cellular debris, and apoptotic cells.32,33 This study demonstrates that G. corticata polysaccharide fractions differentially modulate this critical function in RAW264.7 macrophages. A significant increase in phagocytic activity was observed in cells treated with cold-extracted fractions. Specifically, GC-1B (142.3 ± 5.9%, p < 0.0001) and GC-1A (129.3 ± 5.0%, p = 0.0006) induced the most potent responses, enhancing phagocytosis by approximately 42% and 29%, respectively, compared to the untreated control (NT = 100%). This robust enhancement indicates that these fractions contain potent immunostimulatory components that effectively activate the macrophage’s microbial clearance capabilities. In contrast, the hot-extracted fraction GC-2A (82.95 ± 5.9%, p = 0.0469) significantly suppressed phagocytic activity. This finding presents an intriguing dichotomy: While GC-2A was a powerful inducer of macrophage proliferation at 24 h, it simultaneously impaired the cells' primary immune function. This suggests a potential disconnect between cellular division and functional activation for this particular fraction. The hot extracted fraction GC-3A (93.36 ± 3.7%) did not cause a statistically significant change in phagocytosis compared to the control (NT, p = 0.7179).

Nitric oxide (NO) production

NO is a key inflammatory mediator produced by activated macrophages during innate immune responses.34 In this study, the treatment of RAW264.7 murine macrophages with G. corticata polysaccharide fractions (0.5 μg/μL) resulted in significantly enhanced NO production, with effects varying dramatically based on the extraction method. The cold-water extracted fractions GC-1A (7.18 ± 0.24 μM, p < 0.0001) and GC-1B (10.52 ± 0.22 μM, p < 0.0001) induced higher NO production, with levels approximately 6.5-fold and 9.5-fold higher than the untreated control (NT = 1.11 ± 0.12 μM), respectively. In contrast, the hot-extracted fraction GC-2A (4.00 ± 0.17 μM) did not stimulate a significant increase in NO production compared to the LPS positive control (LPS = 3.25 ± 0.10 μM, p = 0.1664).

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

All GC fractions significantly enhanced SOD activity in HDF cells compared to the untreated control (NT = 4.22 ± 0.81, p < 0.0001 for all comparisons). The hot-extracted fraction GC-2A exhibited the strongest response (25.31 ± 1.27, p < 0.0001), exceeding the positive control (PC = 26.29 ± 1.30). The cold-water fractions GC-1A (22.84 ± 2.47, p < 0.0001) and GC-1B (18.36 ± 0.87, p < 0.0001) also produced robust and significant increases in antioxidant activity. The hot fraction GC-3A also significantly boosted SOD levels (21.48 ± 0.69, p < 0.0001), demonstrating a strong and consistent effect.

Fluorescence microscopy analysis of cell health

Fluorescence microscopy was used to conduct a detailed morphological analysis of HDF cells following treatment with the GC polysaccharide fraction GC-1A (0.5 μg/μL) (Figure 5). A comprehensive suite of fluorescent markers was used to assess nuclear integrity, cytoskeletal architecture, and overall cellular health. Analysis revealed that GC-1A treatment elicited no cytotoxic effects, a finding consistent with the proliferative activity observed in viability assays (see Figure 2H).

Gene expression analysis of GC-2A polysaccharide in HaCaT keratinocytes

The gene expression profiles of key mediators involved in wound healing were evaluated in HaCaT keratinocytes treated with GC-2A. Analysis of tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), a primary pro-inflammatory cytokine, revealed a time-dependent modulation under GC-2A treatment. At 24 h, a moderate increase in expression (5.33 ± 2.57%) was observed, which was substantially lower than the robust response induced by LPS positive control (9.44 ± 1.15%) (Figure 3D). By 48 h, GC-2A treatment resulted in a dramatic downregulation of TNF-α expression, demonstrating a clear transition from pro-inflammatory to anti-inflammatory activity that prevents prolonged inflammation, which can impede healing progression. At 24 h, GC-2A upregulated TGF-β1 expression to 3.20 ± 0.26%, exceeding the LPS response (2.10 ± 0.14%). The most significant effect was observed at 48 h, where GC-2A treatment resulted in a substantial increase in TGF-β1 expression (6.21 ± 2.23%), while LPS surprisingly suppressed expression. At 24 h, GC-2A maintained MMP-1 expression at baseline levels (1.00 ± 3.59%, lower expression), while both LPS (PC) and mitomycin-C (NC) induced substantial upregulation (23.63 ± 1.15% and 8.17 ± 1.82%, respectively). By 48 h, GC-2A treatment resulted in near-complete suppression of MMP-1 expression (Figure 3E). At 24 h, GC-2A caused only a modest increase in IL-8 expression (1.93 ± 3.52%), negligible compared to the robust responses induced by LPS and mitomycin-C (21.50 ± 1.57 and 20.70 ± 0.80, respectively). By 48 h, GC-2A treatment resulted in pronounced suppression of IL-8 expression, while LPS maintained high levels (10.92 ± 0.11%).

Amino acid metabolic reprogramming links polysaccharide structure to bioactivity

Targeted metabolomic profiling revealed that treatment with structurally distinct G. corticata polysaccharide fractions induced cell-type-specific remodeling of intracellular amino acid pools (Table S3). These changes were not random but aligned closely with the observed biological outcomes, including macrophage activation, fibroblast and keratinocyte proliferation, in vitro wound healing responses, and selective anti-cancer activity. Across these three cell lines, branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs; isoleucine/leucine and valine) remained the dominant intracellular amino acid class, reflecting preserved basal metabolic homeostasis.35 However, polysaccharide treatment selectively altered arginine, glutamine, lysine, alanine, and aromatic amino acids, which are key regulators of immune signaling, redox balance, and cellular proliferation. In RAW264.7 macrophages, GC-1A and GC-2A markedly increased intracellular arginine and lysine, accompanied by reduced glutamine levels. In HaCaT keratinocytes, GC-2A promoted increases in glutamine, valine, alanine, and proline, while maintaining balanced BCAA levels. In B16-F10 melanoma cells, GC-1A induced a pronounced elevation of arginine, whereas GC-2A preserved glutamine homeostasis and minimized perturbations in essential amino acids (Figure 6).

Pie charts show the relative distribution of amino acids in three cell lines (RAW264.7 macrophages, B16F10 melanoma cells, and HaCaT keratinocytes) under different conditions: GC-1A, GC-2A, NT (non-treated), and PC (positive control). The schematic panels on the right illustrate the proposed metabolic pathways associated with GC-1A and GC-2A treatments. In macrophages, treatment is associated with increased arginine metabolism, leading to enhanced nitric oxide (NO) production via inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), as well as increased phagocytosis and pro-inflammatory cytokine production, alongside reduced glutamine levels. In keratinocytes, GC-2A is associated with increased levels of glutamine, valine, alanine, and proline, contributing to enhanced cell proliferation and collagen synthesis. In melanoma cells, GC-1A/GC-2A treatment is linked to amino acid dysregulation, altered aromatic amino acid metabolism, mitochondrial stress, and cytotoxic effects.

Amino acid remodeling underpins macrophage immunomodulation

The amino acid shifts observed in RAW264.7 macrophages provide a metabolic explanation for the strong immunomodulatory effects of GC polysaccharides. Arginine plays a central role in macrophage activation, acting as a substrate for inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and supporting antimicrobial and tumoricidal responses.36 The significant elevation of arginine following GC-1A and GC-2A treatment directly correlates with the enhanced NO production and phagocytic activity observed for these fractions. Conversely, the reduction in intracellular glutamine, an amino acid rapidly consumed during immune activation, suggests increased metabolic flux toward anabolic and inflammatory pathways.37,38 This pattern is characteristic of activated macrophages undergoing metabolic reprogramming to support cytokine synthesis, phagocytosis, and proliferation. Importantly, GC-2A induced arginine enrichment without excessive NO production, consistent with its distinct activation profile and reduced phagocytic output. This uncoupling of arginine accumulation from classical iNOS activation suggests that GC-2A may engage non-canonical immunometabolic pathways, aligning with its observed pattern of uncoupled proliferation followed by delayed reduction in cell viability.39 These observations reinforce that macrophage bioactivity is not solely dictated by proliferation, but by how polysaccharide structure directs amino acid utilization toward functional immune outputs.

Amino acid support in fibroblast and keratinocyte proliferation and in vitro wound healing

In HaCaT keratinocytes and HDF cells, GC-2A induced enrichment of glutamine, alanine, valine, and proline, which are amino acids intimately linked to tissue regeneration. Glutamine serves as a major nitrogen donor and energy substrate for rapidly proliferating cells, while alanine and valine support glycolytic flux and protein synthesis.40 Proline plays a critical role in collagen biosynthesis and extracellular matrix remodeling, directly supporting fibroblast-mediated wound repair.41 The preservation of BCAA dominance alongside selective enrichment of growth-associated amino acids explains the robust yet controlled proliferative responses observed in keratinocytes and fibroblasts.42 In particular, GC-2A promoted amino acid profiles conducive to regeneration while avoiding excessive lysine accumulation, which can otherwise be associated with aberrant protein cross-linking and cellular stress. This balanced metabolic profile aligns with the time-dependent proliferation and migration effects seen in HaCaT and HDF cells and supports the conclusion that GC-2A facilitates a regenerative microenvironment rather than indiscriminate mitogenic stimulation.

Amino acid homeostasis and preferential inhibition of B16-F10 melanoma cells

The selective anti-proliferative effects observed in B16-F10 melanoma cells can also be partially explained by amino acid dysregulation. Cancer cells exhibit heightened dependence on glutamine and BCAAs to sustain anabolic growth and redox balance.43 The ability of GC-2A and GC-3A to perturb amino acid availability while sparing normal cells suggests interference with metabolic pathways uniquely exploited by tumor cells. Additionally, altered aromatic amino acid levels (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan) may influence redox signaling and mitochondrial function, contributing to growth inhibition and impaired migration in melanoma cells.44 This metabolic vulnerability is consistent with the observed preferential inhibition of B16-F10 melanoma cells under the tested conditions and further supports the potential of these fractions as metabolism-targeting anticancer agents.

Fatty acid remodeling in response to G. corticata polysaccharides

Fatty acid profile of B16-F10 melanoma cells

Compared with non-treated (NT) B16-F10 cells, polysaccharide treatment markedly altered the fatty acid composition (Table S3). NT cells displayed a heterogeneous profile characterized by a substantial proportion of unidentified fatty acids (23.38%), alongside moderate levels of palmitoleic (18.36%), palmitic (19.70%), and stearic acids (22.12%). GC-1A treatment increased the relative abundance of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), particularly palmitoleic acid (26.36%) and trans-oleic acid (elaidate, 20.76%), accompanied by elevated saturated fatty acids (SFAs), including palmitic (28.31%) and stearic acids (24.57%). Notably, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) were absent under GC-1A treatment. In contrast, GC-2A induced a more diverse and balanced fatty acid profile, characterized by reduced MUFAs and trans-fatty acids, but increased cis-PUFAs, including linoleic (18:2 ω6) and linolenic acids (18:3 ω3), which were absent in NT and GC-1A. GC-2A also reduced the proportion of unknown fatty acids, indicating improved metabolic specificity. The PC (DMSO) control produced intermediate changes but did not replicate the PUFA enrichment observed with GC-2A.

Fatty acid profile of HaCaT keratinocytes

In NT (no treatment) HaCaT cells were dominated by palmitic (18.81%), palmitoleic (15.98%), stearic acids (22.91%), and a substantial unknown fraction (23.14%). GC-1A treatment resulted in a pronounced increase in unsaturated fatty acids, including cis-oleic, trans-oleic, and linoleic acids (each ∼11.5%), accompanied by a moderate reduction in palmitic acid. This shift reflects enhanced lipid desaturation relative to NT cells. GC-2A further accentuated this remodeling by increasing myristic acid (5.86%) and stearic acid (25.43%) while reducing palmitoleic and palmitic acids. This shift reflects the enrichment of longer-chain saturated fatty acids, which may contribute to increased membrane rigidity and organization in keratinocytes. PC treatment led to increased stearic acid (27.02%) and palmitic acid (19.26%), reflecting a more rigid lipid profile relative to GC-treated cells.

Fatty acid profile of RAW264.7 macrophages

In NT (no treatment) RAW264.7 cells exhibited a distinct inflammatory lipid signature, dominated by high levels of short- and medium-chain SFAs, including myristic (35.52%) and pentadecanoic acids (10.01%), indicative of a basal pro-inflammatory metabolic state. GC-1A treatment dramatically reduced these fatty acids and shifted the profile toward longer-chain SFAs and MUFAs, including palmitic (29.64%), palmitoleic (26.20%), stearic (24.40%), and elaidic acids (C18:1 trans, 19.76%). This redistribution suggests enhanced membrane remodeling and activation-associated lipid synthesis. GC-2A induced a more controlled lipid response, reducing total SFAs while maintaining balanced MUFA levels and lowering trans-fatty acid content relative to GC-1A. PC treatment markedly increased stearic acid (32.88%) and elaidic acid (24.46%), consistent with chemical stress-induced membrane rigidification rather than functional immune activation.

Discussion

The present study provides a comprehensive structure-function characterization of sulfated polysaccharides isolated from Gracilaria corticata, revealing that extraction-dependent physicochemical properties govern distinct bioactivities across immune, dermal, epithelial, and cancer cell models. Our integrated approach, combining sequential extraction, detailed structural analysis (FT-IR, NMR, Mw determination, and so forth), functional bioassays (immunomodulation, proliferation, migration, antioxidant activity, and so forth), and metabolomic profiling (amino acid and fatty acid remodeling), establishes a mechanistic framework linking polysaccharide architecture to cellular responses. Critically, this work demonstrates that cold-extracted fractions (particularly GC-1B) promote sustained immunomodulation and dermal regeneration, while hot-extracted fractions (GC-2A, GC-3A) exhibit potent but transient effects with selective anti-cancer activity, and gene expression analysis in HaCaT keratinocytes confirms that GC-2A enhances in vitro wound healing properties through TGF-β1 upregulation and MMP-1 suppression.

Extraction-dependent physicochemical properties

The sequential extraction protocol successfully yielded six polysaccharide fractions with markedly different physicochemical characteristics. Cold-extracted fractions (GC-1A, GC-1B) exhibited lower Mws (100–900 kDa) and moderate-to-high sulfate content (7.0–12.0%), while hot-extracted fractions (GC-2A, GC-2B, GC-3B) possessed substantially higher Mws (1160–1280 kDa). The outlier fraction GC-3A (210 kDa), despite being hot-extracted, suggests that specific extraction conditions (temperature, duration, or alcohol precipitation parameters) can selectively solubilize lower Mw polymers even under elevated temperatures. Sodium phosphate buffer at pH 9 was used for extraction, as alkaline conditions enhance polysaccharide solubility by ionizing sulfate groups, facilitate cell wall disruption, and prevent acid-catalyzed desulfation, thereby preserving native structure and bioactivity.45,46 The substantial sulfate content confirmed across all fractions identifies them as agar-type sulfated galactans, which are critical for biological activities such as immunomodulation.47 High sulfate and phenolic levels notably boost antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, while protein and sugar constituents are crucial for immunomodulatory function and structural properties.48 GC-2A emerged as the fraction with the highest carbohydrate purity, while GC-1B and GC-3A were more highly sulfated. Overall, significant compositional differences were observed; however, they did not translate into significant variations in anticoagulant activity under the tested conditions, suggesting that this specific bioactivity may depend on structural features beyond mere sulfate content or sugar purity.

The universal presence of the 3,6-anhydrogalactose (3,6-AG) band at 931-932 cm−1 across all six fractions confirms their classification as agar-type galactans, consistent with previous reports on Gracilaria species.22,49,50 This structural conservation is significant because the 3,6-AG bridge is known to influence gelation properties, solubility, and biological interactions. The additional presence of sulfate esters (confirmed by S=O stretching at 1224-1230 cm−1) and, in GC-2A, pyruvic acid ketal substitutions (NMR: δ 1.45/27.81 ppm) introduces structural complexity that likely enhances receptor recognition and bioactivity. Several fractions exhibited bands between 842 and 889 cm−1, which are often associated with the C–O–S bending vibration of sulfate groups on galactose C-4.51,52 The presence of bands at 770-771 cm−1 and 694-695 cm−1 (in GC-2A and GC-3A) is characteristic of skeletal bending modes of the galactopyranose rings.53 These physicochemical distinctions formed the foundation for subsequent biological observations: cold fractions' moderate Mw and accessible sulfate groups appear optimal for sustained receptor engagement, whereas hot fractions' high Mw polymers with hydrophobic patches (methoxyl/pyruvate groups) may promote stronger, potentially disruptive membrane interactions.

Establishing safety and dose-dependent profiles across cell lines

Establishing the non-cytotoxicity of a polysaccharide toward target cells is a critical prerequisite for attributing any observed immunomodulatory, wound healing, or anti-cancer effects to genuine biological responses rather than to a simple reduction in cell viability.54

In RAW264.7 macrophages, fractions GC-1A and GC-1B enhanced macrophage proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner, with the most pronounced effect observed at the highest dose (0.50 μg/μL). Hot water-extracted fractions GC-2A and GC-3A exhibited stronger and broader immunostimulatory effects, likely due to differences in their chemical composition.

In HDF fibroblasts, GC-2A showed a particularly promising trend, potentially supporting cellular metabolism. By enhancing metabolic activity within HDFs, these compounds promote fibroblast activation, leading to the upregulated synthesis of collagen and elastin, a fundamental mechanism underpinning anti-aging and skin rejuvenation.55 While GC-3A caused a minor but significant reduction in viability at one concentration (0.50 μg/μL), its overall profile remains favorable for further study, as viability remained high (>87%). These results reveal the safety of GC polysaccharides for dermal applications.

In HaCaT keratinocytes, the cell viability assay revealed a complex, fraction-dependent cytotoxicity profile. Contrary to typical dose-response expectations, fractions GC-1A and GC-1B demonstrated significantly greater cytotoxicity at lower concentrations (0.01–0.06 μg/μL) while being well-tolerated at higher doses. GC-2A showed the safest profile, with minimal effects on keratinocyte viability. GC-3A exhibited the most consistent cytotoxic effects across multiple concentrations. Therefore, isolating non-cytotoxic components through careful fractionation is essential for developing safe cosmetic and dermatological applications.56 The inverse concentration-effect relation revealed for some fractions highlights the importance of careful dose selection for future studies involving keratinocytes.

In B16-F10 melanoma cells, the MTT assay revealed a clear hierarchy in anti-melanoma activity. GC-3A emerged as the most promising fraction, demonstrating significant, dose-dependent cytotoxicity across multiple concentrations, with the strongest effect at 0.02 μg/μL (72.94% viability). GC-2A also showed significant anti-proliferative effects (75.67% viability at 72 h), though with a more complex dose-response curve. GC-1B had a narrow window of activity, while GC-1A showed no efficacy against this cancer cell line. The potent activity of GC-3A highlights its potential as a natural anti-melanoma agent, as cytotoxic responses against B16-F10 cells point to a compound’s promise not just in reducing tumor burden but, more importantly, in combating metastatic spread responsible for the majority of cancer-related deaths.57 Fluorescence microscopy analysis of cell health following GC-1A treatment revealed normal, homogeneous nuclear morphology without signs of condensation or fragmentation, with maintained intact plasma membranes and a well-organized cytoskeletal structure, closely resembling the healthy phenotype of the untreated control group. These visual results provide robust morphological evidence that the GC-1A fraction supports a healthy cellular state conducive to proliferation, confirming its biocompatibility and safety.

Macrophage proliferation: cold vs. hot fractions

The proliferation patterns observed in RAW264.7 macrophages revealed a complex, time-dependent relationship between polysaccharide fraction structure and immune cell responses.58 The most striking finding was the temporal dichotomy between cold and hot fractions. Cold fractions (GC-1A, GC-1B) induced sustained macrophage proliferation. At 72 h, GC-1B maintained remarkably high viability (158.4 ± 7.99%, p < 0.0001), suggesting this fraction contains active elements that promote macrophage survival and ongoing activation without inducing apoptosis. This balanced immunomodulation represents a particularly valuable therapeutic profile and can be directly attributed to the compositional characteristics of the cold-extracted fractions: moderate Mw (900 kDa), intermediate sulfate content (12.04%), and low phenolic content create an optimal structural environment for sustained macrophage proliferation and activation without triggering cytotoxicity.

Hot fractions (GC-2A, GC-3A) produced a biphasic response: potent initial proliferation (168.0 ± 7.36% and 169.2 ± 6.07% viability at 24 h, respectively, p < 0.0001) followed by severe viability reduction (31–38% at 72 h). One possible interpretation of this pattern is metabolic exhaustion or activation-induced cell death (AICD), a mechanism that prevents excessive inflammation.59 However, these interpretations remain hypotheses, as we did not directly measure markers of apoptosis (caspase-3/7 activation, Annexin V staining, and so forth), necrosis, or intracellular ATP levels. Research on sulfated polysaccharides from Caulerpa cupressoides has similarly demonstrated time-dependent effects on macrophage activation and subsequent apoptosis, potentially serving as a regulatory mechanism to prevent excessive inflammation.60 These divergent responses demonstrate that the immunomodulatory potential of G. corticata polysaccharides is closely linked to fraction-specific structural features rather than being a general property of all fractions. Consequently, cold-extracted fractions such as GC-1B may act as effective biological response modifiers, enhancing immune responses while minimizing excessive inflammation, with potential applications in cancer immunotherapy and treatment of immune disorders.61

Implications for in vitro wound healing

The consistent proliferative effects observed across all GC fractions in HDF fibroblasts highlight their potential for wound healing and tissue regeneration applications. The increasingly strong effects from 24 to 48 h demonstrate a time-dependent activation of fibroblast proliferation mechanisms, with particularly impressive results from GC-1A (168.3 ± 4.12%, p < 0.0001) and GC-1B (140.3 ± 2.15%, p < 0.0001) at the 48-h mark. This progressive stimulation suggests these fractions may activate growth factor signaling pathways or modulate extracellular matrix production in fibroblasts, both critical processes in wound healing.62

Although the proliferative effect at 72 h was slightly lower than at earlier time points, it remained statistically significant (116.1–129.6%), reflecting sustained and controlled activation rather than excessive stimulation. This balanced proliferation profile is particularly important for therapeutic applications, as excessive fibroblast activation can lead to fibrotic conditions and tissue scarring.63 Research on sulfated polysaccharides from red seaweeds has similarly demonstrated enhanced fibroblast proliferation and migration,64,65 suggesting this may be a common property of marine algal compounds. These findings have significant implications for the development of marine-based dermatological products and wound healing therapies.

Delayed peak response and re-epithelialization potential

The delayed peak response in HaCaT keratinocytes (GC-2A: 202.0% viability at 72 h) contrasts with the more immediate effects observed in macrophages and fibroblasts, suggesting cell-type-specific mechanisms of action. The implications for wound healing and skin regeneration are substantial, as keratinocyte proliferation and migration are essential processes in re-epithelialization.66 The strong proliferative responses induced by these fractions could accelerate wound closure without signs of toxicity or uncontrolled cell growth, reflecting a controlled and therapeutically relevant response. Research on brown algae polysaccharides has similarly demonstrated enhanced keratinocyte proliferation and migration,67,68 supporting the potential of marine compounds in dermatological applications.

Selective anti-melanoma activity

The differential effects on B16-F10 melanoma cells versus normal cells represent a key finding with therapeutic implications. GC-3A demonstrated the most potent anti-melanoma activity (72.94% viability at 0.02 μg/μL), while GC-2A showed sustained inhibition over 72 h (75.67% viability). Importantly, these same fractions promoted proliferation of normal HDF and HaCaT cells (GC-2A: 202.0% HaCaT viability at 72 h), indicating preferential inhibition of B16 F10 melanoma cells under the tested conditions. In this study, the anti-melanoma activity of the polysaccharide fractions was evaluated exclusively against B16-F10 melanoma cells in comparison with normal skin cells (HDF and HaCaT). Under the tested conditions, the fractions exhibited preferential inhibition of B16-F10 cells. Whether this preferential effect extends to normal melanocytes, other melanoma lines, or additional cancer types remains to be investigated in future studies. Research on marine compounds has similarly demonstrated time-dependent effects on melanoma cells, often involving modulation of key signaling pathways such as MAPK/ERK and PI3K/Akt.69 This selective toxicity profile suggests these compounds may target pathways specifically dysregulated in cancer cells, such as aberrant cell cycle control or metabolic reprogramming.70 Studies on polyphenols from Sargassum polycystum have demonstrated inhibition of tyrosinase activity and downregulation of MITF, TRP-1, and TRP-2 expression in B16-F10 cells.71

Phagocytosis and nitric oxide production

The strong correlation between the potent phagocytosis enhancement by GC-1A and GC-1B and their ability to stimulate massive NO production indicates coordinated and classical activation of macrophage immune functions.49 Cold fraction GC-1B enhanced phagocytosis (142.3%) and NO production (10.52 μM), indicating classical M1-like polarization, which is critical for host defense against pathogens and tumor surveillance.32,33 These findings highlight the specific immunostimulatory potential of cold-extracted fractions from G. corticata, positioning them as promising candidates for applications requiring enhanced immune surveillance, such as adjuvant development or therapeutic agents for immunocompromised states. In contrast, despite initial proliferation, GC-2A and GC-3A suppressed phagocytosis (GC-2A: 82.95%) and NO production (GC-2A: 4.00 μM), indicating a disconnect between cell division and classical immune activation. This disparity between the potent cytotoxic and proliferative effects of GC-2A and its lack of NO induction suggests a distinct and complex mechanism of macrophage activation that is independent of the classical iNOS/NO pathway.72 The subsequent cell viability loss at 48–72 h is more consistent with delayed cytotoxicity or metabolic exhaustion rather than AICD. The significant production of NO by GC-1A and GC-1B is of particular therapeutic interest. While NO plays a crucial role in host defense by promoting cancer cell death and inhibiting microbial growth, it is also a critical signaling molecule in wound healing.73 It is crucial to note that inflammation is a necessary part of the initial wound healing process; the ability of these fractions to potently but transiently activate this pathway supports their potential application in immunomodulation and early-stage wound repair, provided the response is well-controlled. The cold fraction profile (GC-1B) is desirable for immunocompromised patients or as vaccine adjuvants where sustained immune enhancement is needed. The hot fraction profile may be valuable in autoimmune conditions where transient immune activation followed by downregulation could restore homeostasis.

Gene expression analysis in HaCaT keratinocytes

The gene expression analysis of GC-2A polysaccharide in HaCaT keratinocytes revealed a highly favorable wound healing cytokine profile that mechanistically explains the observed proliferative and migratory effects. GC-2A treatment produced upregulation of TGF-β1 (6.21-fold at 48 h) with simultaneous downregulation of TNF-α and MMP-1. TGF-β1 is a master regulator of wound healing, stimulating extracellular matrix production, angiogenesis, and re-epithelialization.74,75 The pronounced upregulation at 48 h indicates activation of proliferative and tissue-remodeling pathways, which aligns with the enhanced keratinocyte migration and proliferation observed in functional assays. The suppression of MMP-1 is clinically significant because elevated MMP levels characterize chronic wounds where uncontrolled proteolysis prevents tissue restoration.76 The simultaneous increase in TGF-β1 and decrease in MMP-1 indicates a favorable shift toward net matrix accumulation and tissue repair. The moderate increase in TNF-α at 24 h followed by suppression at 48 h demonstrates appropriate early inflammatory signaling for pathogen clearance, followed by resolution to prevent healing impairment. This temporal pattern distinguishes GC-2A from many natural compounds that either over-activate or completely suppress inflammation. Similarly, IL-8 expression patterns indicated controlled neutrophil recruitment followed by rapid resolution of inflammation, preventing prolonged neutrophilic activity, which can otherwise lead to secondary tissue damage.77,78 Overall, GC-2A promotes a balanced wound-healing environment characterized by early controlled inflammation, strong activation of regenerative signaling pathways, and suppression of excessive proteolytic and inflammatory activity. The bioactivity of GC-2A may be related to its physicochemical properties, including its high Mw (1180 kDa), high sulfate content (9.00 ± 0.18%), and the presence of pyruvic acid ketal substitutions, which may enhance interactions with cell-surface receptors involved in repair signaling.

While TGF-β1 upregulation in HaCaT keratinocytes supports wound healing, it is important to note that TGF-β can also promote epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in late-stage melanoma.79,80 In our study, the anti-melanoma activity of GC-2A was assessed in B16-F10 monocultures without keratinocyte-derived TGF-β1 exposure. Future studies using co-culture systems or in vivo tumor models are needed to determine whether GC-2A’s anti-proliferative effects override any potential pro-metastatic TGF-β signaling in the tumor microenvironment.

The effects on skin cells revealed a complementary therapeutic profile. The scratch wound assays confirmed functional migration enhancement: GC-1A was most effective for HDF fibroblast migration, while GC-2A excelled in promoting HaCaT keratinocyte closure. This selective inhibition of cancer cell motility while promoting the migration of healthy skin cells is a highly desirable therapeutic property, reflecting a potential effect that could support wound healing without inadvertently promoting cancer metastasis, a critical safety consideration for regenerative therapies.81 The potent stimulation of keratinocyte migration by GC-2A positions it as a leading candidate for wound healing applications aimed at accelerating re-epithelialization. The combination of GC-1A’s effects on fibroblasts and GC-2A’s effects on keratinocytes presents a promising synergistic strategy for comprehensive wound and skin protection.

Antioxidant protection: SOD upregulation as a cytoprotective mechanism

All fractions significantly enhanced superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity in HDF cells, with GC-2A showing the strongest response (25.31% inhibition, comparable to the positive control). SOD is a first-line antioxidant defense enzyme that converts superoxide radicals to hydrogen peroxide, preventing oxidative damage to DNA, proteins, and lipids.82 The correlation between SOD enhancement and HDF proliferation suggests that reduced oxidative stress creates a favorable microenvironment for cell growth and survival. This is particularly relevant for skin aging, where cumulative oxidative damage drives collagen degradation, elastin dysfunction, and cellular senescence.55 These findings confirm that GC fractions are potent enhancers of the intrinsic antioxidant defense system in dermal fibroblasts, supporting their potential role in mitigating oxidative stress, a key factor in skin aging and damage. The differential among fractions, with GC-2A being the most potent, suggests that specific structural features of the polysaccharides, such as higher sulfation or specific monosaccharide profiles, are critical for activating the Nrf2-antioxidant response element (ARE) pathway.83

Amino acid metabolic reprogramming in B16-F10 melanoma cells

Our targeted metabolomic analysis revealed that GC-2A and GC-3A induced distinct amino acid perturbations in B16-F10 cells compared to normal cells. In melanoma cells, GC-2A preserved glutamine homeostasis while GC-1A induced pronounced arginine elevation. Cancer cells exhibit heightened dependence on glutamine and BCAAs for anabolic growth and redox balance.43 The ability of GC-2A to maintain glutamine levels while reducing melanoma viability suggests interference with glutamine-dependent metabolic pathways uniquely exploited by tumor cells. This is consistent with the “metabolic vulnerability” concept, where compounds target cancer-specific metabolic dependencies without affecting normal cells that utilize alternative fuel sources.70

Fatty acid remodeling across cell lines

The GC-MS-based fatty acid profiling revealed structure-dependent lipid remodeling across all cell lines (Figure 7), providing an additional mechanistic explanation for the observed biological effects. Fatty acid composition directly influences membrane fluidity, receptor clustering, signal transduction, and inflammatory mediator production.84,85

In B16-F10 melanoma cells: The absence of PUFAs in GC-1A-treated cells contrasted with GC-2A-induced enrichment of cis-PUFAs (linoleic and linolenic acids). Since PUFAs are precursors for anti-inflammatory lipid mediators and can induce ferroptosis in cancer cells,86 this difference may partially explain the superior anti-melanoma activity of GC-2A. The concomitant reduction in trans-fatty acids and unknown lipid species suggests a shift toward metabolically favorable lipid biosynthesis, consistent with preserved epithelial viability and metabolic stability.87

In HaCaT keratinocytes: GC-1A enhanced unsaturated fatty acid content (cis-oleic, trans-oleic, linoleic) relative to NT cells, supporting membrane plasticity required for proliferation and migration. The increased linoleic and oleic acids under GC-1A treatment are particularly relevant, as these fatty acids are essential for epidermal differentiation and wound healing.88 GC-2A favored the enrichment of SFAs, particularly stearic acid, which may enhance membrane integrity during late-stage differentiation and barrier formation.89 This balanced lipid remodeling aligns with the observed time-dependent proliferative and regenerative responses of keratinocytes.

In RAW264.7 macrophages: The strong reduction of short-chain SFAs following GC-1A and GC-2A treatment reflects a departure from a basal inflammatory state toward an activated, functionally responsive phenotype. Elevated palmitoleic acid, a known lipokine with anti-inflammatory properties,90 under GC-1A treatment may contribute to enhanced phagocytosis and immune activation.

GC-2A induced a more restrained lipid response with reduced trans-fatty acid levels, consistent with its ability to activate macrophages without triggering excessive inflammatory stress. This profile aligns with its observed uncoupled proliferation response, followed by delayed loss of cell viability after an initial proliferative phase.

Our findings align with and extend previous research on Gracilaria-derived polysaccharides. Studies on Gracilaria verrucosa and Gracilaria birdiae have demonstrated immunomodulatory and antioxidant activities, but few have systematically compared cold versus hot extraction or integrated metabolomic profiling. The identification of pyruvic acid ketal substitutions in GC-2A (NMR δ 1.45/27.81 ppm) is particularly noteworthy, as this modification has been associated with enhanced bioactivity in other red algal agars.25,27 The selective anti-melanoma activity of GC-3A (72.9% viability at 0.02 μg/μL) compares favorably with other marine-derived compounds. For comparison, sulfated polysaccharides from Sargassum polycystum have shown B16-F10 inhibition with IC50 values in similar concentration ranges.71 The biphasic dose-response observed for GC-2A (inhibition at lower doses, stimulation at 0.50 μg/μL) suggests concentration-dependent receptor switching or differential activation of proliferative versus apoptotic pathways, a phenomenon requiring further investigation.

Structure-function relationships in bioactivity

The integrated structural and biological data reveal clear, extraction-dependent SARs for the sulfated galactans from G. corticata. These relationships delineate two primary bioactive profiles, each linked to distinct physicochemical properties. Cold-water fractions (GC-1A, GC-1B) exhibited moderate Mws (100–900 kDa) and intermediate-to-high sulfate content (7.0–12.0%). Structurally, they were rich in 3,6-anhydro-l-galactose (3,6-AG), a hallmark of the agarose backbone, as confirmed by a strong FTIR band at ∼931 cm−1. This profile conferred balanced immunostimulatory and cytoprotective bioactivity. The optimal sulfate density (12.0%) and Mw (900 kDa) in GC-1B appear to facilitate effective interaction with pattern recognition receptors on macrophages (TLRs, scavenger receptors, and so forth), triggering sustained activation.91 This is supported by potent, persistent enhancement of phagocytosis (142.3 ± 5.9%) and NO production (10.52 ± 0.22 μM), without cytotoxicity. Moreover, these fractions showed excellent biocompatibility with dermal fibroblasts, significantly promoting proliferation (GC-1A: 168.3% at 48 h) and migration, alongside a strong antioxidant effect via SOD upregulation. This combination of safe immune activation and tissue-repair support identifies the cold fractions as ideal candidates for wound-healing adjuvants and general immunopotentiators.

Hot fractions (GC-2A, GC-3A) possessed markedly higher Mws (GC-2A: 1180 kDa) and more complex substitution patterns, including pyruvic acid ketals (NMR signal at 1.45/27.81 ppm) in addition to 3,6-AG and sulfate groups. This distinct structure underpins a dual-function activating and targeting bioactivity. The high Mw and sulfation are proposed to induce a hyper-stimulatory signal in RAW264.7 macrophages, leading to an initial proliferative burst (168–169% viability at 24 h) followed by a marked reduction in cell viability by 48–72 h, representing a potent but transient immunomodulatory effect. Notably, this structural profile correlated with selective activity against B16-F10 melanoma cells. Fraction GC-3A, with high sulfate (11.2%) and specific structural motifs, showed the strongest anti-proliferative effect (72.9% viability at 0.02 μg/μL). Crucially, while inhibiting cancer cell proliferation and migration, these same fractions (particularly GC-2A) robustly stimulated the proliferation and migration of normal skin cells (HDF and HaCaT). This selectivity likely stems from differential interactions with cancer cell membranes, inhibition of pro-survival pathways, or induction of apoptosis specific to the transformed phenotype.92 Gene expression data from GC-2A-treated HaCaT cells support this pro-healing mechanism, revealing a beneficial cytokine profile (upregulated TGF-β1, downregulated TNF-α and MMP-1).

The key structural determinant distinguishing the two profiles appears to be extraction-driven polymer assembly. Cold extraction yields shorter, more soluble chains with accessible sulfate groups, favoring receptor-mediated immune cell communication. Hot extraction solubilizes larger, more rigid networks with hydrophobic patches from methoxyl/pyruvate groups, which may promote stronger and potentially disruptive interactions with cell surfaces. This leads to selective cancer cell toxicity while providing a robust scaffold that stimulates normal cell adhesion and migration.93 Collectively, amino acid metabolomic data establish a critical mechanistic link between polysaccharide structure and biological function. High-molecular-weight, moderately sulfated fractions such as GC-2A promoted controlled metabolic activation characterized by regenerative amino acid enrichment. In contrast, highly sulfated or phenolic-rich fractions induced more aggressive metabolic shifts leading to immune-uncoupled proliferation or cytotoxicity. These findings show that the biological effects of G. corticata polysaccharides are not solely receptor-mediated but are tightly coupled to intracellular metabolic reprogramming, particularly amino acid utilization. This insight provides a unifying explanation for the diverse bioactivities observed across immune, dermal, epithelial, and cancer cell models.

The distinct fatty acid signatures elicited by GC-1A and GC-2A strongly correlate with their structural features and biological activities (Figure 8). GC-1A, with higher sulfate content and lower Mw, induced pronounced MUFA and trans-fatty acid accumulation, especially in immune cells. This pronounced lipid remodeling aligns with its strong immunostimulatory and phagocytic activity but may also contribute to cellular stress in epithelial systems. GC-2A, with moderate Mw, balanced sulfation, and lower phenolic content, consistently promoted metabolically favorable lipid profiles across all cell lines. Enrichment of cis-PUFAs in B16-F10 cells, balanced SFA/MUFA ratios in keratinocytes, and controlled lipid activation in macrophages underscore its broad biocompatibility and regenerative potential. Together, these results demonstrate that G. corticata polysaccharides modulate cellular function not only through receptor-mediated signaling but also via structure-dependent reprogramming of lipid and amino acid metabolism. This dual mechanism explains their spectrum of immunomodulatory, wound-healing, and selectively bioactive effects.

Overall mechanistic insights and biological implications

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the structure-dependent bioactivity of sulfated polysaccharides isolated from Gracilaria corticata, highlighting their significant potential for therapeutic and nutraceutical applications. By integrating detailed structural characterization with functional assays and targeted metabolomic analyses, we demonstrate that extraction-driven differences in polysaccharide architecture govern distinct cellular responses across immune, dermal, epithelial, and cancer cell models. A central finding of this work is the clear bifurcation of immunomodulatory behavior between cold- and hot-extracted fractions. Cold-water fractions acted as balanced pro-proliferative but functionally suppressive, enhancing macrophage phagocytosis and NO production without inducing cytotoxicity. In contrast, hot fractions triggered transient macrophage uncoupled proliferation followed by delayed cytotoxicity/metabolic exhaustion, while simultaneously exerting selective cytotoxic effects against melanoma cells. These divergent outcomes were not explained by receptor-mediated signaling alone but were strongly supported by LC-MS-based amino acid profiling, which revealed structure-dependent intracellular metabolic reprogramming. Cold fractions promoted controlled enrichment of arginine, glutamine, and regenerative amino acids, consistent with sustained immune activation and tissue repair. Conversely, hot fractions induced more pronounced perturbations in amino acid homeostasis, selectively disrupting metabolic pathways exploited by cancer cells while sparing normal cells. Cell proliferation analyses further revealed strong cell type- and time-dependent effects. Cold fractions (GC-1A and GC-1B) consistently supported proliferation and migration of normal fibroblasts and keratinocytes with minimal impact on melanoma cells. Hot fractions (GC-2A and GC-3A) exhibited dual functionality, simultaneously stimulating normal skin cells while suppressing melanoma proliferation. Notably, GC-2A maintained significant anti-melanoma activity over 48–72 h while preserving normal cell growth, underscoring its promise as a selective anti-cancer agent with reduced toxicity. GC-MS-based fatty acid profiling provided an additional mechanistic layer supporting these biological outcomes. Polysaccharide treatment induced marked remodeling of cellular lipid composition in a structure-dependent manner. Cold fractions promoted MUFA-enriched profiles associated with immune activation and membrane plasticity, whereas GC-2A consistently induced metabolically favorable lipid signatures, including the enrichment of cis-PUFAs in epithelial cells and balanced SFA/MUFA ratios in keratinocytes and macrophages. These lipid profiles are known to support membrane integrity, redox balance, and regenerative signaling, aligning closely with the observed wound-healing and biocompatible responses. In contrast, more aggressive lipid remodeling induced by certain hot fractions correlated with selective inhibition of B16-F10 melanoma cells under the tested conditions.

SAR analysis revealed that variations in Mw, sulfate content, and substitution patterns (notably pyruvic acid ketal motifs identified by NMR) are key determinants of bioactivity. The consistent presence of 3,6-anhydro-l-galactose across all fractions confirms their classification as agar-type galactans, while the differential bioactivities likely arise from the specific combination of molecular size, sulfation degree, and minor substitutions. The agarose-rich backbone of hot fractions underpins their potent but selective biological effects, while the more soluble and accessible sulfate architecture of cold fractions favors sustained and safe immune activation. Collectively, two strategic bioactivity profiles emerge: (1) cold-water fractions (GC-1A and GC-1B), which function as safe, broad-spectrum immunomodulators and promoters of skin cell proliferation suitable for wound-healing and dermocosmetic applications; and (2) hot fractions (GC-2A and GC-3A), which act as dual-function agents capable of stimulating normal tissue regeneration while selectively inhibiting melanoma cell growth, making them attractive candidates for targeted anticancer and advanced wound-care strategies. Overall, this study establishes that G. corticata polysaccharides function as natural biological response modifiers through a dual mechanism involving receptor engagement and structure-dependent reprogramming of amino acid and lipid metabolism. The integration of LC-MS and GC-MS metabolomics provides a unifying mechanistic framework linking polysaccharide structure to cellular function. Future studies should focus on in vivo validation, identification of specific molecular targets, and the development of standardized polysaccharide formulations to advance clinical translation. These findings underscore the promise of marine-derived polysaccharides as multifunctional biomaterials for immunological, regenerative, and anticancer applications.

Limitations of the study

Purity of polysaccharide fractions

One limitation concerns the purity of the extracted polysaccharide fractions. Although dialysis with a 14 kDa Mw cutoff membrane was performed to remove low-molecular-weight contaminants, we acknowledge that this method may not completely eliminate all small molecules, including residual phenolics, peptides, or short-chain oligosaccharides. Ongoing studies in our laboratory are focused on developing LC-MS-based methods to further purify and characterize these fractions and to definitively resolve the contribution of any tightly bound contaminants. Future work should also employ additional analytical techniques such as HPAEC-PAD or size-exclusion chromatography coupled with multi-angle light scattering (SEC-MALS) to confirm absolute purity.

Metabolomic normalization and cell size

Another methodological limitation concerns metabolomic normalization. While we normalized all samples to equal cell number using hemocytometer counting, this approach does not account for potential treatment-induced changes in cell size (hypertrophy). If a polysaccharide fraction causes cells to enlarge without dividing, metabolite abundance per cell could increase even without true metabolic reprogramming. However, microscopic examination of treated cells (Figures 2E and 4D) did not reveal obvious hypertrophy at the 24 h time point used for metabolomics. Future studies using volumetric normalization (by cell diameter or protein content alongside cell number, and so forth) could further validate these findings.

Correlative nature of metabolomic data

Our metabolomic data demonstrate strong associations between metabolic changes and biological outcomes, but they do not establish causation. Although we observed reproducible associations between specific amino acid and fatty acid profiles and distinct biological outcomes (macrophage activation, fibroblast proliferation, melanoma inhibition), we did not perform functional perturbation experiments to definitively establish that these metabolic shifts are causally linked to the observed phenotypes. For example, pharmacological inhibition of iNOS (L-NAME, and so forth), blockade of amino acid transporters, or genetic knockdown of key metabolic enzymes (ARG1, iNOS, glutaminase, and so forth) would be required to test causality. Similarly, metabolic flux analysis using stable isotope-labeled tracers (13C-glutamine or 13C-arginine, and so forth) would provide direct evidence of pathway utilization. Such experiments are technically challenging and beyond the scope of the current discovery-based study. Future studies, including in vivo murine wound healing and melanoma models combined with targeted metabolic interventions, are planned to establish causality.

Scope of gene expression analysis

A limitation of this study is that qPCR analysis was performed only on HaCaT keratinocytes. While we observed that HDF cells treated with GC-1A showed enhanced proliferation and migration, gene expression analysis (for collagen, elastin, or MMPs, and so forth) in HDF cells was not performed. Future studies should include gene expression profiling in HDF cells to fully elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the pro-regenerative effects of these fractions on the wound microenvironment.

Scope of anti-cancer activity assessment

A limitation of this study is that only one melanoma cell line (B16-F10) was tested for anti-cancer activity. Although we demonstrated selectivity by showing no cytotoxicity toward normal skin cells (HDF, HaCaT), our claim of selectivity is currently limited to melanoma versus normal cells. Future studies should include additional melanoma lines (A375, SK-MEL-28, and so forth) and non-melanoma cancer lines (breast, colon, lung, and so forth) to fully establish the selectivity profile.

In vitro nature of bioactivity assays

All bioactivity assays were conducted in vitro. In vivo studies are required to validate the therapeutic potential of these fractions in animal models of wound healing, immunomodulation, and melanoma.

Experimental model and study participant details

Four cell lines were utilized to model distinct biological systems

RAW264.7 murine macrophages (ECACC 91062702), human dermal fibroblasts (HDF, Cell Applications #106-05a), human keratinocytes (HaCaT, CLS GmbH #300493), and B16-F10 murine melanoma cells (obtained from ATCC). All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% (v/v) heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich), 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (complete medium) at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and were negative. Cells were used between passages 5–15 for all experiments.

Seaweed material

Gracilaria corticata (GC) was collected from the coastal waters of Ahangama, Southern Province, Sri Lanka (GPS: N 050 58.006' E 0800 22.482') (pre-monsoon season). The species was identified by morphological examination, and a voucher specimen was deposited at the Tallinn University, Estonia (voucher # GC-2021SL).

Method details

Seaweed material and sample preparation

Freshly harvested G. corticata specimens were thoroughly cleaned, first with seawater from the collection site and subsequently with fresh water to remove adhering sand, debris, and epiphytes, followed by a final rinse with tap water. The cleaned biomass was freeze-dried to obtain a stable dry weight, ground into a fine powder using a water-cooled mill (IKA® A10, Germany), and stored at –20°C until further use.

Sample preparation

Removal of low-molecular-weight substances and pigments

Freeze-dried GC powder (20 g) was suspended in 200 mL of 70% ethanol and incubated in a shaking incubator (Witeg, Germany) at 35°C and 200 rpm for 2 h. The supernatant was decanted. This process was repeated three times to remove low-molecular-weight compounds such as pigments, proteins, and lipids. The residue was then incubated overnight with 200 mL of 96% ethanol under the same conditions. After decantation, the treated material was dried at 35°C and stored at 4°C until extraction.

Extraction of agar-type polysaccharides

Crude polysaccharides were extracted following a modified method of Premarathna et al.46 Purified seaweed powder (17 g) was suspended in 600 mL of sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM, pH 9) at 25°C and stirred (200 rpm) for 20 h. Insoluble residues (SR1) were removed by centrifugation (17,000×g, 10 min, 25°C), and the supernatant was filtered (40–100 μm porous glass). Polysaccharides were precipitated by adding three volumes of ethanol (96% v/v), followed by centrifugation (12,000×g, 10 min, 25°C) to obtain Fraction 1A. Fraction 1B was obtained by adding NaCl (final concentration 50 mM) to the solution (ethanol–phosphate buffer mixture) prior to centrifugation. Both fractions (1A, 1B) were designated as cold extracts. The SR1 residue was subsequently extracted under reflux in 600 mL of sodium phosphate buffer (20 mM, pH 9) at 95°C for 2 h. After centrifugation (17,000×g, 10 min, 50°C), the supernatant was filtered, and polysaccharides were precipitated with ethanol as described above to obtain Fraction 2A. Fraction 2B was obtained through the addition of NaCl (final concentration 50 mM). Re-extraction of the second residue (SR2) under identical conditions produced Fractions 3A and 3B. Fractions 2A, 2B, 3A, and 3B were designated as hot extracts.

Purification of crude extracts

All six polysaccharide fractions were purified by dialysis in deionized water using cellulose membrane tubing (MWCO 14 kDa; Sigma-Aldrich, D9527). Dialysis was performed for three days at room temperature, with water replaced twice per day. To convert samples into their sodium salt form, dialyzed fractions were passed through an Amberlite® IR-120 (Na+) ion-exchange column (1.6 × 60 cm, flow rate 10 mL/min). The eluates were concentrated by rotary evaporation (40°C), freeze-dried, and stored at 4°C until use.

Physicochemical and structural analysis

Molecular weight distribution by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC)

Molecular weight distributions were determined by high-performance size-exclusion chromatography (HPLC-SEC) on a Shimadzu system (LC-30AD pump, CBM-20A controller, RID-10A refractive index detector, CTO-20AC column oven) controlled by LabSolutions software. Chromatographic separations were carried out at 60°C on a series of Shodex OHpak columns (SB-G guard column followed by two SB-806MHQ analytical columns, 300 × 8 mm) using 0.1 M NaNO3 (pre-filtered, 0.22 μm) as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. Polysaccharide samples (0.05% w/v in 0.1 M NaNO3) were dissolved by heating in a boiling water bath for ∼2 min with stirring, filtered (0.2–0.22 μm cellulose acetate filter), cooled to 60–65°C, and injected (100 μL). The weight-average molecular weight (Mw) and polydispersity index (PI) were calculated using the LabSolutions GPC software, calibrated with pullulan standards (0.342–2400 kDa).

Functional group analysis by Fourier-Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

FTIR spectra were acquired on a Nicolet iS50 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Samples were analyzed at room temperature in absorption mode across 400–4000 cm-1 with 64 scans. Data were processed using Omnic software (v9.2).

Structural elucidation by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy

To reduce viscosity, aqueous polysaccharide solutions (0.2% w/v) were homogenized by sonication (Qsonica Q700, microtip, 60–80 W, 30 min) and freeze-dried. To measure nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra, samples (1.5% w/v) were dissolved in D2O and transferred to 5-mm NMR tubes. 10 mM sodium 2,2-dimethyl-2-silapentane-5-sulfonate (DSS) was added to each sample as an internal reference standard. All 1H and 13C chemical shifts were referenced to internal DSS (0.00 ppm for 1H, -1.6 ppm for 13C). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded at 65°C on a 500 MHz Agilent DD2 spectrometer (500 MHz for 1H) equipped with an inverse broadband probe, with 20 min of temperature equilibration time applied before acquisition. Standard one-dimensional (1D) 1H and 13C NMR spectra were acquired using the simple pulse-acquire experiment, with a 90° pulse, relaxation delay (d1) = 25 s and 64 scans (1H) or 45° pulse, d1 = 2.5 s and 40,000 scans (13C, with 1H decoupling). Two-dimensional experiments (COSY, HSQC) were performed with standard parameters. All data were processed and analyzed using MestreNova software (v14.2.2).

Compositional analysis

The chemical composition of the polysaccharide fractions was determined using established assays, including total sugar content quantified by the phenol–sulfuric acid method94; sulfate content measured using a modified BaCl2 method95; phenolic content assessed by the Folin–Ciocalteau assay96; and protein content determined with the BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Absorbance for all colorimetric assays was measured on a Shimadzu UV-1800 spectrophotometer under optimized conditions.

In vitro study

Cytotoxicity assessment (MTT assay)

The cytotoxicity of GC fractions was quantitatively assessed using an MTT assay. Cells (RAW264.7, HDF, HaCaT, B16-F10) were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. At ∼80% confluency, cells were treated with a concentration range of GC polysaccharide fractions dissolved in Milli-Q water (RAW264.7: 0.01–0.5 μg/μL; HDF/HaCaT: 0.02–1 μg/μL; B16-F10: 0.01–1 μg/μL) for 24 h. Following incubation, cells were washed with PBS and incubated with MTT reagent (0.05 mg/mL) for 4 h. The resulting formazan crystals were solubilized, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG LABTECH). Cell viability was expressed as a percentage of untreated controls, and the cytotoxic concentration (CC50) was determined as the concentration yielding 50% viability.

Cell proliferation (AlamarBlue assay)

The influence of agar-type polysaccharide on cellular metabolic activity, an indicator of proliferation, was evaluated using the alamarBlue assay. Cells were seeded at 2 × 104 cells/well, treated with specified concentrations of GC polysaccharide fractions (RAW264.7: 0.01 and 0.5 μg/μL; HDF, HaCaT, B16-F10: 0.01 μg/μL) for 24 h, and then incubated with alamarBlue reagent for 4 h. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm and 630 nm. Lipopolysaccharide (100 ng/mL) and mitomycin-C (0.04 μg/μL) served as positive and negative controls for proliferation, respectively. Results are presented as proliferation percentage relative to untreated controls.

Cell migration assays

The migration capacity of cells was evaluated by two complementary in vitro wound healing assays. For HaCaT keratinocytes, a standard scratch assay was performed. Confluent monolayers in 48-well plates were scratched with a sterile pipette tip, and after washing, the cells were treated with GC polysaccharide extracts (0.02 μg/μL). Wound closure was monitored at 0, 24, and 48 h post-scratch using live-cell imaging (Carl Zeiss Microscopy), and the reduction in wound area was quantified relative to time zero. To provide a more standardized and quantitative assessment of cell migration in both human dermal fibroblasts (HDF) and murine melanoma (B16-F10) cells, an exclusion assay was performed using an ibidi culture-insert system. When tested, cells were grown to confluence within the inserts; upon their removal, a defined cell-free gap was observed. Following treatment with GC extracts, wound closure was tracked at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. This method allowed for the precise calculation of wound closure percentage, as well as detailed morphometric parameters including wound area, perimeter, Feret diameter, and circularity using ImageJ software (NIH).

Anti-inflammatory activity (griess assay)

The anti-inflammatory potential of GC extracts was determined by measuring their ability to inhibit LPS-induced nitric oxide (NO) production in RAW264.7 macrophages. Cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were pre-stimulated with LPS (10 μg/mL) for 1 h and subsequently treated with GC extract (final concentration 0.01 μg/μL) for 24 h. Accumulated nitrite, a stable oxidative end product of NO, in the culture supernatant was quantified using a Griess reagent kit (Sigma-Aldrich) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm using OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, San Diego, USA), and nitrite concentration was interpolated from a sodium nitrite standard curve.

Phagocytic activity (neutral red uptake assay)

Macrophage phagocytic function was assessed via a neutral red uptake assay. RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells/well) were treated with GC extract (0.01 μg/μL) or LPS (25 μg/μL, positive control) for 24 h. Cells were then incubated with neutral red solution (0.075%) for 2 h. After washing to remove non-internalized dye, the phagocytized dye was extracted with an acetic acid-ethanol solution (1:50, v/v), and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, San Diego, USA). Increased absorbance correlates directly with enhanced phagocytic capacity.

Intracellular antioxidant activity (SOD assay)

The ability of GC extracts to modulate intracellular antioxidant defense was evaluated by measuring SOD activity in HDF cells. Treated cells (1 × 105 cells/well, 0.01 μg/μL GC extract, 24 h) were lysed, and SOD activity in the lysate was quantified using a commercial SOD assay kit (Sigma-Aldrich), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using OPTIMA microplate reader (BMG LABTECH, San Diego, USA), with activity expressed relative to untreated controls.

Cell painting assay for cytotoxicity screening

HDF cell were seeded into 96-well microplates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Following the adhesion period, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the G. corticata polysaccharide fraction GC-1A at a test concentration of 0.5 μg/μL. The treated cells were then incubated for an additional 24 h. Subsequently, cells were stained according to the protocol of the PhenoVue™ Cell Painting Kit (PerkinElmer), which utilizes a suite of fluorescent probes to simultaneously label multiple organelles. Fluorescence images were acquired using a ZEISS Axiovert 5 microscope equipped with a 40× objective. Live cells were stained with the following fluorescent markers: PhenoVue Hoechst 33342 (nuclear stain), PhenoVue Fluor 488 – Concanavalin A, Phalloidin, PhenoVue 512 (nucleic acid stain), PhenoVue Fluor 555 – WGA, PhenoVue Fluor 568, and PhenoVue 641 (mitochondrial stain). This morphological analysis was performed to evaluate the potential cytotoxic effects and bioactivities of the polysaccharides on cellular structures, providing insight into their interactions with and responses of HDF cells.

Anticoagulant activity assay (aPTT)

The in vitro anticoagulant activity of GC polysaccharides was evaluated by the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) assay. Polysaccharide extracts were mixed with human plasma at a 1:9 v/v ratio. aPTT was measured using a semi-automatic coagulometer (Erba Mannheim ECL 412) by incubating 50 μL of the plasma-extract mixture with 50 μL of Erba Actime reagent at 37°C, followed by the addition of CaCl2. The time to clot formation was recorded. Heparin (1–40 μg/mL) was used as a reference standard to calculate anticoagulant activity, as previously described.49

Gene expression analysis by quantitative PCR (qPCR)

HaCaT cells were treated with GC extracts (fraction 2A), LPS (positive control), mitomycin-C (negative control), or left non-treated (NT). Total RNA was isolated, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript™ IV CellsDirect™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Scientific). Quantitative PCR was performed using PowerTrack™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix on a 20 μL reaction volume with gene-specific primers for Human TGF-ß1, Human IL-8, Human TNF-α and Human MMP-1 and (β-actin - housekeeping gene; see Table S1). The thermocycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min; 45 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 56–60°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 30 s; followed by a melting curve analysis. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2(-ΔΔCt) method, normalized to β-actin and expressed relative to the NT control group.

Metabolomic profiling of amino acids and fatty acids in response to polysaccharide treatment

Cell culture and treatment for metabolomic sampling

HDF, B16-F10, and RAW264.7 cells were plated in 6-well plates at an initial density of 2×105 cells per well and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37°C, 5% CO2). Following adherence, the medium was replaced with 500 μL of fresh DMEM supplemented with either GC-1A or GC-2A polysaccharide extracts at final concentration of 0.02 μg/μL, or an equal volume of Milli-Q water as a non-treated (NT) control. After an additional 24-hour incubation, cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove extracellular metabolites, gently scraped, pelleted by centrifugation, and stored at -80°C until further processing. To ensure that metabolomic comparisons, cell pellets were first normalized to equal cell number prior to metabolite extraction. Specifically, after treatment, cells were detached by trypsinization, stained with trypan blue (0.4% w/v), and counted using a hemocytometer. An equal number of viable cells (2 × 106) from each treatment group (including non-treated control) were then pelleted by centrifugation (300 × g, 5 min, 4°C) and stored at –80°C until further processing.

Intracellular amino acid extraction and derivatization

Intracellular amino acids were isolated following a modified protocol adapted from Alttoa et al.97 Cell pellets were washed with PBS, centrifuged (1,500 rpm, 5 min, 4°C), and resuspended in 250 μL of ultrapure water containing 5-methyl-DL-tryptophan as an internal standard. Metabolite release was achieved by sonication for 20 min. Proteins were precipitated by sequential addition of 250 μL methanol and 500 μL chloroform, followed by centrifugation (2,000 rpm, 10 min). The upper aqueous phase was collected, dried under a gentle nitrogen stream at 50°C, and reconstituted in 50 μL ultrapure water. Derivatization for LC-MS was carried out according to Oldekop et al.98 by reacting 50 μL of extract with 75 μL of diethyl ethoxymethylenemalonate (DEEMM) reagent (1:50 v/v in methanol) and 175 μL of borate buffer (pH 9.0), followed by overnight incubation in the dark at room temperature.

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis

Amino acid profiling was conducted using an Agilent 6230 Q-TOF mass spectrometer coupled to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II LC system. Separation was achieved on an Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C18 column (3.0 mm × 50 mm, 2.7 μm) maintained at 25°C. Mobile phase A consisted of water/acetonitrile (95:5 v/v) with 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium acetate; mobile phase B was acetonitrile/water (95:5 v/v) with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient elution program was: 0–5 min, 90% A to 73% A; 5–12 min, 73% A; 12–14 min, 73% A to 0% A; 14–17 min, 0% A; 17–20 min, return to initial conditions. Flow rate was 0.5 mL/min with an injection volume of 5 μL. Electrospray Ionization (ESI) was operated in positive-ion mode with the following source parameters: drying gas, 10 L/min at 325°C; nebulizer pressure, 480 kPa; capillary voltage, 4000 V. Compound identity was confirmed by comparison of retention times and spectral characteristics with analytical standards (α-alanine, β-alanine, isoleucine, leucine, and tryptophan etc.).

Fatty acid profiling by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

Fatty acid composition in polysaccharide-treated cells was analyzed by GC–MS using the method described by Premarathna et al.99 Lipids were extracted from frozen cell pellets using a chloroform: methanol (2:1, v/v) mixture. Samples were homogenized with 250 μL methanol and 500 μL chloroform, vortexed for 90 s, and sonicated at 40°C for 30 min. After addition of 200 μL 2% NaCl solution, phases were separated by centrifugation (1,000 × g, 10 min). The organic layer was collected, dried under nitrogen at 35°C, and subjected to fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) derivatization using 150 μL of 5% sulfuric acid in methanol at 50°C for 1 h with periodic shaking. FAMEs were extracted into hexane, dried under nitrogen, and redissolved in 100 μL hexane for analysis. GC-MS was performed on a Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 Ultra system equipped with a Phenomenex ZB-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm). Helium was used as carrier gas at a linear velocity of 30 cm/s. Injection volume was 1 μL in split mode (1:100). Oven temperature program: initial 160°C, ramp at 2.5°C/min to 260°C, then 5°C/min to 298°C, hold for 15 min. Fatty acids were identified by comparison with commercial standards (PUFA-2, Sigma 47015; 37 FAME Mix, Supelco CRM47885) and reported as relative mass percentages of total identified fatty acids.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0.2 for MacOS (San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the effects of polysaccharides on cellular responses, including viability, proliferation, migration, NO production, and phagocytosis. Data were analyzed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate for the experimental design, followed by Dunnett’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons to the control group. All in vitro experiments were performed with four technical replicates per condition within each experiment. All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), where “n” represents the number of independent technical replicates. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, with asterisks denoting significance levels: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.
