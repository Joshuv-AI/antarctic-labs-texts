Deciphering the metabolic patterns of cashew apple ripening process: A comprehensive non-targeted metabolomics analysis
Authors: Haijie Huang, Li Zhao, Weijian Huang, Xuejie Feng, Fuchu Hu, Ya Zhao, Huiliang Li, Yunlu Peng, Yuhan Wang, Zhongrun Zhang, Yijun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolite changes during the ripening process of cashew apples are crucial for their quality development. A total of 2379 metabolites were isolated and identified from fresh cashew apples at four different ripening stages using UHPLC-MS. Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) revealed that the differential metabolites in CA2_vs_CA1, CA3_vs_CA2, and CA4_vs_CA3 comparisons were mainly enriched in amino acids and peptides, steroids, pyrimidines, and fatty acids and conjugates, etc. Volcano plot analysis identified 631, 384, and 392 upregulated metabolites, and 625, 923, and 392 downregulated metabolites in CA2_vs_CA1, CA3_vs_CA2, and CA4_vs_CA3 comparisons, respectively. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated that these differential metabolites were primarily involved in aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, purine metabolism, and glycine, etc. Notably, the differential metabolites in CA4_vs_CA3 showed the highest enrichment in d-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism, as well as phenylalanine. The metabolic profile of cashew apples revealed stage-specific patterns during ripening, offering key insights for optimizing harvest, storage, and processing.

Introduction

The cashew apple, which develops from the swollen pedicel, comprises the bulk of the cashew fruit's biomass, representing in excess of 90% of its total weight. With remarkable versatility, the cashew apple can be eaten in its natural state or converted into an array of processed commodities. Nutritionally rich, the cashew apple contains 87.8% water, 11.6% carbohydrates, 0.2% protein and significant amounts of essential nutrients, including 2.52 g/kg of vitamin C, 100 mg/kg calcium, 100 mg/kg phosphorus, 2 mg/kg iron and 900 mg/g β-carotene, along with trace amounts of vitamins A, B1 and B2 (Wang, Liang, Huang, Zhang, & Huang, 2010). Notably, the vitamin content of cashew apple juice (203.5 mg/100 mL) significantly exceeds that of orange juice (54.7 mg/100 mL), mango juice (30.9 mg/100 mL) and pineapple juice (14.7 mg/100 mL) (Akinwale, 2000). These nutritional properties, combined with its unique flavor profile, make the cashew apple particularly promising for the development of various food products, including juices and alcoholic beverages (Angela, Edna, & Neema, 2023; Kamble, Pawar, Khanvilkar, Kadam, & Kasture, 2024).

Metabolites are direct products of plant growth, development, and environmental responses, with over 200,000 identified, including primary metabolites (organic acids, amino acids, carbohydrates, lipids), secondary metabolites (flavonoids, alkaloids, terpenoids), and phytohormones (Cárdenas et al., 2019; Chiocchio, Mandrone, Tomasi, Marincich, & Poli, 2021; Huang et al., 2025). Primary metabolites support basic metabolic processes, while secondary metabolites are vital for stress adaptation (Pandey, Ramegowda, & Senthil-Kumar, 2015; Raza et al., 2022). Their biosynthesis occurs via pathways like shikimate, phenylpropanoid, and mevalonate (Jan, Asaf, Numan and Lubna, & Kim, K.-M., 2021). Under optimal environmental conditions, the concentration of secondary metabolites in plant tissues remains relatively low (Khare et al., 2020). Under stress, secondary metabolites accumulate, protecting cellular components from oxidative damage (Anna, Nicolas, & Paweł, 2015). For example, flavonoids regulate auxin transport and ROS scavenging (Diana et al., 2008; Hernández, Alegre, Breusegem, & Munné-Bosch, 2008; Peer & Murphy, 2007), while glucosinolates modulate drought responses and circadian rhythms (Jensen et al., 2015; Katz et al., 2015; Salehin et al., 2019; Zhao, Zhang, Stanley, & Assmann, 2008). Phytohormones act as signaling molecules, coordinating stress responses (Alamri, Ali, Khan, Singh, & Siddiqui, 2020; Lal et al., 2018). The metabolic network in plants comprises numerous compounds with stage-specific functions that collectively sustain physiological processes. Characterization of temporal metabolic changes in developing cashew apples offers fundamental understanding of its ripening biochemistry.

As an essential component of systems biology, metabolomics utilizes high-resolution analytical platforms such as NMR spectroscopy, mass spectrometry (both standalone and chromatographically-coupled variants) to systematically characterize the complete set of low-molecular-weight metabolites and their dynamic transformations in biological systems under defined physiological conditions. (Chen, Yao, Zeng, & Zhang, 2023; Khakimov et al., 2016, and Liu et al., 2023). Recent advances in metabolomics have significantly enhanced our understanding of fruit ripening processes. Using metabolomics, 466 metabolites across three ripening stages of pineapple were analyzed, and the ratio of monounsaturated to saturated fatty acids was found to significantly decrease during ripening, revealing lipid metabolism mechanisms (Hong et al., 2021). The critical roles of flavonols, flavonoids, and phenylpropanoids in pineapple fruit yellowing were elucidated (Song et al., 2023). The relationship between banana fruit firmness and hemicellulose/xylan content was investigated, with xylanase activity shown to negatively correlate with firmness, clarifying the role of cell wall hemicellulose in ripening (Yi et al., 2024). Genotype-dependent accumulation patterns of carotenoids in bananas were demonstrated, where soluble sugars were observed to increase postharvest, while amino acids and TCA cycle-derived organic acids exhibited genotype-specific trends (Sheng et al., 2023). Twenty-six anthocyanins in cashew apple peel were identified, with pelargonidin-3-O-galactoside and two other compounds confirmed as primary contributors to red pigmentation (Huang et al., 2024). The study reveals fundamental biochemical processes underlying anthocyanin production in cashew apples, offering new molecular perspectives.

Despite these advancements, the metabolic changes occurring during different ripening stages of cashew apples remain poorly understood. Therefore, this study aims to employ non-targeted metabolomics technology to systematically analyze metabolite profiles across four developmental stages of cashew apples, with the objective of resolving stage-specific metabolic pathway differences. This research will provide a scientific foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying cashew fruit development and offer valuable insights for improving the nutritional value and flavor quality of this economically important fruit.

Materials and methods

Materials

Sample collection was conducted at the Ministry-certified cashew germplasm bank in tropical Hainan Province, representing China's most comprehensive collection of cashew genetic resources maintained for agricultural research purposes. The selected cultivar, B7, was sampled at four distinct developmental stages: initial fruiting (CA1), medium fruiting (CA2), expansion (CA3), and ripening (CA4). The samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen immediately after collection and subsequently lyophilized at −40 °C using a freeze-dryer, in order to preserve their metabolic integrity. Each sample was set up with three biological replicates, and the weight of each sample group exceeded 10 g.

Extraction of metabolites from cashew apples

Metabolite extraction was performed following the method of Wang et al. (2025) and Liu, Qian, et al. (2023) with appropriate modifications. Briefly, approximately 2 g of frozen cashew apples were homogenized using a ball mill with steel grinding balls. From the resulting powder, 50 mg aliquots were precisely weighed and transferred to 2 mL microcentrifuge tubes. Each sample was then mixed with 500 μL of 75% (v/v) methanol-water solution and subjected to ultrasonic extraction at room temperature for 30 min. The extracts were subsequently centrifuged at 17,000 ×g for 10 min at 20 °C. The supernatant was carefully collected and transferred to fresh 2 mL vials for storage at −20 °C until further analysis.

Detection of metabolites from cashew apples

Chromatographic conditions

The determination of metabolites in cashew apple was performed by ultra-high pressure liquid chromatography (H-Class, Waters) coupled to high resolution mass spectrometry (6600 QTOF, AB SCIEX), following the method of Liu et al. (2023). Four μL of cashew apple metabolite samples were analyzed in the presence of mobile phases A (0.1% (v/v) formic acid aqueous solution) and B (0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min through an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters) at a column temperature of 40 °C. The separation was performed on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (1.8 μm 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters) at a flow rate of 400 μL/min. The mobile phase was delivered at 400 μL/min employing the following gradient profile: initial 95% A (1 min), 95–90% A (1.5 min), 90–60% A (5.5 min), 60–5% A (8 min, hold 1 min), rapid re-equilibration to 95% A (0.1 min), followed by column stabilization (2.9 min).

Mass spectrometry conditions

High-resolution mass spectrometric analysis was conducted on an AB Sciex 6600 QTOF system (AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA) operating in data-dependent acquisition mode. The instrument acquired survey scans (m/z 50–1200) followed by MS/MS fragmentation of precursor ions exceeding intensity threshold of 100 counts. Optimal ESI conditions were established with: GS1/GS2 gas flow at 60 psi, curtain gas 35 psi, source temperature 550 °C, and ion spray voltage ±5.5 kV (positive/negative polarity switching). Collision-induced dissociation was performed at 30 eV with 15 MS/MS spectra acquired per cycle.

Data processing and analysis

Mass spectrometry data acquisition

Data analysis was conducted on a high-resolution quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX TripleTOF 6600) equipped with Analyst TF software (version 1.7). Full-scan MS data were acquired in the range of m/z 50–1200, with dynamic exclusion set to 15 s. For MS/MS acquisition, the top 5 most intense precursor ions exceeding 100 counts were selected for fragmentation at 30 eV collision energy.

Mass spectrometry data analysis

Data processing workflow included: (1) .abf format conversion via AnalysisBaseFileConverter, (2) peak processing (detection, alignment) using MSDIAL 4.6, and (3) metabolite identification through combined MS1/MS2 spectral matching against four major databases (Metlin, MassBank, MoNA, HMDB) implemented in MSDIAL's v6.0 search engine. The MSDIAL software set the parameters as follows: in the identification module, the accurate mass tolerance (MS1) was 0.01 Da, and (MS2) was 0.05 Da. In the identification module, a score threshold of 60% was set as the cut-off criterion. For the alignment module, the retention time tolerance was established at 0.2 min, while the mass tolerance (MS1) was maintained at 0.01 Da.

Statistical analysis and graphing of data

Univariate statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS Statistics software (version 22.0, IBM Corp.). Metabolite concentration differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA with Fisher's LSD post-hoc testing at the 95% confidence level. Principal component analysis and data visualization were conducted using Metware Cloud platform (https://cloud.metware.cn), with unit variance scaling applied to all variables prior to analysis.

The physical characteristics of cashew apples at four developmental stages (initial fruiting (CA1), medium fruiting (CA2), expansion (CA3), and ripening (CA4) were illustrated in Fig. 1(a). The developmental progression followed a distinct temporal pattern: CA1 stage transitions to CA2 after 8–10 days, followed by another 8–10 days for progression to the crucial expansion phase (CA3), during which significant growth occurs. Subsequently, CA3 stage developed into the fully ripened CA4 stage within 5–8 days. As shown in Fig. 1(d), the levels of violaceol I, 3-O-neohesperidoside quercetin and tiliroside were higher during the CA1 stage. This study provided a data-driven reference for distinguishing between cashew apples at different stages of maturity.

Analysis of differential metabolite species enrichment during ripening of cashew apples

The Metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) profiles and comprehensive metabolite compositions of cashew apples across different developmental stages were presented in Fig. 2(a) and Table 1, respectively. The analysis revealed distinct metabolic patterns at each transition phase: CA2_vs_CA1 stage exhibited significant enrichment in amino acids and peptides (37 species), steroids (20), pyrimidines (9), fatty acids and conjugates (26), indoles (6), benzoic acids (7), purines (7), aromatic heteropolycyclic compounds (5), monosaccharides (7), and flavonoids (31). CA3_vs_CA2 stage showed predominant enrichment in amino acids and peptides (33), steroids (20), TCA cycle acids (6), benzenes (11), fatty acids and conjugates (30), benzamides (21), pyrimidines (8), cinnamic acids (5), monosaccharides (7), and glycosyl compounds (4). CA4_vs_CA3 stage demonstrated notable enrichment in amino acids and peptides (31), pyrimidines (7), fatty acids and conjugates (22), benzenes (8), benzamides (15), monosaccharides (6), glycosyl compounds (4), cinnamic acids (4), flavonoids (24), and beta-lactams (4). The most variable metabolite category across all developmental stages was amino acids and peptides, followed by steroids, fatty acids and conjugates, pyrimidines, and benzenes. This prominence of amino acids and peptides could be attributed to their fundamental roles as protein building blocks, essential for synthesizing new proteins, maintaining cellular structure and function, and participating in various metabolic processes. Amino acid metabolism was intricately linked with energy and carbohydrate metabolism, carbon‑nitrogen balance, hormone regulation, secondary metabolism, and stress responses (Kensuke, Hiromitsu, Ali, & Yokota, 2023), explaining their significant variations during cashew apple ripening. Steroid hormones, synthesized from steroid precursors, played crucial regulatory roles in plant growth and developed by modulating cellular growth, division, differentiation, and developmental processes (Ma et al., 2025). Screening and analysis of differential metabolites during the maturation of cashew apples

In order to further investigate the metabolic variations of cashew apples across different developmental stages, differential metabolites are identified based on the criteria of ∣log2FC∣ ≥ 1, p-value <0.5, and VIP value ≥1. The volcano plot of cashew apple in different growth stages was presented in Fig. 3(a), while the box plot of the top four differential metabolites was shown in Fig. 3(b). From Fig. 3(a), Fig. 3(b), and Table S3, a total of 1256 differential metabolites were identified during the CA2_vs_CA1 stage. Among these, 631 metabolites were up-regulated, including lovastatin acid (mevinolinic acid), agaric acid, celastrol, myricitrin, and testosterone propionate. Conversely, 625 metabolites were down-regulated, such as epicatechin, hydroquinone, 4-pyrimidine methoamine (hydrochloride), histidine, and glutathione (GSH). The transition from the rapid growth phase (CA1) to the functional differentiation phase (CA2) was accompanied by significant changes in metabolic pathways. Epicatechin, a precursor of proanthocyanidins and flavonoids (Lepiniec et al., 2006), exhibited a notable decrease in concentration. This reduction might be attributed to the decline in phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity during cell differentiation, which likely impeded epicatechin synthesis. Additionally, histidine might be preferentially utilized in protein synthesis or other metabolic pathways, while the consumption rate of GSH might surpass its synthesis rate, leading to a decline in its content (Pratelli & Pilot, 2014).Fig. 3(a), and (b) represent the volcano plot and box plot of differential metabolites (Top 4) at different growth stages of cashew apples.Fig. 3

According to Fig. 3(a), Fig. 3(b), and Table S4, a total of 1307 differential metabolites were identified during the CA3_vs_CA2 stage in cashew apples. Among these, 384 metabolites, including cholic acid, 5-trans U-44069, sanguinarine, zileuton, and cyanidin-3-O-arabinoside, were up-regulated. Conversely, 923 metabolites, such as pyroglutamyl-isoleucine, cypridinid oxyluciferin, betaine, (Z)-5,8,11-trihydroxyoctadec-9-enoic acid, and tricetin, were down-regulated. Notably, the pigment-related metabolites cyanidin-3-O-arabinoside and tricetin exhibited contrasting trends. This divergence might be attributed to the rapid accumulation of tricetin during the CA1 to CA3 stages, followed by its significant consumption in the CA4 stage as a precursor for the synthesis of pigments such as cyanidin-3-O-arabinoside. Consequently, the substantial accumulation of cyanidin-3-O-arabinoside likely contributed to the red coloration of the fruit peel (Ge et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2024).

According to Fig. 3(a), Fig. 3(b), and Table S5, a total of 784 differential metabolites was identified during the CA4_vs_CA3 stage in cashew apples. Among these, 392 metabolites, including 1-O-trans-cinnamoyl-beta-d-glucopyranose, andrimid, Me-STA3C, raclopride, and phenylbutazone, were up-regulated. Conversely, 392 metabolites, such as nandrolone, stearidonic acid, 6-hydroxy-5-acetoxy aureol, cypridinid oxyluciferin, and 6-methoxyquinoline, were down-regulated. Stearidonic acid, a fatty acid involved in the biosynthesis and functional maintenance of cell membranes in cashew apples (Dussert et al., 2013), exhibited an accumulation trend during the CA1 to CA3 stages due to increased demand for membrane synthesis. However, its secretion decreased in the CA4 stage as the fruit reached maturity, reducing the requirement for stearidonic acid. Similarly, the quinoline derivative 6-methoxyquinoline, which served as a precursor in pigment-related metabolic pathways (Tanaka, Sasaki, & Ohmiya, 2008), accumulated rapidly during the CA1 to CA3 stages but was significantly consumed in the CA4 stage as the fruit peel turned red due to pigment synthesis.

Analysis of metabolic pathways of differential metabolites during the maturation of cashew apples

The enrichment results (Top 25) of different metabolic pathways in cashew apples during the CA2_vs_CA1, CA3_vs_CA2, and CA4_vs_CA3 stages were presented in Fig. 4(a), (b), and (c), respectively. As depicted in Fig. 4(a) and Table S6, the differential metabolites in the CA2_vs_CA1 stage were predominantly enriched in several metabolic pathways, including Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (14 differential metabolites), purine metabolism (12 differential metabolites), glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism (8 differential metabolites), β-Alanine metabolism (6 differential metabolites), and steroid hormone biosynthesis (13 differential metabolites). Among these 55 metabolic pathways, Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis exhibited the most significant differences, with metabolites such as L-asparagine, L-histidine, L-phenylalanine, l-glutamine, glycine, L-aspartic acid, L-methionine, l-lysine, L-isoleucine, L-threonine, L-tryptophan, L-proline, L-glutamic acid, and tetrahydro folic acid being identified. Notably, the enrichment rate of d-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism was the highest. During fruit ripening, metabolic pathways involving d-glutamine and D-glutamate were responsible for aminotransferase and deamination reactions. These reactions affected the dynamic balance of free amino acids, providing energy for ripening and enhancing the fruit's antioxidant defense system while delaying oxidative damage (Jin et al., n.d.; Lea & Miflin, 2003). According to Fig. 4(b) and Table S7, the differential metabolites in the CA3_vs_CA2 stage were primarily enriched in several metabolic pathways, including alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism (9 differential metabolites), the citrate cycle (TCA cycle) (7 differential metabolites), aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (10 differential metabolites), phenylalanine metabolism (3 differential metabolites), tyrosine and tryptophan biosynthesis (7 differential metabolites), and glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism (3 differential metabolites). Among these 58 metabolic pathways, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism exhibited the most significant differences, with metabolites such as L-asparagine, argininosuccinic acid, semialdehyde, L-glutamic acid, citric acid, oxaloacetic acid, pyruvic acid, succinic acid, and oxoglutaric acid being identified. Notably, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis showed the highest enrichment rates. This metabolic pathway, in which phenylalanine was converted into compounds such as flavonoids, lignans and anthocyanins, had a direct effect on fruit color, antioxidant capacity and disease resistance. It was also closely related to the balance of sugars and acids in fruit, the formation of aromas and the quality of fruit after harvest (Hong et al., 2025). For instance, the activation of phenylpropane metabolism during the ripening of tomatoes lead to the accumulation of anthocyanins and promotes a change in the color of the fruit skin (Zhao et al., 2024). This explains why the fruit changed from green to light red at the CA3 stage shown in Fig. 1(a).

According to Fig. 4(c) and Table S8, the differential metabolites in the CA4_vs_CA3 stage are predominantly enriched in several metabolic pathways, including aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, and the pentose phosphate pathway. Among these 50 metabolic pathways, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis exhibited the most significant differences, with metabolites such as L-asparagine, L-histidine, L-phenylalanine, l-glutamine, glycine, L-aspartic acid, L-methionine, l-lysine, L-leucine, L-threonine, L-tryptophan, L-tyrosine, and L-proline being identified. Notably, d-glutamine and D-glutamate metabolism showed the highest enrichment rates.

Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis ensured the accurate attachment of amino acids to tRNA through amino acid activation and aminoacyl transfer, serving as the foundation for protein synthesis (Zhao et al., 2022). Alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism played crucial roles in nitrogen assimilation, energy metabolism, stress response, and secondary metabolism, enabling plants to adapt to environmental changes and sustain normal growth and development (Liu et al., 2024). However, significant differences in these metabolic pathways were observed across the four distinct ripening stages of cashew apples. This indicated that amino acid synthesis-related metabolism was highly active during the ripening process of cashew apples, continuously providing essential nutrients to support the growth of cashew kernels.

Conclusion

A total of 2379 metabolites were isolated and identified from cashew apples at four distinct maturity stages using UHPLC-MS. The analysis revealed 631 up-regulated and 625 down-regulated differential metabolites during the CA2_vs_CA1 stage in this study. These metabolites were primarily distributed across 55 metabolic pathways, with Aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis exhibiting the most significant differences. During the CA3_vs_CA2 stage, 384 metabolites were up-regulated and 923 were down-regulated, predominantly distributed in 58 metabolic pathways. Among these, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism showed the most significant differences. In the CA4_vs_CA3 stage, 392 metabolites were up-regulated and 392 were down-regulated, mainly distributed across 50 metabolic pathways, with aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis displaying the most significant differences.

The differential metabolites identified at different maturity stages of cashew apples were primarily associated with amino acids and peptides, steroids, fatty acids and conjugates, pyrimidines, and benzenes. These metabolites were found to be closely related to peel color changes, fatty acid synthesis, and amino acid synthesis during the ripening process, providing a scientific basis for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying cashew fruit development and improving its nutritional value and flavor quality. In future studies, integrating transcriptomics and proteomics approaches will be essential to identify key genes regulating amino acid synthesis, fatty acid synthesis, and peel color changes in cashew apples. This will facilitate the selection of cashew apple varieties tailored for specific uses and provide a scientific foundation for the development of new cashew apple varieties and related products.
