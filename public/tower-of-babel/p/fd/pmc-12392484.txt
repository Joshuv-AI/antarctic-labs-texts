Directed and efficient generation of calretinin interneurons from human pluripotent stem cells
Authors: Xuewei Du, Renli Qi, Congge Li, Wenpei Zhou, Shijiao Wang, Shenxing Wei, Runzhong Wu, Yu Yin, Tianqing Li, Xiaoqing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Background

Interneurons are interconnected with excitatory projection neurons to form neural circuits that regulate various physiological activities. Calretinin (CR) interneurons, which are specifically distributed in the human upper cortical layers (II/III), are generally hypothesized to be necessary for high-level brain functions unique to humans, such as abstract thinking and language [1–4]. Dysfunction of CR interneurons induces various neurological disorders, including epilepsy [5], schizophrenia [6]– [7], and autism [8]. Therefore, exploring their development is crucial for further understanding their function in neuroscience and for developing potential therapeutic strategies.

Calretinin (CR) interneurons represent a highly heterogeneous group comprised of bipolar and multipolar cells, accounting for approximately 8.0% (7.2–8.7%) of the total neuron population in each human cortical area, in contrast to 4% in the rat medial prefrontal cortex [1]. These interneurons belong to the category of accommodating or irregular-spiking interneurons, which primarily utilize γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) as a neurotransmitter to influence the activity of excitatory neurons. Originating ventrally, GABAergic-derived CR interneurons migrate to their target sites and form local synaptic connections with pyramidal neurons to modulate cortical circuitry [9]. Calretinin interneurons target the distal dendrites of pyramidal neurons and provide inhibitory control within neuronal networks [9–13]. Studies indicate that CR interneurons may be particularly vulnerable in conditions such as temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) [14]. Their susceptibility to excitotoxicity can lead to disruptions in inhibitory control, contributing to the hyper excitability characteristic of epilepsy. In summary, CR interneurons are vital components of the network within the brain that maintain neural homeostasis and facilitate complex cognitive functions by synchronizing neuronal activity and connecting with various cell types.

There has been tremendous interest in generating cortical CR interneurons from human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) for both the study of neural development and cell-based therapies. However, current research primarily focuses on generating GABAergic interneurons from hPSCs, which consist of a mix of multiple subtypes of interneurons. Furthermore, the generated GABAergic interneurons exhibit significant shortcomings: (1) The purity is low. The subtypes of GABA neurons generated in these studies are diverse, with CR interneurons accounting for less than 20% of the total population [15–17]. For example, some studies have reported that human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells) can be induced into functional GABAergic interneurons using the ventral inducible morphological molecule SHH. However, these interneurons were later identified as somatostatin (SST) and parvalbumin (PV) neurons but lacked CR interneurons [15, 18, 19]. (2) The differentiation protocol for interneuron generation is complex and time-consuming. Some studies have reported that it takes up to six months [16]. (3) Stable interneuron precursor cells cannot be obtained, which prohibits large-scale expansions. Because currently interneurons are generally induced from hPSCs that are unable to produce stable interneuron precursor cells [15, 19–21]. Therefore, efficiently generating a sufficient number of homogeneous CR interneurons remains a challenge.

In this study, we developed an efficient protocol that enables hPSCs to differentiate into approximately 80% functional CR interneurons through modulation of Shh and Wnt signaling pathways. When grafted into mouse brains, CR interneuron progenitors successfully integrate into host brains, differentiate into CR interneurons, and effectively reestablish specific patterns of synaptic structures. This protocol can be further applied to studying neuronal functions and developing potential therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

hPSC culture and neuroepthelial stem cell induction

Human cell line BG02 (Georgia Biobusiness Center)/ H9 (Rambam Medical Center) embryonic stem cells, and iPS cell line (h1, Institute of Primate Translational Medicine, Kunming University of Science and Technology) were maintained in MEF feeder cells [22], and subsequently induced into NESCs as previously described [23, 24]. The NESCs were cultured in a medium composed of Neurobasal media (Gbico) with 1xB27 (Gbico), 1xN2 (Gbico), 1xNEAA (Gbico), 1% Glutmax (Gbico), 3 µM of CHIR99021 (Selleck), 5 µM of SB431542 (Cellagentec), 10 ng/mL of bFGF (Healthgen Biotechnology), and 1000 U/mL of hLIF (Millipore) [25].

NESCs culture and GABA differentiations

Neuroepithelial stem cells were cultured in NESC culture medium for long-term maintain their identity in vitro. 0.025% trypsin (Gbico) was used to digest NESCs when passaging to encourage cell propagation. NESCs were routinely passaged at a ratio of 1:3 to 1:5 ratios every 3–4 days.

For differentiation, 4000 of NESCs were seeded into one well of 24-well plate in NESC culture medium in day 0, which was previously coated with Poly-L-ornithine (Sigma, 10 µl/well) and Laminin (Gibco, 5 µg/mL). In day 1, the NESC culture medium was replaced by the basic differentiation medium, which contained Neurobasal medium supplemented with 1xN2, 1xB27, 1xNEAA and 1% Glutmax. For ventral telencephalic lineage induction, firstly, 15 wells were selected and evenly divided into 5 groups. Each group were treated consistently with one of the five different concentrations of IWP-2 (Selleck) (0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 µM) during day1 to day 8, thus achieving the optimized concentration of IWP-2 for ventral telencephalic lineage induction. Then, 15 wells after day 0 were selected, added the optimized concentration of IWP-2 and evenly divided into 5 groups. Each group was treated consistently with one of the five different concentrations of SHH (Peprotech) (0, 5, 10, 15 and 20 ng/mL) during day1 to day 8, thus achieving the optimized concentration of SHH. Similar method was also used for five different concentrations of Pur (Sigma)(0,0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 µM) to achieve the optimized concentration of Pur. The optimized CR neuron-induced ISP medium contained basal differentiation medium, 1 μm IWP2, 15ng/ml SHH and 1.5 μm Pur. To obtain functional neurons, firstly, 10 ng/mL BDNF (Gibco) and 10 ng/mL GDNF (Gibco) were added into the medium at day 7 post-differentiation (pdD7) to promote terminal differentiation. Secondly, 2 × 103/cm2 mouse astrocytes, dissociated from the cortex of new-born mouse, were added into differentiated neurons in one well of 24-well plate at pdD12. Prior to co-culturing with neurons, astrocytes were cultured for three passages, and no mouse contaminated neurons were detected in the astrocytes by TUJ1 staining.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, washed with PBS for three times, and treated with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Gibco) for 30 min. Afterward, cells were washed as described above, and incubated with blocking buffer (10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) in PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. Following blocking, cells were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4℃, and then incubated with Alexa 488 or 594 Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen: donkey anti-rabbit, donkey anti-mouse, 1: 600) for two hours at room temperature. Next, cells were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma) and nuclei were visualized by DAPI staining (Sigma). All primary antibodies were described in followings. Primary antibodies were used at 1:200–1:2000: SOX1, goat (AF3369, R&D Systems); TUJ1, mouse (MAB1637, Millpore); NESTIN, mouse (MAB5326, Millpore); PAX6, rabbit (901301, Biolegend Covance); COUP-TF II, mouse (PP-H7147-00, R&D Systems); GFAP, rabbit (z033429-2, Dako); SST, mouse (sc-55565, Santa Cruz); BLBP, rabbit (ab27171, Abcam); MAP-2, rabbit (AB5622, Millpore); Parvalbumin, rabbit (P3088, Sigma); GABA, rabbit (a2052, Sigma); GABA, mouse (SAB4200721, Sigma); SMI312 mouse (SMI-312R-100, Covance); NeuN, rabbit (ABN78, Millpore); PSD95, mouse (ab2723, Abcam); Synapsin I, rabbit (S193, Sigma); Calretinin, rabbit (AB5054, Millpore); SOX2, rabbit (AB5603, Millpore); Ki-67, rabbit (PA5-19462, Invitrogen); VIP, mouse (sc-25347, Santa Curz) and GFP (ab13970, Abcam).

Transplantation and histology

This research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yunnan University (YNU20240907). All mouse experiments were performed in Yunnan University for animal welfare. Approximately 2–3 µL of trypsin-dissociated interneuron progenitors (iNPs) (5 × 10⁵ cells/µL) in PBS were injected into the ventricles of 16 newborn SCID mice (postnatal days 2–5) under anesthesia on ice (No other chemical anesthetics were used). 0.1% food dye was applied to confirm the injection sites. One to three months after transplantation, animals were euthanized by using CO2 at 20% chamber replacement rate and subsequent perfused with 4% PFA. Brains were then extracted, post-fixed in 4% PFA (2–3 days) and dehydrated sequentially in sucrose solution at the concentration of 10%, 20% and 30%, respectively. The transplanted sites were eventually sectioned on a cryostat microtome at 10 μm after embedding in O.C.T (Sakura-Finetek) and stained with GFP and the interneuron (CR, GABA) or astrocyte (S100B) antibodies. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Statistical analyses

All of experiments, including immunohistochemistry, were performed in triplicate. For neuron quantification, TUJ1 positive cells with a neuronal morphology (a circular, three-dimensional appearance that extended a thin process at least three times longer than their cell body) were quantified as neurons. Quantification was performed on randomly selected 5–8 pictures taken under Leica microscopy (Leica SP8 ) using the Leica software package (version LAS AF Lite) and at least one thousand cells were counted for every experiment. All quantifications were based on at least three independent experiments. Quantification data represented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d) using Microsoft Excel STDEV Function. The significance difference between two samples was evaluated by the unpaired two-sample Student’s t-test using Excel software. P < 0.05 was considered as statistical significant differences.

Processing of in-house scRNA-seq data

The scRNA-seq data of in vitro cultured human CR interneurons were processed using the ‘cellranger Count’ pipeline (version 3.1.0), with sequence reads aligned to the GRCh38 reference genome (version 3.1.0, downloaded from the 10x Genomics website) by STAR (version 2.7.2a). Quality control and clustering analysis were performed with Seurat V4 package in R (version 4.3.1). To exclude low-quality cells, filtering criteria were applied based on the number of detected genes (nGene) and the percentage of mitochondrial gene expression (Mito). High-quality cells were defined as those with between 1,200 and 10,000 expressed genes and mitochondrial gene expression less than 10%. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the normalized data, retaining the first 50 principal components for downstream clustering. Clustering was performed with a resolution parameter of 0.8, utilizing 40 nearest neighbors for manifold structure approximation, a minimum embedding distance of 0.35, and 36 random seed iterations. Visualization of the clustering results was enhanced using the SCP package for improved graphical representation.

Processing of externally downloaded scRNA-seq data

To compare with scRNA-seq data of cultured CR interneurons at D36, we analyzed human ganglionic eminence samples spanning gestational weeks (GW) 9–18. CR interneurons maintained in ISP medium were co-clustered with NR2F2/CALB2-expressing cells in human GE datasets after excluding astrocytes. Data integration was performed using the Seurat pipeline with 2,000 highly variable genes as anchors, applying a cluster resolution of 0.2. Following removal of 22 unclassified cells, pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using the monocle 2 package.

Results

Efficient interneuron generation by combining Wnt Inhibition and Shh signaling activation

Since all cortical neurons originated from NESCs, we first generated hPSC-derived NESCs using our previously reported protocol (Fig. 1A-B) [23, 25], which has been demonstrated to produce cortical neurons. All hPSC-derived NESCs uniformly expressed neural progenitor markers, such as SOX2, NESTIN and PAX6 (Fig. 1B), and exhibited long-term proliferative ability according to zhu et al. [23, 25]. Given that the CR interneurons are a subtype of cortical GABAergic neurons, we next tested whether NESCs have the potentials to differentiate into GABAergic neurons (Fig. 1A). When cultured in the basic differentiation medium (the control), NESCs displayed that 10.2 ± 4.1% of differentiated neurons co-expressed GABA and TUJ1 (Fig. 1C). During early neural development, signaling molecules were essential for cell fate specification and determination. Since the inhibition of Wnt signaling induces telencephalic identity and activates ventral specification [15–17, 26], we utilized IWP2, a chemical inhibitor of Wnt signaling, to enhance the rostralization of early NESCs and promote GABAergic neuron generation. Cell differentiation is closely related to the optimal activity of Wnt signaling [25], we therefore evaluated the effects of IWP2 concentration on the differentiation of GABAergic neurons. Compared to the control, the addition of IWP2 significantly (P = 0.015) promoted GABAergic neuron generation, with 1µM of IWP2 exhibiting the highest efficiency (Fig. 1C and D). In contrast, high concentration of IWP2 inhibited the generation of GABAergic interneuron (Fig. 1C and D). Consequently, we chose 1µM of IWP2 for subsequent GABAergic interneuron induction unless otherwise. These results also indicated that GABAergic interneuron differentiation is dependent on the activity of Wnt signaling.

Furthermore, cortical interneurons originate from the ventral telencephalon, where the morphogen SHH plays an important role in the specification of ventral identity [27]. We then examined the functions of Shh signaling for GABAergic neuron differentiation by assessing GABA production on day 12. After activating the Shh pathway with recombinant SHH at a concentration of 20–200 ng/mL, interestingly, in the presence of IWP2, treatment with 20 ng/mL of SHH significantly increased the differentiation of GABAergic neurons by approximately 45% (Fig S1A, S1B). To determine the optimal concentration of SHH for GABAergic neuron differentiation, using different concentration of SHH ranged from 5 to 20 ng/mL, and found that 15 ng/mL of SHH induced the highest proportion of GABAergic neurons at day 12 (Fig. 1C and E). In addition, we analyzed whether purmorphamine (Pur), a smoothened protein agonist, could promote GABAergic neuron differentiation. In the presence of 1 µM of IWP2 and 15 ng/mL SHH, treatment with 1.5 µM of Pur significantly increased the percentage of GABAergic neurons up to 73.0 ± 9.3% (Fig. 1C and F). Together, we developed a suitable culture medium named ISP medium, which includes 1 µM IWP2, 15 ng/mL SHH and 1.5 µM Pur for subsequent differentiation experiments, unless otherwise specified.

CR interneurons as the major GABAergic subtype induced by ISP medium

Calcium-binding proteins, such as Parvalbumin (PV), Calbindin (CB), and Calretinin (CR), are highly expressed in cortical GABAergic neurons and serve as common biomarkers for identifying these neurons [28, 29]. Notably, CR interneurons develop in the caudal ganglionic eminence (CGE), while CB and PV interneurons predominantly originate from the medial ganglionic eminence (MGE). To further explore their development, we first examined the regional identity of GABAergic neurons cultured in ISP medium prior to neurogenesis. Immunostaining for COUPTFII (a CGE marker) and NKX2.1 (a MGE marker) revealed that 93.3 ± 0.8%of cells were positive for COUPTFII + SOX1 + at day 5 post differentiation ( pdD5) (Fig. 2A and B), while the cells cultured in ISP medium were negative for NKX2.1. These findings suggest that the differentiated GABAergic neurons cultured in the ISP medium were predominantly CR neurons. Consistent with these result, immunostaining indicated that we obtained 78.6 ± 8.2%, 12.3 ± 4.7% and 6.5 ± 3.1% of CR, PV and CB, respectively (Fig. 2C and D). To assess the effects of serial passaging on CR neurons generation, we analyzed differentiation from hPSC-derived NESCs (BG02 cell line) at different passages. Over serial passaging, quantifications revealed that NESCs at P16, P32 and P43 give rise to 77.8 ± 3.3%, 74.1 ± 3.2% and 74.9 ± 2.5% GABA interneurons at pdD13, respectively (Figure S1A, S1B). As expected, NESCs at different passages cultured in the ISP medium induced about 74.5%± 6.62 for P16, 74.2%± 3.1 for P32, and 72.6%± 4.9 for P43 CR neurons (CR/TUJ1) (Fig. 2E and F). These results demonstrated that NESCs retained a stable propensity for CR neuron differentiation in the ISP medium. Given that NESCs can be cultured in large-scale [22], this stable differentiation propensity toward CR neurons suggests that this protocol is capacity of generating sufficient numbers of CR neurons.

The optimized ISP medium was compatible for different NESC lines

To assess whether the ISP medium was suitable for different cell lines to produce stable CR neurons, we tested two hPSC lines (BG02, H9) and an iPS cell line (h1) derived-NESCs. In the control differentiation medium, without IWP2, SHH or Pur, we detected few (about 10%) CR neurons. In contrast, in the ISP medium approximately 73% (74.57 ± 6.84% for BG02, 73.3 ± 6.4% for H9 and 71.5 ± 3.3% for h1) of CR neurons were induced at pdD12 (Fig. 2G and H and Fig S2A). Statistical analysis revealed no significant difference among the three different cell lines (Fig. 2H), indicating that the ISP medium dose not preferentially support specific cell lines. To further confirm their identity as inhibitory interneurons, we analyzed Calretinin co-expressed with GABA across the different cell lines. BG02, H9 and h1 cells in the ISP medium produced CR + GABA + double positive interneurons at rates of 72.4 ± 8.5%, 73.3 ± 5.8 and 69.2%±8.0 (Fig. 2I, Fig S2B), respectively. Collectively, these results demonstrated that the ISP medium is stable and suitable for expanding different cell lines.

Calretinin interneurons exhibited mature characteristics

Calretinin (CR) interneurons represent a heterogeneous subpopulation with diverse morphologies [30]. To analyze the morphology of interneurons generated in ISP medium, we performed immunofluorescence staining one month after differentiation. The analysis revealed that GABAergic neurons comprised 78% CR neurons and 10% PV neurons (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, no CB neurons were detected (data not shown). CR neurons exhibited a bipolar morphology, whereas PV neurons displayed a multipolar morphology (Fig. 3A), indicating that most of CR interneurons derived from ISP medium were bipolar neurons. Previous studies have reported that some CR neurons co-express vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) or reelin, accountings for about 5% of total cortical GABAergic neurons [1]. To further classify the subtypes of our differentiated CR neurons, we performed co-staining for CR with VIP or Reelin. We found that about 68 ± 3.5% and 15 ± 5.8% of differentiated CR neurons were VIP neurons and Reelin + neurons, respectively (Fig. 3C and D).

Synapse formation is a critical step in establishing functional neural circuits. To determine whether GABAergic neurons derived from NESCs can form physical synapses in vitro, the differentiated neurons at pdD13 were co-cultured with mouse embryonic cortex astrocytes. After four weeks of growth in the ISP medium, about 80.6% of CR interneurons from different generations co-expressed NeuN, indicating their maturation as functional neurons (Fig. 3E and F). In addition, all differentiated CR neurons co-expressed the axonal marker SMI 312 and the dendritic marker MAP2 (Fig. 3G). After 56 days of co-culture, we observed synaptic structures characterized by the expression of pre-synaptic Synapsin I and post-synaptic PSD-95 proteins, and displaying with a dotted pattern (Fig. 3H). To further evaluate neuronal functionality, we performed whole-cell patch-clamp recordings to examine the membrane properties of the differentiated GABA neurons. After 12-week of culture, all examined neurons (n = 6) generated a train action potential (Fig. 3I). These results demonstrated that CR interneurons induced by the ISP medium can differentiate, mature and form functional synapses.

Characterization of CR interneurons at single cell resolution

To gain insight into the transcriptional programs of CR interneurons derived from the ISP medium, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on CR neurons at pdD36 using 10X Genomics. Our analysis revealed eight distinct cell clusters, from which we identified three major cell types: neural progenitors (NP), astrocytes, and interneurons (Fig. 4A). The NP clusters were characterized by the expression of SOX2 and NR2F2 (COUP-TFII), while the interneuron clusters were marked by TUBB3 and CALB2 (calretinin). Astrocytes expressed the astrocyte-specific marker S100B (Fig. 4B).

Next, we aimed to investigate whether the CR interneurons generated in the ISP medium exhibited a transcriptional profile similar to that of their in vivo counterparts, and whether they could serve as a model for studying neural development. Using the dataset from Shi et al. (2021) [31], we extracted NR2F2 + and CALB2 + cells from the single-nuclei RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) data of the developing human ganglionic eminences. We then integrated these data and identified ten clusters corresponding to distinct developmental stages and regional identities, including NP, radial glial cells (RGC), and clusters representing the CGE, lateral ganglionic eminence (LGE), and MGE (Fig. 4C and D). These clusters were defined using established markers [31, 32], such as ASCL1 (NP), FABP7 (RGC), SCGN and NR2F2 (CGE), FOXP1 (LGE), and LHX6 (MGE) (Fig. 4E). Proportional analysis revealed that CR interneurons generated in the ISP medium were predominantly concentrated in clusters 2, 3, 4, and 5 (Fig. 4F). These findings indicate that the CR interneurons induced by the ISP medium span developmental stages from progenitors to differentiated interneurons, exhibiting transcriptional signatures consistent with the CGE and LGE. Furthermore, cell trajectory and pseudo-time analysis revealed that CR interneuron development followed three distinct trajectories originating from NP, independent of regional identity: NP-RGC-LGE, NP-CGE-Cajal cells, and NP-CGE (Fig. 4G). These results suggest that CR interneurons derived from the ISP medium can be used as a model to study cortical development. Collectively, our single-cell analysis confirmed the successful and efficient generation of CR interneurons.

Integration of interneuron progenitors (iNPs) in mouse brain

To evaluate the potential values of iNPs in clinical application after long-term culture, we assessed their survival and differentiation in vivo. NESCs were first infected with a retrovirus containing EF1a promoter driving green fluorescent protein (GFP). Three days post infection, cells were performed clonal expansion and single cell-derived stable cell lines were established. A single GFP + clonal cell line was randomly selected for subsequent transplantation analysis. Before transplantation, NESCs were differentiated into iNPs over 5 days in the ISP medium. A total of 5 × 105 differentiated iNPs were transplanted into the ventricles of new born BALB/C mice (n = 16). Six mice were randomly selected and examined at one-month post-transplantation. The survival and integration of grafted cells were examined using stereological analysis of serial coronal sections. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that the transplanted iNPs integrated successfully into the mouse brains and located below the ventricle (Fig. 5A and A’). Previous studies have demonstrated that the cell fates of grafted neural progenitor cells are closely influenced by the host brain environment and the intrinsic properties of grafted cells [33–35]. To determine the differentiation potential of the grafted cells into interneurons, we analyzed their morphologies in vivo. Within the cortex, the integrated neurons displayed either multipolar or bipolar morphologies (Fig. 5A’’), consistent with the reported characteristics of GABAergic interneurons [15, 30, 35].

Other 10 mice were examined after three months of post-transplantation. Nearly 80% of H-N + cells were remained positive for GFP (Fig. 5B), implying that GFP signals have been shut down in some differentiated cells. Further immunostaining showed that 4.3 ± 6.5% and 69 ± 7.9% of GFP + cells expressed the astrocyte marker GFAP (Fig. 5B) and the neuron axon marker SMI 312 (Fig. 5B), respectively. Furthermore, these grafted cells expressed the mature neuronal marker NeuN (Fig. 5B), but not for NESTIN and Ki67 (data not shown). These findings indicate that the grafted cells were post-mitotic and safe for further clinical applications.

Next, to assess whether the grafted cells differentiated into interneurons influenced by the host brain environment and their intrinsic properties, we used immunostaining for specific transcription factors to identify the subtypes of neurons in the brain sections. As expected, 61.4 ± 3.8% of grafted GFP + cells in the implant differentiated into GABAergic neurons (Fig. 5C), with 8.6 ± 1.4% of the GFP + cells were identified as CR + neurons, respectively (Fig. 5D). Together, these results demonstrate that iNPs in the ISP medium are capable of differentiating into CR interneurons in vivo following transplantation.

Discussion

We developed a method with combined and stepwise regulation of ventral and rostro caudal signaling pathways, achieving highly effective differentiation of hPSCs to homogeneous calretinin interneurons. NESCs derived from two hESC lines (BG02, H9) and an iPS cell line (h1) were used for generating approximately 80% of CR interneurons. The differentiated CR interneurons matured after treatment by ISP medium for four weeks, expression of matured neuron maker NeuN, Synapsin and PSD 95 and especially exhibited the Neuron spontaneous postsynaptic currents. Efficient generation of homogeneous population of specific progenitors from hPSCs in our study is a key prerequisite to realize brain repair for disease modeling and regenerative medicine.

Multiple researches report that mouse and human stem cells obtain CGE /LGE-like phenotypes by modulation of Shh signaling [36–38]. In addition, purified telencephalic progenitors, after treated with high doses of the Hedgehog (Hh) agonist SAG (Smoothened agonist) or FGF15/19 [37], differentiated into MGE- and CGE-like cells. Foxg1 + telencephalic neural precursors derived both from mouse and human ESCs, after treated with Activin A, can obtain the LGE/CGE identity, because Activin A specifically promotes GSX2 expression and inhibits NKX2.1 in these cells, thus acquiring a CGE fate and generating CR interneurons [36]. To sum up, all these researches indicate that more efficient ventral telencephalic phenotype induction is achieved by early modulation of Wnt and Shh signaling pathways before neuroectoderm formation is completed.

Therefore, through the combined use of dorsoventral and rostrocaudal modulation by Shh and Wnt signaling pathways, our procedure increased CGE-identity induction, thus obtaining homogeneous populations of CR interneurons. Details in our procedure include: (i) inhibition of Wnt signaling, which leads to caudalize [39] and dorsalize [40] differentiating patterns; (ii) mild activation of Shh signaling, resulting in CGE induction [41, 42], in contrast to the previous study which uses Activin A to inhibit Shh induce progenitors into CGE-like cells [36]; (iii) combined regulation of Wnt and Shh signaling pathways, through which we achieved an effective protocol of NESCs differentiation into homogeneous CGE-like CR interneurons. (iv) direct differentiation of CR interneurons from NESCs. The NESCs derived from hPSC using our reported protocol [23], which maintained unlimitedly self-renewal abilities. Therefore, CR interneurons are capable of rapid large-scale production based on NESCs culture.

Over last 15 years, cortical interneuron transplantation has been shown to improve phenotypes in numerous neural diseases model [43, 44]. Calretinin neurons are a subtype of interneurons. The dysfunction of CR neurons leads to various neurological disorders, such as epilepsy and schizophrenia [45]. Our protocol described in this manuscript provide a method to obtain an enriched source of CR interneurons from hPSCs. When transplanted into embryonic mouse brain, NESCs cultured in ISP medium can matured and differentiate into CR interneurons, indicating that they can providing inhibitory inputs onto recipient neurons [14]. Therefore, our work significantly contributes to regenerative medicine by safely and successfully generating CR interneurons from hPSCs.

Conclusion

We developed a robust two-step research process for generation of calretinin interneurons: first, inducing hPSCs differentiated into NESCs, and second, efficiently differentiating these NESCs to reach approximately 80% of calretinin interneurons. This advancement in calretinin interneurons generation will enable cell therapy applications and provide a means to explore the mechanisms underlying cortical evolution.
