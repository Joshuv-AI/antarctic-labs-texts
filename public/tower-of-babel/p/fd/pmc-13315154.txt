Long‐Term Association of Cardiovascular Risk Burden With Dementia Risk: The Role of Life‐Course Confounders in an Analysis of Cumulative and Specific Factors
Authors: Roya Riahi, Mohammad Ali Mansournia, Negin Badihian, Sayed Mohsen Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreventing dementia through risk factor modification is a global health priority. Although individual cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) have been linked to dementia, the cumulative burden of CVRFs, their changes over time, and the role of life‐course confounders, especially early‐life and psychological factors, remain poorly understood. This study aimed to estimate the longitudinal association between changes in CVRF burden and dementia risk under different hypothetical scenarios.MethodsWe analyzed 6051 participants from the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study, with a median follow‐up of 21.9 years. We used the doubly robust g‐formula method to estimate the 20‐year risk difference for dementia under hypothetical strategies that maintain CVRF burden at different levels over time, explicitly accounting for competing mortality and time‐varying confounding.ResultsAt baseline, 23.1% had ≥3 CVRFs. During follow‐up, 1008 participants (17%) developed dementia. Low birth weight, midlife depression, and poor oral health emerged as important confounders. Maintaining a low CVRF burden was associated with a lower estimated risk of dementia compared with the natural course (risk difference, −4.31% [95% compatibility interval, −7.75% to −0.80%]), whereas maintaining a high CVRF burden was associated with a higher estimated risk (risk difference, 10.5% [95% compatibility interval, 5.8%–15.7%). Under model assumptions, 41.3% of dementia cases were estimated to be associated with the presence of CVRFs.ConclusionsHigher CVRF burden over time was associated with greater dementia risk. Findings suggest that early and sustained management of CVRFs, along with consideration of early‐life and psychological factors, may play an important role in dementia prevention strategies.

Dementia is a multifactorial clinical syndrome characterized by impairment of cognitive and emotional functions, particularly memory decline, which is severe enough to interfere with daily functioning. Dementia affects almost 1 in 3 elderly people, with global prevalence expected to increase from 57.4 million cases in 2019 to 152.8 million cases in 2050. 1 , 2 Given this rapid growth, dementia has become a considerable public health challenge in the current century, placing a substantial burden on families and health and social care services. 2 With no effective treatments for dementia currently available, developing preventive strategies has become crucial. 3

In recent years, there has been growing interest in identifying modifiable risk factors that can be targeted through preventive interventions and lifestyle modifications to reduce the incidence of dementia. 4 Among these, cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) are particularly important. 5 , 6 Medical and behavioral conditions, such as diabetes, 7 hypertension, 8 hypercholesterolemia, 9 obesity, 10 and smoking, 11 are reported as key CVRFs contributing to the development of dementia. Previous studies have reported that 35% to 50% of worldwide dementia cases are attributable to CVRFs, and modifying these risk factors might prevent or delay up to 10% of dementia cases. 3 This highlights the strong clinical importance of CVRFs in dementia development.

Prior research within the ARIC (Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities) study cohort has shown that individual CVRFs, including diabetes, hypertension, and midlife smoking, were associated with an increased risk of dementia. 12 Also, another ARIC study reported that diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and hypercholesterolemia were associated with a higher prevalence of dementia in later life. 13 These studies typically focus on individual CVRFs measured at a single time point and analyzed using a standard survival model, without accounting for temporal changes over the life course, which may underestimate or misrepresent their long‐term associations. Most of the previous studies also focus on individual CVRFs, with limited consideration of their cumulative burden or interactions, which may increase dementia risk through a shared vascular mechanism. 14 Given the interrelated nature of CVRFs, analyzing them individually while controlling for others can bias the true association. 15 Additionally, most available evidence on the association between CVRFs and dementia comes from observational studies, which are vulnerable to confounding bias. Addressing these limitations requires methods that explicitly estimate longitudinal association in observational data while accounting for confounding bias, risk factor aggregation, and time‐varying exposure.

In the current study, using data from the large‐scale, long‐term prospective ARIC study, we aim to investigate the longitudinal association of individual and combined midlife CVRFs, including their temporal changes, with 20‐year dementia risk. We used a hybrid confounder‐selection approach and the parametric g‐formula to estimate absolute risks and risk differences (RDs) in a competing‐risks setting, providing interpretable, model‐based estimates of the long‐term implications of midlife cardiovascular risk.

METHODS

Data Availability and Ethics Statement

Data of this study are under the supervision of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, and access was obtained through application. The Isfahan University of Medical Sciences ethics committee approved this study (research approval code: 34001010; and research ethical code: IR.MUI.REC.1401.008). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects in the original ARIC study.

Study Population

The ARIC study is a long‐term, large‐scale prospective cohort study of 15 792 adults aged 45 to 64 years initially recruited from 1987 to 1989 from 4 US counties: Forsyth County, NC; Jackson, MS; the suburbs of Minneapolis, MN; and Washington County, MD. After initial examination, participants completed 9 follow‐up visits: 3 visits at 3‐year intervals (visits 2–4) from 1990 to 1998, followed by 6 additional visits (visits 5–10) conducted over the next 11 years. Annual telephone contacts were done to assess changes in the participants' medical conditions. 16

By focusing on midlife CVRFs, their change over time, and ensuring the temporality of the associations, we considered visit 4 (1996–1998) as the baseline in this study. Of the 11 565 participants who attended visit 4, we excluded those who were not middle‐aged (n=4995), those who had Parkinson disease (n=21), or those who had substantial missing covariate information (n=498). In total, 6051 adults were analyzed.

Assessment of Dementia

The incidence of dementia was assessed using neuropsychological test batteries performed during consecutive visits following the baseline assessment (in 2011–2013 [visit 5], 2016–2017 [visit 6], and 2018–2019 [visit 7]), as well as information from telephone interviews for cognitive status, informant interviews, results from previous longitudinal cognitive assessments, and hospitalization and death certificate codes. 17 , 18 In‐person assessments were conducted using comprehensive neurocognitive tests, including measures of working memory, cognitive function, language, attention, processing speed, and intelligence. Additional tests, including the Neuropsychiatric Inventory, an informant interview using the Clinical Dementia Rating Scale, and the Functional Activities Questionnaire, were performed on a random sample of participants with and without cognitive decline. 19 Participants unable to attend clinic visits were assessed using a telephone interview for cognitive status or an informant interview. 20 , 21 Dementia diagnosis was made by analyzing results with a computer diagnostic algorithm, followed by adjudication by an expert committee in accordance with the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition) and the criteria outlined by the National Institutes of Health/National Institute on Aging. Additional cases of dementia were identified through discharge hospitalization International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9), or death certificate codes collected as part of ARIC study surveillance. 17 , 22

Assessment of CVRFs

Five CVRFs, including hypercholesterolemia, diabetes, hypertension, obesity (general/abdominal), and current smoking, were measured from baseline to visit 7 and considered as the exposure of interest. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or use of antihypertensive medications. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as fasting total blood cholesterol >240 mg/dL or use of cholesterol‐lowering medications within prior examination. 23 Diabetes was defined as having a fasting blood glucose >126 mg/dL, non–fasting blood glucose >200 mg/dL, or self‐reported physician diagnosis or treatment for diabetes. Obesity was defined as a body mass index ≥30 kg/m2. Abdominal obesity was defined based on waist circumferences ≥88 cm for women and ≥102 cm for men. 24 Smoking status was determined based on self‐reported information, and participants were classified as current smokers or nonsmokers. We categorized each CVRF as binary (presence=1, and absence=0), and then summed these values to generate a variable representing the total number of CVRFs present in each participant. 11 , 15

Assessment of Covariates

A comprehensive set of demographics, clinical, lifestyle, behavioral, and apolipoprotein ε4 (APOE‐ε4) genotype data from participants was collected. The baseline covariates set included sex, race, educational attainment, marital status, occupation, low birth weight (LBW), history of nonaspirin NSAIDs, history of pneumonia, bronchitis, goiter, or other thyroid disease, and occupation's activity. Time‐varying covariates evaluated at baseline and 3 consecutive visits included age, total income, medication use, including aspirin, alcohol consumption, physical activity (PA), depression, head injury, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), systemic inflammation, hyperuricemia, triglyceride level, and estimated glomerular filtration rate level. Several covariates included the Healthy Eating Index, dietary nutrient intakes, the diet's glycemic index, the fat quality score, trait anger, perceived social support and social network, compliance with oral hygiene, and vitamin D deficiency and are inherently time varying, but in the present cohort were not collected repeatedly throughout follow‐up. Additional information about the covariates can be found in Data S1.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive data were reported as frequency (percentage) and as median with interquartile range or mean±SD for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. Depending on the type of variable in focus, between‐group comparisons were conducted using the nonparametric independent‐sample test, the Jonckheere–Terpstra test, the linear‐by‐linear test, or the Cochrane‐Armitage test for trend.

Participants were followed for ≈2 decades from baseline. The primary outcome was time to dementia onset from baseline to visit 7. Time to dementia was recorded as the earliest of hospitalization date, the first clinic visit, the first telephone interview for cognitive status, the first informant interview, the hospitalization discharge, or the death certificate code for dementia. To account for the projected lag in reporting dementia onset, 6 months were subtracted from the recorded dates. Death was treated as a competing event. In cases of 2 events, the earliest was recorded as the outcome for that subject, and death without a preceding diagnosis of dementia did not equate to surviving without dementia. Participants without dementia or death were censored at the latest assessment. The unstandardized absolute risk was estimated using a nonparametric Nelson–Aalen estimator over the follow‐up period.

We used a hybrid strategy as a 2‐stage confounder selection framework by combining Bayesian data‐driven selection with theory‐driven time‐varying method. First, the generalized Bayesian causal effect estimation (GBCEE) algorithm 25 with targeted minimum loss‐based estimation 26 was used to identify potential measured confounders at study baseline. This variable selection algorithm identifies measured confounders and predictors of outcomes by modeling both the outcome and the exposure. The algorithm searches for potential models using every combination of covariates. After fitting each model, its posterior probability is estimated using the Bayesian information criterion as a byproduct of the Markov chain Monte Carlo MC 3 algorithm. GBCEE assigns most of the posterior weight to models with potential confounders that best approximate the conditional exchangeability assumption. To minimize the risk of model misspecification, we used an ensemble machine learning algorithm in SuperLearner, including generalized linear models, penalized regression models (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator/elastic net generalized linear model), and Bayesian generalized linear model as candidate algorithms, and selected an optimal combination using 5‐fold cross‐validation. To enhance stability, the GBCEE was implemented using 4 independent Markov chain Monte Carlo chains, each with 15 000 iterations. A total of 3000 iterations were dropped as burn‐in across chains. Convergence of the algorithm was assessed using trace plots, the Gelman–Rubin potential scale reduction factor, and posterior predictive checks. We also modeled the individual components of CVRFs separately to avoid confounding bias and build a sufficient adjustment set. Second, because the GBCEE is limited in accounting for time‐varying confounders, this method was complemented with a time‐varying directed acyclic graph based on prior evidence and temporal ordering assumptions. 27 , 28 This allowed us to incorporate variables whose confounding effects may emerge during follow‐up and therefore cannot be identified by GBCEE. The final adjustment set was defined as the union of baseline confounders with a posterior support from GBCEE and time‐varying confounders identified through the time‐varying directed acyclic graph.

Considering death as a competing event, we estimated model‐based differences in the 20‐year risk of dementia associated with changes in CVRF burden over time, under hypothetical sustained strategies initiated at study baseline and maintained during follow‐up, using iterative conditional expectation g‐formula with a doubly robust estimator. This approach estimates the counterfactual cumulative incidence under hypothetical sustained strategies while explicitly handling time‐varying confounders affected by prior exposure. At each study visit, using a sequential regression framework, outcomes (dementia and death) were modeled separately, conditional on baseline covariates, prior CVRF burden history, and time‐varying confounders updated iteratively under each intervention strategy. This recursive method accounts for exposure‐confounder feedback and generates model‐based estimates of cumulative incidence under each strategy. The following variables were treated as time‐varying confounders and updated at each visit: age, income, PA, COPD, mild and heavy drinker, aspirin intake, depression, head injury, systemic inflammation, estimated glomerular filtration rate, and triglycerides. We considered 3 hypothetical sustained strategies throughout follow‐up: 1, maintaining a low CVRF burden (0–1 CVRFs); 2, maintaining a moderate CVRF burden (exactly 2 CVRFs); and 3, maintaining a high CVRF burden (≥3 CVRFs), each compared against the natural course defined as the observed, data‐generating evolution of CVRF burden without a prespecified strategy. For individual CVRF components, sustained strategies were defined as 1 (persistent absence) or 2 (persistent presence) of the condition, and were compared with the natural course. 29 , 30 Potential model misspecification and stability of the estimates were evaluated by comparing observed and simulated natural‐course estimates. Bootstrapping with 1000 resamples was used to obtain the 95% compatibility intervals (CIs). 31 The longitudinal association was estimated as the difference/ratio of 2 exposure‐specific absolute standardized risks on the 20‐year absolute risk of dementia, and was reported as a percentage with 95% CIs. Data were analyzed using R statistical software (version 4.5.2, R development core team, packages: “survival,” “GBCEE,” “lmtp,” and “gfoRmulaICE”).

Sensitivity Analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the consistency of our results. The robustness of the observed association to unmeasured and uncontrolled confounding was assessed by the E‐values calculation. To address the possible mediating effect of CVRFs on dementia through stroke, we repeated the analysis after excluding individuals who had a stroke before baseline. Also, the 20‐year risk of dementia under hypothetical CVRF strategies, considering death as a censoring event, to reflect the controlled direct effect, which isolates the effect of CVRFs not mediated by death, was estimated. Finally, to measure the estimated fraction of dementia cases associated with each CVRF and their combination, the time‐varying population‐attributable fraction (PAF) with the 95% CIs (iteration =1000) based on the same bootstrapped sample for the other estimation was estimated using our cohort's confounder‐adjusted risk ratio. 32 , 33

RESULTS

At baseline, the analytic sample included 6051 eligible middle‐aged subjects (aged 45–64 years). The participant's mean (SD) age was 58.9 (3.2) years; 58% were women, 79% were married, and 78% were White. The prevalence of individual CVRFs was as follows: hypertension in 47% of participants, obesity in 37%, hypercholesterolemia in 25%, diabetes in 15%, and current smoking in 17%. The total number of CVRFs ranged from 0 to 5; 20.2% had 0, 23.3% had only 1, 33.4% had 2, and 23% had ≥3 CVRFs. As shown in Table 1, individuals with the higher number of CVRFs were generally older, tended to have basic or no education, were more often women, were less often White, and were less likely to have a high income or a professional occupation compared with those with lower CVRFs (P for trend <0.001) (Table 1). Compliance with oral hygiene decreased progressively from 74% in subjects without CVRFs to 57.7% in subjects with ≥3 CVRFs (P<0.001). Conversely, the prevalence of high trait anger (from 51%in 0‐CVRFs group to 61%in ≥3‐CVRFs group; P<0.001) and the prevalence of hard working (from 18%in 0‐CVRFs group to 21%in ≥3‐CVRFs group; P=0.038) demonstrated an increasing trend across CVRF levels (Table 1). The higher level of PA was inversely associated with the higher number of CVRFs (P<0.001) (Table 1). There was an increase in the Keys' score (median [interquartile range]: from 40 [10]in the 0‐CVRFs group to 42 [10]in the ≥3‐CVRFs group) from individuals with no CVRFs to those having ≥3 CVRFs. The healthy eating index showed a decreasing trend across the number of CVRFs (median [interquartile range]: from 73 [13]in the 0‐CVRFs group to 71 [12]in the ≥3‐CVRFs group; P<0.001).

Identifying True Confounders

The primary aim was to identify covariates confounding the association between CVRFs and dementia. The potential confounders/predictors with the largest posterior inclusion probability selected by GBCEE were as follows: age (100%), sex (100%), race (100%), education (100%), APOE‐ε4 carrier (100%), depression (100%), income (91%), LBW (90%), regular intake of NSAIDs (68%), and PA (53%). Compliance with oral health, mild alcohol consumption, occupational activity, and history of COPD had posterior inclusion probabilities of <50%, and all other measured covariates had posterior probabilities of selection of <10%, with most being 0. To integrate theory‐driven knowledge, these confounders were combined with additional time‐varying covariates not selected by GBCEE to construct a time‐varying directed acyclic graph. The final adjustment set included age, sex, race, education, APOE‐ε4 carrier, LBW, history of regular NSAID use, compliance with oral health, income, PA, depression, COPD, systemic inflammation, mild and heavy alcohol consumption, head injury, and aspirin use (Figure 1) (simplified directed acyclic graph is shown in Figure S1). Midlife CVRFs and 20‐Year Risk of Dementia

All subjects were followed for a median (interquartile range) 21.9 (7.5) years from visit 4, during which time 1008 (17%) new cases of dementia occurred: 155 (13%) in the group without CVRFs, 207 (15%) in the group with 1 CVRF, 354 (17%) in the group with 2 CVRFs, and 292 (21%) in the group with ≥3 CVRFs. The nonstandardized absolute risk of dementia according to the number of CVRFs and its components is illustrated in Figures S5 and S6. Having ≥3 CVRFs at baseline was associated with higher 20‐year dementia risk (RD, 7.0% [95% CI, 3.8%–10.3%]) compared with none. Having 2 CVRFs was associated with a 4.5% higher risk, and 1 CVRF with a 2.1% higher risk. The risk change followed a dose–response relationship, increasing from no (10%) to 1 (12.2%) to 2 (14.6%) to ≥3 (17.2%) CVRFs, with considerable differences between having 2 and ≥3 CVRFs versus none.

Changes in CVRF Burden/Components and Risk of Dementia

The pattern of the change of CVRFs composition over follow‐up was as follows: among participants with a moderate CVRF burden, at baseline, the most common combination was hypertension with obesity (51%), followed by hypercholesterolemia with obesity (18%). At visits 5 and 6, the hypertension‐obesity combination remained dominant (60% and 81%, respectively), whereas other combinations were less frequent. By visit 7, hypertension with hypercholesterolemia became the most common pattern (42%), followed by hypertension with obesity (34%). Among participants with high CVRF burden, the combination of hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, and obesity was the dominant pattern at baseline and at the final visit (31.5% and 49.5%), whereas hypertension‐diabetes‐obesity became most frequent during mid follow‐up (72.5% and 77%).

Over 20 years of follow‐up, the observed risk of dementia in the study population was 20.9%, and the estimated risk under the simulated natural course was 21.1% (95% CI, 19.9%–22.1%). Sensitivity Analysis

Maintaining a high CVRF burden was associated with a higher risk (RD, 6.24 [95% CI, 2.75–9.81]), and keeping a low CVRF burden was associated with a lower risk (RD, −4.10 [95% CI, −8.12 to −1.15]), compared with the natural course, with no considerable change in the direction or magnitude of the association, restricting analysis to subjects without stroke (Table S1). Estimates from the controlled direct effect analysis, which treated death as a censoring event, were directionally consistent with the primary competing‐risks analysis (Table S2).

Under a modeled hypothetical scenario in which individuals with multiple CVRFs were assumed to eliminate all risk factors, the PAF for new dementia cases was estimated to be 41.3% (95% CI, 24.1%–56.7%). If the entire population had maintained a low CVRF burden throughout follow‐up, the estimated PAF indicated a 21.0% (95% CI, 12.6%–32.4%) lower estimated incidence of dementia. Allowing a low‐to‐moderate CVRF burden was associated with a smaller estimated fraction of potentially avoidable cases (PAF, 11.0% [95% CI, 6.0%–15.6%]). Among individual CVRFs, diabetes, obesity, and hypertension showed the strongest longitudinal association with dementia risk, corresponding to estimated PAFs of 24.2% (95% CI, 18.3%–29.7%), 22% (95% CI, 14.0%–30.0%), and 19.8% (95% CI, 7.1%–32.4%), respectively. Smaller estimated fractions of dementia cases were associated with hypercholesterolemia (PAF, 9.5% [95% CI, 4.8%–14.3%]) and smoking (PAF, 7.9% [95% CI, 3.1%–12.1%]).

DISCUSSION

This study investigated the longitudinal association between individual and combined CVRFs, including their changes over time, with the 20‐year risk of dementia. The most prominent measured confounders/predictors of the CVRF‐dementia relationship included age, sex, race, education, APOE‐ε4 carrier, depression, LBW, and income. History of regular NSAID intake, compliance with oral health, PA, COPD, systemic inflammation, mild and heavy alcohol consumption, head injury, and aspirin intake were identified as less considerable confounders.

Age is a key nonmodifiable determinant influencing the incidence of dementia and vascular health. 34 Demographic characteristics, including sex, race, education, and income, substantially confound the CVRF‐dementia relationship. 35 As with previous findings, we found a higher prevalence of smoking, central obesity, and diabetes in men, whereas women exhibited a greater prevalence of general obesity and hypercholesterolemia. 36 Women had more CVRFs than men, likely because of their older age, which differs from the previous studies. Notably, co‐occurring CVRFs increase with age for both sexes, but are more common in women. 37

The incidence of dementia was higher among Black participants, supporting previous findings. 38 Disparities can arise from the unequal distribution of CVRFs among racial group subpopulations. Current smoking, obesity, hypertension, and diabetes were more prevalent among Black individuals, who exhibit a higher number of CVRFs than White individuals. This supports prior findings 39 and may be linked to health care access challenges and timely diagnoses in specific subpopulations.

Participants with lower educational attainment and income faced a higher dementia risk, consistent with previous findings. 40 In line with evidence, participants with higher education or income levels had lower CVRF prevalence. These factors affect lifestyle choices, living conditions, stress management, health literacy, and risk behavior knowledge, influencing cardiovascular and brain health. 41

We found that early‐life depression (before the age of 60 years) is linked to dementia and higher rates of co‐occurring CVRFs. Depression and dementia share biological pathways, including hormonal, inflammatory, genetic, and neuroimmune mechanisms, often overlapping with CVRFs. Chronic stress in depression disrupts the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, increasing cortisol levels and contributing to cognitive impairment and diabetes. Stress‐induced glucocorticoid activity may facilitate β‐amyloid accumulation, a hallmark of Alzheimer disease. Depression‐related inflammation promotes neurodegeneration, atherosclerosis, and platelet activation, elevating cardiovascular and dementia risks. Genetic factors, like deficits in brain‐derived neurotrophic factor, link depression to dementia and vascular disease by impairing synaptic plasticity. Furthermore, typical behaviors of depressed individuals, including smoking, poor diet, medication nonadherence, and inactivity, increase cardiovascular risk, reinforcing the connection between depression, cardiovascular risk, and dementia. 42 , 43

We identified LBW as an important confounder of the CVRF‐dementia link, associated with higher dementia incidence and a higher number of CVRFs. Biological CVRFs, like hypertension, diabetes, and hypercholesterolemia, were more common in LBW participants, aligning with recent studies. 44 , 45 LBW, a marker of fetal growth and nutritional status, has been linked to cognitive decline and vascular risks in adulthood. Impaired brain development from LBW can persist, causing structural abnormalities, like reduced brain volume, especially in the hippocampus and white matter, leading to long‐term memory and cognitive issues. 46 Additionally, LBW is inversely associated with adult cardiovascular risk because of metabolic programming in utero, which results in insulin resistance, leptin deficiency, and disruption of insulin signaling pathways. These physiological alterations promote CVRFs, such as obesity, hypertension, and insulin resistance, further increasing the risk of dementia through vascular aging, endothelial dysfunction, and reduced cerebral perfusion. 45 , 47

Poor oral hygiene was a modest confounder for the CVRF‐dementia link. Regular toothbrushing, especially twice daily, may reduce oral bacterial load and systemic inflammation, which could, in turn, influence vascular and brain health. Although regular toothbrushing protects against periodontitis, a condition linked to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and potentially dementia, these mechanisms remain unclear and require further investigation. 48

Moderate alcohol consumption is believed to protect against dementia and CVRFs, as prior studies suggest. 49 , 50 The potential benefits, particularly from red wine, likely arise from its effects on inflammation, oxidative stress, lipid metabolism, and cognitive function, through the release of acetylcholine in the hippocampus. However, social and lifestyle factors may influence these protective effects more than the alcohol itself does. 49

Our findings confirm that APOE‐ε4 carriers face higher dementia risk and more CVRFs than noncarriers. The APOE‐ε4 allele is a key predictor of dementia, significantly influencing its incidence. 51 This genetic component is associated with low high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol and higher diabetes and hypertension risk, and synergizes with CVRFs to accelerate the development of dementia. 52

Regular NSAID intake showed a weak positive association with dementia incidence in this study. The actual relationship is unclear. Some studies suggest NSAIDs may lower Alzheimer disease risk, whereas others indicate the opposite. 53 Type, dosage, duration, and coexisting conditions may influence results, so the NSAIDs' protective role remains unclear.

We found that the persistent burden of CVRFs was associated with a higher 20‐year dementia risk in a dose–response pattern, particularly after 10 years of follow‐up. Maintaining a low CVRF burden throughout follow‐up was associated with a lower dementia risk, whereas sustained exposure to a high CVRF burden from midlife onward was associated with a higher risk, which highlights the importance of accounting for dynamic changes of cardiovascular risk over time. Despite variations in dementia assessments, the definition of risk factors, and exposure, these findings align with previous evidence that maintaining optimal levels of CVRF burden can act as a protective factor against the development of dementia. 54 , 55 , 56

Findings from individual components of CVRFs align with the following patterns: sustained normotension and the absence of diabetes were associated with substantially lower risk, whereas persistent hypercholesterolemia and obesity were associated with modestly higher estimated dementia risk relative to the natural course. These findings are consistent with prior studies, whereas the relationship with obesity appears complex. 56 , 57 , 58 , 59 Because CVRFs are interrelated, analyzing them separately while controlling for others can bias the results. Assessing the overall burden of CVRFs provides a clearer picture of dementia risk. Assuming interdependency, 4 CVRFs explained about one‐fifth of incident dementia. From a biological perspective, several interrelated mechanisms may explain these effects. Chronic hypertension contributes to cerebrovascular damage, white matter lesions, and impaired cerebral autoregulation, factors that are associated with both Alzheimer disease and vascular dementia. Diabetes and hyperglycemia cause dysfunction in small and large blood vessels, increase blood–brain barrier permeability, cause metabolic stress, and accelerate neurodegeneration. Oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and metabolic dysregulation associated with metabolic CVRFs further impair neuronal function and promote amyloid and tau pathology. These pathways suggest that continuing cardiovascular risk, rather than isolated incidents, plays a crucial role in dementia development. 59 , 60 Lifestyle habits directly affect biological factors, which mark their interconnection. Therefore, even slight deviations from an ideal lifestyle can significantly alter biological risk profiles. Although biological risk factors may be more amenable to modification, public health interventions can yield the greatest benefit when targeting lifestyle behaviors early and sustainably, even before biological markers deteriorate. This is because lifestyle has been the modifiable upstream driver, whereas biological factors often serve as the more proximate causes of disease. 61

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths, primarily its large population‐based sample with long‐term follow‐up. We identified confounders of the longitudinal association between CVRFs and dementia, such as LBW, depression, and oral health compliance, which are often overlooked. For example, 2 recent meta‐analyses showed inadequate adjustment for LBW (not considered in any studies) and depression (only adjusted in 3). 26 , 60 Furthermore, our analysis accounts for changes in CVRF burden over time, baseline and time‐varying confounding, and competing risks. Unlike most dementia studies that report hazard ratios, which are subject to built‐in selection bias, temporal variability, and complicate interpretations when an exposure affects both dementia and mortality, we focused on absolute RDs to provide a more robust and clinically interpretable measure of longitudinal association.

Our study has several limitations: First, although our modeled scenarios were initiated at midlife onward, irreversible vascular or neurodegenerative pathology may have accumulated before baseline; therefore, our estimates should be interpreted as late‐life modification rather than complete dementia prevention. Second, data on sleep characteristics and hearing loss were not adequately measured, so it is impossible to include them in our analysis. Third, our estimates apply only to individuals who survived to baseline, meaning findings may be subject to survivor bias, limiting external validity to the broader original cohort. Fourth, we were unable to conduct some planned sensitivity analyses for specific CVRF composition or different prior specification because of positivity violations, and the semi‐Bayesian nature of the GBCEE algorithm limited the investigation of some potentially informative contrasts.

Finally, although the weighted doubly robust g‐formula is a robust method for evaluating longitudinal exposure scenarios, 62 our estimates remain observational. CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our findings suggest that changes in CVRF burden over time are associated with 20‐year dementia risk. Maintaining a high CVRF burden throughout midlife and later life was considerably associated with a higher risk of dementia. Although the estimated RDs may seem small to modest, they are meaningful at the population level, given the prevalence of CVRFs and suboptimal cardiovascular health behaviors. Moreover, our minimal clinically important difference analysis indicated a 95.7% probability that the observed reduction in CVRF burden exceeds the minimal clinically important difference, strengthening the clinical relevance of these findings (Figure S7). 3 These findings highlight the potential role of early and sustained risk factor management, including blood pressure and glucose control, lipid management, and promotion of healthy lifestyle behaviors, such as improved diet, increased PA, and smoking cessation. Incorporating mental health care with the management of early‐life factors, such as fetal growth restriction, may inform more comprehensive dementia risk reduction strategies. Our study, using robust methods to model time‐varying exposure and confounders, provides a clearer view of the longitudinal association between change in CVRF burden and dementia risk. Nonetheless, further longitudinal research with repeated measures of additional time‐varying covariates is warranted to deepen insights into the complex pathways linking CVRF trajectories to dementia.
