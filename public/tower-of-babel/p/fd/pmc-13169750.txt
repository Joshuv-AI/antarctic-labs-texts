Sleep bruxism and daily life behaviors in university students: a cross-sectional analysis of free time management, sedentary behaviors, and smartphone use
Authors: Ömer Faruk Yaşaroğlu, Vesile Tektaş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSleep Bruxism (SB) is increasingly conceptualized as a biopsychosocial phenomenon, emerging from the complex interaction of biological predispositions, psychological factors, and lifestyle-related behaviors. This study aimed to examine the differences between university students with and without SB regarding free time management (FTM), sedentary behaviors, smartphone addiction, and the severity of temporomandibular disorders (TMDs).MethodsA total of 113 healthy university students (59 female, 54 male) participated in this cross-sectional study. Participants were screened for possible SB using the Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI). Data were collected using the FAI to assess TMDs severity, the Free Time Management Scale (FTMS), the Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire (SBQ), and the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV). Independent samples t-tests and Pearson Chi-square tests were utilized for group comparisons.ResultsNo statistically significant differences were observed between SB and non-SB groups in terms of total FTMS scores, sedentary behaviors, sleep onset time, or total daily sleep duration (p > 0.05). However, students in the SB group exhibited significantly higher smartphone addiction scores (30.94 ± 10.03) compared to the non-SB group (27.25 ± 8.68) (p = 0.04). Furthermore, a strong positive association was found between the presence of SB and TMDs severity (p < 0.001). Bruxers were significantly more likely to report moderate-to-severe TMDs symptoms, with an odds ratio of 10.9 compared to non-bruxers.ConclusionWhile FTM and general sedentary habits appear homogeneous among university students, excessive smartphone use emerges as a significant behavioral factor associated with SB. These findings suggest that SB is linked to modern technological lifestyle habits and contributes to increased TMDs severity. Clinical management of bruxism should incorporate a broader biopsychosocial perspective, including digital wellness and behavioral modifications.Keywords: Sleep bruxism, Smartphone addiction, Free time management, Sedentary behavior, Temporomandibular disorders

Background

In accordance with international consensus, bruxism is defined as a motor behavior characterized by masticatory muscle activity that may occur during sleep or wakefulness and is not classified as either a movement disorder or a sleep disorder. Within this framework, Sleep Bruxism (SB) manifests as rhythmic (phasic) or non-rhythmic (tonic) masticatory muscle activity, whereas awake bruxism is characterized by repetitive or sustained tooth contact and/or mandibular bracing or thrusting [1]. SB is a risk factor that can cause several problems such as temporomandibular joint dysfunctions (TMDs), tooth wear, tooth fracture, pain and sensitivity in the masticatory muscles. There are no reference values regarding the level of bruxism that leads to these adverse consequences [2]. The prevalence of SB in adults has been reported to range between 8% and 16%. This rate increases to 37% among university students [3, 4]. SB is increasingly conceptualized as a biopsychosocial phenomenon, emerging from the complex interaction of biological predispositions, psychological factors, and lifestyle-related behaviors [5]. Since university students are highly exposed to behavioral risk factors such as stress, poor free time management, sedentary lifestyle, and smartphone overuse, they represent a relevant general population group to study SB. Indeed, effective free time management has been reported to be associated with general well-being, high performance, and low levels of stress [6]. In contrast, poor free time management, prolonged sedentary behavior, and smartphone addiction have been linked to increased stress, anxiety, and depression [7, 8].

Free time refers to the time when an individual is not working, has no mandatory duties, and can use as they wish. Time management is the process of structuring, protecting, and adapting one’s time to changing circumstances. Research shows that effective time management increases work performance and academic success, enhances satisfaction with life, and reduces stress and anxiety [6, 9]. Most people spend their free time without a plan, which may result in perceiving free time as boring, increased screen-based entertainment, and reduced physical activity [10]. Young people often experience time pressure while trying to balance school, work, and leisure activities [11]. This time pressure can cause stress and may worsen conditions such as bruxism. Effective free time management (FTM) can improve overall well-being and may reduce stress levels, a known factor in bruxism. Therefore, it is important to examine the impact of free time management in university students on both academic and social life and also on stress-related clinical outcomes, particularly behavioral conditions such as bruxism.

Sedentary behaviors are defined as behaviors involving prolonged inactivity and energy expenditure of ≤ 1.5 metabolic equivalents (METs). Sedentary behaviors such as prolonged sitting and screen time are consistently associated with increased cardiovascular and metabolic risks, independent of physical activity levels [12]. Sedentary behaviors are highly prevalent among university students, with a considerable number engaging in prolonged sedentary lifestyles. These behaviors are associated with increased levels of stress and anxiety [7, 13]. Psychological factors such as anxiety and stress are known to be associated with bruxism. For example, teeth clenching and mandibular bracing have shown a moderate to high correlation with symptoms of anxiety and depression [14]. Therefore, sedentary behaviors are considered a critical public health problem not only due to their physical consequences but also because of their psychological effects [15]. However, the possible relationship between SB and sedentary behaviors has not been studied as comprehensively as other health outcomes.

Advances in technology have rendered smart devices an integral component of daily life. However, this situation has brought with it several problems, such as internet or smartphone addiction. Research indicates that, beyond obligatory tasks, individuals habitually devote much of their time to social media or gaming [16]. Smartphone addiction is common among university students and is associated with various psychological problems such as anxiety, depression, and stress [8, 17]. Considering that psychological distress is a well-known contributing factor to bruxism, this association may further support the link between smartphone addiction and bruxism. Indeed, Tınastepe et al. investigated this relationship and reported that the incidence of bruxism was significantly higher among students with high levels of smartphone addiction [18]. However, research in this area is insufficient. Therefore, whether smartphone addiction is a potential risk factor for bruxism needs to be further investigated.

A review of the current literature reveals that bruxism is mostly associated with biological, psychosocial factors, while studies addressing its behavioral dimension are extremely limited. The aim of this study is to examine differences between university students with SB and without SB in terms of FTM, sedentary behaviors, and smartphone addiction.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted at Harran University Department of Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation in line with the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration. The call for participation in the study was made through announcements within the university campus. The target population was healthy university students, representing a general non-clinical population. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment in the study.

Demographic information, including gender, age (years), height (cm) and weight (kg) was recorded for participants selected according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. In addition, participants’ usual sleep onset time and daily sleep duration were also documented. Participants were then asked to complete the Fonseca Anamnestic Index, Free Time Management Scale, Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire, Smartphone Addiction Scale - Short Form. All instruments were administered using their validated Turkish versions.

Fonseca Anamnestic Index (FAI)

The FAI is used to assess the presence and severity of symptoms caused by TMDs. The questionnaire is a highly simple and reliable method. It consists of 10 questions. Each question is answered as yes (10 points), sometimes (5 points), or no (0 points). It is classified as follows according to the total score: 0–15 points no problem, 20–40 mild TMDs, 45–65 moderate TMDs, 70–100 severe TMDs [19]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the questionnaire was conducted by Kaynak et al. in 2020 [20].

The question ‘Do you have any habits such as clenching or grinding your teeth?’ in item 8 of the FAI was specifically designed to assess possible SB. Those who responded “Yes” or “Sometimes” were classified as SB, while those who responded “No” were categorized as non-SB, as both “Yes” and “Sometimes” indicate the presence of clenching or grinding behavior, whereas “No” reflects its absence [21]. Item 8 of the FAI was used because it is conceptually similar to the question included in the widely used Oral Behaviors Checklist for assessing possible SB, and because the FAI simultaneously allows the evaluation of TMDs symptoms. According to the current international consensus, the sleep bruxism evaluated in this study corresponds to self-reported (subject-based) bruxism [1].

Free Time Management Scale (FTMS)

FTMS was developed by Wang et al. in 2011. It consists of a total of 4 sub-dimensions and 15 items: goal setting and technique (6 items), evaluation (3 items), leisure attitude (3 items) and scheduling (3 items). Each item can be coded on a scale of 1 to 5. 1 indicates “I completely disagree,” 2 indicates “I disagree,” 3 indicates “I am undecided,” 4 indicates “I agree,” and 5 indicates “I completely agree” [22]. A high score on the scale means good time management. In 2015, a validity and reliability study was carried out in Turkish [23].

Sedentary Behavior Questionnaire (SBQ)

SBQ is a questionnaire developed to assess the time spent performing nine sedentary behaviors (watching TV, sitting while eating, lying and resting, playing computer games, sitting listening to music, sitting talking on the telephone, office/paper work, reading, playing a musical instrument, arts and crafts, driving/traveling in a vehicle) during the week and at the weekend [24]. The questionnaire offers nine different response options: None, 15 min or less, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 5 h, 6 h, or more than 6 h. When determining the total time, the times are calculated in hours, and weekdays and weekends are calculated separately. Higher total values indicate longer daily sedentary behavior duration.The validity and reliability study of the questionnaire in Turkish was conducted by Kaya and Alpgözen in 2021 [25].

Smartphone Addiction Scale- Short Version (SAS-SV)

SAS-SV is a 10-item, 6-point likert-type scale. Participants rate each item on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 6 (strongly agree). The scale score ranges from 10 to 60 points. As the total score increases, the risk of addiction increases. The scale is a reliable screening tool with high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.91) and validity values [26]. The Turkish validity and reliability were conducted by Noyan et al. [27].

Statistical analysis

The required sample size was estimated using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 through an a priori power analysis for an independent samples t-test. An average effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.50), commonly considered medium in behavioral sciences, was assumed [28]. With a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a desired statistical power (1 − β) of 0.80, the analysis indicated that a minimum of 51 participants per group (a total of 102 participants) would be required to detect a statistically significant difference between the groups. The study was completed with a total of 113 participants due to the possibility of missing data.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of the distribution for quantitative variables was assessed by analyzing skewness and kurtosis values. Data were considered to follow a normal distribution if the absolute value of skewness was less than ± 2.0 and the kurtosis value was below 7.0 at the evaluation stage [29]. All data were found to be normally distributed. In the analysis of the data, descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation) were provided for continuous variables showing normal distribution, and independent samples t-tests were applied for group comparisons. Frequency (n) and percentage (%) values were presented for categorical variables, and differences between groups were evaluated using the Pearson Chi-square test. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

This study examined free time management, sedentary behaviors, and smartphone addiction in university students with and without SB. Our findings show that there was no difference between SB and non- SB groups in terms of FTM, sedentary behaviors, total sleep time and sleep onset time. Furthermore, students with SB were found to have higher levels of smartphone addiction and higher levels of TMDs severity.

The absence of significant differences in FTM, sedentary behavior, total sleep time and sleep onset time may be associated with the relative lifestyle homogeneity of university students, who tend to exhibit similar daily routines and high levels of sedentary behavior [30]. Although effective FTM has been associated with better well-being and lower stress in a meta-analysis [6], university students may not consistently translate positive attitudes toward time management into actual behavioral patterns [10]. Similarly, sedentary behavior represents a heterogeneous construct; some forms involving prolonged static posture have been associated with increased TMD risk [31], whereas leisure-related sedentary activities have been linked to reduced stress [32–34], potentially resulting in counterbalancing associations. Methodological factors may also partly explain the non-significant findings. The SBQ has been reported to underestimate sedentary time compared to accelerometry, and its minimal detectable change (304.4 min/day) suggests limited sensitivity to subtle behavioral differences [35]. Therefore, small variations in sedentary patterns between groups may not have been adequately captured by the questionnaire. Evidence regarding the relationship between bruxism and sleep parameters is also conflicting. One polysomnographic study reported longer sleep latency and shorter total sleep time in individuals with SB [36], whereas Lavigne et al. found comparable total sleep time, sleep latency, and sleep stages between sleep bruxers and controls [37]. Although macro sleep parameters (duration, latency, efficiency) did not differ, bruxers showed longer REM sleep duration, a finding linked to emotional memory processing and amygdala activity and suggesting a possible psychogenic component of SB [38]. Therefore, the non-significant findings may reflect measurement limitations and the relatively homogeneous lifestyle of the university student population.

A key finding is the strong association between SB and smartphone addiction, consistent with recent literature [18, 39]. Several neurobiological mechanisms may explain this relationship. Excessive smartphone use, particularly before sleep, has been suggested to suppress melatonin secretion due to blue light exposure, potentially disrupting circadian rhythms and increasing micro-arousals, which are known triggers for SB. In addition, behavioral addictions such as excessive smartphone use may activate mesolimbic dopaminergic pathways; given that SB is considered a centrally mediated phenomenon, alterations in dopaminergic activity may influence trigeminal motor control [18, 39, 40]. Intensive smartphone use may also promote forward head posture (“text neck”) and increased sympathetic arousal, which may contribute to increased tension in the masticatory and cervical muscles [41, 42]. Stress-related pathways may further contribute to this association. Bruxism has been associated with greater stress sensitivity and is often conceptualized as a maladaptive response to psychological stress [43]. Academic pressure and psychosocial demands may further contribute to increased stress levels among university students, potentially exacerbating bruxism through stress- and anxiety-related mechanisms [44]. Chronic stress may activate the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, increasing cortisol levels and influencing dopaminergic activity in brain regions involved in motor control. In addition, neuroanatomical connections between the amygdala and the mesencephalic trigeminal nucleus (Me5) have been proposed as a potential pathway linking emotional stress to trigeminal motor activity [45]. Sleep-related mechanisms may also contribute to this association. Because neurotransmitters regulating the sleep–wake cycle also modulate oromotor control, trigeminal motor activity varies across sleep stages and contributes to rhythmic masticatory muscle activity (RMMA), which often occurs in association with micro-arousals and sympathetic activation [46]. Consistent with this perspective, smartphone addiction has also been associated with poorer sleep quality and increased stress levels in several studies [47–49]. Taken together, these findings suggest that behavioral factors related to excessive smartphone use—particularly those affecting stress regulation and sleep continuity—may indirectly influence the occurrence of SB through alterations in autonomic and neurophysiological regulation [12].

Regarding TMDs, our findings indicated that bruxers experience higher symptom severity [47, 50], with higher odds of clinically relevant TMDs than non-bruxers (OR = 10.93). Biopsychosocial factors [48, 49] and sensitization mechanisms [51] have been well described in the literature as explanatory frameworks for both SB and TMDs. Stress and anxiety may increase stomatognathic activity and promote parafunctional behaviors [49], with sustained masticatory muscle activity contributing to mechanical overload and pain amplification [52]. Beyond these mechanisms, the coexistence of smartphone addiction and greater TMD severity in our sample suggests that SB may reflect a broader cumulative behavioral load. In university students, TMDs severity may therefore be influenced not only by mechanical loading but also by behavioral patterns associated with technology-intensive lifestyles. Recent conceptual developments also highlight mandibular bracing—sustained low-level muscle contraction without tooth contact—as a clinically relevant mechanism related to muscle fatigue and temporomandibular joint overload [43]. Although bracing has been mainly described in awake bruxism, different bruxism-related muscle activities across the sleep–wake cycle could be associated with cumulative mechanical load on the stomatognathic system and greater TMDs symptom severity. Accordingly, TMDs rehabilitation may benefit from incorporating strategies to reduce excessive digital device use alongside traditional therapeutic approaches.

The identified gaps in dental education—where students and clinicians remain focused on occlusal causes despite acknowledging psychosocial factors [53–55]—may have implications for the interpretation of our findings. By demonstrating the link between SB and behavioral addictions, our study emphasizes that bruxism cannot be explained solely by biological or mechanical factors. Indeed, bruxism is currently conceptualized as a phenomenon with a multifaceted etiology involving biological, psychological, and behavioral components, and its clinical consequences are not clear-cut, as the presence of bruxism does not inevitably result in pathological outcomes [56]. Accordingly, SB should be interpreted within a broader biopsychosocial framework rather than as a direct pathological entity.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, SB was assessed using self-report measures and the FAI, which may introduce misclassification compared with objective multidimensional tools such as polysomnography, electromyography, or the Standardised Tool for the Assessment of Bruxism (STAB) [57–59]. Although self-report methods are widely used in epidemiological research due to their feasibility and ability to recruit large samples, instrumental studies indicate that self-reported bruxism may be associated with a substantial rate of false positives and may overestimate SB prevalence [60, 61]. According to current international consensus, a definite diagnosis of SB requires instrument-based assessment, whereas subject-based assessment corresponds only to possible SB [59] Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly when examining associations between SB and behavioral variables. Second, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference regarding the relationship between smartphone addiction and SB. Future studies should prioritize longitudinal designs and objective methodologies. In this context, 24-hour home-based electromyographic monitoring systems may provide a more ecologically valid assessment of masseter muscle activity across both awake and sleep periods.

Additional limitations relate to potential confounding factors. SB has been associated with poor sleep quality, psychological factors (e.g., depression, anxiety, stress), and behavioral factors such as alcohol, caffeine, and tobacco use [62, 63], which were not specifically assessed or controlled for in this study. Future studies should also examine whether sleep quality or anxiety mediates the relationship between SB and smartphone addiction, and randomized controlled trials targeting behavioral interventions, such as smartphone restriction, may provide stronger evidence for clinical management. Despite these limitations, our study is the first to simultaneously examine SB alongside behavioral dimensions such as FTM and sedentary behavior, providing a broader perspective on SB within a contemporary lifestyle context.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study investigated differences between university students with SB and without SB regarding FTM, sedentary behavior, sleep-related variables, and smartphone use. The findings indicated that students with self-reported SB were similar in FTM, sedentary behaviors, total sleep duration, and sleep onset time, but showed higher smartphone addiction scores. These results suggest an association between bruxism and technology use patterns characterized by behavioral addiction features. However, given the cross-sectional design, no causal or directional inferences can be drawn from these findings. The absence of differences in FTM and sedentary behavior may relate to the relatively homogeneous lifestyle patterns of university students and the heterogeneous nature of sedentary behaviors. Understanding the interaction between daily life behaviors and SB requires further longitudinal studies involving diverse populations and objective assessment methods.
