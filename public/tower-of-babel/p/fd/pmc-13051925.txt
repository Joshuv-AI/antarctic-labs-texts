Hematological Profiles of Pulmonary Tuberculosis and Multi‐Drug‐Resistant Tuberculosis Patients at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, North‐Central Ethiopia: A Comparative Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Ayenew Berhan, Bekele Sharew, Andargachew Almaw, Shewaneh Damtie, Biruk Legese, Mulat Erkihun, Ayenew Assefa, Birhanemaskal Malkamu, Getu Abeje, Birhanu Getie, Yenealem Solomon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsTuberculosis is a major infectious disease that affects hematological profiles. However, limited data assess changes in hematological profiles among patients with pulmonary and multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the hematological profiles of pulmonary and multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis patients at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital from January 1 to May 30, 2023.MethodsAn institution‐based cross‐sectional study was conducted on 159 study participants selected by the consecutive sampling technique. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected through a structured questionnaire and checklist, respectively. A complete blood count was performed using an automated hematology analyzer, and a sputum sample was analyzed using the Gene Xpert MTB/RIF. The data were entered into Epi‐Data software and analyzed using SPSS version 20 software. The median values of hematological parameters were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsThe median and interquartile ranges of red blood cell parameters (red blood cell, hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean cell volume, and mean cell hemoglobin concentration) were significantly lower in pulmonary tuberculosis patients compared to those with multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis and healthy controls. In pulmonary tuberculosis patients, the magnitude of anemia and neutrophilia was 18.2% (29/159) and 9.4% (15/159), respectively. Meanwhile, eosinophilia 8.2% (13/159) and neutropenia 6.3% (10/159) were high among multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis patients.ConclusionPatients with pulmonary tuberculosis show lower red blood cell parameters, higher counts of white blood cells and neutrophils compared to multi‐drug‐resistant tuberculosis and healthy controls. Multi‐drug‐resistant tuberculosis patients also exhibit reduced red blood cells, hemoglobin, and hematocrit levels versus healthy individuals. Both pulmonary tuberculosis and multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis patients displayed various hematological abnormalities like anemia, leucopenia, neutropenia, eosinophilia, and thrombocytopenia. Therefore, assessing these hematological profiles can aid in diagnosing and monitoring tuberculosis patients.Keywords: anemia, hematological parameters, multi‐drug‐resistant tuberculosis, neutrophilia, pulmonary tuberculosis, thrombocytopenia

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is the foremost infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis bacilli (MTB). While it predominantly targets the lungs, it can also affect various other parts of the body [1]. TB is a significant global health threat, ranking as one of the top ten causes of death worldwide and standing as the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent. Based on the 2024 World Health Organization (WHO) Global TB Report, TB affected 10.8 million people (equivalent to 134 incident cases per 100,000 population) and resulted in approximately 1.25 million deaths [2]. The highest number of TB cases is found in the Asian and African continents, with China and India accounting for 40% of the world's TB cases. Ethiopia ranks 10th among 30 countries with a high TB burden, with an estimated incidence of 146 cases of all types of TB per 100,000 people [2].

Antimicrobial resistance poses a significant challenge to global public health and has emerged as a critical issue for global health security [3]. Multi‐drug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB) is caused by strains of MTB that are resistant to first‐line anti‐TB drugs, at least to isoniazid and rifampicin. According to the 2024 reports by WHO, globally, about 3.2% of new cases and 16% of chronic MDR TB cases had already been treated. This equates to almost half a million new cases of MDR‐TB with rifampicin resistance (MDR/RR‐TB) [2].

TB affects the formation and life span (survival) of cellular components in the bone marrow [4]. It disrupts normal hematopoiesis and leads to significant abnormalities in both the bone marrow and peripheral blood. The severity of the disease is especially pronounced in individuals co‐infected with Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) [5]. Changes in hematological parameters may serve as important biomarkers for the diagnosis, prognosis, and assessing treatment responses in patients [6, 7, 8]. Anemia, leukocytosis, leucopenia, neutrophilia, lymphopenia, thrombocytosis, and thrombocytopenia are common hematological abnormalities seen in patients with PTB and MDR‐TB [5].

Anemia is a common hematological abnormality found in both PTB and MDR‐TB patients [9, 10]. The prevalence of anemia among TB patients varies widely, ranging from 20% to 94% [11, 12, 13]. Some studies indicated that MDR‐TB patients may have a lower prevalence of anemia compared to those with PTB [14]. The development of anemia is mainly due to the suppression of erythropoiesis in the bone marrow. This suppression is caused by the release of inflammatory cytokines like tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interferon γ (IFN‑γ), and interleukin 6 (IL‐6). Other contributing factors include nutritional deficiencies and malabsorption syndrome [5, 15, 16]. The most common type of anemia observed in these patients is normocytic normochromic anemia, followed by microcytic hypochromic anemia [13].

The response of leukocytes in TB infection varies, ranging from leukocytosis to leucopenia [17]. Neutrophils play a crucial role in the early innate immune response against PTB infection. In patients with active PTB, neutrophils are significantly recruited as phagocytic cells [18]. Both neutrophilia and neutropenia have been observed in PTB and MDR‐TB patients [10, 19]. However, neutrophilia is more commonly reported than neutropenia during TB infection [20]. Neutropenia is rare and may be associated with inhibition of bone marrow granulopoiesis and micronutrient deficiency [20]. On the other hand, mononuclear cells, including monocytes and lymphocytes, serve as key effector cells in defending against PTB infection. The level of monocytes and lymphocytes in peripheral blood is indicative of an individual's immune response to the infection [21].

Platelets are primarily recognized for their role in hemostasis, but they also have a significant role in chronic inflammation. In studies using murine models, it has been shown that platelet granules contain various mediators, such as chemokines, cytokines, and growth factors, that may be involved in lung damage [22, 23]. TB infection has a significant impact on platelet counts, leading to varying conditions, such as thrombocytosis and thrombocytopenia. Approximately half of the patients with active TB infection are reported to have thrombocytosis [20]. Additionally, different studies have reported thrombocytosis and thrombocytopenia among PTB and MDR‐TB‐infected patients [17, 24, 25].

Different studies have shown the presence of hematological abnormalities among PTB and MDR‐TB patients separately. To our knowledge, limited studies have been conducted in Ethiopia; specifically, no study has been done in our study area to show the degree of change in hematological parameters between PTB and MDR‐TB patients to diagnose hematological abnormalities and monitor patients. Therefore, this study is designed to compare the hematological profiles of PTB and MDR‐TB patients with those of normal, healthy controls.

Methods and Materials

Study Design, Area, and Period

An institution‐based comparative cross‐sectional study was carried out at Debre Tabor Comprehensive Specialized Hospital (DTCSH) from January 1 to May 30, 2023. DTCSH is located in Amhara Regional State, at the administrative town of the South Gondar Zone, in Debre Tabor City. Debre Tabor is located in North Central Ethiopia, about 667 kilometers from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, and 103 kilometers from Bahir Dar, the capital of Amhara Regional State. The hospital is the biggest in the South Gondar Zone, which was founded in 1927, and it serves more than 2.7 million people in the catchment area. The hospital is organized into outpatient and inpatient departments (medical, surgical, pediatric, gynecology, emergency, TB clinic, and intensive care unit) with 125 inpatient beds. There are more than 1000 presumptive TB cases and nearly 100 MDR‐TB cases in the TB clinic.

Study Population

All patients who were diagnosed with PTB and MDR‐TB, and all healthy blood donors who fulfilled the inclusion criteria.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria: All PTB and MDR‐TB patients with a confirmed positive sputum test result done by the Gene Xpert MTB/RIF and who were willing to participate were included in this study. For healthy controls, all healthy blood donors who were willing to participate in this study were included.

Exclusion criteria: Patients who have known hematological malignancy, bleeding manifestations, chronic kidney and liver disease, patients who had extra‐pulmonary TB, and pregnant women were excluded from this study, as these conditions affect hematological parameters.

Sample Size Determination and Sampling Technique

The sample size was determined by using G‐power version 3.1.9.7 statistical software by considering effect size (0.25, medium), α error probability = 0.05, and power (1‐β) = 80%. Therefore, the total sample size was 159 (n = 159). As a result, the study participants were proportionally allocated to each group (53 study participants each).

Group 1: MDR TB patients = 53 study subjects

Group 2: PTB patients (who do not have a resistance pattern) = 53 study subjects

Group 3: Healthy controls = 53 study subjects

The study participants were selected using the consecutive sampling technique.

Operational Definitions

PTB: Patients who have a positive sputum test for TB using the Gene Xpert MTB/RIF test.

MDR‐TB: TB that is resistant to rifampicin and isoniazid, the most effective first‐line TB treatment drugs.

Relapse: A TB patient who had previously been treated for TB was declared cured or had fully completed treatment and was diagnosed with recurrent TB.

Healthy controls: Study participants who were healthy and confirmed non‐TB, HIV‐negative blood donors.

Anemia: defined as a hemoglobin (Hgb) level < 13 g/dL for males and Hgb levels < 12 g/dL [26]. On the other hand, leukocytosis, leucopenia, neutropenia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia were WBC > 10.2 × 103cells/μL, < 3.2 × 103cells/μL, 1.6 × 103cells/μL, 1 × 103cells/μL, and 150 × 103cells/μL, respectively [27].

Data Collection and Sample Processing

The socio‐demographic characteristics of study participants were collected using a pre‐tested, structured questionnaire. Clinical data were collected from patients' medical records using a checklist. Clinical nurses collected the data via chart reviews (medical record reviews) and face‐to‐face interviews.

Four milliliters (4 mL) of venous blood specimens were collected from each study participant using Tri‐potassium Ethylene Diamine Tetra Acetic Acid (K3EDTA) by the trained laboratory technologist. The hematological parameters of the study participants were analyzed using a five‐differential Sysmex XS‐500i Coulter cellular analysis system (Sysmex, Germany). The analyzer uses the principle of Coulter counting, spectrophotometry, and the impedance principle.

TB Diagnosis

Patients were carefully instructed to bring a sputum specimen that was free of saliva and other contaminants in a leak‐proof container. Both PTB and MDR‐TB diagnoses were made based on the Ethiopian Ministry of Health guidelines for TB diagnosis. The Ethiopian national guideline for TB, drug‐resistant TB, and Leprosy recommends the Gene Xpert MTB/RIF test as the initial diagnosis of all presumptive TB patients. A sputum specimen obtained from PTB patients was analyzed via the Gene Xpert MTB/RIF test to diagnose MDR‐TB. MDR‐TB was diagnosed from patients’ sputum according to Ethiopia's national diagnostic algorithm for MDR‐TB. It was detected when MTB is resistant to rifampicin, an effective first‐line anti‐TB drug, and when MTB is resistant to rifampicin, the bacterium is assumed to be 90% resistant to another effective first‐line drug, isoniazid (INH). In addition, a TB diagnosis was made clinically and by chest X‐ray [28].

Data Quality Assurance and Management

First, the questionnaire was prepared in English and then translated into Amharic, the local language. To ensure consistency and accuracy, the Amharic version was then back‐translated into English. The questionnaire was pretested on 5% of TB patients at the Felege Hiwot Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, located in Northwest Ethiopia, and modified based on the results of the pretest. To minimize technical and observer bias, training was given to data collectors before they started data collection.

In the overall laboratory procedures, pre‐analytical, analytical, and post‐analytical phases of quality assurance were maintained. Standard control materials were run to check the quality of working equipment and reagents. The manufacturer's instructions and standard operating procedures were strictly followed during sample collection and all laboratory procedures. In addition, all instruments used in the assay were regularly calibrated and maintained according to the manufacturer's recommendations and our internal protocols. The questionnaire was checked for consistency, clarity, and completeness by the principal investigator. The result of every test was properly recorded, reviewed, and transcribed.

Data Processing and Statistical Analysis

The data were checked for completeness and entered into Epi‐Data software version 3.0.4. Then, the data were cleaned and transferred into the Statistical Package for Social Science version 20 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) for analysis. Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize the descriptive data. The normal distribution of continuous variables was determined by the Shapiro–Wilk test and histogram. The data of hematological parameters were not normally distributed based on the result of the Shapiro–Wilk test and histogram. The homogeneity of variance was checked by Levene's test. The median and interquartile range (IQR) were determined for each hematological parameter and presented in tables and figures. The non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis H test and Dunn‐Bonferroni pairwise comparisons were used to compare the hematological parameters between PTB, MDR‐TB, and healthy control groups. A p‐value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical Consideration

This study was approved by the Research Review Committee of the Medical Laboratory Science Education and Service Directorate, College of Health Sciences, Debre Tabor University (Reference No. CHS/Lab/112/2022). A support letter to conduct a study was received from the Department of Medical Laboratory Science and forwarded to the DTCSH. In addition, a permission letter to conduct research was obtained from the DTCSH.

Written informed consent was obtained from the study participant after the objective of the study was explained. For study subjects below the age of 18 years, informed assent and informed consent were obtained from their legal guardians or parents. Codes were used to identify study participants. All information obtained from the study participants was kept confidential. Study participants who had hematological abnormalities were linked to the DTCSH TB clinic for treatment. All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations (Declaration of Helsinki).

However, PLT count (p = 0.25) and Eosinophil (p = 0.08) count do not show a statistically significant median difference between PTB, MDR‐TB, and healthy controls (Table 3 and Figure 1).

Discussion

This comparative cross‐sectional study assessed the hematological parameters of TB patients (MDR and PTB) compared to normal healthy controls. Based on this study, variations in hematological parameters were demonstrated among MDR groups, PTB groups, and healthy control groups. Hematological profiles should be integrated into routine TB care to aid in the early detection of TB through several mechanisms, although it's crucial to understand that they are not a direct diagnostic tool for TB.

In our study, PTB patients had significantly lower median values of RBC, Hgb, HCT, MCV, MCH, and MCHC when compared with healthy controls and MDR‐TB groups (p < 0.05). In addition, the prevalence of anemia was high (18.2%) among PTB patients. This finding is consistent with previous studies done in Gondar [17]., Addis Ababa [29], Uganda [30], India [31], India [32], South Korea [4], and China [11]. This might be because anemia is the most frequent risk factor for PTB [33]. Also, anemia is a common comorbidity among PTB patients, especially in developing countries [34]. The causes of anemia among PTB patients include iron deficiency and anemia of chronic disease [35, 36], inflammatory mediators of hematopoiesis [4], and the disease itself [5]. Iron deficiency and chronic inflammation are the predominant contributing factors to anemia among PTB patients [13, 37]. The observed phenomenon could be attributed to the effect of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and TNF‐α, which can disrupt erythropoiesis through mechanisms including reduced erythropoietin (EPO) hormone production, suppression of bone marrow response to EPO, and impaired iron metabolism [16, 38].

Although there was no statistically significant median difference between MDR‐TB and PTB patients, MDR‐TB patients had lower median values of RBC, Hgb, and HCT,4.55 (4.15–4.73) × 1012/μL, 13.3 (12.4–14.4)g/dL, and 38.7 (36.2–40.9)%, respectively, when compared with healthy controls.

Anemia was observed in 4.4% of MDR‐TB patients, a rate notably lower than that found in PTB patients. This discrepancy may be attributed to the intensive care approach for MDR‐TB, where patients are admitted to a TB clinic, monitored closely by healthcare providers, and receive prompt treatment. In contrast, many PTB patients are newly diagnosed and have not yet accessed the comprehensive support and timely intervention available to those with MDR‐TB. This is supported by the findings of a study done by Putra PM et al. that showed the incidence of anemia is reduced after anti‐MDR‐TB treatment [14]. However, different studies reported anemia among MDR‐TB patients [10, 39, 40]. The possible reason for the reduction in RBC parameters among MDR‐TB patients might be due to the action of pro‐inflammatory cytokines like IL6, which alter iron homeostasis and impair bone marrow erythropoiesis [18].

Based on the findings of this study, PTB patients had significantly higher median values of WBC count (p < 0.001) and absolute neutrophil count (p = 0.001) when compared to the MDR‐TB group and healthy controls. This finding is in agreement with studies done in India [31], India [5, 41], Jimma [24], and Addis Ababa [29]. The most likely explanation might be due to leukocytosis being a cardinal feature of TB. Neutrophilia might be due to an immune defense against Mycobacterium infection [19]. Therefore, neutrophils (polymorph nuclear cells) are actively involved in immune defense against active TB infection.

This study demonstrated neutropenia among MDR‐TB patients. MDR‐TB patients had a higher prevalence of neutropenia than PTB patients (6.3% vs. 5%). Similarly, different studies have reported the existence of neutropenia among TB patients [9, 10, 17, 25]. The possible explanation might be due to activated T cell‐mediated inhibition of granulopoiesis, excessive neutrophil migration, marrow fibrosis, and hypersplenism [6, 10]. Another explanation for the existence of neutropenia among MDR‐TB patients might be due to the effects of anti‐TB drugs. A study done by Putra PM et al. reported that neutrophil counts were significantly decreased after receiving an anti‐MDR TB regimen [14].

In this study, MDR‐TB patients had significantly lower median (IQR) monocyte counts when compared with healthy controls and PTB groups. Similarly, different studies also supported our findings [42, 43]. Monocytes are precursor cells of macrophages that play a vital role in fighting against microbial infection [44]. The possible explanation might be due to the fact that a reduction in monocytes shows advanced stages of disease (MDR‐TB) [45]. In addition, the formation of granulomas as a means of immunity may reduce peripheral monocytes.

Eosinophilia was more common among MDR‐TB patients than PTB patients. However, the involvement of eosinophils in TB infection is not well understood. A study by Bohrer AC et al. reported a higher frequency of peripheral blood eosinophilia among patients with latent TB infection [46]. Additionally, Putra PM et al. reported that MDR‐TB patients' eosinophil count increases after taking anti‐MDR drugs [14]. However, those studies did not explain the role of eosinophils in TB infection. However, a study conducted in Ethiopia reported the highest burden of asymptomatic helminth co‐infection among TB patients, and there was a correlation between eosinophilia and asymptomatic helminth infection [47]. Prakash Babu S et al. suggested that peripheral eosinophilia (dominant Th2 response) might be due to the prevalence of helminth infection in developing countries [47]. Further studies should be done to assess the impact of eosinophils on TB.

Based on the findings of the study, thrombocytopenia was found in 6.3% of MDR‐TB patients and 10.1% of PTB patients. The prevalence of thrombocytopenia among PTB patients is in line with a study done in Gondar, 8% [17], India (9%) [5], and Pakistan 13% [25]. Rajesh et al. also reported a decrease in platelet count in PTB patients [31]. Different processes have been suggested as potential causes of thrombocytopenia in PTB patients, including immunological processes, bone marrow fibrosis, direct megakaryocyte infection, and hypersplenism [48, 49, 50]. But our finding was lower than the study done in Nigeria,14.29% [51], and Jigawa State, Nigeria,20% [19]. The possible reasons for the discrepancy might be due to differences in sample size (53 PTB patients in our study vs. 150 PTB patients in Jigawa state), geographical location, and study population.

Different studies also reported the existence of thrombocytopenia among MDR‐TB patients. A study with MDR TB patients in Iraq reported a high level of thrombocytopenia,16% [10]. Another study by Putra PM et al. showed a significant decrease in platelet count among MDR‐TB patients after receiving anti‐MDR‐TB drugs [14]. The possible explanation for thrombocytopenia in MDR‐TB patients might be due to immune‐mediated platelet destruction [52].

Lymphopenia is another hematological abnormality reported among MDR‐TB and PTB patients. However, there is no significant median (IQR) difference in lymphocyte count between MDR‐TB, PTB, and healthy controls. Lymphopenia was observed in 10.8% of PTB patients and 5% of MDR‐TB patients. Different studies support lymphopenia among PTB patients [17, 19, 24, 29, 53]. A study by Abdulrazzaq NZ reported lymphopenia among MDR‐TB patients [10]. The possible explanation might be due to the localization of MTB‐specific lymphocytes in the lung for infection clearance. As a result, a lower frequency of lymphocytes in peripheral blood would indicate persistent infection [54].

Conclusion

In this study, changes were observed in the median (IQR) values of hematological parameters in patients with PTB and MDR‐TB compared to healthy individuals. PTB patients exhibited lower median values for RBC parameters, including RBC count, hemoglobin (Hgb), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC). However, they showed elevated white blood cell (WBC) and neutrophil counts. Monocyte levels were significantly reduced in MDR‐TB patients compared to both PTB patients and healthy controls. Anemia was the most commonly observed abnormality in both the PTB and MDR‐TB groups. MDR‐TB patients had higher incidences of leukopenia, neutropenia, and eosinophilia, whereas PTB patients frequently presented with anemia, leukocytosis, neutrophilia, lymphopenia, and thrombocytopenia.

The evaluation of hematological parameters may serve as a useful tool for the diagnosis and monitoring of both MDR‐TB and PTB. Understanding these hematological changes is crucial for tracking disease progression, minimizing complications, and assessing treatment response. Further large‐scale studies are recommended to explore the relationship between hematological profiles and various clinical factors in PTB and MDR‐TB patients.

Limitations of the Study

Due to the cross‐sectional nature of the study, this study did not employ a cause‐and‐effect relationship. Potential inflammatory markers such as cytokines (IL6, TNF‐alpha, and IL‐1), chemokines, and iron pathways abnormalities associated with TB were not evaluated. The effect of anti‐ TB drugs on hematological parameters and the actual treatment status of the patients was not assessed. Therefore, further longitudinal studies with a large sample size are needed to identify robust markers.
