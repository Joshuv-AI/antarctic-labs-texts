Environmental Inequality in Estimated Cancer Risk from Airborne Toxic Exposure across United States Communities from 2011 to 2019
Authors: Patrick Hurbain, Matthew J Strickland, Yan Liu, Dingsheng Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUS Census Bureau data were matched to U.S. Environmental Protection Agency estimated cancer risks from airborne toxics from 2011 to 2019 to explore environmental inequality with nationwide census tract resolution. Spearman correlations showed modest associations between various socioeconomic status factors and estimated cancer risk. Multiple linear regression analyses show positive associations with increased estimated cancer risk (p < 0.05) for high proportions of Blacks in suburban and rural areas. A positive relationship with estimated cancer risk was found for increasing proportions of Asians and Hispanics in nonurban areas. Urban tracts that suffer from the highest estimated cancer risks were concentrated among the communities with a population of higher proportion of minorities. While environmental inequality seems to have improved across the examined years for certain demographics with respect to estimated cancer risk from air toxics, equity is far from achieved, and future work in identifying the sources of environmental inequality could help in achieving a more just environment.Keywords: environmental inequality, air toxics, cancer risk, rural-urban continuum, nationwide

Introduction

Environmental justice is the fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national origin, or income with respect to the environment.1 Within the realm of environmental justice, environmental inequality is included, which examines the distributions of harms and risks.2 Previous work on environmental inequality has shown strong relationships between various social demographic factors and harmful environmental exposures with poor health outcomes. Black and Hispanic families tend to be located closer to Toxic Release Inventory (TRI) sites, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) designated superfund sites, and military facilities.3−5 The proximity to these polluting facilities and other nonstationary sources results in higher exposure to toxic pollutants for communities in low-income areas and areas with increasing proportions of minority residents in the U.S., as demonstrated in statewide5−8 and nationwide9−11 analyses. Previous studies showed regional differences in environmental inequalities based on U.S. EPA regions.12−14 On the finer scales, urban versus rural areas have been shown to have twice the concentrations of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons in air, the majority of which come from diesel fuel.15,16 Additionally, previous research also found similar trends in air toxic exposure and higher rates of childhood asthma among low-income and Black children at the zip code level.17

However, most past studies analyzing environmental inequality have been limited to a single year and have not examined changes over time. Notable exceptions include some recent environmental inequality analysis analyzing particulate matter (PM) concentrations across the U.S. and zip code tabulation areas from 2000 to 2016,18 PM concentrations across the U.S. at the tract level over 36 years,19 and exposure to criteria air pollutants across the U.S. from 1990 to 2010.20 The temporal examination in these studies enhanced our understanding of environmental inequalities in the U.S. However, each study used a selected few targeted compounds, which may not represent the holistic environmental health risks from the hundreds of air toxics to which people are exposed. An earlier study using results based on stationary industrial sources of hundreds of pollutants examined the trend of environmental inequality from 1995 to 2004 and provides a wider coverage of pollutants.14 Nevertheless, complementing stationary industrial sites with other sources of pollution is desired to present a more comprehensive view of how environmental inequality has changed.

To help reduce toxic air pollution and assist stakeholders in understanding where health risks may be high, the U.S. EPA created the National Air Toxics Assessment (NATA), which recently evolved into the Air Toxics Screening Assessment (AirToxScreen). It is a tool which provides hundreds of toxic air pollutant concentrations from all outdoor sources as well as cancer and noncancer risk estimates using plumage, dispersion, and estimated lifetime exposure models for every census tract across the U.S. Thanks to its comprehensiveness in pollutant coverage, consideration of both concentration and toxicity of pollutants, and national scale on census tract resolution, NATA has seen wide use in environmental inequality studies.3,8−12,17 In this study, we used 2011 and 2014 NATA estimates alongside the most recent results from AirToxScreen for 2017–2019 across the United States and various socioeconomic status (SES) factors inclusive of all census tracts with available data and measured pollutants. This allowed us to (1) evaluate the distribution of estimated cancer risk across the U.S. over time; (2) examine how SES factors are correlated with the estimated cancer risk; and (3) infer whether environmental inequality conditions are improving or worsening with respect to various SES factors.

Materials and Methods

Estimated Cancer Risk

Estimated cancer risk from air toxics at the census tract level was retrieved from the NATA and AirToxScreen data sets assembled by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency.21−23 It should be noted that despite the name change, the fundamental methodological frameworks of NATA and AirToxScreen are highly similar to AirToxScreen succeeding NATA in 2022. Details about the methodologies used by NATA and AirToxScreen are described in their technical documents22,23 and summarized here. The NATA and AirToxScreen data sets are evaluations of hundreds of air toxics using plumage, dispersion, and estimated lifetime exposure models, alongside emission sources with information for each census tract in the United States. Both data set types use CMAQ/AERMOD dispersion modeling with HAPEM7 inhalation exposure modeling based on the National Emissions Inventory (including secondary reactions) to estimate lifetime cancer risks considering the different toxicities of different toxics emitted to the outdoor air for the majority of U.S. census tracts, making them comparable instruments within each evaluation. A visualization is shown in Supporting Information Figure 1. The estimated lifetime cancer risks, expressed in per million over lifetime, thus represent the hypothetical additional cancer risk individuals may experience over their lifetimes. This risk assumes that said individuals are exposed to all modeled outdoor air toxics in the census tracts they live in 24 h a day over the hypothetical 70 year lifetime. NATA and AirToxScreen also provide estimated noncancer risks in hazard quotients. However, noncancer risks are based on the assumption that only when exposures exceed thresholds (hazard quotients larger than one) will harm manifest, making them less suitable to be interpreted quantitatively in a continuous manner compared to estimated lifetime cancer risks.24 Thus, estimated cancer risks were chosen for this study. Of course, people do not usually live their entire lives in the same place, and emissions of toxics change constantly. In addition, there are many other factors that can increase the risk of developing cancers. Hence, the estimated lifetime cancer risks should not be viewed as a prediction of the incidence of cancers with accuracy. Nevertheless, these estimates from NATA/AirToxScreen can be viewed as an indicator for air pollution severity in that year's modeled emissions, considering a wide range of pollutants’ chemical properties, toxicities, and diverse human exposures.

The most recently released publicly available data sets for years for NATA (2011 and 2014) alongside the AirToxScreen (2017–2019) were used in this analysis. All emission types were used in this analysis, including on-road, nonroad, major, area, and background sources, as the cancer risk estimates include information regarding all these sources. Despite similarity in the overarching framework used in different years of NATA and AirToxScreen, changes in methodologies such as source inventory improvement, modeling modifications, background calculation revisions, and health benchmark updates do occur over time.25 A notable example is that the EPA updated the risk value for ethylene oxide in 2016 and resulted in systematic elevation of estimated cancer risks for NATA 2014 compared to NATA 2011 in areas where ethylene oxide was the driver of estimated cancer risks.25 In addition, to avoid giving users the sense of “false precision” of estimated cancer risks reported, the AirToxScreen rounded the risk to one significant figure as opposed to many digits after the decimal points in NATA (e.g., an estimated cancer risk of 31.689··· per million in NATA would be 30 per million in AirToxScreen, real examples for national average estimated cancer risks reported). As such, direct comparison of estimated cancer risk across different release years of the NATA or AirToxScreen results can be misleading. Thus, we calculated and used Z-scores for estimated cancer risk from 2011 to 2019 in this study because the estimated cancer risks were approximately normally distributed and Z-scores reflect their standardized rankings. In this way, we can avoid misinterpreting the estimated cancer risks across years while preserving the power to examine the relativity of environmental burdens of air toxicities among communities. We also rounded the estimated risks from NATA 2011 and 2014 to one significant digit before Z-score calculation to be consistent with the practice of AirToxScreen.

Socioeconomic Status Data

A total of 76,728 census tracts are included in the U.S. Census Bureau (USCB).26 Census data provide estimates in multiple ACS formats, including 1, 3, and 5 year estimates for all factors except racial estimates, which are calculated every 10 years from census collection. Among ACS data tables, reliability issues in some estimates for demographic data have been noted due to high margins of error,27 and others have shown that there are higher coefficients of variation that are regional with respect to economic factors like median income;28 however, use of static estimates does not necessarily capture impacts of estimated migration and population changes with respect to demographic makeup of various areas. In lieu of a static population that precedes or exceeds the study period for the NATA data sets (the 2010 or 2020 decennial census), ACS 5 year estimates were chosen for their high reliability, large sample size, and precision in examining tracts. Likewise, the corresponding 5 year ACS estimates for racial, economic, and educational information for each air toxics data set were selected (detail in Table S11) to represent the three most commonly examined facets of SES in studies of environmental inequality and reflect approximate changes in population from data set year releases. Census data was linked at the tract level using geographic identifier (GEOID) codes.

Variables used to represent race were generated to measure the disparity between the majority white population with various minority populations similar to others29 and listed as follows: the ratios of Black Alone to White Alone, Asian Alone to White Alone, Hispanic Alone to White Alone, and individuals of mixed and multiple races alongside native peoples, including Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander and Native American/American Indian, as Other to White Alone. Despite the similar essence of characterizing racial densities in communities, our choice of ratios of minorities to Whites deviates from the more commonly used percentage of minorities as seen in other studies.30 This is because using this ratio enables an interpretation for communities with extremely high minority densities better as it can theoretically be scaled infinitely high, while the percentage is capped at 100%. Median household income was chosen as the metric of economic status for each tract for its simplicity for interpretation, strong correlation with other more structured economic status (e.g., socioeconomic advantage, working class, deprivation, etc.),31 and wide adoption in related studies.6,32 A ratio of percentage of college completion/percentage of high school completion for given census tracts was created to indicate the educational attainment for that tract. Due to the large right skew resulting from many census tracts having very low college attainment, the log of this ratio was taken to normalize the spread of data. The rural-urban continuum code (RUCC) was also included to reflect the urban and rural environments. These environments can be vastly different in terms of pollutants emitted, which can be reflected by the estimated cancer risks due to both population and economic activities. Economic and industrial differences along the rural-urban continuum that affect environmental equality may also exist. Hence, we consider it important to consider this factor in our analysis. The RUCC codes range from 1 to 9, with 1 being populous metropolitan area and 9 being the most rural as defined by the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA).33 We considered RUCC codes 1 through 3 as urban areas, RUCC codes 4 through 6 as suburban areas, and RUCC codes 7 through 9 as rural areas, which can be seen by population and proximity to metropolitan areas in Table S1. These selected data are collectively referred to as SES factors in this study.

Statistical Methods

Data Descriptions and Categories

NATA/AirToxScreen and census estimates were linked by the GEOID code and then analyzed and mapped using SAS V9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). For the Spearman correlation calculations, the RUCC codes were analyzed as discrete ordinal variables. Spearman correlation coefficients between Z-scores of estimated cancer risk and SES factors including the race ratio, median household income, education attainment, and RUCC were calculated for each state and nationwide at the census tract level. The correlation coefficient confidence intervals were calculated using Bonett and Wright transformation to control for the effect of kurtosis of each variable and estimate confidence intervals.34 These correlations were used to provide a broad view of environmental inequality. Race ratios were adjusted into six categories to better represent the spread of the data, including zero, L1 for values 0.01 through 0.09, L2 for values 0.1 through 0.99, L3 for values one through 9.9, L4 for values 10 through 99.9, and L5 for values 100 or higher. Categorizing race ratios into these categories could also help in revealing possible nonlinear relationship between racial status and environmental inequality—e.g., inequality may become disproportionately more severe in the communities of higher minority race ratio categories.

Tests of Normality and Homoskedasticity

Kolomogirov-Smirnoff and Anderson–Darling tests were performed to analyze the normality of each continuous variable. Distribution plots including histograms and Q–Q plots were generated in order to determine the skew and kurtosis of each continuous variable across U.S. census tracts, including estimated cancer risk, race ratios, median income, and education attainment. Observed distributions of skewed data were used to create categories for race ratios used in statistical modeling.

Multivariate linear regression models were generated to analyze the association of selected SES factors and the Z-scored estimated cancer risk. Year Stratified Modeling

To control for the differences in years on the association between SES factors and estimated cancer risk, year stratified multivariate models and RUCC and year stratified models for years 2011, 2014, 2017, 2018, and 2019 were generated, as shown in Supporting Information Text 1.

Spatial Modeling

To test spatial dependence among estimated cancer risk and relevant SES factors, spatial regression models were generated alongside ordinary least-squares (OLS) marginal modeling adapted from the main marginal model described above as a sensitivity test. The Moran test for spatial autocorrelation was performed, demonstrating spatial dependence between SES factors and estimated cancer risks. Lagrange multiplier diagnostic tests for spatial dependence were performed to identify optimal spatial regression methodology, and the spatial autoregressive (SAR) model was selected for eight states, four highly racially diverse states (Hawaii, California, Nevada, and Maryland) and four more homogeneous states (Maine, West Virginia, Vermont, and New Hampshire) using the diversity index within the 2020 decennial census as the measure of diversity,35 as shown in Supporting Information Text 2.

Census Estimate Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis on census demographic estimates was performed. The methodology followed the same analysis as the marginal modeling; only in lieu of the ACS 5 year data products, the NATA/AirToxScreen data sets were paired using the 2010 and 2020 decennial census race estimates, replacing the ACS 5 year race estimates.

Nonparametric Analyses

Additional nonparametric analysis of extreme values of estimated cancer risk was also performed, examining the relationship between the tracts of extreme risks and the chosen SES factors. The year and RUCC stratified 99th percentile (highest risk) and first percentile (lowest risk) of Z-scored estimated cancer risk were extracted at the census tract level alongside SES factors. The ratios of the mean values for SES factors between the worst and best tracts were taken to show the difference between means at the extremes of estimated cancer risk along the SES lines. This analysis was done to explore the heterogeneous aspects of environmental inequality, as pooled analyses of national data may mask such features in environmental inequality.

Since pollution distribution is largely dependent on the location where they are emitted and areas closer to the source tend to have higher concentrations, some spatial analysis may be desired to address spatial autocorrelation. However, we consider this type of analysis outside the scope of this study, as we focus on the exposure to air toxics in the context of environmental inequality. That is, whether the individuals in a census tract were exposed to higher concentrations of air toxics because they had higher emissions within their tract or their adjacent tract(s) had higher emissions is not important to the fact they experience higher risks regardless.

Rural vs Urban Environments

Due to the potential influence of RUCC, results of Model 1 and Model 2 were reanalyzed by RUCC strata. Results of noninteraction RUCC stratified Model 1 can be seen in Table S3 in the Supporting Information. Briefly, across all RUCC strata including urban, suburban, and rural environments, increasing ratios of Black individuals to White individuals are strongly positively associated with increasing Z-scored estimated cancer risk indicating significant environmental inequalities. This trend is seen for Asian to White categories only in urban areas. Mostly negative associations with Z-scored estimated cancer risk are seen for all strata of Hispanic to White and Other to White ratios. Education attainment and income were consistently negatively associated with Z-scored estimated cancer risk, with their effects higher in rural compared to urban or suburban areas. Compared to non-RUCC stratified results, education attainment became a protective factor, and the protective effect from income increased.

Results of RUCC stratified interaction Model 2 (Table S4 in the Supporting Information) demonstrate significant environmental inequalities among increasing fractions of Black to White individuals in urban areas. This environmental inequality appeared to be weakened from 2011 to 2017 but has since rebounded. Similar observation can be made for suburban and rural areas, albeit statistical significance is weaker. Progressively higher fractions of Other to White individuals in urban areas are significantly protective against Z-scored estimate cancer risk. For the other races/RUCC combination, no conclusive observations can be made, as the majority of the pertinent results are statistically insignificant. Median household income remains a significant protective factor for all three RUCC strata, while education attainment is a risk factor in urban areas, protective factor in rural areas for Z-scored estimated cancer risk, and statistically insignificant for suburban areas.

Environmental Inequalities among the Rural Urban Continuum

A visualization of the magnitude of environmental inequalities can be seen in Figure 1 for Black and Asian race ratios in urban and suburban environments and all races in Supporting Information Figure 2. Multiple linear regressions were performed holding SES factors other than race constant at mean values, with education attainment held at −1.396, and median household income held at $60,668, both the average of all the analyzed years. In urban areas, increasing ratios of Black to White individuals, Asian to White individuals, and Hispanic to White individuals (with the exception of Hispanic outnumbered White more than 100 to one) were positively associated with Z-scored estimated cancer risk, while increasing ratios of Other to White individuals were negatively associated with Z-scored estimated cancer risk. Examination of Tracts with High and Low Estimated Cancer Risks

Results of analyses performed exploring the relationship with estimated cancer risk and various SES factors analyzing the top 99th percentile (worst) and the first percentile (best) are shown in Figure 2. Tracts with highest estimated cancer risks were predominately located in urban areas, while those with lowest estimated cancer risks were more evenly distributed across the rural-urban continuum (Table S5 in Supporting Information). The variations of racial factors in the worst and best tracts were much larger than factors of education and income regardless of the year or rural-urban continuum (Table S6 in Supporting Information). The tracts with the highest estimated cancer risk in urban areas have significantly more Black and Hispanic individuals relative to White individuals, although this contrast tends to be less extreme for later years compared with 2011. The opposite is true for Asian and Other individuals relative to White individuals, where there is an apparent inequity in favor of having areas with more Asian (except for 2011 in urban areas) or Other individuals. Populations in the worst tracts are generally with less income, a pattern that does not change over the years. Comparison among the worst and best tracts showed no obvious patterns for education. Explaining this dynamic phenomenon among the extremes in estimated cancer risk distributions and sociodemographic factors becomes difficult in part because of unaccounted for policy changes with respect to air quality through time and by geographic area, methodology improvements in air quality sampling and risk estimation, population migration dynamics, and other systemic confounding variables. Among the most extreme areas, there appears to be a persistent protective factor with respect to increasing income, no meaningful impact of education attainment, and significant differences with respect to demographic makeup across urbanicity.

Results of Year Stratified Models

Results of the analytical models stratified by year to control for the yearly changes between SES factors and Z-scored estimated showed no meaningful difference from the crude marginal model and the marginal interaction model (Table S7 for pooled results and Table S8 for RUCC stratified results). Similar trends and magnitude can be seen with respect to increasing Z-scored risks and increasing proportions of Black to White, Asian to White, and RUCC status, and a similar protective effect can be seen with respect to income, increasing proportions of Other to White and Hispanic to White, with exception of level 4 (a ratio of ten to one Hispanic to White), where an increase in estimated risk was found across all year stratified models.

Results of Spatial Regression Models

Results of spatial regression modeling showed mostly findings similar to marginal models when controlling for spatial autocorrelation among tract risks, as shown in Table S9. Each state’s OLS estimates mirrored the national marginal estimates in the direction of association with respect to race and RUCC. After controlling for spatial correlation, the instances where selected SES factors in this study showing a statistically significant effect decreased compared to the OLS estimates; however, out of 56 pairs (8 states, 7 SES factors each) of estimates from OLS and SAR, the majority of the estimates maintained their effect size, direction of association, and trend with increasing estimated cancer risk, with significant positive spatial coefficient for each state. Spatial analyses in future research are merited due to the nature of spatial dependence and varying effect sizes across states mirroring findings by others;4,14,30 however, model assumptions of homogeneity, stationarity, and error structures are some underlying significant issues in this approach in addition to the difficulty in interpreting findings. Instead, a local model like geographically weighted regression models over large land areas like EPA regions or nationwide spatial analyses explicitly addresses these limitations.

Results of Decennial Census Sensitivity Analysis

Results of the use of decennial census products in lieu of ACS 5 year race estimates and the influence on Z-scored estimated cancer risk can be seen in Table S10. The results between data sets are similar in both magnitude and direction, with two meaningful differences. Using the decennial census products, the magnitude in the association with Z-scored estimated cancer risk is much lower for increasing proportions of Asian to White individuals across census tracts, although the trend is in the same direction as the ACS 5 year marginal model: in the decennial census estimates paired to Z-scored estimated cancer risks, there is no statistically significant association with Z-scored estimated cancer risk, except for L4 and L5, which are positively associated with Z-scored estimated cancer risk. The parametric estimates for all other SES factors are similar.

Overall Status of Environmental Inequality by Estimated Cancer Risk from Air Toxics

This study is one of the first works involving the recently released AirToxScreen data alongside NATA data sets of previous years analyzing environmental inequalities. As a result, the findings from this study serve as a timely update of the evolving environmental inequalities reflected by the estimated cancer risk from diverse ambient air toxic pollutants for all available census tracts across the whole country. Compared to previous studies also examining temporal changes in environmental inequality on the national scale,14,18,19 this study covers more pollutants and sources, making it potentially more comprehensively examining the evolution of environmental inequality in the U.S. over the past years. The census tract level analysis offers higher precision in the estimates, which was attained due to having more coverage than simple county or state level estimates, and thus, the likelihood of spurious findings is lessened by having a more robust data set in this study. By analyzing the association of other SES factors and estimated cancer risk while stratifying by RUCC, we are better able to see the variability by the rural-urban continuum in the association between race and estimated cancer risk because anthropogenic pollution from human activity like transport on freeways or industry proximity is expected to be higher in or near urban environments.

Reported findings in this study indicated that the changes in conditions of environmental inequalities between years are highly variable depending on race and rural-urban continuum, not of other SES factors considered in this analysis. Varying degrees of increased Z-scored estimated cancer risk across SES factors were observed across all included data sets from NATA and AirToxScreen. On average, increasing Z-scored estimated cancer risk from air toxics was observed among census tracts with higher fractions of Black, Asian, and Hispanic individuals relative to White individuals in urban census tracts when holding other SES factors constant. This observation held true for all tested racial groups except for Other to White. Increasing median household incomes of census tracts was protective against estimated cancer risk across all environmental types on the rural urban continuum. Education attainment tended to be a risk factor, although it could be confounded by the correlation between higher education and job prospects in urban areas, where cancer risks are higher in general. Results of RUCC stratified Models 2 suggest potential improvement in environmental inequality for Black communities in 2017 and 2014 compared to 2011 with respect to Z-scored estimated cancer risk; however, this pattern seemingly reversed in 2018 and 2019. These results supplement those from other studies analyzing estimated cancer risk and racially influenced environmental inequality: Apelberg et al.36 who found more risk for higher fractions of Black individuals and lower income; Fang and Tseng37 who found increasing segregation with higher estimated cancer risk; Ferlay et al.38 who demonstrated the relationship between estimated cancer risk and income; and Wilson et al.8 who demonstrated variability in estimated cancer risk and race by RUCC strata. Greater exposure to air toxics has been previously found to be associated with other SES factors including income, RUCC, and gender.3,8,10,12,17

One important finding of this research was the magnitude of the increase in estimated cancer risk from air toxics for increasing racial minorities in tracts with high proportions of Black and Asian individuals across urban environments. Of note, we found that the level of environmental inequality suffered by Asians is second only (and not far) to Blacks, which agrees with results from Grineski et al.10 This finding highlights the need to address this often-overlooked issue in disparities in harmful environmental exposures mirroring prior research.10,12 The largest inequality in estimated cancer risk in this study is among these groups in urban environments, indicating widespread significant environmental inequalities with respect to estimated cancer risk in the most populated areas across the US from 2011 through 2019. Tracts analyzed with high potential exposure to air toxics indicate that exposure can be far from equitable, and tract-level SES factors are significantly associated with that disparity. The disparities observed comparing the high-to-low-risk tracts are stark. It is therefore important to recognize the heterogeneity of environmental inequality, which may not be fully revealed in an aggregated analysis.

Environmental justice focuses on the key concept of equity, where equity in health is the absence of systemic disparity in health or its determinants between groups by wealth, social status, or prestige.39 One notable finding of our work was the observation of complete inequity across the rural-urban continuum. Briefly, while it was observed that the Hispanic to White race ratios approached equity with respect to estimated cancer risk in urban environments, there was nearly complete inequity along race lines for every race ratio in every RUCC. If there were no environmental inequality, we would expect null results with respect to race, education, and income. We found the opposite in every case, where inequality may be improving in some regards or worsening but inequity nevertheless persists. The seemingly improving environmental inequality among some demographics was similar with the finding from a previous study on inequal exposure to six criteria pollutants in the U.S. from 1990 to 2010.20 However, other studies examining different pollutants have reported varying results: environmental inequality largely unchanged for industrial air pollutants from 1995 to 200414 and PM2.5 from 1981 to 201619 or increased for PM2.5 exposure relative to safety standards between 2000 and 2016.18 The different findings of changes in environmental inequality in the U.S. from these studies may be due to the pollutants analyzed, time periods selected, and analytical methods. Hence, the answer to whether environmental inequality improved in the U.S. in the past few decades may be nuanced and highly dependent on how it is investigated. Nevertheless, all of these studies of environmental inequality in the U.S. over the years, including this study, all agree that disparities have been persistent.

Limitations

As we focus on race, it should be pointed out that one potential limitation of this work includes the census racial demographic information and its propensity for error. Briefly, racial composition of a community can be subject to some classification error, where one’s ethnicity self-identifies differently than expected, slightly skewing race ratios. For example, individuals among African American communities or Hispanic communities can be identified as mixed race, Black, Other, Hispanic, or Native, and this effect further can be magnified by areas with heavy crossover with White individuals.40 This potential error is likely to be marginal across all counties and most likely would not skew the net result. Relatedly, we did not further examine the enclaves under the racial categories in this study and could have missed more nuanced related details. Past research indicated that variations can be significant among enclaves within the larger racial groups such as Chinese versus Japanese within the Asian group10 or Arab enclave versus nonenclave within the White group.11

The complex relationship between class and race should not be overlooked because, while increasing incomes among census tracts show lower estimated cancer risk, higher proportions of Black residents also tend to have lower incomes. In addition, other research analyzing similar SES factors found that SES is not as protective for Black populations relative to White and Hispanic populations.14 Similar effects are seen in other works associating income and race in the U.S. with estimated cancer risk and the interaction between race and income’s influence in estimated cancer risk.41,42 This intersectionality among different SES factors is less explored in this study. This is partially limited by the conventional multilevel approach we used; the model will grow exponentially complex as more interactions between factors are considered. Recently, an Eco-Intersectional Multilevel Model was developed and applied to NATA 2014 data to study environmental inequality.30 In this approach, the authors aggregated all census tracts into intersectional strata based on various dimensions of SES. The result was 72,103 census tracts nested in 216 unique social strata for further analysis. While this Eco-Intersectional Multilevel Model offers advantages over conventional approaches in better reflecting intersectionality, the construction of dimensions can greatly determine the results. For example, in the referred study, racial/ethnic composition was constructed in four dimensions: percent of Black or Latinx resident in a census tract below or above the respective median percentiles. This dichotomous construction would prevent the discovery of potential nonlinear effects on environmental inequality related to racial/ethnic composition as presented in our study. Of course, the dimensions can be increased to allow more granular analysis. However, this would lead to challenges stemming from the increased number of strata and small sample size in some. Eventually, intersectionality, simplicity, and granularity likely compete in the study design and may not be best represented in any single study. Hence, studies using different approaches should be viewed holistically to better understand environmental inequality.

There are also some limitations regarding the use of the NATA/AirToxScreen data. While this work used the most recent data sets of nationwide estimated cancer risk available, it may not capture later developments in environmental inequalities or differences across finer spatial scales such as census blocks or zip codes. To indicate and adequately address environmental inequalities regarding environmental exposure and associated health outcomes, more research and monitoring efforts are needed, as current surveillance efforts deliver data sets with lapses in time. In addition, the formation of secondary pollutants can be a complex process that involves emissions of precursors from multiple sources at the same time. As a result, neither NATA nor AirToxScreen can confidently apportion the contributions of individual sources to secondary pollutants. Indeed, a large fraction of the estimated cancer risk from these data sets is labeled as “secondary”, along with other specific sources such as on-road, nonroad, etc. The inability to distinguish sources of some of the most important pollutants prevents additional analysis of potential environmental inequalities that are specific to pollutants and sources of interest. Relatedly, particulate matters are arguably the most important air pollutant and potentially carcinogenic to humans.43,44 Yet, they are not included in NATA or AirToxScreen. This is because although underlying models can predict concentrations of particulate matters, no cancer slope factor is available to arrive with a cancer risk estimate as toxicity can be largely dependent on sources and compositions of the particulate matters.45 This can be an important limitation of relying solely on NATA or AirToxScreen to assess environmental inequalities for exposure to cancer risks, and we recommend viewing our study together with those focusing on particulate matters such as Colmer et al.19 and Jbaily et al.18 As for the data quality of NATA or AirToxScreen, independent evaluation of the NATA concentration data has shown good agreement with model to monitor assessments overall; however, in the few occasions where there was poor agreement with respect to specific hazardous air pollutant, the NATA usually underestimated the concentration of the suspected air toxics, meaning the cancer risk in most areas is probably higher.46 However, if the underestimation is systemic and has no specific bias toward any given subpopulation, the general conclusions from this study should hold true.

Finally, an additional potential weakness in this work involves unused SES factors that have shown influence on environmental inequality, such as sexual orientation,9 religion,47 country of origin,48 and household structure.30 While we focused on the more commonly used SES factors on this multiple year, national wide analysis, future studies could examine other SES factors to enhance our understanding of the evolution of environmental inequality in the U.S.

Policy implications of this research highlight the importance for expanding air pollution controls. One example could be institution of low-emission zones (LEZs), which helps mitigate impacts for vehicular emissions,49 which would benefit urban areas and low-income communities which are often disproportionately negatively impacted by traffic emissions. An added benefit could be reduction of other important contaminants including NO, NO2, and NOx as seen in German cities.50 As on-road sources remain significant contributors to estimated cancer risks from air toxics, increasing the volume of LEZs would likely reduce this estimated risk drastically. Another policy intervention would be expanded community air monitoring programs like the EPA’s EJScreen tools. Increasing the data accumulation, including daily air monitors and PM speciation, would empower communities to advocate for cleaner local environments and push for clean air policies.

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate a broad environmental inequality in the United States. These findings indicate that Black, Asian, and Hispanic individuals may be at significantly greater risk of cancer from air pollution. Findings of this research indicate some progress in environmental inequalities from 2011 to 2019 for certain races in certain urban environments; however, inequitable distribution of cancer risks persists. As a result, there is a need for public policy intervention to mitigate environmental inequality, targeting the underlying causes to promote environmental justice.
