Beyond High-flow Nasal Cannula vs Non-invasive Ventilation: The Need for Dynamic Physiological Monitoring in Pediatric Acute Hypoxemic Respiratory Failure
Authors: Om P Sanjeev, Babu Pathak, Kishore K Talukdar
Published: 2026

The Editor

Beyond the choice of respiratory interface, the management of acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (AHRF) increasingly depends on the timely recognition of the physiological response and impending treatment failure. The study by Kadappanavar et al. offers valuable insights into this issue and raises several clinically relevant questions regarding patient selection, monitoring strategies, and the escalation of respiratory support.1 In an era increasingly focused on preventing delayed intubation and patient self-inflicted lung injury (P-SILI), identifying reliable early markers of noninvasive respiratory support failure may be more important than determining the superiority of one respiratory interface over another.2,3 However, several methodological and clinical considerations merit further discussion.

The most important limitation relates to confounding by indication, as clinician-directed treatment allocation resulted in noninvasive ventilation (NIV) being preferentially used in children with more severe hypoxemic respiratory failure. Children receiving NIV had a significantly lower PaO2/FiO2 (P/F) ratio and a higher prevalence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and capillary leak syndrome, indicating greater baseline disease severity. This disparity creates significant confounding issues, complicating direct comparisons of treatment success rates. Notably, even though it was applied in a more clinically compromised group, NIV showed faster improvement in the clinical respiratory score (CRS), especially in the initial 6–24 hours. This observation might indicate a physiological benefit of positive-pressure assistance in patients with higher lung recruitability, rather than simply demonstrating treatment equivalence between the two approaches.3,4

Another key concern is how we rely on the CRS as the main comparison outcome. Although the CRS is clinically practical, several of its components remain observer-dependent and may be influenced by subjective assessment. More importantly, contemporary evidence increasingly suggests that dynamic physiological trajectories provide greater prognostic value than isolated clinical scores.5 Recent literature evaluating predictors of high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) failure emphasizes that trends in respiratory rate, oxygenation parameters, and serial physiological indices during the first 6–12 hours are more informative than single time-point measurements.6 The growing concept is that respiratory support should not be evaluated using static thresholds alone but through continuous assessment of the physiological response over time.5 In this regard, the findings of the present study are particularly noteworthy because the predictive value appeared to emerge from early changes in the CRS rather than the baseline CRS alone, indirectly supporting a dynamic monitoring approach.

Another notable observation is that the baseline P/F ratio differed significantly between groups, whereas the CRS was largely comparable at initiation. This discrepancy raises the possibility that physiological oxygenation impairment may not be adequately captured by the CRS alone. Future studies should explore whether combining the CRS with objective physiological markers can improve the early identification of children at risk of treatment failure.

The study also raises an important question regarding failure prediction. While CRS improvement was associated with successful outcomes, objective indices such as the SpO2/FiO2 (S/F) ratio, the P/F ratio, and the respiratory rate–oxygenation (ROX) index were not integrated into the primary analysis. The ROX index has emerged as one of the most extensively studied predictors of HFNC outcomes and has demonstrated moderate-to-good discriminatory performance across multiple cohorts.6,7 However, contemporary evidence suggests that even the ROX index should not be interpreted as a fixed binary threshold; rather, serial trends provide greater clinical utility than isolated measurements.5,6 This evolving concept aligns closely with the findings of the present study, where early physiological improvement appeared more meaningful than a single baseline score.

Another clinically relevant observation is the markedly high treatment failure rate among children with ARDS. Although the overall sample size was limited, ARDS accounted for a disproportionate number of failures in both the HFNC and NIV groups. This finding deserves greater emphasis because it raises concerns regarding delayed escalation in severe hypoxemic respiratory failure. Current understanding of P-SILI suggests that prolonged respiratory effort during inadequate noninvasive support may worsen lung injury and adversely affect outcomes.2 Consequently, the clinical objective should not only be the prediction of support failure, but also the identification of the optimal timing for escalation before physiological deterioration becomes irreversible.2,3

The heterogeneity of the included disease population further complicates interpretation. Pneumonia, ARDS, empyema, and capillary leak syndrome represent distinct pathophysiological entities with different responses to oxygenation strategies and positive-pressure support. Pooling these conditions may obscure disease-specific treatment effects. Future studies may benefit from disease-focused analyses, particularly in pediatric ARDS, where the balance between HFNC, NIV, and timely intubation remains uncertain.3

Finally, while the authors conclude that HFNC demonstrated outcomes comparable to NIV, caution is warranted in interpreting these findings. The study was not designed as a noninferiority trial, did not achieve the planned sample size in the NIV arm, and lacked adjustment for illness-severity scores such as the Pediatric Risk of Mortality III (PRISM III) or the Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction-2 (PELOD-2). Therefore, the absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence.

Ultimately, the study might provide less clarity on whether HFNC or NIV is superior and instead emphasize that the initial physiological response in the first few hours of treatment could be the key factor in determining the outcome. Future research should go beyond static clinical evaluations and concentrate on dynamic physiological measurements, objective models for predicting failure, and tailored respiratory support strategies for specific diseases in order to enhance results for children experiencing AHRF.
