Structural studies of the human α1 glycine receptor via site-specific chemical cross-linking coupled with mass spectrometry
Authors: Rathna J Veeramachaneni, Chelsee A Donelan, Kayce A Tomcho, Shaili Aggarwal, David J Lapinsky, Michael Cascio
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBy identifying distance constraints, chemical cross-linking coupled with mass spectrometry (CX-MS) can be a powerful complementary technique to other structural methods by interrogating macromolecular protein complexes under native-like conditions. In this study, we developed a CX-MS approach to identify the sites of chemical cross-linking from a single targeted location within the human α1 glycine receptor (α1 GlyR) in its apo state. The human α1 GlyR belongs to the family of pentameric ligand-gated ion channel receptors that function in fast neurotransmission. A single chemically reactive cysteine was reintroduced into a Cys null α1 GlyR construct at position 41 within the extracellular domain of human α1 homomeric GlyR overexpressed in a baculoviral system. After purification and reconstitution into vesicles, methanethiosulfonate-benzophenone-alkyne, a heterotrifunctional cross-linker, was site specifically attached to Cys41 via disulfide bond formation. The resting receptor was then subjected to UV photocross-linking. Afterward, monomeric and oligomeric α1 GlyR bands from SDS-PAGE gels were trypsinized and analyzed by tandem MS in bottom-up studies. Dozens of intrasubunit and intersubunit sites of α1 GlyR cross-linking were differentiated and identified from single gel bands of purified protein, showing the utility of this experimental approach to identify a diverse array of distance constraints of the α1 GlyR in its resting state. These studies highlight CX-MS as an experimental approach to identify chemical cross-links within full-length integral membrane protein assemblies in a native-like lipid environment.

Why it matters

Advances in crystallography and cryomicroscopy have given us a wealth of high-resolution snapshots of membrane proteins. However, these studies often involve mutating and/or deleting protein regions for thermostability or crystallization. Frequently, large physiologically relevant area of the protein may remain poorly, if at all, resolved. As a complement to other high-resolution methodologies and to better understand how these complex allosteric machines function, novel experimental methods must be developed that are capable of providing direct state-dependent structural information. In addition, membrane proteins provide another level of experimental difficulty, as they must be examined in membrane-mimetic environments that may also affect their structure. Mass spectrometry (MS) advances have positioned MS-based techniques as sensitive accessibility probes and molecular calipers capable of interrogating membrane proteins. Here, we describe proof-of-principle studies showing the potential of a site-specific cross-linking coupled with a MS approach, wherein disulfide bond formation tethers a photoactivatable group to nonspecifically interrogate the structure of the human glycine receptor in its apo state.

Introduction

Despite advances in high-resolution structural methods such as x-ray crystallography, NMR, and cryo-EM, membrane proteins remain relatively underrepresented in the PDB database. These methods can provide invaluable information on the structure of membrane proteins, but they have limitations and difficulties. For example, the presence of the lipid bilayer provides a barrier to both crystallographic and NMR studies of membrane-bound proteins. X-ray crystallography and cryo-EM approaches provide information on static images of otherwise conformationally flexible proteins that are often stabilized in a single low-energy state. In addition, determining high-resolution structures of membrane-bound proteins is frequently accomplished by truncating the more flexible regions of the protein, as well as by mutating some amino acid residues for stability/homogeneity and/or using other entities such as antibodies or ligands to noncovalently bind and stabilize the protein. Although NMR can be used to study the flexible regions of proteins, this experimental technique is impractical for large complexes, particularly membrane-bound proteins.

Crystallographic and cryo-EM studies of ligand-bound pentameric ligand-gated ion channels (pLGICs) have provided snapshots of many of these membrane-bound proteins (for reviews, see (1,2) and references therein), although the large intracellular loops connecting the transmembrane helices of pLGICs remain poorly resolved in all eukaryotic structures. Nevertheless, these studies provide a framework for computationally modeling the complex structures of membrane-bound proteins. Given the critical role of membrane lipids concerning the structure and function of membrane-bound proteins (3), including pLGICs (4), the use of membrane mimetics, while essential, might impose some artifactual limitations in structural studies. Recent cryo-EM studies of pLGICs have begun to initially characterize bound lipids (5,6,7,8,9) and have shown that pLGICs in nanodiscs are structurally different from those observed in micelles (8) and varies as a function of nanodisc diameter (10). As a result, developing practical experimental approaches that can directly interrogate mammalian protein structures in a native lipid environment is necessary to validate and accurately refine models of membrane proteins.

Given recent advances in mass spectrometry (MS) technology and its exquisite sensitivity, chemical cross-linking coupled with MS (CX-MS) can be a powerful and complementary experimental approach concerning structural studies of membrane-bound proteins (11,12,13). Importantly, CX-MS can be used under conditions that challenge or limit other biophysical experimental techniques. For example, CX-MS studies do not require large quantities of the protein to be investigated and are not precluded by the presence of a lipid bilayer. As a result, CX-MS studies are amenable to membrane-bound proteins. In addition, CX-MS studies can be conducted under various physiological conditions (e.g., in the presence or absence of agonists, antagonists, or modulatory ligands) to probe state-dependent allosteric changes of large oligomeric protein complexes. Notably, CX-MS and the exquisite sensitivity of modern mass spectrometers allow one to examine proteins expressed or isolated in relatively low concentrations typical of membrane protein preparations.

Chemical cross-linking using bifunctional cross-linking agents can provide distance constraints between reactive sites within a protein, as defined by cross-linker length. Typical chemistries employed with bifunctional chemical cross-linkers include amine-reactive cross-linkers that make use of abundantly available lysine residues (14,15), thiol-reactive cross-linkers that form a disulfide linkage with reactive cysteines (16,17), carboxylate-reactive cross-linkers with activated carboxyl groups for spontaneous peptide-bond formation with primary amines (18), and nonspecific cross-linkers using photoreactive functional groups, wherein highly reactive radicals can interrogate the local macromolecular environment (19). After chemical cross-linking, mass shifts due to the introduced cross-linking agent can be identified using MS. A bottom-up approach is typically employed wherein proteins of interest are cross-linked and then subjected to enzymatic digestion. Additional information may also be obtained by MS/MS analysis of a selected precursor ion to identify/refine the site of covalent cross-linking within the peptide via analyses of product mass ions after dissociation by collision-induced dissociation (CID), electron transfer dissociation, or electron capture dissociation (20,21,22). Identifying the sites of covalent chemical cross-links generated within a protein complex in a given allosteric state yields a network of cross-links that provide distance constraints, which may be used to define the local protein environment. While the distance constraints determined by CX-MS are longer than those determined from other high-resolution methods such as x-ray crystallography and cryomicroscopy, a network of systematically generated cross-links can critically interrogate and augment structural models of proteins (23,24,25,26,27,28).

Glycine receptors (GlyRs) are inhibitory ligand-gated receptor proteins that belong to the pLGIC superfamily. Specifically, GlyRs mediate neurotransmission in the central nervous system and are typically activated upon glycine binding. Inhibitory members of the pLGIC receptor family, also called the Cys-loop superfamily, include GlyRs, glutamate-gated chloride channel receptors, and GABAA receptors. These ionotropic membrane-bound receptor proteins show promising silencing activity (29,30), as these chloride channels typically allow the passive influx of Cl– that further hyperpolarizes the cell under typical conditions. The receptors in this family are pentamers pseudosymmetrically arranged in a ring around a central ion-conducting pore. Each protein subunit consists of an extracellular N-terminal ligand-binding domain (ECD), four transmembrane helices (M1–M4), and an intracellular domain. The intracellular domain primarily comprise a long loop of variable length between M3 and M4 (31), which currently remains unresolved in all reported structures of eukaryotic pLGICs.

GlyRs can exist in multiple allosteric states as the channel gates and desensitizes. Without a ligand such as glycine, the receptor is essentially in a resting state. GlyRs can be composed of various combinations of α1, α2, α3, α4, and β subunits. However, expression of human α1 GlyR subunits is sufficient to reconstitute a homomeric α1 GlyR with native-like function (32). Here, we report CX-MS studies of full-length homomeric α1 GlyR protein reconstituted in lipid vesicles to identify intra- and intersubunit cross-links in the resting (apo) state of the receptor. For this work, human α1 GlyR homopentamers containing a single chemically reactive thiol at Cys41 in each subunit, were overexpressed in insect cells using a baculovirus expression system, then purified and reconstituted using established methods (32,33). There are seven endogenous Cys residues within a given α1 GlyR subunit, four of which form two essential disulfide bonds, thus leaving three cysteines containing a free nucleophilic thiol. These latter three cysteine residues have been mutated to provide a Cys null α1 GlyR, which allowed for the reintroduction of a single Cys to be used in subsequent CX-MS studies. In this report, a Cys was reintroduced at position 41, and then site specifically targeted by MTS-benzophenone-alkyne (MTS-bzp-alkyne), a chemical cross-linker that is initially bound to the mutated site via a chemoselective disulfide linkage when added to the purified, reconstituted GlyR. The benzophenone (bzp) moiety in the chemical cross-linker was then photoactivated to interrogate the local protein structure of apo α1 GlyR via distance-constrained, radical-mediated covalent bond formation. Intra- and intersubunit α1 GlyR chemical cross-links were then identified by bottom-up MS analysis of trypsinized peptide fragments from monomeric and oligomeric α1 GlyR bands in SDS-PAGE gels, respectively (34). Electrospray ionization-time of flight-mass spectrometry fitted with a chipcube (chip-nESI-TOF-MS) was used to analyze mass-shifted tryptic peptides, wherein analysis of the pattern of product ions allowed for precise identification of the site(s) of chemical cross-linking within the parent peptide. This bottom-up approach using CX-MS provided distance constraints between two chemically reactive amino acid residues within a resting α1 GlyR, thus allowing sensitive and accurate probing of α1 GlyR structure in the resting state in an intact lipid bilayer.

Materials and methods

Human α1 GlyR mutagenesis, purification, and reconstitution

A Cys null construct of human α1 GlyR (i.e., a C41A/C290S/C345A triple mutant, wherein the three reactive cysteine thiols present in wild-type human α1 GlyR are mutated to Ala or Ser) in pFastBac was used as a template for site-directed mutagenesis to reintroduce an endogenous Cys thiol back at position 41, resulting in a mutated form of human α1 GlyR that only has a single chemically reactive Cys41 thiol per protein subunit. This mutation was introduced using a Quick-change site-directed mutagenesis kit (Stratagene, San Diego, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols (35). The forward primer had the sequence CCAGTGAACGTGAGCTGCAACATTTTCATCAAC (the modified codon for Cys41 is underlined). PCR conditions were 16 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 55°C for 1 min, and elongation at 68°C for 7 min. The parental plasmid after PCR was cleaved by Dpn digestion, then the reaction product was transformed into 50 μL of XL-1 blue supercompetent cells by heat shock treatment at 42°C for 45 s. Cells were allowed to recover in NZY+ broth while shaking for 1 h at 37°C, then plated on LB (Luria Bertani) agar plates containing 50 μg/mL ampicillin. After overnight incubation at 37°C, single colonies were isolated. Plasmids were extracted and purified using a Bio-Rad mini prep kit (Hercules, CA), and the mutation was confirmed by sequencing (GENEWIZ, South Plainfield, NJ).

Recombinant baculovirus was produced according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). In brief, the purified mutated pFastbac construct was transformed into MAX Efficiency DH10Bac competent cells by heat shock treatment for 105 s at 42°C, then grown in Super Optimal broth with Catabolite repression medium by incubating at 37°C for 4 h. Serial dilutions (100 μL) of 10–1, 10–2, and 10–3 samples were plated on Luria agar plates containing kanamycin (50 μg/mL), tetracycline (10 μg/mL), gentamycin (7 μg/mL), Bluo-gal (300 μg/mL), and IPTG (40 μg/mL), then incubated for 24–48 h at 37°C. The presence of white colonies amid blue colonies indicated the presence of the recombinant bacmid DNA. White colonies were subsequently picked and grown in LB medium containing antibiotics at 37°C for 24 h, followed by recombinant bacmid DNA isolation. Recombinant DNA (1–2 μg) was then transfected into Sf-9 (Spodoptera frugiperda) insect cells (cell density at 5 × 105 cells/mL) using 6 μL of CellFectin reagent and 100 μL of SFM II medium with antibiotics (penstrep, 0.5×) (Gibco), then allowed to grow for 1 h at 28°C. The supernatant was then replaced with SFM II medium without antibiotics and grown for 72 h at 28°C. After viral amplification of the harvested supernatant, the concentration of plaque-forming units (pfu)/mL was determined by viral titer. In brief, 25 μL of serially diluted (10-2–10-6) virus was added in duplicates to insect cells in seeded wells containing 3-5 × 105 cells/mL, then allowed to bind for 1 h. The virus was aspirated, and the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer (pH 7). Cells were then fixed using 10% gelatin for 10 min. Next, a primary antibody mouse anti-gp64 (a glycoprotein expressed on the surface of infected cells) (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) (36) was added to the plate (1:200), followed by incubation for 1 h at 37°C. The plate was then washed twice with PBS buffer (pH 7.2) and developed by incubation with goat anti-mouse Ab-HRP conjugate (Abcam) (1:1000) for 1 h at 37°C. After washing twice, TMB substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was added, and then the plate was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The number of blue-stained cells per well was subsequently counted, wherein the number of cells and the dilution factor were considered to calculate the pfu/mL. The viral particles were then further used to infect cells in higher quantities for overexpression of the mutated protein (37). Titered virus was then added to a spinner flask (volume determined based on using a multiplicity of infection of ∼5) containing about 600 mL of cells at a concentration of ∼1 × 106 cells/mL and >97% viability. Afterward, the cells were harvested 3 days postinfection (33).

GlyR purification and reconstitution

GlyR purification and reconstitution were performed as described previously (33). In brief, the cell pellet was resuspended and washed three times using an isotonic PBS solution. The cells were then suspended in a hypotonic solution containing 5 mM Tris (pH 8.0), 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA, and 10 mM dithiothreitol for 1 h at 4°C to swell the cells before lysing. An antiproteolytic cocktail containing 16 milliunits/mL aprotinin, l μM benzamidine, l μM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, and 1 μM benzethonium chloride was then added immediately before sonication using a probe tip sonicator with a microtip attachment (Branson Ultrasonics Sonifier Model 250, Brookfield, CT) and 50% duty cycles (1/2 s bursts each second for 15 times) in an ice-jacketed container to minimize sample heating. Lysates were then centrifuged at 150,000 × g for 30 min. The resulting pellet was then resuspended in a hypertonic saline solution (hypotonic solution + 300 mM NaCl), sonicated, and repelleted to extract the peripheral membrane-bound proteins. The pellet was then incubated (by sonication and rocking for 48 h) in a solubilization buffer containing 1% digitonin:0.1% deoxycholate, 1.5 mg/mL mixed lipids (9:1 plant extract (Avanti, Alabaster, AL):egg extract (Avanti)), 25 mM potassium phosphate (pH 7.4), 1 M potassium chloride, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM EGTA, 10 mM DTT, and the antiproteolytic cocktail. The samples were then centrifuged as before, and the resulting supernatant was added to 2-aminostrychnine agarose resin, followed by incubation for 24 h at 4°C with nutation. After extensive washing with PBS (pH 7.4), bound lipid/protein/detergent micelles were eluted by a 2-day equilibration in a solubilization buffer containing 200 mM glycine. Purified protein-lipid vesicles were reconstituted by dialysis (3500 Da cutoff Slide-A-Lyzer dialysis cassette, Thermo Fisher Scientific) against excess 25 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) over 5 days. Postdialysis, the reconstituted human α1 GlyR was sonicated as before and pelleted at 150,000 × g for 30 min to concentrate the vesicles, which were then resuspended in 1 mL of 25 mM potassium phosphate. A modified Lowry assay (38) was used to determine the concentration of the α1 GlyR. For quality control, aliquots from various purification steps were visualized using SDS-PAGE and western blotting. Primary antibody (rabbit anti-GlyR, Abcam) at 25,000 dilution and IR-labeled secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit, LI-COR) at 10,000× dilution were used. Membranes were visualized using an ODYSSEY CLx infrared imager (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE).

Chemical synthesis of S-(2-(4-(4-(prop-2-yn-1-yloxy)benzoyl)phenoxy)ethyl) methanesulfonothioate (MTS-bzp-alkyne)

Sodium methanethiosulfonate (75 mg, 0.56 mmol, 1.4 eq.) was added to a solution of (4-(2-bromoethoxy)phenyl)(4-(prop-2-yn-1-yloxy)phenyl)methanone (39) (140 mg, 0.39 mmol, 1.0 eq.) in DMF (9.3 mL) at room temperature. The resulting mixture was then heated at 50°C for 20 h under argon, wherein TLC analysis (3:7 EtOAc/hexanes) indicated a complete reaction. The reaction mixture was then cooled to room temperature, diluted with H2O, and extracted three times with EtOAc. The combined EtOAc extracts were then dried (MgSO4), filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to give 150 mg (99% yield) of MTS-bzp-alkyne as a sticky yellow solid requiring no further purification based on TLC and NMR results. Rf = 0.61 (95:5 CHCl3/MeOH). 1H NMR (400 MHz, chloroform-d) δ 7.78 (d, J = 8.8 Hz, 4H), 7.04 (d, J = 8.8 Hz, 2H), 6.96 (d, J = 8.8 Hz, 2H), 4.77 (d, J = 2.5 Hz, 2H), 4.37 (t, J = 6.0 Hz, 2H), 3.58 (t, J = 5.9 Hz, 2H), 3.42 (s, 3H), 2.58 (t, J = 2.4 Hz, 1H). 13C NMR (101 MHz, chloroform-d) δ 194.1, 161.1, 160.6, 132.2, 132.0, 131.3, 131.2, 114.3, 113.9, 77.8, 76.1, 66.7, 55.8, 50.7, 35.4. HRMS calculated for (C19H18O5S2)Na+ 413.0488, found 413.0493.

Chemical cross-linking with MTS-bzp-alkyne

Aliquots (250 μL) of α1 GlyR vesicles in 25 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) and MTS-bzp-alkyne (1.9 mg/mL in DMSO) were combined to give a 1:5 GlyR:MTS-bzp-alkyne mole ratio (one cross-linker per subunit of pentameric α1 GlyR) and allowed to react overnight at 4°C in the dark while nutating. The sample was then transferred to a quartz cuvette and exposed to a 420 W Hg Arc lamp (Newport (Irvine, CA), model 97435-1000-1, 260–320 nm) for four sessions of 5 min on ice, with 5-min periods of no exposure in between each UV exposure session to prevent sample warming. Cross-linked samples (20 μL) were then loaded onto a 15% SDS-PAGE gel as described previously, except under nonreducing conditions (i.e., no dithiothreitol) to separate α1 GlyR subunits containing potential intersubunit cross-links (oligomeric α1 GlyR bands) from those containing solely intrasubunit cross-links (monomeric α1 GlyR bands). Given the low concentrations of α1 GlyR used in each trial, marker lanes containing bovine serum albumin (MW = 66.5 kDa) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) were used to direct gel excision. Gel plugs corresponding to low MW monomeric (i.e., intrasubunit α1 GlyR cross-links, <66.5 kDa) and high MW oligomeric (i.e., inter- and intrasubunit α1 GlyR cross-links, >66.5 kDa) were excised.

Trypsinolysis and MS sample preparation

Gel plugs were washed with 50:50 methanol/50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution and dried with acetonitrile (ACN). The α1 GlyR protein in the gel plugs was then reduced using 10 mM DTT, alkylated with 50 mM iodoacetamide, and then soaked in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (pH 7.8) containing 20 μg/mL of trypsin gold (Promega, Madison, WI) for 24 h at 37°C. Digested peptides in the supernatant from the gel plug were subsequently transferred into a fresh tube. Formic acid (FA) (0.1%) in 50:50 ACN/H2O was added to the gel plugs and then incubated for 30 min. The supernatant was then transferred into the initial elution to deactivate the trypsin enzyme. The extracted solution was subsequently evaporated and reconstituted in 20 μL of 0.1% FA in Milli-Q water. While SDS-PAGE denatures the globular structure of the α1 GlyR, trypsin digests α1 GlyR subunits into peptides for MS detection. Peptide samples were further cleaned and concentrated using Zip Tips (EMD Millipore, Oakville, ON), which are 10 μL pipette tips containing a 0.6 μL bed embedded with a very small, reverse-phase C18 resin. The Zip Tips were conditioned using 50% ACN 3 times and equilibrated with 0.1% FA 3 times. The peptide sample was pipetted through the column 10–20 times to allow the α1 GlyR peptides to bind to the column. The column was then washed with 0.1% FA to remove impurities, whereas α1 GlyR peptides bound to the column were eluted using 10 μL of 0.1% FA in 50% methanol.

MS

Samples were run on an Agilent Accurate Mass 6530 nanoelectrospray ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometer (nESI-QTOF-MS) (Santa Clara, CA) fitted with a C18 reverse-phase (75 μm × 150 mm separation column and a 40 nL enrichment column) chip cube. The mass spectrometer was run in a positive ion mode using internal standards (1221.9906 and 299.2944) supplied by Agilent for calibration. Samples were resuspended in 40 μL of 0.1% FA in water. Each run was conducted using a 2 μL injection volume and a linear gradient using solvents A (95% H2O, 5% ACN, 0.1% FA) and B (95% ACN, 5% H2O, 0.1% FA) over 11 min with the profile: 0 min 95% A, 7 min 40% A, 9 min 10% A, and 9.10 min 95% A, m/z of 200–1700 and 175 V fragment or voltage. Qualitative analysis software (Agilent) was used to identify mass-shifted cross-linked precursor mass ions using a 10 ppm cutoff. The cross-linker mass shift of MTS-bzp-alkyne (311.3838 Da), along with other possible modifications (i.e., sodiation, postassiation, alkylation, oxidation, and acrylamidation), were used in identifying the mass-shifted precursor ions. For targeted MS/MS by CID, a linear increase in collision energy by m/z was calculated using the equation y = 3.7x + 2.5 (where y = m/z, and x = collision energy). Data analysis was performed using a 0.1 Da error in product ions using Masshunter (Agilent), Prospector (MS-Product UCSF, San Francisco CA), SkyLine (open source MacCcoss Lab Software, Univ. of Washington, Seattle, WA), and Spectrum Mill (Agilent).

Results and discussion

MTS-bzp-alkyne (Fig. 1) was used for chemical cross-linking of human α1 GlyR, followed by bottom-up LC-MS/MS analysis to identify inter- and intrasubunit covalent attachment sites. A single Cys residue containing a nucleophilic thiol was reintroduced into a human α1 Cys null GlyR background construct via mutation of Ala at position 41 (i.e., A41C). The mutation at position 41 was selected for these proof-of-principle CX-MS studies because it is an endogenous Cys in the wild-type receptor (i.e., it was mutated to create the Cys null construct) and known to be accessible from previous studies in our lab (40). Homopentameric human α1 GlyR was overexpressed in Sf9 insect cells using a baculovirus expression system and whole-cell patch-clamping studies of the infected insect cells showed the receptor retained native-like ion channel activity (see Fig. S1 for representative whole cell patch-clamp current trace in response to saturating glycine concentration). GlyR was purified and reconstituted into vesicles containing cholesterol and mixed lipids from plant and chicken eggs using established methodologies that reconstitute GlyR in a fully functional form (41).

Experimental workflow for chemical cross-linking using MTS-bzp-alkyne. A thiol-reactive MTS group is shown in red, a photoreactive benzophenone is shown in blue, and a terminal alkyne click chemistry handle is shown in green.

The heterotrifunctional cross-linker MTS-bzp-alkyne (Fig. 1) contains an MTS moiety capable of chemoselectively forming a disulfide bond with Cys41 that was engineered into each subunit of a Cys null human homopentameric α1 GlyR, thus providing an initial site for covalent attachment of the cross-linker that is site specific. MTS-bzp-alkyne also contains a photoactivatable benzophenone (bzp) moiety that preferentially targets local Cα backbone hydrogen atoms upon photoactivation (42) and a terminal alkyne that can be further derivatized using click chemistry (43). Recent reports have shown that bzps offer an orthogonal directionality than diazirines, and both provide diverse high-density cross-linking (44). Since the 1,2-diradical formed upon activation of benzophenone reacts preferentially with C-H-bonds over water (45), the expected low frequency of individual cross-linking events would be further reduced by the reactivity of the carbene radical generated upon radiation of diazirines with solvent. The elevated photocross-linking efficiency afforded bzps over diazirines informed the selection of a bzp photocross-linker.

Upon photoactivation, bzp has been reported to undergo either a H-abstraction or a H-abstraction followed by recombination mechanism (42). Given the sensitivity of MS, these mechanisms can be differentiated as the resultant mass shift on the peptides will differ by the mass of a H atom (we utilize a 10 ppm error window in m/z assignments). The observed cross-linking products consistent with the latter H-abstraction and recombination mechanism depicted in Fig. 2.

Proposed benzophenone photocross-linking via H-abstraction and recombination mechanism.

SDS-PAGE separation in the absence of reductant was used to separate α1 GlyR oligomers from monomers. Like many membrane proteins, GlyR runs as both monomers and oligomers on SDS-PAGE even in the absence of any chemical cross-linking. Given this, and the presence of five potential cross-links per pentamer, the mass-shifted cross-links identified in oligomeric bands may contain either intra- or intersubunit cross-links. However, any mass-shifted peptides identified in MS studies of monomeric α1 GlyR must be intrasubunit cross-links. The respective excised oligomeric and monomeric α1 GlyR gel plugs were trypsinized and extracted. The resulting peptides were then reduced and S-alkylated with iodoacetamide, thus allowing the separation of the site-specific Cys41-linked source peptide from photocross-linked target peptides. Unique mass-shifted peptides observed in tryptic extracts from the α1 GlyR oligomeric band but absent in the extracts from the α1 GlyR monomeric band are hypothesized to occur between neighboring α1 GlyR subunits. A similar strategy was validated in Lys-Lys cross-linking studies conducted on the extracellular domain of GlyR (34,46).

Three independent human α1 GlyR preparations were purified, reconstituted, and used in our CX-MS studies. Mass-shifted cross-linker-containing peptides with m/z in the 300–1200 Da mass range with a mass error of ≤10 ppm were identified. A single missed trypsin cleavage was allowed, as were carboxymethylation, Met oxidation, sodiation, potassiation, or acrylamide modification(s). Identified precursor mass-shifted ions were then targeted for MS/MS analysis via fragmentation using CID employing N2 gas to confirm their original assignments and to determine the site(s) of chemical cross-linking within the peptide by analyses of the MS/MS spectra.

CID fragmentation typically produces b and y product ions of the precursor mass ion. Analyses of product ions containing mass shifts due to the attached cross-linker allowed refinement of the covalent attachment site of the cross-linker, sometimes to a single amino acid residue. Representative MS and MS/MS spectra of Cys null α1 GlyR A41C showing the identified site of chemical cross-linking are given in Fig. 3. Given that tryptic peptides could be modified at more than a single site for a given cross-linking experiment, MS/MS spectra were strictly matched to their precursor ion by retention time to differentiate isobaric species differing only by the site of cross-linker attachment within the mass-shifted peptide. For example, multiple amino acids on a given tryptic peptide may be within the nonspecific cross-linking radius from Cys41, thus generating numerous singly cross-linked species; however, each isobaric precursor ion would yield unique patterns of product ions after CID. Given that in-line liquid chromatography can potentially separate isobaric peptides via their differing respective chemical properties, each correlated precursor/product pair (with respect to retention time) can yield a refined cross-linking site(s) even when present at multiple sites. By individually analyzing each product ion fragmentation spectrum at a given retention time, isobars can be unequivocally resolved in MS/MS studies. Only precursor ions assigned to cross-linker-containing peptides (>tetrapeptides) identified as having overlapping sequence coverage in >2 of 3 trials are reported in Table 1. CID fragmentation within the retention time window (±0.2 min) produces product ion fragmentation spectra (2–12 spectra) for each cross-linked peptide.

Product ion scans for mass-shifted precursor ions were individually analyzed to identify the site of covalent attachment and sites of cross-linking within tryptic fragments are shown in bold in Table 1.

All MS runs were carried out in triplicate, and the identified sites of cross-linking listed in Table 1 were observed in more than one ion species (i.e., protonated, sodiated, potassiated, alkylated, and oxidized species). The multiplicity of cross-linking events observed from a single site of covalent cross-linker attachment (i.e., for each single trial, more than 15 sites of cross-linking were observed from a single excised SDS-PAGE gel band) shows the utility of using a nonspecific photoactivatable cross-linker to interrogate the local protein topography. The promiscuity of photoinitiated covalent binding events provides an extensive network of distance constraints that can be used to validate and refine computational structural models of proteins of interest. Of note, the introduction of the single nucleophilic thiol Cys41 mutation presented here, while consistent with current GlyRs and pLGICs models, is insufficient to validate such models rigorously. However, we expect that CX-MS studies conducted using a series of systematically introduced single Cys mutants can provide a large network of constraints capable of refining the resting state of human α1 GlyR, including regions of the protein that are currently unresolved (e.g., the large intracellular loop connecting the M3 and M4 transmembrane helices). These more comprehensive human α1 GlyR cross-linking studies will follow this initial proof-of-principle study.

As expected, peptide extracts from higher MW α1 GlyR oligomeric bands contained many redundant mass-shifted peptides because such oligomers may contain both intra- and intersubunit cross-links (i.e., Table 1 has been simplified to reduce redundancies in assignments, while a complete list of all assigned cross-links from each respective gel slice and the respective modifications observed is provided in the supporting material). This redundancy provides further confidence in the assignments and product ion scans from extracts of oligomeric bands provide additional verification, allowing better refinement of the sites of covalent attachment of the cross-linker within a mass-shifted peptide. There was only one mass-shifted peptide that was reproducibly observed uniquely in peptide extracts from oligomeric bands. The bzp moiety attached at C41 appeared to form intersubunit cross-links to a residue within the range of 194–196 in the pre-M1 region of its neighboring protein subunit. Significantly, as expected, the sites of covalent chemical cross-linking from C41 were reproducibly localized to the extracellular domain (ECD), the M2-M3 linker, or the extracellular ends of the TM helices. No cross-linking events were identified within the intracellular domain or in the more cytoplasmic region of the TM helices (these areas comprise a significant portion of the mass of the receptor), providing confidence that the experimentally observed covalent cross-links are not artifactual (e.g., due to misfolded protein). One cross-linking site that we identified near the N-terminus tail is not indicated in Fig. 4, nor is a distance provided, as this presumably flexible region and is absent in the resolved structure. Notably, the information we obtained about the N-terminal region of human α1 GlyR, whose 3D structure is currently unresolved, emphasizes the applicability of using CX-MS as an orthogonal biophysical technique to complement current high-resolution methodologies for determining protein structure.

The Cα-Cα distance of the MTS-bzp-alkyne cross-linker used in these studies is estimated to be ∼25 Å. Assuming an upper bound of ∼30 Å for cross-linking events, the calculated distances from human α1 GlyR C41 to cross-linked residues in the extracellular domain are consistent with this limit (Table 1). Distances over 30 Å are calculated for cross-links observed to residues identified within the transmembrane regions. However, it should be noted that other investigators have observed conflicts between CX-MS in situ distance constraints and structural models that are attributed to protein dynamics (13,48). In addition, all distances reported in Table 1 were measured using a crystal structure of a strychnine-bound form of human α1 GlyR in detergent micelles, as this is the most closely related structure to the apo state that is currently available. Presently, there is no structure of the apo form of any eukaryotic pLGIC. In addition, it is known that pLGIC structure and function are affected by lipid composition (4,49,50,51); however, no pLGIC structures in native lipid bilayers are currently available. This is particularly relevant because the structure of pLGICs is sensitive to lipid composition and reduced cholesterol, and the structure of nAChRs is predominantly in an uncoupled state. In comparative CX-MS studies using azi-cholesterol our lab has identified differences in the structure of human α1 GlyR in low-cholesterol conditions versus more physiological, elevated cholesterol conditions (52). This highlights a particular strength of CX-MS methods and their potential to complement other high-resolution biophysical methods examining highly allosteric dynamic complexes in a native lipid membrane.

In these studies, milligram quantities of human α1 GlyR were purified in each preparation, and aliquots of these samples were subjected to chemical cross-linking with MTS-bzp-alkyne. Given the loading of a typical SDS-PAGE gel, low-microgram quantities of a cross-linked GlyR samples were separated, and subjected to subsequent in-gel trypsin digestion. The eluted sample from a single excised band was subjected to in-gel trypsin digestion, elution, and clean-up steps. Typically, about 1/10th of the eluted peptide solution was injected into the electrospray ion source for MS analysis.

At best, if one assumes that initial Cys41-cross-linker disulfide bond formation is complete and that photoactivation of the benzophenone within the cross-linker always produces protein-protein covalent cross-links, then picomoles of peptides in total would be expected to be covalently cross-linked. We identified dozens of covalent chemical cross-links in each MS run. If these cross-links are in equal abundance, and an average single run yields >20 cross-linking events, at most, picomoles of each peptide were present and detected. This estimate is clearly high, as yields are not expected to be 100%, nor are the mass-shifted peptides expected to have the same frequency of occurrence. Nonetheless, dozens of cross-linking events were detectable and resolved in our MS/MS studies, showing the sensitivity and capability of high-resolution MS in CX-MS.

Given initial concerns that a low frequency of α1 GlyR cross-linking events could be difficult to detect amid an abundance of noncross-linked tryptic peptides in our extracts, a terminal alkyne click chemistry handle was rationally included as part of the MTS-bzp-alkyne cross-linker. In initial studies using this heterotrifunctional cross-linker, click chemistry with biotin azide was used to chemoselectively biotinylate only those peptides containing a covalently bound cross-linker. After trypsin digestion, soft avidin affinity chromatography was used to isolate only cross-linked peptides that were click tagged with biotin. We anticipated that such an experimental strategy would allow facile, selective purification and postcolumn enrichment of only the cross-linked peptides in a small elution volume for subsequent MS studies. However, prolonged and extensive studies of the biotinylated peptides via this click chemistry strategy yielded only negative results in our hands, as no mass-shifted peptides were observed in MS studies either after or before avidin purification. We hypothesize that the click-mediated biotinylation resulted in ion suppression, thus leading to an inability to identify cross-linked peptides in our MS studies. The salts present in the buffers we used to isolate biotinylated cross-linked peptides via streptavidin affinity enrichment could also cause ion suppression. Surprisingly, only when the click chemistry step was omitted could mass-shifted α1 GlyR peptides be detected in our MS studies. Developing a clean-up protocol involving solid-phase extraction to remove unwanted salts might solve this problem. However, our success in identifying mass-shifted peptides and mapping cross-linking sites within human α1 GlyR, without the need for click chemistry derivatization of the cross-linker with biotin, rendered these additional studies unnecessary.

A limitation of this study is that the frequency of covalent cross-linking events in human α1 GlyR could not be evaluated. This is because observed peak intensities in MS studies reflect the relative ionization of a chemical species and not its concentration; however, such accurate quantification would allow cross-linking frequency discrimination. This particular limitation is a common problem in MS-based studies that do not involve the use of isotopically enriched standards. In future studies, we plan to incorporate a laser-induced fluorescence microfluidic platform (53) to sensitively quantify derivatized/cross-linker peptides.

In summary, CX-MS studies identified both intra- and intersubunit cross-linked peptides for human α1 GlyR in its resting state. MS/MS data for all mass-shifted peptides were used to further refine the sites of covalent chemical cross-linking of an MTS-bzp-alkyne cross-linker and provide distance constraints. In all cases, MS/MS product ion scans of mass-shifted precursor ions identified a single site of covalent chemical cross-linking or refined the site of covalent chemical cross-linking to a few residues within the mass-shifted tryptic peptide fragment. Notably, these CX-MS studies do not require large amounts of purified receptor as picomoles of protein from a single SDS-PAGE gel were sufficient to identify dozens of covalent cross-links. In addition, these studies were conducted in reconstituted lipid vesicles in the absence of detergents that might induce structural artifacts.

Projecting forward, CX-MS studies can be performed state dependently in the presence or absence of agonists, antagonists, or allosteric ligands with any desired lipid composition. In future work, we will apply this experimental approach to homopentameric α1 GlyRs containing single cysteines that are systematically introduced into the protein. Such a strategy is expected to generate a more comprehensive matrix of distance constraints in the resting state and other functional states of human α1 GlyR, a paradigmatic pLGIC. In turn, the resulting information from such studies can be then used to update and refine computational models of pLGICS in different conformational states. Notably, CX-MS is expected to be useful in identifying networks of distance constraints for flexible loops that may be poorly resolved or missing, as is the case with human GlyRs and their structural homologs. A similar approach using azi-cholesterol, a photoreactive derivative of cholesterol, has been utilized by our group to examine the lipid-accessible regions of α1 GlyR (52) and the serotonin transporter (54). As a result, CX-MS offers a complementary experimental approach to other high-resolution biophysical methods to sensitively and accurately map distance constraints for highly allosteric membrane-bound proteins in a native environment.
