Plastomes provide new insights into phylogeny and evolution in Chlorellaceae (Chlorophyta, Trebouxiophyceae)
Authors: Huiyin Song, Tian He, Rongli Guan, Tiantian Li, Zhiwei Fang, Zhaolu Zhu, Lifen Gao, Lihong Chen, Zilan Xiao, Yuxin Hu, Qiufeng Yan, Feng Liu, Hai Peng, Guoxiang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe family Chlorellaceae is a key focus of research on resource microalgae due to its potential applications and economic value. Despite its significance, the phylogenetic relationships and evolution among its members remain poorly understood. Plastomes, which offer numerous informative nucleotide sites for phylogenetic analysis, are expected to provide valuable reference information.ResultsIn this study, 21 Chlorellacean plastomes were newly sequenced and combined with 37 previously published plastome data, resulting in a total of 58 plastomes representing all major clades. Phylogenomic analysis was conducted to reveal relationships among the members. Furthermore, plastome characteristics were compared to analyze Chlorellacean evolution. The results revealed well-resolved phylogenomic relationships among the clade level and genera level, with the inverted repeat (IR) region displaying interesting evolutionary patterns. Finally, adaptive changes in plastome size, gene content, and codon usage bias were observed in obligate heterotrophic taxa compared to autotrophic and mixotrophic taxa.ConclusionsThis study resolved the phylogenetic relationships among the main lineages within the Chlorellaceae. Additionally, our research highlighted prevalent structural variations in the evolution of Chlorellacean plastomes, emphasizing notable adaptive changes in plastome size, gene content, and codon usage bias in obligate heterotrophic taxa compared with autotrophic and mixotrophic taxa. This work not only enhances our understanding of the phylogenetic relationship within the Chlorellaceae but also enriches our comprehension of its evolutionary diversity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07329-5.Keywords: Chlorellaceae, Phylogeny, Plastome

Background

The family Chlorellaceae (Chlorophyta, Trebouxiophyceae) comprises ecologically and economically significant genera, such as Chlorella, Auxenochlorella, Micractinium, Parachlorella, Dictyosphaerium, and Planktochlorella [1–3]. They are used in food, medicine, health products, energy, and agriculture in various ways [4–6]. Especially Chlorella, being a microalga with significant scientific research value and economic potential, has attracted the attention of numerous scholars [7, 8], and a search for Chlorella on Google Scholar yielded approximately 488,000 results (searched on May 8, 2025). This family includes autotrophic species (most members), mixotrophic species (Auxenochlorella), and obligately heterotrophic species (Prototheca and Helicosporidium) [9–11]. This diversity makes them suitable for studying evolutionary patterns across different life strategies.

Many members of Chlorellaceae exhibit simple coccoid morphological characteristics, making them difficult to differentiate [12–14]. Molecular markers such as 18S rDNA and ITS2 have been extensively used for taxonomic and phylogenetic studies [15–17]. The 18S rDNA is highly conserved and thus employed for identification at higher taxonomic levels, while the ITS2 region, exhibiting high sequence variability and conserved secondary structures, is suitable for lower-level classification, such as species identification. These markers (18S rDNA and ITS2) have been widely used in tandem to classify Chlorellacean members. Furthermore, integrating data from both 18S rDNA and ITS2 sequences, along with their secondary structures, enhances the reliability and accuracy of phylogenetic analyses for Chlorella and related taxa [18, 19]. The family Chlorellaceae primarily includes several clades: the Chlorella clade, Parachlorella clade, Nannochloris clade, and AHP clade according to [8, 20, 21] and AlgaeBase (https://www.algaebase.org/), with the Chlorella and Parachlorella clades being the primary focus of research. However, numerous unresolved issues persist within the family Chlorellaceae, including ​​uncertainties​​ in inter-clade and intergeneric relationships, ​​ambiguous​​ evolutionary pathways, and ​ ​polyphyly of genera​​ (e.g., Chlorella) [8, 12]. To enhance the precision of phylogenetic inference, ​​an increased number of informative nucleotide sites​​ is essential.

Plastid genomes (plastomes), found in both eukaryotic algae and plants, are characterized by their moderate size, relatively conserved gene content, and moderate evolutionary rate. These features, along with a high density of polymorphic sites, make plastomes powerful molecular markers for phylogenetic reconstruction across Chlorophyta [22–24]. The unique structure of plastid genomes, which includes single-copy protein-coding genes located in both large single-copy region (LSC) and small single-copy region (SSC), provides significant advantages for evolutionary studies, which helps eliminate paralogous interference while offering a wealth of informative nucleotide sites. Moreover, characteristics including GC content, codon usage bias, and sequence arrangement patterns reflect evolutionary relationships [25, 26]. Codon preference patterns not only reflect phylogenetic relationships but also shed light on strategies for environmental adaptation [27]. Recent research indicates that plastome evolution within Chlorellaceae involves complex adaptive processes such as gene rearrangement, selective gene loss, and dynamic intron proliferation, offering critical insights into green algal genome plasticity under varying ecological strategies [28–30]. However, plastome data for this family are limited, especially for the Parachlorella clade. As of this study, only two plastomes have been reported. To improve the accuracy of phylogenetic inference, broader species sampling and plastome sequencing are essential.

In this study, we have sequenced 21 new Chlorellacean plastomes, including the first plastomes from Actinastrum, Didymogenes, Masaia, Dictyosphaerium, Closteriopsis, Neochlorella, Crucigenia lauterbornii, and Coronastrum ellipsoideum, along with several unidentified taxa. We combined these with data from 37 previously published plastomes, resulting in a total of 58 plastomes that represent all major clades. By employing various phylogenetic methods and analyzing structural characteristics and codon usage bias, this study aims to clarify the phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary pathways within this family.

Results

Characteristics of the Chlorellacean plastomes

We newly sequenced and assembled the plastomes of 21 Chlorellaceae strains (Table 1). Combined with 37 non-redundant plastomes from GenBank, this study analyzed a total of 58 plastomes representing 17 known genera, 26 described species, and several unidentified taxa, encompassing all major clades within the family. Our analyses revealed substantial variation in plastome architecture: plastome sizes ranged from 28,638 bp (Prototheca zopfii) to 176,851 bp (Chlorella vulgaris), while GC content varied between 25.7% (Prototheca stagnorum) and 38.2% (Marvania geminata). Protein-coding gene counts spanned from 19 (Prototheca bovis) to 89 (Pseudochloris wilhelmii). Additionally, rRNA gene numbers ranged from three (single-copy) to six (two copies), and tRNA gene counts varied from 24 (Helicosporidium sp.) to 35 (multiple species).

Phylogenomic analyses of chlorellaceae

Three methods were used for phylogenomic analysis (Fig. 1). First, partitioned supermatrix analyses were conducted using IQ-TREE, with the best-fit model determined for each gene individually. Second, unpartitioned supermatrix analyses implemented IQ-TREE under the FreeRate heterogeneity model. Third, coalescent-based analyses were performed. All three approaches yielded highly congruent topologies, providing robust support for several key relationships. The monophyly of the Chlorella, Parachlorella, Nannochloris, and AHP clades received strong support. The Chlorella and Parachlorella clades formed robustly supported sister groups, whereas the Nannochloris clade was moderately supported as sister to the Chlorella + Parachlorella lineage. A notable exception to this concordance involved Pseudochloris wilhelmii. In both supermatrix analyses (Fig. 1A-B), it weakly clustered with the Nannochloris clade. However, coalescent-based analyses (Fig. 1C) placed it outside the combined Chlorella + Parachlorella + Nannochloris clades, indicating instability of its phylogenetic position.

Within the Chlorella clade, a total of 20 plastomes were obtained from public databases, mostly from Chlorella and Micractinium. This study reported 14 additional new plastomes, including the first plastome of Actinastrum, Didymogenes, Masaia, Dictyosphaerium, Closteriopsis, Neochlorella, Crucigenia lauterbornii, and Coronastrum ellipsoideum. Within the Chlorella clade, most intergroup relationships received strong support. The genus Micractinium was monophyletic with robust support, whereas Chlorella was polyphyletic, consistent with analyses based on nuclear SSU + ITS2 sequences (Fig. S1). Notably, Chlorella vulgaris (the type species of Chlorella), C. variabilis, and N. thermophila formed a strongly supported cluster (100/1.00/100) in plastome-based phylogenies. Chlorella sorokiniana and C. heliozoae also constituted a well-supported clade (100/1.00/100) in plastome analyses. Conversely, in 18S rDNA + ITS2 phylogenies (Fig. S1), C. sorokiniana clustered strongly with Chlorella lewinii (99/1.00/99), whose plastome remains unreported. Crucially, all phylogenetic analyses revealed a distant relationship between C. sorokiniana (and its relatives) and C. vulgaris, indicating that the genus Chlorella requires taxonomic reassessment, especially C. sorokiniana (Fig. 2). Morphologically distinct species, Actinastrum hantzschii and Crucigenia lauterbornii, formed a basal clade within this branch, while Coronastrum ellipsoideum constituted a separate lineage.

Within the Parachlorella clade, only two plastomes existed publicly prior to this study. We report seven additional plastomes, including the first genomes for Masaia oloidia, Dictyosphaerium lacustre, and Closteriopsis acicularis, along with several unidentified taxa. Plastome-based phylogenies (Fig. 2) positioned the needle-shaped C. acicularis and crescent-shaped D. acuatus basally within the clade, while spherical-celled taxa formed a distinct cluster. Strains FACHB-3694 and FACHB-3681 constituted a robustly supported sister group. Conversely, strains FACHB-3712 and FACHB-3697 formed independent lineages. Nuclear SSU + ITS2 analyses (Fig. S2) confirmed strain FACHB-3697 as phylogenetically distinct from established genera. Strains FACHB-3681 and FACHB-3694 clustered with Dictyosphaerium-like unidentified taxa (corresponding to Lineage I in [14]), whereas strain FACHB-3712 grouped with morphologically similar unidentified taxa (Lineage III in [14]).

Analysis of structural characteristics

The evolution of IR regions in the Chlorellaceae shows an interesting trend (Fig. 2). All plastomes of the Parachlorella clade retain the IR region, while most plastomes in the sister Chlorella clade have lost it, except for a branch at the base within the Chlorella clade. The Nannochloris clade, located at the base of the lineage of the Parachlorella and Chlorella clades, has completely lost the IR region. The monospecific clade P. wilhelmii, located outside the Nannochloris clade, has retained the IR region. In contrast, the AHP clade has completely lost the IR region. Combining IR regions with phylogenetic relationships suggests that Chlorellaceae underwent at least three independent loss events of the IR region.

Comparative analysis of inverted repeat (IR) boundary regions across 13 plastomes (Fig. 3) revealed a correlation between phylogenetic relatedness and structural conservation. Conspecific strains (Actinastrum hantzschii strains FACHB-3717 and FACHB-3702) exhibited identical IR boundaries, whereas closely related taxa (Crucigenia lauterbornii strain FACHB-3713 and A. hantzschii) showed similar but distinct boundary architectures. Phylogenetically distant taxa displayed substantially greater IR structural variation. All IR regions contained three rRNA genes and limited tRNAs (Fig. 4). Pseudochloris wilhelmii uniquely encoded additional protein-coding genes (accD, psaI, atpB, atpE, and rps4). Within the Parachlorella clade, the basal lineage (Closteriopsis acicularis and Dictyosphaerium acuatus) retained only the core rRNA/tRNA genes, while derived spherical-celled lineages acquired supplementary genes (rbcL, psbA, rps14, atpA). IR regions were significantly shorter in the Chlorella clade versus Parachlorella clade. There is a trend of gene content reduction from P. wilhelmii to the Parachlorella clade, and subsequently to the Chlorella clade.

Structural variations are prevalent within the Chlorellaceae, even among intraspecific different strains (Fig. 5). A comparative analysis of the plastomes of five C. vulgaris strains reveals that inversions and reversals are common. The results show four types of gene arrangements. While the number of protein-coding genes remains consistent across the strains, there is a notable difference in plastome size, ranging from 154,201 bp to 176,851 bp. This variation is primarily attributed to large segment insertions containing a limited number of open reading frames (orfs) and intergenic regions.

Codon usage bias

The coding regions of 58 plastomes showed a total GC content ranging from 23.6% to 38.3%. The GC content at the third codon position (GC3) was notably lower, ranging from 7.2% to 20.8%, indicating a preference for A/T bases and A/T-ending codons in Chlorellacean plastomes. Examination of the Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) revealed that all plastomes contained 61 codons (excluding stop codons) and encoded 20 amino acids, except for P. bovis (MF197536), P. ciferrii (MF197535), and P. stagnora (AP018372), which lacked the CGG codon. With the exception of Methionine and Tryptophan, which are each encoded by a single codon, the remaining amino acids were encoded by 2 to 6 synonymous codons. RSCU values greater than 1 were observed for 22 codons, all of which were A/T-ending, while RSCU values < 1 was observed for 30 codons, all C/G-ending codons. The most used codon was UUA (encoding leucine, Leu), followed by CGU (encoding Arginine, Arg). A detailed comparison of codon usage bias among different life strategy groups showed significant differences, particularly between obligate heterotrophic groups and autotrophic/mixotrophic species (Fig. 6). Autotrophic and mixotrophic groups demonstrated a high degree of similarity in codon usage, with the main difference being a preference for AUA when encoding Ile and a tendency to select CGG when encoding Arg in the mixotrophic group. In contrast, the obligate heterotrophic group exhibited significant bias in the usage of specific codons compared to autotrophic and mixotrophic groups. The obligate heterotrophic group preferred using GGA for Gly, CAU for His, AUA for Ile, UUA for Leu, AGA and AGG for Arg, and UAU for Tyr. These findings suggest that life strategy plays a significant role in shaping codon usage patterns within Chlorellacean plastomes, with obligate heterotrophs displaying distinct preferences that may reflect adaptations to their unique metabolic requirements.

Plastomes characteristics of different life strategies

Plastomes display unique characteristics based on different life strategies within the Chlorellaceae. Obligate heterotrophic algae have undergone notable changes compared to autotrophic and mixotrophic algae, including a decrease in GC content and a significant reduction in plastid genome length (Fig. 7). These alterations are due to a reduction in the gene spacer region and a decrease in the number of coding genes (CDS). While both mixotrophic and autotrophic groups exhibit similar gene numbers, encompassing genes related to photosynthesis, transcription, and translation, the obligate heterotrophic group has lost all genes associated with photosynthesis, as well as some genes linked to ATP synthase and RNA polymerase. Additionally, analysis of codon usage bias indicates significant differences in codon usage between obligate heterotrophic groups and autotrophic/mixotrophic species. These findings suggest that obligate heterotrophic groups and autotrophic/mixotrophic species have been subjected to distinct selection pressures, leading to diverse evolutionary trajectories.

Discussion

Phylogenetic analyses of chlorellaceae

Previous studies [2, 8, 12, 18] and our 18S rDNA/ITS2 analyses have highlighted persistent issues within Chlorellaceae, such as the polyphyletic nature of the genus Chlorella and unresolved relationships within the family. These problems arose from limited taxon sampling, phylogenetically weak markers, and unresolved taxonomic revisions in key groups. To address these gaps, our study used a comprehensive phylogenomic framework of 58 plastomes representing all major clades. Critically, expanding Parachlorella plastome sampling from two to nine taxa enabled robust clade-resolution. Our analyses yield two key advances: (1) strong support for the monophyly of the four principal clades (Chlorella, Parachlorella, Nannochloris, and AHP) and their interrelationships; and (2) we resolve sister-group relationships among multiple genera with strong support. Furthermore, we validate the distinctiveness of Dictyosphaerium-like lineages, warranting future taxonomic revision. This study establishes a resolved phylogenetic framework essential for taxonomic refinement and evolutionary studies in Chlorellaceae [14].

Our plastome-based phylogeny provides new insights into the phylogenetic placement of Pseudochloris, a taxon with persistently ambiguous positioning. Somogyi et al. (2013), using 18S rDNA data, weakly resolved Pseudochloris as sister to the Chlorella + Parachlorella lineage [20]. Conversely, Lemieux et al. (2014), based on limited chlorellacean plastid genome (ptDNA) sampling, suggested Pseudochloris clusters with Marvania geminata (Nannochloris clade) [31]. In our study, supermatrix methods weakly group Pseudochloris with Nannochloris (Fig. 1A-B), whereas coalescent-based methods place it basally outside the combined Chlorella + Parachlorella + Nannochloris clades (Fig. 1C). This discrepancy likely reflects methodological differences and limited sampling. Although our expanded 79-protein-gene dataset provides enhanced phylogenetic signal, the conflicting results underscore the complexity of resolving Pseudochloris' position. Resolution requires further evidence, including additional taxonomic sampling and plastomes.

The evolution of the IR region

Comparative analyses of Chlorellaceae plastomes reveal dynamic evolutionary patterns in the inverted repeat (IR) regions, marked by three independent loss events: complete IR loss in the AHP and Nannochloris clades, and IR loss within the several members of Chlorella clade. Pseudochloris wilhelmii retains ancestral IR architecture with five unique protein-coding genes, suggesting this mayrepresents the plesiomorphic condition for the family. Ancestral state reconstruction supports IR presence in the common ancestor of Parachlorella and Chlorella clades. However, within Chlorella, the IR persists only in a basal subclade with diminished length and gene content compared to Parachlorella, indicating an evolutionary trajectory of progressive contraction culminating in loss. Our observation of three independent IR losses in Chlorellaceae extends this paradigm, suggesting that IR degeneration is a key driver of plastome architecture diversification. This pattern aligns with broader trends in chlorophyte evolution [22–24]: Turmel et al. (2018) documented frequent IR losses in Trebouxiophyceae, IR loss events often define major clades and are associated with increased genome instability [22].

Distinct characteristics of plastomes in different life strategies

Previous research has documented multiple gene losses related to photosynthesis in the plastomes of non-photosynthetic species such as Prototheca and Helicosporidium [28, 29]. However, these studies were limited by insufficient taxonomic sampling and mainly focused on specific genes. Our study advances this field by providing a more comprehensive analysis of 58 plastomes that represent the phylogenetic diversity of the family Chlorellaceae, revealing previously unrecognized evolutionary patterns. Most significantly, we demonstrate for the first time that obligate heterotrophic taxa exhibit fundamentally different codon usage biases compared to their autotrophic and mixotrophic relatives. These systematic differences in synonymous codon selection—particularly for glycine, histidine, isoleucine, leucine, arginine, and tyrosine—reflect adaptations to divergent intracellular environments in parasitic lifestyles. These findings provide new insights into how both large-scale gene content changes and subtle nucleotide-level evolutionary processes influence plastome adaptation during significant ecological transitions.

Study limitations and future perspectives

While this study offers valuable insights into the phylogeny and evolution of Chlorellaceae plastomes, certain limitations must be acknowledged. Although the taxonomic coverage is significantly increased, it remains incomplete, as many uncommon or unculturable taxa were not included, potentially affecting phylogenetic resolution. Furthermore, the functional implications of plastome structural variations and codon usage biases in heterotrophic lineages require additional experimental validation. Future research should expand taxon sampling, particularly for understudied genera, and incorporate multi-omics approaches to clarify the phylogeny and evolution of this family. Comparative studies with other Trebouxiophycean lineages could also help elucidate broader evolutionary patterns in plastome architecture. Addressing these gaps will enhance our understanding of Chlorellacean diversity and the genomic basis for ecological adaptations.

Conclusion

In summary, our study focuses on the Chlorellaceae, a significant and highly diverse family. Phylogenomic analyses were conducted using a relatively comprehensive dataset and three tree-building methods, successfully resolving the phylogenetic relationships among the main lineages within the Chlorellaceae. Furthermore, our study revealed prevalent structural variations and the evolution of Chlorellacean plastomes, uncovering notable adaptive changes in plastome size, gene content, and codon usage bias in obligate heterotrophic taxa compared to autotrophic and mixotrophic taxa. This research enhances our understanding of the phylogenetic relationships and species diversity within the Chlorellaceae and provides a foundation for the utilization and development of microalgal resources.

Materials and methods

Strain isolation, culture and characterization

The 21 Chlorellacean strains described herein were isolated from water samples collected in 2022–2023 from China. The collection of water samples has been approved by the management personnel. All strains were deposited and available in the Freshwater Algae Culture Collection at the Institute of Hydrobiology (FACHB collection, https://algae.ihb.ac.cn/), Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, and the deposition numbers, species names and 18S rDNA + ITS2 sequences are in Table S1. Single-cell or single-colony isolation was achieved by selecting individual cells using a micropipette and transferring each single cell to 48-well culture dishes for growth and characterization after multiple washes. They grew in BG11 medium [32]. Cultures were maintained at 25 °C under a 16:8 h light: dark cycle with approximately 30 μmol m-2 of cool white fluorescent light. A ZEISS Axio Imager Z2 light microscope (Zeiss, Sliedrecht, Netherlands) was used for morphological observation. Identification of the cultured algae was based on microscopic morphological characters and phylogenetic analysis using 18S rDNA + ITS2 sequences.

DNA preparation and whole genome sequencing

Cultures in the exponential growth phase were harvested and concentrated by centrifugation. Total nucleic acids were extracted using the DNAsecure Plant Kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). DNA degradation and contamination were monitored on 1% agarose gels. DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit® DNA Assay Kit in a Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA). A total of 0.2 μg DNA per sample was used as input material for the DNA library preparations. The sequencing library was generated using the NEB Next® Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations, and index codes were added to each sample. The genomic DNA sample was fragmented by sonication to a size of 350 bp. Subsequently, DNA fragments were end-polished, A-tailed, and ligated with the full-length adapter for Illumina sequencing, followed by further PCR amplification. After PCR products were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA), DNA concentration was measured with the Qubit® 3.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen, USA), and libraries were analyzed for size distribution by NGS3K/Caliper and quantified by real-time PCR (3 nM). The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot Cluster Generation System using the Illumina PE Cluster Kit (Illumina, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following cluster generation, the DNA Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform, generating 150 bp pair-end reads. The sequencing data for each strain was > 6 G, and the sequencing depth of the genome was > 40X. The raw sequencing reads generated in this study have been deposited in the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) under accession number CRA024790 and are publicly accessible at: https://bigd.big.ac.cn/gsa/browse/CRA024790. Additionally, all annotated plastome sequences have been submitted to GenBank, with corresponding accession numbers provided in Table 1.

Quality control, assembly, and annotation

The original fluorescence image files obtained from the Illumina platform are transformed into short reads (raw data) through base calling. These short reads are saved in FASTQ format, which includes sequence information and corresponding sequencing quality data. Sequence artifacts, such as reads with adapter contamination, low-quality nucleotides, and unrecognizable nucleotides (N), pose challenges to reliable bioinformatics analysis. We utilized Fastp (version 0.19.7) to conduct basic statistics on the quality of the raw reads. The data processing steps were as follows: (1) Discard paired reads if they contain adapter contamination; (2) Discard paired reads if more than 10% of bases are uncertain in either read; (3) Discard paired reads if the proportion of low-quality bases (Phred quality < 5) bases exceeds 50% in either read. The 18S rDNA and ITS2 sequences were assembled using GetOrganelle [33] with the following parameter settings: “-R 10 -k 21, 45, 65, 85, 105 -F embplant_nr”, utilizing publicly available 18S rDNA + ITS2 sequences of Chlorella sorokiniana (AB731602.1), Didymogenes sphaerica (AB731603.1), and Didymogenes soliella (AB731605.1) as references. The plastome sequences were assembled using GetOrganelle with the following parameter settings: “-R 15 -k 21, 45, 65, 85, 105 -F embplant_pt”, referencing the plastomes of Chlorella vulgaris (MK948102.1), Micractinium pusillum (MN649872.1), Parachlorella kessleri (NC_012978.1), Dicloster acuatus (NC_025546.1), and Chlorella heliozoae (NC_036805.1). The sequences were validated by aligning sequencing reads to the assembled sequences using BWA [34] and SAMtools (1.9) [35], and visualized with IGV [36]. The plastomes were annotated using MFannot (https://megasun.bch.umontreal.ca/apps/mfannot/) and BLAST homology searches. The ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) were identified using barrnap v0.9 (https://github.com/tseemann/barrnap) and manually verified. The annotated data have been submitted to the GenBank databases (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/), and the accession numbers are listed in Table 1.

Phylogenetic analyses

The taxonomic identification of the 21 newly acquired strains was achieved through an integrated analysis of 18S rDNA and ITS2 sequences, with reference sequences sourced from type species and authentic strains. For secondary structure prediction, we utilized the methods outlined in [18, 19, 37]. Specifically, we used the interactive modeling platform of the ITS2 Database [38] to predict the secondary structures of 18S rDNA and ITS2 sequences, based on the reference structure of Heterochlorella luteoviridis (X73998) and Chlorella sorokiniana (AY323463), respectively. Subsequently, sequence-structural alignments for both 18S rDNA and ITS2 secondary structures were aligned using 4SALE v1.3 [39], followed by the concatenation of the aligned sequences with PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [40].

We have used the sequence part from the sequence-structure alignment for phylogenetic tree reconstruction. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using IQ-TREE multicore v2.0.3 [41] (maximum likelihood with model optimization and Bayesian inference with AU tests). For the Chlorella clade analysis (Fig. S1), the 84-sequence dataset (2,121 aligned positions) employed partitioned substitution models: TIM3e + I + G4 for SSU and GTR + F + I + G4 for ITS2. The Parachlorella clade analysis (Fig. S2) utilized a 52-sequence dataset (2,137 positions) with model specifications of TIM2e + I + G4 for SSU and TPM2 + F + G4 for ITS2.

Phylogenomic analyses encompassed representatives from nearly all major clades within Chlorellaceae. The outgroup consisted of Binuclearia lauterbornii (KM462880) and Neglectella solitaria (FJ968739). The analysis utilized 79 proteins (YCF1, YCF20, ACCD, ATPA, ATPB, ATPE, ATPF, ATPH, ATPI, CCSA, CEMA, CHLB, CHLI, CHLL, CHLN, CLPP, CYSA, CYST, FTSH, INFA, MIND, PETA, PETB, PETD, PETG, PETL, PSAA, PSAB, PSAC, PSAI, PSAJ, PSAM, PSBA, PSBB, PSBC, PSBD, PSBE, PSBF, PSBH, PSBI, PSBJ, PSBK, PSBL, PSBM, PSBN, PSBT, PSBZ, RBCL, RPL12, RPL14, RPL16, RPL19, RPL20, RPL23, RPL2, RPL32, RPL36, RPL5, RPOA, RPOB, RPOC1, RPOC2, RPS11, RPS12, RPS14, RPS18, RPS19, RPS2, RPS3, RPS4, RPS7, RPS8, RPS9, SECG, TUFA, YCF12, YCF3, YCF4, and TILS) for the phylogenomic analyses. Data preparation for the analysis was performed using PhyloSuite v1.2.2 The 79 proteins were aligned with MAFFT v7.471 [42] employing the G-INS-i (accurate) strategy. TrimAl v1.2 [43] was utilized to eliminate poorly matched sites in the alignments through a heuristic method (-automated1).

The phylogenetic trees were constructed utilizing coalescent-based and supermatrix analyses. For the coalescent-based analyses, the ML analyses for each gene were conducted using IQ-TREE with 1000 rapid bootstrap replicates, and the best-fit partition model was selected based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Subsequently, the coalescence-based species tree was generated by combining all single gene trees using the Accurate Species Tree ALgorithm program (ASTRAL) v 5.7.8 [44]. In the supermatrix analyses, 79 proteins were concatenated using PhyloSuite v 1.2.2, and missing genes were replaced with "?". A supermatrix comprising 18,963 positions was created, and the phylogenetic tree was inferred using IQ-TREE under two conditions: (1) gene-based partitioning, employing IQ-TREE with the best-fit partition model determined by the BIC criterion, and (2) no partition, utilizing IQ-TREE with the best-fit partition model determined by the FreeRate heterogeneity model. The resulting phylogenetic tree was visualized using the software Figtree v1.4.4.5 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/).

Analysis of structural characteristics

To analyze the expansion/contraction events of the inverted repeat (IR) region, the boundaries between the LSC, IR, and SSC regions of all 13 plastomes with the IR region were compared and illustrated using the online applications CPJSdraw v1.0.0 [45]. Comparison of gene content and gene order in the IRa regions of the 13 plastomes was drawn with OGDRAW (https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/OGDraw.html). The rearrangements among different plastomes of Chlorella vulgaris were visualized using Mauve v. 2.3.1 in progressive model [46]

Codon usage bias analysis

The dataset comprised 56 protein-coding genes with sequence lengths exceeding 300 base pairs, which were extracted and concatenated using PhyloSuite v1.2.2. For 58 plastomes, the final coding sequences used for the analysis of codon usage bias ranged from 20.4 kb to 60.2 kb across different strains. The Relative Synonymous Codon Usage (RSCU) was assessed using the CodonW 1.4.2 software. RSCU values for codons indicate the average frequency of codon usage for encoding amino acids. An RSCU value of 1 signifies an absence of bias in the usage of synonymous codons. The data were standardized (column scale) and graphically represented using TBtools [47]
