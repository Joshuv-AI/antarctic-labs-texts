Thorough QT/QTc Clinical Study to Evaluate the Effect of Remdesivir on Cardiac Repolarization in Healthy Participants
Authors: Chi‐Chi Peng, Ran Duan, Mazin Abdelghany, Santosh Davies, Deqing Xiao, Anna Kwan, Ana Ruiz‐Garcia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTQT interval prolongation associated with drug administration is an important consideration in drug development. Patients with COVID‐19 are at increased risk for cardiac complications, and QT interval prolongation correlates with higher mortality. This Phase 1 study evaluated the safety, tolerability, pharmacokinetics, and potential effects on QT interval corrected for heart rate (QTc) of a single supratherapeutic intravenous dose of remdesivir (600 mg; 3 times the loading dose [200 mg] of the approved regimen) in healthy participants, as well as the safety of its solubilizing excipient sulfobutylether‐β‐cyclodextrin. The study included 2 cohorts: a dose‐selection cohort (sentinel cohort), followed by a partially blinded, randomized, placebo‐ and positive‐controlled, 3‐period, 6‐treatment sequence, single‐dose crossover cohort (TQT cohort). The clinical endpoints were adverse events (AEs), laboratory abnormalities, plasma pharmacokinetic parameters of remdesivir and its metabolites, ΔΔQTcF (baseline‐adjusted, placebo‐corrected QT interval corrected for heart rate using the Fridericia formula) at each postdose time point, and the relationship between ΔΔQTcF and plasma concentrations of remdesivir by concentration‐QT analysis. Sixty participants completed the study. Plasma pharmacokinetics of remdesivir and its metabolites showed no significant differences between cohorts. Sulfobutylether‐β‐cyclodextrin was cleared within 24 h, with no pharmacokinetic non‐linearities observed. The study found no clinically relevant QTc prolongation from remdesivir at a supratherapeutic dose (ΔΔQTcF < 10 ms). Most AEs were Grade 1 or 2 in severity. No serious AEs or deaths were reported. These findings suggest that a single 600‐mg intravenous dose of remdesivir is generally safe and well tolerated and does not lead to QTc prolongation of safety concern.Keywords: modeling, pharmacokinetics, QT, QTC interval, safety

Introduction

QT interval prolongation (i.e., delay in cardiac repolarization) [1] associated with drug administration may result in the development of a delay in ventricular repolarization, which is associated with a risk of torsade de pointes [2]; therefore, assessment of corrected QT interval (QTc) prolongation has become an important consideration in drug development [3]. Patients with COVID‐19 have an increased risk for cardiac complications [4], and the presence of a prolonged QT interval in these patients has been associated with increased mortality [5]. A previous study reported that mild QTc prolongation at baseline (QTc > 440 ms for males and QTc > 450 ms for females) independently predicted disease severity, myocardial injury, and 1‐year mortality among patients hospitalized with COVID‐19 [6]. Additionally, some widely used medications for managing COVID‐19, including nirmatrelvir/ritonavir and molnupiravir, are associated with potential cardiovascular adverse effects [7].

Remdesivir is an intravenously administered nucleotide analog prodrug that inhibits replication of SARS‐CoV‐2 [8], the causative pathogen of COVID‐19. Remdesivir is globally approved for the treatment of COVID‐19, with a recommended dosage of a single intravenous (IV) loading dose of 200 mg on Day 1, followed by a daily 100‐mg IV maintenance dose for up to 10 days, in adults and pediatric patients weighing ≥ 40 kg; for pediatric patients aged < 28 days and weighing ≥ 1.5 kg and those aged ≥ 28 days and weighing 1.5 to < 3 kg, a 2.5‐mg/kg IV loading dose on Day 1 followed by a daily 1.25‐mg/kg IV maintenance dose from Day 2 is recommended in the United States; for pediatric patients aged ≥ 28 days and weighing 3 to < 40 kg, a 5‐mg/kg IV loading dose on Day 1 followed by a daily 2.5‐mg/kg IV maintenance dose from Day 2 is recommended [9, 10, 11, 12]. Patients receiving the clinical regimen of remdesivir may experience pharmacokinetic (PK) exposure changes due to factors related to COVID‐19 disease, organ impairment, or drug–drug interactions.

There are limited data available on the risk of drug‐induced QT interval prolongation by remdesivir. Data submitted to the European Medicines Agency reported a half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 28.9 μM for in vitro inhibition of the human ether‐a‐go‐go‐related gene (hERG) channel by remdesivir [11]; an analysis that estimated the risk of drug‐induced long QT syndrome based on this IC50 value and the corresponding remdesivir maximum observed concentration (Cmax) suggested that high peak plasma concentrations of remdesivir could carry a risk for QT interval prolongation [13]. Other in vitro studies reported no inhibitory effects of acute treatment with 10‐μM remdesivir on the hERG potassium channel‐related current [14], a key determinant of ventricular repolarization, and acute exposure to remdesivir (100 nM and 1 μM) and GS‐441524 had no effect on hERG currents and the half‐activation voltage [15]. However, prolonged treatment (24 h) with 100 nM and 1 μM remdesivir resulted in reduced hERG currents in vitro. Phase 3 clinical studies indicate that remdesivir is generally safe and well tolerated in patients with COVID‐19 [16, 17, 18]; QT interval prolongation was not assessed in these Phase 3 studies because previous Phase 1 studies showed that neither remdesivir nor its metabolites (GS‐704277 and GS‐441524) cause QT interval prolongation at therapeutic concentrations in healthy adults [19]. A recent retrospective cohort study supported these findings, showing no statistically significant difference in QTc before and after remdesivir treatment (200‐mg IV loading dose on Day 1 followed by a 100‐mg IV maintenance dose for 5 days) in patients with severe COVID‐19 [20].

Furthermore, the formulation of remdesivir contains the solubilizing excipient sulfobutylether‐β‐cyclodextrin (SBECD) at a 30:1 ratio (3000 mg SBECD per 100 mg of remdesivir) [21, 22]. A 3000‐mg dose is below the maximum recommended safety threshold dose for SBECD (250 mg/kg/day) [23]. SBECD is predominantly cleared by renal filtration, with a terminal elimination half‐life (t 1/2) of < 2 h in patients with normal kidney function [12, 24].

To fulfill the regulatory requirement on QT prolongation assessment at a supratherapeutic dose of remdesivir, a Phase 1 thorough QT/QTc study was designed and executed. The safety, tolerability, plasma PK profile of remdesivir, its metabolites, and the SBECD excipient were evaluated in the present study at a single supratherapeutic IV dose of remdesivir in healthy participants (600 mg of remdesivir containing 18,000 mg of SBECD). The highest single IV dose of remdesivir previously evaluated in healthy participants was 225 mg, which resulted in Cmax values of 4420 ng/mL for remdesivir, 315.1 ng/mL for GS‐704277, and 257 ng/mL for GS‐441524 [25]. In healthy participants administered the clinical dosing regimen (200‐mg loading dose on Day 1 followed by 100‐mg daily maintenance doses for 4 or 9 days), Cmax values at Day 5 or 10 were 2230, 246, and 145 ng/mL for remdesivir, GS‐704277, and GS‐441524, respectively [19]. Considering the dose‐proportional PK properties of remdesivir, a single 600‐mg dose (3 times the loading dose of the approved clinical dosing regimen) administered to healthy participants was predicted to provide sufficient exposures (≥ 3‐fold increase in Cmax) to account for anticipated increases in exposures of remdesivir and its metabolites due to COVID‐19 disease, organ impairment, or drug–drug interactions. Both intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including organ impairment and drug–drug interactions, can individually increase the Cmax of remdesivir and its metabolites by ~3.1‐fold [26, 27, 28]. In patients hospitalized with COVID‐19, disease‐related factors contributed to a modest increase in Cmax when compared to healthy individuals and nonhospitalized patients with COVID‐19 [29]. However, Cmax values among patients with COVID‐19, organ impairment, and/or drug–drug interactions who received the clinical dosing regimen remained 1‐ to 2‐fold lower than those observed with a single 600‐mg dose of remdesivir among healthy participants, which represents the highest clinical exposure for remdesivir and its metabolites. Additionally, the potential for QTc prolongation was assessed by time point analysis and linear mixed‐effects analysis of baseline‐adjusted, placebo‐corrected QTc values versus remdesivir plasma concentrations over time (concentration‐QT [C‐QT] analysis).

Methods

Study Design

This was a Phase 1 study with 2 cohorts. The first cohort was an adaptive remdesivir dose‐selection cohort (sentinel cohort), which was followed by a partially blinded, randomized, placebo‐ and positive‐controlled, 3‐period, 6‐treatment sequence, single‐dose crossover cohort (thorough QT [TQT] cohort; Figure 1). The study was conducted at a single study center in the United States.

The protocol and informed consent forms were approved by a duly constituted institutional review board (Advarra; protocol approval number Pro00070005) before study initiation (March 6, 2023). This study was conducted in accordance with the International Council for Harmonisation Guidance for Good Clinical Practice and the original principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Eligible participants were healthy individuals aged 18–55 years with a body mass index between 19.0 and 29.9 kg/m2. Female participants were nonpregnant and nonlactating. Eligibility also required 12‐lead electrocardiogram (ECG) evaluations without clinically significant abnormalities, normal renal function (creatine clearance ≥ 90 mL/min using the Cockcroft‐Gault equation), and liver tests (e.g., alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and total bilirubin) within the normal range at screening and admission. Participants needed to be in good health as determined by the investigator based on medical history and physical examination. The screening evaluation must have been performed ≤ 28 days prior to the scheduled first dose (Day 1).

Key exclusion criteria included the following cardiovascular‐specific criteria at screening: resting heart rate (HR) < 50 beats per minute (bpm) or > 90 bpm; presence or history of cardiovascular disease, cardiomyopathy, and/or cardiac conduction abnormalities; and prolongation of the screening QT interval corrected for HR using the Fridericia formula (QTcF; eg, a prolongation of QTcF > 450 ms for participants assigned male at birth and > 470 ms for participants assigned female at birth). Additional criteria are listed in the Supplementary Methods.

Study Treatments

A sentinel cohort was included in this study to ensure participant safety, as the planned remdesivir and SBECD doses were higher than those evaluated in prior clinical trials. For the sentinel cohort, eligible participants received a single 600‐mg remdesivir IV infusion over 30 min on the morning of Day 1. The 600‐mg supratherapeutic dose was selected based on the PK exposure range of remdesivir and its metabolites observed in patients with COVID‐19 following the clinical regimen (200/100 mg), the potential for drug–drug interactions, and initial results from Phase 1 studies on renal and hepatic impairment in participants without COVID‐19 [26, 27]. Upon review of all safety and available PK data, the 600‐mg remdesivir dose was expected to be well tolerated and to provide an approximately ≥ 3‐fold increase in Cmax; therefore, this dose was selected as the dose for the TQT cohort. For the TQT cohort, eligible participants were randomly assigned 1:1:1 to 1 of 3 treatment arms comprising a 600‐mg remdesivir IV infusion containing 18,000 mg of SBECD (~257 mg/kg/day) over 30 min; a placebo‐to‐match remdesivir IV infusion over 30 min (placebo); and a 400‐mg moxifloxacin tablet (positive control).

Blinding to treatment assignments or data was not performed for the sentinel cohort. For the TQT cohort, participants, investigator, and clinical site staff were blinded to remdesivir and placebo and unblinded to moxifloxacin. Gilead Sciences personnel and the site pharmacist were unblinded. Unblinded study monitors were used.

Endpoints

For the sentinel cohort, the clinical endpoints were: the incidence of adverse events (AEs) and laboratory abnormalities, as well as the plasma PK parameters of remdesivir and its metabolites, GS‐704277 and GS‐441524. PK parameters of interest included area under the concentration‐time curve extrapolated to infinite time (AUCinf), area under the concentration‐time curve from dosing to last measurable concentration (AUClast), Cmax, observed time point of Cmax (Tmax), t 1/2, last observed quantifiable concentration (Clast), and observed time point of Clast (Tlast) throughout the study.

For the TQT cohort, the clinical endpoints were: the incidence of AEs and laboratory abnormalities; the plasma PK parameters of remdesivir and its metabolites; the baseline‐adjusted, placebo‐corrected QTcF (ΔΔQTcF) at each postdose time point; and the relationship between ΔΔQTcF and plasma concentrations of remdesivir by C‐QT analysis.

Plasma PK parameters of SBECD were assessed in both cohorts in a post hoc analysis.

Study Procedures

Blood samples were collected to determine the PK profiles of remdesivir, GS‐704277, GS‐441524, and SBECD. For the sentinel cohort, PK assessments were performed on Day 1 at the following time points: 0.5 h prior to the start time of infusion and 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.33, 1.67, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h following the start time of infusion. For the TQT cohort, PK assessments were performed Days 1, 13, and 25 at 0.5 h prior to the start time of infusion and 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.33, 1.67, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h after the start time of infusion.

For the sentinel cohort, 12‐lead safety ECGs were collected on Days −1, 1, 2, 4, and 6 (discharge); Day 1, ECGs were assessed at predose (≤ 5 min before the start time of infusion) and 2 h following the start time of infusion. For the TQT cohort, 12‐lead safety ECGs were collected on Days −1, 1, 13, 25, and 29 (discharge); Days 1, 13, and 25, ECGs were assessed at predose (≤ 5 min before the start time of infusion) and 2 h following the start time of infusion.

For the TQT cohort only, Holter monitoring using a Mortara Instrument (Milwaukee, WI, USA) X12+ or T12 ECG continuous 12‐lead digital recorder was performed on Days 1, 13, and 25 for each predetermined time point (≥ 3 12‐lead ECGs were collected within 5 min for each time point). The predose time points for baseline assessments were: 1.5, 1 h, and 30 min prior to the start time of infusion. The postdose time points were 0.25 (middle of infusion), 0.5 (end of infusion), 0.75, 1, 1.33, 1.67, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h following the start time of infusion. Holter ECGs were analyzed by blinded ECG readers at a designated core cardiology laboratory (Clario; Philadelphia, PA, USA).

Bioanalytical Methods

Plasma concentrations of remdesivir, GS‐704277, GS‐441524, and SBECD were determined using validated liquid chromatography‐tandem mass spectroscopy methods. Additional details can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

PK Analyses

The PK analysis sets comprised all enrolled participants who received ≥ 1 dose of study drug and had ≥ 1 nonmissing postdose concentration value reported by the PK laboratory for the corresponding analytes. PK parameters were estimated using standard noncompartmental methods with Phoenix WinNonlin software. Plasma concentrations and PK parameters for remdesivir, GS‐704277, GS‐441524, and SBECD were descriptively summarized by analyte, as applicable.

Statistical Analysis

By Time Point QT Analysis

The by time point QT analysis was based on the Pharmacodynamic (PD) Analysis Set, which comprised all randomized participants in the TQT cohort who received ≥ 1 dose of study drug and had ≥ 1 nonmissing baseline‐adjusted QTcF (ΔQTcF) at any postdose time point.

To establish assay sensitivity, a positive control (moxifloxacin) was used to test the study's ability to detect the study endpoint. It was assumed that if QTcF prolongation can be detected upon moxifloxacin administration, then a finding of no QTcF effect of that size for the study drug will constitute evidence that the study drug does not prolong QTcF by the amount of regulatory concern (5 ms). A Bonferroni correction was used in constructing the CIs. Establishment of assay sensitivity was concluded if, for ≥ 1 of the 3 prespecified postdose time points (t = 1, 2, and/or 3 h) around the Tmax of moxifloxacin, the lower limit of the two‐sided 96.67% CI for the mean difference in ΔQTcF between moxifloxacin and placebo was > 5 ms.

To demonstrate a lack of clinically relevant effects of remdesivir on the QTcF as compared with placebo, the statistical framework was based on a noninferiority design. If the upper limits of two‐sided 90% CIs for the ΔΔQTcF were below the predefined clinically relevant threshold (i.e., < 10 ms) at all 14 prespecified postdose time points at which ECGs were collected, a lack of effect of regulatory concern was demonstrated. The CIs were produced by fitting a mixed‐effects model. Further details can be found in the Supplementary Methods.

C‐QT Analysis

The C‐QT analysis was based on the C‐QT Analysis Set, which comprised all randomized participants who received ≥ 1 dose of study drug and had ≥ 1 pair of evaluable ΔQTcF and plasma concentration of remdesivir at any planned postdose time point; for placebo participants, plasma concentrations of these analytes were all assumed to be 0.

The C‐QT analysis is a model‐based approach that was conducted in accordance with the guideline recommendations [30]. Model assumptions were evaluated prior to modeling (Supplementary Methods). To examine whether there were any drug effects on HR, empirically estimated baseline‐adjusted, placebo‐corrected HR (ΔΔHR) were plotted as mean (±standard deviation [SD]) versus time. The selection of correction factor (Bazett or Fridericia) was examined upon confirmation of lack of drug effect on HR. To examine the assumption of no hysteresis effects, the ΔΔQTcF and plasma concentration of remdesivir were plotted separately as mean (±SD) versus time. Graphical display of mean ΔΔQTcF and mean plasma concentration of remdesivir over time was also provided. A similar approach was used to estimate ΔΔHR. To examine the adequacy of linearity and homoscedasticity assumptions, the relationship between ΔQTcF and plasma concentration of remdesivir was plotted to show the trends in the observed data. Since there was no obvious drug effect on HR, no obvious evidence of hysteresis effect, and no obvious violation of the homoscedasticity assumptions observed, linear mixed‐effects models were implemented to investigate the relationship between ΔQTcF and plasma concentrations of remdesivir. Adjustment for placebo was performed within the model. The estimation of the model parameters was based on the restricted maximum likelihood estimation method. The predicted ΔΔQTcF was estimated by a linear mixed‐effect function that included the treatment term and the geometric mean Cmax of remdesivir at a supratherapeutic dose (single IV infusion of 600 mg over 30 min).

Graphical and tabular summaries were generated to assess goodness of fit of the final C‐QT model, including a histogram of scaled residuals, a quantile‐quantile plot of scaled residuals, a plot of scaled residuals versus fitted mean ΔQTcF, a plot of scaled residuals versus covariates, a plot of fitted versus observed ΔQTcF, and a ΔQTcF versus plasma concentration of remdesivir quantile plot with model slope and 90% CI overlaid.

A model based on the final C‐QT model with concentrations of remdesivir, GS‐441524, and GS‐704277 was examined. Predicted mean ΔΔQTcF and 90% CI from this model were obtained for evaluation of all 3 analytes' impact on QT prolongation.

Safety

The Safety Analysis Set comprised all enrolled participants who received ≥ 1 dose of study drug. Clinical and laboratory AEs were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA) Version 26.1. The incidences of treatment‐emergent AEs and graded laboratory abnormalities were summarized by treatment.

Results

Participant Disposition and Demographics

Sixty‐one participants were enrolled: 6 in the sentinel cohort and 55 in the TQT cohort (Figure S1). Sixty participants completed the study (sentinel cohort, n = 6; TQT cohort, n = 54), and 58 participants completed treatment with study drug (sentinel cohort, n = 6; TQT cohort, n = 52). Three participants prematurely discontinued from study drug (all in the TQT cohort) because of AEs (n = 2) and withdrawal of consent (n = 1).

The median (range) age of all participants was 38 (19, 55) years; 33 (54.1%) participants were assigned male at birth, and 53 (86.9%) were White (Table S1). In the sentinel cohort, the median (range) age was 43 (41, 55) years; 2 (33.3%) participants were assigned male at birth, and all were White. In the TQT cohort, the median (range) age was 37 (19, 54) years; 31 (56.4%) participants were assigned male at birth, and 47 (85.5%) were White.

PK Evaluation

There were 60 participants in the remdesivir, GS‐704277, and GS‐441524 PK Analysis Sets from both the sentinel and TQT cohorts. Mean plasma concentrations and PK parameters of remdesivir, GS‐704277, and GS‐441524 were similar between cohorts following the administration of 600‐mg remdesivir, indicating sufficient drug washout from the previous period within the TQT cohort (Figure S2 and Table S2). The mean (%CV) of Cmax in the sentinel cohort was 14,500 (12.4) ng/mL for remdesivir, 1400 (20.5) ng/mL for GS‐704277, and 606 (12.2) ng/mL for GS‐441524. The mean (%CV) of Cmax in the TQT cohort was 14,000 (16.3) ng/mL for remdesivir, 1400 (25.6) ng/mL for GS‐704277, and 557 (17.2) ng/mL for GS‐441524. The geometric mean Cmax for observed remdesivir exposures pooled across study days was used for C‐QT modeling (13,800 ng/mL; Table S3). The geometric mean Cmax was 14,100, 13,400, and 14,100 ng/mL for study Days 1, 13, and 25, respectively.

There were 48 participants in the SBECD PK Analysis Set. PK parameters observed following the supratherapeutic remdesivir dose containing 18,000 mg of SBECD for AUClast (SD) and Cmax (SD) values were 2280 (319) h*μg/mL and 1417 (215) μg/mL, respectively (Figure S3 and Table S4). SBECD was cleared ≤ 24 h postdose and had a median t 1/2 of 1.49 h.

By Time Point QT Analysis

There were 54 participants in the PD Analysis Set. The estimated upper bounds of the two‐sided 90% CI for the least squares mean difference between the supratherapeutic dose of remdesivir and placebo were < 10 ms at all prespecified postdose time points, indicating no clinically relevant QTcF prolongation effect from remdesivir (Table 1 and Figure 2A). No participant had an observed postdose QTcF > 450 ms and a ΔQTcF > 30 ms at the same time point (Table 2). An analysis of the positive control data was also performed to establish the assay sensitivity. The estimated lower bounds of the two‐sided 96.67% CI for the least squares mean difference in ΔQTcF between moxifloxacin and placebo were > 5 ms at 2 of the 3 prespecified postdose time points, establishing assay sensitivity (Figure 2B).

C‐QT Analysis

There were 54 participants in the C‐QT Analysis Set. No obvious model assumption violations for the C‐QT analysis were observed. Therefore, the prespecified model was fitted to obtain the parameter estimates for the previously specified fixed effects. The model evaluation results based on scaled residuals indicated that the model specification was adequate (Figure S4).

In both the observed and model‐derived data, the mean ΔΔHR measurements were < 10 bpm at all time points, indicating no obvious remdesivir effect on HR after correcting for placebo (Figure 3).

Bazett's correction method (QTcB) overcorrected for the trend between the QT and RR intervals, and Fridericia's correction method (QTcF) also did not completely account for the relationship, with a slope estimate that was statistically significantly different from 0 (−0.011, 95% CI, −0.017 to −0.005; Figure S5). QTcF and changes in this dependent variable were considered appropriate for the purpose of this C‐QT analysis.

Visual comparison of the mean remdesivir concentration‐time profiles and mean ΔΔQTcF‐time profiles indicated general concordance in the time course of both PK and cardiac repolarization and no time dependency of the ΔΔQTcF on remdesivir plasma concentration was evident (Figure S6). A counterclockwise pattern in this plot would suggest a time‐delay in QTc prolongation; however, this was not observed. Instead, the ΔΔQTcF profiles generally increased with concentration over the first hour, followed by a decline.

ΔΔQTcF observations by nominal time point are displayed in Figure 4A. The predicted mean ΔΔQTcF at the geometric mean Cmax of remdesivir (13,800 ng/mL) was 0.16 (90% CI, −0.93, 1.21) ms (Table S5). Consistent results were obtained from the sensitivity analysis, which included all 3 analytes in the final model (Table S6). Similar to the by time point QT analysis, the upper bound of the 90% CI model‐derived ΔΔQTcF at the geometric mean Cmax of the supratherapeutic remdesivir dose was < 10 ms, indicating there was no evidence that remdesivir had a clinically relevant effect on QTcF (Figure 4B).

Safety Evaluation

All AEs by participant are reported in Table S7. Most AEs were Grade 1 or 2 in severity, and no serious AEs or deaths were reported (Table 3). In the sentinel cohort, 5 (83.3%) participants experienced AEs considered related to study drug. In the TQT cohort, 30 (55.6%) participants, 6 (11.3%) participants, and 5 (9.3%) participants experienced AEs considered related to study drug in the remdesivir, placebo, and moxifloxacin groups, respectively. In both cohorts, the most frequently reported AEs following administration of 600 mg remdesivir were nausea (sentinel cohort, n = 5 [83.3%]; TQT cohort, n = 26 [48.1%]) and vomiting (sentinel cohort, n = 3 [50.0%]; TQT cohort, n = 21 [38.9%]). One participant (1.9%) in the TQT cohort had a Grade 3 AE of transaminase increased; no other Grade ≥ 3 or 4 AEs, tachycardias, or AEs related to QT prolongation were reported.

Discussion

The clinical evaluation of QT/QTcF prolongation and proarrhythmic potential for non‐antiarrhythmic drugs has become increasingly important in drug development due to the potential adverse cardiovascular outcomes, as outlined in the International Council for Harmonisation E14 guidance [3]. This is particularly relevant for COVID‐19 treatments, since SARS‐CoV‐2 infection is linked to a variety of pro‐inflammatory mediators which may substantially contribute to the development of cardiac and arrhythmic complications [4].

A previous C‐QT analysis of remdesivir was conducted using Phase 1 data in healthy adult volunteers who received daily 60‐min IV infusions of 150‐mg remdesivir for 7 or 14 days [19]. Exposures were sufficient to cover the therapeutic exposures at the clinical regimen of 200 mg on Day 1 followed by 100 mg once daily for up to 10 days [19]. The results indicated that neither remdesivir nor its metabolites induced QT interval prolongation at therapeutic concentrations [19]. This is consistent with studies submitted to the European Medicines Agency reporting an IC50 of 28.9 μM, indicating that remdesivir showed a weak inhibition of the hERG channel in vitro [11]. Although a model‐based analysis often provides greater sensitivity and a more comprehensive assessment, the extremely rapid elimination of remdesivir causes plasma concentration to vary by several orders of magnitude within hours, making it difficult to conduct the linearity analyses of concentration versus QT across the observed range. Therefore, an assessment of the effects of supratherapeutic remdesivir concentrations on QTc was designed and conducted.

In the present study, PK exposures of remdesivir (Cmax: 14,000 ng/mL) and its metabolites (GS‐704277 Cmax: 1400 ng/mL; GS‐441524 Cmax: 557 ng/mL) after a single supratherapeutic 600‐mg IV dose in the TQT ohort were approximately dose‐proportional to those observed in a previous study where 200‐mg remdesivir was administered to healthy participants [19].

The supratherapeutic dose of 600‐mg remdesivir did not induce a clinically relevant QTcF prolongation compared with placebo in the TQT cohort based on the by time point QT analysis. These results indicate that, even at increased PK exposures of remdesivir and its metabolites, remdesivir does not affect QTc prolongation in healthy participants. No participant had an observed postdose QTcF > 450 ms and a ΔQTcF > 30 ms at the same time point. Additionally, the C‐QT analysis estimated the predicted mean ΔΔQTcF and corresponding two‐sided 90% CI were below the geometric mean Cmax of remdesivir at the supratherapeutic 600‐mg dose level. Based on the results of the final model, remdesivir had no clinically relevant effect on QTcF across the range of concentrations evaluated up to 19,400 ng/mL.

SBECD is used as a solubilizing excipient in several IV medications, including 13 US Food and Drug Administration‐approved injectable products on the market and multiple clinical candidates under development [31]. However, evaluating the PK and safety of SBECD at high doses is still important, especially since SBECD is known to accumulate in patients with impaired kidney function [24]. In the present study, 18,000 mg of SBECD was contained in the 600‐mg remdesivir IV infusion as equivalent to ~257 mg/kg/day, which is slightly above the maximum recommended safety threshold of 250 mg/kg/day [23] and, to our knowledge, one of the highest dosages of SBECD tested to date. The SBECD Cmax was 1417 μg/mL, which is 2‐fold higher than the highest Cmax reported previously [32]. SBECD was cleared ≤ 24 h postdose with a t 1/2 consistent with prior clinical observations [33, 34, 35]. The high dose of SBECD tested in the present study led to no new safety concerns.

No AEs associated with QT prolongation, serious AEs, or death were reported after a single 600‐mg IV dose of remdesivir. The most common AEs in both cohorts were nausea and vomiting. These results are consistent with previous Phase 1 studies in healthy participants, in which all AEs reported were Grade 1 or 2 in severity, and no serious AEs or deaths were reported [19, 25].

The study was limited by the inclusion of healthy individuals rather than individuals with COVID‐19. However, the PK exposures of remdesivir and its metabolites observed in this study were 1‐ to 2‐fold greater than those observed in healthy individuals and individuals with COVID‐19 who were administered the clinical dosing regimen in the presence of all relevant intrinsic and extrinsic factors, including renal impairment and drug–drug interactions [28, 29].

Overall, the present study found that a single 600‐mg IV dose of remdesivir was generally safe and well tolerated, resulting in approximately dose‐proportional PK exposures of remdesivir and its metabolites compared with a previous study in healthy participants. The supratherapeutic 600‐mg dose of remdesivir did not affect the QTcF when compared to placebo treatment.
