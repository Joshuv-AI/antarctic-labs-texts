Moisture sorption and hydration dynamics in intrinsically hygroscopic hydrogels
Authors: Weixin Guan, Dongsheng Chen, Meng An, An Hoai Vu, Yaxuan Zhao, Yan Dai, Chuxin Lei, Guihua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hygroscopic materials that capture water vapor across a wide humidity range are important for emerging applications including atmospheric water harvesting, moisture control, and thermal management. Hydrogel sorbents are attractive because they can store large amounts of water while remaining solid-like, yet common hydrophilic gels sorb little vapor at low relative humidity (RH) unless hygroscopicity is introduced by deliquescent salts. Here, we report intrinsically hygroscopic hydrogels (IHHs) assembled from inosine and Zn2+ through pH-controlled supramolecular coordination. The IHHs show a moisture-sorption onset near zero RH, reaching 0.17 g g−1 at 30% RH while retaining swelling-amplified uptake at high RH (1.9 g g−1 at 95% RH) and reversible cycling. Varying gelation pH reprograms the coordination microenvironment and network structure, reshaping early-stage sorption and shifting uptake onset. Experiments and simulations indicate that hydration-accessible Zn-centered motifs serve as important early hydration loci, while pH-dependent network structure further modulates hydration growth. These results provide a coordination-chemistry route to hygroscopic hydrogels without salt deliquescence.

INTRODUCTION

Water vapor is an abundant but dilute resource whose capture and release under mild conditions underpin emerging approaches to atmospheric water harvesting (1–3), passive dehumidification (4, 5), thermal management (6, 7), etc. A key materials challenge is to design sorbents that can initiate hydration at low relative humidity (RH), where the chemical potential of water is low, while still achieving large water contents at high RH, to broaden the operational window over fluctuating ambient humidity (8). In this context, hydrogels are conceptually attractive because they can hold large amounts of water within a percolated three-dimensional network while maintaining a coherent, hierarchically structured, solid-like form (9, 10). However, in the vapor phase, widely used hydrophilic polymer hydrogels often show little uptake at low-to-moderate RH and exhibit substantial uptake only at high RH, despite their excellent swelling in liquid water.

The dominant practical route to enhance vapor uptake of hydrogels at low RH is to introduce hygroscopic salts (such as lithium chloride and calcium chloride) into a hydrophilic, water-swellable polymer matrix, such as poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), poly(acrylamide) (PAM), and related networks (11–13). In these salt-in-gel composites, the polymer network serves as a scaffold that hosts the hygroscopic phase, whereas the salt provides strong water–ion interactions that increase the thermodynamic driving force for hydration at low RH. Although effective, this salt-in-gel paradigm carries potential compromises. Mobile ionic species could leach during cycling and accelerate corrosion of device components (14). Achieving intrinsic (salt-free) hygroscopicity in hydrogels remains far less developed, and crucially, its governing molecular mechanisms are not yet well understood.

Why are pristine “water-absorbing” polymer hydrogels such as PAA and PAM often weakly hygroscopic in their dried states (figs. S1 and S2)? The answer lies in how these networks reorganize upon dehydration. Drying typically drives substantial network densification: pore space collapses, free volume decreases, and segmental mobility is suppressed, producing a glassy densified network (15, 16). At the molecular level, polar functional groups that interact strongly with water in the swollen state can become effectively inaccessible in the dry state because they are internally satisfied by interchain hydrogen bonding (for example, amide-amide and carboxylic acid-carboxylic acid interactions) (17, 18) and by close packing within an entangled polymer matrix that arises from flexible linear chain configurations (19). Under low to moderate RH, incoming water therefore largely forms site-bound adsorption at the surface accessible motifs, while penetration into the bulk is inhibited by a high kinetic barrier associated with disrupting interchain locking and creating free volume (20). Only at sufficiently high RH, where water activity is large enough to substantially increase local hydration, does water-induced plasticization lower this barrier, enabling deeper transport, network relaxation, and the onset of pronounced swelling-assisted uptake, often manifested as a delayed sorption onset followed by a rapid upturn in the isotherm (Fig. 1A). In this picture, moisture sorption is not limited by the intrinsic hydrophilicity of the polymer repeat units, but by the dry-state accessibility of sorption sites and the plasticization barrier imposed by a glassy, hydrogen-bond-locked network.

Here, we introduce a salt-free chemistry design principle for shifting the hydration onset of hydrogels to lower RH without abandoning the fundamental hydrogel advantage of swelling at high RH. We develop intrinsically hygroscopic hydrogels (IHHs) formed through supramolecular assembly of a semi-rigid, low-molecular-weight gelator with metal ions, yielding a coordination-mediated network whose dry-state packing and site accessibility differ qualitatively from those of flexible linear polymer gels. We propose that coordination geometry and steric constraints in these supramolecular gels can frustrate the formation of a tightly hydrogen-bond-locked, densified network, preserving hydration-accessible hygroscopic sites in the dry state. As RH increases, hydration proceeds through clustered hydration around these sites accompanied by progressive network relaxation, before evolving at high RH into a more networked hydration state where swelling becomes a major amplifier of water content (Fig. 1B). Importantly, this approach targets the accessibility barrier that limits PAM- and PAA-type hydrogels, rather than relying on deliquescent salt species to impose hygroscopicity.

In this work, we use the readily available biomolecule inosine and Zn2+ ions as building blocks to construct IHHs and to interrogate their assembly and moisture hydration dynamics (Fig. 1, C and D). We observe a pronounced dependence of moisture sorption on pH-dependent network assembly, revealing a direct coupling between supramolecular coordination structure and vapor-phase hydration. By combining experimental characterizations with molecular dynamics simulations, we identify the metal-centered coordination environments as the early hydration motifs and track how the local water structure evolves from initial binding to clustered and more connected hydration within the gel. Together, these results establish a mechanistic framework for salt-free hygroscopic hydrogels, opening a complementary pathway and a molecular-level understanding for designing hydrogel sorbents whose moisture response is encoded in the network architecture itself.

RESULTS

pH-controlled coordination assembly of IHHs

IHHs were prepared by mixing inosine with Zn2+ in water followed by pH adjustment. The pre-gel solutions remained homogeneous upon mixing and gradually evolved into self-supporting hydrogels within a few hours under quiescent conditions. Gelation was reproducibly observed across a broad alkaline window (pH 8–12, Fig. 1E), providing a broad pH handle to tune network formation through solution speciation. Powder x-ray diffraction (XRD) shows that the as-formed gels are amorphous (Fig. 2A). All samples display a broad halo centered near 2θ ≈ 20° without sharp Bragg reflections. No peaks attributable to crystalline zinc-containing inorganic phases [for example, zinc salts, ZnO, or Zn(OH)2] are observed (fig. S3), supporting a coordination-mediated supramolecular network rather than an inorganic precipitate acting as the scaffold. To examine how the supramolecular network evolves with pH, we performed small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) for gels prepared at pH 8, 9, 11, and 12 (Fig. 2B). The low-q intensity (0.01–0.05 Å−1) rises monotonically with pH, pointing to enhanced large-scale density fluctuations and higher network connectivity as coordination strengthens. In the intermediate-q window (0.05–0.1 Å−1), the spectra shift upward and develop stronger curvature at higher pH, consistent with the emergence of larger supramolecular aggregates with more defined length scales. The pronounced steepening at pH 12 highlights a more developed, higher-contrast network compared with lower-pH gels.

To match the dry sorbent state used for vapor-sorption measurements, the following coordination-related structural characterizations were performed on the corresponding freeze-dried gels. We next examined the pH-dependent inosine–Zn interaction using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Fig. 2C). Two coupled spectral changes point to chelation at the 6-oxopurine core. In the carbonyl region, the dominant C6 = O band of inosine at ~1685 cm−1 becomes a broadened envelope upon Zn addition, featuring a slightly red-shifted component (≈1681 cm−1 at pH 8) together with the emergence of a distinct low-frequency component near ~1641 cm−1 whose intensity increases systematically with pH. The growth of this ~1641 cm−1 component indicates an increasing population of an O6-rich electronic environment in which the effective C6 = O bond order is reduced, consistent with stronger O6 donation under more alkaline conditions. For 6-oxopurines such as hypoxanthine/inosine, alkaline deprotonation is known to access monoanionic forms that are well represented by resonance structures with substantial 6-enolate character, which increases the donor strength at O6 and can amplify metal–O6 interactions (21). More generally, deprotonation in purine frameworks increases π-electron delocalization, supporting enhanced resonance participation in the anionic manifold (22). In parallel, the purine-ring C–N/C=N coupled region shows a pronounced displacement (from ~1296 cm−1 toward ~1350 cm−1 at pH 12), consistent with coordination-linked redistribution of electron density within the conjugated ring. N7 is a principal inner-sphere coordination site for purines, and simultaneous interaction with a 6-oxo (O6) oxygen is well established, making an N7,O6 chelation motif plausible for inosine in this system (23, 24). Given the coordination flexibility of Zn(II) toward mixed N/O donor sets (25, 26), these coupled FTIR signatures support a pH-evolving Zn–inosine coordination environment in which O6 engagement becomes progressively more prominent as pH increases.

Zn K-edge XANES and EXAFS were then used to quantify the Zn local environment across the pH series (Fig. 2, D and E). The Zn K-edge XANES line shapes differ from the ZnO and ZnPc references; therefore, the edge positions are interpreted only qualitatively within the gel series rather than used for absolute valence assignment. Across the series, the absorption edge shifts progressively to lower energy with increasing pH (Fig. 2D, inset), consistent with increased electron donation to the Zn center under more alkaline conditions, for example through more anionic inner-sphere ligation. Fourier-transformed EXAFS shows that all gels are dominated by a first-shell peak at low R, with no evidence for Zn–Zn scattering expected for metallic Zn (Fig. 2E). The first-shell peak positions fall in the Zn–(N/O) regime bracketed by ZnO (Zn–O) and ZnPc (Zn–N) references, showing that the local Zn environment is dominated by light scatterers (N/O). Because N and O contribute similarly in this first-shell EXAFS treatment, the fitting is described using a single average Zn–N/O path, yielding an average coordination number close to four for all samples. From pH 8 to 12, the fitted coordination number increases modestly from ~3.8 to ~4.4 while the average Zn–N/O distance contracts from ~2.06 Å to ~1.98 Å, pointing to progressively tighter first-shell coordination at higher pH (figs. S4 to S7 and table S1).

Integrating the FTIR-identified coordination-site evolution with the EXAFS-derived first-shell coordination numbers and bond distances, together with the gelation conditions and molecular dynamics simulation (fig. S8), we propose representative local motifs that capture the dominant first-shell chemistry (Fig. 2F). At pH 8, the data support a Zn center chelated by inosine through N7 and O6 and completed by aquo ligands, for example [Zn(N7)(O6)(H2O)2]. At pH 12, stronger coordination under alkaline conditions supports a similar N7, O6 chelate in which hydroxo ligation becomes more prevalent, for example [Zn(N7)(O6)(OH)2]. These structures are intended as typical coordination motifs rather than unique, single-species assignments; in the gel, a distribution of Zn–(N/O) coordination environments is expected to coexist and dynamically interconvert. Collectively, these coordination interactions act as reversible junctions that couple inosine-based supramolecular aggregates into a percolated hydrogel network, with pH providing a direct handle to tune both local coordination strength and network development.

Moisture sorption tuned by the coordination microenvironment

Moisture sorption of the IHHs was quantified using a dynamic vapor sorption (DVS) analyzer to evaluate how the pH-tailored network affects water uptake. Stepwise measurements at 30% and 60% RH show a pronounced pH dependence (Fig. 3A). At 30% RH, the equilibrium uptake increases from ~0.05 g g−1 for IHH_pH 12 to ~0.17 g g−1 for IHH_pH 8. At 60% RH, the uptake increases from ~0.12 g g−1 (pH 12) to ~0.36 g g−1 (pH 8). Across the full pH series (8–12), lowering pH systematically increases uptake in both the low-RH and moderate-RH regimes. The full sorption isotherms show that pH primarily tunes the hydration onset and the low-to-intermediate RH response (Fig. 3B and fig. S9). IHH_pH 12 remains weakly hygroscopic up to high humidity and then exhibits a sharp uptake onset around ~70% RH. In contrast, IHH_pH 8 sorbs measurable moisture already at very low RH and increases continuously across the RH range, followed by the high-RH upswing characteristic of swelling-amplified uptake in hydrogel sorbents. After desorption from saturation, the low-RH end of the IHH_pH 8 desorption branch remains open, retaining a non-negligible water content relative to the adsorption branch, whereas IHH_pH 12 shows much weaker low-RH retention. Because both gels approach similar uptake near saturation, these trends potentially point to differences in the accessibility and effectiveness of early hydration motifs as a key determinant of the onset behavior, rather than differences in ultimate swelling capacity at high RH.

This pH dependence is compatible with a Zn-centered hydration mechanism in which the identity and lability of ligands in the Zn microenvironment control the earliest binding events. Zn(II) is coordination-flexible in N/O donor environments (4–6 coordination) (26), and hydration can proceed through ligand exchange and progressive build-up of a Zn-centered hydration microenvironment even when the average first-shell coordination is close to four. Under mildly alkaline conditions, the dominant N7, O6 chelation motif is expected to be completed by more labile aquo ligation, providing hydration-accessible Zn sites that nucleate bound-water formation at low RH and promote subsequent progressive hydration. Under strongly alkaline conditions, hydroxide competes effectively with water for inner-sphere ligation at Zn(II), leading to a more hydroxo-rich and less hydration-accessible coordination microenvironment (27). In that case, substantial uptake emerges only at high RH, when water activity is sufficient to build up hydration shells and drive network relaxation and swelling-amplified uptake.

To further examine the role of Zn coordination environments in sorption, we modified the Zn precursor from nitrate to acetate. Nitrate is weakly coordinating in aqueous coordination chemistry, whereas acetate commonly binds metal centers as an inner-sphere ligand and can occupy coordination positions (28, 29). The acetate-derived gel shows strongly suppressed moisture sorption across the entire RH range (Fig. 3C), with a particularly diminished low-RH response and a much lower overall uptake compared with the nitrate-derived IHH. Together with the FTIR characterization of the acetate-derived gel (fig. S10), these results are consistent with acetate partially occupying/capping Zn-associated coordination positions and thereby reducing access to early hydration sites. At the same time, because acetate can also influence supramolecular assembly, accompanying changes in network structure may also contribute to the reduced uptake, particularly toward the mid- to high-RH regime.

Hydration dynamics in IHHs

We next tracked the evolution of sorbed water states using RH-dependent FTIR. Because inosine contributes both N–H and O–H stretching signals in the 2800–3600 cm−1 region, we analyzed difference spectra by subtracting the dry-gel spectrum from spectra collected after equilibration at 30%, 60%, and 90% RH, thereby approximately isolating the dominant changes associated with water O–H vibrations (Fig. 3D). The resulting bands can be assigned to strongly hydrogen-bonded water (~3250 cm−1), weakly hydrogen-bonded water (~3350 cm−1), and non-hydrogen-bonded (or weakly interacting) water (~3550 cm−1) (30). At 30% RH, the spectrum is dominated by strongly hydrogen-bonded water, showing that early uptake is primarily tightly bound water associated with specific polar microenvironments. At 60% RH, strongly bound water remains dominant, while contributions from less strongly bound water become apparent. At 90% RH, the intermediate and higher-frequency components become more prominent, reflecting a shift toward weaker binding and more extended hydrogen-bond structures as hydration progresses. Consistent signatures of local chemical-environment evolution are also observed in the inosine fingerprint region (Fig. 3E). The O6-associated carbonyl band near 1681 cm−1 progressively redshifts and broadens with increasing RH, reflecting strengthening hydrogen bonding and polarization around the O6-rich microenvironment as sorbed water accumulates at, and in the vicinity of, Zn-coordinated motifs. In parallel, the purine C–N/C=N coupled region near ~1296 cm−1 shifts toward higher wavenumber as RH increases. This trend can be rationalized by hydration-induced competition and dynamic modulation within the Zn coordination sphere: increasing water incorporation perturbs inner-sphere interactions at the Zn–purine motif and redistributes electron density across the conjugated purine framework. Together, these RH-dependent FTIR changes support a picture in which the Zn-centered coordination microenvironment serves as an important early hydration locus. As RH increases, progressive Zn-site hydration continuously tunes the local binding and electronic environment of inosine, and this evolving microenvironment potentially couples to network relaxation during the hydration process.

Finally, the sorbed water can be reversibly released under mild heating (fig. S11). The gel also shows stable cycling behavior, maintaining its adsorption–desorption response without detectable decay over 45 humidity cycles at 30% RH (Fig. 3F and fig. S12), indicating that the hydration process associated with these motifs is reproducible under repeated operation.

To further infer the spatial organization of hydration associated with the pH-dependent sorption onset, we carried out molecular dynamics simulations of IHH network models representing the pH 8 and pH 12 coordination states, and examined the spatial organization of water at nominal 5%, 30%, and 60% RH (Fig. 4, A and B). At pH 8, even at 5% RH the adsorbed water localizes preferentially around Zn2+ sites, forming Zn-centered hydration microdomains. Increasing RH drives a clear progression from localized binding to clustered hydration at 30% RH, followed by an extended, percolated hydration pattern at 60% RH in which water-rich regions span the network (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the pH 12 model adsorbs far fewer water molecules at low RH and remains spatially sparse; even at 60% RH, the water distribution does not evolve into a connected hydration network within the simulated window (Fig. 4B). This divergence parallels the experimental isotherms, where IHH_pH 8 shows measurable uptake at very low RH, whereas IHH_pH 12 remains weakly hygroscopic until much higher humidity (Fig. 3B).

Two structural factors emerge from the simulations to account for this difference. First, the pH 12 network adopts a more compact, space-filling architecture. The supramolecular aggregate exhibits a larger radius of gyration at pH 12 than at pH 8 (Fig. 4C), in line with the emergence of larger structural length scales at higher pH inferred from SAXS (Fig. 2B). At the same time, under NPT conditions the pH 12 model equilibrates to a smaller simulation volume (Fig. 4D), reducing the effective free volume available for water accommodation. Representative snapshots similarly highlight a denser framework at pH 12 than at pH 8 (Fig. 4E). Together, these features constrain the growth and coalescence of water-rich domains, delaying the transition from localized hydration to clustered and networked hydration. Second, hydration at low RH is controlled by the accessibility of Zn-centered binding motifs. The Zn–water radial distribution functions (RDFs) show a sharp first-shell feature for IHH_pH 8 in the 2.0–3.5 Å range already under ultralow humidity, and the integrated coordination increases systematically with RH (Fig. 4F). In the pH 12 model, the first-shell Zn–water peak is markedly attenuated and its RH-dependent growth is much weaker (Fig. 4G), pointing to fewer Zn sites participating in direct water association over the same humidity range. These results suggest that the delayed uptake of IHH_pH 12 arises from the combined effects of reduced accessibility of Zn-associated early hydration motifs and a more compact network that limits the accommodation and growth of hydrated domains.

Beyond Zn-centered binding, the simulations resolve how the hydrogen-bond network evolves with RH. At low RH, hydrogen bonding involves inosine polar sites (W–Ino) (fig. S13). As RH increases, water–water hydrogen bonding (W–W) rises more rapidly and eventually dominates (Fig. 4H), marking the transition from localized, site-associated hydration to more bulk-like water structures that support clustered and then connected hydration. This evolution aligns with the FTIR-derived water-state progression (Fig. 3D), where strongly hydrogen-bonded water dominates at low RH and weaker, more extended hydrogen-bond populations become increasingly prominent at higher RH.

DISCUSSION

This work introduces intrinsically hygroscopic hydrogels (IHHs) as a salt-free approach to broaden the humidity window over which traditional pristine hydrogels can sorb water vapor. By building the network through supramolecular assembly and metal–ligand junctions, the IHHs retain the swelling-amplified uptake that hydrogels naturally provide at high RH, while shifting the hydration onset toward much lower RH through hydration-accessible coordination microenvironments. Conceptually, this offers a complementary route for settings where mobile hygroscopic salts are undesirable, and where achieving moisture sorption across a broad RH spectrum is a primary objective.

Beyond the material concept, the results point to two coupled determinants of salt-free hygroscopicity in soft networks. First, progressive hydration with increasing RH is shaped by the motifs that stabilize the initial bound-water population. In this Zn-inosine system, lower gelation pH within the mildly alkaline range favors a more hydration-accessible Zn coordination microenvironment, whereas higher pH shifts the local coordination toward tighter O6-rich and more hydroxo-involved binding, reducing the accessibility of these early hydration motifs. Once these motifs are populated, additional water grows outward from site-associated hydration into clustered hydration and, at higher RH, into more connected water structures that couple to network relaxation and swelling. Second, the same pH-tuned coordination chemistry also modulates the mesoscale compactness of the network. In the present system, stronger coordination and densification at higher pH reduce the effective free volume available for water accommodation and hinder the growth and coalescence of water-rich domains, whereas a less compact network facilitates the expansion and connection of hydration clusters. Together, these observations show that low-RH behavior cannot be inferred from the hydrophilicity of the organic component alone; instead, it reflects the interplay between primary-site accessibility and network openness, both of which are tunable through coordination microenvironments.

Finally, gelation and the associated humidity response are metal-dependent under the conditions tested. Inosine forms IHHs with Zn2+ across pH 8–12, whereas Mn, Fe, Co, Ni and Cu did not yield comparable hydrogels at pH 8 under otherwise identical conditions. This selectivity may reflect the coordination chemistry of Zn2+, including its d10 electronic configuration (no ligand-field stabilization), coordination-number and geometry flexibility with N/O donors, and redox-inert behavior, which together can favor reversible junction formation. By contrast, several first-row transition-metal ions exhibit stronger geometric preferences and distinct hydrolysis or precipitation tendencies under alkaline conditions, which may bias assembly away from a dynamic percolating network required for gelation (31).

Overall, IHHs expand the design space for hygroscopic soft materials by showing how supramolecular, coordination-defined networks can couple low-RH hydration onset with high-RH swelling behavior, and by providing a molecularly grounded view of how early hydration motifs evolve into progressive hydration across humidity.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and chemicals

Inosine (98%) was purchased from Ambeed. Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (98%) and zinc acetate dihydrate (ACS reagent) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Sodium hydroxide (ACS reagent) was purchased from Fisher Scientific. All chemicals were used without further purification.

Synthesis of IHHs

IHHs were prepared by adjusting the pH of a 0.1 M inosine aqueous solution to the desired pH using NaOH, followed by mixing with an equal volume of 0.1 M Zn(NO3)2 aqueous solution. The resulting homogeneous precursor solutions were left undisturbed at room temperature to form self-supporting hydrogels within several hours. All hydrogels were freeze-dried prior to water vapor sorption measurements.

Characterization

FTIR spectra were acquired on a Bruker INVENIO R spectrometer equipped with a liquid-nitrogen-cooled narrow-band mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector and a Ge ATR crystal. Powder x-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were collected using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600 diffractometer. Water vapor sorption and desorption were measured using a dynamic vapor sorption analyzer (DVS Adventure, Surface Measurement Systems). Small-angle x-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements were performed on a SAXSLab Ganesha instrument. Zn K-edge x-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra were collected at the BL14W beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF); the storage ring was operated at 3.5 GeV with a stable current of 200 mA. A Si(111) double-crystal monochromator was used, and data were collected at ambient conditions in transmission mode with an ionization chamber.

Molecular dynamics simulations

All-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to construct representative inosine–Zn coordination network structures and to analyze the local coordination environment of Zn2+. Inosine was modeled using the OPLS-AA force field (32), and Zn2+ parameters were taken from the ion force field reported by Li et al. (33) Simulations were carried out in LAMMPS (34) with periodic boundary conditions in all three dimensions. A time step of 1 fs was used throughout. Non-bonded interactions were truncated at 12.0 Å, and long-range electrostatics were treated using Ewald summation with an accuracy of 1 × 10−4. Initial configurations were first energy-minimized to remove close contacts, then equilibrated in the NPT ensemble at 500 K and ambient pressure for 500 ps to facilitate network formation. The systems were subsequently annealed to 300 K over 100 ps (NPT), equilibrated at 300 K for a further 500 ps (NPT), and finally simulated at 300 K in the NVT ensemble for 1.0 ns to obtain representative network structures (Fig. 4E). Structural metrics, including radius of gyration and simulation cell volume, were extracted from the equilibrated trajectories.

Grand canonical Monte Carlo simulations

Water adsorption in the coordination networks was evaluated using grand canonical Monte Carlo (GCMC) simulations to obtain equilibrium water configurations under different humidity conditions. In the GCMC calculations, the gel network was treated as a rigid framework; thus, the simulations were limited to ≤60% RH and were not intended to quantitatively describe swelling-dominated behavior at higher RH. Water molecules were represented using the SPC/E model (35) and were sampled using translation, rotation, insertion, and deletion trial moves with equal probability. Relative humidity (RH) was expressed as the reduced pressure, RH/100 = P/P0, where P0 is the saturated vapor pressure at 300 K. Simulations were performed at 300 K for the targeted RH values, and the resulting configurations were analyzed to obtain spatial water distributions (Fig. 4, A and B), Zn–water radial distribution functions and coordination statistics (Fig. 4, F and G), and hydrogen-bond metrics (Fig. 4H). Representative adsorption configurations were visualized using VMD (36).
