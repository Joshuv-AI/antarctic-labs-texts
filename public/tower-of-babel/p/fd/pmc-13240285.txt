Recent advances in diagnostic methodologies for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries
Authors: Jiaoyang Chen, Zhuan Zhong, Hanyang Zhang, Bingzhe Huang, Zhaoyan Li, Zhende Jiang, Fei Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article focuses on the various diagnostic methods for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, as well as the advantages and disadvantages of each diagnostic method. The purpose of this study is to help clinicians make a more accurate diagnosis, so as to improve the prognosis of patients with tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries.Diagnostic methods for injury to the tibiofibular syndesmosis include physical examination, imaging techniques, arthroscopy, and novel kinematic assessment.Radiographic examination serves as the foundational diagnostic tool. CT imaging has significantly advanced in detecting minor injuries of the tibiofibular syndesmosis. MRI is currently recognized as the most sensitive and specific imaging modality, capable of accurately diagnosing ligament injuries. Ultrasonography is a valuable tool for demonstrating significant diagnostic utility in chronic tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries.Arthroscopy is the gold standard for diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries.The modified stability test appears to be more aligned with current clinical diagnostic requirements. Its reliability and accuracy surpass those of traditional imaging techniques.Tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, often resulting from high-energy trauma or rotational forces, represent a significant subset of ankle injuries that may occur either concurrently with or independently of ankle fractures. This condition can lead to ankle instability and secondary osteoarthritis, which severely impact patients’ functional recovery and quality of life. Due to the anatomical complexity and varied injury patterns, accurately diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries remains a clinical challenge. This review summarizes current diagnostic methods and research advancements in tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, encompassing physical examination, imaging techniques, arthroscopy, and novel kinematic assessment, to assist clinicians in achieving more precise diagnoses and ultimately improving patient outcomes.

Introduction

The tibiofibular syndesmosis is a fibrous joint structure composed of the distal tibiofibular articulation and the inferior tibiofibular ligament complex, which plays a crucial role in maintaining normal ankle joint function (1). The tibiofibular syndesmosis stabilizes the malleolar region of the distal tibiofibular articulation, effectively preventing lateral dislocation of the talus (2). Simultaneously, the fretting characteristics of the joint impart a degree of flexibility to the ankle, helping reduce wear and degeneration of the ankle joint.

Tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries are common in sports-related trauma. Studies show that the prevalence of concurrent tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries in patients with ankle joint injuries varies from 1 to 18% (3, 4). Based on the duration of the tibiofibular injuries, such injuries can be categorized into three types: acute tibiofibular injuries (less than 6 weeks), subacute tibiofibular injuries (6 weeks to 6 months), and chronic tibiofibular injuries (more than 6 months). If not treated promptly, an acute tibiofibular syndesmosis injury may progress to a chronic condition, characterized by persistent pain and impaired ankle function. Therefore, prompt detection and intervention can improve patient outcomes and quality of life, reducing the risk of subsequent complications such as ankle instability and traumatic arthritis (5). This article aims to review and evaluate the diagnostic methodologies for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries.

Anatomical structure of the tibiofibular syndesmosis

The tibiofibular syndesmosis consists of the distal tibiofibular joint and the interosseous ligament connecting the tibia and fibula. The fibular notch of the tibia and the distal portion of the fibula form the tibiofibular joint cavity, providing the osseous foundation for the syndesmosis. The stability of the syndesmosis is mainly maintained by a complex fibrous structure of four ligaments providing robust support. The tibiofibular ligament complex comprises the anterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (AITFL), posterior inferior tibiofibular ligament (PITFL), inferior transverse ligament (ITL), and interosseous ligament (IOL). These ligaments play a crucial role in stabilizing the ankle joint (6). The AITFL, situated anterior to the tibiofibular syndesmosis, is recognized as the most susceptible ligament to injury. This ligament undergoes significant tensile stress during the external rotation of the ankle joint. It typically consists of three bundles: the upper bundle is shorter, the middle bundle is the strongest, and the distal bundle is the weakest. There is a gap of approximately 2 mm between these bundles (7, 8). The PITFL, located posterior to the tibiofibular syndesmosis, primarily restricts posterior displacement of the tibia relative to the fibula. It is one of the strongest ligaments in the tibiofibular syndesmosis. It consists of multiple ligamentous bundles, with the most distal portion covering the posterolateral region of the ankle joint cavity. The ITL, located deep to the PITFL, further stabilizes the ankle joint. The ITL and PITFL can be challenging to differentiate due to their parallel orientation. The IOL arises from the thickened interosseous membrane, which has a unique grid-like structure, providing elasticity and allowing subtle separation of the malleoli during foot dorsiflexion (7, 8). Due to the intricate anatomical structure and significant interindividual variability, accurately diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries remains a challenge.

Biomechanics and injury mechanisms of the tibiofibular syndesmosis

The tibiofibular syndesmosis is a diarthrodial joint, and its fretting characteristics facilitate precise positional adjustments of the talus within the ankle mortise, thereby enabling refined ankle joint movement. Research has demonstrated that varying ankle positions result in differential stress levels on the AITFL and PITFL of the tibiofibular syndesmosis (9). External rotation of the talus is the primary mechanism responsible for injuries to the tibiofibular syndesmosis (10, 11, 12, 13). When the foot undergoes external rotation, the fibula simultaneously shifts posteriorly and externally rotates, leading to increased stress on the AITFL, thereby increasing susceptibility to injury (6). Furthermore, injuries to the tibiofibular syndesmosis are frequently associated with ankle fractures. According to the Danis–Weber classification for ankle fractures, injuries to the tibiofibular syndesmosis occur in approximately 50% of Weber-type B fractures and in all Weber-type C fractures (14). The fracture lines in Weber-type B fibula fractures are predominantly located at the level of the tibiofibular syndesmosis, indicating potential instability in this region. In contrast, the fracture lines in Weber-type C fibula fractures typically extend above the plane of the tibiofibular syndesmosis, suggesting a high likelihood of complete disruption and instability of the tibiofibular syndesmosis.

Diagnostic methods of tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries

X-ray imaging analysis

X-ray examination remains the most commonly used method for the assessment and diagnosis of tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. Standard X-ray protocols typically include anteroposterior and ankle mortise views, with stress-position imaging performed as necessary. Several studies have evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of X-ray imaging for both anteroposterior and mortise views. The results indicated that the sensitivity and specificity of anteroposterior X-ray images were 44.1 and 100%, respectively, while those for mortise views were 58.3 and 100%, respectively (15). This suggests that X-ray examinations exhibit a relatively high false-negative rate, with the sensitivity of mortise views being notably higher than that of anteroposterior views. Additionally, several studies have indicated that the high false-positive and false-negative rates of X-ray diagnosis for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries can be attributed to factors such as varying radiographic angles, individual anatomical differences, and measurement errors. Consequently, the reliability of diagnosing such injuries, particularly in cases of isolated tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, is notably limited (16). Based on the aforementioned perspectives, we conclude that X-ray imaging can serve as an initial diagnostic tool for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. A positive radiographic finding may indicate the presence of such an injury. However, a negative result does not rule out the possibility of injury; therefore, a more definitive diagnosis should be made through clinical evaluation and additional diagnostic modalities.

Due to the frequent association between tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries and ankle fractures, X-ray imaging is commonly used to evaluate tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries by assessing the ankle joint space and characterizing fracture patterns. Harper and Keller established the standard X-ray examination standard (17), which involves measuring the tibiofibular clear space (TFCS) and tibiofibular overlap (TFO) 10 mm above the ankle joint. Additionally, isometric parameters including TFO, medial clear space (MCS), and superior clear space (SCS) were used to evaluate the integrity of the distal tibiofibular syndesmosis, as shown in Fig. 1. The study demonstrated that in a normal syndesmosis, the TFCS at both the anteroposterior and mortise views was less than 6 mm; the anteroposterior TFO exceeded 6 mm, or 42% of the fibula width, and the TFO at mortise views was greater than 1 mm (18). Based on this, several scholars have suggested that the criteria for X-ray diagnosis of tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries could include a TFCS greater than 6 mm, TFO less than 0 mm, MCS/SCS exceeding 1 mm, or MCS surpassing 4 mm (19). However, it has been observed that MCS exhibits relatively lower reliability compared to TFCS and TFO and shows less consistency when comparing the bilateral ankles of patients. Due to the influence of the X-ray angle and subtle rotation of the ankle joint on the measurement accuracy of MCS, as well as the impact of the curvature of the medial border of the talus on the reliability and reproducibility of MCS, the sole reliance on the MCS parameter for diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries may be significantly limited (20).

Several studies suggest that relying solely on the threshold values of these parameters for diagnosis may not be sufficiently accurate. It is recommended that greater emphasis should be placed on comparing and analyzing bilateral ankle parameters in patients to achieve a more precise diagnosis. However, given that the aforementioned parameters are subject to variation due to the rotation angle of the patient’s ankle joint, and considering that X-ray images of the ankle joint are predominantly obtained from a single perspective, different rotation angles of the patient’s ankle joint, along with other factors, may influence the comparative analysis of these parameters between the two ankles. To more accurately assess bilateral ankle parameters, the hindfoot line of force view has been used to evaluate injuries of the tibiofibular syndesmosis. The study results demonstrated that, compared to the anteroposterior view, the posterior foot force line view exhibited a smaller measurement error when assessing the parameters of bilateral ankle joints. This approach effectively mitigates the aforementioned limitations. However, this method is not suitable for acute trauma patients who are unable to undergo weight-bearing X-ray examinations. Consequently, this technique may hold greater clinical significance in diagnosing subacute and chronic injuries of the tibiofibular syndesmosis (20).

Computed tomography (CT) imaging

Routine CT scan

CT provides a more precise visualization of the tibiofibular syndesmosis compared to X-ray imaging. Research has confirmed that CT scans exhibit higher sensitivity in detecting syndesmotic injuries. Specifically, CT can accurately identify syndesmotic separations of 2 mm or greater, whereas X-rays can detect only about half of separations measuring 3 mm or more (21).

Currently, CT imaging is primarily used to assess tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries by quantifying changes in TFO and TFCS. Several studies suggest that changes in TFCS are more reliable for diagnosis compared to changes in TFO, and axial CT imaging provides superior evaluation of ligaments compared to X-ray films. Consequently, greater clinical emphasis should be placed on monitoring changes in TFCS. Due to the complexity and variability of measurement methods for various parameters, both domestically and internationally, different methodologies can result in significant discrepancies. Therefore, the CT diagnostic criteria for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries remain inconsistent. The most commonly used measurement methods for the inter-tibiofibular gap include assessing the anterior, medial, and posterior tibiofibular gap. Specifically, these measurements are taken from the distance between the anterior and posterior tibiofibular tubercles, as well as the midpoint of the line connecting them, to the nearest points on the anterior end, posterior end, and midpoint of the medial border of the fibula, respectively. The median gap is considered the inter-tibiofibular gap value. The normal threshold for inter-tibiofibular gap is 4 mm. Additionally, the difference between the anterior and posterior tibiofibular gap should not exceed 2 mm, as shown in Fig. 2A. The posterior tibial tubercle was used as the reference point for constructing a curved tangent line along the latter half of the tibiofibular notch, extending upwards. Subsequently, the medial and lateral tangent lines of the fibula were drawn parallel to the tibial tangent line. The distance between these two lines was measured as the TFCS. A diagnosis of tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries can be made when TFCS exceeded 6 mm or when it was greater than 2 mm on the contralateral side (22), as shown in Fig. 2B. The distance between these two lines is defined as the TFO (23), as shown in Fig. 2C. Furthermore, 3D reconstruction CT technology not only facilitates the diagnosis of subtle injuries to the tibiofibular syndesmosis but also enables the detection of subtle changes in the fibula through bilateral comparisons, such as displacement, rotation, and shortening. This effectively addresses the challenges posed by significant anatomical variations among individuals.

Nevertheless, given the inconsistency in the current CT diagnostic criteria, a novel anatomical concept, the tibiofibular line, has been introduced to refine and simplify the existing complex measurement methodologies (24), as shown in Fig. 3. The tibiofibular line is defined as a straight line that is tangential to the flat anterolateral surface of the fibula on a CT scan, located 1 cm proximal to the ankle joint. Existing studies have assessed the reliability and accuracy of the tibiofibular line, indicating that it may be more suitable for patients with an anterolateral fibular cortex contact area ranging from 6 to 10 mm. The use of the tibiofibular line parameter in evaluating patients within this range has demonstrated high consistency. However, clinical data revealed that the fibula morphology of approximately 61% of patients conformed to the aforementioned criteria (24). Therefore, it can be reasonably inferred that using the tibiofibular line as a parameter for evaluating tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries has a certain degree of reliability. Based on the anatomical characteristics of Chinese individuals, domestic studies suggest that it is considered normal when the shortest linear distance between the tibiofibular line and the lateral tibial border is less than 4 mm. Conversely, if this distance exceeds 4 mm, it may indicate a potential injury to the tibiofibular syndesmosis (24). Based on the aforementioned criteria, clinical studies have been undertaken to identify patients suspected of sustaining a tibiofibular syndesmosis injury. For those who met the specified criteria, internal fixation treatment was provided, yielding satisfactory outcomes (24). Compared to previous parameters, the tibiofibular line is relatively simple to measure due to its fixed position, which helps minimize measurement errors. However, current clinical data on the tibiofibular line are limited, making it challenging to assess its long-term effects and diagnostic value, which require further evaluation.

Weight-bearing computed tomography (WBCT)

Conventional CT is unable to objectively depict the actual condition of the foot under load-bearing stress. In contrast, load-bearing CT can evaluate the changes in the tibiofibular syndesmosis under physiological loading conditions, thereby enabling functional evaluation. Within WBCT, two-dimensional area measurements and three-dimensional volume assessments are used to evaluate injuries to the tibiofibular syndesmosis. In patients with tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries and ankle instability, the cross-sectional area of the tibiofibular syndesmosis was observed to increase. This increase appears to correlate with the severity and frequency of syndesmotic injuries (25). Based on this, we propose a reference value for the transverse area of the tibiofibular syndesmosis: when measured 1 cm proximal to the tibial plafond, the mean transverse area is 106.7 ± 18.3 mm2 (26). Since tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries are inherently three-dimensional in nature, three-dimensional volumetric measurements may provide a more comprehensive evaluation of such injuries. Therefore, it is recommended that a volumetric map of TFCS extending 5 cm proximally be used as the preferred method for diagnosing instability (27). Based on WBCT findings, it has been suggested that ankle instability resulting from tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries can be further diagnosed through the application of increased physiological stress. This approach aims to identify occult chronic tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries (28).

Four-dimensional computed tomography (4D-CT)

4D-CT, also known as dynamic CT, can accurately capture the position of the syndesmosis during joint movement through real-time imaging. As this examination method achieves registration accuracy at the subvoxel level and automatically measures relevant parameters, it ensures both precision and reproducibility of results. This technique overcomes the limitation of non-standardized ankle joint positioning in existing imaging examinations, thus preventing adverse events such as inadequate reduction and diagnostic errors resulting from improper joint positioning (29).

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

MRI is considered the most reliable imaging modality for diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. Research has shown that MRI achieves 100% sensitivity and 94% specificity in diagnosing injuries to the AITFL, as well as 100% sensitivity for injuries to the PITFL (30). A healthy tibiofibular syndesmosis shows low signal intensity on MRI. In the case of ligament injury, increased signal intensity is observed on T2-weighted images. If the ligament is ruptured, imaging reveals a localized disruption in continuity. Axial, coronal, and sagittal MRI scans are routinely performed. Coronal views typically display the characteristic ‘lambda sign’ (31), while axial images can accurately identify the specific ligaments involved in injuries of the tibiofibular syndesmosis. Considering the anatomical features of the AITFL, it may display an incomplete axial orientation, potentially leading to false-positive results (32). Several studies have shown that the AITFL can be fully visualized on coronal images when the angle between the oblique imaging plane and the horizontal line of the tibial-talar articular plane is within the range of 35°–55°. Therefore, an angle of 45° is recommended as the optimal setting for the oblique imaging plane (32). MRI currently shows superior sensitivity and specificity among imaging modalities. However, due to the extended processing time for MRI images, this may increase patients’ discomfort during acute injuries of the tibiofibular syndesmosis. Additionally, the diagnostic reliability of MRI tends to decrease over time. Therefore, guidelines recommend early MRI examination to improve diagnostic accuracy for tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries (18).

Ultrasound examination

Currently, numerous studies have highlighted the diagnostic utility of ultrasonography in acute ankle ligament injuries (33). Due to its cost-effectiveness, lack of ionizing radiation, and capability to detect subtle changes in soft tissue injuries, ultrasonography holds significant clinical value in diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. Studies have quantified TFCS under both unstressed and externally rotated conditions to assess whether patients have tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. A critical threshold of 4 mm for TFCS has been established in these studies. The findings indicate that in acute tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, the widening of TFCS can be evaluated by comparing bilateral ankle joints in an unstressed condition. For chronic injuries, applying external rotational stress improves diagnostic sensitivity (34). Simultaneously, several studies have assessed the intraoperative stability of the tibiofibular syndesmosis using standardized ultrasonic external rotation stress test parameters. These studies show that a significant widening of TFCS can be observed when a torque of 4.5 N·m is applied, and this torque value is more tolerable for patients (35). Furthermore, dynamic ultrasound examination can assess the displacement of the tibiofibular syndesmosis in the sagittal plane by simultaneously comparing both sides of the patient’s joint, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and aiding in the diagnosis of ankle instability (36). Additionally, this technology compensates for significant anatomical variations between individuals by bilateral ankle joint comparisons. Injuries to the tibiofibular syndesmosis can manifest as coronal displacement, sagittal displacement, and internal/external rotation of the fibula. Therefore, focusing solely on sagittal displacement is insufficient. Some studies have also proposed using portable handheld ultrasound to assess internal and external rotation of the fibula in tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, introducing the concept of gap penetrance. The findings indicate that posterior gap penetrance demonstrates high accuracy and specificity in evaluating rotational defects (37), effectively complementing previous research.

Arthroscopic examination

While the aforementioned imaging techniques provide a more reliable means of diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, they still have certain limitations in identifying unstable injury types. Arthroscopy, however, allows for direct visualization of changes in the tibiofibular syndesmosis and provides the most accurate diagnosis, serving as the gold standard for diagnosing such injuries. The tibiofibular syndesmosis instability is a multifactorial condition characterized by coronal tibiofibular separation, sagittal fibular translation, and fibular rotation. Existing literature has linked the tibiofibular syndesmosis instability to fibular displacement; however, the definition of a critical threshold remains contentious, with a particular focus on distinguishing between 2 and 3 mm as potential critical values. According to the APKASS Consensus Statement on Chronic Syndesmosis Injury, an unstable injury can be diagnosed if there is a separation of the tibiofibular syndesmosis exceeding 3 mm when probed under imaging (38, 39). Furthermore, several studies have proposed a novel diagnostic criterion: the separation of the tibiofibular syndesmosis may be considered present when it exceeds 2 mm (40). Based on the aforementioned criteria, a comprehensive meta-analysis evaluating both criteria has been conducted (41). The results demonstrated that the displacement threshold for the fibula was 2.9 mm in the anterior aspect of the tibiofibular syndesmosis and 3.4 mm in the posterior aspect, suggesting that a critical value of 2 mm in the coronal plane may lead to overdiagnosis. A threshold of 3 mm may be more appropriate in the coronal plane. Furthermore, changes in the sagittal plane were found to be more pronounced than those in the coronal plane; however, insufficient clinical studies exist to establish a definitive critical value for the tibiofibular syndesmosis in the sagittal plane, requiring further research in this area (41). Additionally, the study indicated that arthroscopy may not be the primary method for assessing external rotation of the fibula (41).

Kinematic analysis verification

Despite the high diagnostic efficacy of arthroscopy, it remains an invasive procedure that imposes a physiological burden on patients. A novel stability test has been proposed for diagnosing chronic tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. This method involves applying an inelastic band around the tibiofibular syndesmosis to alleviate pain and enhance postural control (42). Based on this experiment, subsequent studies have enhanced the stability test by securing a pneumatic brace around the tibiofibular syndesmosis and applying a pressure of 60 kPa while the patient is in an upright position (43). The findings indicate that, compared to imaging techniques, the modified stability test exhibits superior diagnostic value, with results corroborated by arthroscopy findings. Additionally, the reliability and reproducibility of the improved stability test surpass those of the traditional method, thereby enhancing the objectivity and accuracy of the diagnostic outcomes. However, given the limited sample size in this study, further research is warranted to comprehensively evaluate the clinical diagnostic efficacy of this method.

Conclusion

The diagnosis of tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries is typically confirmed through clinical evaluation and imaging studies. Radiographic examination serves as the foundational diagnostic tool, offering distinct advantages in cases involving concomitant tibiofibular fractures. However, for minor or isolated ligamentous injuries, the sensitivity and specificity of X-ray imaging are limited, necessitating further evaluation with CT. Currently, CT imaging has significantly advanced in detecting minor injuries of the tibiofibular syndesmosis. WBCT enhances diagnostic accuracy at the functional level by capturing changes in the tibiofibular syndesmosis under physiological load. Moreover, 4D-CT technology now enables dynamic comparison of bilateral tibiofibular syndesmosis changes, further improving diagnostic accuracy. However, CT’s resolution for soft tissues and ligaments remains limited, which restricts its ability to accurately diagnose simple tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. MRI is currently recognized as the most sensitive and specific imaging modality, capable of accurately diagnosing ligament injuries and effectively addressing the limitations of CT scans. It plays a crucial role in diagnosing chronic tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. However, given the longer duration and higher costs associated with MRI, ultrasound may be considered as an alternative in emergencies. Ultrasonography is a valuable tool for comparing changes in the bilateral tibiofibular syndesmosis under both unstressed and stressed conditions, demonstrating significant diagnostic utility in chronic tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries. While these tests are effective in diagnosing the majority of such injuries, they lack sufficient reliability to accurately differentiate between stable and unstable injuries. As the gold standard for diagnosing tibiofibular syndesmosis injuries, arthroscopy not only enables direct visualization of the changes in the tibiofibular syndesmosis but also facilitates the assessment of potential instability. However, the diagnostic criteria used in arthroscopy remain subjects of debate and require further investigation and discussion. Considering that arthroscopy is an invasive procedure, it is recommended for patients with a high preoperative suspicion of tibiofibular syndesmosis instability. However, arthroscopy is limited in its ability to provide dynamic comparative analysis of bilateral ankle joints, which may complicate diagnosis due to significant individual anatomical variations. Therefore, we propose integrating arthroscopy with weight-bearing CT or ultrasound, among other modalities. This approach would not only facilitate comparative anatomical assessment of both ankles but also enable dynamic functional evaluation. Combining anatomical and functional analyses can better meet clinical requirements. The feasibility and efficacy of this integrated method warrant further investigation and evaluation through clinical studies. Based on the principles of noninvasiveness and non-radiation, the modified stability test appears to be more aligned with current clinical diagnostic requirements. Its reliability and accuracy surpass those of traditional imaging techniques, and its findings can be corroborated with arthroscopic results. Given the limited number of existing clinical studies on this method, further evaluation of its clinical value is warranted in future research.
