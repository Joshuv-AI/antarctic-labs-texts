A bioprinted periosteum organoid enables functional repair of critical-sized bone defects
Authors: Zhengrong Chen, Kui Huang, Ziyang Zhang, Peng Chen, Qixiu Yu, Yong Tang, Jiulin Tan, Jie Zhang, Ying Qu, Qijie Dai, Shiwu Dong, Fei Luo, Ce Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe periosteum is an essential yet often neglected regulator of bone regeneration, and its loss represents a major barrier to healing critical-sized bone defects. Existing periosteum-mimicking strategies rely primarily on static architectures or exogenous factor delivery and fail to reconstruct a living periosteal interface. Here, we introduce an organoid-based approach for engineering functional periosteum organoids capable of restoring periosteal identity and regulatory function. A bioprinted GelMA/SilMA interpenetrating composite hydrogel integrated with black phosphorus nanosheets is developed to create a mechanically stable and bioactive microenvironment that supports osteogenic–angiogenic self-organization. Under three-dimensional culture, the constructs exhibit the potential to form periosteum organoids, with coordinated osteogenic differentiation and pro-angiogenic niche formation. Following in vivo conditioning, the constructs develop into periosteum organoids that exhibit dense collagen-rich fibrous architecture, pronounced periostin expression, enrichment of osteogenic lineage cells, and stabilized microvasculature, closely recapitulating the structural and functional hallmarks of native periosteum. When applied to a critical-sized rat calvarial defect model, the periosteum organoids significantly enhance bone regeneration, improve trabecular microarchitecture, reconstruct a functional periosteal interface, and rebalance bone remodeling without detectable systemic toxicity. This work establishes periosteum organoids as a living regenerative interface for skeletal repair and provides a new organoid-based paradigm for functional periosteal reconstruction in large bone defects.Keywords: Periosteum organoids, Critical-sized bone defects, 3D bioprinting, Osteogenic–angiogenic coupling

Introduction

Critical-sized bone defects represent one of the most refractory problems in orthopaedic surgery, frequently resulting from severe trauma, tumor resection, or infection and often progressing to non-union despite repeated interventions [1]. Although current clinical strategies primarily emphasize the optimization of bone grafts and synthetic substitutes, these approaches have achieved limited success in large defects, indicating that restoration of mechanical continuity alone is insufficient [2]. Substantial evidence demonstrates that the periosteum, a highly active fibrous connective tissue enriched with progenitor cells, collagenous matrix, and a dense microvascular network, serves as the principal cellular and signaling source for early callus formation and as a key driver of coupled osteogenesis–angiogenesis during bone regeneration [3,4]. In pathological conditions, however, the periosteum is frequently destroyed or surgically removed, and its complex regulatory interface—integrating progenitor recruitment, paracrine signaling, and mechanosensory functions—is rarely reconstructed even after graft implantation. Consequently, the regenerative microenvironment is profoundly compromised, leading to impaired cell homing, insufficient vascularization, dysregulated remodeling, and ultimately the high failure rate observed in critical-sized bone defect repair [[5], [6], [7]]. These observations suggest that a central challenge in the treatment of critical-sized bone defects is not only how to provide structural support, but also how to reconstruct a periosteum-like living interface capable of participating in and regulating the regenerative process.

The periosteum is not a passive membrane but a highly specialized, organ-level regulatory system essential for skeletal development and repair [8]. Structurally, it comprises an outer fibrous layer that provides mechanical integrity and an inner cambium layer enriched with skeletal stem/progenitor cells, osteoprogenitors, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and perivascular populations [9,10]. This unique cellular architecture endows the periosteum with the capacity to serve as a primary reservoir for bone-forming cells while simultaneously acting as a source of angiogenic, osteogenic, and immunomodulatory signals [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. During bone repair, periosteal progenitors are rapidly activated and constitute the dominant cellular origin of early callus, whereas the intrinsic periosteal vasculature drives neovascular ingrowth into the defect, thereby coordinating the tightly coupled processes of osteogenesis and angiogenesis [[15], [16], [17]]. In addition, the periosteum functions as a mechanosensory interface, translating mechanical cues into biochemical signals that regulate local remodeling dynamics [18,19]. Clinically, preservation of periosteal integrity is strongly associated with superior healing outcomes, whereas periosteal stripping or destruction markedly increases the risk of delayed union and non-union, underscoring its indispensable role as a dynamic regulator rather than a structural bystander in bone regeneration.

Driven by the pivotal role of the periosteum in skeletal regeneration, a variety of periosteum-mimicking strategies have been developed, including decellularized periosteal extracellular matrix scaffolds, osteogenic or mesenchymal stem cell–based cell-sheet engineering, bilayer constructs integrating osteogenic and endothelial components, and microstructured membranes loaded with growth factors such as BMP-2 or VEGF [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. These approaches have undoubtedly improved cell adhesion, early osteogenesis, and partial vascularization in critical-sized defects. Nevertheless, they remain fundamentally limited by their static design logic: periosteum is treated as a predefined structural template or a depot for exogenous signals rather than as a living regulatory system. Such constructs largely lack the capacity for cellular self-organization, dynamic remodeling, and endogenous signal integration that characterize native periosteum, and therefore fail to reconstruct the highly coupled, adaptive periosteal interface that governs progenitor recruitment, vascular maturation, and spatiotemporal coordination of bone regeneration [26]. This conceptual gap highlights the necessity for a paradigm shift—from fabricating periosteum-like materials to engineering a periosteum-like living system capable of autonomous organization and functional maturation [27,28]. In this context, three-dimensional bioprinting represents an attractive strategy because it enables spatially controllable architecture, high shape fidelity, and integration of cells and matrix within a single construct [29]. However, printing itself mainly defines the initial structural framework, whereas the acquisition of periosteum-like function still depends on subsequent cell-matrix interaction, tissue remodeling, and microenvironmental maturation. Therefore, for periosteum reconstruction, the key challenge is not merely to produce a membrane with a desired shape, but to establish a bioactive printed system that can support the progressive formation of a functional periosteum-like living interface. In this sense, the present strategy is not intended to recapitulate a developmental organoid in the strict classical sense, but to provide a bioactive printed framework for progressive periosteum-like organization under regenerative conditions.

To address this unmet need, we developed a bioprinted periosteum organoid-like construct based on a gelatin methacrylate (GelMA)/silk fibroin methacrylate (SilMA) interpenetrating composite hydrogel integrated with black phosphorus (BP) nanosheets, designed to provide a permissive microenvironment for osteogenic–angiogenic self-organization. In this design, black phosphorus nanosheets were incorporated as a bioactive inorganic component to compensate for the limited intrinsic bioactivity of the polymeric hydrogel matrix and to provide additional osteogenic and pro-angiogenic cues. Rather than simply fabricating a periosteum-shaped scaffold, this strategy was intended to support the progressive formation of a periosteum-like living interface with regenerative activity through a bioactive bioprinted matrix. We demonstrate that this construct autonomously matures into a periosteum-like tissue with dense collagen architecture, enriched osteogenic lineage commitment, and functional microvasculature, and markedly enhances the repair of critical-sized cranial defects in vivo, establishing an organoid-based paradigm for functional periosteal regeneration (Scheme 1).

Results

Fabrication and physicochemical characterization of Gel/SilMA + BP bioinks for periosteum‐mimicking bioprinting

To construct a periosteum‐mimicking bioink with both biological activity and structural robustness, gelatin and silk fibroin were first methacrylated to obtain photo-crosslinkable GelMA and SilMA, respectively (Fig. 1a–e). Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra confirmed the successful introduction of methacrylate groups into both polymers, as evidenced by the characteristic vinyl proton peaks at δ 5.3–5.6 ppm and methyl peaks at δ ∼1.8–1.9 ppm, accompanied by attenuation of lysine‐related signals in GelMA [30] (Fig. 1b–f). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra further verified the preservation of the major amide bands and the successful functionalization without disrupting the secondary structure of silk fibroin [31] (Fig. 1c–g). Upon ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, both GelMA and SilMA bioinks rapidly formed stable hydrogels, enabling subsequent three-dimensional printing (Fig. 1d–h). GelMA and SilMA were then blended to integrate the high cell adhesiveness of GelMA with the superior viscoelasticity of SilMA [32], and black phosphorus nanosheets were incorporated to yield Gel/SilMA + BP composite bioinks. The composite bioink could be printed into thin membrane-like constructs with high fidelity (Fig. 1i, Figs. S1–S3, and Movie S1). Notably, the Gel/SilMA + BP membranes exhibited strong adhesion to diverse substrates, including plastic, wood, glass, metal, and biological tissues (Fig. 1j), a property that may facilitate close contact with the defect surface and improve handling and local retention during implantation [33]. In addition, supplementary video evidence showed that the hydrogel remained attached to the bottom of a culture dish under wet conditions (Movie S2), supporting its adhesive behavior in a hydrated environment.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that, compared with GelMA alone, Gel/SilMA and Gel/SilMA + BP scaffolds possessed a more refined and interconnected porous architecture with coexisting micro- and macro-pores (Fig. 1k and Fig. S4), providing a biomimetic three-dimensional framework for cell infiltration and nutrient diffusion [34]. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that the 3D-printed GelMA, Gel/SilMA, and Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels exhibited similar porosity, with no significant differences among the three groups (Fig. S5). Although X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization of Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels did not show a distinct characteristic peak of BP, likely due to the relatively low BP content in the composite hydrogel (Fig. S6), the successful incorporation of BP was supported by complementary analyses. The energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) spectrum revealed a detectable phosphorus signal, and elemental mapping further confirmed the presence of BP-associated elements in the hydrogel matrix (Figure S7 and Fig. 1l). Moreover, Cy5.5 fluorescence imaging showed the distribution of labeled BP in Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels, and the merged images further verified the presence and distribution of BP throughout the hydrogel network (Fig. S8). Mechanical testing demonstrated that the introduction of SilMA markedly enhanced the tensile performance of the hydrogels. Both Gel/SilMA and Gel/SilMA + BP exhibited significantly higher tensile strength, Young's modulus, and elongation at break than GelMA alone (Fig. 1m–o and Fig. S9), endowing the constructs with improved strain tolerance and structural stability suitable for periosteal applications [35]. Swelling and degradation assays further showed that GelMA degraded more rapidly and displayed a higher equilibrium swelling ratio, whereas Gel/SilMA and Gel/SilMA + BP maintained prolonged structural integrity over 28 days (Fig. 1p and q), consistent with the formation of a reinforced interpenetrating composite architecture [36]. Collectively, these results demonstrate that Gel/SilMA + BP bioinks possess the essential physicochemical properties required for stable bioprinting and provide a robust foundation for the subsequent construction of functional periosteum organoids.

Gel/SilMA + BP bioinks simultaneously promote osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and angiogenic activity of HUVECs in vitro

To evaluate the cytocompatibility and biological activity of the printed hydrogels, their effects on osteogenic differentiation and angiogenic activity were first assessed under two-dimensional conditions. In these two-dimensional experiments, the hydrogels were used as acellular material treatments rather than cell-laden constructs. Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were first co-cultured with GelMA, Gel/SilMA, and Gel/SilMA + BP under two-dimensional conditions (Fig. 2a). CCK-8 assays demonstrated that all three hydrogels supported sustained cell viability over 1, 3, and 7 days, with no significant differences compared with control cultures (Fig. 2b), indicating excellent cytocompatibility. We next examined the osteogenic potential of the materials. After 7 days of culture, ALP staining and quantitative analysis revealed that GelMA and Gel/SilMA elicited only modest effects, whereas Gel/SilMA + BP significantly enhanced ALP activity (Fig. 2c and d). Consistently, alizarin red staining after 14 days showed markedly increased mineralized nodule formation in the Gel/SilMA + BP group (Fig. 2e and f). Gene expression analyses further substantiated these findings. At day 7, BMSCs co-cultured with Gel/SilMA + BP already exhibited significantly higher mRNA expression levels of Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2), osterix (Osx), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), collagen type I alpha 1 (Col1a1), and osteocalcin (OCN) than those in the GelMA and Gel/SilMA groups (Fig. S10). At day 14, the osteogenesis-related markers Runx2, Osx, ALP, Col1a1, and OCN remained significantly upregulated in the Gel/SilMA + BP group compared with the control groups (Fig. 2g–k), confirming that incorporation of BP endows the hydrogel matrix with a potent osteoinductive microenvironment [37,38].

Given the essential role of periosteal vasculature in bone regeneration, we next assessed the angiogenic performance of the hydrogels using human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (Fig. 2l). Similar to BMSCs, HUVECs maintained normal proliferation on all hydrogels (Fig. 2m). However, scratch wound assays revealed that Gel/SilMA + BP markedly accelerated endothelial migration compared with GelMA and Gel/SilMA (Fig. 2n and o), suggesting enhanced angiogenic activation. Tube formation assays further demonstrated that Gel/SilMA + BP induced the formation of more extensive and continuous capillary-like networks (Fig. 2p), with significantly increased mesh numbers and total tube length (Fig. 2q and r). In parallel, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression was substantially elevated in HUVECs cultured on Gel/SilMA + BP (Fig. 2s), indicating reinforcement of pro-angiogenic signaling. These results demonstrate that Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels exert dual biological functions, simultaneously promoting osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and angiogenic activity of HUVECs in two-dimensional culture. This coupled enhancement of osteogenesis and angiogenesis provides a critical biological basis for the subsequent formation of constructs with the potential to develop into functional periosteum organoids [39].

Transcriptomic profiling reveals coordinated multicellular programs underlying Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels mediated regeneration

To elucidate and validate the osteogenic and angiogenic capacities of the material, transcriptomic profiling was performed after Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels were separately co-cultured with BMSCs and HUVECs for 7 days. Transcriptomic analysis of the BMSC compartment revealed 1086 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), comprising 689 upregulated and 497 downregulated transcripts relative to the control group (Fig. 3a and Fig. S11). The upregulated gene subset encompassed classical osteogenic markers (SPP1, IBSP, PHOSPHO1, etc.), as well as key regulators mediating matrix–cell communication and bone–vascular coupling (WNT5A, ITGA5, DKK1, etc.). Western blot analysis confirmed the upregulated expression of key osteogenic proteins, including RUNX2 and IBSP (Fig. S12). Gene Ontology Biological Process (GO-BP) enrichment analysis demonstrated that these upregulated genes were predominantly associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) organization, ossification, bone mineralization, skeletal system development, angiogenesis, wound response, and tissue regeneration (Fig. 3b). Furthermore, GO Cellular Component and Molecular Function assessments indicated a preferential localization to the ECM and plasma-membrane-associated complexes, concurrent with significant enrichment in transmembrane receptor activity, G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling, and cell-adhesion molecule binding. KEGG pathway profiling revealed a pronounced enrichment of the PI3K–Akt signaling cascade and ECM–receptor interaction/integrin signaling pathways (Fig. 3c). Western blot validation demonstrated enhanced phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, verifying the activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling axis (Fig. S12). GO-BP chord diagrams delineated specific gene clusters bridging ossification and angiogenesis, notably including SPP1, ITGA5, WNT5A, etc. (Fig. 3d). Consistently, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) confirmed the significant positive enrichment of ECM–receptor interaction and focal adhesion pathways (Fig. 3e and f).Parallel evaluation of the HUVEC transcriptome identified 305 DEGs following Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels treatment, consisting of 155 upregulated and 150 downregulated genes (Fig. 3g and Fig. S13). The upregulated genes included endothelial activation and chemotactic mediators (ICAM1, VCAM1, CXCL1, etc.), alongside angiogenesis-related transcription factors ID1 and ID3. Western blot validation confirmed the augmented protein-level expression of critical endothelial and angiogenic markers, namely VCAM-1 and VEGFR2 (Fig. S14). GO-BP enrichment revealed significant associations with blood vessel morphogenesis, angiogenesis, chemotaxis, and the regulation of cell–cell adhesion (Fig. 3h). KEGG analysis highlighted the enrichment of cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction and chemokine signaling pathways (Fig. 3i). Chord plots and GSEA further verified the positive enrichment of endothelial cell migration, angiogenesis involved in wound healing, and endothelial development programs (Fig. 3j–l). In summary, Gel/SilMA + BP enhanced osteogenesis-related transcriptional programs in BMSCs and upregulated angiogenesis-related expression patterns in HUVECs. These findings suggest that the material possesses the biological potential to promote both osteogenesis and angiogenesis.

Structural stability, cellular organization, and osteogenic–angiogenic features of Gel/SilMA + BP constructs in a three-dimensional culture system

To evaluate the behavior of the bioprinted constructs in a three-dimensional culture system, constructs composed of rat BMSCs and rat endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) were cultured in vitro (Fig. 4a). It should be noted that, whereas HUVECs were used in the two-dimensional angiogenesis-related assays as a standard endothelial model, rat EPCs were selected for three-dimensional bioprinting and the subsequent in vitro and in vivo studies in order to maintain species consistency with rat BMSCs and the animal models. All constructs maintained their lattice architecture during short-term culture; however, prolonged culture revealed pronounced structural instability in GelMA constructs, which exhibited evident shrinkage and partial collapse by day 21, whereas both Gel/SilMA and Gel/SilMA + BP constructs preserved regular three-dimensional geometries, indicating that incorporation of SilMA markedly enhances long-term structural stability required for sustained tissue assembly (Fig. S15–S16). Live/dead staining at days 3 and 7 demonstrated high cell viability in all groups, with more than 80% of cells remaining viable after 7 days of culture (Fig. 4b,c and Fig. S17), confirming that all hydrogels provided a permissive 3D microenvironment for cell survival. To further examine the early cellular state during short-term 3D culture, nuclear/F-actin staining was performed after 1 and 3 days of culture (Fig. S18). At day 1, cells in the GelMA group were mostly rounded with limited spreading, whereas those in the Gel/SilMA and Gel/SilMA + BP groups already exhibited more evident cytoskeletal extension. By day 3, cell spreading became more pronounced in all groups, particularly in the Gel/SilMA + BP constructs, where cells displayed more extensive elongation and interconnected F-actin structures. Consistent with these early observations, more striking differences in cytoskeletal organization were observed at day 7.

Cells within GelMA constructs largely retained a rounded morphology with limited spreading, whereas Gel/SilMA constructs supported enhanced cytoskeletal extension [40]. Notably, cells in the Gel/SilMA + BP group exhibited the most extensive spreading with pronounced bridge-like F-actin networks spanning the surrounding matrix, reflecting robust cell–matrix interactions and the establishment of an integrated three-dimensional cellular network (Fig. 4d). Osteogenic lineage commitment within the 3D constructs was further assessed. At day 7, ALP staining and quantification revealed significantly higher enzymatic activity in Gel/SilMA + BP constructs compared with the other groups (Fig. 4e–g). By day 21, ARS staining demonstrated abundant and densely distributed mineralized nodules exclusively in the Gel/SilMA + BP group, whereas GelMA and Gel/SilMA constructs exhibited only sparse calcium deposition (Fig. 4f–h). These findings indicate that BP incorporation sustains osteogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization within the 3D microenvironment.

To characterize the emerging cellular niche, Osx and VEGF-A immunofluorescence was performed at day 21 (Fig. 4i). Gel/SilMA + BP constructs exhibited markedly stronger Osx signals, indicating enrichment of osteoprogenitor populations characteristic of the cambium layer of native periosteum. In parallel, VEGF-A expression was significantly elevated, suggesting the establishment of a pro-angiogenic microenvironment. Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher expression levels of both Osx and VEGF-A in the Gel/SilMA + BP group compared with GelMA and Gel/SilMA (Fig. 4j and k). These data demonstrate that under three-dimensional culture, Gel/SilMA + BP constructs undergo coordinated cellular organization, cytoskeletal remodeling, osteogenic differentiation, and angiogenic niche formation, indicating their potential for the construction of periosteum organoids. Such organization is likely supported by the combined effects of the structural microenvironment.

In vivo maturation of periosteum organoids recapitulates key periosteal features

To assess whether the in vitro–initiated periosteum organoids could further mature under physiological remodeling, bioprinted lattices were implanted subcutaneously in nude mice and harvested at 15 and 30 days (Fig. 5a). Gross examination showed that all implants became surrounded by host tissue by day 15 (Fig. 5b); notably, the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids exhibited more uniform tissue coverage, suggesting faster host integration and early remodeling. By day 30, GelMA implants displayed substantial degradation and loss of shape, whereas Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids largely retained their geometry and presented conspicuous surface microvessel-like structures, consistent with ongoing vascular infiltration and stabilization. SEM analysis at day 30 revealed extracellular matrix deposition on all explants, but the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids displayed thicker, more continuous, and highly interconnected fibrous bundles compared with the sparse, disorganized fibrillar networks seen in GelMA and Gel/SilMA groups (Fig. 5c). Importantly, multiple cell morphologies (discoid, rounded, and ovoid) reminded in the Gel/SilMA + BP group (Fig. 5d), supporting stable colonization by heterogeneous host cells—an essential prerequisite for organoid-like tissue organization. Histologically, H&E and Masson staining showed progressive tissue ingrowth and matrix replacement over time (Figure S19 and Fig. 5e); by day 30, the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids exhibited a dense, continuous collagen fiber network, whereas collagen deposition remained sparse and discontinuous in the other groups. This transition toward a compact fibrous architecture is consistent with the structural hallmark of the native periosteal fibrous layer [41]. To further characterize extracellular matrix features after 30 days of in vivo incubation, immunofluorescence staining of Collagen I, Sostdc1, and Collagen III was performed (Fig. S20a). The Gel/SilMA + BP constructs exhibited stronger Collagen I and Sostdc1 staining but lower Collagen III staining than GelMA and Gel/SilMA groups, and quantitative analysis confirmed the same trend (Fig. S20b–d), providing additional evidence of periosteum-related matrix remodeling.

To verify periosteal identity, periostin immunofluorescence was examined. Periostin signals were detectable across groups but were strongest and most continuous in the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids at both day 15 and day 30, with quantitative analysis confirming a significant increase relative to GelMA and Gel/SilMA (Fig. 5f and g). To probe the emergence of a cambium-like osteogenic compartment, osteocalcin (OCN) staining demonstrated significantly higher osteogenic lineage enrichment in Gel/SilMA + BP organoids than in the other groups at both time points (Fig. 5h and i). Finally, vascular maturation—a defining periosteal feature—was evaluated by CD31/α-SMA dual staining. While endothelial signals were present in all groups, Gel/SilMA + BP organoids showed more continuous CD31-positive luminal structures, and by day 30, α-SMA–positive perivascular coverage surrounding CD31-positive tubes became evident, indicating vascular stabilization; fluorescence quantification supported higher CD31 (and corresponding vascular maturation) in this group (Fig. 5j,k and Fig. S21). Together, these findings demonstrate that Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids undergo robust in vivo maturation, developing dense collagen-rich fibrous architecture, strong periostin expression, enrichment of osteogenic lineage cells, and a stabilized microvascular network, thereby acquiring some structural and functional attributes of native periosteum. Such organization is likely supported by the combined effects of the structural microenvironment, BP-associated bioactivity, and cell-mediated interactions within the constructs.

Periosteum organoids restore periosteal interface and accelerate functional bone regeneration in critical-sized calvarial defects

To determine whether the engineered periosteum organoids could functionally restore the periosteal microenvironment and promote bone regeneration, a 5-mm critical-sized rat calvarial defect model was established (Fig. 6a). Micro-CT imaging at 4 and 8 weeks showed that untreated defects remained largely empty, confirming the negligible self-healing capacity of critical-sized cranial defects [42] (Fig. 6b). In contrast, all organoid-treated groups exhibited new bone formation along the defect margins, with the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids producing the most extensive bone ingrowth, particularly at 8 weeks. Quantitative micro-CT analysis demonstrated that the Gel/SilMA + BP group consistently achieved the highest bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) values among all groups at both time points (Fig. 6c and d). In addition, trabecular number (Tb.N) and trabecular thickness(Tb.Th) were also increased in the Gel/SilMA + BP group, particularly at 8 weeks (Fig. 6e and f), and this trend was accompanied by reduced trabecular separation (Tb.Sp) (Fig. S22). These findings indicate that periosteum organoid treatment enhanced bone formation and improved trabecular microarchitecture, supporting accelerated structural maturation of regenerated bone.

Histological analyses further confirmed the superior regenerative performance of periosteum organoids. At 4 weeks, H&E and Masson's trichrome staining revealed that control defects were filled mainly with fibrous tissue, while GelMA- and Gel/SilMA-treated defects showed sparse collagen deposition and limited woven bone formation (Fig. 6g and h). In contrast, defects treated with Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids already exhibited distinct woven bone and continuous collagen-rich matrix. By 8 weeks, this group displayed dense, well-organized collagen fibers with pronounced bridging of new bone from the defect margins toward the center, leaving only a small residual defect. Additional immunohistochemical staining of Collagen I at 8 weeks further showed stronger collagenous matrix deposition in the Gel/SilMA + BP group than in the other groups, with quantitative analysis confirming significantly higher Collagen I intensity in the defect area (Fig. S23).

To examine whether periosteal identity was restored at the bone–tissue interface, periostin immunofluorescence was performed. In control defects, periostin signals were weak and discontinuous, whereas GelMA and Gel/SilMA groups exhibited partially reconstructed periosteum-like layers (Fig. 7a and b). Notably, the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoid group showed the strongest, continuous periostin expression that seamlessly integrated with the adjacent native periosteum, indicating successful reconstitution of a functional periosteal interface. OCN staining further revealed extensive osteogenic activity in the Gel/SilMA + BP group, with significantly higher fluorescence intensity distributed across both central and peripheral regions of the defect compared with all other groups (Fig. 7c and d), reflecting enhanced maturation of newly formed bone. Consistent with these findings, CD31/α-SMA dual immunostaining demonstrated a markedly higher density of vessel-like structures in this group, many of which were surrounded by α-SMA–positive perivascular cells, indicating vascular maturation and stabilization (Fig. 7e–g). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly elevated CD31 and α-SMA signals, supporting the notion that the periosteum organoids act as a localized angiogenic regulatory niche. To further evaluate the early host response, inflammatory markers in rat blood were measured at 7 days postoperatively. No significant differences were observed in IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, or CRP among the groups (Fig. S24), suggesting that no obvious systemic inflammatory response was induced under the present experimental conditions. In parallel, immunofluorescence staining of iNOS and CD206 was performed in the defect area at day 7 (Fig. S25). Compared with the other groups, the Gel/SilMA + BP group exhibited lower iNOS expression and markedly higher CD206 expression, suggesting a local immune microenvironment more favorable for tissue repair. Finally, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining revealed a significantly reduced osteoclast-positive area in the Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoid group compared with controls and other treatments (Fig. 7h and i). Additional TRAP staining under different material conditions showed no significant difference in osteoclast area among the Control, GelMA, Gel/SilMA, and Gel/SilMA + BP groups (Fig. S26), suggesting that the reduced osteoclast activity observed in vivo is less likely to result from a direct inhibitory effect of the material system itself, and may instead be associated with the overall reconstructed regenerative microenvironment. At 7 days postoperatively, quantitative analysis of phosphorus (P) levels in the liver and kidney showed no significant differences among the groups (Fig. S27), suggesting that no obvious liver or kidney accumulation of phosphorus was detected at this early time point. In addition, no pathological abnormalities were detected in major organs at 8 weeks, providing further support for the in vivo biosafety of the organoid system (Fig. S28). Together, these results demonstrate that Gel/SilMA + BP periosteum organoids not only enhance bone formation but also reconstruct a functional periosteal interface characterized by periostin-defined identity, mature osteogenic compartments, stabilized microvasculature, and regulated osteoclast activity, thereby enabling efficient regeneration of critical-sized cranial bone defects.

Discussion

This study establishes periosteum organoids as a living regenerative interface for the repair of critical-sized bone defects. Unlike conventional strategies that emphasize osteoconductive scaffolds or exogenous growth factor delivery, our work demonstrates that reconstruction of periosteal regulatory function is both feasible and therapeutically decisive. In this sense, the innovation of the present study lies not merely in scaffold fabrication, but in using a bioactive bioprinted matrix to support the formation of a periosteum-like living interface with regulatory potential for bone repair. By integrating a mechanically robust interpenetrating composite-network hydrogel with black phosphorus–mediated bioactivity, we created a permissive microenvironment that supports osteogenic–angiogenic self-organization, in vivo maturation, and functional restoration of periosteal identity. The resulting periosteum organoids exhibit dense collagen architecture, strong periostin expression, enriched osteogenic compartments, stabilized microvasculature, and balanced bone remodeling, ultimately enabling efficient regeneration of otherwise non-healing cranial defects [43]. These findings shift the paradigm of skeletal regeneration from material replacement toward reconstruction of a living periosteal interface that actively governs tissue repair.

Organoid research has classically relied on pluripotent stem cell–based de novo self-organization to recapitulate developmental programs, as exemplified by seminal studies on cerebral, intestinal, hepatic, and vascular organoids derived from iPSCs or ESCs [44,45]. These systems have provided unprecedented insights into tissue morphogenesis and disease modeling but typically require prolonged differentiation protocols, multi-step growth factor modulation, and stringent control of developmental trajectories, which complicate reproducibility and large-scale application [46]. In parallel, periosteum-mimicking strategies in bone tissue engineering have largely adopted predefined combinations of mature cell types, decellularized matrices, or layered cell sheets, achieving partial reconstruction of periosteal functions but generally lacking intrinsic self-organizational capacity [47]. Our periosteum organoid strategy occupies an intermediate conceptual space between these two approaches. Rather than reconstituting periosteal development from pluripotent stem cells or assembling fixed cellular architectures, we employ a bioactive microenvironment to facilitate host-driven cellular recruitment and organization. This matrix-guided mode of tissue assembly aligns with recent reports describing environmentally instructed tissue self-patterning, suggesting that appropriately engineered extracellular niches may substitute for complex genetic or developmental programming [46,48]. Such a framework may provide an alternative route for constructing organ-like interfaces in regenerative settings where faithful developmental recapitulation is not feasible or necessary.

Transcriptomic profiling provides mechanistic insight into how Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels promote bone regeneration. RNA-seq analysis revealed that Gel/SilMA + BP simultaneously activated extracellular matrix–receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and PI3K–Akt signaling pathways in BMSCs, together with endothelial activation programs in HUVECs involving cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, chemokine signaling, and angiogenesis-related biological processes. These signatures indicate that the material supports both osteogenic and angiogenic responses relevant to bone repair.The enrichment of ECM–integrin and focal adhesion pathways suggests that the interpenetrating composite-network hydrogel provides a biologically active matrix that reinforces cell–matrix interactions and mechanotransductive signaling, while activation of the PI3K–Akt pathway further supports a role for Gel/SilMA + BP in promoting osteogenic differentiation [49,50]. Importantly, the parallel induction of osteogenic and angiogenic gene networks suggests that Gel/SilMA + BP functions not merely as a passive scaffold, but as a bioactive matrix, providing a molecular basis for the enhanced bone formation and vascularization observed in vivo [35].

Periosteum biology provides a useful benchmark for interpreting what “periosteal reconstruction” should entail. Native periosteum is a layered, highly dynamic interface in which a collagen-rich fibrous compartment and an inner cambium niche collectively supply progenitors, coordinate vascular ingrowth, and regulate remodeling during repair [51]. Lineage-tracing and single-cell–enabled studies consistently support that periosteal progenitors constitute a dominant source of reparative cells in fracture callus and contribute broadly to bone regeneration programs [16]. Meanwhile, periostin has emerged as a characteristic periosteal-associated matrix regulator that contributes to collagen fibrillogenesis/crosslinking and mechanosensitive remodeling, and periostin-expressing periosteal-associated cell populations have been linked to regenerative outcomes in bone repair contexts [52,53]. Against this biological backdrop, current “artificial periosteum” efforts have made important progress by engineering bilayer architectures, using decellularized periosteal ECM, or applying cell-sheet–based and growth factor–loaded membranes to enhance early osteogenesis and vascularization [54]. However, most of these platforms are designed as pre-defined constructs whose cellular composition and signaling output are largely specified a priori, and thus their ability to reproduce periosteum-like adaptability—particularly the coordinated coupling among matrix organization, vascular stabilization, and remodeling control—remains an open question and may depend strongly on implantation context, host cell recruitment, and the degree of in vivo remodeling achieved. In this context, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and comparatively: rather than implying equivalence to native periosteum, the data suggest that organoid-like maturation can recover several periosteal hallmarks within a single interface, while leaving important aspects of periosteum biology—such as regional heterogeneity, immune–stromal coordination, and load-responsive remodeling—topics for future validation and head-to-head comparison with established artificial periosteum platforms. Moreover, the present hydrogel construct remains mechanically weaker than native periosteum and should therefore be regarded as a bioactive periosteum-reconstructive interface rather than a full mechanical substitute. In addition, although the present study provided in vitro degradation data and qualitative evidence of structural persistence in vitro and in vivo, the matching relationship between material resorption and new bone deposition has not yet been rigorously quantified under in vivo conditions. Therefore, longer-term in vivo follow-up will still be required to determine whether scaffold degradation and new bone formation become truly synchronized over time. In addition, the long-term in vivo fate of BP, including its prolonged biodistribution and clearance, remains to be further clarified in future studies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study establishes periosteum organoids as a living regenerative interface capable of restoring periosteal identity and regulatory function in critical-sized bone defects. By integrating a bioactive GelMA/SilMA interpenetrating composite-network hydrogel with black phosphorus nanosheets, we present a matrix-guided strategy that supports osteogenic–angiogenic self-organization, in vivo maturation, and functional reconstruction of the periosteal niche without reliance on pluripotent stem cell programming. The resulting organoids promote robust bone regeneration, restore a periostin-enriched periosteal interface, stabilize microvasculature, and rebalance bone remodeling, collectively recapitulating key biological attributes of native periosteum. This work highlights the importance of reconstructing regulatory tissue interfaces rather than delivering static materials and introduces periosteum organoids as a broadly applicable platform for functional skeletal regeneration in large and otherwise non-healing bone defects.

Experimental section

Synthesis and characterization of GelMA and SilMA

Gelatin methacrylate (GelMA) was synthesized as previously described with minor modifications [30]. Briefly, 2 g of gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was dissolved in 20 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 60 °C under stirring. Subsequently, 1.6 mL methacrylic anhydride (MA; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was added dropwise and the reaction proceeded for 3 h at 60 °C. Then, 80 mL prewarmed PBS was added to dilute the reaction mixture. The GelMA solution was dialyzed against distilled water for 7 days. Silk fibroin methacrylate (SilMA) was synthesized according to a previously reported protocol [55]. Silkworm cocoons were first degummed by boiling in 0.02 M sodium carbonate solution (Aladdin, China) for 1 h to remove sericin. The resulting silk fibroin was rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and dried. Then, 2 g of degummed silk fibroin was dissolved in 10 mL of 9.3 M lithium bromide solution (Aladdin, China) at 60 °C. Methacrylated glycidyl ester (glycidyl methacrylate, GMA; 350 mM, Aladdin, China) was added and the reaction mixture was stirred for 3 h at 60 °C. The SilMA solution was dialyzed against distilled water for 7 days. Dialyzed GelMA and SilMA solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter prior to lyophilization. The degree of methacrylation of GelMA and SilMA was evaluated by 1H NMR spectroscopy and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, using native gelatin and silk fibroin as controls.

Preparation of GelMA, Gel/SilMA, and Gel/SilMA + BP

To prepare the Gel/SilMA + BP bioink, 100 mg GelMA, 100 mg SilMA, 200 ng black phosphorus (BP; Macklin, China), and LAP photoinitiator (0.1% w/v) as a photoinitiator were added to 1 mL deionized water and dissolved at 60 °C. The resulting solution was used as the Gel/SilMA + BP bioink. Under identical conditions, Gel/SilMA bioink (without BP) and GelMA bioink (without BP and SilMA) were prepared as controls. Prior to 3D printing, the bioinks were precooled at 4 °C to adjust viscosity. After printing, the constructs were photocrosslinked by UV irradiation for 30 s to obtain stable hydrogel structures.

Morphological and physicochemical characterization of hydrogels

For morphological observation, hydrogel samples (GelMA, Gel/SilMA, and Gel/SilMA + BP) were frozen, lyophilized, sputter-coated with gold, and examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM; SU8020, Hitachi, Japan). Elemental mapping and composition were analyzed using an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) system attached to the SEM. To further visualize the distribution of BP within the hydrogel network, Cy5.5-labeled BP was incorporated into the Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogel precursor. After photocrosslinking, the hydrogels were examined under a fluorescence microscope, and bright-field, Cy5.5 fluorescence, and merged images were collected. The tensile properties of hydrogels were measured using a universal testing machine (Instron 5943, Instron, USA). Rectangular samples (20 mm × 10 mm) with identical thickness were prepared for all groups. The tensile strength and related mechanical parameters were recorded and compared. For swelling measurements, the initial weight of each sample was recorded as W0. The samples were then immersed in PBS. At predetermined time points, the hydrogels were removed, gently blotted with filter paper to remove surface water, and weighed (Wt). The swelling ratio was calculated as: swelling ratio = (Wt - W0)/W0. For degradation studies, lyophilized hydrogel samples were weighed (W0) and incubated in PBS at 37 °C. At predetermined time points, samples were collected, lyophilized, and weighed (Wt). The mass remaining was calculated as: Mass remaining(%) = Wt/W0 × 100%.

Biocompatibility evaluation

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China). Briefly, bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were seeded into 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Then, 500 μl of the photocured hydrogel (GelMA, Gel/SilMA, or Gel/SilMA + BP) was added to each well. Cells were co-cultured with hydrogels for 1, 3, and 7 days. At each time point, cells were incubated with fresh culture medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent at 37 °C for 1.5 h in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Cytation 5, BioTek, USA). For 3D bioprinted constructs, the dissolved bioink was sterilized by passage through a 0.22 μm filter. BMSCs and EPCs were then suspended in the sterile bioink at a total density of 2 × 106 cells/mL with a cell ratio of 1:1, followed by thorough mixing to obtain a homogeneous cell-laden bioink. The mixture was printed into membrane-like constructs using an extrusion-based 3D bioprinter under the following parameters: extrusion pressure of 70 kPa, printing speed of 15 mm/s, 25 G nozzle, layer height of 0.2 mm, and 4 printed layers. The printed constructs were subsequently crosslinked by exposure to 405 nm light for 30 s. After printing and photocrosslinking, the viability of encapsulated cells within the 3D bioprinted constructs was evaluated by live/dead staining. Calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI) (Solarbio, China) were used according to the manufacturer's protocol. Live cells were visualized as green and dead cells as red under a fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis of live/dead cell ratios was performed using ImageJ software. After 7 days of culture, 3D constructs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The actin cytoskeleton was stained using an actin-tracking probe (C2223S, Beyotime, China), and cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were acquired to assess cell spreading and morphology. To evaluate in vivo biosafety, bioprinted constructs were implanted into calvarial defects in Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats as described below. At 8 weeks post-implantation, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested and subjected to histological examination. At 7 days postoperatively, rats were euthanized and the liver and kidney tissues were harvested. The tissues were thoroughly rinsed with PBS to remove residual blood, weighed, and then digested in concentrated nitric acid. After complete digestion, the samples were diluted to the appropriate volume with deionized water, and the phosphorus (P) content was quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS).

In vitro osteogenic and angiogenic evaluation

To assess osteogenic differentiation, rat BMSCs were co-cultured with GelMA, Gel/SilMA, or Gel/SilMA + BP hydrogels for 7 days. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity was evaluated by BCIP/NBT ALP staining kit (Beyotime Biological, China), following the manufacturer's protocol. For mineralization assessment, BMSCs were co-cultured with the hydrogels for 14 days and then stained with Alizarin Red S (ARS; Beyotime Biological, China). Stained samples were imaged under a light microscope, and quantitative analysis of ALP and ARS staining was performed using ImageJ. The angiogenic potential of HUVECs was evaluated by scratch wound and tube formation assays. For the scratch assay, 5 × 105 HUVECs were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to confluence. A linear scratch was created using a sterile pipette tip, followed by gentle washing with PBS to remove detached cells. Subsequently, 500 μl of the photocured hydrogel was added in each well. Images of the wound area were acquired at 0 and 24 h using an optical microscope, and the migrated rate was quantified using ImageJ. For the tube formation assay, 300 μL Matrigel (Corning, USA) was added to each well of a 24-well plate and allowed to gel at 37 °C. HUVECs (4 × 105 cells/mL) were resuspended in culture medium, and 300 μL of the cell suspension was seeded onto the Matrigel layer. After 6 h of incubation under different treatment conditions, tube-like structures were imaged under a microscope, and total tube length and node numbers were quantified using ImageJ. Total RNA was extracted using an RNA-Quick Purification Kit (Yishan Biological, China). cDNA was synthesized, and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR) was performed on a LightCycler 480 system (Roche, Germany). The expression of osteogenic genes (Runx2, Osx, ALP, Col1a1, and OCN) and the angiogenic gene VEGF was evaluated. GAPDH was chosen to normalize the expression levels of target genes. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. For cell-laden 3D constructs cultured in vitro, ALP and ARS staining were conducted as described above. In addition, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect the osteogenic marker osterix (Osx) and the angiogenic marker VEGF-A.

It should be noted that the endothelial cell experiments described above were limited to two-dimensional in vitro evaluation using HUVECs. In contrast, all cell-laden 3D bioprinted constructs used for in vitro three-dimensional culture, in vivo incubation, and calvarial defect repair were prepared using rat BMSCs and rat EPCs.

Animal experiments

Nude mice and SD rats were purchased from SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China). All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Army Medical University (AMUWEC20252165) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. To investigate in vivo development of the bioprinted constructs, different cell-laden bioinks containing rat BMSCs and rat EPCs were bioprinted and maintained in 3D culture for 3 days to allow initial cell adaptation and early cell–matrix interaction before implantation, and implanted subcutaneously into the dorsal region of nude mice. Samples were harvested at 15 and 30 days post-implantation to evaluate the self-assembly of the constructs into periosteum-like organoids. To assess the effects on bone defect repair, an 8-week-old female SD rat calvarial defect model was established. Rats were randomly assigned into four groups: defect only (Blank), GelMA, Gel/SilMA, and Gel/SilMA + BP. After shaving and disinfecting the cranial region with povidone-iodine, two bilateral critical-sized defects (5 mm in diameter) were created in the parietal bone using a trephine bur under constant saline irrigation. Bioprinted cell-laden constructs containing rat BMSCs and rat EPCs corresponding to each group were implanted into the defects. At 7 days postoperatively, blood samples were collected from rats for measurement of systemic inflammatory markers, including IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, and CRP. In addition, defect-region specimens harvested at day 7 were subjected to immunofluorescence staining of iNOS and CD206 to evaluate the early local immune microenvironment. Animals were sacrificed at 4 and 8 weeks post-implantation for further analyses.

Micro-CT analysis

Calvarial specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and scanned at 4 and 8 weeks using a micro-computed tomography system (micro-CT; Bruker, Belgium) to evaluate new bone formation within the defect region. Three-dimensional reconstruction and two-dimensional imaging were performed using SkyScan software (CTvox and DataViewer). A semi-automated region of interest (ROI) was selected in the bone defect region, and parameters, including bone mineral density (BMD), bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), were quantitatively analyzed.

Histological and immunofluorescence analysis

For histological evaluation, calvarial specimens were decalcified in EDTA solution (solution changed every 3 days) until complete decalcification, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining, Masson's trichrome staining, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining according to standard protocols. For immunohistochemical staining of Collagen I, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity and nonspecific binding. The sections were then incubated with a primary antibody against Collagen I overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Immunoreactivity was visualized using a DAB substrate kit, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were captured under identical settings, and quantitative analysis of Collagen I-positive staining in the defect area was performed using ImageJ software. Periosteum-like constructs retrieved from subcutaneous implantation in nude mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and processed using the same staining procedures. Panoramic scanning technology was used to capture characteristic images of the stained sections. For immunofluorescence staining, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were processed according to established protocols. Primary antibodies against periostin (to evaluate periosteal layer reconstruction), osteocalcin (OCN, to assess bone formation), and CD31 and α-SMA (dual staining to assess neovascularization) were used, followed by appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured using a panoramic digital slide scanner (DESK system), and semi-quantitative analysis was performed with ImageJ.

SEM analysis of biological structures

Explanted periosteum-like constructs retrieved from the subcutaneous of nude mice were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Samples were then rinsed three times with PBS (15 min each). Post-fixation was performed in 1% osmium tetroxide solution for 1–2 h at room temperature, followed by three additional PBS washes (15 min each). Samples were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, dried, sputter-coated with gold, and observed under SEM to evaluate surface morphology and cellular organization.

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from samples using TRIzol™ Reagent (15596018, Thermo Fisher Scientific). RNA purity was assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-ONE-W, Thermo Fisher Scientific), and RNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit RNA BR (Broad-Range) Assay Kit (Q10210, Yeasen). RNA integrity was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis. For each sample, 0.1–1 μg of total RNA was used for mRNA enrichment with the Hieff NGS® mRNA Isolation Master Kit V2 (12629ES96, Yeasen), followed by mRNA library construction using the Hieff NGS® EvoMax RNA Library Prep Kit (dNTP) (12341ES97, Yeasen). Library concentration was determined using the Equalbit 1 × dsDNA HS Assay Kit (EQ121-02, Vazyme), and fragment size distribution was analyzed on an Agilent 4200 system (Agilent Technologies). Libraries were subsequently circularized into single-stranded circular DNA libraries compatible with the MGI platform using the Hieff NGS® Dual Barcode Fast-Pace DNA Cyclization Kit for MGI (13340ES96, Yeasen), and circular libraries were quantified with the Equalbit ssDNA Assay Kit (EQ311-02, Vazyme). Finally, paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on a high-throughput sequencing platform.

Transcriptome data processing and differential expression analysis

RNA-seq and downstream bioinformatics analyses were conducted by OE Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Raw sequencing data in FASTQ format were quality-filtered using fastp (v1.x) to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads, yielding clean reads. Clean reads obtained from rat BMSC samples were aligned to the rat reference genome, while clean reads obtained from human HUVEC samples were aligned to the human reference genome, using HISAT2. Gene-level read counts were generated with HTSeq-count, and FPKM values were calculated for expression visualization. Sample reproducibility and global transcriptomic variation were evaluated by principal component analysis (PCA) in R (v4.5.2). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) defined as those with adjusted P value (Q value) < 0.05 and a fold change ≥2 or ≤ 0.5. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was used to characterize expression patterns across groups. The top 30 upregulated and downregulated DEGs were displayed using radar plots. Functional annotation and enrichment analyses of DEGs were performed using hypergeometric tests for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was additionally conducted based on ranked gene lists. Significant enrichment results were visualized in R using Venn diagrams, heatmaps, chord diagrams, and bubble plots.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed using at least three independent samples (n ≥ 3). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software. One-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was used for multiple group comparisons, followed by Tukey's post hoc test for pairwise comparisons. For experiments involving two factors, such as treatment and time in the CCK-8 assay, two-way ANOVA was employed. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001; ns, not significant.
