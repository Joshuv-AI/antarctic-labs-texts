There, but Invisible: Warning Messages in Czech Gambling Advertising during Football Broadcasts
Authors: Filip Kovařík, Steve Sharman, David Fiedor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGambling advertising during live sports broadcasts raises significant public health concerns, yet regulatory compliance (particularly regarding warning messages) remains underexplored. This study aims to evaluate how common warning messages are and how much screen space they occupy in gambling adverts during live top-tier Czech football broadcasts.MethodsA total of 1,824 gambling adverts were systematically analysed across 32 top-tier Czech football matches. Variables included the presence of mandatory warnings (about age restrictions and potential addiction) and safe gambling messages (SGMs), along with their delivery methods, gambling types, match segments, and screen coverage. Warning message sizes were measured using pixel-by-pixel calculations. Nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test and Cramér’s V) were used for statistical analysis.ResultsOverall, 77.7% of all adverts complied with legal requirements. SGM appeared in 58.1% of adverts. Warning messages occupied an average of 1.58% of the screen (SD = 0.47%), with significantly greater coverage in adverts featuring an SGM (p < 0.001). Screen coverage varied significantly across different gambling types (p < 0.001) but not across match segments (p = 0.834).ConclusionThe limited screen coverage and compliance issues identified highlight notable regulatory gaps, particularly regarding minimum size, font, and colour contrast requirements for warning messages. The simultaneous display of warnings alongside promotional content also reduces their effectiveness. Current regulations should be strengthened to better protect consumers from gambling-related harm.

Introduction

Warning messages (WMs) are applied to hazardous products, such as hot surfaces, flammable substances, or electrical components, to inform users of existing risks and help them avoid dangerous situations [1]. In the context of consuming health-harmful commodities, informing potential consumers about risks and negative health impacts represents a crucial element of public health prevention strategy. Effective communication of these risks requires a systematic approach to consumer education through various communication tools and interventions. Among the most widely used methods of risk communication are WMs and targeted health communications aimed at increasing awareness of potential hazards associated with the consumption of specific products (such as alcohol or tobacco).

However, the effectiveness of these messages depends on their adequate design and implementation. Beyond the content and visual design elements of WMs, the delivery method – how the message is presented to consumers – plays a crucial role in its effectiveness. Messages can be delivered as standalone presentations, where the warning appears independently without competing stimuli, or simultaneously with promotional content, where the warning must compete for attention with other information. Additionally, the delivery format (e.g., static text overlays vs. dynamic pop-up messages) significantly influences attention allocation and message processing [2, 3].

Like alcohol and tobacco, gambling disorder represents a significant public health issue whose products can cause a broad spectrum of negative impacts at both individual and societal levels [4]. Harms associated with disordered gambling can include increased suicide risk [5], while further evidence indicates that one disordered gambler affects, on average, six other individuals [6]. Although extensive research has already examined the effectiveness of WMs on electronic gambling machines (e.g., [7, 8]), little research has focused on sports betting. In recent years, sports betting has significantly increased in popularity, with its expansion facilitated primarily by the development of mobile betting applications [9], the gradual legalisation in some jurisdictions [10], and its status as the only permitted online form of gambling in some countries [11]. Despite sports betting representing a rapidly growing segment of the gambling market with potentially serious public health impacts, research on safe gambling messages (SGMs) for this form of gambling remains underdeveloped [12]. Only in recent years have new studies focused on this issue been regularly conducted [13–17], attempting to fill existing gaps in scientific knowledge and provide empirical foundations for effective preventive interventions.

The issue of SGMs becomes particularly complex in the sporting environment. During sporting events, gambling operators employ integrated and impulsive marketing strategies [18], with sports betting characterised by easy accessibility and attractive features for bettors [19]. Marketing strategies also portray sports betting as quirky and cool [20], which may lead to unplanned betting spending [21]. Sports betting is most prominently associated with football, as the two have a long-term historical interconnectedness [22, 23], evident in the sponsorship of football clubs [24] and the presence of gambling advertisements in football stadiums [25]. This extensive integration of gambling elements into the football environment has led to the formulation of the concept of sport “gamblification” [26, 27]. Gambling advertisements during football broadcasts typically take several forms: pre-match and half-time promotional spots aired during breaks, in-match graphic overlays displayed during live play, and sponsored content segments integrated into commentary or studio coverage. These formats differ substantially in their duration, visual prominence, and the degree to which WMs must compete with other on-screen content for viewer attention. In the context of intensive marketing exposure, WMs in sports broadcast advertisements are particularly significant. For this study, the following operational definitions are applied consistently throughout. A gambling advertisement refers to any commercial communication promoting gambling products or services broadcast during or around a football match, including promotional spots and sponsored segments. WMs are an umbrella term encompassing all textual or visual risk communications appearing in gambling advertisements. Within this umbrella, a distinction is made between two components: mandatory warning elements, legally required messages stipulated by Czech legislation, namely the addiction warning and the age restriction notice (detailed below), and SGMs, voluntary communications encouraging responsible gambling behaviours. When both a mandatory element and an SGM appeared in a single advertisement, each was coded independently.

Importantly, the majority of existing research on gambling WMs and sports betting advertising has been conducted in Western European and Global North contexts – predominantly in Australia, the UK, and North America. This geographic concentration limits the generalisability of findings, as regulatory frameworks, gambling cultures, and advertising practices differ substantially across jurisdictions. Research from Central European countries, where gambling markets are rapidly expanding yet remain understudied, is therefore critically needed to broaden the empirical evidence base and inform region-specific policy responses.

Gambling represents a significant and growing market in the Czech Republic, generating approximately EUR 2.9 billion in 2025, with sports betting accounting for an increasing share [28]. The Czech market is notable for its high density of gambling venues and for the rapid growth of online gambling following legislative reforms introduced by the Gambling Act in 2017.

Football occupies a prominent position in the Czech sporting landscape. The nation’s Premier Division comprises 16 clubs, and live coverage of all fixtures is available on Oneplay, attracting over 120,000 viewers per match [29]. Prior research has established that match broadcasts contain an average of 57 gambling-related advertisements [30]; these advertisements vary in type and placement across match segments, yet their content characteristics (including the presence, size, and positioning of WMs) have not been systematically examined. All advertisements should comply with the relevant legislation of the applicable jurisdiction and with established regulatory requirements. In the Czech Republic, the gambling sector is regulated by the Gambling Act (Act No. 186/2016 Coll., on Gambling) [31], establishing a licensing system administered by the Ministry of Finance as the main regulatory body. The Advertising Regulation Act (Act No. 40/1995 Coll.) [32] governs gambling advertising, restricting content and targeting, including a prohibition on portraying gambling as an alternative to employment.

Under this Act, gambling advertisements must include two mandatory elements: (1) the standardised addiction warning reading “Ministry of Finance warns: Participation in gambling may lead to addiction!” and (2) a statement explicitly prohibiting participation of persons under 18 years of age. The legislation specifies the required wording of both elements but does not prescribe minimum size, duration, or placement within the advertisement, leaving these implementation parameters to the discretion of advertisers. These two elements (the addiction warning and the age-restriction statement) constitute the legally mandated WMs under Czech law. SGMs, by contrast, are voluntary communications encouraging safer gambling behaviours and are not required by legislation.

Although Czech legislation defines the basic content requirements for mandatory WMs, to our best knowledge, no evaluation of their practical implementation has yet been conducted in the context of live sports broadcasts. The identification of this research gap motivates the present study. Building on our prior work [30], which documented the presence, content, and frequency of gambling advertisements in Czech football broadcasts, the present study extends this analysis by examining the implementation of WMs; specifically, their compliance with legislative requirements, screen size, placement, and the characteristics of voluntary safe gambling messaging. The aim is to evaluate the prevalence and screen coverage of WMs in gambling advertisements during live broadcasts of Czech League football matches. Specifically, we focus on the following research questions:(1) To what extent do gambling advertisements during live football broadcasts comply with legislative requirements?(2) What is the prevalence of SGMs in gambling advertisements, how are they delivered, and does their presence vary across match segments and gambling forms?(3) What proportion of screen space do WMs occupy within gambling advertisements, and does this vary across match segments and gambling forms?

Methods

Data Collection

A previous study used this dataset to examine the content and characteristics of gambling advertisements during football broadcasts [30]. The prior study focused on the presence, frequency, and general characteristics of gambling advertisements (including form of gambling, operator, call to action, celebrity appearance, or match segment distribution); it did not examine warning or SGMs, their screen size or placement, or compliance with legislative requirements. The present study, therefore, introduces an entirely new set of variables and research questions not addressed in the prior publication. Specifically, the variables analysed here, the presence of mandatory warning elements, age restriction notices, and SGMs; their screen coverage; their placement and presentation format; and their compliance with Czech legislative requirements, represent a distinct analytical focus with no overlapping analyses having been published. Secondary analysis enables the answering of new research questions without the need for repeated data collection, which aligns with the principles of ethical and transparent utilisation of research resources [33].

For each identified gambling advertisement, a set of characteristics relevant to the analysis of WMs and their compliance with legislative requirements was recorded. The following variables specific to this study were systematically coded for every advertisement: the presence of a WM, age restriction, and SGM, each coded dichotomously (yes/no); the type of SGM and its presentation, categorised as either simultaneous (displayed concurrently with active gambling advertising content) or standalone (displayed in the absence of promotional gambling content). An initial validation stage took place on August 2 and 3, 2025, during which two coders (F.K. and D.F.) independently analysed gambling advertisements in four matches of the Czech top football league. Percentage agreement was used as the measure of reliability, given that the distributions of several key variables were heavily skewed (e.g., the vast majority of advertisements contained a mandatory WM), which can artificially inflate or deflate Cohen’s kappa. Agreement was assessed across the following variables: operator, match segment, advertisement length, call-to-action, form of gambling, celebrity appearance, age-restriction message, mandatory WM, and safe-gambling message. Overall percentage agreement was 91.8%, exceeding the 70% threshold considered acceptable [34]. The primary analysis covered four rounds of the Czech top football league, comprising 32 matches (August to September 2025), broadcast via the IPTV service Oneplay (O2 TV). The sampling frame of four rounds represents approximately 11.6% of all matches played across the full Czech league season. This window is likely representative of Czech league gambling advertising, as gambling advertising contracts between operators and broadcasters are typically negotiated on a seasonal basis, meaning the format, content, and regulatory compliance of advertisements remain relatively consistent throughout the season. After establishing high inter-rater reliability in the first phase, both coders continued analysing 16 matches each, thus guaranteeing complete analysis while preserving methodological reliability. All advertising spots were systematically recorded in a unified database.

Advert Types

Due to specific defining characteristics, adverts were coded into two types: in-play adverts and broadcast adverts. In-play adverts were uniformly presented as banners, appeared during the live match segment (when the match was actually being played), and were dynamic rather than static. In-play adverts came in two forms; the first, referred to subsequently as type 1 in-play, were positioned within a designated field in the lower portion of the broadcast screen, displayed the text “Live betting,” followed by the logo of the respective gambling operator (shown in Fig. 1a). The other in-play advert form, referred to subsequently as type 2 in-play, displayed live odds for the ongoing fixture (shown in Fig. 1b), showing the current odds for a home win, a draw, and an away win, alongside the respective gambling operator’s logo.

Type 1 in-play banner advert (a) and type 2 in-play live odds (b) advert displayed during a match.

Broadcast adverts, by contrast, appeared exclusively during the pre-match, half-time break, or after-match segments, never during live play. Unlike In-play adverts, which occupied only a designated portion of the screen as dynamic banners, broadcast adverts typically occupied the full screen. Match segments were defined based on the referee’s whistle as the objective boundary marker. The pre-match segment includes all content before the referee’s opening whistle. The half-time segment began at the referee’s whistle, ending the first half, and concluded at the referee’s whistle signalling the start of the second half. The after-match segment began at the referee’s final whistle.

Measurement of WMs

The sizes of WMs were measured relative to the total area of advertisements displayed during football broadcasts. Except for in-play adverts, which occupied only a designated portion of the screen, all gambling advertisements occupied the full screen; for in-play adverts, the total advertisement boundary was defined accordingly before measuring the WM area within it. Proportional coverage was calculated as the percentage of screen space each message occupied. The percentage ratios were computed by dividing the area of the WM by the total area of the advertisement. All measurements were conducted using pixel-by-pixel area calculations, where both the message area and the total advertisement area were measured in pixels and subsequently converted to proportional values. Because all measurements are expressed as proportional ratios of pixel areas within the same image frame, the results are inherently independent of screen resolution, display size, or aspect ratio. The proportional coverage of a WM relative to the total advertisement area remains constant regardless of the device or format on which the broadcast is viewed, ensuring the comparability and generalisability of the reported values. Measurements were performed using Adobe Illustrator. For each advertisement, the boundaries of WMs were manually delineated, and the software automatically calculated the pixel area. Advertisements occupying only a portion of the screen were measured by first defining the total advertisement boundary and then measuring the message areas within this boundary. Messages were defined as any text or visual element containing warnings about gambling risks or information promoting safer gambling practices. This approach allowed for a standardised comparison of message prominence across different advertisements and broadcast contexts. A quantitative approach was adopted because precise proportional measurements enable systematic comparisons across advertisement types and contexts, provide an empirical basis for evaluating regulatory compliance, and allow findings to be compared across jurisdictions and objectives. Screen coverage was subsequently compared across match segments and gambling forms to address differences in the prominence of WMs within these categories. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, and ranges, were calculated for both message types. Statistical analyses were conducted in Statistica. Given the nonnormal distribution of screen coverage data, nonparametric tests were used. The Kruskal-Wallis H test was used to compare WM screen coverage across three groups (match segments and gambling form categories); where a significant overall effect was found; post hoc pairwise multiple comparisons were conducted, with p values adjusted to control for familywise error rate. A separate Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare WM screen coverage between advertisements with and without an SGM to assess whether the inclusion of an SGM was associated with a reduction in the mandatory WM size. Cramér’s V was calculated to assess the strength of association between categorical variables. A significance level of α = 0.05 was applied throughout.

Results

Legal Compliance

Advertisements were coded as legally compliant if both mandatory warning elements (addiction warning and age-restriction statement) were visibly present. During the monitored period, across 32 matches, a total of 1,824 advertisements were broadcast, of which three-quarters (77.7%) included both mandatory elements, thereby complying with legal requirements.

In-play banner adverts comprised 22.3% of all gambling adverts (n = 406). There were 112 type 1 in-play adverts. All of this advert type (100%) failed to adhere to the legislatively mandated conditions (shown in Fig. 1a). Type 2 in-play adverts appeared 294 times and included both a mandatory WM and an SGM. However, the mandatory WM occupied only 0.47% of the screen area, while the SGM accounted for an even more negligible proportion of just 0.01%, consisting solely of the Czech abbreviation for “responsible gambling” rendered as the initials “zh” (shown in Fig. 1b).

There were a total of 1,124 broadcast adverts across 32 matches. As outlined in the methods section, a WM may contain either solely the legally mandated elements (age restriction and addiction warning) or also an SGM promoting safer gambling practices.

SGM Prevalence and Delivery

As mandatory warning elements represent a binary legal requirement (either fully met or not), their presence was captured through the compliance analysis reported above. Inferential analysis of the presence of mandatory elements across match segments or gambling forms was therefore not conducted; only SGM presence was subjected to such analysis. The presence of SGMs across the different match segments (pre-match, half-time break, and after-match) varied only slightly (shown in Fig. 2). Overall, SGMs appeared in 766 broadcast advertisements (68.1%), with a marginally higher proportion during the half-time break. The relationship between the variables is minimal (Cramér’s V = 0.07) and not statistically significant (p = 0.06). A similar pattern is observed when comparing SGM presence across different gambling forms (i.e., sports betting, online casino, and lottery), with even smaller differences (Cramér’s V = 0.04, p = 0.38).

Presence of SGMs by match segment and gambling form.

Regarding the delivery method, the vast majority (99.7%) of SGMs appeared simultaneously during the promotion of the gambling content within the advertisement. Only two advertisements contained a standalone SGM; both took the form of a spoken-word message (“Do not forget, play responsibly.”) lasting 3 s, appearing at the end of a 30-s advertisement after the promotional content had concluded. Even after the active promotional content concluded, the gambling operator’s logo remained prominently displayed on-screen throughout the SGM. As these two advertisements conveyed their SGM solely through spoken word, with no accompanying visual or textual elements, they are excluded from the subsequent analyses, which focus on the visual presentation of WMs. Across all subsequent analyses, the authors focus on the 764 advertisements whose WMs contained an SGM in visual form.

SGMs occupied an average of 0.26% of screen space. The format of the SGMs differed in both their presented form and visual appearance. A total of 4 distinct textual SGMs were recorded (shown in Fig. 3).

Typology of SGM: (1) responsible gambling logo; (2) responsible gambling logo + “play reasonably” slogan; (3) “play responsibly” slogan; and (4) “play reasonably” slogan.

The presence of only an RG logo (Responsible Gambling; ZH – zodpovědné hraní in Czech) was recorded in 550 adverts. The RG logo, accompanied by the slogan “play reasonably,” appeared in 84 ads. 56 advertisements featured only the “play reasonably” slogan, while 72 ads displayed the “play responsibly” slogan. Although the match segment shows only a marginal association with the use of a particular type of SGM slogan – with a declining proportion of adverts featuring only a logo and a concurrent increase in those combining a logo with the slogan “play reasonably” – a considerably stronger relationship is seen in gambling forms. While the association with the match segment is statistically significant but weak (Cramér’s V = 0.11, p = 0.005), the link with gambling form is strong (Cramér’s V = 0.87, p < 0.001). The slogan “play reasonably” is exclusively linked to lottery adverts, where no other type of SGM appears. “Play responsibly” occurs only in online casino adverts and constitutes the type of SGM featured in more than half of all online casino advertisements (shown in Fig. 4).

WM Screen Coverage

An important indicator of WM visibility is its size. On average, WMs covered only 1.58% of the screen, with a standard deviation of 0.47%. A preliminary comparison examined whether advertisements containing both the mandatory warning elements and a voluntary SGM allocated more screen space to warning communications overall than those containing only the mandatory elements. This comparison was motivated by the concern that advertisers might respond to the requirement to include an SGM by reducing the size of the mandatory WM, thereby maintaining the overall message area rather than expanding it, thereby undermining the visibility of the legally required content. Using the Mann-Whitney test, the null hypothesis of equal medians for screen coverage in advertisements with an SGM and those without was rejected at α = 0.05 (p < 0.001), indicating that advertisements including an SGM dedicated a significantly greater proportion of screen area to warning communications than those displaying only the mandatory elements (shown in Fig. 5).

Screen coverage of WMs by SGM presence.

The size of WMs does not differ statistically significantly across match segments (KW-H(2, 764) = 0.36; p = 0.834); however, for gambling forms, the differences are considerably more pronounced (shown in Fig. 6) and statistically significant (KW-H(2, 764) = 172.74; p < 0.001). Post hoc pairwise multiple comparisons confirmed significant differences between all three gambling form categories (all p < 0.001). WMs occupy the greatest screen area in lottery advertisements, while online casino advertisements display the smallest WMs, with a median difference of 1 percentage point.

Screen coverage of WMs by match segment (a) and gambling form (b).

Discussion

This study sought to understand the frequency, content, and delivery mechanisms of warning and safer gambling messages shown alongside gambling adverts during live coverage of top-level Czech league football matches. Although three-quarters of advertisements (77.7%) contained legally required warnings, over one in five (n = 406; 22.3%) failed to meet all legislative requirements. Compliance was particularly poor in in-play banner adverts. All type 1 in-play adverts (n = 112) failed to adhere to mandated conditions, while Type 2 in-play adverts, despite including both a mandatory warning and an SGM, displayed these in a manner that renders their communicative value highly questionable. The mandatory warning occupied just 0.47% of the screen, and the SGM an even more negligible 0.01%. Across all advertisements, the average WM accounted for only 1.58% of the screen area, with the SGM accounting for just 0.26%. Furthermore, the vast majority of SGMs (99.7%) were delivered simultaneously with promotional gambling content, and more than four-fifths referenced the concept of “responsible gambling.”

Safe Gambling Messages

SGMs – which are not mandatory under Czech law – were present in 58.1% of all advertisements, and invariably took the form of one of two phrases: either “hraj zodpovědně” (“play responsibly”) or “hraj s rozumem” (“play reasonably”). More specifically, four distinct SGM formats were identified: two text-only slogans (“hraj zodpovědně” and “hraj s rozumem”), a standalone RG logo (ZH – zodpovědné hraní, “responsible gambling”), and a combined format pairing the RG logo with the slogan “play reasonably.” All four formats represent generic, non-specific calls to responsible behaviour, with no message providing concrete guidance, loss-framing, or actionable harm-reduction information. The internationally widely used slogan “gamble responsibly” is regarded by researchers as ineffective and overly generic [2, 35], and was present in 81.5% of SGMs in the current study. This message also presents a significant terminological problem in Czech translation, arising from the absence of a direct equivalent for the verb “to gamble.” The standard translation “hraj zodpovědně” (“play responsibly”) uses the verb “hrát” (“play”), which, however, encompasses a broad spectrum of activities in Czech, ranging from sports to board and computer games, acting, and general manipulation with objects, as well as gambling. This semantic ambiguity weakens the specificity and effectiveness of the safety message because it cannot clearly distinguish between gambling and ordinary play as harmless activities.

The precise wording and content of WMs play a critical role in determining their effectiveness [36]. The best-rated messages with significant possible impact proved to be “Chances are you’re about to lose,” “You win some, you lose more,” or “Imagine what you could be buying instead” [13], or “Only bet what you can afford” [14]; broadly, these represent messages designed to prompt critical reflection on gambling behaviour [3]. None of these messages, or their Czech equivalent, were identified in the current study.

The primary purpose of SGMs should be to capture attention easily, convey relevant, personally meaningful information, and suggest specific procedures or preventive measures to mitigate potential risks associated with gambling [2]. Yet, even setting aside concerns about message content, the present findings raise serious doubts about whether the observed messages could fulfil this purpose in any form. SGMs, when present, occupy a screen area so negligible that they are unlikely to capture viewer attention, let alone convey meaningful information or prompt behavioural change. Research indicates that the relative size of a warning is a primary determinant of its visual salience, and that more salient warnings are significantly more likely to be consciously noticed and to elicit protective responses [37].

Regulatory Gaps

Despite the content requirements, the current legislation contains significant gaps regarding the presentation parameters of WMs. The regulatory framework does not specify minimum font size requirements, which would otherwise ensure that warnings remain legible across various media formats and devices. Similarly, there are no stipulations regarding the minimum percentage of screen area that warnings must occupy, potentially allowing for diminutive presentations that minimise visibility. These regulatory gaps manifest in practice through remarkably small and highly variable message sizes, with WMs covering only 1.58% of the advertisement area on average – and sometimes as little as 0.48% of the screen.

The absence of regulations governing the placement of warnings within advertisements allows operators to position them in less prominent locations. The legislation also lacks specifications regarding colour contrast requirements, which could otherwise ensure that warnings remain distinct from background elements and maintain optimal visibility [1]. These regulatory gaps may significantly undermine the effectiveness of WMs [38].

The inclusion of SGMs in gambling advertisements is not mandatory under Czech legislation, which represents a significant regulatory limitation, as evidenced by SGM’s presence in only 58.1% of total adverts (including banners). This voluntary approach to safe gambling communication creates inconsistencies in consumer protection, as operators may choose to omit these potentially beneficial messages entirely. It is particularly problematic, given that such communications can provide essential harm-reduction information [35] and promote safer gambling behaviours among consumers [39]. Research across multiple jurisdictions demonstrates that voluntary and self-regulatory approaches to harm reduction are inadequate for ensuring meaningful consumer protection [40], a failure clearly reflected in the absence of minimum size or placement requirements for WMs.

These regulatory gaps are further reflected in the statistically significant differences in WM size observed across gambling forms (p < 0.001). Lottery advertisements displayed the largest WMs, whereas online casinos featured the smallest, with a median difference of approximately one percentage point. Although this may appear marginal in absolute terms, relative to the overall minimal screen coverage of WMs identified in this study, it represents a substantial disparity. This finding is particularly concerning from a public health perspective, as online casino gambling is widely regarded as the most potentially harmful form of gambling [41]. The inverse relationship between gambling harm potential and WM prominence thus represents a critical regulatory failure.

Message Delivery

All textual WMs were positioned at the bottom of the screen. The placement strategy, with small font and low-contrast colours, represents a classic example of “dark pattern” design [42], in which companies deliberately minimise the visibility of crucial safety information. This problematic approach is further compounded when WMs are delivered simultaneously with promotional content. While the warning text appears on screen, auditory stimuli continue to actively promote gambling or the operator’s brand. This practice mirrors the simultaneous presentation of multiple messages commonly employed in television news programming, where weather forecasts or sports scores are displayed concurrently while news anchors deliver stories [43]. However, while this format may be acceptable for informational content, research [44] demonstrates that simultaneously displayed, semantically divergent messages divide viewer attention and create processing difficulties, potentially compromising message comprehension and retention. This conflicting messaging significantly undermines the effectiveness and impact of warnings. The two standalone advertisements identified exemplify this tension: although the spoken-word message appeared after the promotional content had concluded, a persistent visual display of the gambling operator – a combination that, according to Sharman et al. [45], may have increased brand awareness while simultaneously reducing the viewer’s opportunity to fully process the message itself.

Recommendations

Several key recommendations emerge for improving the design and implementation of warning communications in gambling advertising. Regarding WMs, substantial improvements are needed in their design, particularly in the size implementation. The current minimal screen coverage identified demonstrates the urgent need for regulatory specifications regarding minimum size requirements. To achieve high noticeability, warnings must be as conspicuous as possible to attract the attention of individuals engaged in other activities [1]. Given that 99.7% of SGMs were delivered simultaneously with promotional content, regulators should require a standalone presentation to reduce competing cognitive demands. Regulators should require that these messages be presented separately from promotional material, rather than being superimposed on it. Furthermore, findings from the current study indicate that safe gambling and WMs occupy negligible screen space; legislation could mandate a minimum size requirement for all messaging. Finally, the textual content should move beyond the conventional “responsible gambling” approach towards more effective messaging strategies that have demonstrated greater impact from consumers’ perspectives. Research has shown that messages prompting financial consideration [13] and loss awareness [14] are significantly more effective.

Limitations

Some limitations should be acknowledged in this study. A significant limitation is the absence of empirical research involving respondents, which would enable an evaluation of current practices regarding WMs in advertisements from the consumer’s perspective. Such research would provide valuable insights into message perception, comprehension, and behavioural impact. Additionally, this study evaluates only the prevalence and size of warnings, without assessing their actual effectiveness or impact on viewer behaviour. A related limitation concerns ecological validity: while content analysis captures WMs as they appear under natural broadcast conditions, it cannot account for how viewers perceive or process these messages in a real viewing context.

Another potential limitation is the focus exclusively on football broadcasts. However, gambling is most strongly interconnected with football among all sports [22], making this focus methodologically justified for examining gambling advertising practices. It should further be noted that other forms of gambling-related advertising may have been visible during broadcasts (e.g., electronic pitch-side hoardings or shirt sponsorship), but these were outside the scope of the present study, which focused exclusively on the content of television advertising spots. Data collection was conducted during only a portion of the league season. Nevertheless, the total of 32 matches across different months may have helped mitigate potential variations in the content and delivery of WMs over time. Further research should extend this work nationally to other sports and competitions that are significant to Czech culture (e.g., ice hockey) and internationally to include international tournaments such as the FIFA World Cup, to assess whether the patterns identified here are specific to top-level Czech football or reflect broader trends in gambling advertising practices.

Conclusion

Despite being legally mandated, WMs in Czech gambling advertising during football broadcasts remain largely ineffective in their current form. Nearly a quarter of all advertisements failed to meet even the basic legislative requirement to include a WM, and those that did displayed it in a minimal, potentially unnoticeable way, occupying, on average, only 1.58% of screen space (SD = 0.47%). The fact that voluntary SGMs, present in 58.1% of advertisements, were associated with significantly greater screen coverage suggests that operators are capable of more prominent warning display, but do so selectively. Taken together, these findings indicate that existing regulations are insufficient to ensure meaningful consumer protection and that compliance alone does not guarantee visibility. Strengthening regulatory requirements, particularly regarding the size and placement of WMs, appears necessary. Future research should assess whether WMs achieve their intended harm-reduction goals, combining objective measures (e.g., eye-tracking) with subjective assessments of noticeability and perceived effectiveness.
