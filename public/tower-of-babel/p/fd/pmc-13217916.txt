Quantifying virtual education preferences in medical sciences during crises: a discrete choice analysis
Authors: Maryam Jahanbakhsh, Arefeh Mousavi, Mostafa Amini-Rarani, Farimah Rahimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic and other global emergencies accelerated the shift to virtual learning in higher education, ensuring instructional continuity but also revealing significant challenges. Disparities in technology access, instructional design, and student engagement highlighted critical gaps in preparedness. Understanding the factors that shape students’ preferences for virtual education is essential for building resilient, equitable, and adaptable systems.MethodsA discrete choice experiment (DCE) was conducted to identify determinants of student preferences for virtual learning. Six attributes were examined: technology infrastructure, support structure, e-learning method, student characteristics, teacher characteristics, and evaluation. Attribute levels were derived from the literature review and qualitative analysis. Data were collected via an online questionnaire, including demographics and 15 choice scenarios designed using orthogonality and D-efficiency principles. Sampling was stratified by faculty and field of study, with non-random recruitment continuing until 300 completed responses were obtained. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics and nominal logistic regression in SAS JMP.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 22.3 years (SD = 3.38); most were female (66.3%), single (93.1%), and enrolled in bachelor’s (48.2%) or M.D. programs (47.2%). Overall, digital, economic, and educational preparedness was adequate. Regression analysis indicated that the e-learning method and technology infrastructure were the most influential factors, followed by evaluation methods and teacher characteristics. Blended learning significantly increased selection likelihood compared to synchronous learning (OR = 1.85), while asynchronous learning showed a smaller positive effect (OR = 1.21). Enhanced tools and technologies increased preferences relative to basic platforms (OR = 1.40). Communication competency and virtual exam quality had lower odds compared to time-management ability (OR = 0.80) and exam type (OR = 0.88). Demographic factors were generally non-significant, but prior virtual learning experience, academic/technical requirements, and economic status had meaningful effects.ConclusionBlended learning models, robust technology infrastructure, and context-sensitive strategies are critical for effective and resilient virtual education. Faculty development in digital pedagogy, infrastructure investment, and adaptive course design aligned with learner profiles is essential for improving student experiences and outcomes. Future studies should explore longitudinal effects and broader student populations to validate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09016-x.

Introduction

Natural disasters and large-scale emergencies significantly disrupt higher education systems worldwide, yet comprehensive and resilient frameworks to ensure educational continuity during such crises remain underdeveloped. These disruptions threaten not only institutional functioning but also students’ access to learning, academic progression, and professional development.

Disasters are broadly classified as natural or man-made events, both of which can severely affect educational systems [1]. Over the past decade, higher education institutions have faced repeated disruptions from earthquakes, floods, armed conflicts, political instability, and infectious disease outbreaks. These crises have resulted in loss of life, physical injuries, financial strain, infrastructure damage, and prolonged interruptions to education and research activities [2]. In disaster settings, displacement of families into temporary housing, coupled with limited educational facilities, further constrains students’ ability to participate consistently in academic activities [1].

Emergencies affecting education are not limited to natural disasters such as earthquakes or floods; they also include communicable disease outbreaks, armed conflicts, and political unrest. These events can cause short-term disruptions or evolve into prolonged, multifaceted crises with lasting adverse consequences for learning environments [3, 4]. The abrupt nature of this transition posed significant challenges, especially in developing and under-resourced contexts where digital infrastructure and human capacity were insufficient. For well-prepared systems, such disruptions may remain temporary; for others, they risk precipitating long-term educational crises [5].

Medical education has been particularly vulnerable to such disruptions. Evidence shows that COVID-19 significantly affected internships, clinical rotations, surgical residencies, and continuing professional education. The suspension of case discussions, departmental meetings, and scientific conferences reduced both formal and informal learning opportunities [6]. These challenges were compounded by technical barriers such as unstable internet connectivity, limited access to digital tools, and cybersecurity concerns, necessitating rapid adaptation by both students and faculty members [7].

Lessons from past emergencies underscore the importance for policymakers of building resilient educational systems capable of adapting rapidly to changing conditions. The integration of advanced technologies into teaching has long been a feature, making many components amenable to virtual delivery [8]. The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted this imperative, compelling institutions to pivot almost overnight from traditional, in-person instruction to distance learning models to preserve continuity and quality of education [3]. Distance learning encompasses a wide range of modalities — online or offline, synchronous or asynchronous — delivered through multimedia resources and accessible via devices such as smartphones, tablets, laptops, and desktop computers [4].

Existing studies offer valuable but varied insights. For example, a UK-based national survey of medical students during the pandemic found that online education effectively sustained medical training despite campus closures [9]. However, resistance emerged due to limited familiarity and digital skills among both instructors and students [10].

On the other hand, Learners also reported a diminished sense of connection, reduced access to teaching staff and resources, and fewer face-to-face interactions — factors that can undermine perceived quality of education. Particularly in clinical training, virtual modalities may restrict practical experience, potentially affecting the competence and confidence of future physicians [11]. Nursing students have expressed concerns over diminished educational quality, loss of direct patient interaction, and stress from abrupt transitions [12]. Surveys in Jordan and Pakistan found that many medical and dental students preferred traditional in-person instruction and were dissatisfied with online alternatives [13] and [14]. Conversely, research in China reported that strategic enhancements to online dental education proved both feasible and effective [15]. Similarly, a systematic review of Iranian students’ experiences revealed wide variability in satisfaction, from as low as 29% to as high as 85% [16].

In this context, maintaining rigorous academic and clinical standards, safeguarding assessment integrity, and designing effective virtual learning environments during emergencies become essential goals [17]. Therefore, understanding students’ preferences for virtual education is critical for developing targeted interventions that balance quality, accessibility, and engagement.

Despite the growing body of literature, a critical knowledge gap remains. Most existing studies describe satisfaction levels or general attitudes toward online learning, while limited evidence systematically identifies and prioritizes the factors that shape students’ preferences for virtual education during emergencies. Moreover, few studies employ preference-elicitation methods capable of quantifying trade-offs students make among different components of virtual learning environments.

To address this gap, the present quantitative study applies a discrete choice analysis (DCA) to identify and evaluate the relative importance of factors influencing students’ preferences for virtual education in emergency contexts. This study introduces a methodological approach that moves beyond descriptive evaluations toward preference-based decision support.

Accordingly, the central research question guiding this study is: Which factors most strongly influence students’ preferences for virtual education during emergencies, and how do students prioritize these factors when faced with alternative virtual learning configurations? Answering this question will provide higher education leaders and policymakers with evidence-based insights to design resilient, equitable, and student-centered virtual education systems capable of ensuring academic continuity and quality during future crises.

Method

Study design

This study used a cross-sectional Discrete Choice Experiment (DCE) design to examine students’ preferences regarding key components of virtual education during emergencies. The Methods section follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting standards.

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IUMS). Inclusion criteria were enrollment at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences and consent to participate. A sample was identified using faculty-level and departmental stratification to ensure representation across academic majors and educational levels. Because recruitment relied on students who were both willing to participate and accessible to the data collection team within each stratum, sampling was non-probability (stratified convenience-based).

Instrument development

Attributes and levels included in the discrete choice experiment were derived from a prior qualitative study conducted in the same institution. In that study, purposive maximum-variation sampling was used to recruit students with at least two semesters of virtual learning experience. Sixteen semi-structured interviews were conducted via Skype between May 2021 and April 2022, continuing until thematic saturation was achieved. Interviews were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis framework. Coding reliability and thematic consistency were established through multiple researcher meetings and member checking. Six main themes emerged as requirements of virtual education, including:

Technology infrastructureSupport structureE-learning methodStudent characteristicsTeacher characteristicsEvaluationThese themes were subsequently operationalized into experimentally testable DCE attributes. Levels within each attribute were selected based on: frequency and salience in qualitative coding, consistency with existing literature on online learning quality, and feasibility and interpretability within real educational policy environments.

Evaluation

To ensure clarity and appropriateness of wording, all attribute definitions and level labels were reviewed by two experts in medical education, one expert in health services research, and five students not previously involved in the qualitative phase. Minor revisions were made to simplify wording and avoid ambiguity.

Finally, levels were specified as follows: for technology infrastructure, tools and technologies, and educational platforms were chosen as levels. For support structure, levels included hardware support, library facilities, financial support, psychological support, orientation to the academic environment, and software support. The e-Learning method attribute included synchronous, asynchronous, and blended approaches as levels. Student Characteristics comprised factors such as student circumstances and amenities, technological aids for class time management, and peer engagement. For Teacher Characteristics, levels were content creation, technological proficiency with virtual content, time management skills, and communication competency. Lastly, Evaluation levels involved virtual exam quality, virtual exam type, and virtual exam conditions. All categorical variables were dummy-coded, and the reference level for each attribute has been explicitly specified.

Questionnaire design

The questionnaire used in this study was developed specifically for this research based on a review of the literature on virtual education preferences and crisis management in higher education. An English-language version of the questionnaire has been uploaded as a supplementary file 1. The questionnaire was designed in two sections. The first section contained demographic questions covering age, gender, marital status, academic degree, faculty, and living conditions (area, city, and living arrangements). This section also included questions on the participants’ experience with virtual learning at the university level, availability of necessary e-learning resources, and self-assessment of educational, digital, and economic status, using a five-point Likert scale ranging from “very poor” to “excellent.” The second section focused on designed scenarios. Scenarios were developed using orthogonality and D-efficiency principles, resulting in 15 sets of choices, each containing two different options. This design was generated using a discrete choice experiment and choice modeling conducted in SAS JMP software. The final design demonstrated a D-efficiency of 0.94 with an adequate level of balance. Given the nature of the study—and the fact that each attribute could plausibly hold importance for respondents—no dominant alternative was specified or assumed in the design. Respondents were allowed to review their answers at the end of each questionnaire to ensure clarity and consistency. Additionally, to draw participants’ attention to the differences between alternatives, key distinctions within the scenarios were highlighted using bold text and contrasting colors.

Sampling

The sampling framework was defined using stratification by faculty, academic department, and field of study to ensure representation across majors and educational levels. Recruitment within each stratum was non-random and based on students’ willingness to participate and accessibility to the data-collection team, as probability-based sampling was not feasible due to the lack of a comprehensive student contact list. Sampling continued until 300 fully completed questionnaires were obtained, and only complete responses were included in the final analysis.

This sample size is consistent with methodological recommendations for discrete choice experiments, Although the Orme rule of thumb [18] suggests a minimum sample size of approximately 100 respondents, a sample size of 300 was adopted in this study to account for the diversity of disciplines and faculties represented in the study population. In health-related DCEs, design efficiency is as important as sample size for obtaining reliable estimates [19]. To assess representativeness, the demographic distribution of the study sample (N = 303) was compared with the entire university population (N = 7,899) across faculties and educational levels. The sample broadly reflected the overall student structure, particularly in major faculties such as medicine, nursing, dentistry, and pharmacy (Medicine: 42% of population vs. 30% of the sample, Nursing: 14% vs. 21%, Pharmacy: 9% vs. 9%, and Nutrition: 3% vs. 3%). Minor deviations such as higher representation in Management (9% sample vs. 5% population) are expected in voluntary participation designs. More details are presented in Appendix Table 4.

Data gathering

Data collection occurred from March 6, 2024, to December 3, 2024, in electronic form. The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (Approval code: IR.MUI.RESEARCH.REC.1399.678). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Analysis

The analysis began with a descriptive analysis to provide an overview of the data, allowing for an initial understanding of the distribution and variability among variables. This step offered insights into demographic and contextual factors and their potential influence on e-learning preferences.

The multinomial logit model, which is the most commonly used approach for analyzing DCE data [20], was employed to evaluate the influence of six attributes on e-learning preferences. This nominal logistic regression analysis aimed to determine the significance of each variable in predicting the likelihood of specific e-learning choices. Model fit and factor significance were tested using the whole model test, likelihood ratio test, and Wald test. These tests validated the significance of each factor in influencing e-learning choices. Furthermore, odds ratios were calculated to interpret the relative likelihood of selecting various e-learning options based on each factor’s level.

The analysis was conducted in SAS JMP software to robustly assess the effects of these variables on e-learning preferences.

Results

Table 1 provides descriptive statistics for the study participants, highlighting key demographic and contextual factors (More details are presented in Appendix Table 5). The average age is 22.25 years (SD = 3.38), with a majority of female participants (66.34%) and 93.07% being single. Educational levels vary, with 48.19% holding a bachelor’s degree, 47.19% in M.D. programs, and smaller proportions in master’s and Ph.D. programs. Faculty of study is diverse, including medical (30.03%), nursing (20.79%), management, and allied medical sciences, among others. Most participants live in urban areas (98.35%) and either with family (54.46%) or in dormitories (43.23%). E-learning readiness was assessed across several factors: experience in virtual learning averaged 1.28 (SD = 1.38), and digital, economic, and educational statuses were rated on a 1–5 scale, with the majority falling at level 3 or 4 across these categories. This summary captures an overview of the participants’ demographics and their preparedness for engaging in virtual learning environments.

In analyzing 9,090 choice observations derived from the DCE tasks on students’ preferences for virtual learning, key influential factors identified were e-Learning method, technology infrastructure, evaluation, and teacher characteristics. The nominal logistic model indicated that the e-Learning method and technology infrastructure had the most significant effects on students’ choices (Logworth = 27.901 and 14.038, respectively, both with p < 0.0001). Evaluation and teacher characteristics also had notable impacts, with p-values of 0.0033 and 0.0052, respectively (see Table 2). The model test results, showing a significant ChiSquare value (p < 0.0001), confirm the overall model fit.

In examining students’ preferences for virtual learning, based on the results of the nominal logistic model, as shown in Table 2, positive and statistically significant estimates indicate an increased likelihood of choosing that level compared with the reference category, whereas negative estimates reflect a decreased likelihood of selection relative to the baseline. The most influential factors were the e-Learning method, technology infrastructure, evaluation, and teacher characteristics. Ratios further emphasized blended learning substantially increased the likelihood of selection compared with synchronous learning (OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.66–2.07), while asynchronous instruction also showed a positive but smaller effect (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.09–1.34). Within the technology infrastructure domain, using enhanced tools and technologies significantly improved preference compared with the basic educational platform (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.29–1.53). Regarding teacher characteristics, higher communication competency decreased the odds of choosing the alternative anchored on time-management ability (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.71–0.90), indicating a meaningful trade-off perceived by respondents. For evaluation attributes, improved virtual exam quality was associated with lower odds relative to exam type (OR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.79–0.98), suggesting sensitivity to assessment rigor and structure. Other contrasts showed directionally consistent but statistically non-significant effects.

The Joint Factor Tests examine the significance of several demographic and contextual variables on preference patterns as presented in Table 3, with the following results: Factors such as age, sex, marital status, degree, faculty, area of residence, city, living situation, and digital status all show non-significant relationships with virtual learning preferences (p > 0.05). This suggests that these factors do not strongly differentiate preferences in this context. However, significant results are found for virtual experience (p = 0.0341), requirements (p = 0.0218), and economic status (p = 0.0173). These variables meaningfully influence students’ preferences, highlighting that experience in virtual learning, the academic or technical requirements, and economic conditions shape students’ expectations and evaluations of virtual education.

The results examine how various factors influence individuals’ preferences in virtual learning environments:

Virtual Experience & Evaluation: For students with little to no experience in virtual learning at universities, the quality of virtual exams holds greater importance, likely due to unfamiliarity and a focus on transparency and reliability. In contrast, students who are more experienced with virtual learning prioritize the type of virtual exam format, suggesting a nuanced understanding and possibly greater confidence in adapting to different exam types.

Requirements & e-Learning method: Students with the lowest academic or technical requirements show a preference for asynchronous methods, valuing the flexibility and self-paced nature of these formats. However, as students’ requirements increase, they tend to prefer blended learning, which combines both asynchronous and synchronous elements, offering a more structured yet flexible learning experience that meets higher academic or technical demands.

Economic Status & Teacher Characteristics: Students with lower economic status place a higher value on teacher characteristics like communication competency and technological skills in managing virtual content. This preference suggests that these students may rely more heavily on clear and competent virtual interactions with instructors to compensate for possible gaps in resources or external support. As economic status improves, students begin to place importance on all teacher characteristics, showing a balanced approach to valuing diverse competencies in educators.

Overall, pedagogical approach and technological robustness dominate preference formation, while experience level, academic/technical demands, and economic resources subtly but meaningfully shape the fine details of student preferences.

Discussion

The present study reaffirms blended learning as the most influential determinant of student preferences in virtual education. This finding aligns with global evidence that blended formats — thoughtfully integrating synchronous and asynchronous elements — optimize both flexibility and structured engagement [21]. Blended learning caters to diverse learning styles, supports self-pacing, and enhances retention by combining the immediacy of live interaction with the autonomy of online study. Such designs are particularly effective in skill-based programs, where digital tools mirror real-world professional environments [21].

Equally critical is the role of technology infrastructure. Robust, reliable, and user-friendly platforms are not simply delivery mechanisms; they are enablers of equitable access and sustained engagement [22]. Studies highlight that performance, availability, and security issues can erode trust and learning continuity, while scalable, secure systems enhance participation and inclusivity [22]. In contexts where infrastructure is inconsistent, disparities in access can exacerbate educational inequities [23].

Instructor competencies — particularly communication skills and digital pedagogy — also emerged as significant preference drivers. Effective virtual communication fosters social presence, clarity, and trust, which are essential for learner satisfaction [24]. Beyond interpersonal skills, digital competencies such as online instructional design, assessment literacy, and adaptive use of technology are increasingly recognised as core professional requirements [24, 25]. These competencies enable instructors to create inclusive, interactive, and responsive learning environments.

The quality of online assessments is another influential factor. Well-designed e-assessments, with transparent criteria and timely feedback, can increase engagement, reduce procrastination, and improve performance [26, 27]. Conversely, poorly designed assessments risk disengagement and perceptions of unfairness. Our findings echo prior research highlighting that clear and fair evaluation processes strengthen trust in virtual education.

Our contextual analysis revealed that virtual learning experience, academic/technical requirements, and economic status meaningfully influence preferences. Prior exposure to online learning shaped the relative emphasis on exam quality versus format, consistent with findings that familiarity promotes adaptability in digital environments [28]. Higher academic or technical demands correlate with a preference for blended formats, likely due to their structured flexibility [21]. Economic status influenced the weight placed on teacher competencies; students from lower-income backgrounds relied more on clear, supportive communication to offset potential resource gaps, while those from higher socioeconomic backgrounds valued a wider range of competencies [29, 30].

Finally, the results underscore the urgency of faculty training in online pedagogy. Many instructors still lack the digital skills and pedagogical strategies required for effective virtual teaching. Without such investment, even the most advanced platforms and pedagogical models risk underperformance.

Limitation

This study presents several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the sampling strategy was non-random and conducted within a single medical university, which may limit the generalizability of the results to other institutions or educational contexts. Additionally, the study employed a cross-sectional design, preventing causal inferences or assessment of changes in preferences over time.

As with all discrete choice experiments, the results may be subject to hypothetical bias since participants expressed preferences in a simulated choice environment rather than real decision-making settings. Moreover, the study focused on stated preferences and did not link responses to objective indicators such as academic performance or clinical competency, which could have strengthened the interpretation of educational impact. Finally, the nominal logistic model demonstrated modest explanatory power, a common characteristic in DCE studies, and results should therefore be interpreted as identifying meaningful directional and policy-relevant signals rather than precise predictions.

We analyzed the individual attribute level only. Examination of interactions or more complex models, such as mixed logit or latent-class analyses, was beyond the scope of the current study but could be explored in future research to capture potential preference heterogeneity. Although the design ensures statistical efficiency, the absence of respondent-level validity checks limits the ability to assess the stability and reliability of individual choice patterns. Future research is recommended to incorporate dominance tests, repeated choice tasks, or block-level consistency metrics to enhance internal validity and provide stronger evidence for response rationality.

Conclusion

This study identified blended learning, robust technology infrastructure, effective evaluation practices, and strong teacher competencies as the most influential factors shaping students’ preferences for virtual education during emergencies. Among these, the blended format — integrating synchronous and asynchronous modalities — emerged as the dominant preference, reflecting a need for both flexibility and structured interaction. Reliable, accessible platforms and clear, competent instructional communication further enhanced satisfaction, while high-quality, transparent online assessments reinforced trust in virtual environments.

Contextual variables, including prior virtual learning experience, academic/technical requirements, and economic status, meaningfully moderated these preferences. These findings highlight the interplay between universal quality markers in virtual education and the specific needs shaped by learners’ circumstances.

For policymakers and educational leaders, the results underscore the importance of investing in digital infrastructure, designing responsive blended learning models, and building faculty capacity in digital pedagogy and assessment design. Doing so will help ensure continuity, quality, and equity in higher education during future disruptions. Future research should explore these factors across varied cultural and institutional contexts, and track the long-term effects of blended learning adoption on both academic outcomes and student well-being. In summary, we suggested the following Implications:
