Farnesyltransferase inhibitor LB42708 disables oncogenic RAS signaling and overcomes gefitinib resistance in NSCLC via FTase α-subunit and RAS degradation
Authors: Guancong Luo, Dexuan Li, Jun Peng, Rongsheng Liu, Yajing Meng, Richard Ward, Yangyang Zhang, Xinyu Zhang, Fengxian Cui, Jiwei Wang, Xiuyu Yang, Mo Chen, Yanjun Xu, Sheng Chen, Wenyu Jiang, Le Zhang, Yurong Li, Yang Yang, Cheng Xiang, Xiaoxi Guo, Jiameng Dai, Jun Sang, Tian-Rui Xu, Su An
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
RAS mutations are among the most common oncogenic drivers in human cancers, particularly in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Direct targeting of RAS proteins remains difficult due to the lack of suitable drug-binding pockets on their surfaces. LB42708 is a potent and selective farnesyltransferase (FTase) inhibitor that disrupts RAS farnesylation and downstream signaling. This study evaluates the anti-tumor effects of LB42708 in KRAS- and HRAS-mutant NSCLC cells, as well as gefitinib-resistant PC9 (PC9GR) cells. LB42708 significantly inhibits the proliferation, migration, invasion, stemness, and clonal growth of RAS-mutant NSCLC cells, while inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest. The inhibitory effects are further validated in patient-derived organoids and xenograft models. Mechanistically, LB42708 suppresses FTase activity, reduces RAS protein levels through proteasome-dependent degradation, and induces caspase-3–mediated degradation of the shared α-subunit of FTase and geranylgeranyltransferase-1 (GGTase-1). Combination treatment with LB42708 and the AKT inhibitor AZD5363 (capivasertib) produces synergistic anti-tumor activity in RAS-mutant NSCLC. Moreover, LB42708 enhances the sensitivity of PC9GR cells to gefitinib. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that LB42708, alone or in combination with AZD5363 or gefitinib, represents as a promising therapeutic candidate for NSCLC harboring RAS mutations or resistant to tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02798-z.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common cancers worldwide, with approximately 2.2 million new diagnoses each year. It is also the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, accounting for approximately 1.8 million deaths annually [1]. Based on histology, lung cancer is classified into non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and small cell lung cancer (SCLC). NSCLC is the most common subtype, comprising about 85% of all cases [2]. It is now widely recognized that a significant proportion of NSCLC patients harbor genetic alterations that drive cancer progression. Since 2018, the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) has recommended routine testing for mutations in driver genes, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), and ROS1, in patients with metastatic NSCLC [3]. The EGFR oncogene mutation occurs in 50% of Asian patients with lung adenocarcinoma, compared to 15% in Western patients. Currently, several EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) have been approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as the standard treatment for NSCLC patients with activating EGFR mutations [4].

RAS mutations are among the most prevalent oncogenic alterations found across a wide range of human cancers. It is estimated that approximately 30% of all cancers harbor mutations in the RAS family of genes, including KRAS, HRAS, and NRAS. These mutations are particularly common in cancers of the pancreas, colon, lung, and other solid tumors, making RAS one of the most critical targets in cancer research [5, 6]. In NSCLC, KRAS mutations are the most frequently observed, occurring in approximately 25–30% of cases, with an even higher prevalence in smokers [5, 7]. The high incidence of RAS mutations in lung cancer underscores their significance in the pathogenesis of this malignancy. RAS proteins are small GTPases that cycle between an active GTP-bound state and an inactive GDP-bound state. The activation of RAS is tightly regulated by the exchange of GDP for GTP, a process facilitated by guanine nucleotide exchange factors (GEFs). Common mutations in RAS genes predominantly occur at specific hotspot regions within the coding sequence, such as codons 12, and 13. These mutations often result in a substitution of a critical amino acid that prevents the hydrolysis of GTP, locking the RAS protein in its active state. This constitutive activation of RAS signaling pathways leads to the deregulation of cell cycle progression, enhanced cell survival, and promotion of tumorigenesis. In particular, KRAS mutations, most commonly seen at codon 12, result in a GTPase-deficient RAS protein that continuously signals through key pathways, such as the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and PI3K-AKT pathways, contributing to the malignant phenotype of various cancers including lung cancer [5].

For RAS proteins to be fully activated and perform their functions, membrane localization is essential. This process is facilitated by a type of post-translational modification known as prenylation, which includes both farnesylation and geranylgeranylation. These lipid modifications are catalyzed by FTase and geranylgeranyltransferase-1 (GGTase-1), two cytosolic heterodimeric enzymes with a shared α-subunit but distinct β-subunits. FTase and GGTase-1 add a C15 farnesyl group and a C20 geranylgeranyl group to the cysteine residue at the C-terminus of RAS proteins, respectively. These proteins contain the conserved CAAX motif, in which C stands for cysteine, A represents any aliphatic amino acid, and X can be any amino acid. This prenylation process is critical for anchoring RAS proteins to cellular membranes, thereby enabling them to engage in signal transduction and activate downstream pathways that drive tumorigenesis [8].

The recent FDA approval of KRAS G12C-specific covalent inhibitors, such as sotorasib and adagrasib, represents a major breakthrough in the treatment of KRAS G12C-driven cancers, particularly NSCLC [9]. Meanwhile, next-generation tri-complex RAS inhibitors, including RMC-7977 and its analogs are under development [10–12]. Nevertheless, RAS family oncoproteins have long been considered “undruggable” due to the smooth surface of RAS proteins and the lack of small molecule binding pockets. As an alternative strategy, inhibiting FTase and geranylgeranyltransferase-1 (GGTase-1) activity provides an indirect means to regulate RAS activity, making it a promising approach for treating RAS-driven cancers [13]. Several FTase and GGTase-1 inhibitors have been developed, with tipifarnib being FDA-approved for the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma in patients harboring HRAS mutations [14, 15].

LB42708 is a selective and potent FTase inhibitor. Currently, no systematic studies have explored the anti-tumor activity of LB42708 against RAS-mutant NSCLC cells or its underlying mechanisms of action. In this study, we demonstrate that LB42708 not only inhibits FTase activity and RAS signaling, but also promotes the degradation of RAS proteins via the proteasome and the shared α-subunit of FTase and GGTase-1 via caspase-3. Consistently, LB42708 significantly suppresses the proliferation, migration, invasion, and stemness of KRAS- or HRAS-mutant NSCLC cells, and induces apoptosis. Notably, both in vitro and in vivo experiments, as well as PDO models, reveal that the AKT inhibitor AZD5363 (capivasertib) enhances the anti-tumor effects of LB42708 in a synergistic manner. Furthermore, LB42708 is capable of overcoming gefitinib resistance in PC9GR cells. These findings suggest that LB42708 represents a promising strategy for treating RAS-mutant NSCLC and sensitizing EGFR-mutant NSCLC to tyrosine kinase inhibitors.

Materials and methods

Reagent and chemicals

LB42708, tipifarnib, and gefitinib were purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE) (Shanghai, China). Z-DEVD-FMK and Heclin were obtained from AbMole BioScience (Shanghai, China). Bortezomib and AZD5363 were acquired from Topscience (Shanghai, China). The extracellular matrix components fibrinogen, fibronectin, and collagen IV were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China).

Cell lines and establishment of gefitinib-resistant PC9 (PC9GR) and osimertinib-resistant H1975 (H1975OR) cells

The cell lines NCI-H1915 and NCI-H1299 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). A549, PC9, NCI-H1975, NCI-H520, and HPAEpiC were obtained from the Kunming Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) analysis and tested negative for mycoplasma contamination. HPAEpiC was used as a normal lung cell control to assess potential drug toxicity, while the other cell lines are derived from non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and carry distinct genetic mutations. The specific genetic status of the NSCLC cell lines and the corresponding culture media used are detailed in Supplementary Table S1. All cell cultures were kept in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2.

The method used to establish PC9GR cells was described in our previous report [16]. In brief, the parental PC9 cells were initially exposed to 0.2 µM of gefitinib for approximately 7 days. Subsequently, the cells were transferred to a drug-free medium and cultured for around 14 days. After this period, cells showing exponential growth were collected and seeded in 96-well plates, with gefitinib concentrations gradually increased from 0.2 µM to 5 µM. After approximately six months, the surviving cells that were able to proliferate in the presence of gefitinib were cultured in drug-free medium for an additional 2 weeks and designated as PC9GR cells.

Using a similar stepwise selection strategy, osimertinib-resistant H1975 cells were established. Briefly, parental H1975 cells were initially treated with 0.1 µM osimertinib for approximately 5 days, followed by recovery in drug-free medium until the surviving cells resumed stable exponential growth. The selection process was repeated with stepwise escalation of osimertinib concentrations from 0.1 µM to 5.0 µM. After approximately six months of continuous drug exposure, the cells capable of sustained proliferation under osimertinib treatment were considered resistant. The resulting cells were subsequently maintained in drug-free medium and designated as H1975OR. Throughout the selection process, parental NCI-H1975 cells were cultured in parallel under drug-free conditions as controls.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NSCLC cells or HPAEpiC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. The cells were then treated with increasing concentrations of LB42708 (ranging from 0.1 to 80 µM) for 60 h. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1.5 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a PheraStar FS multi-well plate reader (BMG Labtech, Offenburg, Germany). IC50 values for LB42708 were calculated using GraphPad PRISM software (version 9.4.1).

Additionally, for A549 and NCI-H1915 cells, different concentrations of LB42708 (0, 5, 10, and 20 µM) were applied for various time points (0, 24, 48, and 72 h) to investigate the impact of both concentration and treatment duration on the proliferation inhibition of these cells.

Colony formation assay

To evaluate colony formation following drug treatment, 800 logarithmic-phase cells were seeded into 12-well plates and treated with LB42708 at concentrations of 0, 0.5, 1, and 5 µM for NCI-H1915 cells, or 0, 5, 10, and 20 µM for A549 cells. The cells were incubated for 14 days, with the culture medium replaced every three days. When visible colonies formed, the medium was removed, and the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After fixation, the colonies were washed with PBS and stained with crystal violet for 15 min. Colony counting was performed under an inverted microscope using ImageJ software (NIH, NY, USA). Technical triplicates were carried out for each condition.

EdU cell proliferation assay

A549 and NCI-H1915 cells were seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 103 cells per well) and treated with different concentrations of LB42708 (0, 5, 10, 20 µM) for 48 h. Following treatment, 10 µM of 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) (C0075L, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS for 15 min at room temperature. Once the fixative was removed, cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 15 min. After three washes with PBS, 50 µL of a click reaction mixture containing Azide 555 was added to each well, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. DAPI reagent (C1005, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was then applied for nuclear staining. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the proportion of EdU-positive cells was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, NY, USA).

Sphere formation assay

To investigate the effect of LB42708 on lung cancer stem cells, sphere formation assays were performed. Single-cell suspensions of cell lines (1 × 104 cells/mL) were cultured in 6-well ultra-low attachment plates (Corning, 3473) in DMEM/F12 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) containing B-27 supplements(1:50, Invitrogen), 20 ng/mL recombinant human epidermal growth factor (rhEGF) (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF) (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), 4 µg/mL insulin (MCE, Shanghai, China) and 1% methylcellulose (MCE, Shanghai, China). The cells were co-cultured with or without the indicated concentrations of LB42708, starting 48 h after seeding. The medium was refreshed every 3 days. After approximately 2 weeks of incubation with LB42708, cell spheres with diameters ≥ 50 μm were counted using an inverted microscope.

Wound healing assay

The migratory behavior of lung cancer cells in response to LB42708 was assessed using a wound healing assay with a culture-insert (ibidi GmbH, Martinsried, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A 200 µL cell suspension (1.5 × 105 cells/mL) was seeded into the Culture-Insert of each well and allowed to form a confluent monolayer at 37 °C in 5% CO₂ for 24 h. The insert was carefully removed using sterile tweezers, and the cells were washed twice with PBS to eliminate any detached cells. Fresh medium containing mitomycin was then added. Lung cancer cells were treated with LB42708 at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, or 20 µM. Images were captured at 0, 24, and 48–72 h to evaluate cell migration into the wound using a light microscope.

Transwell migration and matrigel invasion analysis

Migration and invasion assays were performed using transwell chambers (Corning Costar, Cambridge, Massachusetts, pore size 8 μm). Lung cancer cells were pretreated with LB42708 (0, 5, 10, 20 µM) for 24 h. For the invasion assay, Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) was diluted with culture medium at a 1:3 ratio and 70 µL of the solution was added to the upper chamber, where it was allowed to solidify at 37 °C for 2 h. Migration and invasion assays were conducted using uncoated transwell chambers and Matrigel-coated chambers, respectively. Afterward, lung cancer cells (2 × 104 per well) were seeded in the upper chamber in serum-free medium, and complete medium was placed in the lower compartment to act as a chemoattractant. Following the designated incubation period, the inserts were washed 3 times with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Invading cells on the lower surface of the transwell insert were observed under a light microscope and counted in 5 randomly selected fields. The assays were conducted in triplicate.

Cell adhesion assay

Lung cancer cells were exposed to LB42708 at concentrations of 0, 5, 10, and 20 µM for 24 h. Following treatment, 5 × 104 cells in 100 µL of serum-free medium were seeded onto 96-well plates pre-coated with 10 µg/mL fibrinogen, fibronectin, or collagen IV, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the medium was removed, and non-adherent cells were carefully washed away by rinsing the wells three times with PBS. The remaining attached cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. Excess crystal violet was washed off with PBS, and 100 µL of acetic acid was added to each well and gently shaken for 10 min to dissolve the violet crystals. Absorbance was then measured at 590 nm using a Microplate Reader. The cellular adhesion measurements were normalized to the control cells, which were cultured on fibrinogen-coated plates without LB42708 treatment, and expressed as relative absorbance.

Lung cancer organoid establishment and cell viability assays

Fresh lung cancer tissue samples were obtained from patients who underwent curative surgical resection at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, the First People’s Hospital of Yunnan Province. All patients had their diagnoses confirmed by experienced pathologists, and none had received adjuvant chemotherapy or radiation therapy prior to surgery. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kunming University of Science and Technology (protocol code KMUST-MEC-2024-070, issued on April 3, 2024) and adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. According to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines, tumor samples from NSCLC patients involved in this study were tested for mutations, fusion or amplification in 9 genes closely associated with the development and progression of lung cancer, and also related to targeted therapies. These genes include EGFR, KRAS, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, MET, RET, HER2, and NTRK [17]. The clinicopathologic characteristics and gene mutations of the 8 patients are presented in Supplementary Table S2.

The lung cancer organoids were established according to the method of Kim et al. with minor modifications [18]. Briefly, resected tumor specimens were collected and carefully dissected into approximately 1 mm3 pieces using ophthalmic scissors. These fragments were immediately placed in cold Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) containing 100 U/ml Antibiotic-Antimycotic (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and transported to the lab on ice within 1 h of resection. After three washes with cold HBSS supplemented with Antibiotic-Antimycotic, the samples were further sectioned using sterile blades. The fragments were then incubated in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 0.001% DNase (Sigma-Aldrich Shanghai Trading Co., Ltd, China), 1 mg/ml collagenase/dispase (Roche, IN, USA), and 100 U/ml Antibiotic-Antimycotic at 37 °C for 3 h, with gentle agitation and occasional resuspension. After incubation, the suspensions were carefully dispersed by pipetting and filtered through 70-µm cell strainers (BD Falcon, CA, USA). The filtered cells were then centrifuged at 112 × g for 3 min, and the resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of minimum basal medium (MBM), which consisted of serum-free DMEM/F12 supplemented with 20 ng/mL of bFGF (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), 50 ng/mL of rhEGF (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), N2 Supplement (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), B27 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), 10 µM ROCK inhibitor Y-27,632 (MCE, Shanghai, China), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Subsequently, 200 µL of Matrigel (MCE, Shanghai, China) was mixed with 100 µL of the cell suspension, and the mixture was allowed to solidify on pre-warmed 6-well plates at 37 °C for 30 min. Once the Matrigel had gelled, 3 mL of MBM was added to each well. The medium was refreshed every four days.

Organoids were passaged every two weeks at a 1:2 ratio. For organoid passaging, the organoids were collected by incubating in cold PBS for 1 h at 4°C, then dissociated using 1× TrypLe Express (Gibco). The dissociated organoids were resuspended in a mixture of MBM and Matrigel at a 1:3 ratio and transferred to a Petri dish, followed by the addition of medium after the gel had solidified. For cell viability assays, organoids were dissociated into single cells, counted, and seeded in a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. The organoids were then cultured for 10–14 days until they reached an appropriate size (approximately 100–200 μm in diameter). Once sufficiently developed, they were treated with various concentrations of LB42708 for 5 days, followed by cell viability assessment using the CellTiter-Glo® 3D luminescent ATP assay (Promega BioSciences Inc., San Luis Obispo, CA). Additionally, to evaluate the combinatorial effects on organoid viability, organoids were treated with 15 µM LB42708, 15 µM AZD5363, or their combination.

Western blot analysis

To assess protein levels, western blotting was performed. Cells were first washed with PBS, followed by the addition of an appropriate volume of RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein extraction was carried out on ice for 30 min. The protein concentration of the cell lysates was determined using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein from the cell lysates were loaded onto an SDS-PAGE gel for separation, then transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. Following this, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system (GeneGnome, Syngene, UK). The specific antibodies used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Active RAS determination by Raf-1 RBD pulldown assay

RAS activity was assessed using a Raf-1 RBD Agarose Beads Pull-Down and Detection Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). A549 cells were treated with LB42708 (20 µM) or left untreated for 48 h. Cells were then scraped, lysed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. For the pull-down assay, 100 µL of supernatant from LB42708-treated cells was incubated with 25 µL Raf-1 RBD agarose beads. As controls, 100 µL of supernatant from untreated cells was supplemented with either GppNHp (final concentration 1 mM) or GDP (final concentration 1 mM) as positive and negative controls, respectively, followed by incubation with 25 µL Raf-1 RBD agarose beads. All samples were incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with constant rocking. After incubation, the beads were pelleted and washed three times, resuspended in 25 µL 1 × SDS-PAGE sample buffer, and boiled at 95 °C for 5 min. The samples were then subjected to Western blotting to detect RAS-GTP levels.

Combination study with AZD5363, gefitinib or osimertinib

To evaluate the combination effects of LB42708 and AZD5363 on A549 and NCI-H1915 cells, as well as LB42708 and gefitinib on PC9GR cells, the doses of each drug were determined based on their IC50 values. The appropriate drug combination ratios were then established according to these IC50 values. The degree of synergy and the combination index (CI) were calculated using the Chou-Talalay method with CompuSyn software (Biosoft, Ferguson, MO, USA) [19]. CI values of < 1, =1, and > 1 were indicative of synergism, additivity, and antagonism, respectively.

To identify the synergistic concentration ranges of LB42708 in combination with osimertinib in H1975OR cells, drug interactions were evaluated using the Zero Interaction Potency (ZIP) model implemented in the SynergyFinder platform (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi). Briefly, H1975OR cells were treated with a dose–response matrix of LB42708 and osimertinib at multiple concentrations, and cell viability was assessed after drug exposure. The expected responses under the assumption of no drug interaction were calculated using the ZIP model and compared with the observed responses. Synergy scores were generated across the entire dose–dose matrix, where positive and negative ZIP scores indicated synergistic and antagonistic interactions, respectively. Based on the identified synergistic concentration regions, selected drug combinations were subsequently subjected to combination index (CI) analysis using CompuSyn software.

Cell cycle analysis

Cells were treated with LB42708, AZD5363, gefitinib, or their combinations for 48 h, then digested with pancreatic enzymes and fixed overnight at 4 °C in 75% ethanol. After two washes with PBS, cells were incubated with 100 µg/mL RNase solution and 50 µg/mL propidium iodide (PI) for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Cell cycle analysis was conducted using the Agilent NovoCyte Flow Cytometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

Flow cytometry for quantification of apoptosis

To assess the effect of drugs on apoptosis, cells were stained using a Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGenBioTech, Nanjing, China). NSCLC cells were plated in 6-well plates (2 × 105 cells per well) and cultured for 24 h. After the specified treatments, both floating and adherent cells were collected. Floating cells in the culture supernatant were first harvested, and adherent cells were subsequently collected by trypsin digestion. The cells were washed twice with PBS, and then incubated for 10 min in a buffer solution containing Annexin V-FITC and PI. Apoptosis analysis was performed by flow cytometry (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA).

Human lung cancer xenograft in nude mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of Yunnan University (protocol code YNU20241147, issued on February 7, 2024), and all experiments were performed in accordance with the approved protocol and other relevant guidelines and regulations. Male BALB/c nude mice, aged 4–5 weeks and weighing 13–17 g, were purchased from the Animal Research and Resource Center at Yunnan University (Kunming, Yunnan). The mice were housed at 25 °C under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle, with free access to a standard rodent diet and water. Before inoculation, NCI-H195 cells were washed twice, counted, and approximately 5 × 106 cells were resuspended in PBS containing 50% Corning Matrigel Matrix (Corning, New York, USA), then subcutaneously injected into the left flank of each mouse. Mice were randomly divided into four groups: vehicle (DMSO), LB42708, AZD5363, and LB42708 + AZD5363. Treatment began 5 days post-inoculation when tumors reached a size of 100–150 mm³. The experimental groups were treated daily with intraperitoneal (i.p.) injections of LB42708 (25 mg/kg), AZD5363 (25 mg/kg), or LB42708 + AZD5363 (25 + 25 mg/kg) for 15 consecutive days, while the control group received an equal volume of vehicle. Mice were weighed daily throughout the treatment period, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: 0.5 × length × (width)2. One day following the last treatment, the mice were sacrificed, and tumors were excised, weighed, and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining along with immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. The maximal permitted tumor size or burden was approved by our ethics committee and was not exceeded during the study.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining and Immunohistochemical (IHC)

Lung cancer tissues from NSCLC patients, patient-derived organoids, and xenograft tumors in nude mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded for H&E and IHC staining. The organoids or tissue sections were first deparaffinized using xylene, followed by a graded ethanol dehydration series and final hydration in distilled water. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval was performed by immersing the slides in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and boiling them for 30 min. IHC was conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, and non-specific binding was prevented using 5% normal goat serum in TBST. The sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table S3. Following primary antibody incubation, the slides were treated with Signalstain Boost IHC detection reagent (HRP, rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology, Cambridge, USA) for 30 min at room temperature, and signals were developed using the Signalstain DAB substrate kit (Cell Signaling Technology, Cambridge, USA). Counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. Apoptotic cells in tumor tissues were detected by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay following the manufacturer’s instructions (KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China). Images were captured using an Olympus microscope and analyzed using the ImageJ software.

Bioinformatics analysis

The expression data and clinical data of LUAD and LUSC were obtained from the TCGA database (http://cancergenome.nih.gov/), Kaplan–Meier survival analyses of overall survival and disease-free survival in LUAD and LUSC patients from TCGA, stratified by median FNTA expression, were performed using GEPIA2 (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/). The expression data of GSE229070, GSE229070, GSE199627 and GSE249721 were retrieved from GEO datasets (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1 software. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistically significant differences between groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA or an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

FTase inhibitor LB42708 inhibits the proliferation and stemness of RAS-mutant and other types of NSCLC cells

Farnesylation, a post-translational modification catalyzed by FTase, is essential for the membrane localization and activation of RAS. The α subunit of FTase is encoded by the FNTA gene. To investigate the prognostic value of FNTA expression in lung cancer, we performed Kaplan–Meier survival analyses using GEPIA2 online tool based on TCGA and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) datasets. As shown in Fig. S1, the expression level of FNTA is significantly higher in both LUAD and LUSC tissues than in normal lung tissues (Fig. S1A and D). In addition, LUAD patients with high FNTA expression exhibited significantly shorter overall survival (OS) (HR = 1.5, P = 0.0041) (Fig. S1B) and disease-free survival (DFS) (HR = 1.9, P = 3.7e–05) (Fig. S1C) compared with those with low FNTA expression. In contrast, FNTA expression showed no significant association with OS (Fig. S1E) or DFS (Fig. S1F) in LUSC patients. These findings suggest that elevated FNTA expression is specifically correlated with poor prognosis in LUAD but not in LUSC, which may be attributed to the fact that RAS mutations predominantly occur in LUAD rather than in LUSC.

LB42708 is an orally active farnesyltransferase (FTase) inhibitor (FTI) with a pyrrole-based structure (Fig. 1A). First, we determined the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of LB42708 in RAS-mutant NSCLC cells and several other types of NSCLC cells, including KRAS-mutant A549, HRAS-mutant NCI-H1915, NRAS-mutant NCI-H1299, EGFR-mutant PC9, and p53-mutant NCI-H520 cells. These cells were treated with different concentrations of LB42708 for 60 h. The results showed that LB42708 significantly inhibited the proliferation of NSCLC cells, with IC50 values of 4.58, 6.08, 7.99, 1.07, and 11.33 µM for NCI-H1915 (Fig. 1B and C), A549 (Fig. 1D and E), NCI-H1299 (Fig. 1F), NCI-H520 (Fig. 1G), and PC9 (Fig. 1H), respectively. Importantly, LB42708 exhibited minimal cytotoxicity in normal human pulmonary alveolar epithelial cells (HPAEpiC), with an IC50 value greater than 80 µM (Fig. 1I), indicating a favorable therapeutic window. Notably, the IC50 values of LB42708 for NCI-H1915 and A549 were nearly identical to that of tipifarnib, an FDA-approved FTase inhibitor for treating head and neck squamous cell carcinoma in patients with HRAS mutations. Moreover, under different treatment time conditions, LB42708 consistently inhibited the proliferation of NCI-H1915 and A549 cells in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1C and E).

LB42708 effectively inhibits the migration, invasion, and adhesion of NSCLC cells

Tumor progression is marked by increased cell motility, invasiveness, and adhesion. To evaluate the inhibitory effects of LB42708 on metastasis, we examined cell migration, invasion, and adhesion under LB42708 treatment using wound healing, transwell, and cell adhesion assays. As shown in Fig. 2A-D, LB42708 significantly inhibited cell migration in a dose-dependent manner in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells. Moreover, treatment with 20 µM LB42708 for 48 h led to approximately a 50% reduction in wound closure compared to the control group (Fig. 2A and C). In NCI-H1915 cells, a significant inhibitory effect was observed at 72 h, whereas no noticeable change was detected at 24 h (Fig. 2B and D). The inhibitory effects of LB42708 on the migration and invasion of A549 and NCI-H1915 cells were further assessed. Results from transwell and matrigel transwell assays consistently demonstrated that LB42708 effectively suppressed cell migration and invasion, respectively (Fig. 2E-J). Compared to the control group, A549 cells treated with the highest concentration of LB42708 (20 µM) exhibited a substantial reduction in the number of migrated cells and invasive area by approximately 90% and 77%, respectively (Fig. 2E-G). Similarly, in NCI-H1915 cells, these reductions reached 59% and 57%, respectively (Fig. 2H-J). Given the essential role of cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion in cancer cell migration and spreading, we investigated the inhibitory effects of LB42708 on A549 and NCI-H1915 cell adhesion to three key ECM substrates: fibrinogen, fibronectin, and collagen IV. The compound exhibited dose-dependent suppression of cellular adhesion to all three targets, with significant inhibition observed in both cell lines (Fig. 2K and L).

LB42708 promotes the degradation of FTase α-subunit via caspase-3 and that of RAS via the proteasome

LB42708 is known for its FTase inhibitory activity. In this study, we further investigated its effect on FTase protein levels. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, A549 and NCI-H1915 cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of LB42708 or 20 µM etoposide for 48 h, LB42708 reduced the FTase α-subunit protein levels in a dose-dependent manner, while etoposide treatment at 20 µM also led to a reduction in FTase α-subunit levels. However, at the tested concentration (20 µM), another FTase inhibitor, tipifarnib, activated caspase-3 but failed to reduce FTase α-subunit levels in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells. Notably, its ability to activate caspase-3 was markedly weaker than that of LB42708 and etoposide (Fig. S2). Since etoposide is a potent apoptosis inducer known to activate caspase-3 and other cysteine-aspartic proteases, we hypothesized that LB42708 and etoposide might promote FTase α-subunit degradation through activated caspase-3. Consistent with this hypothesis, pre-treatment with the caspase-3 inhibitor Z-DEVD-FMK (10 µM) effectively rescued the LB42708-induced degradation of the FTase α-subunit in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells (Fig. 3C and D). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that LB42708 not only directly inhibits FTase activity but also promotes the degradation of the FTase α-subunit through activated caspase-3, thereby reducing its protein levels.

The farnesylation of RAS enables its membrane localization, which may influence its stability. Therefore, we further examined the effect of LB42708 on RAS protein levels. After treatment of A549 and NCI-H1915 cells with 5 to 20 µM of LB42708 for 48 h, we observed a significant reduction in KRAS and pan-RAS protein levels in A549 cells (Fig. 3E), and HRAS and pan-RAS protein levels in NCI-H1915 cells (Fig. 3F). LB42708 inhibits cell proliferation via the MAPK signaling pathway and exhibits synergistic antitumor effects with AZD5363 in RAS-mutant NSCLC cells

The MAPK and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways are the two main downstream pathways of RAS oncoproteins. Therefore, we investigated the effects of LB42708 on these two pathways to explore its potential mechanisms in inhibiting the proliferation of RAS-mutant NSCLC cells. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, treatment of A549 and NCI-H1915 cells with LB42708 (5–20 µM) for 48 h significantly decreased the phosphorylation of ERK and the protein level of its downstream effector, c-Fos. Tipifarnib did not significantly affect ERK phosphorylation in A549 cells and even increased ERK phosphorylation in NCI-H1915 cells. Nevertheless, it suppressed c-Fos expression in both cell lines. Notably, it is unexpected that both LB42708 and tipifarnib increased the phosphorylation of AKT, although there was no significant effect on the activation of the downstream signaling molecule GSK-3β.

Given that LB42708 significantly increases AKT phosphorylation, the next step was to investigate whether the AKT inhibitor such as AZD5363 could enhance the cytotoxicity of LB42708 in RAS-mutant NSCLC cells. By performing cell viability assays, we assessed the cell fraction affected (Fa) by the treatment of LB42708 or AZD5363 alone for 60 h. Both A549 (Fig. 4C) and NCI-H1915 (Fig. 4F) cells exhibited a dose-dependent reduction in viability across a wide range of LB42708 or AZD5363 concentrations. Based on the IC50 values obtained from single-agent treatments, the combination effects of LB42708 and AZD5363 were further evaluated at fixed molar ratios of 2:5, 1:1, and 5:2 for A549 cells (Fig. 4D and E), and 1:3, 1:1, and 3:1 for NCI-H1915 cells (Fig. 4G and H), and the combination index (CI) values were calculated using the CompuSyn software (Supplementary Table S4). All CI values for both A549 and NCI-H1915 cells were less than 1, indicating a synergistic effect between the two compounds. The 1:1 combination ratio of LB42708 and AZD5363 showed the lowest CI value among all ratios tested and was therefore used in subsequent experiments (Fig. 4E and H, Supplementary Table S4). Furthermore, we investigated the effects of LB42708 and AZD5363 on the phosphorylation of ERK and AKT, as well as the expression levels and activation of their downstream effectors. As shown in Fig. 4I and J, cells were treated with LB42708 or AZD5363 alone, or in combination, for 48 h. Both LB42708 and the combination of LB42708 with AZD5363 effectively reduced ERK phosphorylation. The combination treatment showed a more pronounced effect in decreasing ERK phosphorylation compared to either treatment alone. However, neither LB42708, AZD5363, nor their combination could inhibit AKT phosphorylation. In fact, they increased AKT phosphorylation. Despite this, both AZD5363 and its combination with LB42708 effectively inhibited the phosphorylation of the AKT downstream effector GSK-3β, with the combined treatment showing a more significant inhibitory effect.

Global transcriptomic alterations and enrichment of cell cycle and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways in sotorasib-resistant KRAS-mutant NSCLC cells

To investigate the mechanisms underlying resistance of KRAS-mutant NSCLC to RAS activity inhibitors, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analyses on parental NCI-H358 versus sotorasib-resistant NCI-H358 cells, and on parental NCI-H23 versus sotorasib-resistant NCI-H23 cells, using datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, accession number GSE229070). Genes with more than a two-fold change in expression were selected for further analysis.

In NCI-H358 cells, volcano plot analysis revealed 3,274 upregulated and 2,874 downregulated genes in sotorasib-resistant cells compared with parental cells (Fig. S3A). Hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis further demonstrated distinct and reproducible expression profiles, with the six samples clearly separated according to treatment status (Fig. S3B). Similarly, in NCI-H23 cells, 2,843 genes were upregulated and 2,448 genes were downregulated in resistant cells relative to parental cells (Fig. S4A). Heatmap analysis confirmed clear clustering between parental and resistant groups, reflecting consistent transcriptomic reprogramming across biological replicates (Fig. S4B). Collectively, these results indicate substantial and reproducible transcriptomic alterations associated with the acquisition of sotorasib resistance in KRAS-mutant NSCLC cells.

GO enrichment analysis showed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mainly enriched in cell adhesion and cell division (Fig. S3C and S4C). KEGG pathway analysis indicated that DNA replication, mismatch repair, and cell cycle may contribute to sotorasib resistance (Fig. S3D and S4D). GSEA further revealed significant enrichment of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway in sotorasib-resistant NCI-H358 and NCI-H23 cells (Fig. S3E and S4E), which is consistent with our experimental observation that LB42708 increases AKT phosphorylation in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells (Fig. 4A and B), thereby providing a mechanistic rationale for the subsequent evaluation of the synergistic effect between LB42708 and the AKT inhibitor AZD5363 (Fig. 4C-J).

The combination of LB42708 and AZD5363 increases cell cycle arrest and induces apoptosis

The impact of LB42708 and AZD5363, both individually and in combination, on the cell cycle was evaluated in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells. In A549 cells, combination treatment resulted in a higher proportion of cells arrested at the G2/M phase (40.27%) compared to DMSO (8.15%), LB42708 (10.53%), and AZD5363 (14.08%). Additionally, the combination treatment caused a significant delay in the G1 and S phases when compared to both DMSO and the monotherapy groups (Fig. 5A). However, in NCI-H1915 cells, whether LB42708 and AZD5363 were used individually or in combination, both treatments induced cell cycle arrest at the G1 phase, with a significant reduction in the number of cells in the S and G2/M phases. The effect was more pronounced with the combination treatment compared to the single-agent treatments (Fig. 5B). To investigate the effects of LB42708 and its combination with AZD5363 on cell death induction, apoptosis was analyzed using annexin V FITC/PI double staining. As shown in Fig. 5C and D, treatment of A549 and NCI-H1915 cells with LB42708 and AZD5363 alone, or their combination for 48 h, effectively induced apoptosis, with the combination showing a more pronounced effect. Cleavage of caspase-3 and caspase-7 was significantly increased in the combination treatment compared to LB42708 or AZD5363 alone in both A549 and NCI-H1915 cells, further confirming the enhanced induction of apoptosis in these cells (Fig. 5E and F).

Differential gene expression and MAPK pathway activation in gefitinib- or osimertinib-resistant EGFR-mutant NSCLC cells

To explore the molecular basis of resistance to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (EGFR-TKIs) in EGFR-mutant NSCLC, we analyzed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets comparing parental HCC827 cells with either gefitinib-resistant (GSE199627) or osimertinib-resistant (GSE249721) counterparts. Genes exhibiting greater than two-fold changes in expression were subjected to further investigation.

In gefitinib-resistant HCC827 cells, volcano plot analysis identified 1,709 upregulated and 1,510 downregulated genes relative to parental cells (Fig. S5A). Hierarchical clustering heatmaps revealed distinct and reproducible transcriptomic profiles, with clear segregation of resistant and parental samples (Fig. S5B). Likewise, osimertinib-resistant HCC827 cells displayed 2,062 upregulated and 2,541 downregulated genes (Fig. S6A), and heatmap analysis confirmed robust clustering between parental and resistant groups (Fig. S6B). These findings highlight pronounced and reproducible transcriptomic reprogramming associated with acquired EGFR-TKI resistance in HCC827 cells.

GO enrichment analysis indicated that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched in processes related to cell adhesion and cell migration (Fig. S5C and S6C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways such as “EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance” and “ABC transporters”, suggesting their potential involvement in gefitinib and osimertinib resistance (Fig. S5D and S6D). Moreover, GSEA demonstrated marked activation of the MAPK signaling pathway in both gefitinib- and osimertinib-resistant HCC827 cells (Fig. S5E and S6E), providing a mechanistic rationale for evaluating the synergistic effects of LB42708 with gefitinib or osimertinib, as LB42708 effectively suppresses ERK phosphorylation and MAPK pathway activation.

LB42708 enhances the sensitivity of PC9GR cells to gefitinib and H1975OR cells to osimertinib

The acquisition of the secondary EGFR T790M mutation following first- and second-generation EGFR-TKI treatment represents the predominant mechanism of gefitinib resistance and can lead to reactivation of the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway, this mutation can be effectively overcome by the third-generation EGFR-TKI osimertinib. In addition, RAS mutations or RAS-MAPK pathway reactivation also contribute to gefitinib resistance and have been implicated in the development of resistance to osimertinib. Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether LB42708 could modulate the sensitivity of PC9GR cells to gefitinib and H1975OR cells to osimertinib. According to the method described in our previous report [20], we successfully developed a gefitinib-resistant PC9 cell line (PC9GR) from the originally gefitinib-sensitive PC9 cells after approximately six months of selective pressure with gefitinib. As illustrated in Fig. 6A and B, the IC50 values of gefitinib for parental PC9 and PC9GR cells are 67.7 nM and 2.9 µM, respectively, resulting in a resistance index (the ratio of IC50 for the resistant cell line to the IC50 for the parental cell line) of approximately 43. Generally, a resistance index greater than 5 is considered indicative of the successful establishment of a drug-resistant cell line. However, PC9GR cells did not develop resistance to osimertinib. To investigate the differential effects of gefitinib on signaling pathways in parental PC9 and resistant PC9GR cells, both cell lines were treated with 100 nM gefitinib for 12, 24, 48, and 72 h. As shown in Fig. 6C and D, gefitinib markedly inhibited the phosphorylation of AKT and almost completely suppressed the phosphorylation of ERK in PC9 cells at all examined time points. In contrast, in PC9GR cells, gefitinib only partially inhibited AKT phosphorylation and had minimal effect on ERK phosphorylation throughout the treatment period. These findings suggest that the acquired resistance in PC9GR cells is associated with sustained activation of downstream signaling pathways, particularly the MAPK pathway.

To evaluate the effect of LB42708 on the sensitivity of PC9GR cells to gefitinib, we first assessed the cell fraction affected (Fa) by treatment with gefitinib or LB42708 alone for 60 h (Fig. 6E). Based on the IC50 values of gefitinib and LB42708 in PC9GR cells, the combination treatment was designed at fixed molar ratios of 5:1, 1:1, and 1:5, with each ratio containing a broad range of LB42708 and gefitinib concentrations (Fig. 6F). To assess the combined effects of LB42708 and gefitinib, combination index (CI) values were calculated based on cell viability assay following treatment with fixed molar ratios of the two drugs (Fig. 6G, Supplementary Table S5). As shown in Fig. 6G, treatment for 60 h resulted in log(CI) values < 0 across a broad range of fraction affected (Fa) values, regardless of the molar ratio applied, indicating a synergistic interaction between LB42708 and gefitinib. To investigate the molecular alterations in PC9GR cells following combined treatment with gefitinib and LB42708, we analyzed the expression and phosphorylation levels of key proteins involved in the MAPK and AKT signaling pathways. As shown in Fig. 6H, treatment with LB42708 (1 or 5 µM) alone unexpectedly increased the phosphorylation of AKT compared with the control group (Fig. 6I). While 1 µM LB42708 had minimal effect on ERK phosphorylation, 5 µM induced a moderate reduction (Fig. 6H and K). Similarly, gefitinib alone exhibited only partial inhibitory effects on the phosphorylation of AKT, GSK3β, and ERK. Notably, the combination of 5 µM gefitinib and 5 µM LB42708 led to a marked and simultaneous decrease in p-AKT, p-GSK3β, and p-ERK levels (Fig. 6H-K).

Building upon these findings, we next assessed whether this combination treatment could enhance apoptosis in PC9GR cells. As illustrated in Fig. 6L, both gefitinib and LB42708 alone induced moderate levels of apoptosis after 48 h of treatment, with apoptotic rates of 16.06% and 6.78%, respectively. However, the combinatorial treatment led to a significantly greater apoptotic response, reaching 36.71%. This enhanced effect was further validated by immunoblotting, which revealed a pronounced increase in the cleavage of caspase-3 and caspase-7 in the combination group compared to either monotherapy (Fig. 6M). These results suggest that LB42708 sensitizes PC9GR cells to gefitinib by not only enhancing suppression of survival signaling pathways but also by promoting activation of the apoptotic machinery.

In light of the ability of LB42708 to restore gefitinib sensitivity in PC9GR cells, we further examined whether LB42708 could overcome acquired resistance to osimertinib in H1975OR cells and explored the underlying molecular mechanisms. An osimertinib-resistant derivative of the NCI-H1975 cell line (H1975OR) was generated through prolonged exposure to increasing concentrations of osimertinib for approximately six months. As shown in Fig. S7A, H1975OR cells exhibited a marked reduction in sensitivity to osimertinib, with IC₅₀ values of 0.19 µM in parental NCI-H1975 cells and 21.53 µM in H1975OR cells, corresponding to an approximately 113-fold increase in resistance.

Given that H1975OR cells displayed limited sensitivity to either LB42708 or osimertinib alone (Fig. S7B and C), we next employed the ZIP model implemented in SynergyFinder to define the concentration ranges at which the combination of LB42708 and osimertinib might exert synergistic effects. The dose-response matrix, two-dimensional synergy map, and three-dimensional synergy map are shown in Fig. S7D, E, and F, respectively. As shown in Fig. S7D, the dose-response matrix indicated that the combination of LB42708 and osimertinib produced enhanced growth inhibition of H1975OR cells compared with either agent alone, predominantly at higher concentrations of LB42708. Consistent with this observation, the corresponding two-dimensional ZIP synergy map (Fig. S7E) revealed positive synergy scores mainly clustered in regions with elevated LB42708 concentrations. The three-dimensional synergy map (Fig. S7F) further visualized these regions, confirming a modest synergistic tendency between LB42708 and osimertinib that was largely confined to higher LB42708 dose ranges. While the overall ZIP synergy score was 3.63 and the most synergistic area score was 7.15, local δ-scores within specific narrow concentration ranges (e.g., LB42708 at 25–50 µM) exceeded 10 or even 20 (Fig. S7F). Accordingly, high concentrations of LB42708 (25 and 50 µM) were combined with a range of osimertinib concentrations in a non-constant ratio design, and the synergistic effects and corresponding CI values were calculated using CompuSyn software. The resulting dose-effect relationships are presented in Fig. S7G, which shows the Dose-Fa curves, while Fig. S7H depicts the distribution of fraction affected (Fa) values in relation to Log (CI). The corresponding combination doses, effect levels, and calculated CI values are summarized in Table S6. Together, these data indicate that the combination resulted in limited synergy within a narrow concentration range, primarily at high concentrations of LB42708, while exhibiting additive to weakly synergistic effects overall in suppressing H1975OR cell growth.

Building on these findings, we next investigated the potential mechanisms by which LB42708 enhances the sensitivity of H1975OR cells to osimertinib. As shown in Fig. S7I and J, H1975OR cells were treated for 48 h with drug concentrations that produced synergistic effects. Osimertinib alone (1 or 10 µM) effectively suppressed the phosphorylation of EGFR, ERK, and AKT, but was accompanied by a marked increase in total EGFR protein levels. In contrast, LB42708 alone (25 or 50 µM) did not inhibit the phosphorylation of EGFR, ERK, or AKT. However, it significantly attenuated the osimertinib-induced upregulation of total EGFR.

We further examined whether a similar mechanism operated in PC9GR cells. As shown in Fig. S7K-M, treatment of PC9GR cells for 48 h with synergistic concentrations revealed that gefitinib (1 or 5 µM) moderately inhibited EGFR phosphorylation, and this effect was not further enhanced by LB42708. Similar to the effect of osimertinib in H1975OR cells, gefitinib increased total EGFR levels in PC9GR cells. However, unlike in H1975OR cells, LB42708 did not counteract the gefitinib-induced EGFR upregulation. Nevertheless, LB42708 markedly potentiated the inhibitory effects of gefitinib on ERK and GSK3β phosphorylation and significantly enhanced apoptosis in PC9GR cells (Fig. 6H-M).

Collectively, these results indicate that LB42708 enhances sensitivity to osimertinib in H1975OR cells and to gefitinib in PC9GR cells through distinct molecular mechanisms.

LB42708 inhibits the growth of NSCLC patient-derived organoids

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs), grown in vitro within a 3D environment, faithfully mimic the structural organization and preserve the heterogeneity of primary tumors, making them valuable models for assessing drug efficacy. To establish PDOs, we obtained tumor tissue samples from 8 NSCLC patients, among whom pathological examination identified 2 cases of lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) and 6 cases of lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD). According to the NCCN guidelines, all patients underwent genetic testing for mutations associated with lung cancer progression and targeted therapy. Following the method described by Kim et al. [18] (Fig. 7A), we generated 5 PDO lines with a success rate of 62.5%. Notably, PDO4 and PDO6 were classified as LUAD and harbored KRAS G12C and KRAS G12V mutations, respectively (Fig. S8). Detailed clinicopathologic characteristics, genetic mutations, and the organoid formation status of NSCLC tissues are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. To compare the morphological and histological characteristics of PDOs with their corresponding tumor tissues, we conducted H&E staining and IHC analysis. Consistent with their original tumors, PDO4 and PDO6 derived from adenocarcinoma exhibited acinar or large glandular structures and maintained the expression of lung adenocarcinoma markers, including napsin-A, thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF-1), and cytokeratin 7 (CK7) (Fig. 7B and C).

LB42708 and its combination with AZD5363 show potent antitumor effects in vivo

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of LB42708 alone or in combination with AZD5363, we established an NSCLC cell-derived xenograft model using the HRAS-mutant H1915 cell line (Fig. 8A). Compared with the vehicle-treated group, administration of LB42708 or AZD5363 at 25 mg/kg for 15 days significantly suppressed both tumor volume and tumor weight (Fig. 8B-D). Notably, the combination treatment of LB42708 and AZD5363 exhibited a more pronounced inhibitory effect on tumor growth in the H1915 xenograft model than either monotherapy alone (Fig. 8B and C). Consistent with this, the combination treatment also resulted in a remarkable reduction in tumor weight (Fig. 8D). Throughout the dosing period, all mice tolerated the treatment well. Although the body weight of mice in the combination group was slightly lower than that of the control group, the difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 8E). In addition, LB42708 and AZD5363, administered individually or in combination, did not induce detectable hepatic or renal toxicity in treated mice, as indicated by unchanged liver and kidney weights and gross morphology (Fig. S9A, C and D). Histological analysis further showed no pathological alterations in hepatic or renal tissue, supporting the preservation of organ integrity (Fig. S9B).

Discussion

RAS oncogenes, including NRAS, HRAS, and predominantly KRAS, constitute some of the most frequently mutated genes in human cancers, particularly lung cancer. The hotspot mutations are commonly found at codons 12, 13, and 61, serving as key driver events in tumorigenesis [21]. Given that RAS oncogenes are among the most frequently mutated genes across various cancers, considerable efforts have been devoted to developing targeted inhibitors against RAS [22–24]. RAS was long considered undruggable due to its smooth surface lacking prominent drug-binding pockets [22, 24]. However, this notion has recently changed with the FDA approval of sotorasib and adagrasib for the treatment of NSCLC patients harboring KRAS G12C mutations, which represents a breakthrough in KRAS targeted therapy. Both sotorasib and adagrasib are KRAS G12C-specific covalent inhibitors, which selectively bind to cysteine 12 within the switch II pocket of the KRAS-G12C protein, locking KRAS in its inactive GDP-bound state, hence referred to as KRAS G12C (OFF) inhibitors [25]. KRAS G12C inhibitors exert their effect through covalent binding between the small-molecule inhibitor and the mutant cysteine residue. However, this covalent targeting approach has been primarily applied to cysteine substitutions. Non-covalent inhibitors represent a promising strategy to overcome this limitation.

In recent years, several KRAS mutation-specific inhibitors, as well as pan-RAS inhibitors, have entered clinical development, with more candidates advancing through late-stage preclinical testing. Among them, MRTX1133, a highly selective, non-covalent KRAS G12D inhibitor, has shown promising preclinical efficacy. A phase 1/2 clinical trial evaluating MRTX1133 in advanced solid tumors harboring KRAS G12D mutations is currently ongoing [26]. BI-2865, a novel pan-KRAS inhibitor, selectively targets a range of KRAS mutant variants as well as wild-type KRAS, while sparing other RAS family members [27]. Recently, Tri-complex inhibitors, such as RMC-7977 and its analogs (RMC-6236, RMC-4998, and RMC-6291), have garnered considerable attention as a novel class of allele-specific or pan-allele RAS-targeting agents. These inhibitors function by remodeling the surface of cyclophilin A to create a neomorphic interface with high affinity and selectivity for the active (ON) state of RAS proteins, hence referred to as RAS (ON) inhibitors. The resulting cyclophilin A-inhibitor-RAS tri-complex disrupts effector binding to active RAS, thereby inactivating oncogenic signaling [10]. Among tri-complex inhibitors, RMC-7977 and RMC-6236 are reversible pan-RAS(ON) inhibitors that selectively target the active state of both mutant and wild-type KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS variants, exhibiting broad-spectrum antitumor activity, particularly in non-small cell lung cancer and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, with minimal effects on normal tissues [11, 12, 28]. RMC-4998 and RMC-6291 predominantly inhibit the activity of G12C-mutant KRAS, NRAS, and HRAS without interfering with their wild-type counterparts [29, 30].

However, emerging preclinical and clinical evidence reveals various potential mechanisms by which tumors may develop primary, adaptive, or acquired resistance to RAS inhibitors [12, 31–34]. Therefore, it is essential to explore alternative or combination therapeutic strategies to overcome RAS inhibitor resistance in patients with RAS-mutant tumors. Among these strategies, targeting farnesyltransferase (FTase) to indirectly suppress RAS activity has attracted increasing attention. As described above, membrane localization is indispensable for the full activation and functional execution of RAS proteins. This process relies on a post-translational lipid modification known as prenylation, comprising farnesylation and geranylgeranylation. These modifications are catalyzed by FTase and geranylgeranyltransferase-1 (GGTase-1), two cytosolic heterodimeric enzymes that share a common regulatory α-subunit but possess distinct catalytic β-subunits [8]. Accordingly, several FTase inhibitors (FTIs), such as tipifarnib and lonafarnib, have been developed as potential therapeutic agents to target RAS-driven cancers [35]. However, it is important to note that HRAS undergoes prenylation exclusively by FTase, whereas KRAS and NRAS can be modified by both FTase and an alternative “bypass” GGTase pathway [35]. Consequently, in the presence of FTIs, KRAS and NRAS can still undergo geranylgeranylation, enabling them to associate with membranes and retain their functional activity [35]. As a result, only one FTI, tipifarnib, has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma harbouring HRAS mutations [14].

LB42708 is a potent and selective FTI [36], and prior studies have demonstrated its ability to inhibit the growth of HRAS- and KRAS-transformed rat intestinal epithelial cells as well as suppress vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-induced angiogenesis [36]. However, there has been no investigation into its therapeutic potential in RAS-mutant NSCLC, particularly in the context of gefitinib resistance. Our study provides novel evidence that LB42708 effectively inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of RAS-mutant NSCLC cells while also promoting apoptosis (Figs. 1, 2 and 5). Moreover, LB42708 enhances the sensitivity of gefitinib-resistant PC9GR cells to gefitinib, suggesting a potential role in overcoming acquired resistance mediated by the reactivation of RAS-MAPK signaling (Fig. 6).

In addition, our study further supports that LB42708 not only inhibits FTase activity but also promotes the degradation of the FTase and GGTase-1 shared α-subunit via caspase-3 activation (Fig. 3). This differs from tipifarnib, which inhibits FTase enzymatic activity but does not markedly induce α-subunit degradation (Fig. S2). The observed difference may be related to LB42708-induced conformational changes in the FTase α-subunit, which expose a caspase-3 cleavage site within the subunit, making it susceptible to hydrolysis. In contrast, structural studies have shown that tipifarnib binds to the FTase active site within a deep hydrophobic cleft at the α- and β-subunit interface without causing significant conformational alterations [37]. Further studies are needed to confirm whether LB42708-induced conformational changes contribute to α-subunit degradation.

Another potential mechanism underlying the differential effects of LB42708 and tipifarnib on FTase α-subunit degradation may be related to their varying abilities to induce apoptosis and activate caspase-3. Etoposide, a topoisomerase II inhibitor, is known to effectively activate caspase-3 and induce apoptosis, and it is widely used in the treatment of various malignancies, including small cell lung cancer and acute leukemia. In this study, we found that etoposide significantly promoted caspase-3 activation and FTase α-subunit degradation in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells. Given that no prior studies have reported a direct interaction between etoposide and FTase, our findings suggest that the extensive activation of caspase-3 can mediate FTase α-subunit cleavage, indicating that FTase α-subunit is a substrate of caspase-3. However, tipifarnib only induces moderate activation of caspase-3, with significantly weaker activation capacity compared to both LB42708 and etoposide.

Both our study and previous research indicate that tipifarnib exhibits relatively weak activity in activating caspase-3 and inducing apoptosis [38]. In fact, tipifarnib has been reported to prevent hypoxia-induced Ras activation in the lung and inhibit the proliferation of hypoxia-induced human pulmonary artery endothelial cells (HPAECs) and pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (HPASMCs) without affecting cell viability or apoptosis in vitro or in vivo [39]. Furthermore, tipifarnib has been shown to suppress concanavalin A-induced hepatocyte apoptosis and caspase-3 activation in a mouse liver injury model and to dose-dependently (up to 1 µM) inhibit TNF-α-induced primary hepatocyte apoptosis in vitro [40, 41]. It is important to note that the ability of a drug to induce apoptosis is highly dependent on its concentration, the specific cell line used, and the duration of treatment. Studies have shown that for tipifarnib, even at a concentration as high as 10 µM, it still had minimal effects on caspase-3 activation in NCI-H157 NSCLC cells after 16 h of treatment [38]. Consistently, our study demonstrated that tipifarnib was comparatively less effective than LB42708 and etoposide in inducing apoptosis and caspase-3 activation in A549 and NCI-H1915 cells. Therefore, the inability of tipifarnib to promote FTase α-subunit degradation may be attributed to its limited capacity to activate caspase-3.

In addition to promoting the degradation of the FTase α-subunit via caspase-3 activation, LB42708 also facilitates proteasomal degradation of KRAS in A549 cells and HRAS in NCI-H1915 cells (Figs. 3 and 8I). In contrast, although etoposide robustly activates caspase-3 and induces degradation of the FTase α-subunit, it has little effect on RAS protein levels (Fig. S2). This may be because, despite promoting FTase α-subunit degradation, etoposide does not inhibit FTase activity; the remaining active FTase is sufficient to maintain RAS post-translational modification and membrane localization, thereby protecting it from proteasomal degradation. Tipifarnib induces degradation of HRAS, but has minimal effect on KRAS levels. This is because HRAS undergoes farnesylation exclusively by FTase, whereas KRAS can be prenylated by either FTase or a bypass pathway mediated by GGTase [35]. Since Tipifarnib selectively inhibits FTase but not GGTase, KRAS can still be prenylated via the alternative GGTase-dependent route, thus maintaining its protein stability and function.

Since the PI3K/AKT and MAPK signaling pathways are the two main effector pathways of RAS, we examined the effect of LB42708 on their activation. As expected, LB42708 significantly inhibited ERK phosphorylation and reduced the expression of the downstream early gene product c-Fos in RAS-mutant NSCLC cell lines A549 and NCI-H1915 (Fig. 4). However, unexpectedly, instead of suppressing AKT phosphorylation, LB42708 promoted its activation, while having no significant effect on the phosphorylation of the downstream effector molecule GSK3β. Similarly, tipifarnib also enhanced AKT phosphorylation but did not affect ERK activation (Fig. 4).

It is well established that there are extensive cross-talk and intricate interactions between the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways. In many cases, inhibition of one pathway may lead to compensatory activation of the other, contributing to intrinsic or acquired resistance [42–44]. For example, suppression of MAPK pathway activity can attenuate the negative feedback exerted by MAPK signaling on the PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby resulting in aberrant activation of this pathway (Fig. 8I and J). Previous studies have demonstrated that hyperphosphorylated AKT is commonly observed in KRAS-mutant lung cancer cells or tumor tissues resistant to KRAS inhibitors such as sotorasib, adagrasib, and MRTX1133, suggesting a strong link between aberrant AKT activation and resistance to RAS inhibitors [45–47]. Consequently, to overcome resistance to KRAS inhibitors, they are frequently combined with PI3K-AKT-mTORC1 pathway inhibitors in cancer therapy [46, 48, 49].

To counteract the AKT hyperactivation induced by LB42708, we evaluated the efficacy of LB42708 in combination with the AKT inhibitor AZD5363 in suppressing the proliferation of RAS-mutant NSCLC cells. AZD5363 (capivasertib) is an FDA-approved AKT inhibitor for the treatment of breast cancer. As expected, LB42708 and AZD5363 exhibited a synergistic effect in inhibiting RAS-mutant NSCLC cell growth (Fig. 4). However, neither AZD5363 alone nor its combination with LB42708 suppressed AKT phosphorylation; instead, it further increased pAKT levels. This phenomenon is consistent with previous preclinical and clinical findings, as AZD5363 has been reported to induce AKT hyperphosphorylation while rendering the kinase catalytically inactive due to compound binding [50, 51]. Indeed, AZD5363, either alone or in combination with LB42708, effectively inhibited the phosphorylation of the downstream effector molecule GSK3β. The synergistic anti-tumor effect of LB42708 and AZD5363 against RAS-mutant NSCLC was further validated in nude mouse xenograft models and patient-derived organoids (PDOs) (Figs. 7 and 8). Furthermore, aberrations in the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway have been well-documented as a key mechanism underlying resistance to EGFR-TKIs in EGFR-mutated NSCLC [52–55]. To explore potential therapeutic strategies, we evaluated the effect of LB42708 on gefitinib efficacy in a gefitinib-resistant PC9 cells (PC9GR). Our findings demonstrate that LB42708 significantly restored gefitinib sensitivity in these resistant cells while effectively suppressing abnormally activated ERK phosphorylation (Figs. 6 and 8J). It should be noted that the synergistic effect was observed only at high concentrations of LB42708 and under specific dose ratios in H1975OR cells. Further studies are required to validate this interaction in other osimertinib-resistant models with diverse resistance mechanisms.

Taken together, our study demonstrates that LB42708, a potent and selective FTase inhibitor, exerts strong anti-tumor activity in various preclinical models harboring KRAS or HRAS mutations. Furthermore, its combination with the AKT inhibitor AZD5363 in KRAS- or HRAS-mutant NSCLC cells, as well as with gefitinib in PC9GR cells, enhances therapeutic efficacy, highlighting a promising strategy for improved treatment outcomes.

Conclusions

Our comprehensive research has revealed that the potent FTase inhibitor LB42708 exhibits remarkable antitumor activity against RAS-mutant NSCLC both in vitro and in vivo. Notably, LB42708 not only inhibits FTase activity but also promotes the degradation of the shared α-subunit of FTase and GGTase-1 through caspase-3 activation. This disruption impairs RAS post-translational modification, leading to its subsequent proteasomal degradation. Furthermore, the combination of LB42708 with the AKT inhibitor AZD5363 significantly enhances its antitumor efficacy. LB42708 also increases the sensitivity of gefitinib-resistant PC9GR cells to EGFR inhibition. Collectively, these findings strongly support LB42708 as a promising therapeutic candidate for RAS-mutant and EGFR-TKI–resistant NSCLC.
