Influence of shaking method, shaking time, and frequency of resin reuse on the flexural strength of 3D-printed fixed restoration resins
Authors: Hamad S. AlRumaih, Fatimah F. Almalki, Riham A. Alzahrani, Reem R. Alasbly, Shrouq A. Aljuaid, Reem Abualsaud, Faris A. Alshahrani, Essam A. Nassar, Soban Q. khan, Sultan Akhtar, Mohammed M. Gad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe manipulation and handling of three-dimensional (3D) printing liquid resins prior to printing have not been reported in the literature. Therefore, this study was designed to assess the effect of different shaking methods and shaking times, together with the number of times the resin bottle was opened for resin reuse on the flexural strength (FS) of 3D-printed specimens, in conditions simulating routine laboratory handling protocols.MethodsTwo different resins (ASIGA DentaTOOTH and NextDent C&B MFH) were used for specimens’ fabrication. Rollers and horizontal shakers were used to shake the resins for 30 and 60 min before printing. A total of 240 specimens were fabricated and grouped according to shaking method (roller and horizontal) and shaking time (30 and 60 min). Further subdivisions were performed according to resin reuse cycles. The resins were reused at defined 7-day intervals, with each resin bottle reopening considered one reuse cycle, resulting in three groups: first use, second use, and third use (n = 10). Bar-shaped (25 × 2 × 2 mm) specimens were printed according to manufacturers’ recommendations and thermally cycled (5000 cycles), followed by 3-point bending test for FS testing. Data analysis was performed by using two-independent samples T-test, One-Way ANOVA and K-factors ANOVA. P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.ResultsK-factors ANOVA results indicated that repeated opening and reuse × material, shaking time × shaker, shaker × material, and shaking time × shaker × material all had significant interacting effects on the flexural strength (p < 0.001). For ASIGA, the roller shaker significantly produced higher FS values across all groups at 60 min shaking time (p < 0.001), but this effect was absent at 30 min shaking time. First use showed significantly higher FS compared to third use at 30-minute shaking time for both shakers (roller, p = 0.005; horizontal, p = 0.004). However, at 60-minute shaking time, no significant differences in FS were observed between the different reuse groups (p > 0.05). For NextDent, no significant differences were found between the two shakers for any reuse groups at either shaking times (p > 0.05). However, first use consistently demonstrated higher FS than second and third uses for both shaking times and shakers (p < 0.001). ANOVA revealed significant interactions among material types, shaking method, shaking time, and reuse cycle on FS (p < 0.001). For ASIGA resin, the roller shaker improved FS at 60 min shaking time, whereas no shaker-related differences were observed at 30 min. FS decreased with repeated use, particularly at shorter shaking times. For NextDent resin, shaker type had no significant effect; however, FS progressively decreased with repeated use cycles regardless of shaking conditions.ConclusionResin type, shaking method, shaking time, and repeated bottle opening significantly influenced the FS of 3D-printed resin. Proper resin handling may therefore help maintain the mechanical performance of 3D-printed resins.

Introduction

Three-dimensional (3D) printing, also known as additive manufacturing, is an advanced manufacturing technology that uses computer-aided design (CAD) digital models to create 3D objects following an automated process [1]. This process can be achieved with the aid of either solidifying resin or sintering powder [2]. The use of 3D-printing technology in prosthodontics, particularly for provisional restorations, has proven to be a valuable tool for providing a comprehensive analysis for both functional and aesthetic perspectives [3]. 3D-printing has emerged as a technology with significant potential in engineering, owing to its ability to offer rapid production, high accuracy, and personalization [1]. This technology can accurately produce complex shapes, work with different materials, speed up production, and even reuse remaining resin, making it an efficient and cost‑effective manufacturing method [4–7].

Provisional crowns and fixed dental prostheses (FDPs) are crucial in dental treatment, acting as temporary solutions before permanent fixtures are installed. They help maintain tooth alignment, protect dental pulp, support periodontal health, and ensure functionality and aesthetics [8]. In long-term treatments, like dental implants or extensive mouth rehabilitations, it’s vital that these provisional materials possess strong physical and mechanical properties to withstand prolonged use and prevent failures [9, 10].

3D-printing technology enables the creation of these provisional restorations using various resins, each with unique compositions and curing processes that influence their flexural strength (FS) [9, 10]. Many studies investigated the effect of printing and post printing curing parameters on the mechanical properties of printed objects [11–15]. However, pre-printing parameters such as resin composition, flowability, fillers, resin handling, shaking protocol (direction and time), and repeated resin use (representing consecutive bottle reopening over time) have been poorly addressed in the literature, although they can potentially affect the printed material’s quality and mechanical performance. Inadequate mixing of the resin can lead to sedimentation or incomplete dispersion of pigments and fillers, resulting in non-homogeneous polymerization and poor mechanical performance [11, 13, 14, 16]. Additionally, repeated bottle opening and prolonged storage between reuse cycles may expose the material to air, moisture, or light, resulting in degradation or premature polymerization and eventually weakening its strength and reliability [11, 17]. Despite their potential impact on print quality, pre-printing parameters such as resin-shaking protocols and repeated resin use remain largely unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of shaking method, shaking time, and repeated resin use on the FS of photopolymerized 3D-printing resins. The null hypothesis stated that shaking method, shaking time, and repeated use have no effect on the FS of 3D-printed provisional resin.

Materials and methods

This in vitro study was conducted at the College of Dentistry Research Laboratory. Before conducting the study, ethical approval (Approval date: 06/05/2024) was obtained from the Deanship of Scientific Research and Innovation at Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University. The sample size calculation for this study was performed using an online calculator. Means and SD were obtained from a previously published study in which mean1 = 89.33, mean2 = 79.88, and SD = 3.68 [18] with 90% power and 1.6 effect size, resulting in a minimum sample size of 10 per group. In this study, two 3D-printed resins (ASIGA DentaTOOTH (ASIGA, Erfurt, Germany), and NextDent C&B MFH (Micro Filled Hybrid) (NextDent, Vertex-Dental B.V., Soesterberg, The Netherlands) were investigated. Further division into two main groups representing the shaker type, and two subgroups representing shaking time. These subgroups were tested at three evaluation times yielding a total of 24 groups requiring 240 bar-shaped specimens. The grouping of resin specimens is shown in Fig. 1.

All resin materials were stored under controlled conditions in accordance with the manufacturers’ recommendations to ensure consistency and reproducibility. ASIGA DentaTOOTH resin was stored at a stable temperature of 20–25 °C, protected from light in its sealed, opaque container, and placed in a dry environment free from temperature fluctuations or UV exposure. The resin was not agitated during storage, as no shaking or mixing is recommended outside of the experimental protocols. NextDent C&B MFH (Micro Filled Hybrid) was stored at 18–25 °C in its original, light‑proof container, ensuring protection from ambient light and humidity. Similar to ASIGA DentaTOOTH, the resin was kept stationary during each 7‑day interval, as the manufacturer does not recommend shaking or agitation during storage. For both materials, all containers were used within their manufacturer‑specified shelf life, and no additional handling procedures were applied beyond those required for the experimental shaking protocols described in this study.

Two shakers were used: a roller shaker (LC 3D - Mixer, NextDent, Soesterberg, The Netherlands), and a horizontal shaker (Horizontal vibrating shaker (3016, Gesellschaft fur labortechnik mbH D-30938 Burgwedel, Germany)). The roller shaker allows the movement of the resin in vertical, horizontal, and spiral directions within the container, while the horizontal shaker permits unidirectional movement of the base on which the container is placed, allowing lateral (horizontal) movement of the resin (Fig. 2). Each resin group was divided according to shaker type (roller shaker and horizontal vibrating shaker) with two different shaking times (30- and 60-min). These procedures (shaker type and time) were repeated at different times of resin container opening and reuse (first use, second use, and third use) with a 7-day interval between each two openings and container storage according to manufacturer recommendations. The leftover resin after each printing cycle was returned to the original resin bottle, and the container was immediately secured. This procedure was done swiftly to minimize the time each bottle was kept open.

Specimens were designed using an open-source CAD system (123D design (version 2.2.14, Autodesk, Inc., San Rafael, CA, USA)), creating a standard tessellation language (STL) file for a bar-shaped specimen (25 × 2 × 2 mm) according to ANSI/ADA 27-2016 (Polymer-based Restorative Materials) [19, 20].The STL file was sent to each resin’s respective printer (NextDent® 5100 3D-Printer (3D systems, Vertex Dental B.V., Soesterberg, The Netherlands) and ASIGA MAX UV (ASIGA, Erfurt, Germany)), and specimens were printed according to manufacturers’ recommendations (50 μm layer thickness and 90-degree printing orientation, in which specimens were printed perpendicular to the build platform). The printed specimens were cleaned and then subjected to post-curing conditions according to manufacturers’ recommendations (See Table 1). After post-curing, the specimens were finished and polished according to manufacturers’ recommendations and as detailed in a previous study [21], then stored in distilled water for 48 h at 37 °C. All specimens were thermally stressed by subjecting them to 5000 cycles at 5–55 °C with 30 s dwell time and 5 s of transfer time using a thermocycler (Thermocycler THE-1100, Mechatronik GmbH, Feldkirchen-Westerham, Germany), which simulates 6 months of clinical use [22]. For standardization of specimen preparation, one investigator was assigned to prepare the specimens at different time intervals and complete specimen finishing and polishing, as well as the thermal cycling protocol.

For flexural strength (MPa) testing, each bar was fixed horizontally on the customized jig that was mounted on a universal testing machine (Electropuls E3000, Instron, UK). A three-point bending test was performed where the load was applied at the center of the specimen with a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until specimen failure occurred. The fracture load was recorded in newtons (N) to calculate the FS as detailed in a previous study [19].

After fracture, a scanning electron microscopy (SEM, TESCAN VEGA3 LM; TESCAN Orsay Holding, Kohoutovice, Czech Republic) was used for surface analysis. The specimens were gold sputtered (Q150R ES; Quorum Technologies, Lewes, UK) in order to minimize the charging effects during electron-specimen interaction. The gold-sputtered specimens were mounted on metal plates, where the fracture side was scanned using a secondary electron detector and a 20 kV accelerating voltage. The topography of fractured specimens was analyzed to determine the fracture patterns at different magnifications (100x -2000x) [19].

SPSS v.23 (Statistical Package for Social Sciences) was used to perform the statistical analysis. Averages and standard deviations were computed as a part of the descriptive analysis of the data. However, before conducting the inferential analysis, normality of the data was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Insignificant results from the test indicated that the data were normally distributed. Hence, parametric tests were used. To study the variation in the averages caused by the categorical variables with two categories, Two-independent samples T-test was used. Similarly, to study the variation in the averages caused by the categorical variables having more than two categories, One-Way ANOVA was used. Tukey post-hoc test was used in case of significant One-Way ANOVA results. Furthermore, to study the interacting effects of factors (like storage time, shaking time, shaker type, and material) on the flexural strength, k-factors ANOVA was used. All p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

To evaluate how the experimental factors jointly influence flexural strength, a k‑factor ANOVA was performed. Table 2 summarizes the interaction effects among the variables. Several two‑ and three‑way interactions were statistically significant. A significant interaction was found between reuse cycle × material (P < 0.001), indicating that the effect of reuse differed between the two materials. Additional significant interactions were observed for shaking time × shaker (P < 0.001), shaking time × material (P < 0.001), and shaker × material (P < 0.001), demonstrating that the combined levels of these factors influenced flexural strength. The three‑way interaction of shaking time × shaker × material was also significant (P < 0.001), representing that the flexural strength was influenced by these three factors combined. All other interaction terms were not statistically significant.

Discussion

The research tested the effect of pre-printing preparations (shaking time and shaker type), in addition to repeated opening of resin containers; referred to as different “reuse cycles” or “times of use”, on the FS of 3D-printed dental resins [17]. This aspect of the experimental design aimed to mimic real-life laboratory or clinical conditions when resin containers are not consumed all at once but reopened over time and reused several times. Based on the study’s findings, the FS was affected by shaker type, shaking time, as well as repeated resin use. Therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

The long-term durability of the printed resins was simulated using thermal cycling to mimic the oral environment. This procedure simulates the intraoral thermal challenge from repeated intake of hot and cold foods and drinks and will induce repeated cycles of expansion and contraction of dental materials [22]. These changes can cause micro-cracks and the accumulation of internal stress, eventually resulting in mechanical performance degradation. In our study, specimens were subjected to 5000 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C, a protocol widely used in dental research to mimic aging and assess the long-term performance of restorative materials [22].

Shaking of the resin bottle is an important step in the preparation process. It helps to ensure that the resin components, such as photo-initiators, pigments, and filler particles, are uniformly mixed before it is used for printing, minimizes sedimentation, and allows for uniformity in polymerization [1]. Additionally, the shaking method influences how pigments and fillers are freed from the resin and thus impacts the mechanical properties [1, 23]. The current study employed two shaking methods; a roller shaker and a horizontal shaker. Each was combined with two shaking durations (30- and 60-minute) to assess their influence on the FS of 3D-printed dental resins. The roller shaker employs a multidirectional movement, rolling motion around the lumen of the container and a wavy up and down motion at the same time, which allows the dispersion of all particles throughout the whole container. In contrast, the horizontal shaker provides a simpler unidirectional movement involving side-to-side motion of the container, which may promote localized accumulation and sedimentation of heavier constituents (Fig. 2).

The influence of shaker type on the strength of 3D‑printed resins has not been clearly established in the literature. Although direct comparisons between different shaker mechanisms are limited, studies on material processing indicate that achieving a more uniform resin distribution may contribute to improved mechanical performance. Additionally, the manufacturers of 3D-printed resins warned about possible color discrepancy or printing failure due to insufficient resin mixing. For ASIGA resin, 60-minute shaking provided a significantly higher FS than 30-minute shaking, especially when using the roller shaker. This might indicate that 30 min is too short to produce a measurable difference in ASIGA, while longer shaking times allowed better homogeneity of the resin and improved particle distribution [1, 15, 24]. The authors hypothesized that longer shaking time can homogenize the constituents of the 3D-printed resin and result in a uniform print. This assumption must be cautiously considered until further investigations of the effect of shaking on resin molecular dynamics take place. On the contrary, shaking time had less pronounced effects on NextDent, suggesting that material properties play a role in how much shaking time impacts final strength. It is possible that the fillers in NextDent have a density closer to that of the resin matrix, which may limit sedimentation. This assumption is supported by the higher viscosity of NextDent resin (800–1400 mPa·s) compared with ASIGA resin (≈ 400 mPa·s), which may have reduced particle settling during storage and shaking [25, 26]. The SEM analysis revealed a clear material‑dependent difference in microstructural behavior following repeated use. Voids and internal porosities were observed exclusively in the ASIGA specimens, whereas NextDent samples did not exhibit comparable voids under identical imaging conditions. This distinction suggests that the two resins respond differently to repeated mixing and handling, which may contribute to the mechanical trends observed in the study. Although surface irregular lamellae and micro‑cracks were noted in NextDent specimens, these features did not correspond to internal void formation. The absence of voids in NextDent explains why representative SEM images for this material were not included in Fig. 4, as no comparable internal defects were present. These findings highlight that microstructural changes associated with reuse appear to be material‑specific, reinforcing the need for resin‑dependent guidelines in clinical and laboratory workflows.

This research examined the impact of 3D-printing resin storage and consecutive reuse cycles on the mechanical properties of printed resin. A reduction in FS was noticed in the consecutive batches of printed resin specimens. However, the pattern of FS reduction differed between the two resins. ASIGA showed significant reduction at the third use and only with 30-min of shaking regardless of the shaker type, while NextDent showed the reduction at the second use with both shakers and shaking time. The reduction in FS is most likely due to degrading environmental factors such as humidity, light, and oxygen to which the polymers got exposed [17]. Repeated opening of resin containers increases its exposure to environmental factors and could lead to constituent evaporation, oxidation, or partial polymerization, altering the resin’s subsequent polymerization efficiency during printing. A published paper reported increased porosity, microstructural changes, and poorer chain bonding after prolonged storage and humidity exposure of printed polylactic acid [27]. Another study [17] compared different storage times and conditions on the strength of Bis-Acryl interim resin and concluded that increasing the cartridge storage time at ambient laboratory conditions induced partial premature aging with a marked degradation of mechanical properties. Moreover, repeated handling of resin, such as transfer of the resin to the tank, or holding unused material in the container, may lead to exposure of the resin to ambient light or heat from surrounding equipment [25, 26]. Short-term exposure to these processes may start the photoinitiation reaction, silently altering the resin composition and consistency [28]. Over time, such changes could reduce flowability, affect interlayer adhesion, and reduce the mechanical properties of the printed item [29]. The manufacturers, ASIGA and NextDent, recommend storing the resin in the original bottles, resin tank within the closed printer, or tray garage, at room temperature and in a cool, dry, and dark environment [25, 26]. Till now, there is no standardization or protocol for the storage and reuse of remaining resin, therefore, further investigations of different storage conditions (temperature, container, lighting, humidity, exposure to air, number of container opening/reuses, how long a resin container may remain open and still safe to reuse, etc.) are needed to come up with a protocol or recommendation for handling leftover resin.

From a clinical standpoint, the findings of this in vitro study suggest that proper resin handling may play a role in maintaining the mechanical performance of 3D‑printed resins. The use of fresh resin and consistent mixing procedures such as roller shaking appeared to support more stable flexural strength outcomes under the conditions tested. However, these implications should be interpreted cautiously, as only two materials were evaluated, and clinical behavior may differ under intraoral conditions. Further in vivo research is needed before definitive clinical recommendations can be made. While this study provides valuable insights into the effects of pre-printing parameters (shaking technique and time, as well as the number of reuse cycle of remaining resin) on 3D-printed resins, it has some limitations. The study was conducted in vitro, and clinical conditions may introduce additional variables affecting resin performance. Only two brands of 3D-printed resins were tested, and results may vary with different formulations and manufacturers. Future studies must explore a wider range of resin materials, experiment with long-term storage and reuse conditions in real-world scenarios and define other mechanical and physical characteristics. Such studies will help introduce standardized protocols for the success of 3D-printed dental products. This study is limited by the absence of both a non‑shaken control group and degree‑of‑conversion measurements, which restricts the ability to fully isolate the effects of shaking and confirm the proposed polymerization mechanism. Moreover, the limited availability of directly comparable studies posed a challenge for deeply interpreting the results within a broader context. These gaps highlight the need for future investigations that incorporate an unshaken baseline and analytical methods like FTIR. Such methods would allow the researcher to directly assess polymerization behavior and more thoroughly understand the mixing conditions’ impact on resin performance.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that resin type, shaking method, shaking time, and reuse cycle of the resin influenced the flexural strength of the two evaluated 3D‑printed resins under the tested in vitro conditions. Within the limits of this study, longer shaking times using a roller shaker were associated with higher flexural strength, whereas repeated resin use and frequent lid opening were associated with lower values. These observations highlight the potential importance of consistent resin handling; however, any recommendations regarding the use of fresh resin or specific mixing durations should be considered preliminary and applicable only to the materials and parameters examined in the present study. Additional studies involving a wider range of materials, storage conditions, and clinical simulations are needed before broader practice guidelines can be established.
