Expanding the phenotypic Spectrum of ZNF711: Autism and epilepsy in two siblings
Authors: Gloria Urciuoli, Nicola Simeone, Marica Rubino, Francesca Madia, Leonilda Bilo, Marika Comegna, Gabriella De Vita, Antonietta Coppola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zinc Finger Protein 711 (ZNF711) is a Krüppel-type zinc finger transcription factor highly expressed in the brain and whose mutations have been associated with neurodevelopmental disorders such as intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, and epilepsy. While most pathogenic variants previously described affect the C-terminal DNA-binding domain, we report two siblings carrying a rare hemizygous missense variant in the N-terminal transactivation domain, NM_001330574.2: c.65 T > C (p.Ile22Thr), inherited from their heterozygous unaffected mother. Patient #1 presents with autism spectrum disorder, epilepsy, moderate intellectual disability, and mild facial dysmorphisms, whereas Patient #2 shows milder autism spectrum disorder traits and no clinical seizures, although electroencephalogram abnormalities are present. In silico analyses predict that this variant, affecting a highly conserved residue, may disrupt protein structure and thus impair its proper function. The recurrence of this variant in the two siblings reported here and in those reported in Minerva et al. (2025) sharing overlapping phenotypes strengthens its potential pathogenicity, despite current classification as a variant of uncertain significance.

Background

ZNF711 is a transcriptional regulator crucial for human brain development [1], [2], [3]. The gene encoding ZNF711 is located on the X chromosome (Xq21.1; OMIM #314990) and comprises eight coding exons that undergo alternative splicing events resulting in three different protein isoforms (Genome Browser, UniProt). ZNF711 belongs to the Krüppel-type C2H2 zinc finger protein family and binds to promoters downstream of CpG islands, thereby regulating gene expression [4], [5], [6]. While ZNF711 is expressed across multiple human tissues, its expression is enriched in the brain [4], [7]. The dysregulation of its epigenetic pathway is implicated in a spectrum of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including microcephaly, epilepsy, intellectual disability (ID), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) [3], [8], [9], [10], [11]. Pathogenic variants of ZNF711 have been identified in a limited number of individuals with non-syndromic X-linked intellectual disability (XLID), typically presenting with clinical features including speech impairment, mild facial dysmorphism, and autistic traits [1], [7], [12], [13], [14]. A recent study reported a novel missense variant in the exon 1 of ZNF711, c.65 T > C (p.Ile22Thr), in two siblings with moderate ID and epilepsy, marking the first association of this variant with epilepsy [13]. Here, we report two further siblings presenting with ASD and epilepsy and carrying the same ZNF711 variant.

Case presentation

Patient #1

This is a 19-year-old male, first-born of healthy unrelated parents. His family history reports a cousin on his mother's side affected by febrile seizures, and an aunt on his father's side affected by epilepsy with auditory features.

He was born at term via vaginal delivery after an uneventful pregnancy. Auxological parameters at birth were: weight 3400 g (51st percentile), head circumference 33.7 cm (7th percentile), length 50 cm (45th percentile). Apgar score was 8 at 1 min, and 9 at 5 min.

Neurological and epilepsy history: He began walking independently at 15 months, he started speaking at 15 months albeit with a very limited vocabulary. Sphincter control was achieved at 5 years old. From the age of 18 months, he was noticed to have rare eye contact, behavioural and emotional control difficulties, and stereotypes. A comprehensive neuropsychiatric evaluation concluded for a diagnosis of ASD at the age of 2.

At the age of 12 years old, the patient underwent a cognitive assessment using the Leiter-R scale, which revealed a moderate cognitive delay with a total Intelligent Quotient (IQ) of 45. Specifically, impairments in perceptual-analogical and logical-abstract thinking processes were observed, while memory and attention levels resulted within the normal range.

Physical examination showed mild facial dysmorphisms including hypertelorism and short palpebral fissures. Seizures onset was at the age of 13 years old with a generalized tonic-clonic seizure preceded by gaze staring and eyelid myoclonia. At onset seizures were characterized by major episodes with loss of awareness, gaze staring, head and eye deviation to the left, followed by ocular wandering, with or without eyelid myoclonia. These were occurring on a monthly base. He also suffered from minor and rarer episodes characterized by focal clonic movements of the left arm, eyelid myoclonia and impaired awareness. Valproic acid titrated up to the dosage of 1250 mg/day reduced the frequency to annual occurrence. At the age of 15 years old, due to persistence of seizures lacosamide was added on and titrated up to 500 mg/day; later Clobazam 10 mg/day was also added achieving complete seizure control. The patient has been seizure free for over a year and continues the treatment with Valproic Acid 1250 mg/die, Lacosamide 500 mg/die, Clobazam 10 mg.

Video-EEG monitoring

The video-EEG monitoring, performed during wakefulness at the age of 13 years old, showed a normal background activity. Interictal activity was characterized by multifocal spike-wave and polyspikes-wave complexes, isolated or in brief sequences, over the right temporo-occipital region. Asynchronously and less frequently epileptiform abnormalities were also seen over the left temporo-occipital region. (Fig. 1B). A video-EEG performed a year later, showed a physiological Non-Rapid Eye Movement (NREM) sleep structure and rare plurifocal spikes or spike-wave complexes.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) performed at 13 years old showed normal findings except for an incomplete inversion of the left hippocampus (Fig. 1C).

Patient #2

This is a 16 years old boy. Pregnancy and delivery were uneventful. He was diagnosed with ASD at 2 years old. He underwent a cognitive assessment with the Leiter-R scale at the age of 7 years old showing a normal IQ (total IQ: 110). He demonstrated a good level of perceptual-analogical and logical-abstract thinking processes, excellent attention levels, and strong visuospatial skills.

Physical examinations performed at the age of 14 years old disclosed mild facial dysmorphisms including short philtrum and full lower lip. (Fig. 1D)

He has never had clinical seizures, however an EEG performed at the age of 13 years old showed isolated epileptiform abnormalities (spike-wave complexes or sharp waves over the right anterior and mid-temporal regions) (Fig. 1E).

Genetic investigations

Patient #1 underwent Karyotype (400 bands, Fragile X Site A (FRAXA), array-based Comparative Genomic Hybridization (array-CGH), Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) panel for 85 genes for ASD (2015). All were reported as negative. A Whole Exome Sequencing (WES) (performed in 2023) disclosed a hemizygous missense variant affecting ZNF711, a gene located on the X chromosome (NM_001330574.2, GRCh38.p12, OMIM #314990: c.65 T > C, p.Ile22Thr). The variant is currently classified as variant of uncertain significance (VUS) according to American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines criteria. The same variant was found in patient#2 and was inherited from their heterozygous healthy mother.

Computational and molecular study

The ZNF711 gene comprises eight coding exons and undergoes alternative splicing events that predominantly affect the C-terminal region of the encoded protein. According to annotations from the RefSeq database, at least three transcript variants are produced. Isoform 1 encodes a 761 amino acid protein, isoform 2 contains 791 amino acids, and isoform 3, the longest variant, comprises 807 amino acids and is distinguished by an extended C-terminal domain (Genome Browser; UniProt). Structurally, the protein features a N-terminal acidic transactivation domain, twelve zinc finger motifs forming the DNA-binding domain located in the C-terminal region and a nuclear localization signal in the middle. Based on structural data from the Protein Data Bank (PDB), ZNF711 is predicted to adopt the canonical ββα-fold configuration typical of C2H2-type zinc finger motifs, facilitating sequence-specific DNA binding. Nonetheless, AlphaFold-based structural modelling indicates a high degree of uncertainty in the configuration of the N-terminal region, particularly surrounding the site of the c.65 T > C variant (Fig. 2B). This single nucleotide substitution results in a missense mutation at amino acid position 22 replacing isoleucine, a hydrophobic residue, with threonine, which possesses a polar group on the side chain (Fig. 2B, lower panel). Given the elevated pathogenicity prediction scores (Fig. 2C), this substitution is likely to impair proper protein folding and, consequently, its function.

Multiple sequence alignment performed using Clustal Omega reveals strong evolutionary conservation of residue I22 and the two flanking methionines across several species (Fig. 2D), suggesting functional importance of this region. Potential pathogenic mechanisms may include disruption of local protein conformation due to altered polarity, interference with post-translational modifications, impaired coordination of zinc ions, or abrogation of interactions with molecular partners, all of which could affect DNA binding capacity. As ZNF711 encodes a transcription factor, correct nuclear localization is critical for its function. It is therefore possible that the missense mutation affects nuclear import, leading to cytoplasmic retention and consequent loss of transcriptional activity.

Real-time RT-PCR analysis performed on control human fibroblasts and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), the only non-invasively accessible tissues available, revealed the absence of ZNF711 expression in both cell types (Supplementary Fig. S1), thereby limiting molecular analyses in these primary cells.

Discussion

Previous studies have demonstrated that mutations in the C-terminal region of the ZNF711 gene, which may disrupt its transcriptional regulatory function during neural development, are associated with several NDDs, including ID, XLID and ASD. Many of these pathogenic variants result in premature protein truncation and are located within the DNA-binding domain, impairing the protein's ability to interact with its target sequences [4], [14]. Despite increasing interest, ZNF711 remains relatively understudied at molecular level. Here, we describe two additional clinical cases involving two siblings carrying the ZNF711 variant (c.65 T > C), inherited from the mother who is clinically healthy, underlying the occurrence of epilepsy besides ASD in these patients [13]. Notably, this variant is located within the N-terminal region of the gene, specifically in exon 1 of the Coding DNA sequence (CDS), which is shared by all three known ZNF711 isoforms. As this region encompasses the transcriptional activation domain of the protein, predicting the functional consequences of the variant is more challenging than for variants affecting the DNA-binding domain. Nevertheless, this observation suggests that the c.65 T > C variant may exert its clinical effects through mechanisms distinct from those previously characterized. This highlights the need for deeper molecular investigations.

Clinically, the two affected siblings exhibit variable severity of their clinical features: patient #1 is affected by epilepsy, a more severe ASD phenotype, moderate ID and speech delay, while patient #2 presents with milder ASD symptoms and no seizures to date notwithstanding epileptiform abnormalities on EEG. Due to the younger age of the patient #2, late-onset epilepsy cannot be ruled out. Compared to the previously reported cases by Minerva et al. ASD, epilepsy, moderate ID, speech delay and minor facial dysmorphisms is an overlapping phenotype. Since ZNF711 is involved in the regulation of different epigenetic pathways, it is possible that the observed phenotypic heterogeneity may result from additional, unidentified genomic variants or the presence of modifier genes [1], [2], [3], [4].However the exome analysis did not find additional rare variants. Future investigations should be aimed at identifying impaired cellular pathways potentially underlying clinical variability. Chromatin Immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) may contribute to the identification of direct genomic targets of ZNF711, thereby enhancing our understanding of its role in the molecular mechanisms underlying ASD and its possible association with epilepsy. A more detailed characterization of ZNF711-associated transcriptional networks could provide insights relevant to the improvement of therapeutic strategies for symptoms associated with this and other pathogenic variants involved in ID and ASD.

Conclusion

The two clinical cases described in this report further support the role of ZNF711 as a critical player in neurodevelopmental processes, particularly in the context of ASD associate with other neurological conditions such as epilepsy. This rare missense mutation c.65 T > C (p.Ile22Thr), previously reported in only two individuals with ASD and epilepsy, is here identified in two additional siblings with ASD, one of whom also presents with epilepsy.

Differently from previously identified pathogenic variants, which predominantly localize to the C-terminal DNA-binding regions of the protein, this mutation affects a highly conserved residue in the N-terminal region shared by all three ZNF711 isoforms, within the predicted transcription activation domain. Although functional studies are limited by the lack of ZNF711 expression in patient-derived fibroblasts and PBMCs, in silico analyses suggest that the p.Ile22Thr substitution may alter protein structure potentially impairing its transcriptional regulatory functions.

Currently, the c.65 T > C variant is classified as a VUS according to ACMG criteria. However, its identification in two independent pairs of siblings presenting with overlapping clinical phenotypes, together with in silico pathogenicity predictions, strong evolutionary conservation and its absence from population databases (such as gnomAD), provide additional evidences suggesting its potential pathogenic role. These findings indicate that the variant may have deleterious functional consequences, raising the possibility of a re-evaluation of its current classification. Additional functional studies could help establish its definite pathogenicity.
