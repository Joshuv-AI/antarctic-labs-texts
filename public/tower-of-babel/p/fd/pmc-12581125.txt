Monitoring Response to Combinatorial Immunotherapies by Tracking both T Cells and Natural Killer Cells In Vivo via ImmunoPET-Raman Multimodal Gold Nanostars
Authors: Gabriel P. Cutshaw, Siddhant Kothadiya, Saji Uthaman, Darpan N. Pandya, Xiaona Wen, Ansuja Mathew, Akesh Sinha, Irine Antony, Anna E. Vilgelm, Thaddeus J. Wadas, Jason S. Mitchell, Rizia Bardhan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Real-time imaging of multiple immunomarkers in vivo is crucial to predict treatment response and to understand the complex interplay of immune cells such as CD8+ T cells and natural killer (NK) cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME). Since dynamic changes occur in these immune cells during combinatorial immunotherapies, these changes cannot be captured by single biomarker tracking or by ex vivo measurement of receptors at the endpoint of treatment. There is an unmet need for imaging probes that cohesively integrate the merits of multiple imaging modalities while surmounting the challenges of each modality. Here, we have developed multimodal gold nanostars (MGNs) functionalized with Raman reporters, immune-targeting antibodies, and 1,4,7-triazacyclononane-1,4,7-triacetic acid (NOTA)-chelated 64Cu radiolabels. These MGNs integrate the high sensitivity and whole-body imaging of immunoPET (positron emission tomography) with high spatial resolution and multiplexing of Raman spectroscopy for real-time in vivo detection of recruited CD8+ T cells and NKp46+ NK cells in the TME. MGNs predict treatment response to antiPD-L1 + antiCD47 combination immunotherapies in both immunologically “hot” CT26 murine tumors that are responders of therapy and immunologically “cold” 4T1 murine tumors that are poor responders. In vivo endpoints were validated with conventional assays (immunofluorescence, ELISA, and flow cytometry) to confirm immune cell recruitment. Our results show that MGNs enable imaging capabilities that cannot be achieved with either modality alone, allowing early detection of immunomarkers to improve therapeutic outcomes for responders and accelerate clinical decisions for poor responders.

Introduction

Combination checkpoint blockade immunotherapies have revolutionized therapeutic outcomes of cancer patients by overcoming the immune evasion of cancer cells and enabling innate and adaptive immune responses. Recruitment of activated CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) is a strong indicator of therapeutic response. Yet, tracking T cells in tumors alone is often not predictive of response specifically in immunocompromised patients or in tumors with a low major histocompatibility complex (MHC) I presentation where T cells are not effective in recognizing and killing cancer cells. , It is imperative that in addition to T cells, other immunomarkers are also detected in vivo to allow translatable methods for predicting response to immunotherapies. Natural killer (NK) cells are cytotoxic innate immune effector cells capable of recognizing and eliminating infected, stressed, or malignant cells independently of antigen specificity or MHC restrictionunlike adaptive T cells. In the TME, NK cells exhibit a dual role: while they typically suppress tumor growth through direct cytotoxicity and cytokine secretion (e.g., interferon γ), they can also adopt an immunosuppressive phenotype under persistent TME pressures, paradoxically promoting immune evasion and tumor progression. Interferon γ (IFN-γ) released by NK cells can upregulate MHC on cancer cells rendering them vulnerable to detection and deletion by cytotoxic CD8+ T cells. , Perfectly balanced orchestration of T cells and NK cells in the TME enables a complete response to immunotherapies, ,, and tracking both of these immune cells directly in vivo before, during, and after therapy is crucial to early assessment of treatment response. Clinical imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) are used to measure the treatment response but due to poor sensitivity, specificity, and resolution, CT imaging has high variability and low accuracy as a diagnostic tool. Therefore, CT can only detect relapses at a much later time point after resistance has already developed and the tumor has progressed. While positron emission tomography (PET) with radiolabeled antibodies (i.e., immunoPET) is useful for single immunomarker imaging in vivo, immunoPET does not allow multiplexing as all radionuclides show similar indistinguishable contrast in vivo. Therefore, dynamic changes in the recruitment of both T cells and NK cells in the TME during the course of combinatorial immunotherapy cannot be simultaneously assessed with immunoPET alone. Further, in current clinical standard, immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis of biopsies is performed that is both time- and labor-intensive and also does not capture an accurate measure of response. An unmet clinical need exists for accurate, dynamic, and noninvasive diagnostic tools that address the limitations of current approaches and enable multiplexed detection of both NK cells and CD8+ T cells in vivo.

In this study, we use an innovative approach to address this need with multimodal gold nanostars (MGNs) that integrate the merits of immunoPET with surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (RS), a noninvasive and rapid optical technique. MGNs leverage the high sensitivity and whole-body, deep tissue imaging capabilities of immunoPET, combined with high spatiotemporal resolution and exceptional in vivo multiplexing of RS. The generally weak Raman scattering effect is overcome by conjugating Raman-active reporters to shape- and size-controlled gold nanostars (GNs). GNs’ anisotropic structure enables intense electromagnetic fields along the nanostar tips, resulting in a significant enhancement effect. , In addition to electromagnetic enhancement, covalently attached Raman reporters also leverage chemical enhancement through molecular and electronic interactions with the GNs. Collectively these effects enable strong surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), which allows high sensitivity and spatiotemporal resolution for dynamic multiplexing and has been leveraged by our group and others in vitro − and in vivo, − to overcome the limitations of clinical imaging methods such as immunoPET. Here, MGNs are synthesized by conjugating monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) targeting CD8+ T cells and NKp46+ NK cells, two different Raman reporters associated with each immune cell type, and a 64Cu radiolabel, which is chelated with 1,4,7-triazacyclononane-1,4,7-triacetic acid (NOTA) for immunoPET. MGNs successfully tracked both T cells and NK cells noninvasively in vivo in both a high tumor infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL) “hot” CT26 murine tumor model that is responsive to checkpoint blockade immunotherapies and low-TIL immunologically “cold” 4T1 tumor model. , Both models were treated with combination immunotherapy with mAbs blocking the programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and CD47 receptor, which elicits innate immune responses. The PD-1/PD-L1 axis has emerged as a mechanism that cancer cells use to evade immune recognition by T cells, and blockade of this axis enhanced cytotoxic CD8+ T cell activity. PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapies have been successful in patients but only in tumors that express PD-L1, which also motivated our choice of the murine models where CT26 tumors overexpress PD-L1 and 4T1 tumors having minimal expression. Combination immunotherapy where both PD-L1 and CD47 receptors are blocked has shown benefits in both primary and abscopal tumors that were nonresponsive to monotherapy, , and recent innovation in bispecific antibodies that dual-target PD-1/CD47 simultaneously was effective in mouse models and patients. , CD47 receptors interact with signal regulatory protein α (SIRPα) in the TME to inhibit phagocytosis by tumor-associated macrophages. NK cells also express CD47, and interaction of NK cell CD47 with thrombospondin-1 expressed by cancer cells has been found to downregulate NK cell energetics. , Therefore, the combination of antiPD-L1 + antiCD47 investigated in this study is highly relevant for tracking both activated CD8+ T cells and NKp46+ NK cells in vivo with MGNs to corroborate treatment response in CT26 tumors that are responders to this therapy and treatment resistance in 4T1 tumors that are poor responders. Our in vivo findings were validated with flow cytometry analysis (FACS), immunofluorescence (IF), and Luminex analysis of cytokines to confirm MGNs ability to track multiple immunomarkers in vivo. Our study will ultimately allow early clinical decisions on the most effective treatment choices and limit issues associated with treatment failures, such as unjustified toxicities and costs.

Materials and Methods

Fabrication and Characterization of Multimodal Gold Nanostars (MGNs)

The fabrication of MGNs was a multiday process. First, targeting antibodies anti-CD8a (BioXcell, clone: 53–6.7) and NKp46 (BD Biosciences, clone: 29A1.4) were prepared separately at concentrations of 1 mg/mL in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate. 2000 kDa ortho-pyridyl disulfide polyethylene glycol succinimidyl valerate (OPSS-PEG-SVA) linker (Laysan Biosciences,) was prepared at a concentration of 69 mg/mL in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate and was mixed with the 1 mg/mL antibodies at a mole ratio of 1:500 and a volume ratio of 9:1 antibody solution to linker solution. They were then placed on an inverting rotator at 4 °C for 16–24 h to produce antibody-PEG-OPSS conjugates. The following day, one or more 30 mL batches of gold nanostars were fabricated via an unseeded process. Gold­(III) trichloride (Sigma) was prepared at a stock concentration of 100 mM in milli-Q water and stored at 4 °C. 2-[4-(2-Hydroxyethyl)­piperazin-1-yl]­ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer (Sigma) was prepared at a concentration of 0.2 M in Milli-Q water and pH-corrected with 1 M sodium hydroxide (Sigma) to a pH of 7.4 (± 0.02 pH) as measured by an electronic pH meter (Mettler-Toldeo). For nanostar batch fabrication, 12 mL of 0.2 M HEPES was mixed with 18 mL of milli-Q water in a 50 mL falcon tube and mixed by gentle inversion up to 30 times. For each batch, 300 μL of 16.7 mM gold chloride trihydrate (Sigma) prepared from the 100 mM stock was added to the HEPES solution in a darkened room, and then the solution was mixed by gentle inversion 50 times. The gold nanostar solution was allowed to undergo crystallization at room temperature in a darkened area for 75 min, after which the suspension was placed on ice. All batches of nanostars being prepared were mixed, and the absorbance spectrum of the nanostars was characterized to confirm the primary spectral peak fell within the 760–780 nm region.

Following initial absorbance characterization, the nanostars were subdivided into two groups: one set was labeled with para-mercaptobenzoic acid (pMBA) (TCI America) and one group with 5,5-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) (TCI America). Labels were prepared in biomolecular-grade ethanol (Thermo Fisher) at a concentration of 2 mM and were added at a ratio of 10 μL of label solution per 20 mL of nanostar solution. The thiol–gold reaction was conducted at 4 °C for 15 min with gentle rotation. Following this reaction, the labeled nanostars’ absorbance was characterized to observe an 8–12 nm red shift in the primary spectral peak. The labeled nanostars were then spun down at 3620 cfg for 20 min, and the supernatant was removed. The absorbance of the nanostars was again measured to observe a 1–3 nm blue shift in the spectral peak. The labeled and concentrated nanostars were diluted based on the primary peak absorbance value to an estimated concentration of 0.175 mg/mL. The antibody–linker conjugates that had been reacted overnight the previous day were then added to the dilute nanostars at a ratio of 55 μL of antibody–linker solution for each 5.625 mL of diluted nanostar solution. The anti-CD8 antibody-PEG-OPSS conjugate was added to the pMBA-labeled nanostars to create nanostar-pMBA-CD8 probes, and then anti-NKp46 antibody-PEG-OPSS conjugate was added to the DTNB-labeled nanostars to create nanostar-DTNB-NKp46 probes. The dilute nanostar–antibody solution was then reacted for 20–24 h by a rotating inverter at 4 °C.

The next day, the 2-S-(4-isothiocyanatobenzyl)-1,4,7-triazacyclononane-1,4,7-triacetic acid (NOTA) chelator (Macrocyclics) was prepared at a concentration of 8.33 mg/mL in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate and pH-corrected to pH 8.4 (±0.2) with 0.5 M sodium carbonate. OPSS-PEG-SVA linker was prepared at a concentration of 184 mg/mL and mixed with the NOTA solution at a molar ratio of 1 mol of NOTA per 1.25 mol of linker. This mixture was then allowed to react for 8–12 h at 4 °C on a rotating inverter. Later that day, the nanostar–antibody conjugate was diluted by 50% with Milli-Q water and spun down at a speed of 1430 cfg for 10 min to remove the unconjugated nanostar and excess reagents. The supernatant was then removed and replaced once more to enhance recovery. The absorbance of the concentrated nanostar–antibody conjugate was then characterized to observe a 20–25 nm red shift. The concentrated nanostar–antibody conjugate was then diluted to an estimated concentration of 0.263 mg/mL. The completely reacted NOTA-linker conjugate was then added to the rediluted nanostar–antibody solution at a ratio of 134.4 μL of NOTA-linker solution per 3.75 mL of nanostar–antibody solution. This solution was allowed to react mixed on an inverting rotator for 10–12 h at 4 °C overnight.

The following day, the complete MGN (labeled nanostar–antibody–chelator) was removed from the rotator and spun down at 1430 cfg for 10 min. The supernatant was removed and spun down to enhance recovery, and this process was repeated once more for 3 total rounds of centrifugation. The concentrated MGNs had their absorbance characterized to observe an 8–12 nm red shift. MGNs were subjected to other characterizations to determine their quality. Hydrodynamic diameter (estimated size) and zeta potential (surface charge) were measured by a Malvern Zetasizer. SERS spectra were obtained by characterizing a droplet plated on a 20 mm calcium fluoride (CaF2) substrate. Raman measurements were conducted using a 5× Lexica objective for 10 s per point, with 5 replicate points per measurement.

Preparation of Cancer Cells and Tumor Inoculation

4T1 murine breast cancer cells and CT26 murine colorectal cancer cells were used for tumor inoculation. 4T1 cells were grown in RPMI media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Sigma) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) (cRPMI), while CT26 cells were grown in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (cDMEM). Both cell lines were cultured in T-75 flasks (Thermo Fisher) and grown in a humidified incubator at 37 °C under an atmosphere of 5% CO2. Subculture was conducted by washing the cells with warmed (37 °C) sterile Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (DPBS) and then detaching the cells with warmed 4 mL 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Sigma) (1–2 min for CT26 cells, and 4–5 min for 4T1 cells.) The trypsin-EDTA was neutralized by adding warmed 6–7 mL of complete media and the suspended cells were spun down at 250 cfg for 4 min. The supernatant was removed by an aspirator and the cells were resuspended in 1–2 mL of warmed DPBS and then spun down again at 250 cfg to wash any remaining media. The supernatant was once again removed, and the cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of warmed DPBS. Cell count was conducted using an Invitrogen Countess II automated cell counter with Trypan blue dye and a double-chamber cell counter slide (Invitrogen). The average of the two chambers was recorded as the cell count, and the cells were diluted with DPBS to an appropriate concentration. 4T1 cells were inoculated at a density of 0.5 million cells in 100 μL of DPBS, and CT26 tumors were inoculated at a density of 1.0 million cells in 100 μL. Cell suspensions were kept on ice prior to inoculation. Following subcutaneous flank tumor inoculation, mice were observed daily with weight and tumor measurements until the termination of the study.

In Vivo Raman Imaging

Prior to small animal imaging, hair was removed from the tumor site of each imaged mouse with Nair hair removal cream, which was applied for 1 min and then gently washed off with warm water. In vivo Raman imaging was conducted under isoflurane anesthesia with the mouse resting on a pad heated to 37 °C. Raman imaging was run in high confocal mode with the focal point offset −100 μm below the surface of the tumor. The confocal offset nature of the imaging was found to be crucial in obtaining a high SNR signal. Imaging with a “standard” confocality, as well as high confocal imaging focused on the tumor surface, saw increased tissue background signal. Offsetting the focal point below the surface allowed for a rejection of the surface-level tissue background, allowing for subcutaneous imaging of accumulated nanoparticles. Optimization of the imaging found the −100 μm as the empirical optimal point, with variations from that offset giving no further return to the SNR. Imaging was conducted with a 5× Lexica optic with 10 points measured per tumor. Each scan was conducted for 6 accumulations of 20 s (120 s total) to produce high SNR measurements. Points were sampled randomly across the tumor margin at the fixed −100 μm depth. For SERS MGN imaging, the mice received intraperitoneal (IP) injections of 200 μL of 3 mg/mL aqueous nonradiolabeled MGN (0.6 mg total). Following injections, Raman imaging was conducted at 6, 20, and 48 h time points. Raman measurements were then smoothed with a Savitsky–Golay filter, and the background was subtracted to produce processed spectra. The processed spectra were then normalized to the 1448 cm–1 background band, which corresponds to long-chain and structural biomolecules such as proteins and lipids. Finally, the results were averaged and further normalized to the background tissue signal in each of the Raman label signal bands (1075 cm–1 for GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/64Cu-NOTA and 1325 cm–1 for GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/64Cu-NOTA).

Radio-Biodistribution

Balb/cJ mice at the age of 6 weeks were inoculated with CT26 tumors. On day 12 after inoculation, radiolabeled MGNs were prepared. 64Cu activity was acquired from Washington University. The radiolabeled MGNs were mixed with unlabeled MGNs for an activity of 0.1 mCuire and a concentration of 0.6 mg in a volume of 200 μL per dose. In addition to the mouse doses, a standard was prepared for each time point for time normalization. Tumor-bearing mice were divided into multiple groups based on the time point (6, 24, and 48 h) and blocked versus nonblocked. All mice were injected with radiolabeled MGNs intraperitoneally. For the blocked group, 5 min prior to nanostar injection, the mice were also intraperitoneally injected with a solution of 150 μg of anti-CD8 and 100 μg of anti-NKp46 (total volume of 100 μL in sterile PBS) to serve as a competitive receptor blocker. At each of the three time points, mice were euthanized, and their tissues and blood were removed for characterization using a γ counter. Counts per minute (CPM) reported via the γ counter for each tissue were converted to %ID/g via the following formula ID%g=100%×1masstissue×(CPMtissue−CPMblank)CPMstandard

Combination Treatment Study

Balb/cJ mice were inoculated with either 4T1 or CT26 tumor cell lines at the age of 6 weeks. For the treatment of 4T1, treatment began when the tumors reached an average diameter of ∼5 mm. For the treatment of CT26, treatment began when the tumors reached an estimated average volume of 80–100 mm3. Immunotherapy combination treatment consisted of 200 μg of anti-PD-L1 (BioXcell, clone B7–H1) and 120 μg of anti-CD47 (BioXcell, clone MIAP301) delivered via IP injection every other day for 3 doses for 4T1 and 4 dose for CT26. The dosage was selected based on studies of combination immunotherapy in these and similar tumor models. Immunotherapy and chemotherapy combination treatment consisted of 5 mg/kg oxaliplatin (Selleckchem) delivered via IP injection on the first day of treatment, followed by antibody combination on the second and fourth days of treatment. A second dose of oxaliplatin was delivered on the sixth day of treatment, and a third dose of antibodies was delivered on the seventh day of treatment. Control mice received façade treatment of 120 μg IgG2a (BioXcell, clone 2A3) and 200 μg IgG2b (BioXcell, clone LTF-2) on the first, third, and fifth days of treatment. Two days following treatment, mice were either with MGNs injected for immunoPET-Raman imaging or were euthanized for ex vivo sample preparation.

In Vivo ImmunoPET/CT Imaging and Analysis

In vivo immunoPET/CT imaging was conducted with a Sofie G8 microPET/CT instrument. Imaging was conducted under isoflurane murine anesthesia for 20 min of PET imaging and 1.5 min of CT imaging. Reconstructed PET/CT image data was analyzed using VivoQuant software. Subcutaneous tumors were analyzed by using segmentation ROIs to sample the entirety of the tumor. Other organs (liver, spleen, and kidney) were sampled at hotspots using a fixed ROI of 40 voxels for the liver, 20 voxels for each kidney, and 20 voxels for the spleen. Flow Cytometry Sample Preparation

Single-cell flow cytometry suspensions were collected from tumors post-euthanasia. After removal from the euthanized mouse, the tumor was portioned using a sterile scalpel and incubated in 1 mL of DMEM with 4.4 mg/mL collagenase IV for 30 min at 37 °C. The enzyme solution was then neutralized with 0.7 mL of chilled 2 mmoL EDTA in DPBS (EDTA-DPBS) and homogenized for 30 s three times with a zirconium oxide bead homogenizer at 300 rpm. The homogenate was run through a cell strainer twice and was washed with 4 mL of chilled EDTA-DPBS. The suspension was then spun down at 500 cfg for 5 min and resuspended in 0.5 mL of chilled FACs buffer (3% FBS and 10 mmol EDTA in DPBS). Cell count for the suspension was conducted using an Invitrogen Countess II automated cell counter using Trypan blue dye and a double-chamber cell counter slide (Invitrogen). One million viable cells were used for each test. Two panels were utilized, a T cell, NK cell and cancer cell panel and a Treg panel. The T cell, NK cell and cancer cell panel consisted of APC/Cyanine7 antimouse CD3a (Biolegend, clone: 17A2), Brilliant Violet 711 antimouse CD8b (Biolegend, YTS156.7.7), PE/Cyanine7 antimouse CD4 (Biolegend, clone: GK1.5), PE antimouse NKp46 (Biolegend, clone: 29A1.4), APC 421 antimouse PD-L1 (Biolegend, clone: 10F.9G2), FITC antimouse CD45 (Biolegend, clone: 30-F11), Brilliant Violet 421 antimouse CD47 (MIAP301), and Zombie Aqua dye (Biolegend) for assessing viability. The Treg panel consisted of PerCP-Cyanine5.5 antimouse CD3e (eBioscience, clone: 145–2C11), PE/Cyanine7 antimouse CD4, FITC antimouse CD25 (Biolegend, clone: PC61), APC antimouse FoxP3 (eBioscience, clone: FJK-16s), and Zombie Aqua dye for assessing the viability. Selecting a single tumor (with more cells), two tubes of 1 million cells were pipetted for every marker in the stain, one for a single stain control and one for a fluorescence-minus-one control (FMO) that was used during gating.

Prior to adding markers, 1 μg of Ultra-Leaf purified antimouse CD16/32 (Biolegend, clone: 93) was added to block nonspecific immunoglobulin IgG binding. Then, one test of Zombie Aqua dye was added as a live/dead stain and allowed to incubate at room temperature for 5 min. Following that, each of the sample extracellular panels were added, and the cells were allowed to incubate for 30 min at 4 °C and then spun down at 500 cfg for 5 min. For the T cell, NK cell and cancer cell panel, the cells were resuspended in 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS fixative for 5 min. The fixed cells were then spun down once more at 500 cfg, and the cells were finally suspended in ∼100 μL of FACs buffer. The cells with the FoxP3 intracellular stain, following incubation with extracellular stains, were resuspended in 1 mL of a FoxP3 fixation/permeabilization working solution in which they were incubated for 30 min at room temperature, after which, 2 mL of 1× permeabilization buffer was added, and the samples were spun down at 500 cfg for 5 min. They were resuspended in ∼100 μL of permeabilization buffer, and 1 μg of Foxp3 intracellular antibody was added, and they were allowed to incubate for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were once again washed by adding 2 mL of 1× permeabilization buffer and spun down at 500 g for 5 min.

Following preparation, the samples were submitted to the ISU Office of Biotechnology Flow Cytometry for analysis. Compensated FACs data was analyzed with gating strategies using FlowJo software and as depicted in Figures S3 and S5.

Ex Vivo Immunofluorescence Imaging

Following necropsy, a portion of tumor tissue was frozen in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) solution for 5-μm-thick sections taken on poly-l-lysine-coated slides (Electron Microscopy Sciences) and fixed in ice-cold methanol for 15–20 s. The slides were then washed twice with 37 °C PBS for 5 min to remove the remaining OCT. Tissues were incubated in a blocking buffer (0.1% Tween, 1% BSA (Sigma), and 22.52 mg/mL glycine (Sigma) in PBS) for 30 min at room temperature. Following incubation, the buffer was decanted, and the sections were incubated with a fluorescence-tagged primary antibody overnight at 4 °C diluted in 0.1% Tween in PBS (PBST). Antibodies used included FITC-labeled antimouse CD8a (Biolegend, clone: 53–6.7), FITC-labeled antimouse NKp46 (Biolegend, clone: 29A1.4), Alexa Fluor 647-labeled antimouse PD-L1 (BD Biosciences, clone: 10F.9G2), and Alexa Fluor 647-labeled antimouse Ki67 (Biolegend, clone: 16A8). The next day, they were washed with PBST for 5 min five times, and then DAPI Prolong Gold (Invitrogen) was added to stain for cell nuclei. The slides were sealed with coverslips, and the DAPI was allowed to incubate overnight at 4 °C. Fluorescence images were captured with an Echo Revolve microscope with both 5× and 20× magnification and an inverted fluorescent microscope Leica DMI4000 B with 20× magnification, and the 5× images were quantified using ImageJ of the fluorescence signal channel.

Ex Vivo Serum Cytokine Multibead Assay

Cytokine assays were performed using homemade microbeads and Bioplex 2000 system for a multibead assay. Serum collected during necropsy was diluted 3-fold with Luminex working buffer. 96-well plates were prepared with magnetic microbeads and a magnetic plate washer, and 50 μL of dilute serum was incubated with the bead cocktail for 2 h. Excess volume was removed with the plate washer, and biotin-labeled secondary antibodies were added. These antibodies included antimouse IL-1β (Invitrogen, polyclonal), antimouse IL-2 (Invitrogen, clone: JES6-5H4), antimouse IL-6 (Invitrogen, clone: MP5-32C11), antimouse IL-12p40 (Invitrogen, clone: C17.8), antimouse IFN-γ (Invitrogen, clone: R4-6A2), and antimouse TNF-α (Invitrogen, clone: MP6-XT3 and MP6-XT22). After incubation with the secondary antibodies for 1 h, streptavidin/PE (SAPE) dye (Sigma) was added. SAPE was incubated for 30 min, and the plates were washed with Luminex working buffer for 3 min before running the multibead assay. Cytokine concentrations were determined by comparison to a standard curve.

Ex Vivo Spleen Immunohistochemistry

Murine spleens were collected during autopsy and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (Sigma) for 48–72 h. Following fixation, samples were transferred into 70% ethanol and submitted to Iowa State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratories for paraffin embedding, staining, and whole tissue scanning. CD8 marker area % was quantified with ImageJ by using negative control to set the binary brightness threshold.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Functionalized MGNs

GNs were synthesized with a one-step seedless process using a 2-[4-(2-hydroxyethyl)­piperazin-1-yl]­ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer as a capping and reduction agent to facilitate nanostar formation. To achieve multiplexing in vivo, two different Raman reporters were conjugated to GNs (Figure a). These reporters included para-mercaptobenzoic acid (pMBA) and 5,5-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB). DTNB and pMBA are aromatic thiol molecules that can covalently bind to the gold surface and enable a strong Raman signal generating high signal-to-noise in vivo. − Targeting of multiple immune cell types was enabled by functionalizing pMBA-conjugated GNs with anti-CD8 mAbs to target CD8+ T cells, and DTNB-conjugated GNs with anti-NKp46 mAbs to target NKp46+ NK cells. Anti-CD8 and anti-NKp46 mAbs on GNs bind to the respective surface markers, which are unique to these specific lymphocytes enabling targeted detection in vivo with high specificity. , Subsequently, GNs were further functionalized with 2-S-(4-isothiocyanatobenzyl)-1,4,7-triazacyclononane-1,4,7-triacetic acid (NOTA), a high-yield chelator for 64Cu that has been used in preclinical and clinical PET. , The mAbs and chelator were covalently conjugated to GNs via a bifunctional linker OPSS-PEG-SVA (ortho-pyridyl disulfide polyethylene glycol succinimidyl valerate, 2000 kDa). Binding between the chelator and OPSS-PEG-SVA was verified by using liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LC-MS) (Figure b). The disulfide bond breaks facilitating covalent Au–S bonds, and the succinimidyl valerate that is an NHS-activated linker allowed for the formation of an amide bond with either the chelator or antibodies. Postfunctionalization, the complete MGNs were concentrated through centrifugation and resuspended at 3–6 mg/mL in sterile Milli-Q water.

The MGNs were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to assess their overall anisotropic morphology (Figure c). MGNs showed a ∼40 nm red shift over the functionalization process via UV–vis–NIR spectroscopy (Figure d). A ∼10 nm shift was seen after Raman reporter labeling, an ∼20 nm shift was seen after mAbs conjugation, and an ∼10 nm shift was seen after chelator functionalization. Despite this shift, MGNs demonstrated a stable near-infrared (NIR) resonance that overlapped with the 785 nm Raman laser wavelength to enable a strong SERS effect. Average hydrodynamic diameter indicated a final average size of 67.6 ± 1.0 nm postfunctionalization, amenable to in vivo studies (Figure e). Surface charge measurements showed a near-neutral charge postfunctionalization (−10.6 ± 1.1 mV) ideal for in vivo localization (Figure f). Raman spectra of the two different MGNs (GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/64Cu-NOTA and GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/64Cu-NOTA) at a 1:1 ratio indicated that both immunomarkers can be imaged simultaneously with minimal overlap (Figure g). In addition to the primary peaks of pMBA (1075 cm–1) and DTNB (1325 cm–1), the secondary peaks at 1555 cm–1 for DTNB and 1585 cm–1 for pMBA were also distinct. The chelation of MGN-bound NOTA with 64Cu (i.e., radiolabeling) was measured with radio-instant thin layer chromatography (iTLC), using 50 mM pH 5 ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) as a competitive chelator. MGNs had a high radiolabeling efficiency (84.8% ± 7.2%) after 1.5 h of radiolabeling at room temperature (Figure h) with minimal aggregation (Figure i) that enabled serum stability in vivo. To validate chelator stability in biological conditions, 64Cu radiolabeled MGNs were incubated for 20 h at 37 °C in a 1:1 ratio with fetal bovine serum (FBS) to approximate a biological environment. Radiolabeling efficiency was measured before and after incubation and showed a statistically insignificant decrease in activity over a period that corresponded to the immunoPET/CT imaging time point (Figure j).

Biodistribution and Targeting of MGNs

The ability of MGNs to target tumors and show stable and targeted imaging of T cells and NK cells was assessed by first examining Raman imaging and radio-biodistribution in 4T1 and CT26 murine tumors without any treatment (Figure a). Mice bearing an average of 80–100 mm3 4T1 tumors (at 10 days after inoculation) (Figure b–d) and CT26 tumors (at 12 days after inoculation) (Figure e–g) were imaged before MGNs delivery to obtain 0 h Raman spectral data. Mice then received intraperitoneal (IP) delivery via injection through the lower abdomen into the peritoneal cavity of 200 μL of 3 mg/mL aqueous MGNs (0.6 mg total) chelated with cold CuCl2 at 1:1 ratio of GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/CuCl2-NOTA + GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/CuCl2‑NOTA. To assess the specificity of MGNs in targeted detection of CD8+ T cells and NKp46+ NK cells, in the blocked control group, mice received an IP injection of blocking mAbs consisting of 150 μg of anti-CD8 and 100 μg of anti-NKp46 in 100 μL of PBS. The blocker injection was followed by the delivery of MGNs 5 min later. Our rationale was that the blocking mAbs delivered in vivo was at a 300–500 molar excess to the mAbs conjugated to MGNs and hence would serve as a competitive receptor blocker to prevent binding of MGNs to the respective receptors. Raman spectral imaging was conducted at 6, 20, and 48 h time points post MGNs delivery. These time points were chosen based on the 12.7 h half-life of 64Cu as well as logistical limitations for immunoPET and RS imaging. For in vivo RS imaging, points were sampled randomly through the tumor periphery. To obtain high SNR in vivo, the confocal Raman imaging parameters were optimized where the focal point was offset −100 μm below the surface of the tumor to reject any surface-level tissue background. More details are provided in the methods section (Materials and Methods, In vivo Raman imaging ). The processed spectra were then normalized to the 1448 cm–1 background band, which corresponds to long-chain and structural biomolecules such as proteins and lipids. The final spectra were averaged and further normalized to the background tissue signal in each of the Raman labels signature bands, i.e., 1075 cm–1 for GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/64Cu-NOTA, and 1325 cm–1 for GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/64Cu-NOTA. Raman spectral imaging showed that the highest sensitivity was at the initial 6 h time point in both 4T1 (Figure b) and CT26 models (Figure e) with a significantly higher signal in the experimental group relative to blocked controls. Longitudinal imaging showed a reduction in signal over time (Figure c,d,f,g). It is possible that MGNs also bind to other cell types in the TME nonspecifically via Fc or scavenger receptors, yet based on the blocked control Raman signal seen in Figure , we expect such off-target effects do not overshadow the targeted detection of T cells and NK cells through our approach. Radio-biodistribution of 64Cu radiolabeled MGNs was also measured in the CT26 tumor model. Radiolabeling was performed in an aqueous, unbuffered solution with a gentle inversion for 1.5 h, with a ratio of 0.8–1 milliCurie (mCi) of 64Cu activity per 1 mg of MGNs. 0.1 mCi of radiolabeled MGNs were mixed with nonradiolabeled GNs such that 0.1 mCi of activity is achieved in vivo (sufficient for biodistribution in organs) and the total amount of MGNs injected (0.6 mg) was equivalent to that in the Raman imaging experiment. Longitudinal radio-biodistribution was performed in both experimental and blocked control groups. At each time point (6, 24, and 48 h) the mice were euthanized, and the tumor and other tissues were collected for quantitative activity measurement with a γ counter. Radiolabeling biodistribution indicates a different sensitivity for the immunoPET signal with MGNs where maximum sensitivity is observed at the 48 h time point (Figure h). The differences observed in Raman and immunoPET maximum sensitivity time points are attributed to the intrinsic differences between each technique. Raman enables early periphery binding, while the immunoPET signal from MGNs is observed in the deep tissue over time enabling a key multimodal insight. Raman has a limited depth penetration, , and the main contributions to the signal are when MGNs are at the tumor periphery at early time points. At longer time points as MGNs are transported away from the periphery and deeper within the tumor, a decrease in Raman signal is seen. The radio-biodistribution measurements also present some challenges and limitations. At early time points, any unchelated activity can migrate more freely than radiolabeled MGNs bound to the target immune cells, increasing the noise in the data collected and reducing sensitivity. As unbound activity is excreted, bound 64Cu activity to the MGNs remains in the TME that enabled γ count measurements. These differences in sensitivity between immunoPET and Raman spectral imaging works to our advantage and can be leveraged with MGNs for Raman-based spatial profiling of tumors at early time points and immunoPET-based distribution of MGNs within the tumor interior at later time points for comprehensive immunomarker imaging. Radio-biodistribution of MGNs in most other tissues (Figure i) shows overall low distribution, except in organs of the reticuloendothelial system (RES, liver, spleen, kidney). MGNs distribution in RES organs peaked at 48 h indicating the time frame when clearance begins.

ImmunoPET and RS Imaging of Treatment Response in CT26 Tumor Responder Model

Next, MGNs were used for treatment response to immunotherapies where Balb/cJ mice bearing immunologically hot CT26 tumors were given 4 doses of 200 μg of anti-PD-L1 + 120 μg of anti-CD47 in 100 μL of PBS via IP injection every other day (Figure a). Control mice were given isotype-matched 200 μg of IgG2a + 120 μg of IgG2b in 100 μL of PBS. Treatment began once the tumors reached an average estimated volume of 80–100 mm3 to allow for lymphocyte infiltration into the tumor. The day after the fourth dose, baseline in vivo Raman measurements were taken for each tumor. Two days following the last treatment dose, 0.6 mg of MGNs radiolabeled with 0.7–0.9 mCi of 64Cu (GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/64Cu-NOTA + GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/64Cu-NOTA) were IP-delivered in mice, and immunoimaging in vivo was conducted with immunoPET/CT-Raman. Note that this imaging regimen is predictive of early response to treatment and recapitulates imaging criteria adopted in patients to allow for pseudoprogression observed in immunotherapies (NCT02453984 and NCT02478099). CT26 is responsive to this combination immunotherapy, as evidenced by tumor growth suppression (Figure b). The bodyweight and tumor volume of each individual mouse are represented in Figure S1. Raman spectral imaging showed a significant treatment response in CT26 tumors relative to the IgG control (Figure c). Recruitment of both NK cells tracked with GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/64Cu-NOTA (Figure d) and T cells tracked with GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/64Cu-NOTA (Figure e) enabled significant longitudinal differences between the treated and control groups up to the 18–20 h time point. The ∼18 h time point was therefore considered appropriate to acquire immunoPET/CT images to measure treatment response and to account for 12.7 h half-life of 64Cu. For the immunoPET/CT imaging, 20 min was sufficient to achieve a signal for PET imaging followed by 2 min of CT imaging. Tumor PET signal (bq/cc) was quantified via ROI analysis, time-normalized based on the 64Cu half-life to the injection time, and then normalized to the injection dose for values of a unit of ID%/cc. The immunoPET/CT images showed significant differences in response in the treated tumors (Figure f). MGNs accumulation in the organs of the RES (liver, spleen, and kidney) showed no difference between the treatment groups compared to the IgG control (Figure g–j).

Ex Vivo Validation of Treatment Response in CT26 Tumor Responder Model

Treatment response to combinatorial immunotherapies in vivo with immunoPET-Raman was also validated with standard clinical and preclinical assays including serum cytokine analysis with Luminex, and tissue immune analysis with IF imaging of tissues and FACS of tumor cells to examine activated T and NK cell recruitment in the TME. Figure S2 shows tumor volumes, tumor weight, bodyweight, and spleen weight for these validation studies. The tumors were divided appropriately for preparing sections for the IF and single-cell suspensions for FACs. IF images were captured at ×5 for quantification of fluorescence signal and at ×20 for representative detail images. Quantitative IF analysis (representative images in Figure a) showed an increase in CD8+ T cell and NKp46+ NK cell recruitment (Figure b,c) in the treatment group relative to the IgG control that corresponds well to the observed differences in Raman imaging in vivo. We also observed a significant reduction in PD-L1 expression in tumors in the treatment group (Figure d) as well as a decrease in proliferation marker, Ki67 expression (Figure e). CD8 T cell recruitment was also assessed in spleen via immunohistochemistry, but no significant difference was seen in recruitment at this early treatment response time frame (Figure S3). Cytokine analysis in mice sera via Luminex (Figure f–k) showed that IFN-γ, a key inflammatory cytokine produced by T cells and NK cells, was significantly higher in the treated mice (Figure j) indicative of a cytotoxic antitumor immune response facilitated by the combination immunotherapy. In addition, IFN-γ also upregulates PD-L1, which may improve the function of anti-PD-L1 mAbs in our combinatorial immunotherapy in maintaining the immune response. A significant increase in serum IL-12p40 is also observed in the treatment mice (Figure i); IL-12 facilitates the activation of both NK cells and T cells, promoting a Th1 response in T helper cells specifically and promoting production of IFN-γ and TNF-α. , While no significant differences in IL-2 were seen between the two groups (Figure g), IL-2 levels were detectable in both treated and control CT26 tumor-bearing mice. In contrast, it was below the limit of detection in the poor responder 4T1 tumor-bearing mice (shown in Figure ). IL-2 drives the survival, proliferation, and differentiation of activated T and NK cells but IL-2 balances immunostimulation with immunosuppression in the TME and therefore changes in IL-2 are not always immediate after cancer therapies. FACS of tumor cells was conducted to stain for cytotoxic T cells and NK cells and tumor cells (Figure S4). These flow cytometry results corroborate the findings of NKp46+ NK cells (Figure l) and CD8+ T cell (Figure m) infiltrations in tumors that were also tracked with MGNs (shown in Figure ). No significant changes are seen for CD4+ T cells (Figure n), but as both antitumor CD4 subtypes (Th1) and protumor subtypes (Treg) may be influenced by treatment, this lack of a significant net shift in recruitment is expected. While we do not see a decrease in CD47 expression by tumor cells (Figure o), we do see a significant decrease in PD-L1+ expression, showing that the treatment was able to modulate the PD-1/PD-L1 axis of immune evasion, thus leading to a robust immune response in PD-1-expressing cells like NK cells and CD8+ T cells.

We also assessed the impact of chemotherapies combined with immunotherapies, where oxaliplatin was combined with antiPD-L1 + antiCD47 in the CT26 tumor model (Figure S5), with a treatment schedule that alternates IP delivery of oxaliplatin doses with the immunotherapy delivery. Our hypothesis was oxaliplatin will induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) that drives danger-associated molecular patterns in the tumor microenvironment and triggers an immune response by elevating proinflammatory cytokines and strengthening the immunostimulatory effects of native tumor antigens. ICD activates previously nonresponsive “cold” tumors to “hot” phenotype by stimulating antigen-presenting cell cross-presentation of tumor antigens that then recruits cytotoxic T cells in the TME. However, from the tumor volumes and tumor weight (Figure S5a,b), we found that the chemotherapy + immunotherapy group was equivalent in treatment efficacy relative to the immunotherapy only group, although the chemotherapy + immunotherapy group did have a significantly lower spleen weight (Figure S5c). Further, the recruitment of CD8+ T cells in the TME was not significantly different between the chemotherapy + immunotherapy and immunotherapy only group (Figure S5d), and we saw a significant decrease in CD4+ T cell recruitment for the chemotherapy + immunotherapy versus the immunotherapy only group (Figure S5e). We also stained for regulatory T cells (Tregs) using an additional intracellular panel (CD3e, CD4, CD25, and FoxP3) (Figure S6), which showed chemotherapy + immunotherapy failed to decrease Treg infiltration in the TME versus the control and immunotherapy only groups (Figure S4f). Based on these findings, we chose to pursue the antiPD-L1 + antiCD47 immunotherapy (immunotherapy only) treatment, as the addition of the alternating chemotherapy doses did not show improved outcomes.

ImmunoPET and RS Imaging of Treatment Response in 4T1 Tumor Poor Responder Model

Next, the ability of MGNs to distinguish responders from poor responders to immunotherapies was examined in an immunologically cold 4T1 model that is nonresponsive to antiPD-L1 + antiCD47 immunotherapy. The 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were treated with a similar regimen as the CT26 tumor model with combinatorial immunotherapy and IgG mAbs in control mice, and a mixture of MGNs (GNs/anti-NKp46/DTNB/64Cu-NOTA probes + GNs/anti-CD8/pMBA/64Cu-NOTA) was IP delivered 2 days after the last treatment dose. In the 4T1 model, treatment was started early when the tumors became palpable at a volume around 25 mm3, which was necessary due to the rapid and aggressive growth of the tumors (Figure a). The tumor growth curves show that the treatment was ineffective in this model with no statistical difference between the immunotherapy-treated and IgG control groups (Figure b). Tumor volumes of individual mice and body weights can be seen in Figure S7. There was minimal change observed in the Raman spectra (Figure c) of treatment groups, and longitudinal tracking of NK cells via the signal of DTNB-conjugated MGNs (Figure d) and that of T cells via the pMBA-conjugated MGNs (Figure e) showed minimal differences between the immunotherapy-treated and IgG control groups as anticipated for poor responders. Representative immunoPET/CT images acquired at the ∼18 h time point (Figure f) and the corresponding quantification in tumors (Figure g) showed no significant difference between the treatment and control groups. Likewise, no significant difference was seen in the organs of the RES (Figure h–j). Note that the biodistribution study in the 4T1 tumor shown in Figure confirmed that MGNs allow active targeting of immune cells in the tumor and tumor periphery, while this treatment study indicates that immunologically cold 4T1 tumors are resistant to this combinatorial immunotherapy and immunoPET-Raman enabled with MGNs is a powerful approach to predict response in poor responders in real-time in vivo.

Ex Vivo Validation of Treatment Response in 4T1 Tumor Poor Responder Model

Treatment response in vivo with immunoPET-Raman in the 4T1 tumor model was also validated with standard assays, including cytokine analysis and immune cell analysis. These ex vivo analyses corresponded well to our in vivo end points, where no significant difference was observed in tumor volumes or weights between the treatment tumors and IgG control (Figure S8a,b), although a significant increase in spleen weight was seen (Figure S8c). The tumor volumes and body weights of individual mice are represented in Figure S8d–g. For the IF imaging (representative images in Figure a), there were no significant differences in either CD8+ T cells or NKp46+ NK cell recruitment (Figure b,c) and no differences were observed in PD-L1 or Ki67 expression (Figure d,e) between the immunotherapy-treated and IgG control groups. No significant differences were also observed across cytokines (Figure f–j), except in IL-6 that showed a significant increase in the treated mice (Figure g), and IL-2 levels were below the limit of detection. An increase in serum IL-6 may indicate systemic increase in inflammatory response from the immunotherapies but that may not directly correlate to the tumor response. Further, no significant difference was observed in NK cells (Figure k), CD8+ and CD4+ T cells recruitment (Figure l,m), and minimal change in expression of CD47 but some decrease in PD-L1 expression in the cancer cells (Figure n,o). These results validate the in vivo imaging results, where immunoPET-Raman demonstrated the limited ability of this specific immunotherapy to elicit a successful immune response in the 4T1 model.

Conclusions

In summary, this impactful work demonstrates the design of MGNs as an effective contrast probe that synergistically integrates clinical immunoPET with preclinical Raman imaging, allowing the accurate assessment of dynamic changes in multiple immunomarkers simultaneously in vivo. Our results show that MGNs accurately monitor response to combinatorial checkpoint blockade immunotherapies by multiplexed tracking of both CD8+ T cells and NK cells in vivo to distinguish responders (CT26 tumor model) from poor responders (4T1 tumor model) of treatment. The in vivo immunoPET-Raman imaging results were validated ex vivo with immune cell and cytokine analyses that confirmed the overall merit of our approach. The outcomes of this work will ultimately guide clinical decisions in treatment planning, improve patient response to immunotherapies, and identify alternative therapies for those resistant to therapies early in the treatment regimen. MGNs are also highly clinically translatable as gold nanoparticles are in use in human pilot studies, − ImmunoPET is already in the clinic, and Raman imaging has been implemented in patients, in clinical trials (NCT00060580, NCT06384924), and was recently adopted in Veteran’s Affair Boston and Tampa healthcare systems. , MGNs also represent a highly generalizable platform that can ultimately be mapped onto various cancer models and other disease models such as infection and neurodegenerative diseases where these immune cells play a key role. Our future work will also focus on improving the “multiplexed color palette” for in vivo imaging, enabling the simultaneous targeting of a larger subset of biomarkers with a greater number of Raman reporters. We envision that our approach will enable a powerful tool for profiling heterogeneous response in cancer and other diseases and ultimately have significant clinical implications on saving time, unnecessary toxicities, and high costs of failed therapies.
