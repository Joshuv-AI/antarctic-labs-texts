Addressing the Challenges of Translating LRRK2 Biology into Disease‐Modifying Therapies: The LRRK2 Investigative Therapeutics Exchange Initiative
Authors: Esther Sammler, Francesca Tonelli, Michelle Durborow, Jim McNasby, Todd Sherer, Mahmood Ahmed, Stevan W Djuric, Kalpana Merchant, Alastair Reith, Darryle Schoepp, Christine Klein, Shalini Padmanabhan, Dario R Alessi
Published: 2026

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a common and growing neurological disorder without a therapy that slows or halts progression, despite decades of investment and an increasingly sophisticated mechanistic understanding of its underlying biology. Among targets emerging from the genomic era, leucine‐rich repeat kinase 2 (LRRK2) stands out for its robust genetic validation, mechanistic clarity, and pharmacological tractability. Importantly, a pipeline of LRRK2‐targeted therapies has emerged. The first major efficacy readout from an LRRK2 kinase inhibitor program has now been reported: the Phase 2b LUMA study of BIIB122 (DNL151) did not demonstrate a clinical benefit in an unstratified cohort of early‐stage PD patients. 1 Rather than diminishing the rationale for targeting LRRK2, these findings highlight critical questions that remain unresolved, including the degree of target engagement required to achieve therapeutic benefit in the brain and the identification of patient populations most likely to respond to LRRK2‐directed therapies. The results therefore reinforce the need for a coordinated, multidisciplinary strategy to develop next‐generation LRRK2‐targeted interventions, biomarkers, and patient stratification approaches. 1 , 2 The successful delivery of LRRK2‐targeted therapies will depend on actively diversifying therapeutic strategies, refining trial design through biomarker integration, and advancing precision neurology approaches aligned with disease biology. The limiting factor is not the target but how it is being tested.

LRRK2 occupies a unique position in the PD landscape. It is one of the most common causes of autosomal dominant PD and a significant risk factor for sporadic disease. 3 The G2019S variant alone accounts for ~1% to 2% of PD cases globally, increasing to ~30% in certain North African Arab and Ashkenazi Jewish populations. 4 Beyond coding variants, genome‐wide association studies implicate noncoding LRRK2 variants in modulating PD risk, 5 whereas environmental exposures linked to PD activate the pathway. 6 , 7 Together, these data position LRRK2 at the intersection of genetic predisposition and environmental susceptibility across the PD spectrum.

Pathogenic LRRK2 variants converge on a shared functional consequence: kinase hyperactivation that disrupts endolysosomal biology, autophagy, and ciliogenesis across neuronal and peripheral compartments. This biological coherence is matched by pharmacological tractability. 8 LRRK2 is currently being targeted by multiple therapeutic modalities in clinical trials, including ATP‐competitive type I kinase inhibitors, targeted degradation strategies (PROTACs), and antisense oligonucleotides (ASO) (https://zenodo.org/records/15604012). However, the full therapeutic potential of LRRK2 targeting remains only partially realized, and additional approaches, particularly those that exploit alternative mechanisms of modulation, represent important opportunities for future therapeutic development.

The central challenge is therefore not biological validity but trial architecture. Studies restricted to carriers of pathogenic LRRK2 variants face operational challenges, whereas trials in unselected PD populations risk diluting treatment effects within biologically heterogeneous participants. These limitations are compounded by uncertainty around optimal disease stage, endpoint sensitivity, optimal doses and target modulation(s) needed for meaningful clinical benefit, and safety at effective doses—particularly in peripheral tissues such as the lung. A coordinated, biomarker‐informed strategy is required to address these interdependent constraints simultaneously.

The LRRK2 Investigative Therapeutics Exchange (LITE) (https://www.michaeljfox.org/lrrk2-investigative-therapeutics-exchange-lite), established by The Michael J. Fox Foundation for Parkinson's Research in 2024, provides such a framework. Organized across three integrated pillars, LITE aligns biomarker‐driven patient stratification, safety definition, and mechanistic diversification to enable interpretable and ultimately successful clinical trials. In this viewpoint, we argue that the limiting factor for LRRK2 is no longer biological plausibility but how, when, and in whom the target is being tested. We propose that fully realizing the potential of LRRK2 as a precision‐medicine target will depend on three interdependent elements: (1) rigorous definition of pulmonary and systemic safety, (2) biomarker‐guided identification of sporadic PD patients with “LRRK2‐like” biology, and (3) systematic comparison of mechanistically diverse therapeutic strategies within a shared precompetitive framework. We outline how the LITE Initiative has been structured to address these requirements and suggest that similar collaborative models will be essential for advancing other validated targets in PD and beyond.

Translational Challenges in LRRK2‐Targeted Therapy

Safety: The Lung Liability

All clinical programs targeting LRRK2 kinase activity with small molecules to date have exploited type 1 inhibitors—ATP‐competitive compounds that bind the active kinase conformation. These agents potently suppress LRRK2 activity and reduce phosphorylation of the substrate Rab10. 10 However, sustained suppression of LRRK2 signaling in the lung, particularly at exposures that likely ablate the pathway, has been associated in preclinical studies with vacuolization of alveolar type II cells and a fibrotic phenotype. 11 , 12 This concern is not unique to kinase inhibitors: any strategy that substantially reduces LRRK2 activity or protein levels in peripheral tissues—including degraders and antisense Oligonucleotides (ASOs)—may carry analogous risks. Emerging human genetic evidence further supports an essential physiological role for LRRK2 in pulmonary homeostasis. 13 Encouragingly, inhibitors currently in clinical trials have not produced reported pulmonary safety signals, suggesting that the partial pathway inhibition with current agents may be sufficient for preserving lung function, but therapeutic benefit at these doses is unknown. Nevertheless, a major unresolved challenge is to define the extent, duration, and tissue‐specific context of LRRK2 pathway suppression required to deliver therapeutic benefit while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. Addressing this question is complicated by the limited understanding of how currently available peripheral and cerebrospinal fluid biomarkers such as pRab10 levels relate to target engagement and pathway modulation within the disease‐relevant regions and cell types of the brain. These priorities are reflected in the LITE framework (Table 1).

An additional mechanistic concern with type 1 inhibitors is that they stabilize LRRK2 in an active, pathogenic conformation, promoting dissociation from 14‐3‐3 proteins and aberrant association with microtubules—effects linked to impaired intracellular transport and a potential propensity for protein aggregation. 14 The clinical relevance of these findings remains uncertain, but they underscore the importance of developing mechanistically distinct inhibitor classes that stabilize LRRK2 in its inactive, 14‐3‐3‐associated conformation for direct comparison. We therefore advocate prospectively incorporating lung‐specific functional readouts and exposure limits into LRRK2‐targeted trials, and prioritizing regimens that achieve partial, tissue‐tuned pathway inhibition rather than maximal systemic suppression.

Patient Selection and the LRRK2‐Like Disease Concept

Pathogenic LRRK2 variants account for ~4% to 5% of familial PD and 1% to 2% of sporadic cases, figures driven largely by the p.G2019S and p.R1441 hotspot variants. 15 Moreover, these variants show incomplete penetrance, with many carriers never developing disease, further restricting eligible trial populations. 16 Recruiting sufficient numbers for early‐stage and registration trials requires extending eligibility to sporadic patients in whom LRRK2 pathway hyperactivation is a biologically relevant driver—yet no validated biomarker currently enables this with adequate sensitivity or specificity. 17 Measurement of Rab10 phosphorylation in peripheral blood cells, although informative in certain genotype‐specific contexts, has not proven sufficiently robust to identify LRRK2‐like pathology in sporadic disease. 18

LRRK2‐associated PD is also characterized by substantial biological heterogeneity. Postmortem studies reveal pleiomorphic neuropathology, with classical Lewy body pathology observed less frequently than in idiopathic PD, and a higher prevalence of tau pathology, pure nigrostriatal degeneration, and mixed pathologies. 19 In vivo, positivity for the α‐synuclein seed amplification assay (αSyn‐SAA) in the cerebrospinal fluid is significantly lower in LRRK2‐associated PD than in idiopathic disease. 3 Recent studies have delineated biologically and clinically distinct αSyn‐SAA‐positive and αSyn‐SAA‐negative subgroups within LRRK2‐PD, differing in olfactory function, rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder, and sex distribution. 20 However, αSyn‐SAA negativity should not necessarily be interpreted as absence of α‐synuclein involvement, as studies using complementary approaches, including proximity ligation assays, suggest that α‐synuclein may still contribute to pathogenesis even in biomarker‐negative LRRK2‐PD cases. 21 , 22 These observations indicate that LRRK2 parkinsonism does not represent a single biological entity and that therapeutic responses, including dose and mechanisms of target modulation, may vary substantially across mutation carriers and nonmutation carriers with an LRRK2 signature—with direct implications for trial stratification. Wherever feasible, future disease‐modifying trials should move beyond purely genotype‐based inclusion and be powered and stratified around biomarker‐defined “LRRK2‐like” biology, accepting added complexity in exchange for interpretable signals of target engagement and clinical response.

The LITE Initiative: A Precompetitive Framework

LITE is organized around three integrated pillars, each targeting a distinct translational bottleneck (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Pillar 1: Diversifying LRRK2 Therapeutic Approaches

Pillar 1 is founded on a no‐stone‐unturned philosophy to identify and characterize new modalities for targeting the LRRK2 pathway beyond conventional type 1 kinase inhibitors or regulation of LRRK2 protein level. LRRK2 is a large, multidomain protein with multiple pharmacologically tractable regions. 8 Rather than betting on a single modality, pillar 1 tests the hypothesis that different clinical questions may require distinct ways of modulating LRRK2, ranging from conformational rewiring of the kinase to indirect pathway modulation.

Key approaches being explored include type 2 LRRK2 kinase inhibitors, which unlike type 1 compounds suppress kinase activity by stabilizing the inactive, 14‐3‐3‐associated conformation 23 ; allosteric inhibitors targeting intramolecular regulatory interactions to achieve the same outcome; LRRK2 degraders and molecular glue compounds, including efforts to enhance LRRK2 binding to 14‐3‐3 (Fig. 2); and indirect strategies that counteract aberrant LRRK2 signaling, including the activation of the PPM1H phosphatase, which opposes LRRK2 by dephosphorylating Rab substrates, 24 and enhancement of lysosomal function through modulation of bis(monoacylglycero)phosphate (BMP) levels, a key lipid regulator of lysosomal homeostasis. 25 The full portfolio, including eight pharmacologically tractable binding pockets and five complementary pathway‐based strategies, is detailed in Supplementary Section S1.

All projects are supported by a standardized assay platform (S2. LITE Standardized Assay Platform) and structural enablement to guide rational optimization, with predefined go/no‐go decision points overseen by senior advisors with large‐pharma drug discovery experience. A critical priority within pillar 1 is head‐to‐head benchmarking of alternative LRRK2‐targeting strategies against established type 1 inhibitors. The development and validation of lung‐specific screening platforms, including alveolar organoid and in vivo models developed with an independent pulmonary biology team, to enable early detection of compound‐specific effects on surfactant secretion and alveolar cell function, is central to this effort. By incorporating these readouts upstream, pillar 1 aims to triage compounds in preclinical in vitro and in vivo assays to define exposures and target modulations that may be beneficial without the risk of suppressing physiological LRRK2 function in the lung. Collectively, these efforts are designed not simply to expand the menu of LRRK2‐targeting agents but to generate the comparative data needed to decide whether LRRK2 should be inhibited, rewired, or indirectly modulated in different clinical contexts.

Pillar 2: Accelerating Industry LRRK2 Therapeutic Programs

Pillar 2 provides preclinical‐ and clinical‐stage companies with access to standardized in vitro and in vivo assay platforms (S2. LITE Standardized Assay Platform), enabling objective, benchmarking‐grade comparative profiling of LRRK2‐targeting molecules (S3. Pillar 2: Precompetitive Model and Benchmarking Framework). LITE is currently engaging with about 25 companies spanning a wide range of LRRK2‐targeting modalities. Operating under a precompetitive “give‐and‐take” model, LITE conducts experimental work on behalf of participating companies while proprietary data remain confidential to the company contributing the testing agent. In return, companies are encouraged to contribute compounds, datasets, or clinical trial samples back to the consortium, with findings intended for open publication. Initial benchmarking has incorporated advanced type 1 tool compounds contributed by two companies participating in pillar 2, and there are plans to extend to type 2 inhibitors and degraders. This shared infrastructure enables companies to make better‐informed development decisions, de‐risk programs, and advance the most promising assets more efficiently toward clinical evaluation.

Pillar 3: LRRK2‐Relevant Clinical Biomarkers

Pillar 3 is dedicated to accelerating the discovery and validation of biologically informed clinical biomarkers to enable effective patient stratification for LRRK2‐targeted trials as well as provide objective evidence of LRRK2 pathway modulation (Fig. 3; Supplementary Section S4). A central conceptual aim is to move beyond categorical, genotype‐based definitions toward a biologically grounded framework for “LRRK2‐like” PD. This encompasses biomarker assessment in established pathogenic LRRK2 variants (eg, p.G2019S, p.R1441G/C/H), more common Asian risk variants (eg, p.G2385R, p.R1628P), rare kinase‐activating LRRK2 variants, 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 and pathogenic variants in genes that converge on the LRRK2 pathway, most notably VPS35 p.D620N 30 and emerging candidates such as RAB32 p.S71R. 31

Pillar 3 biomarker study design, showing participant groups recruited across 10 international sites and the range of biological matrices collected for LRRK2 (leucine‐rich repeat kinase 2)–relevant biomarker discovery, including novel matrices not previously prioritized in large PD (Parkinson's disease) cohort studies.

Pillar 3 is centered on a prospective, cross‐sectional, global biomarker study (ISRCTN12620335) recruiting up to 1000 participants across 10 international sites, with recruitment having commenced in the autumn of 2025. The study complements and extends the Parkinson's Precision Markers Initiative (PPMI) 32 and is closely aligned with the Global Parkinson's Genetics Program (GP2) 33 to maximize the impact of genetic discoveries across diverse ancestries. A strategic priority is the intentional inclusion of Asian LRRK2 variant carriers, who remain substantially underrepresented in existing cohorts and in preclinical research but in whom specific risk variants are highly prevalent. 34 The cohort also includes individuals with GBA1‐associated PD, providing a biologically informative lysosomal comparator, 35 enabling systematic comparison of shared and distinct lysosomal signatures across LRRK2‐relevant genotypes.

A key innovation within pillar 3 is the application of the “tagless LysoIP” method 9 to clinical samples, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells and monocytes, enabling direct enrichment of native lysosomes. This approach has not previously been applied in a clinical PD cohort at scale and offers the potential to detect lysosome‐associated molecular signatures that are masked in whole‐cell analyses. Due to LRRK2's central role in lysosomal biology and the high LRRK2 expression in peripheral immune cells, this technology is particularly well suited to capture LRRK2‐pathway‐relevant biology in accessible human samples.

To complement lysosome‐focused analysis in immune cells, a broad range of biomatrices is being investigated, including cerebrospinal fluid, plasma, urine, and exploratory specimens such as sebum from skin swabs, skin biopsies, and exhaled breath (Fig. 3). These samples enable interrogation of LRRK2 pathway activity across multiple biological compartments. Targeted approaches, including mass spectrometry of phosphorylated Rab substrates 36 and quantification of urinary BMP phospholipids, 37 , 38 provide pathway‐specific readouts of LRRK2 activity and lysosomal exocytosis. In parallel, unbiased proteomic, metabolomic, and lipidomic profiling is being applied to identify novel biomarker candidates.

In parallel, a comprehensive biomarker program on existing PPMI samples using multiplexed proteomic, metabolomic, and targeted lipid platforms is underway to identify and prioritize candidates for prospective validation in LITE pillar 3 participants. Together, these efforts are intended not only to enable adequately powered LRRK2‐targeted trials in LRRK2‐like PD but also to define a reproducible blueprint for linking genetically validated PD targets to mechanism‐based biomarkers.

A structured analysis plan for LITE biospecimens is being developed and will be implemented iteratively as the study progresses, in line with the framework shown in Figure 1. Data sharing will follow findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR) principles 39 and align with The Michael J. Fox Foundation initiatives, with de‐identified data and biospecimens made available through controlled access mechanisms in accordance with established governance frameworks.

Forward‐Looking Perspective

The LRRK2 field is entering a decisive phase in which therapeutic programs, biomarker platforms, and clinical datasets are maturing in parallel. Realizing their full potential will require more than isolated trial readouts: it depends on integrating mechanistically diverse compounds, biologically grounded biomarkers, and well‐characterized patient cohorts within a unified framework. LITE is designed to deliver this infrastructure—enabling direct comparison of therapeutic strategies, early identification of safety signals, and data‐driven refinement of patient selection. The recent Phase 2b LUMA study provides a timely illustration of several of the translational challenges discussed throughout this viewpoint, including patient selection, biomarker‐guided stratification, therapeutic modality, timing of intervention, and defining the degree of LRRK2 pathway modulation required for efficacy. 1 , 2

Biological heterogeneity and variable responses across modalities should be viewed as somewhat daunting, but the work to better define relevance to LRRK2 disease will be informative to further guide future clinical efforts. Interpreting these signals requires context, standardization, and scale—capabilities that no single study can provide in isolation. By harmonizing datasets, aligning methodologies, and fostering collaboration across academia and industry, LITE establishes the conditions necessary to translate biological insight into clinical impact.

Beyond its immediate objectives, LITE is already shaping the next phase of the field by enabling biomarker discovery in PPMI samples for prospective validation, harmonizing global LRRK2 cohorts to define disease progression, and informing the design of a future LRRK2 platform trial. Together, these efforts signal a shift toward coordinated, precompetitive translational research.

Currently, LRRK2 is the most tractable precision‐medicine target in PD research. Realizing this opportunity will depend less on the success of any single compound than on the field's collective capacity to establish safety, identify the appropriate patient populations, and deploy mechanistically diverse interventions within an integrated strategy. If LRRK2 is instead treated as “just another target” pursued through fragmented and siloed programs, negative or equivocal trials will be difficult to interpret, and the promise of this biology may be lost. We therefore contend that initiatives such as LITE should serve as the default model for translating genetically validated PD targets into disease‐modifying therapies.
