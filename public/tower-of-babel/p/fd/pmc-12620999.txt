Meta‐Analysis of Genetic Parameters for Growth and Carcass Traits in Japanese Quail
Authors: Maryam Sokhan, Sheida Varkoohi, Ali Hossein Piray
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe considerable variability observed in genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese quails necessitates a meta‐analysis for their accurate estimation. Such a precise estimation is crucial for the successful optimisation of breeding programs.ObjectivesThis pioneering comprehensive systematic review and meta‐analysis aimed to derive precise genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese quail.MethodsFor this meta‐analysis, data were systematically extracted from 50 eligible studies published between 1974–2024. Genetic parameters including, heritability, genetic and phenotypic correlations for body weight at different ages and carcass traits, were estimated using random‐effects models within metafor package.ResultsMeta‐analysis results showed that hatch weight had the highest heritability estimate (0.38 ± 0.003). Weekly body weights (W1–W6) demonstrated heritability estimates ranging from 0.23 to 0.30. Genetic correlations among body weights at different ages were consistently positive and strong, particularly between consecutive ages, whereas phenotypic correlations were comparatively weaker. The study found moderate to high heritability estimates (0.24 to 0.33) for carcass traits. Furthermore, positive genetic correlation was observed between slaughter and carcass weight (0.96 ± 0.07). Similar patterns were noted for other carcass traits, like breast weight and thigh weight. However, genetic correlation between slaughter weight and abdominal fat weight was notably lower. The heterogeneity index (I 2) was over 90% for most estimates.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate that selecting for increased body weight can improve carcass yield without promoting excessive adiposity. This study provides reliable genetic parameters to develop quail breeding programs for enhanced productivity and meat quality.

Introduction

Poultry breeding is a fundamental strategy for enhancing productivity and economic viability in the livestock industry. Among poultry species, the Japanese quail (Coturnix Coturnix Japonica) is a prominent subject in breeding research owing to its high feed efficiency, desirable meat quality, early sexual maturity, short generation interval, low maintenance costs attributable to its diminutive size and disease resistance (Nunome et al. 2020; Varkoohi and Kaviani 2014 ). To achieve effective genetic progress, accurate estimation of genetic parameters, including heritability, genetic and phenotypic correlations for growth and carcass traits, is essential. However, the variability in reported estimates across different studies presents challenges in result interpretation (Daikwo et al. 2014; Varkoohi et al. 2010 ).

As a powerful statistical method, meta‐analysis combines results from multiple studies to improve the precision and reliability of estimates. This approach is particularly valuable in quantitative genetics. The main advantages of this approach include: (1) increased statistical power through pooling of small samples, (2) improved estimation accuracy by reducing standard errors, and (3) enhanced generalisability and applicability of findings across diverse populations. However, meta‐analysis has some limitations including: (1) heterogeneity between studies arising from methodological differences, population structures, and environmental conditions, (2) publication bias, (3) quality of primary data, (4) inappropriate model selection (e.g., fixed‐effects vs. random‐effects models) (Borenstein et al. 2010; Jembere et al. 2017).

The meta‐analysis approach has been extensively employed to estimate genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits across livestock species, including pigs (Akanno et al. 2013); goats (Campos et al. 2017; Jembere et al. 2017); beef cattle (Gathura et al. 2020; Lobo et al. 2000); and sheep (Abasi‐Mousa et al. 2022; Medrado et al. 2021). In contrast, limited meta‐analyses have investigated genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in poultry populations (Sokhan et al. 2023a; Sokhan et al. 2023b). For instance, Sokhan et al. (2023a) reported heritability estimates for body weight, feed conversion ratio, and carcass weight in broiler chickens as 0.408, 0.247, and 0.332, respectively. They also found a strong genetic correlation between body weight and carcass weight (0.938) and a weak negative correlation with feed conversion ratio (−0.31). Phenotypic correlations between body weight and carcass weight at 42 days of age were 0.893 (Sokhan et al. 2023a). Additionally, Sokhan et al. (2023b) found heritability estimates for carcass traits in broilers ranging from 0.31 (abdominal fat weight) to 0.37 (breast weight). Their results showed the highest genetic correlation between breast weight and thigh weight (0.515), and the lowest between breast weight and abdominal fat weight (0.201). The strongest phenotypic correlation was observed between carcass weight at 42 days of age and breast weight (0.817) (Sokhan et al. 2023b). Although multiple studies have estimated genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese Quail, significant heterogeneity exists among reported estimates (Narinc and Genc 2021; Sari et al. 2011; Varkoohi et al. 2011). However, no comprehensive meta‐analysis to date has systematically evaluated genetic parameters for growth traits and carcass characteristics in this species. Given the documented variability and inconsistencies among individual studies, we hypothesise that a systematic meta‐analysis will produce more precise and consistent estimates for genetic parameters in Japanese quail. This would support a breeding strategy focused on improving body weight to simultaneously enhance productivity and meat quality. Therefore, this study aimed to conduct a comprehensive meta‐analysis to integrate existing evidence and derive precise genetic parameters for key growth traits, including hatch weight (HW), body weight at weeks 1–6 (W1–W6), and slaughter weight at 42 days of age (SW). It also estimated parameters for relevant carcass traits: carcass weight (CW), breast weight (BW), thigh weight (TW), back weight (BAW), and abdominal fat weight (AFW) in Japanese quail using random‐effects models.

Materials and Methods

Systematic Literature Review and Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This meta‐analysis study was conducted to estimate reliable genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese quail. A systematic literature review was conducted across multiple databases, including Web of Science (https://www.webofknowledge.com), Scopus/Elsevier (https://www.elsevier.com/), Science Direct (https://www.sciencedirect.com), PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), Google Scholar (http://scholar.google.com), IranDoc (https://ganj.irandoc.ac.ir/), Magiran (https://www.magiran.com) and Scientific Information Database (SID) (https://www.sid.ir/). To identify studies suitable for inclusion in this meta‐analysis, we systematically searched each database using the keywords: ‘heritability’, ‘genetic and phenotypic correlation’, ‘body weight’, ‘carcass traits’, and ‘Japanese quail’. An initial systematic literature review identified 450 studies pertaining to genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese quails. After the removal of 186 duplicate records, 264 studies advanced to a preliminary screening process based on their titles and abstracts. Of these, 157 articles were subsequently excluded due to irrelevance (e.g., studies focusing on non‐quail species). The remaining 107 full‐text articles then underwent a comprehensive eligibility assessment. This meta‐analysis was conducted based on a rigorous set of inclusion criteria: (1) studies were required to report genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese quails; (2) full texts of the studies had to be available; (3) the publication period for included studies was restricted to 1974–2024; (4) studies were required to provide heritability or correlation estimates and their corresponding standard errors or confidence intervals; (5) studies were published in English or Persian (with an English abstract); and (6) each study had to report at least one estimate listed in Table 1. Exclusion criteria included studies which the full text was not available, studies unrelated to the topic, studies without sufficient data, and duplicate studies. Although a minimum of two studies is accepted in meta‐analytic research, the reliability of findings from such small datasets is often low. Consequently, to ensure the robustness of our pooled estimates, we established a more stringent inclusion criterion of at least three studies (Bahrampour et al., 2025). Following the initial database search, reference lists of all eligible articles were manually searched to identify additional relevant publications. The final meta‐analysis incorporated 50 studies that satisfied all inclusion criteria (Table 1). The compiled literature was then imported into EndNote X9 (Hupe 2019), which was used to detect and exclude duplicate records. The selection of studies and data extraction was performed by authors (MS, SHV and AHP), and any discrepancies were resolved through collective consultation. Data were extracted from the included studies using a standard template in Excel. This meta‐analysis examined a comprehensive set of traits, including growth traits such as hatch weight (HW) and weekly body weights (BW) from 1 to 6 weeks, and carcass traits (at 42 days of age) such as slaughter weight (SW), carcass weight (CW), breast weight (BW), thigh weight (TW), back weight (BAW), and abdominal fat weight (AFW).

Data Extraction and Meta‐Analytic Procedures

For each selected paper, we recorded basic bibliographic information, including the title, the first author's name, and the publication year. Data extraction focused on all reported genetic parameters – specifically heritability (h 2), genetic correlation (r g​), and phenotypic correlation (r p) for both growth traits and carcass traits. These estimates were accompanied by their corresponding confidence intervals (CIs) and standard errors (SEs).

Statistical analyses were performed using the R software package (version 4.1.3) metafor (version 3.4‐0). The threshold for statistical significance was established at p ≤ 0.05.

Assessment of Heterogeneity and Publication Bias and Sensitivity Analysis

The heterogeneity among studies was calculated using I 2 index as: I2=Q−dfQ×100. where Q is Cochran's heterogeneity statistic and df represents degrees of freedom (k – 1, with k being the number of studies). Genetic parameter heterogeneity was assessed by testing the null hypothesis of equal effect sizes across all studies (Cochran et al., 1954). The I 2 index values were interpreted using a predefined scale to classify the degree of heterogeneity as very low (< 25%), low (25%–50%), moderate (50%–75%), and high (> 75%) (Higgins et al. 2003; Oliveira et al. 2017).

Given the critical decisions inherent in data collection and quantitative analysis, sensitivity analysis is a key component of a systematic review, employed to evaluate the robustness of findings against variations in these methodological choices (Rezaei Ahvanooei et al. 2024). This was achieved by systematically identifying and excluding influential studies based on an inspection of diagnostic plots for externally standardised residuals, DFFITS values, Cook's distances, covariance ratios, estimates of τ2, and test statistics for residual heterogeneity (Sun et al. 2021). Table 2 presents the results of the sensitivity analysis. The exclusion of influential studies, which reduced the number of eligible studies to two, rendered three traits r g (BW, BAW), r p (42 d BW, BAW), and r p (TW, BAW) unanalysable. For one trait r g (CW, AFW), the removal of an influential study significantly altered the estimated value and made it statistically non‐significant. For the remaining traits, the exclusion of influential studies did not substantially alter the estimated values but severely reduced the observed heterogeneity. Accordingly, the final analysis results for parameters with influential studies are presented, and the initial results for traits without outliers are also provided.

Publication bias is defined as the tendency for research with significant findings to be published more readily. This is particularly problematic for smaller studies. Beyond the magnitude and direction of results, the publication of studies may be influenced by factors such as funding sources, cultural or political factors, and researchers’ motivation to publish. It is also important to note that publication bias encompasses language bias, wherein studies published in certain languages are more likely to be included in meta‐analyses than those published in others. The reliability of funnel plots decreases when the number of studies is limited. Furthermore, statistical tests used to detect publication bias have inherent limitations, as true heterogeneity can lead to false‐positive results. To mitigate this, Begg's funnel plot and Egger's regression asymmetry test were employed to assess for publication bias in data subsets where the I 2 value was less than 50% and at least 10 trials were available. Due to the observed heterogeneity exceeding 50% for all estimates, publication bias tests were not conducted in this meta‐analysis, despite the number of included studies meeting the minimum requirement of 10 for many traits (Higgins et al. 2003; Sterne et al. 2011; Young et al. 2015).

Results

This meta‐analysis indicated that HW exhibited the highest heritability (0.38 ± 0.003), followed by W6 (0.30 ± 0.002) and W5 (0.28 ± 0.004). Conversely, the lowest heritability was observed for W1 (0.23 ± 0.003). A high between‐study heterogeneity (I 2: 92.87–99.73%) was evident for these estimates, implying substantial variation across studies. Nevertheless, the narrow confidence intervals for most estimated traits (e.g., W3: 0.22–0.31; W4: 0.21–0.29) underscore relatively precise pooled estimates, even in the presence of the observed heterogeneity (Table 3).

Table 4 comprehensively presents the estimated genetic correlations estimated between body weight traits at various ages. All estimated values were consistently positive, with a notable ranging from 0.32 (95% CI: 0.30–0.58) for HW and W6, extending up to 0.96 (95% CI: 0.88–0.94) for W5 and W6. The meta‐analysis further revealed moderate genetic correlations (0.32 to 0.43) between HW and subsequent weekly body weights in Japanese quails, characterised by a progressive decline in correlation strength as the age intervals broadened. Genetic correlations between weekly body weights at different ages exhibited a consistent pattern of decline as age intervals increased from earlier developmental stages. Despite this, the I 2 values for genetic correlations between weekly body weights, ranged from 90.74% (for W3–W5) to 99.84% (for W2–W6), indicated extremely high between‐study heterogeneity. Nevertheless, the relatively narrow CIs for most estimates underscore the precision of the pooled estimates, particularly for adjacent age intervals.

Phenotypic correlations between different body weight traits are comprehensively presented in Table 4. All estimated values were consistently positive, ranging from 0.1 (95% CI: 0.05–0.28) for HW–W6 to 0.69 (95% CI: 0.61–0.86) for W4–W5. Specifically, phenotypic correlations between HW and subsequent weekly weights were generally weak to moderate (0.10–0.27), whereas correlations between consecutive weekly weights (e.g., W1 and W2, or W4 and W5) were markedly stronger. Despite the high heterogeneity indicated by I 2 values ranging from 92.98% to 99.77%, the confidence intervals for most estimates remained relatively narrow.

The results of a meta‐analysis encompassing 12 eligible studies provide estimated heritability values for various carcass traits in Japanese quails, which are comprehensively presented in Table 6. The heritability for SW at 42 days of age, CW, and BW was estimated at 0.31 ± 0.008, 0.33 ± 0.008 and 0.28 ± 0.008, respectively. TW and AFW exhibited comparable heritability values (0.27 ± 0.009 and 0.27 ± 0.006, respectively). Notably, BW displayed the lowest heritability among all assessed carcass traits (0.24 ± 0.008). The I 2 index ranged from 93.78% to 98.50%, signifying extremely high between‐study heterogeneity (Table 6).

The meta‐analysis results for genetic and phenotypic correlations among carcass traits in Japanese quails are comprehensively presented in Tables 7 and 8. The analysis revealed highly significant positive genetic correlations between SW at 42 days and CW (r g = 0.96 ± 0.07). Similar strong associations were observed between SW with other key carcass traits, including BW (r g = 0.93 ± 0.05) and TW (r g = 0.90 ± 0.07). In stark contrast, the genetic correlation between SW and AFW was substantially lower (r g = 0.33 ± 0.02). Notably, the lowest genetic correlations were between AFW and both BW (r g = 0.18 ± 0.07) and TW (r g = 0.23 ± 0.06).

Phenotypic correlations generally mirrored these trends, exhibiting strong correlations between SW and CW (r p = 0.89 ± 0.11) and a weaker correlation between SW and AFW (r p = 0.36 ± 0.02). Overall, the genetic and phenotypic correlations between certain desirable carcass traits, specifically CW, BW, and TW, were significantly elevated. Conversely, correlations involving AFW with other carcass traits remained comparatively modest. Despite these findings, the heterogeneity index (I 2) consistently exceeded 90% for most estimates, indicating considerable between‐study variation in the genetic and phenotypic parameter estimates.

Discussion

This meta‐analysis revealed moderate heritability estimates for growth traits in Japanese quails (ranging from 0.23 to 0.38), with the highest value observed for HW and the lowest for W1. The narrow confidence intervals and low standard errors underscore the precision of the pooled estimates, even in the presence of substantial between‐study heterogeneity (I 2 > 90%). Consequently, these findings offer valuable insights for breeding programs, highlighting HW as the most promising trait for selective breeding due to its consistently high heritability (Table 3). This meta‐analysis revealed a distinct pattern of genetic correlations between body weights at various ages. The strongest genetic correlation was consistently observed between consecutive weekly measurements (e.g., weeks 4–5 or weeks 5–6), with the correlation strength gradually diminishing as the age interval increased. Specifically, intermediate growth stages (such as week 3) exhibited moderately strong genetic correlations with both preceding and subsequent weeks. In contrast, comparisons between early growth phases (weeks 1–2) and later stages (weeks 5–6) demonstrated substantially weaker genetic correlations (Table 4). The strong genetic correlations observed between consecutive weekly body weights align with previous studies in Japanese quail (Narinc and Genc 2021) and broilers (Sokhan et al. 2023a), suggesting that these traits are influenced by common genetic mechanisms during adjacent growth stages. This pattern supports the idea that selection for body weight at later ages (e.g., W5 or W6) could effectively improve overall growth performance, as these stages exhibit high heritability and strong genetic relations to earlier weights. Conversely, the weaker genetic correlations between hatch weight (HW) and later growth stages (e.g., W6) indicate that early growth may be more influenced by maternal or environmental effects, as noted by Varkoohi et al. (2010). These findings have important effects for breeding strategies: targeting intermediate or later growth stages (e.g., W4–W6) may yield more consistent genetic progress, while early weights (e.g., HW or W1) could benefit from improved management practices. The results also suggest that multi‐trait selection incorporating both early and late growth traits could optimise genetic improvements while minimising adverse effects on other traits, such as abdominal fat deposition, which showed low genetic correlations with desirable carcass traits. This result aligns with the recommendations of Toelle et al. (1991).

Significant positive phenotypic correlations were observed between body weight traits across various developmental ages (Table 5). These correlations consistently exhibited greater strength between consecutive or closely spaced measurements, yet displayed weaker associations between HW and later growth phases. This observed pattern suggests a substantial influence of environmental and management factors on early growth in Japanese quails. Conversely, as the birds mature, the strengthening phenotypic correlations between consecutive weights indicate a more consistent and integrated influence of both genetic and environmental determinants.

The meta‐analysis revealed distinct differences between genetic (r g) and phenotypic (r p) correlations for body weight traits across various ages (Tables 4 and 5). Genetic correlations consistently surpassed phenotypic correlations, a finding that corroborates results reported by Toelle et al. (1991). Both type of correlations diminished with increasing age intervals, though this decline was more pronounced in the phenotypic data. Notably, correlations were highest between adjacent ages (e.g., W4–W5), suggesting shared genetic and environmental regulatory mechanisms during consecutive growth stages. The stronger genetic correlations imply that selective breeding for weight at specific ages (e.g., weeks 5–6) could effectively enhance growth in subsequent stages. Conversely, weaker phenotypic correlations underscore the importance of optimising environmental factors (e.g., nutrition) for early growth traits, such as HW. These findings highlight the complementary roles of genetics and environmental management in successful quail breeding programs.

The meta‐analysis results for carcass traits in Japanese quail consistently indicate moderate to high heritability estimates (Table 6). These findings suggest that these traits are significantly influenced by genetic factors and, consequently, hold considerable potential for improvement through selective breeding. These align with previous research conducted in broilers (Sokhan et al. 2023b). Furthermore, Narinc and Genc (2021) similarly reported moderate heritability for CW and BW, which corroborates our present findings. However, it is notable that some studies, such as those by Becker et al. (1985) and Sari et al. (2011), have reported a higher heritability for AFW. This discrepancy could potentially be attributed to variations in statistical methodologies or prevailing environmental conditions.

This study demonstrated strong genetic correlations between SW and various carcass traits, including CW, BW, TW. These findings align with previous research conducted by Narinc and Genc (2021) in Japanese quail and Sokhan et al. 2023a,b) in broilers. Consequently, these results suggest that selection for increased body weight could simultaneously enhance carcass yield traits. Conversely, the low genetic correlations between SW and AFW, as well as other carcass traits, indicate that abdominal fat deposition can be reduced without adversely affecting other desirable carcass traits. These observations are consistent with the findings of Sokhan et al. (2023b) in broilers. Furthermore, the high phenotypic correlation between CW and BW (0.89 ± 0.04) underscores a robust phenotypic association, which warrants careful consideration in future breeding programs.

The significant heterogeneity (I 2 > 90%) in the genetic parameter estimates – except for seven traits shown in Table 2 – in this meta‐analysis can arise from multiple sources. Among the potential sources are variations in population genetics (e.g., genetic structure and selection methods), differences in environmental conditions (e.g., nutrition, climatic conditions, and rearing systems), and methodological disparities (e.g., variations in experimental design, sample size, statistical models, and measurement precision). Furthermore, the long time span (1974–2024) of the included studies likely contributes to the overall heterogeneity. Despite this substantial between‐study heterogeneity, which reflects significant variation in genetic parameter estimates, the relatively narrow CIs for most estimates indicate reasonable precision. This precision further underscores the utility of random‐effects models in effectively addressing between‐study variability.

Progressive genetic improvement in poultry, driven by selective breeding for rapid growth and high production, has led to significant negative consequences. Modern broilers exhibit severe musculoskeletal disorders, including leg weakness and cardiovascular diseases, with nearly a third showing poor locomotion and 3.3% being non‐ambulatory, indicative of chronic pain (Rioja‐Lang et al. 2020). Additionally, muscle abnormalities such as white striping and wooden breast have emerged, compromising meat quality and causing annual economic losses of up to $1 billion in the United States alone (Rajcic et al. 2021). Laying hens face welfare issues like keel bone fractures and feather pecking, with mortality rates close to 8% by 60 weeks (Fernyhough et al. 2020). Genetic diversity has also declined, with commercial lines losing at least half of their ancestral variability, increasing susceptibility to environmental stressors (Nawaz et al. 2024). These challenges underscore the trade‐offs between productivity and sustainability in poultry breeding. This meta‐analysis revealed a low genetic correlation (r g = 0.33) between slaughter weight and abdominal fat, which indicates that carcass yield can be improved without promoting excessive fat deposition. By integrating multi‐trait selection that includes welfare indicators, the quail industry can achieve sustainable genetic improvements.

Despite the rigor of our meta‐analysis, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the search was restricted to publications in English and Persian, which may have excluded relevant studies from other languages. Second, an assessment of study quality and risk of bias was not conducted for the included studies. Third, we did not adjust for potentially confounding effect modifiers.

Conclusion

This meta‐analysis comprehensively evaluated the genetic parameters for growth and carcass traits in Japanese quails. It revealed moderate heritability for various growth traits and strong positive genetic correlations between body weights at successive growth stages. Hatch weight consistently exhibited the highest heritability estimate, thereby identifying it as a critical trait for selection in Japanese quail breeding programs. Carcass traits also demonstrated significant heritability, coupled with high genetic correlations between body weight and favourable carcass traits. The meta‐analysis approach significantly improved precision by reducing standard errors and narrowing confidence intervals, thereby demonstrating the considerable benefits of data integration. Overall, this meta‐analysis provides a consolidated scientific foundation for optimising breeding strategies in Japanese quails, furthermore offering valuable insights for enhancing both production efficiency and carcass quality through targeted genetic selection.
