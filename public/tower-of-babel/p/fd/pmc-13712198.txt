In Vivo Monitoring of Thrombo‐Inflammatory Biomarkers via Molecularly Imprinted Polymer‐Integrated Hydrogel Microneedles
Authors: Mahmoud Ayman Saleh, Peyman GhavamiNejad, Arash Khorrami Jahromi, Sanjana Srikant, Sripadh Guptha Yedire, Fatemeh Keyvani, Roozbeh Siavash Moakhar, Sajad Shiekh, Mackenzie Graham, Joe Quadrilatero, Mahla Poudineh, Sara Mahshid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThrombotic disorders are a leading cause of cardiovascular mortality worldwide; however, real‐time, point‐of‐care monitoring technologies for timely detection of evolving coagulopathies remain inaccessible. Wearable, minimally invasive tracking of thrombo‐inflammatory activity could enable earlier risk assessment and more effective therapy monitoring than conventional episodic blood tests. Here, we present a reagent‐free, wearable electrochemical platform that integrates an on‐chip Prussian Blue (PB) redox transducer with a signal‐off molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) layer and a biocompatible hydrogel microneedle (HMN) array for interstitial fluid (ISF) sampling, enabling direct electrochemical detection of thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers. The electrochemical PB/MIP (e‐MIP) biosensor was configured to quantify thrombin (thrombotic biomarker) as well as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) (inflammatory biomarkers) directly in dermal ISF extracted via the integrated HMNs. The wearable e‐MIP was characterized in vitro and ex vivo, where porcine skin tests preserved linearity and achieved limits of detection (LODs) of 0.26 ng mL− 1 for thrombin and ≤ 0.41 pg mL− 1 for IL‐6 and TNF‐α, confirming sensitive performance in a skin model. Also, selectivity studies against potential interferents (e.g., prothrombin or cardiac troponins) were conducted to assess the possible cross‐reactivity. in vivo, HMN‐integrated patches applied to lipopolysaccharide (LPS)–challenged rats sampled ISF and delivered it to the e‐MIP, which captured the rise‐and‐fall kinetics of thrombin and IL‐6 over 0–24 h. The results were validated against parallel enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays (ELISAs) performed on plasma collected at corresponding time points. Its versatile architecture and demonstrated in vivo performance position it as a promising platform that can enable early thrombotic risk assessment and therapeutic monitoring, with potential applications in personalized cardiovascular management following clinical validation.Keywords: electrochemical sensors, microneedle arrays, molecularly imprinted polymers, thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers, wearable point‐of‐care biosensors

Introduction

Thrombosis is the primary underlying pathology of three major cardiovascular disorders, including ischemic heart disease, stroke, and venous thromboembolism. It is increasingly recognized as part of a broader thrombo‐inflammatory process in which coagulation and innate immune signaling amplify one another [1]. Figure 1a depicts the coupled thrombo‐inflammatory cascade initiated by vascular or tissue injury. Cellular damage and platelet activation release damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that recruit and stimulate white blood cells (WBCs). Activated immune cells elevate pro‐inflammatory cytokines, most prominently tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), and shed microvesicles bearing activated tissue factor (TF) [2]. TF, in complex with factor VIIa, triggers the extrinsic coagulation pathway, activating factor X (FXa) and converting prothrombin (PT) to thrombin [3]. Thrombin then cleaves fibrinogen to fibrin, driving clot formation, while a portion of active thrombin is rapidly neutralized by antithrombin to form the thrombin–antithrombin (TAT) complex. Because free thrombin is short‐lived and rapidly neutralized by antithrombin, clinical assessment of coagulation activation often relies on TAT complexes as a surrogate marker of recent thrombin generation; however, TAT reflects inhibited thrombin and should not be interpreted as a direct substitute for active thrombin levels [4]. This process is a feed‐forward loop between coagulation and inflammation, as the fibrins can further stimulate pro‐inflammatory cytokines. The concurrent rises in IL‐6/TNF‐α, thrombin generation, and TAT formation provide complementary, time‐resolved readouts of evolving coagulopathy, motivating our selection of these biomarkers for early detection, risk assessment, and therapeutic monitoring of thrombotic events [5, 6, 7, 8]. Particularly, thrombotic biomarkers such as thrombin and its complex with antithrombin hold promise as preventative indicators of ischemic events, including ischemic stroke and acute coronary syndromes (ACSs), as they exhibit earlier elevation than conventional cardiac markers such as troponins or creatine kinase, which are only detectable after clinical onset of these syndromes [9, 10]. We selected thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α as a minimal sentinel panel that provides complementary information across thrombo‐inflammation while keeping the assay compact and practical. TNF‐α and IL‐6 represent key inflammatory signals, and thrombin reports the coagulation output. Together, this triad enables multiplex tracking of thrombo‐inflammatory status without expanding the panel to many markers, and the platform can be readily extended to additional biomarkers when needed [11, 12, 13].

Current clinical assessment of thrombo‐inflammatory risk and therapy relies on periodic blood‐based measurements such as PT time (PTT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), D‐dimer, thrombin generation potential, and ELISA for TAT, TNF‐α, and IL‐6. While analytically robust, these workflows are invasive, time‐consuming, infrastructure‐dependent, and sampled episodically [14, 15, 16], conditions that can miss the rapid dynamics of evolving coagulopathy and delay clinical decision‐making [17, 18]. A non/minimally invasive, and real‐time monitoring system that measures thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers could therefore enable therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of antithrombotic therapy and early‐stage diagnosis. Several studies have confirmed that thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α are present in skin interstitial fluid (ISF), indicating that wearable ISF biosensing could offer a real‐time, continuous, skin‐interfaced alternative to conventional blood‐based assays for managing thrombotic disorders [19, 20, 21, 22, 23].

Wearable biosensing of ISF has emerged as a compelling route to continuous molecular monitoring [24, 25]. ISF closely resembles plasma composition for numerous analytes (encompassing a wide range of molecular weights [26, 27]) and can be accessed minimally invasively with microneedles, improving patient comfort and potentially the prognosis. By contrast, sweat sensors have been widely explored for systemic biomarkers, but owing to the low abundance of high‐molecular‐weight proteins, high inter‐individual variability in composition, and reliance on sweat‐induction methods, their utility for detecting thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers is limited [28]. Among microneedle formats, hydrogel microneedles (HMNs) offer favorable biocompatibility, mechanical compliance, and swelling‐mediated transport that facilitate analyte transfer from dermis to an integrated transducer [29]. For molecular recognition, molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs), particularly those formed by electropolymerization of o‐phenylenediamine (o‐PD), provide template‐defined binding sites that can be tailored to diverse protein targets and operate label‐ and reagent‐free, features well suited to low‐power, on‐skin sensing [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. On the transduction side, Prussian Blue (PB) is a robust redox mediator that can be electrodeposited on‐chip; its low operating potential and high signal fidelity make it ideal for wearable electrochemical readout [37, 38].

Many reported ISF biosensing platforms rely on aptamers, enzymes, or antibodies [39, 40, 41]. Aptamers offer high specificity but require extensive SELEX optimization and can be affected by ionic strength and nuclease degradation in biological fluids [42, 43]. Enzyme‐based sensors are limited to analytes compatible with catalytic reactions, restricting their application to many protein biomarkers [44]. Antibody‐based sensors often require labeling or secondary reagents, complicating reagentless wearable integration and potentially reducing long‐term stability [45]. In contrast, MIPs are fully synthetic recognition elements with improved chemical stability, lower cost, and compatibility with reagentless electrochemical detection [46]. In addition, unlike hollow microneedle systems that require active ISF extraction and complex microfluidics, the polymeric microneedles used here enable passive sampling and simpler wearable sensor integration.

Figure 1b outlines the architecture and operating principle of the HMNs‐integrated electrochemical PB/MIP (wearable e‐MIP) platform proposed for minimally invasive ISF sampling and detection of the thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers. Upon insertion, the HMN array breaches the stratum corneum and rapidly swells into a hydrated, porous network, enabling sampling of dermal ISF enriched in thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers near sites of vascular activation. The swollen microneedles then wick ISF to the on‐patch e‐MIP biosensor (Figure 1b(i)). As illustrated in (Figure 1b(ii)), the e‐MIP is composed of a carbon working electrode coated with an electrodeposited PB redox film and overlaid with an electropolymerized o‐PD MIP. The MIP presents template‐defined imprinted sites for the targets (e.g., thrombin, IL‐6, TNF‐α). Target binding modulates electron transfer at the PB interface, producing a measurable signal‐off change in the PB peak that is recorded electrochemically via Linear Sweep Voltammetry (LSV). Indeed, target binding to the imprinted sites impedes electron transfer, resulting in a decreased LSV signal proportional to the biomarker concentrations. (Figure 1b(iii)) shows HMN array and its in vivo deployment. Optical images (scale bars: 4 and 2 mm) highlight the geometry and uniformity of the HMNs used to traverse the stratum corneum. The wearable e‐MIP platform was systematically optimized and structurally characterized using electrochemical measurements and surface/mechanical‐analytic techniques. Performance was evaluated in vitro, ex vivo porcine‐skin models, and in vivo in Sprague–Dawley rats, with ISF readouts benchmarked against time‐matched plasma ELISAs. Collectively, these results establish a skin‐interfaced route for single‐step, label‐free monitoring of coupled coagulation–inflammation dynamics, enabling earlier risk assessment and therapy management at the point of care.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Physical Characterization of e‐MIP Biosensor

To create the e‐MIP platform, we coated screen‐printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs) with PB to serve as a redox mediator that is further modified with o‐PD MIP for molecular recognition of biomarkers of interest. First, carbon electrodes were electrochemically activated to enhance surface functionality and allow efficient deposition of PB. Repeated deposition cycles yielded a stable PB layer, confirmed by a consistent LSV peak current of ∼75 µA, demonstrating reliable electrochemical behavior. The PB layer provided stable redox properties, which are essential for reproducible sensor performance. Subsequently, an ultrathin o‐PD MIP film was electropolymerized and stabilized on the PB‐coated surface. Removal of the template molecules using acid treatment generated selective recognition template sites within the MIP (Figure 2a). The resulting imprinted electrodes were either stored in PBS for immediate use or preserved in a desiccator for long‐term storage.

To investigate the morphological and structural properties of the e‐MIP platform, we integrated the analyses of high‐resolution imaging techniques, including SEM, AFM, and TEM. As demonstrated in (Figure 2b(i,ii)), SEM micrographs revealed a uniform, porous o‐PD film conformally covering the PB‐modified carbon electrode. This interconnected porous network ensures complete surface coverage and provides an expanded electroactive area, facilitating efficient ion and analyte diffusion toward the recognition sites. The porous structure in (Figure 2b(iii)) reflects the nanochannel‐rich architecture that represents the imprinted sites of the MIP layer, showing high binding efficiency and the reduction of diffusion limitations. TEM micrographs (Figure 2b(iv)) further revealed the internal nanostructure of the e‐MIP platform, showing a crystalline PB core encapsulated by an ultrathin (∼5–10 nm) conformal o‐PD layer uniformly coating nanoparticles of 20–40 nm in diameter. This intimate interface between the conductive PB layer and the thin MIP overlayer preserves the redox activity of PB while ensuring high‐fidelity template recognition, confirming the structural integrity and functional integration of both layers within the e‐MIP system.

Various fabrication parameters were optimized to enhance the biosensor performance, including the number of cycles, the concentration of o‐PD monomer of the MIP precursor solution, the washing time required to remove most of the template biomarkers, and the incubation time of the sample. The effect of o‐PD concentration on biosensor selectivity was evaluated by cyclic scanning the electrode (25 electropolymerization cycles) in MIP precursor solutions containing 200 µg mL− 1 thrombin with o‐PD at 5, 10, 15, or 20 mm. After polymerization, 75 µL of 100 ng mL− 1 thrombin as the template biomarker was drop‐cast onto the electrode surface and incubated for 2 min before LSV measurement to quantify the current change. The negative normalized current was calculated using (Equation S1), where I 0 represents the peak current obtained at zero concentration, while Ip represents the current obtained at the given concentration (Figure 2c(i)). These results indicate that increasing the o‐PD concentration (at constant template biomarker concentrations) thickens the MIP layer, resulting in increased polymer–biomarker interactions and enhancing selectivity up to 15 mm.

The selectivity factor (Equation S2) of the IL‐6‐templated biosensor relative to the closest interfering agent, TNF‐α, increased from 1.58 ± 0.09 at 5 mm to 3.03 ± 0.20 at 15 mm (Table S1). A further increase in o‐PD concentration to 20 mm resulted in a selectivity factor of 3.87 ± 0.24, which was accompanied by a significant decrease in overall signal intensity due to diffusion limitations imposed by the thicker polymer matrix. At higher monomer concentrations, the recognition mechanism becomes increasingly influenced by steric constraints rather than precise o‐PD–template interactions, leading to a system that is more size‐selective than imprint‐specific [47, 48, 49]. The number of electropolymerization cycles influenced the sensitivity of the biosensor through controlling the thickness of the o‐PD polymer layer [47]. To optimize this parameter, we evaluated 5, 10, 20, 25, and 30 CV cycles using 200 µg mL− 1 thrombin in 15 mm o‐PD (Figure 2c(ii)). Hypothetically, at low cycle counts, the o‐PD film is too thin to provide adequate sensitivity. Conversely, excessive cycles produce a film that is too thick, hindering electron transfer at the electrode interface and attenuating the analytical signal [47, 48, 49]. Therefore, 25 cycles were identified as the optimal condition, providing a balanced film thickness that ensures both efficient electron transfer and well‐defined molecular recognition. The extraction duration (at 200 µg mL− 1 of thrombin in 15 mm of o‐PD, 25 CV cycles) for the template biomarker was then optimized to maximize the availability of accessible binding sites while preserving the electrode stability. A 150‐min extraction yielded optimal performance (Figure 2c(iii)), while longer durations caused PB layer degradation and a corresponding drop in redox signal. Thus, extended extraction improves template removal only up to a threshold, beyond which electrochemical activity deteriorates [50]. Finally, the effect of incubation time on analyte binding was evaluated using (200 µg mL− 1 thrombin in 15 mm o‐PD, 25 CV cycles). An incubation time of 2 min was identified as optimal, enabling efficient binding of thrombin (used here as a representative biomarker) to the imprinted sites. The rapid association kinetics indicate that the optimized MIP layer is well‐suited for real‐time detection of thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers (Figure 2c(iv)). To verify that the optimization parameters identified for thrombin are applicable to other biomarkers, the same optimization procedures were performed for IL‐6‐ and TNF‐α‐templated MIPs. The influence of o‐PD electropolymerization cycles, monomer concentration, and template‐removal duration on the normalized PB current was evaluated under identical experimental conditions. As shown in Figure S1, similar trends were observed for both biomarkers at (50 µg mL− 1 of TNF‐α and IL‐6), confirming that the selected parameters provide balanced film thickness, efficient template removal, and reproducible electrochemical response across different target proteins.

Ch aracterization and Optimization of HMNs

The wearable e‐MIP platform integrates the MIP‐functionalized PB platform with methacrylated hyaluronic acid (MeHA) HMN patches, enabling skin ISF collection for in situ detection of thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers in skin. The HMN were fabricated utilizing a multistep process based on our previously established protocol [24, 50]. Briefly, MeHA polymer was synthesized through the chemical modification of hyaluronic acid (HA) with methacrylic anhydride (MAA) that composes the MeHA polymer backbone (Figure S2(i)). The chemical structure of MeHA and the degree of methacrylation were determined by hydrogen‐1 nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) (Figure S2(ii)). Following our previous work, we utilized different ratios of N,N′‐ methylenebisacrylamide (MBA), a crosslinking agent, to MeHA at 0.03% and 0.045% w/w to generate lowly crosslinked (LCD‐MeHA) and highly crosslinked MeHA (HCD‐MeHA), respectively [51]. The HCD‐MeHA was utilized for the fabrication of HMN patches, while the LCD‐MeHA was used as a glue to attach the HMN patches to the sensor. The photoinitiator (PI) was added to the polymer solution to enable UV crosslinking [24, 51]. The complex mixture was transferred into a 10 × 10 PDMS microneedle mold (needle height: 850 µm) to replicate the microneedle geometry. After the HMN patches were dried, the patches were exposed to UV for 10 or 20 min to initiate the crosslinking process caused by the covalent attachment between carbon−carbon double bonds of MeHA with the MBA in the presence of the PI (Figure 2d) to yield the MeHA HMNs, as previously described in our literature [51]. Following HMN fabrication, 75 µL of the LCD‐MeHA was added to the surface of the prepared e‐MIP platform as a thin layer to act as a binder between the sensor and the HCD‐MeHA HMNs, generating the fully integrated wearable e‐MIP (Figure 2e). The platform was then exposed to UV for 10 or 20 min. Two hydrogel crosslinking durations (10 and 20 min) under UV exposure were investigated to assess their porosity and degree of crosslinking. To evaluate crosslinking efficiency, the hydrogel films were immersed in water overnight. No dissolution was observed, confirming successful network formation. Further characterization using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed that the 20‐min UV‐exposed hydrogels (Figure 2f(i)) had less porosity compared to the 10‐min group (Figure 2f(ii)). Image analysis software was used to quantify the pore areas, revealing values of 45% for the 10‐min and 33% for the 20‐min crosslinked hydrogels. This indicates that the 10‐min crosslinked hydrogels possessed larger and more abundant pores, which would likely result in enhanced ISF extraction. Next, we characterized a dried HMN patch fabricated with 20 min UV treatment. SEM imaging of HMN patches confirmed the successful formation of sharp and well‐defined microneedles (Figure 2g). The swelling behavior of the patches was further evaluated using porcine skin (Figure 2h). Both the 10‐ and 20‐min crosslinked patches showed saturation of swelling after 15 min; however, the 10‐min crosslinking group exhibited a higher swelling ratio compared to the 20‐min group. We also observed the swelling behavior of MeHA patches on agarose hydrogel, where swelling saturation occurred after approximately 20 min. (Figure S3). Mechanical testing of microneedles crosslinked under different UV exposure times showed that the force required to fracture the fully dried needles exceeded 0.3 N, the minimum threshold for effective penetration of the stratum corneum (Figure 2i) [24].

Analytical Performance of the Wearable e‐MIP

In Vitro and Ex Vivo Performance

To validate the e‐MIP biosensor before integration into a wearable platform, it was tested in vitro against the three thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers of interest, including thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, spiked in artificial ISF (Figure S4). Artificial ISF, containing serum albumin, was used as the calibration medium for both in vitro and ex vivo to partially mimic protein‐associated matrix conditions present in biological ISF while maintaining controlled analyte concentrations. Figure 3a–c shows the LSV recorded ex vivo and the principle of the e‐MIP biosensor, which relies on biomarker binding to its own imprinted sites within the MIP matrix, which hinders electron transfer kinetics at the electrode surface. This hindrance decreases the peak current of PB, producing a concentration‐dependent signal‐off response. EIS measurements were performed to further verify the sensing mechanism. A progressive increase in charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) was observed with increasing TNF‐α concentration, indicating progressive inhibition of electron transfer between the redox probe and the electrode surface as analyte binding occurs (Figure S5). To represent this signal‐off effect quantitatively, peak currents were normalized utilizing Equation S1. The biosensor was exposed to increasing concentrations of thrombin (5–100 ng mL− 1), IL‐6 (10–200 pg mL− 1), and TNF‐α (10–200 pg mL− 1) in artificial ISF (Figure S4a–c) that represent the physiological and pathological conditions in humans. Calibration plots were constructed by plotting the negative normalized current response against the logarithm of the biomarker concentration. While the MIP electrodes exhibited a clear concentration‐dependent signal‐off response, the NIP controls produced only minimal signal variation across the tested ranges, indicating negligible non‐specific binding. The calculated LOQs of thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were 1.91 ng mL− 1, 2.75 pg mL− 1, and 2.40 pg mL− 1, while the corresponding LODs were 0.28 ng mL− 1, 0.44 pg mL− 1, and 0.38 pg mL− 1, respectively. These results confirm that the biosensor exhibited high signal fidelity and stable electrochemical performance in artificial ISF, validating its in vitro analytical capability.

Before advancing to in vivo studies, the wearable e‐MIP platform, assembled as in Figure 2e, was evaluated ex vivo using a model of porcine ear skin, a well‐established model that closely mimics the structural and biochemical properties of human dermis [23, 36]. The porcine skin samples were soaked in artificial ISF containing varying concentrations of target analytes overnight. The next day, the wearable e‐MIP patch was applied to the skin for 10 min to allow the transfer of the biomarkers and ISF through the HMN to e‐MIP. Subsequently, the e‐MIP electrode was scanned using LSV with the same parameters utilized for the in vitro validation. As illustrated in (Figure 3d–f), the biosensor platform exhibited a concentration‐dependent signal‐off response, which indicates effective target binding and suppression of electron transfer at the imprinted sites. The agreement between the in vitro and ex vivo results indicates successful integration of the e‐MIP with HMNs, negligible interference from tissue debris, and effective sample extraction and analyte delivery to the sensing layer. The LOQs of thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were 1.80 ng mL− 1, 1.70 pg mL− 1, and 2.54 pg mL− 1, while the LODs of them were 0.26 ng mL− 1, 0.23 pg mL− 1 and 0.40 pg mL− 1.

To further evaluate the e‐MIP's specificity toward the thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers of interest, we conducted selectivity tests by exposing the biomarker‐specific PB/MIP biosensor to potential interferents commonly present in ISF. The biosensor response was measured in artificial ISF spiked either with the target biomarkers or with structurally/functionally related proteins. Measurements were conducted at physiologically relevant concentrations of biomarkers, including thrombin (100 ng mL− 1), PT (1.4 µg mL− 1), cardiac troponin I (TnI) (40 pg mL− 1), cardiac troponin T (TnT) (10 pg mL− 1), IL‐6 (100 pg mL− 1), and TNF‐ α (100 pg mL− 1). As illustrated in (Figure 3g–i), the normalized signal response clearly indicates that each target‐specified PB/MIP biosensor exhibited high specificity toward its target analyte. Notably, for the thrombin‐specific PB/MIP biosensor responses to non‐target biomarkers were negligible. In contrast, the sensor exhibited a ∼33% signal to the thrombin–antithrombin complex (TAT 100 ng mL− 1), reflecting recognition of the thrombin moiety by the imprinted sites. This cross‐reactivity prompted a differential‐recognition study to quantify the biosensor's relative responsiveness to thrombin vs. TAT at relevant concentrations detailed in the following section. To assess short‐term durability under protein‐rich conditions, thrombin MIP electrodes were incubated in protein‐loaded artificial ISF for up to 3 days before measurement at a fixed thrombin concentration of 200 ng mL− 1 (Figure S6). A gradual reduction in response was observed over time, consistent with partial fouling or site occupation under sustained protein exposure, although measurable sensor performance was retained throughout the tested period.

Differential Recognition and Characterization of Biomarker Binding

During coagulation, active thrombin is rapidly neutralized by antithrombin III to form the TAT [52]. TAT is therefore an inevitable downstream marker of thrombin generation and is abundant in real biological samples. Because TAT retains an intact thrombin moiety covalently bound to antithrombin, a MIP templated with thrombin could partially recognize TAT [53]. To assess practical selectivity under physiologically relevant conditions, model how mixed samples will behave (since TAT can equal or exceed thrombin in vivo), and build a calibration that remains accurate in clinical matrices, the thrombin‐specific e‐MIP biosensor was challenged with mixtures of thrombin (0–150 ng mL− 1) and TAT (10–100 ng mL− 1). Figure 3j shows a heat map of the normalized current change ((−ΔI/Io), dimensionless) recorded for the full 4 × 4 matrix of mixtures. The signal increases monotonically along both axes, with the steeper gradient along the thrombin axis. Figure 3k demonstrates Varying TAT at fixed thrombin. It shows cross‐sections of the matrix at four thrombin backgrounds (0, 25, 50, 150 ng mL− 1). Increasing TAT shifts the response upward at each thrombin level, i.e., TAT contributes an approximately additive baseline. The curves become increasingly elevated as thrombin rises, consistent with thrombin‐dominated signal generation and weak interaction between the two analytes. Also, Figure 3l, depicts cross‐sections at four TAT backgrounds (10, 20, 50, 100 ng mL− 1). The response grows linearly with thrombin across the tested range; the four traces are nearly parallel, indicating that TAT primarily offsets the baseline while exerting only a modest effect on the thrombin slope (slight flattening at higher TAT as predicted by the model). Figure S7 shows the calculated Thrombin to TAT responsiveness ratio at different concentrations of the components (see more details in the Supporting Information). The thrombin‐specified PB/MIP biosensor was, on average, ∼2.5× more responsive to thrombin than to TAT across the tested range (Table S2). These data confirm that the biosensor maintains preferential recognition of free thrombin while still responding proportionally to TAT, a property crucial for operation in complex biological matrices where both species coexist.

Molecular docking was performed to explore the possible interactions between the o‐PD–based MIP matrix and thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers (Figure 3m–o). Across targets, the o‐PD monomer formed multiple hydrogen‐bonding and electrostatic contacts with polar amino‐acid residues on the protein surfaces. For thrombin, sixteen recurrent binding sites were identified, with five high‐affinity clusters dominating the interaction landscape. In comparison, IL‐6 and TNF‐α each displayed a larger number of dispersed binding regions (∼29 per target) but generally weaker or comparable docking scores. Median binding energies were on the order of −4 to −3.5 kcal mol− 1 (TNF‐α, −4.049 kcal mol− 1; thrombin, −4.014 kcal mol− 1; IL‐6, −3.533 kcal mol− 1). These docking results qualitatively suggest a preferential interaction of thrombin with the o‐PD MIP, consistent with the lower electrochemical response magnitudes observed for the cytokines (Tables S5–S7).

In Vivo Thrombin and IL‐6 Measurement Using Wearable e‐MIP

After validating the wearable e‐MIP platform in vitro and ex vivo, we assessed its in vivo performance in Sprague–Dawley rats following intraperitoneal (IP) injections of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) or saline (negative control). A moderate LPS dose (1 mg kg− 1) was used to induce a controlled thrombo‐inflammatory response in rats. LPS‐induced endotoxemia is widely used to model the early thrombo‐inflammatory cascade associated with systemic infection, including rapid elevation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and dysregulation of platelet and coagulation pathways that contribute to thrombin generation [54, 55, 56]. These processes correspond to the biomarker panel targeted in this study (thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α), providing a reproducible framework to evaluate thrombo‐inflammatory biomarker dynamics using the wearable e‐MIP platform. IL‐6 was selected as a representative inflammatory biomarker owing to its rapid acute‐phase kinetics, while thrombin was tracked as a key thrombotic biomarker to enable dynamic, time‐resolved monitoring across multiple time points (Figure 4a). HMNs were integrated with the e‐MIP biosensor to create a skin‐conformal patch. The dorsal skin of rats was prepared and disinfected before fixing the patch with medical adhesive films. After LPS or saline injection, the patch was applied to the skin and secured using medical‐grade adhesive tape (Figure 4b(i)) [19, 34]. To verify HMN penetration, a histological study was performed using a patch applied to the shaved dorsal skin of a rat. After patch removal and humane euthanasia, the application site was excised, fixed, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H and E), confirming microneedle penetration through the stratum corneum with no apparent inflammation observed (Figure 4b(ii)). Electrochemical measurements (using LSV with the same parameters used in the in vitro/ex vivo studies) commenced approximately 10 min after patch application at each time point. Importantly, the in vivo concentrations reported by the wearable e‐MIP were calculated based on ex vivo calibration curves generated separately for each biomarker. Thrombotic biomarkers were measured at 0, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, and 24 h (h) post‐injection in three LPS‐treated and one saline (negative) control, while IL‐6 measures were taken at 3, 6, and 24 h in one LPS‐treated and one control animal, as shown in Figure 4a. After patch removal, transient needle imprints were visible immediately and faded over ≤ 25 min (assessed at 5, 10, 15, and 20 min), confirming effective microneedle penetration and rapid skin recovery (Figure 4c). At each time point, four e‐MIP patches per rat were applied (Figure 4e–h), and blood samples were collected for ELISA (Figure 4f–i), based on standard protocol (see section 4.13).

Plasma ELISA in LPS‐challenged rats showed a rapid rise in thrombin within 0.5–1 h, peaking at ∼3 h and then declining toward baseline by 24 h (Figure 4f). TAT followed a similar early trajectory but exhibited a late secondary increase at 24 h. The wearable e‐MIP reported ISF kinetics that closely paralleled the plasma profile, exhibiting an early rise, a pronounced peak at 3 h, and a subsequent fall, indicating that the device captures the dynamic thrombotic response to LPS (Figure 4e). Detailed concentrations are provided in the figure caption. To build an ELISA‐derived reference that reflects the composite thrombotic signal sensed in vivo by the e‐MIP, we used the instantaneous thrombin‐to‐TAT responsiveness ratio (TTRR), calculated based on studies described in Section 2.3.2 (Figure 4d(i–iii)), to convert measured TAT concentrations into thrombin‐equivalent values (TEV). These TEVs were then added to the corresponding ELISA thrombin concentrations (TEV top‐up; Figure 4g). The TEV derived from TAT was 28.96 ng mL− 1 at baseline, peaked at 56.7 ng mL− 1 at 3 h, and declined to 34.9 ng mL− 1 at 6 h. The ISF trajectory measured by the e‐MIP closely followed the thrombin + TEV ELISA curve, with a modest divergence at 24 h (Figure 4g). These results align with the docking analysis, which identified 16 thrombin‐specific and 34 TAT‐specific o‐PD binding sites, including 14 shared sites. Notably, the two strongest thrombin sites (ΔG = −5.19 and −4.98 kcal·mol− 1; Table S5) are occluded in TAT by the antithrombin moiety, explaining the preferential thrombin recognition and the observed TTRR ∼2.52 (Figure 4d(i,ii)). As expected for high‐molecular‐weight proteins, absolute ISF concentrations differed from those in plasma due to partitioning and diffusive lag [57]. Importantly, ISF levels can vary substantially relative to blood depending on biomarker identity, sampling method, and inflammatory state; nevertheless, the biosensor faithfully captured the dynamic thrombo‐inflammatory response following LPS challenge [58, 59, 60]. In LPS‐treated rats, ISF IL‐6 rose sharply from baseline (∼10 pg mL− 1) to a 3 h peak (∼384 pg mL− 1) before declining toward baseline (Figure 4h). Plasma ELISA showed the same timing with larger magnitudes, increasing from baseline (∼420 pg mL− 1) to a 3 h peak (∼1009 pg mL− 1) and then falling thereafter. Saline controls remained at baseline by both readouts (Figure 4i). These kinetics align with prior reports that IL‐6 peaks 2–4 h after LPS and then declines over the subsequent ∼12 h (Figure 4j) [61]. Hypothetically, the divergence between the wearable e‐MIP and plasma ELISA at 0 h (pre‐LPS) and 24 h (recovery) likely reflects transient, LPS‐induced microvascular hyperpermeability and extravasation that enhance blood‐to‐ISF transport. During the early post‐injection window (0.5–6 h), this heightened permeability causes ISF levels to closely track plasma. As permeability normalizes at baseline and during recovery, plasma concentrations remain comparatively higher while ISF levels decline, producing the observed offsets [62].

To further evaluate measurement reproducibility, intra‐ and inter‐animal variability were analyzed using the responses from the four wearable e‐MIP patches applied simultaneously to each rat. As shown in Figure S8, individual sensor responses are presented as black scatter points, while bars represent the mean signal for each biomarker condition. Sensor responses within individual animals exhibited tight clustering, indicating good device‐to‐device reproducibility, while the larger spread between animals primarily reflects biological variability in biomarker levels following the LPS challenge. Electrodes that became mechanically detached during the experiment or produced abnormal overcurrent signals due to unstable skin contact were excluded from the analysis; therefore, fewer than four electrodes are represented in some cases.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we present a next‐generation, skin‐interfaced electrochemical biosensor, the wearable e‐MIP, that integrates PB redox mediator with o‐PD MIP layer and a MeHA HMN array, enabling real‐time, reagent‐free monitoring of thrombo‐inflammatory biomarkers in dermal ISF. The wearable e‐MIP platform was systematically optimized and structurally characterized using electrochemical measurements and surface/mechanical‐analytic techniques. Across in vitro and ex vivo studies, the e‐MIP delivered sensitive, linear, and selective readouts, achieving LODs of 0.26 ng mL− 1 for thrombin and ≤0.41 pg mL− 1 for IL‐6/TNF‐α. In LPS‐challenged rats, the wearable e‐MIP patch captured the rise‐and‐fall kinetics of thrombin and IL‐6 that tracked time‐matched plasma ELISAs. Molecular docking simulations further clarified the dominant hydrogen‐bonding and electrostatic interactions between o‐PD‐based imprinted sites and the targeted biomarkers. Taken together, this versatile platform is readily adaptable to additional protein and cytokine targets, supporting scalable translation to point‐of‐care monitoring of cardiovascular, inflammatory, and coagulopathic disorders. By coupling electrochemical precision with a biocompatible, skin‐interfaced design, the wearable e‐MIP provides a foundation for early thrombotic risk assessment and therapeutic drug monitoring. Although the current PalmSens‐based configuration, consistent with conventional benchtop potentiostat platforms, still requires tethered operation, future work will focus on hardware miniaturization and wireless integration to enable fully ambulatory deployment.

Materials and Reagents

Recombinant human thrombin and PT were obtained from Sigma–Aldrich (Oakville, Canada), while α‐thrombin (native protein) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA). Recombinant IL‐6 and TNF‐α were sourced from Sino Biological (Beijing, China). Recombinant human TnI was acquired from Sigma–Aldrich, and TnT from Cedarlane (Burlington, Canada). Artificial ISF was purchased from (Biochemazone, BZ254, Canada).

o‐PD (≥99%) was used as the electropolymerizable monomer for MIP synthesis (Sigma–Aldrich). PB was electrodeposited from precursor salts including ferric chloride (FeCl3, anhydrous, ≥97%), potassium ferricyanide (K3[Fe(CN)6], ≥99%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%), and potassium chloride (KCl, ≥99%) (all from Sigma–Aldrich). Buffer solutions were prepared using sodium acetate, glacial acetic acid, sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 10 m solution, Sigma–Aldrich), and phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

MeHA was synthesized by reacting pharmaceutical‐grade HA (∼300 kDa, Sigma–Aldrich) with methacrylic anhydride (MAA, ≥94%, Sigma–Aldrich). For microneedle fabrication, MeHA was crosslinked with N,Nʹ‐methylenebisacrylamide (MBA, ≥99%, Sigma–Aldrich) in the presence of the photoinitiator 2‐hydroxy‐4ʹ‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)‐2‐methylpropiophenone (Sigma–Aldrich). Acetone (analytical grade, Fisher Scientific), ethanol (absolute, 99.9%, commercial grade), and deionized water (DI) (Milli‐Q purification system, Millipore) were used as solvents. The dialysis tubing cellulose membrane (Sigma–Aldrich, MWCO = 14000). Microneedles were cast in polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard‐184 kit) molds and crosslinked under a UV system (365 nm).

SPCE (C110) were purchased from Metrohm DropSens. A standard three‐electrode setup was employed, consisting of the SPCE as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (BASi, USA), and a platinum wire counter electrode (BASi, USA). Electrochemical measurements for in vitro and ex vivo studies were performed using a Metrohm Autolab M101 potentiostat/galvanostat (Netherlands), while in vivo electrochemical recordings were conducted using a portable potentiostat (PalmSens4, Netherlands).

Methacrylated hydrogels were freeze‐dried using a FreeZone 2.5 L lyophilizer (Labconco, USA). Mechanical compression tests were performed using an Instron 5944 microtester equipped with a 1000 N load cell. 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Ascend 300 MHz spectrometer.

Agarose (molecular biology grade, Sigma–Aldrich) was used for phantom swelling tests. Isoflurane (USP‐grade, Baxter Healthcare) served as the anesthetic for in vivo experiments. LPS, E. coli O55:B5 (Sigma–Aldrich, USA) was used to induce systemic inflammation and thrombosis in rats. Depilatory cream (Nair, commercial grade, Canada) was used for dorsal hair removal before patch application. Medical‐grade adhesive film (Tegaderm, 3M, USA) was used for sensor fixation. Citrated and EDTA microtainer tubes (BD Biosciences, USA) were used for blood collection.

Rat‐specific sandwich ELISA kits were used for quantitative analysis of plasma biomarkers, including IL‐6 (Antibodies Online, Cambridge, UK), thrombin (Antibodies Online, Cambridge, UK), and TAT complex (Creative Diagnostics, Shirley, NY, USA).

Preparation of e‐MIP

Carbon electrodes were first electrochemically activated through scanning them in the potential range from −0.2 to 1.2 V at a scan rate of 100 mV s−1 for 50 cycles utilizing cyclic voltammetry (CV). A layer of PB was electrodeposited on the surface of the activated carbon electrodes through scanning the electrodes in 75 µL of precursor solution that is composed of 3 mm FeCl3, 3 mm K3Fe(CN)6, 0.1 m HCl, and 0.1 m KCl for 20 CV cycles between −0.2 to 0.6 V at a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. After deposition, the electrodes were rinsed with deionized water and conditioned electrochemically in 0.1 m HCl / 0.1 m KCl by continuous CV scanning (−0.2 to 0.6 V, 50 mV s− 1) to stabilize the PB film. Stabilization was defined quantitatively as the point at which the anodic and cathodic peak currents reached a reproducible plateau of 600–700 µA in CV (corresponding to 61–75 µA in LSV measurements in PBS), with a variation of less than 3% across three consecutive cycles. Typically, stabilization occurred after approximately 65–75 conditioning cycles. Subsequently, the PB‐coated electrode was covered with an ultrathin layer of MIP that is composed of o‐PD polymer through scanning the electrode in a solution of 15 mm o‐PD monomer in 0.5 m acetate buffer, 5.2 pH in the range of 0 to 1.2 V with a scan rate of 50 mV s−1. The o‐PD layer was then stabilized through scanning for ten cycles in 1X PBS (−0.2 to 0.6 V with a scan rate of 50 mV s−1). To release the template biomarker from the MIP, the electrodes were immersed in 0.1 m HCl solution for 3 h to remove most of the biomarker molding molecules. The template molecule concentration for thrombin was 200 µg mL− 1 and for TNF‐α and IL‐6 was 50 µg mL− 1.

Optimization of Template Concentration During MIP Preparation

For thrombin (Figure S9a), the MIP prepared with 200 µg mL− 1 template yielded a broader and more practically relevant analytical range (5–100 ng mL− 1) together with a stronger measurable signal across the calibration window. For IL‐6 (Figure S9b) and TNF‐α (Figure S9c), the 50 µg mL− 1 condition was preferred, as it provided improved binding performance within the relevant 15–200 pg mL− 1 range.

Together, these results show that template concentration was target‐dependent and required empirical optimization for each protein. Template loading was therefore selected individually to maximize analytical performance within the relevant working range for each biomarker.

Electrode‐to‐Electrode Reproducibility

To assess electrode‐to‐electrode consistency, seven independently fabricated electrodes (E1–E7) were prepared under identical conditions and tested across the IL‐6 concentration range of 0–200 pg mL− 1. The inter‐electrode RSD values were 6.81% at 25 pg mL− 1, 3.23% at 50 pg mL− 1, 4.80% at 75 pg mL− 1, 3.26% at 100 pg mL− 1, 2.99% at 150 pg mL− 1, and 1.96% at 200 pg mL− 1. Except for the lowest concentration, where relatively higher variation is expected near the detection limit, all RSD values remained below 5%, with mid‐to‐high concentrations showing particularly good agreement. These results confirm good fabrication reproducibility and consistent electrochemical response across independently prepared sensors. The corresponding concentration‐response data and statistical summary have been added as Figure S10 and Table S3. In addition, IL‐6 was selected as a representative mid‐sized cytokine between TNF‐α and thrombin in molecular size, enabling evaluation of the PB/MIP platform across a relevant steric range. Its central role in thrombo‐inflammatory signaling also makes it a biologically relevant analyte for this study.

Surface Characterization Of e‐MIP

For PB/MIP porosity study, the sample were prepared by deposition of o‐PD on PB layer the analyses were conducted utilizing JEOL JSM‐7600F Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope. The nanoscale roughness in addition to the morphology of the PB/ MIP were assessed using Bruker Dimension Icon AFM, equipped with a silicon nitride cantilever (resonance frequency ∼300 kHz). The internal nanostructure of the PB/ MIP layer was imaged utilizing FEI Tecnai G2 F20 Transmission Electron Microscope operated at 200 kV.

Fluorescence Validation of Template Extraction and Imprinted Cavity Rebinding

Removal of the template molecules by acid washing generated selective recognition sites within the MIP matrix (Figure S11). To further confirm successful template extraction and the formation of functional imprinted cavities, fluorescence microscopy experiments were performed using a fluorescently labeled model template (ovalbumin–Alexa Fluor 647). Prior to extraction, strong fluorescence was observed across the polymer surface, confirming the presence of the embedded template molecules. Following acid washing, the fluorescence signal decreased to near‐background levels, indicating efficient removal of the template. To verify that the extraction process generated functional recognition cavities rather than simply reducing non‐specific adsorption, the polymer was subsequently challenged with a non‐specific protein (Annexin V–FITC), which did not restore the Alexa Fluor 647 fluorescence signal and remained close to the post‐extraction background level. In contrast, re‐incubation with ovalbumin–Alexa Fluor 647 resulted in a pronounced recovery of fluorescence intensity, confirming selective rebinding within the imprinted cavities. Quantitative analysis was performed by averaging the background‐subtracted mean fluorescence intensity from three randomly selected regions of interest in each image, with the results summarized in the accompanying fluorescence intensity analysis (Figure S12). The decrease in signal after washing confirms template removal, whereas signal recovery after template re‐incubation confirms rebinding to the imprinted cavities. Error bars represent the standard deviation of three independent regions of interest.

Synthesis and Characterization of Methacrylated Hyaluronic Acid (MeHA)

MeHA was synthesized following our previous literature [24, 51]. Briefly, hyaluronic acid (2.0 g) was dissolved in 100 mL of Millipore water and stirred overnight at 4°C. Methacrylic anhydride (1.6 mL) was then added, and the pH was adjusted to 8–9 by gradual addition of 5 N NaOH (3.6 mL). After an overnight reaction at 4°C, the product was precipitated in acetone, washed three times with ethanol, redissolved in Millipore water, and dialyzed for 5 days to remove unreacted reagents. The purified MeHA was lyophilized for 3 days and stored in a desiccator until use.

1H NMR spectra were collected at room temperature using an Ascend 300A spectrometer with D2O as the solvent. Samples (4 and 20 mg mL− 1) were loaded in 5 mm NMR tubes, and data were analyzed using TopSpin 4.3.0 software to confirm methacrylation.

Fabrication of MeHA HMN Array

Lyophilized MeHA (50 mg), N,N′‐methylenebisacrylamide (MBA; 1.5 mg), and photoinitiator (PI; 1.5 mg) were dissolved in 1 mL of DI water and stirred continuously for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous precursor solution. The MeHA‐MBA/PI mixture was then cast into a 10 × 10 silicone microneedle (MN) mold. Air bubbles entrapped within the mold tips were removed by applying a vacuum for 2 min. The mold was subsequently placed in a nitrogen desiccator at room temperature for 72 h. The fully dried hydrogel MN array was then gently demolded and stored in a desiccator until use.

HMN Characterization (Imaging, Swelling, and Mechanical)

The porosity of hydrogel thin films crosslinked for 10 and 20 min under UV exposure was characterized using field‐emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, HITACHI 5000). Prior to imaging, samples were swollen to equilibrium by immersion in DI water overnight, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and subsequently freeze‐dried. The dried films were sputter‐coated with a 2–3 nm gold layer before analysis. SEM micrographs were processed using ImageJ software, and porosity was quantified as the ratio of dark pixels in hollow regions to the total image area.

Mechanical testing was performed on 2 different HMN arrays using a microtester equipped with a 1000 N compression cell. Arrays were mounted vertically between compression platens separated by 2 mm, and a compressive load was applied at 0.5 mm min− 1 up to a maximum of 100 N. The compression test result was recorded as the platens getting closer to each other.

For swelling analysis, agarose hydrogels (1.4% w/v) were prepared by dissolving 140 mg agarose in 10 mL of DI water. Crosslinked microneedle patches (10 and 20 min UV groups) were inserted into the agarose substrate for 5, 10, 15, 20 and 30 min, and swelling ratios were determined by the percentage increase in mass relative to the initial dry weight.

Molecular Docking Simulation

Molecular docking studies were performed using a multi‐software workflow. AutoDock Tools was employed to prepare the receptor (protein) structures by adding hydrogens, defining torsions, and generating the PDBQT input files [63, 64]. The o‐phenylenediamine (o‐PD) ligand was constructed and energy‐minimized in Avogadro before conversion to PDBQT format [65]. Docking simulations were carried out with AutoDock Vina, which provided binding affinities (ΔG) and predicted binding poses. The resulting protein–ligand complexes were analyzed and visualized in PyMOL (v3.1.4.1), using the educational licence, enabling detailed plotting of binding sites, hydrogen bonding, and contact residues.

Normalization Equation

All electrochemical responses were processed using the negative normalized current equation applied across all datasets (Equation S1; Figures 2, 3, 4), where I 0and Ip denote the baseline (blank) and post‐binding currents, respectively.

Target recognition within the MIP layer restricts charge transfer and ion diffusion at the electrode interface, leading to a concentration‐dependent decrease in current. This signal‐off response reflects the progressive occupation of imprinted sites and the resulting increase in interfacial resistance. For each analyte concentration, a separate I 0 −Ip pair was recorded to account for minor electrode‐to‐electrode variations and to isolate the true molecular binding contribution to current suppression. This normalization strategy yields a dimensionless and reproducible response metric that allows direct comparison across electrodes, analytes, and experimental conditions.

In Vitro Preparation, Optimization, and Specificity Tests

Optimization experiments were designed using biomarker‐specific parameters. (i) IL‐6 was employed to assess the effect of o‐PD concentration owing to its intermediate molecular size between thrombin and TNF‐α, thereby minimizing size‐exclusion effects. (ii) Thrombin was used to evaluate the influence of the electropolymerization cycle number and washing duration. (iii) TNF‐α was selected to determine the optimal incubation time, given its lowest calculated Gibbs free energy of binding. All optimization studies were performed on SPCEs to ensure reproducibility and comparability.

In Vitro Measurements

In vitro electrochemical measurements were conducted using LSV on 75 µL of PBS containing varying concentrations of each biomarker. The potential was scanned from 0.4 to −0.5 V at a rate of 5 mV s− 1 with a 2 mV step potential with a different electrode each time to eliminate the possibility of signal drift. The current obtained in the absence of the analyte (I 0) served as the baseline, whereas Ip represented the post‐binding current following target recognition. For thrombin, and IL‐6/ TNF‐α, the tested concentration ranges were 5–200 ng mL− 1 and 10–200 pg mL− 1, respectively, utilizing Autolab M101 multichannel potentiostat/galvanostat.

The PB/MIP biosensors exhibited strong linear correlations between the normalized signal‐off response (−ΔI/ I0) and the logarithm of analyte concentration (Figure S4a–c). The regression equation for thrombin was (−ΔI/Io) = 0.3497 (log Cthrombin) + 0.2921 (with an R2 of 0.9929), while for IL‐6 and TNF‐α the equations were (−ΔI/Io) = 0.3252 (log CIL‐6)–0.2543 (with an R2 of 0.9910) and (−ΔI/Io) = 0.3409 (log CTNF‐α)–0.2369 (with an R2 of 0.9987), respectively.

Incubation‐Time‐Dependent Binding Analysis

To better characterize the binding dynamics underlying the selected 2 min incubation time, LSV datasets were collected at analyte incubation times from 0 to 300 s. The resulting calibration curves were used to determine the analytical sensitivity (slope), and the corresponding datasets were further fitted using a nonlinear Langmuir binding model (Equation S3) to estimate the apparent dissociation constant (Kd) and maximum binding response (Bmax). The complete datasets and fitting results are provided in the (Figure S13 and Table S4).

As summarized in Table S4, the calibration slope increased progressively with incubation time, from 0.188 at 0 s to 0.226 at 30 s, 0.298 at 60 s, and 0.340 at 120 s. Only marginal additional improvement was observed at longer incubation times (0.346 at 180 s and 0.349 at 300 s, corresponding to an increase of only ∼2.6% from 120 to 300 s), indicating that the analytical response approaches saturation by 120 s.

Consistently, Langmuir fitting showed that the apparent Kd converged to a stable lower range of approximately 80–90 pg mL− 1 at longer incubation times (120–300 s), whereas greater variability was observed at shorter times, likely reflecting incomplete equilibration. The estimated Bmax values also stabilized over this interval, further supporting the attainment of near‐equilibrium binding conditions. Hence, these results identify 120 s as the optimal incubation time, as it provides near‐maximal analytical sensitivity together with stabilized Langmuir‐derived Kd and Bmax values, with no meaningful benefit at longer incubation times.

Storage Stability

The same electrochemical measurement parameters of the in vitro analyses were applied in this study. The operational shelf life of the wearable PB/ MIP biosensor platform was evaluated by monitoring signal stability over a 21‐day period under ambient and desiccated storage conditions. As illustrated in (Figure S14a–c), all three biosensors retained significant electrochemical activity over the 21‐day period, while retaining their own distinctive decay behavior. Thrombin specified PB/ MIP showed the slowest decay in the normalized signal response with a 40.9% decrease in the signal. The TNF‐α biosensor showed the highest decline in signal response from 0.668 (in the fresh state) to 0.372 (after 21 days of desiccation), which corresponds to 44.3% signal loss. Comparatively, the IL‐6 specified biosensor showed a signal decrease of 44.1% from 0.662 when freshly prepared to 0.370 after 21st days. Regardless of the moderate signal attenuation during the storage process, all the prepared biosensing platforms were capable of maintaining detectable and analytically significant signals beyond a three‐week period.

Ex Vivo Preparation and Optimizations

Ex vivo studies were conducted using freshly excised porcine ear skin to simulate ISF biomarker diffusion and detection conditions. Electrochemical measurements were performed under the same conditions as in vitro (LSV; 0.4 to −0.5 V, 5 mV s− 1, 2 mV step). Skin samples were trimmed into 3 cm × 3 cm sections, rinsed with DI water, and equilibrated overnight (16 h) in artificial ISF spiked with known concentrations of thrombin, IL‐6, or TNF‐α. This equilibration ensured homogeneous diffusion of biomarkers throughout the dermal matrix. Following equilibration, the PB/MIP HMN sensors specific to each biomarker were applied onto the corresponding skin sections. Each HMN array was gently inserted into the equilibrated skin, followed by placement of the flexible electrode layer on top to establish electrical contact. After 10 min of stabilization, LSV measurements were performed to quantify analyte concentrations under the following conditions: potential range, 0.4 to −0.5 V; scan rate, 5 mV s− 1; step potential, 2 mV.

Three independent sensor platforms corresponding to thrombin, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were tested in parallel at physiologically relevant ISF concentrations. Thrombin concentrations ranged from 25 to 200 ng mL− 1, representing basal to pathological levels during coagulative disorders. For IL‐6 and TNF‐α, concentration ranges of 25–500 pg mL− 1 were used to capture both basal and elevated inflammatory states in dermal ISF.

The regression equations for the ex vivo calibration curve of thrombin were as follows (−ΔI/Io) = 0.6916 (log C thrombin)–0.6598 (with an R 2 of 0.99287). While for IL‐6 and TNF‐α the regression equations were (−ΔI/Io) = 0.6319 (log C IL‐6) – 0.6681 (with an R 2 of 0.9785) and (−ΔI/Io) = 0.6653 (log C TNF‐α) – 0.7563 (with an R 2 of 0.9491), respectively, as illustrated in (Figure 3d–f).

In Vivo Experiments

Ethical Approval and Animal Model

All in vivo experiments were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and the Animal Welfare Act Regulations. Protocols were reviewed and approved by both the University of Waterloo Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), AUP Number 46548, and the McGill University Central Animal Facility (CAF). All electrochemical settings (potential window, scan rate, step size) matched those used in vitro. Naïve genetically non‐modified male Sprague–Dawley rats (250–350 g) that are two‐month old were anesthetized with isoflurane (3%–4% induction; 1%–2% maintenance) and injected IP with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, E. coli O55:B5, 1 mg kg− 1; Sigma–Aldrich, USA) to induce a controlled thrombo‐inflammatory response. This dose was selected to produce a moderate and reproducible endotoxemia model suitable for evaluating thrombo‐inflammatory biomarker dynamics, although the magnitude and temporal evolution of cytokine and coagulation responses in human disease may differ due to the greater complexity of clinical inflammatory conditions.

Patch Application and Electrochemical Interrogation

The dorsal skin (∼7 × 10 cm2) was shaved, depilated using commercial Nair cream, and cleaned with 70% ethanol. A wearable e‐MIP/HMN platform was gently inserted into the dermis and secured with medical adhesive film (Tegaderm, 3M, USA). Electrochemical measurements were performed under light anesthesia using a PalmSens 4 potentiostat (PalmSens, USA) in LSV mode, with identical parameters to the in vitro and ex vivo studies. Readings were acquired for 20–25 min at each post‐LPS time point (0, 0.5, 1, 3, 6, and 24 h) for thrombin, while the points were (0, 3, 6, and 24h) in the case of IL‐6. Between measurements, rats were recovered and housed in groups.

Blood Collection and ELISA Quantification

Tail‐vein blood (100 µL) was collected at baseline and at each time point using citrated tubes for thrombin and TAT assays, and EDTA tubes for cytokines. Samples were kept on ice, centrifuged (3000 × g, 10 min), and plasma was stored at −80°C until analysis. Plasma concentrations of thrombin, TAT, and IL‐6 were quantified using rat‐specific sandwich ELISA kits: Rat IL‐6 and Thrombin ELISA Kits (Antibodies Online, Cambridge, UK) and the Native Thrombin–Antithrombin Complex ELISA Kit (Creative Diagnostics, Shirley, NY, USA). All assays were performed in accordance with manufacturer instructions, and calibration curves were fitted using four‐parameter logistic regression.

Consistency of Baseline Signal in Saline‐Injected Rats

To verify that the wearable e‐MIP/HMN platform does not exhibit spurious drift in the absence of a target, we monitored the normalized current change (−ΔI/Io) in negative‐control rats injected with saline. Four electrodes were placed along the dorsal skin and read at multiple time points (8 total reads). Across all measurements, the baseline signal stayed tightly centered near zero (mean (−ΔI/Io) = 0.0079, S.D. = 0.0236, S.E.M. = 0.0083; n = 8), with a range from −0.0256 to 0.0363 and a maximum absolute deviation of 0.0363 normalized units. A one‐sample t‐test vs. zero showed no evidence of systematic drift (t(7) = 0.95, two‐tailed p∼0.37), as in (Figure S15). These data indicate that, in vivo, the electrode readout remains stable in the absence of thrombin, supporting the specificity of signal decreases observed in thrombin‐exposed conditions.

Differential Recognition and Computational Characterization

To quantitatively analyze thrombin and TAT co‐binding behavior, a full 4 × 4 response matrix was constructed by varying thrombin (0, 25, 50, 150 ng mL− 1) and TAT (10, 20, 50, 100 ng mL− 1) concentrations within the physiological–pathological range. The corresponding normalized current changes ((−ΔI/Io), dimensionless) are summarized in (Figure 3j), which presents a heat‐map of the mean electrochemical response (n = 3, ± S.D.). The signal increased monotonically along both analyte axes with a stronger gradient for thrombin, indicating a dominant contribution from the free enzyme. Cross‐sections extracted at fixed TAT concentrations (10, 20, 50, 100 ng mL− 1) showed nearly parallel slopes, confirming that TAT primarily offsets the baseline with minimal alteration of thrombin sensitivity, as in (Figure 3k–l).

A linear‐interaction model, I = 0.119 + 0.003686T + 0.001740C − 4.8 × 10−6 TC, was fitted to the experimental dataset (Table S2). The instantaneous sensitivity ratio, (∂I/∂T)/(∂I/∂C), calculated from the model, ranged from ∼1.9 to 3.6 across the concentration window, with a grand‐mean value of ∼2.52. This analysis indicates that, on average, the sensor is about 2.5 times more responsive to thrombin than to TAT.

Evaluation of Electrode Fouling Post ‐Ex Vivo Skin Contact

To assess electrode fouling during skin contact, an ex vivo experiment was performed using porcine skin. Three conditions were examined: a pristine electrode (Figure S16a), an electrode integrated with the hydrogel microneedle (MN) layer (Figure S16b), and an unprotected electrode placed in direct contact with skin (Figure S16c). All electrodes were maintained on porcine skin for 20 min under identical conditions. SEM analysis after exposure showed clear debris accumulation and partial surface obstruction on the unprotected electrode, whereas the MN‐integrated electrode remained comparatively clean and closely resembled the pristine control. These results indicate that the hydrogel MN layer functions as a protective interface that minimizes direct tissue contact with the sensing surface while preserving ISF transport, thereby reducing fouling during ex vivo measurements.

Statistical Analysis

Data are reported as mean ± S.E.M. (or mean ± S.D. where indicated), with the number of replicates (n) stated in figure captions. All curve fitting and statistical tests were performed in OriginPro 2025b (OriginLab). Calibration curves were obtained by ordinary least‐squares regression of the negative normalized current (−ΔI/I0) vs. log10(concentration). Limits of detection and quantification were computed as LOD = 3.3 × σlow / slope and LOQ = 10 × σlow / slope, where σlow is the standard deviation at the lowest (non‐zero) concentration level and “slope” is the calibration slope. Group comparisons for optimization (e.g., o‐PD concentration, electropolymerization cycles) and selectivity assays were assessed by one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post‐hoc test. In vivo/time‐course comparisons were evaluated with one‐way ANOVA at each time point; when applicable, control baselines were tested against zero using one‐sample tests. Assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were inspected via residual diagnostics in OriginPro; if violated, non‐parametric alternatives (Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's multiple comparisons) were used and specified in the captions. Two‐tailed α = 0.05 was adopted throughout. Significance is annotated as n.s. (p ≥ 0.05), * (p < 0.05), ** (p < 0.01), *** (p < 0.001), and **** (p < 0.0001).
