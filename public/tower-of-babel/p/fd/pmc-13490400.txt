Unravelling the bi-functional role of cation-regulated surface hydroxyl kinetics in Pt-catalyzed alcohol fuel cells
Authors: Zhangyan Mu, Zhibin Ma, Weiyi Zhao, Luhan Dai, Dan Xu, Pan Ran, Panke Zhang, Xuejun Wu, Junjie Ge, Mengning Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrocatalytic alcohol oxidation is pivotal for renewable energy conversion, yet its widespread application is hindered by generally low efficiency, necessitating the development of advanced catalytic systems with boosted electro-oxidation kinetics. In an alkaline electrolyte, the effect of alkali metal cations has been identified as a key impacting factor on the alcohol oxidation activity, but the underlying mechanistic interactions remains insufficiently explored. Here we elucidate the fundamental cation effects on hydroxyl (OHads) adsorption and subsequent surface bi-molecular kinetics in Pt (and alloys)-catalyzed alcohol electro-oxidations through combined electrochemical voltammetry, in situ surface enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (SEIRAS) and in situ electrical transport spectroscopy (ETS). Systematic evaluations reveal that alkali metal cations regulate the micro-electro-kinetics by modulating the surface coverage of OHads, which acts bi-functionally as both reactant species and spectator in alcohol oxidations. A matching adsorption kinetics of OHads and alcohol is key towards the optimal alcohol oxidation activity, providing valuable insights into the cation-mediated alloy catalyst systems and high-concentration conditions in alcohol fuel cells.Subject terms: Fuel cells, Electrocatalysis, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

The electrochemical oxidation of alcohols serves as a fundamental process in various energy conversion technologies, including electrochemical organic synthesis, biomass conversion and direct alcohol fuel cells1–3. For example, methanol can be oxidized to formic acid, a value-added chemical products, using non-precious Ni-based catalysts4,5. Moreover, methanol fuel cells have attracted research interests due to the intrinsic advantages of methanol-based fuel, such as high energy density, ease of storage and transport, and renewability as a biomass-derived compound6. To reduce costs while improving oxidation activity and durability, various Pt-based alloys such as PtAu, PtBi, PtPd, PtRu, PtCo, and PtCu have been developed7–11. The introduction of alloying elements enables fine-tuning of the adsorption free energies of key intermediates such as COads, CHOads, CH3Oads, and OHads. Rossmeisl et al. identified two distinct pathways for methanol oxidation: a direct route involving the CHOads intermediate and an indirect route involving COads intermediate12,13. The latter pathway requires the combined evaluation of OHads and COads adsorption energies as an activity descriptor. Moreover, You et al. demonstrated that OHads participates in the direct pathway by reacting with CHOads14, suggesting that a bifunctional mechanism facilitates the methanol oxidation reaction. Despite notable progress in mechanistic understanding and catalyst design, the performance of methanol oxidation and fuel cells remains unsatisfactory. Existing studies indicate that the power output of direct methanol fuel cells (DMFC) is generally one or two orders of magnitude lower than that of proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs)15,16. Enhancing the oxidation kinetics is still crucial for advancing alcohol-based fuel cells and related electrocatalytic alcohol oxidation systems.

The electrolyte conditions have been recently discovered to significantly impact (and therefore potentially modulate) the performance of fuel cells17. In an alkaline fuel cell that allows for the use of nonprecious electrocatalysts and exhibits better stability18,19, the presence of alkali metal cations (M+) in alkaline electrolyte can effectively regulate the chemical microenvironment within the electrode–electrolyte interface17–27. Strmcnik et al. demonstrated that Li+ can stabilize the OHads adsorption on Pt (111) in alkaline media as a result of the non-covalent interaction between M+(H2O)x clusters and surface OHads21. This effect follows the order of increasing hydration energies of the cations: Li+>Na+>K+>Cs+. The formation of M+(H2O)x-OHads cluster on Pt surface leads to reduced active sites through steric effect, resulting in decreased activity of hydrogen oxidation, oxygen reduction and methanol oxidation reactions. Similar theory has been applied in the following studies demonstrating that Li+ primarily decreases the reaction activity in a variety of electrochemical reactions28–31. For reactions requiring the presence of OHads on electrode surface, however, controversy phenomena were also observed. Koper and co-workers reported the enhanced CO oxidation through stronger OHads adsorption in presence of Li+/Be2+32, and speculated that the Li+ inhibition of methanol oxidation could be attributed to the surface poisoning effect from stable OHads adlayer on Pt(111) at high potentials, rather than the previously proposed direct site-blocking effect from the large (H2O)x·Li+-OHads clusters within the electrochemical double layer. It was later discovered that the specific influence of Be2+ on the methanol oxidation on Pt(111) shows an obvious dependence on the density of (110) steps, which may be attributed to the changes in the surface oxide coverage33. Lopes et al. proposed the correlations between surface oxophilicity and ethanol oxidation reactivity with different alkali cations, claiming that the OHads promotes the ethanol oxidation reaction at low potentials and act as spectator at high OHads coverage34. In addition to the unsettled mechanisms of cation effect on the alcohol oxidation kinetics, alternative model structure of the alkaline cation clusters at the double layer has also been proposed in recent fundamental electrochemical studies on the Pt surface35,36. Nevertheless, the definitive pivotal factors regarding the cation effects on alcohol oxidation kinetics remain unresolved, and practical utilization of such effect for boosted alcohol oxidation performance is yet to be developed.

To this end, in situ identification and quantification of surface species critical to the electro-oxidation-kinetics (such as OHads), particularly during active alcohol oxidations under varying complicated catalytic environments with different cations, are key to elucidating how cations alter the local chemical microenvironment at the electrode–electrolyte interface, and to settle the controversy models regarding the cationic regulation pathway. However, this is a long-standing challenge, as typical electrochemical investigations heavily rely on voltammetry-based quantification for redox-based surface processes, which becomes ineffective under the background of the high Faradaic (i.e., alcohol oxidation) currents during active reaction processes21,28,33. Moreover, the alkali metal cations readily undergo hydration in aqueous solution, posing significant challenge in direct characterization by either ex situ or in situ vibrational spectroscopies (such as IR and Raman), with water interference and low signal intensity37,38.

Here we present a comprehensive study on the cation–OHads interaction and its impact on alcohol oxidation kinetics using combined electrochemical voltammetry, in situ surface enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (SEIRAS) and in situ electrical transport spectroscopy (ETS). The specific on-chip experimental setup and general working principle of ETS are illustrated in Fig. 1a (also see Supplementary Fig. 2). This technique enables real-time monitoring of the electrical conductivity of catalysts under potential sweeping conditions. The resulting signal, denoted as IETS, is highly sensitive to the evolution of adsorbed species such as Hads, COads, and OHads in response to the specific electrode potentials under operando reaction conditions. Concurrently, to gain a more comprehensive view of the reaction process, we employed complementary in situ SEIRAS, of which the signals are sensitive to the key reaction intermediate (COads) during alcohol oxidations. By systematically exploring the interfacial and kinetic characteristics during oxidation reactions with varying types of alcohol molecules, electrolytes, pH values and metal surfaces, we revealed that the OHads species functions as both reactive species and spectator on Pt surface, and the potential dependence of these different roles can be rationalized from the surface electro-kinetic perspectives. The adsorption kinetics of both alcohol and OHads species, and their optimal balance for the consequent surface bi-molecular reactions, play a crucial role in determining the overall oxidation activities, leading to distinct cation effect at varying levels. The electro-kinetic analyses were further demonstrated to provide insights into the cation effect in alloy systems and high-concentration alcohol fuel cells, achieving the practical utilization of cationic regulations from dynamic design principles.

Results and discussion

Cation-dependent Pt surface states and alcohol oxidation activities

The adsorption characteristics on polycrystalline Pt surface and its correlation on the alkali metal cations (Li+, Na+ and K+) were first examined by cyclic voltammetry (CV) using Pt nanowires (PtNWs) at pH13 (Fig. 1c). As the cationic radius decreases from K+ to Na+ and Li+, the Hads oxidation peak (inset in Fig. 1c) exhibits a negative shift, consistent with previous reports23–25,35. Compared to K+ and Na+, Li+ can enhance the OHads adsorption at low potentials, thereby facilitating the Hads oxidation. At higher potentials (~0.8 VRHE), the strong OHads adsorption on LiOH hinders oxide formation21,38, resulting in low oxidation current. In the case of CsOH (Supplementary Fig. 3a), the large Cs+ cation with a soft solvation shell may specifically adsorb on the Pt surface27,35,39, resulting in low CV current (compared to KOH) across all tested potentials. Considering that the coverage of OHads species upon cation regulation tends to show relatively minor variation with scanning potentials particularly at the low potential range, and CV mainly characterizes the changes in surface adsorbates accompanied by noticeable Faraday currents, in situ ETS was further conducted for characterization. As shown in Fig. 1d, the ETS of Pt in KOH shows characteristics similar to the reported results, which can be divided into three regions: Hads oxidation (region I), double-layer region (D.L., region II), and reversible adsorption of hydroxyl groups and surface oxide formation (M−OH and M−O, region III). At potentials ranging from 0.05 to 0.60 VRHE, the IETS exhibits a distinct decrease in LiOH (compared to KOH and NaOH), suggesting more adsorbed OHads on Pt surface, leading to increased surface electron scattering35,40,41. However, at 0.8 VRHE, close IETS were observed in LiOH, NaOH and KOH, indicating comparable surface coverage of oxygenated species (OHads and Oads) under these conditions. In sharp contrast to all other alkali metal cations, a decreased change in IETS was observed for CsOH. While previous studies utilizing molecular dynamics simulations, in situ SEIRAS, and AFM have substantially demonstrated the specific adsorption of Cs⁺ ions27,35,39, the present ETS results provide additional and direct evidence for the specific adsorption of large-radius cations. Given the markedly distinct adsorption behavior, Cs+ will not be subjected to further investigation or emphasized within this paper.

Based on the observed trend in surface adsorption of oxygenated species on Pt, we further investigated the cation effect under operando alcohol oxidation conditions, beginning with methanol for its simplest molecular structure and practical significance of methanol oxidation in fuel cell technologies. Figure 1e shows the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) of methanol oxidation with an activity trend of Li+<Na+<K+, which is consistent with the previous findings observed on Pt(111)21. However, the presence of comparable oxygenated species on Pt surface at high potentials (Fig. 1d) in different cation-containing electrolytes does not exhibit a clear correlation with the methanol oxidation activities (Fig. 1e). For example, the IETS are close at 0.8 VRHE, but the alcohol oxidation activities vary significantly with different cations. This observation seems to support that the inhibition of active sites cannot be solely attributed to the presence of surface-adsorbed OHads and Oads21. Alternatively, as Li+(H2O)x-OHads exhibits the strongest interaction energy, it presumably results in the highest coverage of such cluster and significantly hinders the adsorption of reactants through enhanced site-blocking effect21.

ETS measurements were further conducted to investigate the Pt surface states during methanol oxidation. As shown in Fig. 1f, the Hads adsorption is clearly inhibited by methanol oxidation in KOH (arrow in region I), which can be attributed to the poisoning effect caused by COads intermediate generated during the multi-loop potential cycles42. A higher IETS in region III during methanol oxidation also indicates that the OHads adsorption is hindered due to the competitive adsorption of alcohol species. Furthermore, at a fixed methanol concentration (0.1 M), a declining IETS was observed from K+ to Na+ and Li+ (arrow in region III). This result suggests that the strong OHads adsorption in LiOH persists even during the active methanol oxidation. More sensitive differentiated ETS (dETS, δIETS/δE) signals (Fig. 1f) further reveal that the stronger OHads adsorption in LiOH (as compared to KOH) begins around 0.61 VRHE. Importantly, this potential is well correlated to the different methanol oxidation activities observed in LiOH and KOH (Fig. 1e). Therefore, it can be concluded that the primary factor leading to the reduced methanol oxidation activity in LiOH is the strongly adsorbed OHads (Fig. 1b), rather than the presence of Li+(H2O)x-OHads clusters. To assess the generality of our findings, we evaluated a commercial Pt/C catalyst in MOH and MOH + CH3OH (M = Li, Na, K, Rb and Cs, Figs. S4 and S5). The resulting voltammetric profiles of Pt/C closely align with those of the PtNWs (Fig. 1c, e), validating the general applicability of cation effects across different Pt-based catalysts.

In situ SEIRAS was further employed to probe the adsorbed intermediates during alcohol oxidation on Pt surface. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 6b, the CV curve of deposited Pt film in a SEIRAS cell set-up exhibited voltammetric features similar to PtNWs (Fig. 1c) and Pt/C catalysts (Supplementary Fig. 4a), despite a lack of well-defined oxidation peak (Fig. 1g) due to the roughened surface morphology of the electrodeposited Pt film43. The trend in cation-dependence of methanol oxidation activity (K⁺ > Na⁺ > Li⁺) aligned well with both Pt/C and PtNWs (Supplementary Fig. 4b and Fig. 1e). Figure 1h and Supplementary Fig. 7 present the in situ SEIRAS results of Pt film in 0.1 M MOH (M = Li, Na, K) + 0.1 M CH₃OH. Characteristic bands corresponding to linearly bonded CO (COL, 2030–2065 cm⁻¹) and bridge-bonded CO (COB, 1800–1840 cm⁻¹) were clearly detected44. At a reference potential of 0.1 VRHE, the potential-dependent trend of the COL band intensity in different cation environments was tracked (Fig. 1h) in the range of 0.2 VRHE to 1.2 VRHE. In KOH, the COL band intensity strengthens as the potential rises from 0.2 VRHE to 0.6 VRHE, indicating the accumulation of COL on the Pt surface. Subsequently, the intensity declines at potentials above 0.6 VRHE, which can be ascribed to the oxidative removal of COL by OHads and surface oxides. Notably, the COL band intensity in LiOH is stronger than that in KOH. Given that the in situ SEIRAS signal reflects changes relative to the reference potential (0.1 VRHE), this higher intensity indicates that a greater amount of pre-adsorbed surface species (such as OHads) is displaced by COads, which corroborates the conclusion that Li⁺ cation promotes a higher initial OHads coverage than K⁺. At higher potentials (0.8–1.2 VRHE), the COL band intensity in LiOH decreases, eventually falling below KOH. This indicates that the strongly adsorbed OHads promoted by Li+ more effectively competes for Pt sites, thereby displacing the adsorbed COads.

Electrocatalytic oxidation and cationic effects of different alcohols

The above observations lead to the necessary consideration of competitive surface co-adsorption of both OHads and alcohol molecule (or corresponding molecular intermediate) on the overall electro-kinetics of oxidation. To further probe this co-adsorption process and their impact on the oxidation kinetics, different alcohol molecules with varying carbon chains (lengths or hydroxyl numbers) were further examined for the cation effect. From practical point of view, other alcohol molecules such as ethanol, ethylene glycol and glycerol, are also candidates for alcohol fuel cells due to their high energy densities45,46. The tested alcohols were divided into two groups based on the number of hydroxyl groups (Fig. 2a, b) and the length of carbon chains (Fig. 2c, d).

As shown in Fig. 2a, ethylene glycol (EG) exhibits higher oxidation activity than methanol. In theory, its longer carbon chain enables a larger amount of electron transfer (per molecule) upon complete oxidation. However, the oxidation of alcohols can proceed through parallel pathways that generate various byproducts (rather than complete oxidation to CO2). For instance, EG oxidation produces a more diverse mixture including glycolate, formate and oxalate (Supplementary Figs. 8, S9), leading to considerable complexity in its oxidation behavior and interfacial conditions. The ETS results indicate that EG oxidation tends to suppress OHads adsorption at high potentials (>0.8 VRHE, Fig. 2b), as reflected by the higher IETS signal compared to both pure KOH and methanol-containing KOH electrolytes. This observation was further verified by the in situ SEIRAS measurements (Fig. 2f), which showed that EG oxidation generates more COads than methanol oxidation, providing additional evidence for the inhibitory effect of COads on surface OHads species. The COads during EG oxidation presumably originates from C–C bond cleavage47, via key intermediates such as HOCH2–CO and CHO–CHO48,49. To gain further insight, density functional theory (DFT) calculations (Supplementary Fig. 10) suggest that the C–C cleavage via both HOCH2–CO and OHC–CHO has a thermodynamically favorable ΔG of −0.63 eV and –1.4 eV, respectively. These results support that EG oxidation can proceed via COads intermediate, promoting oxidation via an indirect pathway. Notably, product analysis also reveals a high Faradaic efficiency (80.2%) for glycolate formation (Supplementary Fig. 8), indicating a parallel C2 reaction pathway (without COads formation) that also significantly contributes to the enhanced oxidation activity. When the carbon chain is further extended, glycerol oxidation yields reduced COads and activity (Fig. 2f). This can be attributed to the steric hindrance imposed by the longer carbon chain, along with the increasing difficulty of activating chemical bonds at its terminal positions. Since the electron-transfer rate across a solid/electrolyte interface decays exponentially with the donor−acceptor distance50, the activation of chemical bonds located at the far end of a long carbon chain consequently becomes less efficient51,52. Based on these findings, we can further elucidate the trend in cation effect (Fig. 2i). The oxidation activities of methanol, ethylene glycol and glycerol all follow a trend of Li+ <Na+ <K+, indicating that the strong OHads adsorption in LiOH can generally inhibit the oxidations. Quantitatively, variations in the level of cation effect are observed among different alcohols, as indicated by the jK/jLi ratios. A lower jK/jLi ratio (Fig. 2i) was found to correlate with higher oxidation activity (Fig. 2a), enhanced COads adsorption (Fig. 2f) and weakened OHads adsorption (Fig. 2b). Therefore, an active surface adsorption and oxidation significantly counteracts the site blocking effect caused by the strong OHads adsorption in LiOH, resulting in weakened cation effect.

Among the alcohols with one hydroxyl group (Fig. 2c), ethanol exhibits higher oxidation activity than methanol. The activity decreases for alcohols with longer carbon chains (1-propanol and 1-butanol). ETS results indicate that all the alcohols inhibit the OHads adsorption at high potentials (>0.8 VRHE, Fig. 2d). However, the alcohols with long carbon chains (1-propanol and 1-butanol) exhibit reduced ISD at 0.8 VRHE and thus more pronounced OHads adsorption compared to methanol (arrow ① in Fig. 2d), underscoring the influence of the carbon chain on the structure of electrocatalytic interfaces. Although ethanol exhibits higher oxidation activity than methanol (Fig. 2c), it does not suppress OHads adsorption more significantly. Moreover, at low potentials (<0.4 VRHE) prior to the onset of alcohol oxidation, the OHads adsorption is enhanced for ethanol, 1-propanol, and 1-butanol (arrow ② in Fig. 2d), in sharp contrast to ethylene glycol (Fig. 2b). We propose that the long carbon chain of adsorbed alcohols can disrupt the electrochemical interfaces and hinder the accumulation of cations at the outer Helmholtz layer (inset in Fig. 2d). This view is consistent with previously reported cation effects35,53. In a hypothetical pure water environment without cations, OHads adsorption on the Pt surface would be stabilized35,53. SEIRAS results further show that ethanol generates fewer COads intermediates as compared to methanol (Fig. 2h). Previous studies suggest that C–C bond cleavage in ethanol is relatively challenging, and it can be promoted by doping Pt with elements such as Ga, Au, and Rh54–56. Our DFT calculations further show that ΔG for C–C cleavage in ethanol is –0.19 eV (Supplementary Fig. 10), which is less favorable compared to methanol and ethylene glycol oxidations (–0.78 eV, –1.4 eV and –0.63 eV). These results are consistent with the product analysis of ethanol oxidation, which shows the formation of acetate with a high Faradaic efficiency of 81.3%, suggesting that the high oxidation activity is mainly achieved via a direct oxidation pathway. For 1-propanol and 1-butanol oxidation, COads formation is further reduced (Fig. 2h), consistent with their lower current densities in LSV. Compared to methanol, C2–C4 alcohols all exhibit a decreased ISD at 0.8 VRHE (Fig. 2d). Regarding the cation effect, the jK/jLi ratio decreases monotonically with the increasing length of the carbon chains, which can be attributed to the disturbance of electrocatalytic interfaces. The presence of long carbon chains leads to fewer cation accumulation in the outer Helmholtz plane and weakens interactions within the M⁺(H₂O)ₓ–OHads cluster, thereby diminishing the cation effect. We further investigated the potential influence of steric hindrance of carbon chain on the oxidation kinetics and the cation dependence, using 1-butanol and 2-butanol as the model substrates. It was found that the branched structure of 2-butanol markedly suppresses its oxidation activity (Supplementary Fig. 16a), exhibiting a jK/jLi ratio of 1.27 (Supplementary Fig. 16b) that is lower than 1-butanol (jK/jLi = 1.34, Supplementary Fig. 14b). These results reinforce the critical role of steric hindrance in affecting the oxidation kinetics and modulating the cation effects during alcohol oxidations.

Methanol oxidation kinetics upon cation regulation

To further elucidate the role of OHads species and cations in alcohol oxidation, we performed systematic catalytic kinetic studies by varying the concentrations of methanol, OH−, and cations (Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. 18). At pH12 (low OH⁻ concentration), increasing methanol concentration from 10 mM to 1000 mM in KOH results in decreased oxidation activity, indicating that excessive methanol adsorption causes significant poisoning of Pt surface. In contrast, increasing methanol concentration in LiOH enhances the oxidation activity, suggesting that more strongly adsorbed OHads in LiOH react with methanol and facilitate the oxidation. At pH13, overall oxidation activities are generally higher than those at pH12, due to the faster OHads formation kinetics under higher OH⁻ concentration. Due to the rapid supply of OHads, the oxidation activity in KOH increases monotonically as the methanol concentration rises from 10 mM to 500 mM. Further increasing the methanol concentration to 1 M in KOH also reduces oxidation activity due to the poisoning effect caused by excessive methanol adsorption. In an electrolyte with high OH− concentration, increasing the methanol concentration promotes the oxidation in both LiOH and KOH. However, the strong OHads adsorption in LiOH hinders the oxidation reaction (compared to KOH) at all tested methanol concentrations. Therefore, we conclude that the strongly adsorbed OHads (with fast adsorption kinetics) in LiOH can function as both reactive species and spectators, and a well-matched adsorption kinetics of methanol and OHads will effectively enhance the oxidation reaction (Fig. 3c). It should be additionally noted that the corresponding high cation concentration (1 M) in pH 14 electrolyte may also cast a cation-mediated OHads regulation (as supported by the suppressed oxidation activity upon addition of Li⁺ to 0.1 M KOH in Supplementary Fig. 19), making it difficult to directly compare activities between pH=14 and pH12/13. Nevertheless, the conclusion derived from methanol concentration dependence remains robust under different pH values. In addition to the analyses of peak current densities, a comparison of overpotentials (at a constant current density of 0.5 mA cm⁻2) was also conducted at varying methanol concentrations and pH values, and similar trends were observed (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Based on the above explorations, both the adsorption kinetics of alcohol and OHads upon cation regulation are found crucial for alcohol oxidations. Importantly, we recognize that the OHads adsorption kinetics are insufficient at high methanol concentrations. At pH 12 and pH 13 with a CCH3OH/COH− ratio of about 10, the strong OHads adsorption in LiOH can support/boost the methanol oxidation. It is thus expected that a similar phenomenon can also be observed at pH 14 (from a perspective of kinetics matching) when the methanol concentration exceeds 10 M. To prove this hypothesis, we examined the effect of methanol concentration (from 1 M to neat) on methanol oxidation in 1 M KOH. As shown in Fig. 4a, the oxidation activity increases with the increasing methanol concentration until 10 M, indicating that the OHads adsorption kinetics is indeed insufficient at high methanol concentrations. Since the LiOH solubility is low in an alcohol/water mixture, we introduced a small amount of Li+ (10−3 ~ 10−2 M) into 1 M KOH to enhance the OHads adsorption kinetics (Fig. 4b). The addition of Li+ leads to a 160 mV decrease in the peak potential, indicating that the strong OHads feeding in LiOH can effectively promote the oxidation reaction. Microkinetic modeling was further employed to confirm the key surface kinetic factors (for details, see “Methods”). While the actual electrocatalytic processes are inherently complex, involving multiple intermediates and pathways, our modeling only focused specifically on the cation effect by increasing the kinetic coefficient k for OHads adsorption on Pt: OH−+*→OHads+e−. As shown in Fig. 4c, d, the methanol oxidation can indeed be promoted by the fast OHads adsorption kinetics with increasing k. To achieve high specific energy comparable to the conventional Li-ion batteries, the direct methanol FCs require a high methanol concentration up to 9 M (assuming an overall efficiency of 20% for a direct methanol FC system)57–59. The cation regulation strategy developed in this report thus provides insights for further optimization of alcohol FC systems. When the methanol concentration was increased to 20 M (Supplementary Fig. 21), the cation effect vanished, which can be attributed to excessive methanol adsorption and reduced non-covalent Li+(H2O)x-OHads interactions in an electrolyte with low water content. Based on our kinetic explorations of methanol oxidation, we have also promoted the glycol oxidation reaction by adding 10−3 M Li+ into 1 M KOH (Supplementary Fig. 22), demonstrating the universality of cation regulation for different alcohol oxidation reactions. To validate the practical utility of the cation-regulation method, we further conducted measurements in a direct methanol fuel cell. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 23, the addition of 10⁻2 M LiClO4 to the electrolyte (1 M KOH + 10 M CH3OH) yielded a notable rise in cell voltage and power output. For example, at a current density of 20 mA cm⁻2, the cell voltage increased from 0.22 V to 0.26 V upon Li⁺ addition, corresponding to a rise in power density from 4.5 mW cm⁻2 to 5.2 mW cm⁻2.

Cation effect on Pt-M alloy surface

Alloying is commonly employed as a means to regulate the adsorption energy of key reaction intermediates, typically through the modulation of electronic properties of active sites. However, the potential effects of surface co-adsorption kinetics, particularly involving cation effects, have been rarely investigated. Herein, Pt-Pd and Pt-Au alloys were further tested, representing the most widely studied alloy catalysts11,14,60–62. Pt-Pd and Pt-Au with varying alloying degree were successfully prepared (see “Methods”), and characterized by high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, Supplementary Figs. 24, S25) and X-ray diffraction (XRD, Supplementary Fig. 26).

As shown in Fig. 4e, in 0.1 M KOH + 0.1 M CH3OH, Pt75Pd25 and Pt50Pd50 exhibit higher oxidation activities compared to pure Pt in KOH. In Pt-Pd bimetallic systems, methanol adsorption is accompanied by rapid C–H bond cleavage at Pd sites11,14. The reaction intermediates can migrate between Pt and Pd sites, facilitating methanol dehydrogenation. Although the COads generated during oxidation adsorb on the catalyst surface and poison active sites, it can be effectively removed by reacting with strongly adsorbed OHads on PtPd(111) (as compared to pure Pt)11,14. Consequently, the enhanced activity is attributed to favorable methanol dissociation kinetics (C–H bond cleavage) coupled with more efficient OHads-assisted oxidation of COads on the PtPd(111) surface. As the Pd content increases to 75%, the oxidation activity is decreased, which can be explained by the overdilution of Pt sites. In LiOH, the oxidation activities are overall lower than in KOH due to the poisoning effect of strongly adsorbed OHads. However, the accelerated methanol adsorption kinetics on Pt-Pd (compared to pure Pt) can also obviously boost the oxidation despite the strong inhibition by OHads in LiOH, leading to decreased jK/jLi ratios (Fig. 4f) and weakened cation effect. For Pt-Au catalysts (Fig. 4e), Pt89Au11 also exhibits higher methanol oxidation activity compared to pure Pt in KOH, which is previously attributed to the weakened CO poisoning effect on Pt-Au surface14,62. As C−H and O−H bond cleavage are not advantageous at either Pt or Au sites, the enhanced alcohol oxidation activity primarily stems from the relatively low proportion of indirect COads pathway on Pt–Au, leading to more efficient oxidation14,62. The oxidation activity decreases with further increasing Au content (Pt72Au28, Pt37Au63) due to the overdilution of Pt sites. Notably, compared to pure Pt, Pt89Au11 only exhibits a slight increase in oxidation activity in LiOH. This observation aligns well with the unfavorable kinetics of methanol dissociation adsorption and indirect COads pathway on PtAu(111), which is ineffective in mitigating the poisoning effect of strongly adsorbed OHads. Additionally, it is noteworthy that although Pt89Au11 and Pt75Pd25 exhibit similar oxidation activities (~6 mA/cm2) in KOH, the cation effect of Pt89Au11 (jK/jLi = 1.84) is significantly higher compared to Pt75Pd25 (jK/jLi = 1.33) (green arrow in Fig. 4f). This indicates that it is not the total oxidation activity but rather the adsorption kinetics of methanol that primarily affects the cation effect. With the diminished oxidation activities in LiOH, the further introduction of Au (Pt72Au28, Pt37Au63) is capable of weakening the strong inhibition by OHads62 around Pt sites, leading to enhanced oxidation activity.

The above analyses of alloy systems primarily emphasize the importance of methanol adsorption kinetics in affecting the cation effect on the bimetallic surface. To further elucidate such phenomenon, we attempted to enhance the methanol adsorption kinetics (correspond to a steady OHads kinetics) by increasing the methanol concentration from 0.1 M to 1 M on Pt50Pd50 at pH13 (Supplementary Fig. 28). As a result, the peak current densities become comparable in LiOH and KOH, and the poisoning effect by OHads in LiOH is weakened, indicating that the observed cation effect in alloy systems is indeed also affected by the kinetic factors. Besides, in 1 M KOH with high methanol concentration (10 M), the addition of 10−2 M Li+ can effectively accelerate the OHads adsorption kinetics and weaken the poisoning effect by alcohol intermediates, thus boosting the oxidation (Fig. 4g). These results highlight that for Pt-M alloy catalysts, there is also an optimal electrolyte composition that leads to the most favorable surface kinetic balance and yield the highest oxidation activity.

In conclusion, electrochemical voltammetry, in situ SEIRAS, and in situ ETS were employed to monitor the OHads adsorption and reaction intermediates upon cation regulation during Pt-catalyzed alcohol oxidations. The strongly adsorbed OHads species in LiOH inhibit the oxidation of various alcohols by occupying active sites, and this effect is weakened by the enhanced adsorption capacities of alcohols. Further kinetic investigations with different methanol concentrations and electrolyte pH reveal that OHads also acts as a reactant species during alcohol oxidations. Importantly, a matching adsorption kinetics between OHads and alcohols is crucial for enhancing the overall oxidation activity and inducing varied cation effects, providing valuable insights for understanding the cation effect in alloy systems and advancing the high-concentration fuel cells. In the case of alloy catalysts (Pt-Pd), the inhibition by OHads in LiOH can be weakened by the fast methanol adsorption kinetics, leading to weakened cation effect. When the OHads adsorption kinetics is insufficient at high methanol concentrations (10 M) and pH 14, the strong OHads adsorption in LiOH can effectively boost the alcohol oxidations.

Methods

Materials

Commercial carbon-supported Pt catalyst (Pt/C, 20 wt% Pt nanoparticles on carbon black), nafion solution (5 wt%) and perchloric acid (HClO4, 48–50%) were bought from Alfa Aesar. Chloroplatinic acid (H2PtCI6·6H2O, 99.9%) and isopropanol (C3H8O, 99.5%) were bought from Adamas-Beta. Palladium acetylacetonate (C10H14O4Pd, 99%), glycerol (C3H8O3, 99%), ethylene glycol ((CH2OH)2, 98%), 2-butanol (C4H10O, 99%), tert-butanol (C4H10O, 99.5%), iso-butanol (C4H10O, 99.0%), lithium hydroxide (LiOH, 99.99%), ammonium fluoride (NH4F, 99.99%), sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3, 99%), sodium sulfite (Na2SO3, 98%), ammonium chloride (NH4Cl, 99.99%), sodium hydroxide (NaOH, 99.9%), patassium hydroxide (KOH, 99.99%) and lithium perchlorate (LiClO4 98%) were purchased from Aladdin. Methanol (CH3OH, 99.5%), hydrofluoric acid (HF, 40%), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 38%), sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98%), nitric acid (HNO3, 68%), ethanol (C2H5OH, 99.7%), 1-butanol (C4H10O, 99.8%) and N,N-dimethylformamide (C3H7NO, 99.5%) were supplied by Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chloroauric acid (HAuCl4·3H2O, 48–50% Au basis) and propanol (C3H8O, 99.0%) were bought from Macklin. Cesium hydroxide solution (CsOH, 50 wt%, 99.9%) and rubidium hydroxide (RbOH, 100%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals were used as received without further purification. All Electrolytes were prepared with Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ/cm) and degassed with Ar for 30 min before use.

Synthesis of Pt and Pt-based nanowires

0.1 M H2PtCl6 aqueous solution (400 μL) was added to a mixed solvent containing 12 mL EG, 12 mL DMF and 1 g KOH. After stirring for 30 min, the resulting mixture was transferred into a 50 mL Teflon-lined autoclave. The autoclave was maintained at 170 °C for 8 h and then cooled to 25 °C63. The products (Pt nanowires) were washed several times with ethanol and DI H2O. Pt-Au and Pt-Pd alloy nanowires were also prepared using the same method, with the addition of Au and Pd precursors. Specifically, Pt37Au63, Pt72Au28, and Pt89Au11 nanowires were prepared by adding 1200, 400, and 133 μL of 0.1 M HAuCl4, respectively. Pt24Pd76, Pt50Pd50, and Pt76Pd24 nanowires were prepared by adding 36.56, 12.19, and 4.06 mg of C10H14O4Pd, respectively.

Preparation of free-standing films from nanowires

Supplementary Fig. 2 shows the preparation process of nanowire films. In a typical self-assembly experiment40,41, 1 mL EtOH dispersion of Pt nanowires (1 mg/mL) was mixed with 2 mL H2O, followed by ultrasonication for 5 min. 2.5 mL 1-butanol was then added to the mixture, followed by ultrasonication for another 1 min. The final suspension was then added drop by drop into a flask (about 7.5 cm in diameter) filled with Milli-Q water. A piece of nanowire film was finally formed at the water/air interface and was later transferred onto the Si/SiO2 wafer.

Fabrication of the device for ETS measurements

Si wafer (p++ with 300 nm thermal oxide) with pre-patterned Au electrodes (Ti/Au, 20 nm/50 nm) was used as substrate. A poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA, A8, MicroChem Corp.) film (~1000 nm thick) was spin-coated on the Si wafer, and consequent E-beam lithography (EBL) was used to open windows with defined shape. The pre-prepared (by co-solvent evaporation) free-standing film of nanowires (on the water surface) was then deposited onto the substrate surface. For a fine-controlled deposition, the substrate was treated with oxygen plasma before deposition. After the removal of the PMMA template, nanowires were then patterned on the device substrate with the desired designs. To eliminate the influence of electrolyte and to avoid electrochemical reactions on the metal electrodes, another layer of PMMA (~500 nm thick, electrochemically inert) was then deposited on the chip by spin-coating. A smaller window that only exposes nanowires was opened by e-beam lithography. A drop (2 μL) of Nafion solution (0.1 wt% in ethanol) was added on top of the sample. The final device, with exposed nanowires and PMMA-protected electrodes was used for in situ ETS measurements.

Concurrent voltammetry (CV) and conductance (ETS) measurements

Concurrent CV and ETS measurements were performed at the temperature range of 23–25 °C. A two-channel source-measure unit (SMU, Keysight B2902a) was used for ETS measurements. A first SMU channel was used as a potentiostat to apply the potential of source/drain electrode (acting as working electrode) as to the reference electrode (Ag/AgCl), while collecting the current (IG) through the counter electrode (Pt wire). A second SMU channel was used to record ETS signals by supplying a small bias potential (50 mV) between source and drain electrodes and collecting the electrically conductive current (IETS). For a typical ETS measurement in this study, the contributions of IG to IETS during the measurement of on-chip nanowires can be properly deducted by the equation (IETS = I2 + IG/2). The in situ ETS measurements were performed once. The measured potential vs. EAg/AgCl was converted to reverse hydrogen electrode potential (RHE) based on the Nernst equation, ERHE = Evs. Ag/AgCl + 0.059 × pH+ 0.197 V. The electrolytes were freshly prepared prior to each experiment and used immediately without storage.

Electrochemical measurements and product analyses

The electrochemical experiments were conducted in a typical three-electrode cell using a CS3004 multichannel potentiostat (CorrTest Instruments) at a temperature range of 23–25 °C. An Ag/AgCl electrode and platinum wire were used as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. The working electrode was a glassy carbon electrode (RDE) (geometric area: 0.196 cm2). The measured potential vs. EAg/AgCl was converted to reverse hydrogen electrode potential (RHE) based on the Nernst equation, ERHE = Evs. Ag/AgCl + 0.059 × pH + 0.197 V. The volume of electrolytes in the three-electrode cell was 20 mL. The Pt/C catalyst ink was prepared by ultrasonically mixing 1 mg of Pt/C powder with 500 μL of ethanol, 495 μL of water and 5 μL of 5 wt% Nafion solution for 1 h. 10 μL of the Pt/C catalyst ink was then dropcast onto the glassy carbon electrode to achieve an areal loading of 10.186 μgPt/cm2. The inks of Pt-based nanowires were prepared by ultrasonically mixing nanowires with 0.8 mg carbon black (Vulcan XC-72), 500 μL of ethanol, 495 μL of water and 5 μL of 5 wt% Nafion solution for 1 h. The actual mass concentrations of the Pt species in the nanowire inks were controlled at 0.20 mgPt/mL. The nanowire catalyst inks were then dropcast onto the glassy carbon electrode to achieve a areal loading of 10.186 μgPt/cm2. Prior to the testing in alkaline electrolytes, the working electrodes were cycled in 0.1 M KOH with a scan rate of 40 mV/s between the potential range of 0.05 and 1.2 VRHE for 30 cycles. The CV results obtained in this study were all recorded at a scan rate of 40 mV/s. Product quantification was performed using an H-cell configuration. The working electrode was prepared by casting Pt/C catalyst ink onto carbon paper (loading: 1 mg cm⁻2). Both compartments of the H-cell were filled with 30 mL of electrolyte (0.1 M KOH + 0.1 M methanol, ethylene glycol, or ethanol), and a magnetic stir bar was placed in each compartment and rotated at 600 rpm. A Hg/HgO electrode served as the reference electrode in the anode compartment. A Pt foil (geometric area: 1 cm2) was used as the counter electrode in the cathode compartment. The measured potential vs. EHg/HgO was converted to reverse hydrogen electrode potential (RHE) based on the Nernst equation, ERHE = Evs. Hg/HgO + 0.059 × pH + 0.098 V. A commercial Fumasep FAA-3-50 membrane (Sinero, geometric area: 1.77 cm2, thickness: 50 μm), initially in the bromide counter-ion form, was converted to the hydroxide form by immersion in 1 M KOH solution for 24 h to ensure complete ion exchange. The membrane was then used to separate the anode and cathode compartments in the H-cell assembly. Following constant-potential oxidation at 0.8 VRHE for 1 h, the products were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Fuli LC5090) equipped with an ultraviolet detector at 200 nm. The products were quantitatively analyzed using a C18-AQ column (250 mm × 4.6 μm × 5 μm) maintained at 50 °C, with 25 mM H2SO4 as mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. A 10 μL sample of the electrolyte solution was injected into the HPLC system for the detection of organic acids. All the samples were acidified with 6 M HCl before analysis. The calibration curves for product quantification are shown in Supplementary Fig. 9. The Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) of products were calculated using the equation: FE(%) = mol of product/[total passed charge/(n × F)] × 100%, where n is the number of electrons transferred for the formation of the product, and F is the Faraday constant. The product characterizations were performed once. All electrolytes were freshly prepared prior to each experiment and used immediately without storage.

In situ SEIRAS experiments

The Pt film working electrodes used for SEIRAS measurements were prepared through a two-step deposition process, including the initial chemical deposition of an Au thin film on a hemispherical Si prism followed by galvanostatic electrodeposition of a Pt thin film on the Au substrate64. Before the chemical deposition of Au, the Si prism was first polished using 1, 0.3, and 0.05 μm Al2O3 powder for around 20 min until totally hydrophobic. Then, the Si prism was sonicated several times with water, acetone/ethanol (volume ratio, v/v = 1:1) to clean the surface. The hydrophobic and clean Si prism underwent hydroxylation treatment with piranha solution (the volume ratio between concentrated H2SO4 and 30% H2O2 is 7:3) for 15 min. Then the IR reflection plane was dried by N2 flow and treated by 40%wt NH4F for 180 s to make the surface terminated with hydrogen. The treated reflection plane was dried by N2 flow and immediately immersed into a mixture of 6.4 mL of Au plating solution and 85 μL of HF solution at 56 °C for 6 min. The plating solution was prepared by dissolving 120 mg HAuCl4, 42 mg NaOH, 378.12 mg Na2SO3, 158.11 mg Na2S2O3, and 53.49 mg NH4Cl in 10 mL of H₂O. The resulting mixture was then stored overnight at 25°C before being diluted to a final volume of 40 mL with H2O. The ohmic resistance along the Au film diagonal was around 5-6 Ω. A PARSTAT electrochemical workstation (VersaSTAT 3 F) was used for the electrochemical measurements during in situ SEIRAS characterizations. The measurements were performed in a three-electrode cell filled with 40 mL of electrolyte at temperature range of 23–25 °C (Supplementary Fig. 6). The Au thin film was cycled in an Ar-saturated 0.1 M HClO4 solution at a scan rate of 50 mV/s between 0.5 VRHE and 1.7 VRHE until repeatable CV curves were obtained. Electrodeposition of the Pt film was conducted in an Ar-saturated 4 mM H2PtCl6 + 0.7 M Na2HPO4 solution at a current density of −0.32 mA/cm2 for 900 s, followed by −0.16 mA/cm2 for 480 s. The Pt thin film was washed with water several times and used as working electrode (geometric area: 3.14 cm2). The measured potential vs. EHg/HgO was converted to reverse hydrogen electrode potential (RHE) based on the Nernst equation, ERHE = Evs. Hg/HgO + 0.059 × pH+ 0.098 V. A Pt foil (geometric area: 1 cm2) served as the counter electrode. The in situ SEIRAS measurements were carried out on a BRUKER INVENIO-R FTIR spectrometer equipped with a mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector cooled with liquid nitrogen. Unpolarized infrared radiation froman Elema source was focused at the interface at an incident angle of 60°, and the reflected radiation was detected. Real-time spectra with a resolution of 8 cm−1 were recorded during stepping the working electrode potential, and each spectrum was integrated by 64 scans. All spectra are shown in the absorbance units −log(R/R0), where R and R0 represent the reflected intensity of the sample and reference spectrum, respectively. Reference spectra were taken at 0.1 V vs RHE. The in situ SEIRAS measurements were performed once.

General material characterizations

HADDF-STEM images and corresponding STEM-EDS elemental mappings were obtained on Talos F200S and SUPER X. XRD was conducted on a PANalytical X-ray diffractometer with a Cu Kα resource.

DFT calculation

The DFT computations were carried out using Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) in conjunction with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method65,66. The revised Perdew−Burke−Ernzerhof functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) framework was employed to describe electron exchange−correlation interactions. A kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was used. The zero−damping method of Grimme (DFT-D3) is added to consider the dispersion corrections67. For structure optimization, the convergence criteria were set to 10−6 eV for energy and 10−2 eV/Å for force. A four-layer 2 (√3 × √3)R30° Pt(111) slab model was constructed to represent the surface, where the bottom two layers were fixed, and a vacuum of 15 Å was adopted along the z-axis. Fabrication of membrane electrode assembly and electrochemical measurement of direct methanol fuel cells

A precisely weighed amount of Pt/C (20 wt%) catalyst was dispersed in a mixed solvent (isopropanol: H₂O = 1:1 by volume) and ultrasonicated to form a homogeneous catalyst ink with a catalyst concentration of 3.0 mg/ml. Subsequently, FAA-3 ionomer was added into the ink, achieving an ionomer content of 20 wt% relative to the Pt/C. This ink was then drop-coated onto the carbonaceous gas diffusion layers (geometric area: 1 cm2) to achieve a Pt loading of 1.5 mg/cm2 for the anode and 1.0 mg/cm2 for the cathode. A commercial Fumasep FAA-3-PK-75 membrane (Sinero, geometric area: 1 cm², thickness: 75 μm), initially in the bromide counter-ion form, was converted to the hydroxide form by immersion in 1 M KOH solution for 24 h to ensure complete ion exchange. The membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was assembled by cold-pressing this hydroxide-form membrane between the as-prepared anode and cathode GDEs. The assembled MEA was then integrated into the fuel cell hardware. During DMFC measurements, an aqueous solution of 1 M KOH + 10 M CH3OH was supplied to the anode at a flow rate of 5 mL/min, while humidified oxygen was fed to the cathode at a flow rate of 300 mL/min. The DMFC measurements were performed once. The fuel cell temperature was maintained at 30 °C using heating rods inserted into the end plates, with thermocouples inserted into the bipolar plates for temperature monitoring.
