Finding ferritin footprints in 5-fluorouracil-induced cardiotoxicity
Authors: Grace M Ferri, Manuel Urina-Jassir, Alexander V Stanisic, Jack M Hensien, Grace Lee, Neal Sullivan, Omar K Siddiqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract (200 words)Fluoropyrimidines, including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), used in the treatment of patients with gastrointestinal malignancies, are associated with cardiotoxicity. Although the mechanism of this cardiotoxicity remains unknown, rat models treated with 5-FU have higher iron concentrations in myocardial tissue compared to controls. As a result, we sought to examine whether ferritin could be a clinical biomarker of cardiotoxicity among patients receiving 5-FU-based chemotherapy. We conducted a retrospective chart review on adult patients receiving 5-FU-based intravenous chemotherapy at a single academic center from June 2022 to 2024. Potential cardiotoxicity was defined by obstructive coronary artery disease, new wall motion abnormalities on transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), left ventricular ejection fraction decrease ≥10% from pre-treatment TTE, coronary vasospasm, pericardial effusion, or incident heart failure. Of our 93 patients, those with ferritin ≥ 100 ng/mL were nearly 5 times as likely to exhibit potential cardiotoxicity (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 4.7, confidence interval [CI] 1.0-23.5) (Fig. 1A). Similarly, patients with ferritin ≥ 100 ng/mL were approximately 3 times as likely to experience treatment failure (aOR 3.2, CI 1.1–9.7). Based on the apparent relationship between ferritin ≥100 ng/mL, cardiotoxicity, and treatment outcomes, we encourage oncologists to routinely obtain iron studies in addition to pre- and post-treatment TTE among patients receiving 5-FU.Keywords: Cardiotoxicity, Chemotherapy, Coronary artery disease, Fluoropyrimidines, Gastrointestinal malignancies

Fluoropyrimidines, including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), used in the treatment of patients with gastrointestinal malignancies, are associated with an increased risk of atherosclerotic coronary artery disease (CAD), coronary artery vasospasm, and heart failure (HF) [1]. As a result, baseline transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) and consideration of CAD screening are recommended before drug administration [2]. Although the mechanism of 5-FU-induced cardiotoxicity remains unclear, in vivo studies have suggested that 5-FU triggers iron-dependent cell death, or ferroptosis, by enhancing transferrin receptor expression [3]. Moreover, rat models treated with 5-FU have higher iron concentrations in myocardial tissue than controls [4]. Based on these molecular findings, we sought to examine whether ferritin could be a clinical biomarker of cardiotoxicity as manifested by obstructive CAD, coronary vasospasm, or incident HF among patients receiving 5-FU-based chemotherapy.

We performed a retrospective study on patients aged 18 and older receiving 5-FU-based intravenous chemotherapy (5-FU, FOLFOXIRI, FOLFIRI, FOLFIRINOX) at a single academic center (Boston Medical Center, Boston, USA) from June 2022 to 2024. Ferritin ≥ 100 ng/mL and TSAT ≥ 20% cutoffs were used to reflect biologically available iron in patients with cancer, consistent with established definitions of functional iron sufficiency [5]. Data were collected by manual chart review with laboratory studies obtained closest to and within 24 months following first receipt of 5-FU-based chemotherapy; when multiple post-treatment troponin values were available, the maximum was used. Values for iron studies were excluded if within 6 weeks of iron infusion. Our main outcome was potential cardiotoxicity defined by the presence of obstructive CAD detected on coronary angiography, new wall motion abnormalities (WMA) on echocardiography, left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] decrease ≥ 10% from pre-treatment TTE, coronary vasospasm, pericardial effusion, or incident HF. Pre-treatment echocardiogram measurements were extracted as close to or within 2 weeks of initial treatment; post-treatment echocardiogram measurements were extracted at least 2 weeks after initial treatment but no later than1 year after final treatment. Statistical analyses including multivariable logistic regression and Kaplan-Meier analysis were performed in R. Single imputation was employed to address missing covariates in the dataset. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Boston University (H-45297).

Baseline characteristics of adult patients (N = 93) receiving intravenous 5-FU are summarized in Table 1. Most patients were between 40 and 80 years of age (n = 50, 86%), male (n = 55, 59%), diagnosed with colorectal cancer (n = 64, 69%), and receiving FOLFOX chemotherapy (n = 85, 91%). Upon analysis, those with ferritin ≥ 100 ng/mL were nearly 5 times as likely to exhibit potential cardiotoxicity (aOR = 4.7, confidence interval [CI] = 1.0-23.5) after adjusting for age and gender (Fig. 1 A). Similarly, those with ferritin ≥ 100 ng/mL were approximately 3 times as likely to experience treatment failure (aOR 3.2, CI 1.1–9.7) (Fig. 1B). No statistically significant relationships were identified between TSAT ≥ 20% and cardiotoxicity or treatment failure.

Our findings suggest that elevated ferritin may foreshadow cardiotoxicity and treatment failure among patients receiving 5-FU. We suspect that 5-FU-induced myocardial iron overload at the molecular level may clinically translate to observed ferritinemia and provoke cardiotoxicity.

This study is limited by its retrospective nature and small sample size, resulting in incomplete data for a subset of patients. To address missing ferritin values, we used the conservative worst-case single-imputation approach, presuming missing ferritin values to be <100 ng/mL. This strategy was selected to reduce the risk of falsely classifying patients as iron-replete and to bias estimates toward the null. Multiple imputation using chained equations was explored as a sensitivity analysis; however, this method was not statistically feasible due to model non-convergence arising from small cohort size and multicollinearity, which resulted in computationally singular imputation models. Given these constraints, multiple imputation could not be reliably implemented.

We acknowledge that ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and may be elevated in the setting of acute or chronic illness. However, in a cohort largely composed of patients with gastrointestinal malignancies, the ability to mount elevated ferritin levels may reflect underlying iron metabolism and disease biology rather than inflammation alone. Finally, due to multicollinearity and limited degrees of freedom, expanded multivariable models incorporating additional comorbidities or cancer stage produced unstable estimates and were not included.

Our study is the first to suggest a potential role for ferritin as a biomarker predicting elevated risk of cardiotoxicity in patients with malignancies treated with a 5-FU-based regimen. While this hypothesis requires validation in larger cohorts with corresponding sensitivity analyses, a ferritin level of ≥100 ng/mL may identify a subgroup of patients who merit closer surveillance for incident cardiotoxicity.
