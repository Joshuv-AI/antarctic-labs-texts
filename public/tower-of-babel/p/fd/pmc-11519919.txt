Semi-synthesis of a DNA-Tagged Polyketide–Peptide Hybrid Macrocycle Using a Biosynthetically Prepared Fungal Macrolide as a Synthetic Component
Authors: Soya Koremura, Akihiro Sugawara, Yohei Morishita, Taro Ozaki, Teigo Asai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPresented herein is a synthetic biological method using genome mining and heterologous expression systems that provides access to natural products with desirable structural features as building blocks. In this investigation, we synthesized polyketide–peptide hybrid macrocycles with DNA tags, which have the potential to access a DNA-encoded library containing over one million compounds. This study demonstrates that synthetic biology offers a tool for expanding the diversity of building blocks, facilitating the exploration of unexplored chemical space.

Recent genome analytic campaigns on microorganisms have revealed a tremendous gene resource for natural products. With accumulating natural product biosynthetic knowledge and improving heterologous expression systems of bacteria and fungi,1 we can mine biosynthetic gene clusters and obtain corresponding natural products.2 Accordingly, in this study, we applied a synthetic biological method based on genome mining and heterologous expression to explore natural products with new structures and biological activities.3 Notably, genome mining enables us to mine biosynthetic gene clusters for natural products with not only new structures but also our desirable structural features. Heterologously biosynthesized natural products can be supplied in sufficient quantities by repeatedly cultivating the corresponding transformants, making this an advantageous approach for generating natural products for various applications, including semi-synthesis using natural products as synthetic components. Previously, we produced pseudo-natural polyketides by combining heterologous biosynthesis of multi-potent intermediates and diversity-oriented synthesis, significantly expanding the chemical space of polyketide-related molecules.4

Today, middle-sized molecules, such as those beyond the “rule of 5” compounds,5 are under the spotlight in drug discovery because they combine the macromolecular feature of a highly specific binding affinity with proteins with the small-molecule features of oral bioavailability and low production costs.6 In particular, macrocycles tend to show stronger binding affinities to targeted proteins compared to linear compounds7 and natural macrocyclic compounds, such as erythromycin and cyclosporin, thus can make major contributions to drug discoveries.8 Accordingly, the construction of compound libraries that mimic natural macrocycles is being reported for several approaches.9−12

DNA-encoded library (DEL) technology is a hit-finding method that can screen a million-plus-membered library using only a few processes.13 Given the significant potential of DELs in drug discovery, there have been numerous reports on the development of macrocyclic DELs.14 However, fragments that form macrocyclic rings other than amino acids often rely on readily available substrates, such as aromatic moieties. The preparation of substrates that closely resemble those in natural products is time-consuming, as considerable time and effort are required for synthesis from a simple starting material. To the best of our knowledge, there are no reports of polyketide moieties akin to natural products being utilized. Polyketide–peptide hybrid natural products, such as metacridamide A15 and emericellamide A (Figure 1a),16 have potential as drug discovery resources. Therefore, constructing DELs that mimic these hybrid compounds presents valuable opportunities for exploring potential pharmaceutical lead candidates.

Our recent genome mining efforts have revealed the biosynthetic mechanisms of fungal macrolide, which involve a highly reducing polyketide synthase (HR-PKS) and standalone thioesterase (TE), leading new fungal macrolides being obtained with various polyketide chain structures with the difference in carbon numbers and modifications.17 The results enabled us to mine putative biosynthetic gene clusters for macrolides using homology-based searches, resulting in the discovery of hundreds of putative biosynthetic gene clusters. The current information suggests that a variety of macrolide scaffolds can be obtained. Furthermore, the corresponding carbon frameworks can be chemically modified to give acyclic polyketide building blocks with potentially reactive functional groups, which offer suitable footholds for further chemical transformation.

On the basis of this strategy, we designed a polyketide–peptide hybrid macrocycle comprising a polyketide derived from a macrolide natural product, a synthetic tetrapeptide, and a DNA tag building block (Figure 1b). The reaction connecting the two fragments must occur in aqueous environments, given the context of DNA-based synthesis. It was proposed that the polyketide and peptide fragments be joined through amide condensation, with the cyclization of the resulting linear compound facilitated via nucleophilic substitution reactions involving thiol groups and chloroacetyl moieties, in a reaction exhibiting high chemoselectivity under basic aqueous conditions, as reported by Suga and co-workers.9b Our study was aimed at achieving a sustained expansion of the library by uncovering different natural PK chains through genomic approaches and gaining access to untapped chemical space differently from macrocyclic peptides.

We herein report a strategy for the synthesis of a DNA-tagged polyketide–peptide hybrid macrocycle, utilizing a combination of synthetic biology and organic synthetic chemistry. Notably, we derived the natural polyketide framework using the aforementioned methods and chemically modified it into a suitable building block, which facilitated the formation of the polyketide–peptide hybrid macrocycle via two orthogonal coupling reactions involving a polyketide and a tetrapeptide unit. The synthesis of a DNA-encoded macrocycle comprising the desired DEL was also accomplished, which has the potential to access the corresponding DELs containing over one million compounds (Figure 1b).

Initially, we sought a suitable polyketide building block. With ApmlA and ApmlB as queries for the genome mining of a macrolide scaffold, we identified the peml cluster on the genome of Penicillium expansum in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database (Table S2 of the Supporting Information). In addition to a HR-PKS (PemlA) and a TE (PemlB), the cluster encodes putative short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR, PemlC), a berberine bridge enzyme (BBE, PemlD), a P450 (PemlE), and an O-acetyltransferase (PemlF) (Figure 2a). On the basis of the previous results,17 it is hypothesized that the macrolide is initially formed by PemlA and PemlB, and then the scaffold is modified by surrounding enzymes. To elucidate the macrolide backbone constructed by PemlA and PemlB, we prepared the Aspergillus oryzae (AO)-pemlAB strain harboring pemlA and pemlB. After a new peak, compound 1, not present in the control was detected (Figure 2b), isolation and structural analysis revealed it to be a 16-membered macrolide (Figure 2c), indicating that the peml cluster is a macrolide biosynthesis gene cluster. Next, we investigated the function of PemlC, PemlD, and PemlE. First, AO-pemlABCD and AO-pemlABE strains were constructed and analyzed in the same manner. No conversion of compound 1 was observed in any transformants of AO-pemlABCD (Figure S1 of the Supporting Information). However, two new peaks, compounds 2 and 3, were detected in the medium and mycelial components of AO-pemlABE strains (panels a and b of Figure 2). Structural analyses revealed that compound 2 is a macrolide with a hydroxy group introduced at the C-4 position and compound 3 is a 4,5-diol derivative of compound 1 (also known as berkeleylactone J).18 According to these results, PemlE is P450 that sequentially hydroxylates the C-4 and C-5 positions of the 16-membered macrolide. Next, to generate macrolides modified by the remaining enzymes, AO-pemlABEC, AO-pemlABED, and AO-pemlABECD strains were constructed and analyzed for their medium and mycelial components (Figure S1 of the Supporting Information). No changes were observed in AO-pemlABEC, while a new peak, compound 4, detected only in the medium extracts of AO-pemlABED and AO-pemlABECD was identified (Figure 2b). Structural analysis revealed that compound 4 is a carboxylic acid analogue of the 16-membered macrolide (Figure 2c and Table S4 of the Supporting Information). The hydroxy group at the C-4 position of compound 3 was oxidized to a ketone, and the α,β-unsaturated carbon–carbon double bond was reduced to ethylene. Similar changes were observed in strains with pemlD or pemlCD introduced, suggesting that compound 3 is converted to compound 4 by PemlD and PemlC is not functional. The remaining acyltransferase pemlF was investigated, resulting in no modification on the macrolides (Figure S1 of the Supporting Information).

We found that compound 4 exhibits an unexpected structure, differing from predictions based on the structure of compound 3 and the typical activity of BBE. To investigate the biosynthetic pathway of compound 4, we fed compound 3 for AO-pemlD and analyzed the resulting metabolites using liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS) (Figure S2 of the Supporting Information). Here, compound 4 was detected in a culture broth of AO-pemlD but not in A. oryzae NSPlD1, indicating its involvement in the transformation of compound 3 to compound 4 by PemlD. A homology search of PemlD identified a BBE-like enzyme, PylE (44% identity and 58% similarity), which catalyzes the oxidation of an allylic hydroxy group to ketone and the hydrogenation of the adjacent olefin during the biosynthesis of macrodiolide.19 Together with the results of our feeding experiments, these findings lead us to propose that the biosynthetic pathway of compound 4 originates from compound 3, which might undergo oxidation on the hydroxy group at C-4 and reduction of the olefin at C-2/C-3 by PemlD, followed by hydrolysis of the ester linkage by native enzymes of the A. oryzae host.

To establish a synthetic pathway for a model containing polyketide moieties, a quantitative supply of a polyketide moiety is essential. Among our various heterologous expression systems of macrolide, we selected compound 3 as the most productive (111 mg/L, obtained 2.0 g).17 Additionally, compound 3 possesses typical functional groups, such as an olefin, a hydroxy group, and enone moieties, commonly found in fungal macrolides (Figure S3 of the Supporting Information). We thus envisioned that, if chemical transformations of compound 3 could be performed, such transformations would also be applicable to other fungal macrolides. Before embarking on the synthesis on a DNA tag, we synthesized model compound 13 without a tag (Figure 3). TBS protection of the diol of compound 3 (98% yield) and methanolysis with NaOMe produced alcohol 6 in 90% yield. To construct the orthogonal coupling moiety, the secondary alcohol of compound 6 was replaced by a chloroacetylamide moiety via tosylation (84% yield), SN2 substitution by phthalimide (64% yield), Gabriel amine synthesis, and chloroacetylation (45% yield over two steps). Removal of the TBS group with TBAF (94% yield), followed by hydrolysis with NaOH (74% yield), produced target fragment 11. Then, fragment 11 was coupled with the tetrapeptide Gly-Leu-Phe-Cys(Acm)-OMe, resulting in cyclic precursor 12 in 53% yield. Our final tasks were removing the protecting group and the macrocyclization reaction. We found that the addition of PdCl2 effectively removed the Acm group after purification.20 Immediately adjusting the crude reaction mixture to basic conditions successfully converted it to the desired compound 13 in 39% yield.

Synthesis of compound 13 without a DNA tag: (a) TBSCl, imidazole, DMF, 50 °C, 97%, (b) NaOMe MeOH, 24 °C, 90%, (c) TsCl, Et3N, DMAP, DCM, 25 °C, 84%, (d) PhthK, DMF, 25 °C, 64%, (e) NH2NH2·H2O, MeOH, reflux, (f) chloroacetyl chloride, DCM, 23 °C, two steps in 45%, (g) TBAF, 23 °C, 94%, (h) 1 M NaOH (aqueous), MeOH/THF (1:1, v/v), 22 °C, 74%, (i) Gly-Leu-Phe-Cys(Acm)-OMe, EDC, HOBt, DIPEA, and DMF/DCM (1:1, v/v), 53%, and (j) PdCl2, DMSO, then DIPEA, 24 °C, 39%.

We then moved on to synthesizing the same molecule on a DNA tag, aiming at the construction of DELs (Figure 4). Generally, chemical reactions for DEL construction require that conditions do not harm DNA tags recording the structure of the compounds.21 Thus, we planned to use a phenylacetamidomethyl (Phacm) group to protect a thiol group of Cys instead of an Acm group. The Phacm group exhibits comparable deprotection properties to the Acm group when protecting cysteine and can be removed through straightforward hydrolysis in aqueous solutions, facilitated by the enzyme penicillin G acylase (PGA).22 Starting from the 15-amino-4,7,10,13-tetraoxapentadecanoic acid (AOP)-linked headpiece,13c compound 16 was assembled by Fmoc-based peptide synthesis, using a DNA tag. Although multiple attempts were made at removal of the Phacm group and the macrocyclization reaction with PGA solution, no DNA materials, such as compound 17 or 18, were detected with LC/MS, presumably due to an affinity of the resulting macrocyclic compound with PGA. Addressing this issue, we utilized immobilized PGA. After verifying the Phacm group removal, we subjected PGA to thermal denaturation and subsequently purified it using a tip column. This enabled an acceptable recovery rate (86%, calculated with AU260) of a DNA-containing compound (Table S6 of the Supporting Information). After HPLC purification of the residue, we obtained compound 18 in a 20% yield. Notably, we also prepared other derivatives with a different amino acid sequence of tetrapeptide on DNA tags (Figure S4 of the Supporting Information). To establish the feasibility of constructing a DEL, we synthesized compounds with DNA tags corresponding to the sequences of the four amino acids. Initially, we prepared the tetrapeptide Gly-Leu-Phe-Cys(Phacm) with a DNA tag (44/46 bases) encoding the sequences of these amino acids. Then, condensation with compound 11, removal of the Phacm group, and macrocyclization reactions were conducted, following the established synthetic route, which produced results consistent with prior findings (Scheme S1 of the Supporting Information).

Synthesis of the macrocyclic polyketide–peptide hybrid compound with a DNA tag: (a) Fmoc-Cys(Phacm)-OH, DMT-MM, then piperidine, 97%, (b) Fmoc-Phe-OH, DMT-MM, and then piperidine, 90%, (c) Fmoc-Leu-OH, DMT-MM, then piperidine, 82%, (d) Fmoc-Gly-OH, DMT-MM, then piperidine, 87%, (e) 11, HATU, DIPEA, 83%, and (f) immobilized PGA and HPLC purification, 20% yield.

In summary, we have demonstrated an approach to molecular assembly that integrates synthetic biological techniques, specifically genome mining and heterologous expression systems, with synthetic organic chemistry. Accordingly, the desired natural polyketide scaffold 3 was obtained in sufficient quantity via synthetic biological methods and chemically modified into a suitable building block. With the aim of constructing a macrocyclic library inspired by polyketide–peptide hybrid natural products, we designed the polyketide–peptide hybrid macrocycle and achieved the synthesis of one with/without DNA tags corresponding to sequences of the four amino acids (S8/13). This study has also established the detailed reaction conditions and a synthetic route for constructing the unprecedented DELs mimicking macrocyclic polyketide–peptide hybrid natural products. A notable aspect of this strategy based on synthetic biology and chemical synthesis is that we can apply any macrolides with a variety of structural features hidden in the gene resources into this library construction and easily expand the structural diversity of the library. In other words, it facilitates exploration of previously unexplored chemical space, accelerating the drug discovery.
