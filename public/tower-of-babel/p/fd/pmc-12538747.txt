Building a bovine blood DNA methylation epimap related to disease phenotypes
Authors: Lotfi Bouzeraa, Helene Martin, Jessica C.S. Marques, Ronaldo Cerri, Marc-Andre Sirard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpigenetic variations, particularly in response to environmental factors, play a crucial role in shaping immune identity and function in hematopoietic cells. This study investigates interindividual differences in DNA methylation among dairy cows, with the aim of enhancing our understanding of the adaptive capacity essential for sustainable animal production. We conducted whole-genome sequencing and DNA methylation analysis using enzymatic methyl-seq on whole blood from 60 Holstein cows. The study included five phenotype groups: mastitis, lameness, infertility, metabolic disorders and healthy controls.ResultsAmong the 50 million CpG sites, 5.1% were identified as variable methylated cytosines (VMCs) and 94.9% as conserved methylated cytosines (CMCs). VMCs displayed variability in distal promoter regions, suggesting potential plasticity in the associated genes, while CMCs exhibited a bimodal methylation pattern near the transcription start site, indicative of tissue-specific functions. Notably, we identified motif enrichments related to genes potentially expressed in blood. An age-related analysis revealed a 1.4% faster decline in CMCs methylation compared to VMCs. Additionally, disease risk assessment may be achievable using as few as 586 methylation biomarkers, which could be used to select which cows to keep in the herd for additional lactation.ConclusionOur results suggest a dual role for VMCs and CMCs: while the stability of conserved sites is potentially associated with essential functions in cell development and homeostasis, variable sites may be involved in dynamically regulating gene transcription in response to internal or external stimuli. These insights underscore the epigenome’s role in immune regulation and adaptive resilience in cattle.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12112-9.

Background

Epigenetics is a captivating field in biology that gradually unveils the complex mechanisms linking molecular processes to environmental factors.

The epigenome consists of epigenetic marks established by mechanisms such as chromatin remodeling, DNA methylation, histone types and tail modifications, and the action of non-coding RNAs.

Notably, it is increasingly evident that stable alterations to the epigenome during embryonic development can lead to molecular changes observed across one or more generations, a phenomenon known as intergenerational or transgenerational epigenetic inheritance [1]. In mammals, environmental stimuli can directly affect a developing embryo, including pre-formed oocytes within a female embryo [2, 3], leaving lasting epigenetic marks. Altered phenotypes appearing in the second generation (for paternal transmission) or the third generation (for maternal transmission) are considered transgenerational inheritance, while shorter-term effects are generally considered parental or intergenerational inheritance [1].

Studies in humans have shown that individual variations in DNA methylation have significant potential in population epigenetics [4]. This process is gaining attention as it challenges the idea that an individual’s biological fate is determined solely by the genetic information encoded in DNA.

Epigenetic dynamics are not confined to early development but continue throughout life, playing a crucial role in health regulation and well-being. After birth, through growth and development, epigenetic mechanisms continuously mediate between environmental factors (exposome) and the host’s genotype, remaining dynamic across the host’s lifespan and contributing to the regulation of diverse cellular processes and phenotype expression [5]. DNA methylation patterns can be influenced by environmental events, generating a source of epigenetic variability [6]. This type of epigenetic inheritance, particularly intergenerational inheritance, illustrates how environmental factors can directly influence the biology and health of multiple generations.

Given the influence of epigenetic modifications on gene expression, variations in DNA methylation patterns among individuals may contribute to phenotypic diversity [7]. It is suggested that epigenetic variations related to age, lifestyle, and environmental and nutritional exposures are all considered modulators of acquired human diseases [7]. There is a progressive increase in interindividual variation with age [8], indicating that both time and environment influence DNA methylation variation among individuals.

Earlier research identified systemic interindividual variation in DNA methylation through genome-wide profiling across multiple tissues from various individuals [9, 10]. Epigenetic regulation within internal organs and tissues varies among individuals and can be inferred from peripheral blood DNA [10]. Interindividual DNA methylation variability has been observed to contribute to natural human variation [11].

Measurements of DNA methylation in tissues like peripheral blood offer insights into epigenetic regulation across the body, where such variation is often referred to as systemic interindividual variation [4]. The conserved regions across tissues that exhibit systematic variability between individuals, known as correlated regions of systemic interindividual epigenetic variation (CoRSIVs), have also been identified in cattle and are associated with important biological processes and potential susceptibility markers for certain diseases [4].

CpG methylation has thus become a central focus for understanding how individual epigenetic variations affect agriculturally significant phenotypic traits. Over the last decade, this has led to growing interest in exploring the causes and consequences of individual epigenetic variation in cattle [12]. DNA methylation is sensitive to nutritional and environmental factors, and changes in methylation patterns can alter gene expression profiles, resulting in various phenotypes that may impact productivity and disease susceptibility [13, 14].

Natural DNA methylation variation plays a crucial role in shaping complex traits that are vital for cattle productivity and health [15]. However, despite its potential significance, research on interindividual methylation variations in cattle remains in its early stages, highlighting the need for further investigation. Expanding this understanding could ultimately contribute to more informed and refined selective breeding practices.

In our study, we utilized 60 dairy cows with distinct phenotypes to investigate the persistence of methylation marks despite external and internal influences, aiming to identify both conserved and highly variable methylation sites among individuals. The objective is to use methylation patterns in response to environmental influences and interindividual variability for the early identification of cows susceptible to disease ahead of time for replacement selection purposes.

Results

High conversion rates and extensive sequencing

Table S2 details the full set of sequence quality control parameters. All samples underwent deep sequencing, achieving an average of 665 million reads per sample. This coverage spanned approximately 97.29% of the genome, with an average sequencing depth of 32.51X. During the library preparation process, each sample was paired with an unmethylated lambda sequence to serve as a control for the enzymatic conversion rate. The methylation levels of lambda in all samples varied between 0.1% and 0.4%, indicating a high conversion rate of 99.6% to 99.9%.

The median methylation percentage for VMCs is 59.13%, compared to 89.10% for CMCs. Methylation levels for VMCs range from 5% to 94%, whereas for CMCs, they vary from 0% to 100% (Fig. 3C).

Over half of these positions, specifically, 50.24% of CMCs and 60.83% of VMCs, were found in distal intergenic regions, while 49.76% of CMCs and 39.17% of VMCs were located in genic regions.

Specifically, 5.55% of CMCs and 2.83% of VMCs targeted promoters, 37.45% of CMCs and 34.13% of VMCs targeted introns, and 4.91% of CMCs and 1.56% of VMCs targeted exons (Fig. 3D). These values indicate that CMCs are more frequently found in genic and regulatory regions compared to VMCs, suggesting a greater potential involvement of conserved methylation in the regulation of gene expression, while variable methylation tends to occur more frequently in intergenic regions, possibly reflecting increased epigenetic plasticity or environmental responsiveness.

The principal component analysis (PCA) of the variable methylated cytosines (VMCs) reveals that the first two principal components account for 6.6% of the total variance across the 60 individuals analyzed. This analysis highlights the presence of three distinct clusters within the dataset. These clusters suggest underlying patterns or groupings based on the methylation profiles, even though the explained variance is relatively low (Fig. 3E). This result indicates that other factors, such as individual epigenetic variability, may be more influential in driving the observed methylation patterns.

Correlation analysis of VMCs indicates associations between diseased and control cows, with specific pairwise correlations observed: BFR7022 and GD2043 (r = 0.61), BMS5055 and GD3059 (r = 0.61), BFR6121 and GD5008 (r = 0.58), and BFL9060 and GD4047 (r = 0.48) (Fig. 3F).

Age-Related methylation

To investigate the relationship between age and DNA methylation levels, we analyzed age correlations in both CMCs and VMCs. Age (1.8 to 9.2 years) was found to be negatively correlated with methylation levels in both contexts. In CMCs, the Pearson correlation coefficient is −0.53, indicating a moderate negative correlation, where methylation levels consistently decline as age increases. The R-squared values support these findings, with 28.27% of the variance explained by age in CMCs and 30% in VMCs. The slightly higher R-squared value in VMCs suggests that age has a marginally stronger overall influence on methylation variability in this dataset compared to CMCs (Supplementary Data).

Regression coefficients reveal a negative relationship between age and methylation in both datasets. In CMCs, the age coefficient of −5.38E-4 indicates a stronger direct effect of age, whereas, in VMCs, the coefficient of −3.90E-4 suggests a slightly smaller effect (Supplementary Data). This implies that, while age has a more pronounced direct impact on CMCs, the overall explanatory power of age on methylation variability is slightly greater in VMCs. However, this interpretation remains speculative due to differences in distribution between the two groups.

Furthermore, the analysis suggests that the correlation between age and methylation is stronger in CMCs than in VMCs. The model predicts a 1.96% reduction in methylation over 10 years for CMCs and a 1.42% reduction for VMCs, emphasizing the differential impact of age on these two types of methylation sites (Supplementary Data).

The age distribution across 60 individuals was divided into four categories: Young (1.8–3.6 years), Middle (3.7–5.4 years), Old (5.6–7.3 years), and Senior (7.4–9.2 years). In VMCs, the methylation shows more heterogeneity within each group compared to CMCs (Fig. 4B and C).

Motifs potentially implicated in control of expression

We selected sequences surrounding each CpG site, using a window of + 25 bp to −25 bp. A total of 19,797 CMCs were located between − 25 bp and − 975 bp upstream of the TSS, while 1,485 VMCs were found between − 1,025 bp and − 2,975 bp. To avoid redundancy, overlapping sequences were merged to obtain unique sequences of varying lengths.

For the 19,797 CMCs targeting 1,017 gene symbols and the 1,485 VMCs targeting 567 gene symbols expressed in blood, we generated 2,790 FASTA sequences for VMCs, with lengths ranging from 51 to 181 bp, and 3,850 FASTA sequences for CMCs, ranging from 51 to 675 bp.

Motifs were selected based on the presence of at least one CG site, E-values < 0.05, and motif matching criteria. CMCs primarily target regions that could be associated with transcription factors (TFs) from the KLF family (KLF10, KLF12, and KLF15), which play crucial roles in various biological processes, as well as HOXD12 :: ELK1, HOXB2 :: ELK1, and ETV6_full_2. VMCs, however, are linked to motifs that are potentially associated with factors such as EBF1, Duxl_1286.2, and Nkx2-6 (Table 1).

Differential methylation analysis between disease subgroups

Differential methylation analysis was conducted to identify differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs) between disease subgroups. This analysis identified 12,845 DMCs when comparing the four disease subgroups (n = 30) to the control group (n = 30), using a q-value threshold of < 0.01 and an absolute methylation difference greater than 25% (Table 2).

Among the disease subgroups, infertility exhibited the highest number of DMCs, followed by mastitis, lameness, and metabolic disorders.

In total, 539,597 unique DMCs were identified across all comparisons. Discussion

This study provides novel insights into methylation variability across distinct phenotypes in cattle. We analyzed whole-genome DNA methylation profiles from mature female cows representing five distinct phenotypes, examining 50,355,887 CpG sites across both DNA strands to ensure analytical robustness. Of these, only 5.1% exhibited variability, highlighting the specificity of methylation dynamics.

Environmentally induced variations in DNA methylation may serve functional roles, enabling phenotypic variability within a genetically homogenous population. In addition, genetic variation can partly explain inter-individual differences in DNA methylation. Sequence variants, such as SNPs, may influence methylation levels, Furthermore, other potential confounding factors, including differences in diet, management practices, or unmeasured genetic background, may have also contributed to the observed epigenetic variability. The epigenome acts as an additional layer of genetic regulation, playing a crucial role in organismal development and function. A significant portion of methylation sites remains stable or conserved across individuals. These conserved positions share characteristics within a species or breed, contributing to the epigenetic identity of each group. Stable, heritable epigenetic marks are often associated with genomic regions involved in fundamental biological functions that support homeostasis [17].

Conversely, approximately 5% of epigenetic positions vary between individuals, potentially influenced by environmental factors. These variable positions shape each individual’s unique epigenetic signature, enabling specific responses to various environmental stimuli. Such epigenetic variations can significantly impact health and well-being, influencing disease susceptibility, response to medical treatments, and adaptation to environmental changes. Variable positions within the epigenome, therefore, provide potential flexibility, allowing an organism to adapt dynamically. Considering this context, diseases could be viewed as a form of adaptation.

DNA methylation is a flexible mechanism capable of modulating gene expression to facilitate adaptation to changing environments [18]. Regions distant from the TSS show more variation and are potentially more flexible and susceptible to environmentally induced epigenetic changes. This flexibility, driven by the action of various demethylases, enables rapid and reversible adjustments while balancing silencing mechanisms with the maintenance of active gene transcription [18], potentially involving the roles of both VMCs and CMCs.

Methylation in the TSS region typically silences gene expression, while demethylation can increase expression; for example, demethylation in oncogene TSS regions has been shown to promote tumor development [19].

Around the TSS regions, the methylation levels of VMCs are generally moderate, and these regions tend to be located farther away and wider compared to CMCs. In contrast, CMCs are hypomethylated in expressed genes upstream of the TSS. The variability in these positions across different cows may indicate that these promoters are responsive to environmental or physiological changes, leading to differential gene expression and potentially influencing cellular functions and phenotypic diversity. This distinction between conserved and variable methylation in promoters underscores the complexity of gene regulation in blood tissue, where conserved regions ensure stable gene expression necessary for fundamental processes, while variable regions allow for adaptability and responsiveness to changing conditions. Moreover, blood can serve as a valuable surrogate tissue, providing systemic epigenetic information relevant to organismal health [4, 9].

Promoter-proximal regions are likely subjected to stronger selective pressure to maintain consistent methylation, whereas more distal regions, such as enhancer regions, may exhibit greater variability. This variability provides flexibility by influencing the formation of enhancer-promoter loops and the three-dimensional structure of chromatin [20].

Notably, hypermethylation, when associated with enhancers, makes the region less accessible, reducing enhancer-promoter proximity and directly preventing the binding of certain TFs [21–23].

The identified motifs associated with CMCs and VMCs may reveal the biological roles of TFs in gene regulation. CMC regions with stable methylation are often linked to essential processes like housekeeping genes, stress response, or cellular differentiation. TFs associated with these motifs likely play key roles in these processes.

Krüppel-like factors (KLFs) are DNA-binding TFs involved in regulating pathways controlling metabolism and other cellular processes and associated with metabolic functions at cellular, organ, and systemic levels [24]. Dysregulated KLF expression or activation has been implicated in metabolic disorders, including obesity, diabetes, and heart failure [24].

The presence of these motifs among CMCs suggests that genes regulated by KLFs are under stable epigenetic control, ensuring consistent expression in specific biological contexts like development or stress response. HOX, a highly conserved family of TFs, plays an important role in morphogenesis [25]. Homeobox genes encode a conserved family of TFs essential for embryonic development and tissue homeostasis [26]. Motifs associated with CMCs may indicate conserved gene regulation, essential for stable cellular maintenance and differentiation, likely contributing to functional stability in key biological contexts.

VMC regions, with methylation variability between individuals or conditions, may indicate that associated motifs and TFs are involved in more dynamic processes, such as environmental response, cellular plasticity, or reversible epigenetic modifications.

In contrast, VMCs associated with motifs and TFs, such as EBF1, play a role in regulating gene plasticity and environmental response, dynamically influencing gene expression according to internal or external factors. EBF1, for example, is essential for maintaining B cell identity and preventing alternative fates in committed cells [27]. In addition, NKX2-6, a homeobox gene, is implicated in cardiac phenotypes [28].

The differential methylation analysis between the control and diseased groups allowed us to gain a deeper understanding of the biological significance of variability and to address our hypothesis that variable positions could provide critical insights for the early identification of cows susceptible to disease or resilient to health challenges. Indeed, the gene SPP1, which is significant in our analysis, is commonly associated with inflammation and immune response. Its variable methylation may influence inflammatory responses, particularly in blood immune cells. High levels of secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) are linked to macrophage infiltration and poor prognosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [29]. Reduced methylation in the TSS region of SPP1 in HCC may account for its elevated expression in this cancer type [29]. HCC in cattle has been associated with symptoms such as lethargy and weight loss [30, 31].

CMSS1 also appears significant. In humans, this gene suggests a primary function in T cells related to organelle biogenesis, particularly for mitochondria and ribosomes [32]. Additionally, CMSS1 has been identified in other studies as a candidate gene linked to bacterial and viral infections in wild boar populations [33].

We observed significant enrichment in lipid metabolic pathways, along with functions related to protein binding and the immune system. Lipid metabolism is closely linked to cellular energy balance [34]. This combination suggests potential roles in cellular mechanisms and metabolic adaptations.

The high significance levels for genes like INSIG1, a lipid metabolism regulator involved with severe fatty liver in dairy cows [35], and NSD1, associated with chromatin remodeling [36], suggest that methylation changes may influence lipid biosynthesis and epigenetic modulation. Additionally, the presence of MIR2467, a microRNA gene found among the list of DMRs and the 586 biomarkers, indicates potential impacts on post-transcriptional regulation. This microRNA was associated with gestational diabetes mellitus in pregnancy [37].

Exceptionally high q-values for MAB21L1, previously reported in comparative transcriptomics to reveal muscle-specific molecular differences in early postmortem stages of Chinese Jinjiang yellow cattle [38], highlight its potential relevance, particularly in pathways related to developmental processes.

MGAT5 was also targeted by some of the 586 markers at the promoter region. This gene, specifically, was identified in a previous study on blood methylation related to mastitis [39].

Comparing chronological age with methylation patterns reveals that conserved positions decrease more rapidly than variable ones. This suggests that while aging may have a genetic predisposition, unfavorable conditions can accelerate the process. Moreover, as individuals age, their adaptability to the environment diminishes due to a decrease in plasticity.

In summary, age significantly influences DNA methylation, with a stronger direct effect observed in CMCs, as indicated by the regression coefficient. However, the overall influence of age, as reflected by the R-squared value, is slightly more pronounced in VMCs. These findings highlight the nuanced impact of age on epigenetic regulation, with both direct and broader effects observed across different methylation contexts.

Our promoter-focused analysis of genes potentially expressed in blood allowed us to distinguish the roles and locations of VMCs and CMCs. We identified gene targets mostly associated with VMCs, revealing differential methylation between control and diseased cows. This research is the first to establish a link between age and methylation variability in cattle. Additionally, identifying potential transcription factor motifs introduces a framework for exploring interactions between TFs and methylation variability. Through promoter analysis, we further identified a set of genes targeted by the most significant DMRs.

Collectively, these findings enabled us to compile a refined list of biomarkers with the potential for predicting a cow’s resilience to multiple diseases, providing a promising foundation for future applications in cattle health and selective breeding. These biomarkers could be integrated into breeding programs to guide the selection of more robust animals or used as early indicators of disease susceptibility to improve herd management and preventive care strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides a comprehensive analysis of DNA methylation variability across distinct phenotypes in cattle, offering new insights into the interplay between epigenetic regulation, immune function and phenotypic diversity. By distinguishing between conserved and variable methylation patterns, our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of how epigenetic modifications may influence health, disease susceptibility and adaptation in cattle. This research lays the groundwork for future studies exploring the role of DNA methylation in cattle health and productivity, ultimately supporting the development of more sustainable and efficient livestock management strategies.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

Ethical approval and consent

Ethical approval to conduct the study was provided by the Animal Protection and Ethics Committee of Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (approval number [A20-0196]). The study adhered to the guidelines set by the Canadian Council on Animal Care.

Animal selection and sample collection

Blood samples were collected from cows at a Vancouver farm between November 2020 and January 2021. A year later, we selected 30 cows that had been culled during the year due to complications related to infertility (BFR, n = 6), mastitis (BMS, n = 6), lameness (BFL, n = 12), or metabolic disorders (BMT, n = 6), which were not identified at the time of sampling. This culling reason was recorded in farm management logs and represented the primary cause for culling. These cows formed the “diseased group.” For comparison, we selected a second group of 30 cows that remained in the herd without any health issues. This second group represented the “control group.” The ages of the cows in both groups ranged from 1 to 9 years (Table S1).

The pairing of each subgroup with controls (6 vs. 6, 12 vs. 12, or 30 vs. 30) for the comparison analyses was performed by selecting individuals as close in age as possible, even though age was excluded as a covariate in the differential analyses. This allowed us to minimize age-related effects while focusing the analysis on disease-associated methylation differences.

Enzymatic Methyl sequencing

One mL of blood from each cow was sent to the Génome Québec sequencing platform for DNA extraction, enzymatic methyl-seq (EM-seq) library preparation [40], and whole-genome sequencing. Libraries were pooled at equimolar concentrations and sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq technology, generating approximately 450 million 150-bp paired-end reads per library.

During library preparation, there is a DNA conversion step that involves three primary enzymes: TET2 and T4-BGT detect and transform 5mC and 5hmC into a product that cannot be deaminated by the APOBEC3A enzyme. Subsequently, APOBEC3A deaminates unmodified cytosines by converting them to uracils. As a result, unmethylated cytosines are transformed into uracils and then into thymines during polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification, while methylated cytosines remain as cytosines. To verify the extent of DNA conversion of each sample, two internal controls were added before the conversion step: unmethylated lambda (Table S2) and CpG methylated pUC19. By estimating the methylation levels of lambda and pUC19, we assess the quality of DNA conversion.

Methyl calling

Reads were filtered, trimmed, and deduplicated using fastp version 0.23.1 with default parameters [41]. Read conversion, alignment to the Bos taurus reference genome (ARS-UCD1.2.104), and methyl calling were conducted using Bismark version 0.23.1 [42]. This process was implemented in the DRAGEN Methylation Pipeline, which was run on an Illumina DRAGEN server using default parameters. In brief, reads from the EM-seq library were converted into both a C-to-T and G-to-A version and then aligned to a correspondingly converted reference genome. This approach ensured that sites from both DNA strands were captured. Methyl calling was achieved by comparing the originally sequenced read with its corresponding original reference genome. Methylation calls from Bismark were filtered to retain CpG sites covered by at least six reads in a minimum of 20 individuals per group.

Clustering analysis

A descriptive analysis of methylation data, including hierarchical clustering based on Ward.D correlation and PCA of cellular methylomes, was performed. These analyses were conducted using the R package MethylKit (version 1.28.0) [43].

Markers to predict the proportion of white blood cell populations

The cows selected for this study showed no pathological signs at the time of blood collection. To validate that our blood samples harbored similar proportions of white blood cell populations, we used the markers identified by Powell et al. (2023) [16]. These markers were derived through a deconvolution CIBERSORTx approach adapted for hematopoietic DNA methylation and validated to predict blood count in the Holstein Friesian breed and other breeds. They allow the estimation of the proportions of seven immune cells: B cells, CD4 αβ T cells, CD8 αβ T cells, γδ T cells, NK, monocytes, and granulocytes from 646 blood CpG biomarkers.

Differential methylation analysis

Differential methylation analysis was conducted for individual cytosines (sites) and regions using the R package MethylKit (version 1.28.0). Methylation calls from Bismark were filtered to retain CpG sites covered by at least six reads. The regional analysis (DMR) used a 500-bp sliding window with a 250-bp overlap and a minimum of five CpGs per region.

For each CpG site (and region), a logistic regression model was fitted to calculate p-values. These p-values were then adjusted to q-values using the SLIM method [44] without making corrections for overdispersion. The methylation level of lambda in the CpG context was included as a covariate in the analysis to adjust for disparities in conversion rates between samples. Cow age at sampling and differences between the parents (defined as differing sire and/or dam between individuals) were also used as covariates to distinguish the impacts of age and parental genetics, respectively, from the effects of diseases.

Methylation differences were calculated as: mean methylation level in diseased minus mean methylation level in control. Our analysis focused on CpG sites and regions with q-values less than 0.01.

Annotation

Differentially methylated sites or regions were linked to adjacent genes or the closest genes using the annotatePeak function in the default mode of the R package ChIPseeker (version 1.38.1) [45]. The annotation considered regions spanning 3 kb upstream of the TSS, designating them as promoters. Depending on the location relative to the closest gene, each annotated site or region was categorized as “Promoter,” “5’ UTR”, “3’ UTR”, “Exonic,” “Intronic,” “Downstream,” or “Distal intergenic.” For these analyses, the reference genome used was Bos_taurus.ARS-UCD1.2 from Ensembl (version 108) with supplementary genomic annotations provided by the org.Bt.eg.db package (version 3.18.0).

Functional enrichment analysis

Gene clusters, based on their functional similarity, were examined using the DAVID tool, focusing on five resource categories: BP, CC, MF, KEGG pathways, and Reactome pathways. This tool uses the hypergeometric test algorithm. This analysis was conducted using Bos_taurus.ARS-UCD1.3. The 30 most significant categories were selected. A p-value threshold of < 0.05 and the presence of at least five genes per class were required for the analysis. Functional enrichment analysis was performed on DMCs in promoters.

Genes potentially expressed in blood

Genes were examined based on blood gene expression data from Holstein lactating cattle downloaded from the supplementary data of Gao et al. (2022) [46]. This study utilized single-cell transcriptomics of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) to profile seven major cell types, including CD4 T cells, CD8 T cells, B cells, monocytes, NK, innate lymphoid cells (ILC), and dendritic cells (DC).

Statistical analyses and visualization methods

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 4.3.1). To identify the most discriminating positions, we employed Partial Least-Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) and sparse Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA). Additionally, a clustered image map (CIM) was generated. These methods are implemented in the mixOmics package (version 6.26.0) [47]. PLS-DA is a linear classification model that can predict the class of samples [48]. The background.predict function illustrates the separation of class labels. For validation, we used parameters: validation = “Mfold,” folds = 5, nrepeat = 100, and measure = “BER” (leveraging the balanced error rate from the dist measure).

Heatmaps were created with the Enrichedheatmap package (version 1.32.0), TxDbBT9, and the Bos_taurus.ARS-UCD1.2 genome assembly (version 108). The graphical abstract was created by Biorender.com.

Motif predictions

Consensus sequence identification and motif prediction were performed using MEME Suite 5.57, incorporating the following tools: MEME [49], Tomtom [50], and XSTREME [51].
