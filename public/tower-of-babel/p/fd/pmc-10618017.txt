Effect of forearm positions on scapular movements and trunk rotation angles
Authors: Yuuki Homma, Takeru Kato, Akito Chiba, Sachi Takada, Chiho Honda
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
[Purpose] The study investigated the impact of distinct forearm positions on scapular movement and trunk rotation angles. [Participants and Methods] We enrolled 23 healthy young males. Measurements of trunk rotation and relative scapular segment angles were acquired under three conditions employing three-dimensional motion analysis, each executed in a seated posture: (1) both forearms in a neutral position, (2) the forearm on the rotational side adopted supination while the opposite forearm utilized pronation, and (3) the forearm on the rotational side employed pronation while the opposite forearm assumed supination. [Results] The scapular angles in all three scenarios reflected downward rotation, posterior tilt, and external rotation of the rotational-side scapula. Conversely, the contralateral scapula exhibited upward rotation, anterior tilt, and internal rotation. Although the scapular and trunk rotation angles corresponded with the three forearm positions, all three were significantly different and were significantly increased when the rotational-side forearm was in supination (opposite side pronation) and decreased when the rotational-side forearm was in pronation (opposite side supination). [Conclusion] During trunk rotation, regular scapular movements were observed, which varied depending on forearm position and influenced the range of trunk rotation.

INTRODUCTION

Trunk rotation is an essential trunk movement not only in sports that feature rotation, such as golf, tennis, and baseball, but also in walking and daily activities. The need for a detailed assessment of trunk rotation is widely recognized, and many previous studies have addressed this topic.

Trunk rotation involves multiple elements and their interactions with adjacent segments. First, the range of trunk rotation is approximately four times greater in the thoracic spine than in the lumbar spine1), with approximately 80% of the rotation occurring in the thoracic region2). The thoracic vertebrae form joints with the ribs, allowing the ribs to move asymmetrically in the horizontal plane due to rotation of the thoracic vertebrae, enabling rotational movement (The ribs on the side of rotation rotate posteriorly, while the ribs on the opposite side rotate anteriorly)3). In the related segments, the scapula is located behind the thorax extending from the levels of the second to seventh thoracic vertebrae4), and the position and movement of the scapula and thorax influence each other because the scapula and thorax form a functional joint called the scapulothoracic joint5). Previous studies have reported that the lumbar spine, pelvic limb position, and posture influence rotation6,7,8).

In clinical practice, there are cases where limitations in trunk mobility can stem from the forearm or scapula, and interventions targeting these areas often result in improvements. It may be easy to assume that the forearm and trunk are unrelated because they are positionally separated segments. However, this clinical experience demonstrates the interplay between these apparently unrelated segments and the trunk, and elucidating these relationships is expected to contribute to the improvement of exercise therapy and sports performance.

Therefore, in this study, we focused on the upper limb girdle and hypothesized that scapular movement is associated with rib rotation during trunk rotation, and this movement is influenced by forearm position. This study aimed to measure the trunk rotation angle and the corresponding scapular angle and investigate the influence of forearm position on these parameters.

PARTICIPANTS AND METHODS

The participants in this study were 23 healthy males with no history of upper limb or spinal disorders (age: 24.2 ± 4.2 years, height: 171.4 ± 6.4 cm, weight: 62.9 ± 7.8 kg, body mass index: 21.4 ± 2.0 kg/m2; mean ± standard deviation). Before the study, the purpose and significance of the research was explained to the participants, and they provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Bunkyo Gakuin University, Japan (approval no. 2021-0002) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 1964 and its subsequent revisions.

Rotational trunk movements, with the head and thorax considered a single unit, were measured. The participants were seated on a chair in an upright position to maintain trunk alignment. Measurements were taken under three conditions: (1) both forearms in a neutral position, (2) forearm on the rotational side in supination and that on the opposite side in pronation, and (3) forearm on the rotational side in pronation and that on the opposite side in supination (Fig. 1Fig. 1. These three scenarios were performed with the upper limb at 90° flexion at the elbow joint with the upper limbs positioned at the sides of the body. Participants were instructed to avoid compensatory movements of the shoulder or elbow joints associated with forearm pronation/supination. After performing trunk rotation under the first condition with both forearms in a neutral position, the order of the remaining two conditions was randomized for each participant.

A three-dimensional motion analysis system employing ten infrared cameras (Vicon Nexus 2; Vicon Motion Systems, Ltd., Oxford, UK) was used. The sampling frequency was set to 100 Hz. A total of 22 reflective markers were placed on each participant’s body surface, and three-dimensional coordinate data were obtained. Local coordinate systems were defined for the thorax, pelvis, and the left and right scapulae. The thoracic segment was represented by four points: the sternal notch, xiphoid process, and spinous processes of the first and tenth thoracic vertebrae. The pelvic segment was also defined by four points, namely the left and right anterior and posterior superior iliac spines. The trunk rotation angle was calculated as the angle between the thoracic and pelvic segments, with positive values indicating rotation in the designated direction.

The scapular segment was defined using a cluster of three markers attached to the acromion process, in accordance with previous studies9,10,11,12). The angles of the scapular segment relative to the thoracic segment were calculated as the upper and lower rotation angles of the scapula (positive values for upper rotation), anterior and posterior tilt angles (positive values for the anterior tilt direction), and internal and external rotation angles (positive values for the internal rotation direction).

All measurements were conducted in the resting end-expiratory position to exclude the influence of chest movement during breathing. The participants were instructed to maintain a resting end-expiratory position for 3 seconds. Subsequently, they slowly rotated to their maximum range over 3 seconds, ensuring minimal compensatory pelvic and lower limb movements in the pelvis and lower limbs. Subsequently, a 3-second static period was maintained at the final position of maximum rotation. We extracted stable 1-second data segments during the 3-second static periods before and after trunk rotation. The magnitude of change for each parameter was calculated as the average value from three trials, which served as the representative values for each of the 23 participants. A total of 46 data points (combining the rotational and non-rotational sides after applying polarity inversion) were analyzed.

The normality of each parameter was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For group comparisons in which the data from the three conditions were not normally distributed, the Friedman test was used with Bonferroni-adjusted p-values. Statistical significance was defined as p<0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

In all three scenarios, the scapula on the rotational side displayed downward rotation, posterior tilt, and external rotation, whereas that on the opposite side showed upward rotation, anterior tilt, and internal rotation (Table 1).

The scapula on the rotational side rotated downward by −4.8 ± 3.6° when both forearms were in a neutral position, −6.1 ± 4.1° when the ipsilateral forearm was in supination, and −3.8 ± 3.5° when it was in pronation. The scapula on the opposite side rotated upward by 7.0 ± 5.8° when both forearms were in a neutral position, 8.8 ± 7.6° when the contralateral forearm was in supination, and 5.7 ± 4.5° when it was in pronation. Significant differences were observed between all three conditions (Table 1).

Furthermore, the rotational-side scapula exhibited a posterior tilt of −11.0 ± 2.7° with both forearms in a neutral position, −12.6 ± 3.1° when the ipsilateral forearm was in supination, and −10.0 ± 2.7° when it was in pronation. The opposite-side scapula displayed an anterior tilt of 9.6 ± 3.3° when both forearms were in a neutral position, 11.3 ± 3.8° when the contralateral forearm was in supination, and 9.0 ± 3.1° when it was in pronation. All three conditions produced significantly different results (Table 1).

Finally, the rotational-side scapula rotated externally by −12.4 ± 3.6° when both forearms were in a neutral position, −14.2 ± 4.4° when the rotational-side forearm was in supination, and −11.5 ± 3.4° when it was in pronation. The opposite-side scapula rotated internally by 12.4 ± 3.7° when both forearms were in a neutral position, 14.0 ± 4.5° when the rotational-side forearm was in supination, and 11.5 ± 4.0° when it was in pronation. Again, we observed significant differences between all three conditions (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

We investigated the influence of forearm position on trunk rotation angles and scapular movement. Changes in forearm position during trunk rotation systematically and regularly altered scapular and trunk rotation angles.

In this study, scapular movement demonstrated consistent patterns under all three conditions. On the rotational side, the scapula exhibited downward rotation, posterior tilt, and external rotation, whereas that on the opposite side it displayed upward rotation, anterior tilt, and internal rotation. The ribs are known to be affected during trunk rotation, with the rotational-side ribs undergoing posterior rotation and the opposite-side ribs undergoing anterior rotation3). The shape of the thoracic cage controls the movement of the scapula5). Because of the close and conforming articulation of the scapula with the thoracic cage at the scapulothoracic joint, it glides smoothly along the surface of the thoracic cage during movement. Therefore, the scapula undergoes downward rotation, posterior tilt, and external rotation associated with the posterior rotation of the ribs on the rotational side. In contrast, the scapula exhibits upward rotation, anterior tilt, and internal rotation associated with the anterior rotation of the contralateral ribs.

Furthermore, scapular movement on the rotational side, such as downward rotation, posterior tilt, and external rotation, increased when the rotational-side forearm was in supination and the opposite side was in pronation and decreased when the rotational-side forearm was in pronation with the opposite side in supination. The same was true for the rotation angle of the trunk. The contralateral scapula showed opposite results. These observations suggest that the direction of scapular motion changes with forearm position, supporting our hypothesis. The coordinated movement of the scapula and humerus, the so-called scapulohumeral rhythm, is widely known13). The Empty-Can test, which involves internal rotation of the shoulder, includes external rotation, retraction, and anterior tilt of the scapula, while the Full-Can test, which involves external rotation of the shoulder, involves movement of the scapula in the direction of internal rotation, anterior extension, and posterior tilt14). Unlike the limb positions in the previous study, the present measurements were performed with the upper limb at 90° flexion at the elbow joint with the upper limbs positioned at the sides of the body. Therefore, it was difficult to observe the kinetic chain involved with the direct internal and external rotation of the humerus due to the supination and pronation of the forearm. However, similar coordinated scapular movements were observed during the internal and external rotation of the scapula and during the anterior and posterior tilts. These results suggested the influence of the bicipital muscles. It is known that the biceps brachii muscles attach to the forearm and scapula and are associated with elbow joint flexion and forearm supination actions. Therefore, it is possible that the scapular movement was affected by the biceps brachii muscles. On the other hand, since the pronator muscles are not directly attached to the scapula, the role of the pronator muscles in the mechanism of the kinetic chain of the upper limb needs to be further studied.

This study had some limitations. The mechanism of linkage of the forearm to the upper arm and scapula was outside the scope of this study; however, from the regulation of the method, it was considered to be due to the influence of the biceps brachii muscle. Whether the other muscles attached to the scapula are also involved, remains to be ascertained. Furthermore, it is necessary to examine the provisions of the shoulder and elbow joints to suit various sports and daily life activities, and to verify whether similar results can be obtained with rotation exercises involving the hip joint in the standing position by changing the limb position. In addition, since scapulo-thoracic orientation is affected by age15), it is necessary to verify whether similar results can be obtained in elderly patients with kyphosis or other postural problems.

In the present study, regular scapular movements were observed during trunk rotation. These movements varied according to the forearm position and influenced the range of trunk rotation. Therefore, we believe that in physical therapy evaluation and intervention for trunk rotation movements, it may be helpful to consider not only the local problems but also the interlocking of the shoulder girdle and upper limbs as segments related to rotation.
