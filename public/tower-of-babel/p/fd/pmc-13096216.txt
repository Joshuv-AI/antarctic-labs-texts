Investigating the impact of carbamazepine on tomato plant metabolism using genome-scale metabolic modelling
Authors: Samyuktha Srinivasan, Karthik Raman, Smita Srivastava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA comprehensive mechanistic analysis of emerging pharmaceutical pollutants’ stress response in plants is crucial to understand their chronic impact on food-chain contamination and agricultural productivity. To unravel this at the systems level, stress response of carbamazepine (CBZ), an emerging recalcitrant anticonvulsant pollutant, was simulated in tomato. For this, an updated genome-scale metabolic model of tomato leaf, CBZ_iSL3433, augmented with CBZ detoxification reactions based on green-liver concept was developed. Under phototrophic conditions, CBZ_iSL3433 predicted energy and co-factor competition-induced biomass reduction under CBZ stress. Further, the study provides an in silico mechanistic insight for abiotic stress response induced by CBZ in tomato with altered flux states in nutrient assimilation, synthesis of key precursors of leaf biomass, and secondary metabolites. Additionally, potential ameliorative effects of biostimulants such as proline, spermine, glycerol, and ethanol were investigated through model predictions. Through systematic computational analysis, 154 significantly altered reactions were identified in the presence of CBZ stress, of which 92% were ameliorated with biostimulants. Notably, based on simulations, amino acid biosynthesis was the most significantly altered pathway under CBZ stress. Overall, the proposed framework can aid in screening and developing rational strategies to maintain agricultural yields amid rising plant stress due to such anthropogenic pollutants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40259-2.Keywords: Green-liver, Biostimulants, Plant stress, Constraint-based modelling, Chemical pollution, Water pollutionSubject terms: Biotechnology, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Pharmaceuticals have emerged as pseudo-persistent contaminants due to their low removal efficiency in conventional wastewater treatment1,2. With ~ 70% of freshwater required for agriculture and to address the global water stress with a projected 53% increased water demand by 2050, efforts are increasing to reuse wastewater for agriculture3. However, this exposes agricultural soil and crops to pharmaceuticals, leading to their bioaccumulation in the food chain. Pharmaceuticals, being biologically active, pose the risk of high ecological imbalance and are also known to aggravate antimicrobial resistance4. This impact can be compounded by the projected increased usage of reclaimed water worldwide and the global rise in pharmaceutical production and consumption5. Therefore, understanding the impact of pharmaceuticals in reclaimed water used in agriculture can directly or indirectly address Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), mainly SDG 6 (improved wastewater management), and SDG 15 (sustainable agricultural practices development).

Pharmaceuticals have been recognized for their disruptive effects on plant homeostasis1. The extent of impact on plants depends on the soil properties, temporal variations, and physio-chemical properties of the pharmaceutical(s) and their synergistic and antagonistic effects when present as mixtures. Moreover, comprehensive mechanistic insights into both pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics in plants are limited. Also, relying only on whole-plant studies for investigating pharmaceutical stress response considering these variations, while informative, is arduous and time-consuming. Thus, there is a need for a rational framework to flag pharmaceuticals that may present a greater risk of affecting major plant functions in terms of metabolic perturbations6. Such an approach will enable a comprehensive scrutiny of the critical metabolic changes, facilitating focused experimental investigations. Thus, this will help in reducing trial and error approach, thereby minimizing time, cost, and resource utilization.

For this, we propose a framework for exploiting the predictive power of GEMs (GEnome-scale Metabolic models) approach. The GEM of an organism is an in silico stoichiometric model curated to capture its metabolic inventory from available knowledge such as genome annotation mapped to gene-associated reactions and metabolites. Though a eukaryotic plant GEM is complex due to multi-tissue and multi-organ metabolism, they have proven to be capable of robust predictions of metabolic states observed during abiotic stress response7. Further, GEM has been used for understanding drug-induced perturbations on liver toxicity8and for investigating the effects of diet on drug metabolism in humans9. However, xenobiotic stress response in plants has not yet been explored using GEM.

To fill this gap, we propose to adapt ‘green-liver’ concept10, recognizing that plant xenobiotic metabolism closely resembles that of animal liver metabolism. Pharmaceuticals are among the class of chemicals with rich data in terms of toxicology in mammalian systems11. The sequence of xenobiotic metabolism has been reported to be homologous in terms of cDNA sequence, enzyme classes, and metabolite patterns between plant and animal liver systems, with avenues of applications for pharmacovigilance in plants1,6,11,12.

In the present study, emerging recalcitrant pollutant carbamazepine (CBZ) has been selected as a representative pharmaceutical for investigation due to its ubiquitous presence, teratogenic13 and antimicrobial resistance-inducing4 nature. CBZ is known to be easily taken up by various plants, and has been reported to trigger a typical abiotic stress response14. However, a comprehensive systems-level understanding of CBZ-induced metabolic alterations is not yet explored in plants. With the available information on CBZ transformation15 along with the existence of GEM16, a rational approach for evaluating CBZ-specific stress response was investigated in an agronomically important crop, tomato (Solanum lycopersicum).

Moreover, addition of phytoprotectant metabolites as biostimulants has been considered as non-invasive effective way to improve abiotic stress tolerance17 but their effect on pharmaceutical stress remains unexplored. Thus, the current study was undertaken to capture pharmacodynamics of CBZ in tomato using genome-scale metabolic modelling and establish it as a predictive tool for rapid screening of potential nutritional supplements that can serve as biostimulators of CBZ stress tolerance.

Methods

Genome-scale metabolic model of tomato leaf: curation and modification of tomato GEM

The GEM module16 of the multi-organ metabolic model of Virtual Young TOmato Plant (VYTOP) was adapted to simulate the CBZ stress response. Among the vegetative tissues, having the photosynthetic machinery, the leaf is one of the most metabolically active parts with respect to pharmaceutical stress tolerance. With a log Kow of 2.4518, signifying its neutral state, CBZ-specific transformation reactions have been reported to occur extensively in leaf15,19. Also, Lu et al.20reported that leaf edges are primary sites for CBZ transformation with elevated oxidative stress response in tomato plants exposed to CBZ. Based on this rationale, among the different tissues, the GEM module was converted as a leaf module for pharmacodynamic evaluation. Improvements to the base model connectivity and the input constraints to the model are detailed in Supplementary Text section S1.1. The details of the reactions added and improved model in SBML file format have been provided in supplementary as ‘Supplementary_1.xlsx’ and ‘CBZ_iSL3433.xml’ respectively.

Curation of CBZ pharmacokinetics module

The overview of the CBZ pharmacokinetic (CBZ_PK) module developed in the current study is depicted in Fig. 1. According to Riemenschneider et al.15, in tomato plant, as a part of phase-I metabolism, CBZ has been reported to be transformed via epoxidation to carbamazepine-10,11-epoxide (CBZE) and hydroxylated at different positions to 2-Hydroxycarbamazepine (2-OH-CBZ), 3-Hydroxycarbamazepine (3-OH-CBZ) and 4-Hydroxycarbamazepine (4-OH-CBZ) majorly via cytochrome-P450 mediated transformation. Further, via epoxide hydrolase, CBZE is reported to be converted predominantly to 10,11-Dihydroxycarbamazepine (DiOH-CBZ), which has been known to be converted to oxcarbazepine. Next, with phase-II metabolism, most of the phase-I metabolites (CBZE, 2-OH-CBZ, 3-OH-CBZ, 4-OH-CBZ) have been observed to undergo O-glycosyltransferase mediated conjugation leading to the formation of glucose conjugated CBZ. Also, glutathione-S-transferase (GST) mediated conjugation of glutathione to CBZE has been reported as a part of phase-II metabolism. Further, CBZE conjugates of glutathione, are reported to be transported to vacuole via ATP-dependent pumps, where they have been reported to be degraded to cysteine conjugates by glutamyl transpeptidase as a part of phase-III metabolism21. The information on the representation of CBZ transformation reactions is detailed in Supplementary Text section S1.2, and the details for this module are provided in the supplementary Excel file ‘Supplementary_2.xlsx’.

A schematic representation of the CBZ_PK module. Figure illustrates a schematic representation of the various key steps included for CBZ detoxification in the model.

To evaluate the balanced model with the CBZ_PK module, a MEMOTE22 report was generated and compared to the base model version. Further, the updated model’s reproducibility was evaluated with FROG test suite utilizing the ‘FBC Curation MATLAB Toolbox’23. Phototrophic, stress, and mutant conditions were simulated and verified as per the prior version of tomato model16. In addition, sanity checks24 were implemented to test for the quality of the addition of CBZ_PK module to the updated tomato leaf model.

Simulation for predictions of CBZ pharmacodynamics

Simulation of the effect of CBZ stress on specific growth rate

The effect of CBZ was simulated with a pharmacokinetic module of CBZ along with an exchange reaction for CBZ input and corresponding exchange fluxes simulating the output of CBZ-transformed metabolites. CBZ uptake was enforced by fixing both the upper and lower bounds of the CBZ exchange reaction to study the effect of CBZ stress. The simulations were done under phototrophic mode to represent a physiologically relevant stress condition as per Table 1, with further details provided in Supplementary Text section S1.1. Due to the non-availability of information on non-growth associated maintenance (NGAM) and change in biomass precursors under the presence of CBZ, similar values as mentioned in Table 1 for NGAM and the same leaf biomass reaction were used for simulating CBZ stress. With increasing CBZ uptake rate in the range of 0 to 1000 mmol gDW− 1 h− 1 under phototrophic conditions, as mentioned in Table 1, the specific growth rate was evaluated using flux balance analysis (FBA) (l1-norm) with maximizing biomass reaction as the objective function. Simulation for identification of altered metabolism with CBZ stress

Flux variability analysis (FVA) was performed to determine the optimal flux solution space for each reaction with maximizing biomass reaction as the objective function. Based on FVA, parameters such as Jaccard similarity index have been reported to be useful metrics for identifying differentially altered reactions25. In this study, to find differentially altered reactions with CBZ stress, FVA was estimated under two conditions: Control (Uptake flux of CBZ of zero) and CBZ_IC50 (Uptake flux of CBZ inducing 50% reduction in specific growth rate compared to control conditions). Further, Similarity Index (SI) was estimated based on Jaccard index as per Eq. 1 to represent a quantitative measure for similarity between two model conditions. With the minimum and maximum flux ranges obtained for all reactions from FVA for both conditions, SI was computed using COBRA Toolbox function ‘fvaJaccardIndex’. Reactions with zero SI implying no overlap in flux solution spaces between two conditions was considered to represent significant metabolic alterations.

where, A : (minA, maxA) flux range under Control condition, B : (minB, maxB) flux range under CBZ_IC50 condition, Intersection of flux ranges: min(maxA, maxB) − max(minA, minB), Union of flux ranges: max(maxA, maxB) − min(minA, minB).

The reactions were then clustered into pathways on the basis of their metabolic functionalities to investigate the alterations with CBZ stress over the network. Further, for pathways known to be altered under pharmaceutical stress in plants, additionally the percentage difference in flux ranges were calculated as per Eqs. 2 and 3 and the reactions were categorized as per Supplementary Fig. S2. The details of the reactions and the pathways investigated in this study are provided in supplementary excel file ‘Supplementary_1.xlsx’.

All simulations were performed in MATLAB R2021b (MathWorks Inc., USA) using the COBRA Toolbox v3.0 and Gurobi solver v9.5 as the linear programming solver.

Investigating the impact of biostimulants for improving the CBZ stress tolerance in plants

Simulating the effect of biostimulant on specific growth rate in presence of CBZ stress

Novel sustainable agricultural practices like the application of exogenous biostimulants have been reported to be a promising lead to tackle abiotic stress. Biostimulants include products of organic/inorganic origin and/or microbes known to stimulate plant nutrition process independent of their nutrient content26,27. In terms of pharmaceutical stress tolerance, the role of biostimulants has not yet been explored.

For this, the ameliorative effect on growth rate of leaf biomass in the presence of CBZ was evaluated by mimicking the exogenous addition of the following four common biostimulants under each category of: an amino acid – proline, a polyamine – spermine, an alcohol – ethanol and a polyol – glycerol. To mimic the exogenous foliar addition, an exchange reaction for the corresponding biostimulant was added and its effect was compared in the presence and absence of CBZ stress. With increasing uptake rates of biostimulants, FBA (l1-norm) with maximizing biomass reaction as objective function was estimated under Control and CBZ_IC50 conditions.

Simulation for the ameliorative effect of biostimulants in the presence of CBZ stress

Similar to Sect. "Simulation for identification of altered metabolism with CBZ stress", FVA and SI were estimated to identify significantly altered reactions with biostimulants. As per Supplementary Fig. S2, the altered reactions were categorized for two conditions, CBZ_IC50_Biostimulant and CBZ_IC50 representing effect of the presence and absence of biostimulant under CBZ stress respectively. Further, the effect of biostimulants was evaluated on the production capability of individual biomass components and transformed metabolites of CBZ. The details of the analysis are provided in Supplementary Text section S1.4.

Results and discussion

CBZ Pharmacokinetic module based on green-liver hypothesis

The updated tomato leaf model with CBZ_PK module (CBZ_iSL3433) consists of 2145 metabolites and 2328 reactions (Fig. 2). Phase-I reactions in the CBZ_PK module were based on homologous pharmaceutical metabolism between plants and animal liver as per the green-liver concept. CBZ in animal liver systems is known to be detoxified majorly via Cytochrome-P450 mediated phase-I transformation, UDP-Glucuronosyltransferase mediated phase-II transformation, and finally, the converted, relatively more hydrophilic version of the detoxified transformation product is excreted from the system28,29. Also, potential variations specific to plant systems were considered for adding phase-II and phase-III metabolism. It has been reported that plant UGTs conjugate a variety of molecules majorly with UDP-Glucose rather than UDP-Glucuronate as substrate30. Further, for phase-III, glutathione conjugated phase-II metabolites were transported to vacuole as observed in plant systems12.

Overview of steps in the development of updated tomato leaf GEM integrated with CBZ_PK module.

In support of the green-liver concept, upregulation of Cytochrome-P450, epoxide hydrolases and O-glucosyltransferase genes has been reported in tomato leaves exposed to CBZ20. In lettuce roots, increased expression of cytochrome P450 81Q32 and cytochrome P450 82C4 proteins have been observed with CBZ31. Also, in Arabidopsis thaliana roots exposed to emerging contaminants, ibuprofen32, naproxen and praziquantel33, several transcripts of cytochrome-P450, UDP-glycosyltransferases and GST were reported to be upregulated. Further, based on docking studies for mycotoxin aflatoxin B1, it has been reported that Zea mays cytochrome-P450 (CYP450 81D11) mirrored its binding to that of human cytochrome-P450 (CYP450 1A2) with sequence identity of 30.65%34. Interestingly, the transformation of Medicago sativa plants with the human cytochrome-P450 (CYP2E1 gene) and GST gene improved phytoremediation of mercury-trichloroethylene contamination35.

Based on the MEMOTE report, CBZ_iSL3433 had an overall score of 51% with improved consistency in comparison to base model. Notably, charge balance was improved from 55.4% to 97.1% and mass balance was improved from 81.8% to 95.5%. The results for the evaluation of consistency of the updated model have been provided in Supplementary Text section S2.1 and Supplementary Table S1. The reproducibility was verified for models of CBZ_IC50 and Control, with their respective FROG assessment reports provided in the supplementary. Further, as detailed in Supplementary Text section S2.2, with only the leaf module, the prediction of updated tomato leaf GEM in determining the trend in biomass decrease in the presence of nitrogen limitation was in alignment to VYTOP prediction following a similar pattern as reported experimentally by Groot et al.36. This confirms the updated model robustness in predicting the trend in specific growth rate under stress conditions.

Energy and Co-factor competition induced biomass reduction upon enforced CBZ uptake

In this study, CBZ uptake was enforced by fixing the upper and lower bounds of its exchange reaction to understand how plant metabolism is altered in response to its detoxification, as it is now its a prerequisite for survival. The maximum possible specific growth rate was estimated for increasing CBZ uptake rates with constraints as per Table 1. As observed in Fig. 3, the specific growth rate was found to be significantly decreased with increasing CBZ uptake rates.

Effect of increasing theoretical enforced uptake rates of CBZ on specific growth rate. (A) Straight line representing ‘CBZ_0.01’ with maximum biomass constrained value of 0.01 h− 1. (B) Dotted line representing ‘CBZ_1’ with maximum biomass constrained value of 1 h− 1.

This observation was in accordance with literature wherein CBZ has been reported to hinder the growth of several plant species such as Cucurbita pepo37, Typha latifolia18, lettuce38, depending on dose and exposure time. Furthermore, a concentration of 1000 ppb of CBZ-containing psychoactive pharmaceutical cocktail was found to be highly detrimental to tomato plants, reportedly resulting in plant collapse14. Further, the simulation was repeated with biomass reaction upper bound value of 1 h− 1 to evaluate the effect of constrained biomass value in model prediction of the impact of CBZ. As observed from Fig. 3, a decrease in specific growth rate in the presence of increasing CBZ uptake rates was estimated to be independent of the initial constrained upper bound value of biomass except with a difference in the rate of decrease between the two conditions. CBZ uptake rates used currently are theoretical constraints and may not reflect absolute, environmentally realistic uptake constraints. Although the framework captures the trend of decreasing specific growth rate with increased CBZ uptake rates, discrepancies in absolute values are due to limitations in the absence of cellular regulation.

Plants, being sessile, have evolved a wide range of tolerance mechanisms to endure the forced environmental fluctuations. Unlike microbes, plants majorly employ detoxification mechanisms to avoid the potential build-up of toxicity due to xenobiotics like CBZ for their elimination. CBZ transformation is facilitated via utilization of metabolites like reduced form of Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH), Oxygen (O2), proton (H+), water (H2O), Uridine diphosphate glucose (UDP-Glucose), Pumped-proton (energetic proton representing ATP synthesis driven metabolic transfer across compartments) and Glutathione. Thus, tomato metabolism requires an additional expenditure of these metabolites in the presence of CBZ, thereby posing a metabolic burden to the cell, disrupting its homeostasis and inducing stress. This was also observed in drug-induced toxicity in the animal liver system, wherein the induced demand of drug detoxification has been observed to perturb cellular homeostasis8.

CBZ uptake is predicted to elicit abiotic stress response in plants

Plants have evolved perfect metabolic plasticity to sustain challenging conditions with robust solutions for a sessile lifestyle. To orchestrate this dynamic response in nature, plants have evolved abundant linear and non-linear feedforward/feedback regulations to maintain metabolic steady state39,40. This forms the core basis of the mathematical framework of constraint-based modelling, which employs linear optimization by maximizing/minimizing an objective function for the determination of steady-state flux distribution of reactions in a metabolic network41–43. In CBZ_iSL3433, the presence of CBZ was captured by enforced higher CBZ uptake rates. A biologically relevant standard measure of 50% reduction in the specific growth rate of 0.005 h− 1 from experimental values under control conditions of 0.01 h− 1 was chosen as the benchmark reference point to mimic CBZ stress. The corresponding theoretical CBZ flux rate (249.6465 mmol gDW− 1 h− 1) inducing this growth rate was used for further simulations. This uptake rate is used as a theoretical constraint to capture the trend in decreasing specific growth rate without regulatory constraints, and it does not represent absolute environmental exposure levels.

Assessing biomass is a widely used method for measuring stress response, as reduced growth represents the overall cumulative responses within the plant. To investigate the mechanistic insights on the predicted decrease in specific growth rate with increasing CBZ uptake rates, FVA and SI based framework was employed for identifying significantly altered metabolism. Approximately 31.3% (728/2328) reactions had their SI in the range of 0 to 0.1 representing enhanced metabolic reprogramming in the presence of CBZ (Supplementary Fig. S4). Among these, 154 metabolic reactions were identified as significantly altered reactions with zero SI, indicating non-overlapping flux spaces between Control vs. CBZ_IC50 conditions.

Further, the predicted list of altered reactions was verified to ensure that the predictions were independent of objective function choice and chosen CBZ uptake flux rate (Fig. 4a & b; Supplementary Text section S3).

Verification of CBZ pharmacodynamics: in Silico predictions vs. literature data

The pathway classes of reactions representing significant alterations with CBZ are represented in Fig. 4c. The flux ranges for reactions of major pathway classes known to be altered under pharmaceutical stress are shown in Supplementary Fig. S6. Though directly are not a part of biomass components, the alterations predicted in the core pathways of pentose phosphate pathway, folate biosynthesis and transformation, photorespiration and Calvin cycle highlight the indirect control of these pathways in biomass synthesis.

Interestingly, majority of pathway classes altered with CBZ, represented in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. S6, correlated well with literature on CBZ exposure in plants14,20,31,44. Based on untargeted metabolomics in Phragmites australis plants, alterations in carbon fixation, pentose phosphate pathway, fatty acid biosynthesis, folate biosynthesis, purine and pyrimidine metabolism and amino acid metabolism (arginine, proline, tyrosine, β-Alanine, and tryptophan) has been reported in the presence of CBZ44. Further, in lettuce plants exposed to CBZ, alterations in oxidative stress response, cellular respiration, cell wall metabolism and phenylpropanoid metabolism have been observed31.

Specifically in tomato plants, based on an integrated transcriptomics and metabolomics study, exposure to CBZ has been reported to induce spatial specific alterations in leaf edges20. This has been reported to alter antioxidant enzyme expression, photosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism. Based on model predictions, alterations in glutathione metabolism (for instance reaction of ID ‘GTHOr’ representing Glutathione Reductase enzyme of EC number: 1.8.1.7), starch and sucrose metabolism (for instance reaction of ID ‘STARCH300S2’ representing Starch synthase enzyme of EC number: 2.4.1.21) were observed with SI for reactions in the pathway classes falling in the range of 0 to 1, demonstrating good correlation with experimental observations (Supplementary Table S3). Also, altered synthesis of carotenoids, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenes, and nucleotides have been experimentally observed20. Despite the model not representing the complete pathway of secondary metabolism, the model indicated alterations in key precursors of secondary metabolites. Chorismate biosynthesis was predicted to be altered, which is a key precursor for flavonoids. Additionally, amino acid metabolism was predicted to be altered, which is a key precursor for alkaloids. Also, mevalonate (MVA)/methylerythritol phosphate (MEP) pathway-mediated terpene biosynthesis was predicted to be altered, which is the key precursor for carotenoids. Further, Lu et al.20, verified the trend in the alteration of photosynthesis and antioxidant expression with quantitative polymerase chain reaction. The comparison between CBZ_iSL3433 predictions and experimental observations of transcript levels of these two pathways is represented in Supplementary Table S2. CBZ_iSL3433 was able to capture the reduced photosynthesis but was not able to capture the increased antioxidant levels.

Plant stress responses are species-specific and influenced by stressor dosage, timing, and environmental factors. Omics-integrated GEM has been successfully used for investigating stress response in plants7. In lettuce, based on molecular functions of differentially expressed proteins in leaves, CBZ induced 38.5% of binding, 42.3% of catalytic, 7.7% of molecular function regulator activity and 11.5% of structural molecular activity31. For nitrogen stress prediction in the maize root model, Chowdhury et al.45reported that, transcriptomics integration to GEM improved the robustness of model prediction with true prediction of metabolite fold change improved from 18% to 70% and false prediction rate reduced from 82% to 30%. Thus, this explains the discrepancy in model prediction for antioxidant expression, which can be improved by integrating regulatory constraints in the model.

Further alterations in chlorophyll biosynthesis, ascorbate biosynthesis, photorespiration and nutrient assimilation of phototrophic input were predicted with CBZ presence. Chronic exposure of pharmaceuticals is known to have the potential to induce alterations in ammonium and nitrite assimilation31. In Cucurbita pepo plants, reduction in chlorophyll pigments and altered nutrient composition, including phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, manganese, and total nitrogen of leaves has been reported in the presence of CBZ37,46. In lettuce plants exposed to pharmaceuticals, alterations in sulphur assimilation have been observed31. Ascorbate biosynthesis and photorespiration being key-players of abiotic stress response and tolerance, are known to aid in redox homeostasis47,48. Alterations predicted by the model for these pathways are novel predictions under CBZ stress response in plants which need further validation. Thus, this highlights the potential of network-level approach to understand the metabolic perturbations. Based on transcriptomics analysis, glycerophospholipid, alpha-linolenic acid, and linoleic metabolism were reported to be altered in tomato plants exposed to CBZ20. Though model predicted altered lipid metabolism, these specific pathways are gaps in the model highlighting the future scope for improvement in the model.

In Silico proof for reduced toxicity of CBZ in presence of biostimulants

Under challenging conditions of (a)biotic stress, plants have developed on-demand synthesis of array of phytochemicals to protect cells from injury induced from stress39. Exogenous application of these metabolites has been reported as an economical approach to alleviate abiotic stress response. Furthermore, GEM has been successfully used for screening nutrient supplements to improve the bioremediation of herbicides in microbial systems49,50. However, its potential use in plant systems for prediction of pharmaceutical stress mitigation strategies is limited.

In this study, the effect of four biostimulants, including proline, spermine, ethanol, and glycerol, was investigated with respect to CBZ stress. In the presence of CBZ, all the biostimulants under investigation demonstrated increased specific growth rate as observed in Fig. 5a and were predicted as non-toxic under control conditions (Supplementary Fig. S7). Notably, when the biostimulant flux rate was set beyond 0.07 mmol gDW− 1 h− 1 with any of the biostimulants, the effect of CBZ was found to be negligible. Hence, this flux rate was chosen to compare the effects among the biostimulants for ameliorating CBZ stress. These uptake rates used currently are theoretical constraints and do not reflect absolute, environmentally realistic uptake rates due to absence of regulatory constraints in the model.

The uptake rates mentioned are theoretical and do not reflect absolute values. This was in accordance with literature wherein biostimulants have been reported to improve plant growth under stress. Proline, an osmolyte and energy supplier, is known to maintain the NADPH: NADP+ ratio. In tomato plants exposed to salinity stress exogenous application of proline has been known to improve growth51. Spermine, a tetramine, is known for prophylactic effects under stress conditions. In tomato plants, exogenous spermine has been known to improve plant growth under combined salinity and paraquat tolerance52. Ethanol has been reported to maintain cell energy status under stress conditions. In tomato plants exposed to heat stress, exogenous ethanol aided in alleviating heat damage by supporting reproductive development and vegetative growth53. Glycerol, sugar alcohol with pronounced osmolytic function has been known to improve plant growth under stress. For instance, improved growth was demonstrated under salt stress in Ricinus communis L.54, maize55 and pistachio56 with exogenous application of glycerol.

Further, as observed from Fig. 5b, the presence of biostimulants aided in significant enhancement of individual biomass components. These predictions align with literature wherein exogenous proline has been reported to improve chlorophyll, total soluble carbohydrates, total free amino acids and ascorbic acid under varied abiotic stress57. Similarly, spermine has been reported to upregulate accumulation of aconitic acid, gallic acid, sugars, amino acids under salt stress58. Further, under drought stress, ethanol addition has been known to induce osmolytes such as amino acids and sugars59. Also, glycerol application has been known to improve chlorophyll under salt stress56.

Interestingly, significant increase in production flux of phase-II and phase-III transformed metabolites of CBZ were observed in the presence of biostimulants (Fig. 5c). This could be due to increased availability of precursors of phase-II and phase-III mediated transformation in the presence of biostimulants. As observed from Fig. 5c, among biostimulants, proline induced higher fold changes, and this could be due to increased production capacity of UDP-Glucose and glutathione in the presence of proline (Supplementary Fig. S8). This was corroborated based on reports of enhanced xenobiotic detoxifying enzymes and improved glutathione with biostimulant exogenous addition under abiotic stress. Improved expression of genes of Cytochrome-P450 family, UDP-glycosyltransferases under drought stress59and GST under salt stress60in Arabidopsis has been reported with ethanol addition. Also, improved GST activity in soybean under salt stress61and drought stress62have been reported with ethanol addition. Further, with spermine addition, improved GST lambda-1 protein was reported in oats under alkali stress63. Further, spermine addition improved GST activity and glutathione content in Vigna radiata L. under cadmium stress64. Enhanced expression of genes of UDP-glycosyltransferase has been reported with glycerol addition under powdery Mildew disease in wheat65. Also, under stress, proline is known to be positively associated with glutathione and proline addition has been known to improve GST activity66.

Interestingly, in animal liver system, biomolecules like sugar conjugation mediated phase-II transition have been known to reduce the toxicity of the xenobiotics making it more polar to be excreted from the system. Also, in context of plants, phase-II metabolites are reported as non-toxic or less phytotoxic molecules than its corresponding phase-I metabolites, with phase-II being an important protective phase in detoxification of xenobiotics12. Thus, in this study, the model predicted reduced toxicity of CBZ via improved specific growth rate and increased flux towards CBZ phase-II & phase-III detoxification in the presence of biostimulants is a novel prediction and warrants further experimental investigation. This prediction can also be employed for facilitating easier analytical detection and quantification of phase-II and phase-III metabolites of pharmaceuticals like CBZ in the presence of biostimulants.

In Silico proof for ameliorative role of biostimulants for CBZ stress in tomato

To comprehensively investigate the perceived impact of biostimulants under CBZ stress, FVA and SI based framework was employed for identifying significantly altered metabolism under CBZ_IC50 condition in presence and absence of biostimulants. It was observed that, biostimulants addition enhances significantly all the major pathways, thus improving biomass precursors production (Supplementary Fig. S10).

Interestingly, ~ 90-96.6% of the significantly altered reactions in the presence of CBZ between the biostimulants were found to be in common, as represented67 in Fig. 6a. This could be likely due to interplay among biostimulants, where their overlapping effects have been known to aid in fine-tuning adaptations to environmental cues. For instance, in Agrostis stolonifera plants under salt stress, exogenous spermine, improved glycerol58. Further, in switchgrass, exogenous proline was reported to improve polyamine metabolism under salt stress68. Also, with ethanol treatment in Arabidopsis, spermidine and glycerol were detected59. Further, glycerol treatment was known to alter proline under salt stress in maize55.

Thus, in the current study, 144 reactions were found to be in common among the significantly altered reactions with biostimulants in the presence of CBZ. Notably, 98% (142 out of 144) of these common significantly altered reactions with biostimulants are a subset of 154 reactions known to be altered under CBZ stress, as mentioned in Sect. Also, each of the primary breakdown step of the biostimulant was predicted among the uniquely altered reactions with respect to each biostimulant. Reactions including ‘PROD2m’ (Proline dehydrogenase, EC Number:1.5.99.8), ‘SMOX’ (Spermine oxidase, EC Number: 1.5.3.16), ‘ALCD2x’ (alcohol dehydrogenase, EC Number: 1.1.1.1), ‘GLYK’ (Glycerol kinase, EC Number: 2.7.1.30) representing the major breakdown of proline, spermine, ethanol and glycerol to pyrroline 5-carboxylate, spermidine & 3-aminopropionaldehyde, acetaldehyde, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate respectively was predicted as uniquely altered with respect to each biostimulant.

Further, in Fig. 6c, the pathway classes for the 144 reactions significantly altered are represented, and among them, the overview of metabolic alterations is depicted in Fig. 7 with predominant reprogramming in the plastid compartment. This was consistent with trends observed with biostimulants in alleviating other stress. Under various stress conditions, proline has been reported to aid in improved nutrient uptake, photosynthesis and antioxidant enzyme activity57. Spermine is known to improve photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, antioxidant capacity, Krebs cycle, Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) shunt, glycolysis, and nucleotide metabolism with exogenous application under abiotic stress58,63,69. Exogenous addition of ethanol has been known to improve nutrient uptake, photosynthesis, photorespiration, sucrose/starch metabolism, phenylpropanoids biosynthesis, glycolysis, fermentation, amino-acid biosynthesis, antioxidant defence response, gluconeogenesis under various abiotic stress53,59,61. Glycerol has been reported to improve nutrient uptake, chlorophyll, fatty acid metabolism under (a)biotic stress54,56,65.

Key metabolites have been known to be integral part of plant stress response. According to Chowdhury et al.70, reactions incorporated with transcriptomics constraints are classified as bottleneck reactions of stress response if the expansion of their flux bounds improved biomass flux. In the current study, as detailed in Supplementary Text section S1.5, the same analysis was adapted for exchange reactions to screen for metabolites, which, upon exogenous addition, alleviate CBZ stress. Based on model prediction, 10 metabolites spanning major components of core pathways and 36 direct biomass precursors were identified as key metabolites for CBZ stress response. Some of these metabolites, as compatible osmolytes, have been known to accumulate as a part of stress adaptation in plants. In tomato plants,alterations in metabolites including proline, sucrose, glucose, fructose, leucine, isoleucine, valine, and GABA were observed under CBZ stress14,71. Further, CBZ-induced alterations in primary metabolites, including glucose, fructose, GABA, isoleucine, leucine, valine, and threonine, have also been reported in Zea mays at the 4th leaf phenological stage72. In the current study, CBZ induced energy and co-factor demand reduced availability of these key metabolites. Further, biostimulants improved the availability of these major components of core pathway (Supplementary Text section S6.3) and subsequently the biomass precursors (Fig. 5b), thus aiding in improved biomass under CBZ stress. Thus, with the GEM serving as a screening tool, this approach can be further extended to explore plant-microbial interactions for alleviating CBZ stress. Additionally, it can aid in screening cost-effective biostimulant alternatives that induce similar metabolic responses by increasing the availability of key metabolites. The list of key metabolites and significantly altered reactions is provided in the supplementary file ‘Supplementary_2.xlsx’.

CBZ_iSL3433 predictions are based on stoichiometric model, thus model connectivity and composition of biomass equation play a crucial role in model predictions. CBZ-induced stress may alter biomass composition, and such changes have a multifactorial effect depending on CBZ dose, exposure duration, and the specific tomato cultivar. As this model is a base model for qualitative analysis, a future version with context-specific experimental constraints would aid in improving the quantitative predictive accuracy of the model. The base model contains a few imbalanced reactions due to missing co-factor information which warrants future scope for improvement. Also, the model, does not explicitly represent transcription factor activity, signalling cascades or metabolic regulation and hence cannot capture hormone-induced or regulatory control of metabolic fluxes.

This study thus highlights the pipeline providing first-guess type estimates for rational investigation of plant stress response to xenobiotics like pharmaceuticals. The prediction accuracy can be improved by incorporating transcriptomic/proteomic data as regulatory constraints, enabling additional analyses such as flux sampling, for more rigorous statistical testing. These predictions can be experimentally validated using tomato seed or seedling exposure assays, including measurements of the germination index, growth parameters (fresh and dry biomass weight, chlorophyll content), histochemical assays for reactive oxygen species, and transcriptomic and targeted metabolomic analyses to verify predicted shifts in amino acid metabolism, photosynthesis, and glutathione metabolism.

Conclusion

Innovative strategies have to be devised to achieve higher crop yields with diminishing land areas to meet the food security needs, especially in the face of climate change. By integrating the green-liver concept with metabolic modelling, this study provides a computational framework for understanding the metabolic impact of pharmaceutical pollutants on crops, offering a predictive tool for screening stress mitigation strategies.

The developed model, CBZ_iSL3433 was able to predict the energy and co-factor mediated demand of CBZ stress leading to reduced biomass. Based on model predictions, 46 key metabolites and 154 significantly altered reactions were identified as a part of CBZ stress response. With a stoichiometric network, based on the law of mass action and thermodynamics, model was able to indicate the key altered metabolic reprogramming states in the presence of CBZ in core metabolism ( photosynthesis, pentose phosphate pathway, folate metabolism, biomass precursors synthesis ), synthesis of precursors of secondary metabolites (chorismate, terpenoid) and nutrient assimilation reported in CBZ exposure in plants. Also, the model predicted the impact of mitigation strategy with foliar addition of biostimulants (proline, spermine, ethanol, and glycerol) to combat CBZ stress.

The developed CBZ_PK module and proposed framework serve as a screening tool prior to experimentation, thus aiding in reducing contaminant exposure by minimizing hit-and-trial experimental investigation, and can be extended to other plants. Further, the distribution of pharmaceutical mixtures in various parts of a plant can be influenced by their physicochemical properties, and the current model can be extended to build a robust whole-plant-based model for understanding the pharmaceutical mixture response. Also, the framework assumes steady-state metabolism and constant photon input, thereby limiting its ability to represent dynamic temporal metabolic reprogramming. Physiologically based pharmacokinetic modelling for pharmacokinetics, combined with integrated omics and diel cycle-based GEM, will be a valuable tool in the way forward for plant pharmacovigilance.
