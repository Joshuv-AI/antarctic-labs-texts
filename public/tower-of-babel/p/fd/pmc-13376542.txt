A microbiome meta-transcriptomics pipeline identifies a neutrophil elastase inhibitor that protects the colonic epithelial barrier
Authors: Bojan Stojkovic, Matthijs Bekkers, Jake P Violi, Brett A Neilan, Tan Hui Ying, Simon Keely, William A Donald, Carlos Riveros, Gerard E Kaiko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory Bowel Diseases (IBD) are lifelong conditions. Current therapeutic approaches target inflammatory signalling rather than improving barrier permeability or repair. The gut microbiome provides an exciting opportunity for novel drug discovery to leverage its role in healthy gut homeostasis. There is a clear need to identify bioactive molecules within the microbiota that could protect the intestinal barrier. Our group has developed a systematic pipeline using metatranscriptomic data to identify, produce, purify, and test microbial proteins in IBD, pinpointing multiple novel microbiota-derived proteins linked to disease activity. We identified a new microbiota protein (BMG-1), that specifically inhibits human neutrophil elastase, a pathogenic protease in IBD. This protease inhibition allows protection of the intestinal epithelial barrier from permeability and promotes epithelial healing. BMG-1 also reduces colon damage in a mouse model of colitis. Finally, we show that the native BMG-1 protein is not only present in human stool, but also significantly decreased in patients with high IBD activity. These findings demonstrate the gut microbiota can specifically regulate the balance of protease/anti-protease activity in the colon, and this represents a novel therapeutic strategy for IBD.Subject terms: Gastrointestinal diseases, Microbiology

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) affect more than 7 million people worldwide. IBD, which includes ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease (CD), is a lifelong condition in which the intestinal barrier is recurrently damaged, leading to increased permeability of the gut, transepithelial migration of commensal microorganisms, excessive inflammation, and poor mucosal healing.1–3 The advent of biologic therapeutics has significantly improved the disease course; however, these drugs primarily aim to alleviate specific symptoms of IBD by dampening inflammation. This results in up to 50% of patients either failing to respond initially or losing response after 12 months, leading to a delay rather than prevention of the need for surgical intervention.4,5 There are multiple hallmark features in IBD that are predictors of disease course but are not targeted by current therapies. This includes impaired function of the epithelial barrier that leads to ‘leakiness’ or increased intestinal epithelial barrier permeability, as well as impaired mucosal healing. This leakiness allows for infiltration and/or exposure of immune cells to luminal contents and microbes, which is thought to perpetuate the chronic inflammatory response. This increased permeability is a predictor of both relapse and the initial onset of IBD.6,7 In addition, mucosal healing is considered one of the best predictors of long-term remission in IBD.2,8,9 The mass mucosal infiltration of neutrophils is another feature of IBD pathology and is readily observed in histological biopsy specimens as part of the pathology diagnostic assessment.10 Uncontrolled neutrophil proteolytic activity leads to intestinal crypt damage and abscess formation.1,11 In addition, it has been demonstrated that the proteolytic enzymes that create this epithelial barrier damage are enriched in clinically active IBD patients,12,13 and known to precede the onset of IBD.14 Therefore, the development of therapies that protect the intestinal barrier by inhibiting these processes is an underexplored and innovative approach to IBD treatment.

The gut microbiome represents a vast and largely untapped resource of potential therapeutic molecules, and its dysbiosis has been consistently linked with IBD. The main signature of the gut microbiome change that occurs in both UC and CD is the overall loss of diversity and reduction in obligate anaerobic bacteria.15,16 Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), a method of transplanting healthy donor faeces, shows ~30% endoscopic remission rate in UC patients with mild/moderate disease.17–20 However, exactly what microbes and what bioactive components within the FMT mixture are driving the positive response remains unclear. Furthermore, the stable engraftment of bacteria and the heterogeneous nature of this uncontrolled mixture remain a major problem with this approach. Far less research has gone into the bioactive proteins that healthy microbiota produce and how they may contribute to gut homeostasis or remission in IBD. Harnessing these molecular components could drive a much higher efficacy rate and would represent a more controllable and druggable pathway. Microbiome-derived molecules capable of protecting the epithelial barrier or promoting mucosal healing represent novel therapeutic avenues, targeting disease aspects currently not addressed by existing therapies.

Commensal microbes can influence intestinal homeostasis through metabolite production, but also through secreted proteins that could interact with the host. These molecules potentially regulate many aspects of host intestinal biology, including the epithelium, the mucus layer, innate and adaptive immune function, and tissue repair pathways, which are all important in the pathogenesis of IBD.21–23 While significant recent research attention has been given to microbiota metabolomics, less focus has been given to the array of proteins produced; this is partly because of the difficulty in directly studying protein content of faecal material.21,22 In contrast to live single biotherapeutic or consortia approaches, defined microbiome-derived proteins offer several translational advantages, including clearer mechanisms of action, improved manufacturability, dose control, reduced genetic drift, and a reduced risk of unpredictable off-target effects. Despite this promise, the functional components of gut microbial proteins remain relatively poorly characterised, and there are few studies that have systematically examined their capacity to modify intestinal barrier biology or underlying immune mechanisms. Identifying protein bioactive molecules with key functional roles in the intestinal tract may uncover a new class of therapeutics that complement existing clinically used immune-targeted therapies by directly strengthening barrier resilience and accelerating mucosal repair.

In this study, we developed a pipeline to identify microbial genes/proteins strongly linked to IBD diagnosis and disease activity, and then a platform to produce and purify these molecules. This pipeline used metatranscriptomic datasets as they enable the identification of genes that are actively transcribed, as opposed to metagenomics, which detects genes in dead bacteria, silent genes, and those with altered abundance but not expression. Therefore, changes in gene expression in metatranscriptomic data are more directly linked to changes in microbial protein activity. Our metatranscriptomic pipeline pinpointed uncharacterised novel microbial genes strongly associated with IBD disease activity indices and identified those likely to be secreted by bacteria at the intestinal barrier. These candidates were identified as potential microbiota drug targets in IBD. We then developed a platform to produce these microbial proteins and functionally test them for activity on human intestinal organoids. Using this pipeline, we have identified a novel protein, which we named BMG-1, found to be a serine protease inhibitor. Our results demonstrate the ability of BMG-1 to specifically inhibit the human neutrophil elastase, which is elevated in IBD and is responsible for intestinal mucosal damage.24,25 Importantly, we have also demonstrated a protective effect of BMG-1 on the epithelial barrier using colon organoids, and in vivo using the dextran sodium sulphate (DSS) model of colitis.

Results

Systematic analysis of the gut microbiome transcriptome identifies microbial genes associated with UC and CD diagnosis as well as disease activity

Microbial gene expression is often overlooked in the gut meta-omic analysis of IBD, as it has mainly prioritised taxonomic profiling of species or identifying changes in broad categories of genes measured by abundance through metagenomics. Using our bioinformatic pipeline and the largest publicly available metatranscriptomic datasets from the Human Microbiome Project 2 (HMP) substudy on IBD,26,27 we aimed to identify specific microbial gene/protein candidates linked to IBD. The HMP metatranscriptomics cohort included 103 participants, with 49 Crohn’s disease, 28 ulcerative colitis, and 27 non-IBD participants. The groups showed no significant differences in sex, age, or race, and had comparable age at diagnosis between Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis (Supplementary Table 2). Based on the bioinformatic pipeline, we prioritised a subset of the genes for further functional testing in the laboratory (Fig. 1a). We utilised an E. coli-based system for gene candidate cloning and protein overexpression. The protein products of top gene candidates were purified and tested for their functional properties using in vitro and in vivo models of intestinal disease (Fig. 1b).

The summary of the bioinformatic gene filtering is shown in Fig. 2a. The transcriptomic sequencing reads were mapped with an efficiency of ~80-90%, to the personalised genome references, which was further improved upon additional mapping to the global reference (as described in methods; Supplementary Fig. 1a). The de novo ORF identification was then conducted, producing 1.43 million ORFs across all metatranscriptomic samples. Genes were further filtered for translational redundancy by gene clustering, resulting in 1.1 million ORFs (Fig. 2a). The gene clustering was based on the amino acid homology of >98% and protein length similarity >98% (Supplementary Fig. 1b), aiming to identify the groups of highly homologous genes likely among the strains of the same species with similar functions. To identify microbial genes differentially expressed in disease, the differential gene expression (DE) analysis was carried out for three pairwise comparisons independently: UC vs nonIBD, CD vs nonIBD and low vs high disease activity. The disease activity classification was determined using the common clinical faecal calprotectin threshold of 200 µg/g, which stratified the samples into two distinct categories (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Genes were filtered using the p-value, and a log2-fold difference of 2 (4-fold) (Fig. 2b). In addition, a disease relevance filter was applied which only included genes detected in at least 30% of patients from either classification group. This threshold was chosen based on the observation that the total number of detected reads per sample stabilises when approximately 30% of samples are considered (Supplementary Fig. 1d, e). Due to the nature of microbiome data two statistical models for DE were used, DESeq2 and Variance Partition. We only considered DE genes detected by both statistical models. The DE analysis for all three comparisons (UC vs nonIBD, CD vs nonIBD and low vs high disease activity) was performed on both 98% and 100% gene clusters, producing a total of 24,680 differentially expressed microbial genes (Fig. 2a). We then carried out the shared feature analysis to identify genes present across both statistical methods and clustering groups, resulting in 797, 182, and 1431 genes in low vs high (Fig. 2c), CD vs nonIBD (Supplementary Fig. 1f) and UC vs nonIBD comparison (Supplementary Fig. 1g), respectively. Importantly, functional annotation of differentially expressed genes showed that more than half of the genes are of unknown function, when using both clustering of orthologous genes (Fig. 2d–f), or categorisation based on the enzyme commission numbers (Supplementary Fig. 1h–j).

Characterisation and prioritisation of differentially expressed genes in the metatranscriptomic analysis of the gut microbiome in IBD. a Discovery pipeline for top candidate microbial gene selection in IBD. Genes were filtered and prioritised based on several features such as homology clustering, magnitude of differential expression, likelihood of secretion and wet lab compatibility for cloning. b Volcano plots of top DE genes generated by 98% clustering analysis for 3 comparisons CD vs non-IBD (752 genes), UC vs non-IBD (2134 genes), and high vs low disease activity (1177 genes). c Venn Diagram on gene lists from 4 comparisons; high vs low disease groups for the Variance Partition (VarPart) and DESeq2 model, and two clustering strategies (98 and 100%) to find DE genes common to all. d–f Pie charts representing Clusters of Orthologues Genes (COGs) from top DE genes. g 10 microbial genes selected for protein synthesis and functional testing

Subsequent filtering by prioritising genes with predicted signal peptides, yielded 172 DE gene candidates predicted to be secreted. We then employed additional gene selection to accommodate practical considerations of E. coli protein production. Specifically, genes encoding proteins below 5kD and over 50kD, and genes with a specific restriction enzyme binding site, were excluded, resulting in a final list of 98 genes (Fig. 2a), of which 10 were successfully synthesised and sufficiently purified in the laboratory and selected for further testing (Fig. 2g).

To examine the taxonomic profile among the top differentially expressed genes from metatranscriptomic analysis (LFC > 2 and P-value < 0.0001, ~9000 genes), we listed the top 120 species for all disease comparisons in both 98% and 100% clustering strategies (Supplementary Fig. 2). This selection covered a high number of species that have previously been associated with IBD, including consistently reported taxa such as Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia hominis, Roseburia inulinivorans and Veillonella parvula.28–31 In addition to exploring taxonomic features in our analyses, we investigated the potential of our DE genes as predictive biomarkers. We performed Random Forest classifier-based feature selection and pairwise feature combinations. ROC curves and area under the curve (AUC) scores for each contrast group are shown in Supplementary Fig. 3. The Random Forest classifier provided the ROC curve performance of a multi-feature classifier from which a Mean Decrease Accuracy ranking was generated. For the pairwise analysis, CD vs non-IBD, UC vs non-IBD and high vs low activity showed AUC values of 0.83, 0.9, and 0.85, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). For the Random Forest classifier, AUC values of 0.96, 0.99, and 0.83 were obtained for CD vs non-IBD, UC vs non-IBD and high vs low comparisons, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3d–i). This data strongly suggests a high accuracy for the expression level of bacterial protein-coding genes, especially in pairs, to predict both UC and CD diagnosis, as well as IBD disease activity.

Functional screening of top gene candidates identified BMG-1 as a microbial protein with protective properties on colonic epithelial cells

Identifying microbial proteins with the ability to protect the colon epithelium from damage or mediate epithelial repair is of great importance, as mucosal healing is critical to long-term remission in IBD.2,8,9 Therefore, we employed a human gut organoid epithelial barrier assay to test the ability of microbial proteins to either induce mucosal damage or protect the epithelial surface. As neutrophils are the most abundant immune cells elevated in IBD patient mucosal biopsies, and a known damaging agent for the intestinal barrier, we used neutrophil contents (lysate) to induce damage in order to assess the ability of microbial proteins to protect against damage (Fig. 3a). Among the 10 tested microbial protein candidates, microbial protein P3 (referred to as BMG-1) exhibited the greatest, dose-dependent improvement in epithelial barrier permeability (Fig. 3b). We analysed BMG-1 gene expression in the metatranscriptomic data and found it to be highly upregulated (~20 fold) in IBD faecal samples with low disease activity and reduced in states of high disease activity (Fig. 3c). This observation remained consistent even after excluding samples without observed gene expression for BMG-1 (Fig. 3d). The BMG-1 gene was expressed in a high portion of samples with reads detected in 70%, 79%, and 56% of samples from nonIBD, low, and high disease activity group, respectively (Fig. 3e). Next, we investigated BMG-1 gene expression in IBD samples and its correlation to the disease activity biomarker faecal calprotectin and as expected found a strong inverse correlation (Spearman r = −0.35, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3f). Even after including nonIBD samples, this correlation decreased but remained significant (Spearman r = −0.16, P = 0.0242) suggesting a role of this protein in disease activity in IBD (Fig. 3g). Lastly, we correlated BMG-1 gene expression with relative abundance of Flavinofractor plautii, one of its predicted bacterial species of origin, within the HMP2 cohort and found a strong correlation between the gene and taxa (Spearman r = 0.77, P = 0.0001) (Fig. 3h). A post-hoc analysis using a mixed linear regression model showed that treatment category status (Antibiotics, Chemotherapy, Immunosuppressants, or Biologics) had no significant effect on the correlation between BMG-1 gene expression and fecal calprotectin/disease activity (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, variance partition analysis was performed again on the microbiota gene dataset with and without stratification by high and low activity groups, but this time the model was adjusted for each of the different treatment categories (Antibiotics, Chemotherapy, Immunosuppressants, Biologics). Results showed that individual treatments explained a negligible amount of variance in the microbiota gene expression data overall (Supplementary Fig. 3j). The model was then adjusted by treatment category and the ranked p-values were compared for the microbiota genes (those significantly linked to disease activity) in the non-adjusted Versus treatment-adjusted model using linear regression (Supplementary Fig. 3k). This analysis showed a tight correlation (Spearman r = 0.96) for the top microbiota gene hits linked to disease activity (including BMG-1) between both the adjusted and non-adjusted models, suggesting that treatment had minimal effect.

BMG-1 is a potent neutrophil protease inhibitor

BMG-1, identified via functional screening as a molecule with colonic barrier protective properties, was then further validated for its ability to protect the colon epithelial layer, over a 6 h time-course (Fig. 4a). BMG-1 was produced and purified with and without a His-tag, HisBMG-1 (N-terminal 6xHis-tag) or BMG-1, and both were tested throughout our experiments. A clear dose effect was observed, with the highest dose of HisBMG-1 significantly reducing the epithelial permeability over this time course experiment (Fig. 4a). This effect was replicated using BMG-1, without the 6xHis-tag (Supplementary Fig. 4a), and was comparable to elafin, a known mucosal protease inhibitor used as the positive control (Supplementary Fig. 4b). As BMG-1 prevented damage to colonic epithelial barrier cells that was induced by neutrophil contents, we hypothesised that BMG-1 may act through inhibiting neutrophil proteases, which are the main contents of neutrophil granules. To test this, we assessed the HisBMG-1 inhibitory function in a cell-free protease assay, using human neutrophil lysate and a fluorescent protease substrate. A statistically significant dose dependent inhibition of neutrophil protease activity was observed at 40 min, 1 h, 2 h and 3 h (Fig. 4b). Similarly, supernatant from stimulated neutrophil cultures was tested and showed a statistically significant dose dependent inhibition effect upon incubation with HisBMG-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4c). We then went back to our metatranscriptomic datasets and explored the homology clustering results around BMG-1 amino acid sequence and identified BMG-2, a highly similar protein characterised by 13 amino acid substitutions compared to BMG-1, with an overall similarity of 98% (Supplementary Fig. 4d). We successfully cloned and purified this microbial protein and tested its ability to likewise inhibit protease activity. Interestingly, HisBMG-2 completely failed to suppress neutrophil protease activity (Fig. 4c), highlighting the sequence specificity of BMG-1 and strongly supporting our decision to analyse genes from the 100% and 98% amino acid clustering homology lists separately in our bioinformatics pipeline. The HisBMG-1 neutrophil protease activity was successfully replicated using untagged BMG-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4e), and elafin also achieved this inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 4f).

In order to better understand the functional properties of BMG-1, we performed in silico multiple alignments of the BMG-1 protein sequence, with 5 known human genes that function as serine protease inhibitors, belonging to the serpin superfamily: Serpin A1, Serpin B1, Serpin C1, Serpin D1 and Serpin E1; and three gut bacterial serpin protease inhibitors from Eubacterium saburreum Saburopin,32 Eubacterium siraeum Siropin 133 and Bifidobacterium longum BL010834 (Fig. 4d). While overall homology was very low (below 30%), we identified a sequence of 26 amino acids in a domain in the C terminal region of the protein with significant homology across most of these molecules (Fig. 4d). This suggested a relatively similar domain among these protease inhibitors, which may be the key enzyme active domain. Further 3D modelling of BMG-1 using AlphaFold 3,35 revealed a typical serpin-like structure with 9 α-helices, and a central β-sheet forming the main body of the protein. Albeit with relatively weak confidence, the reactive central loop (RCL) region and the signal peptide were predicted in this structure and are observed protruding from the main body of the protein (Fig. 4e). Importantly, we found that this RCL region lies within the conserved 26 amino acid C terminal domain, suggesting that this region may be critical for the protease inhibition function. To test these in silico predictions more directly, we then engineered four mutated versions of HisBMG-1, by scrambling the domains of the RCL region, and successfully purified 4 versions of a HisBMG-1 scrambled control protein (Supplementary Fig. 4g). Mutations in the RCL region abrogated the protease inhibition in all four proteins to varying extents, with scrambled-1, -2, and -4 having the greatest reduction in activity (Supplementary Fig. 4h). Protein Scr2 was selected for all downstream experiments and will be referred to as ‘HisBMG-1Scr’ throughout the text. In contrast to HisBMG-1, HisBMG-1Scr failed to protect the colon epithelial cells from damage by neutrophil granules (Fig. 4f), and also lost the ability to inhibit the neutrophil proteases in the cell-free protease assay (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Fig. 4c). In order to test the impact of BMG-1 on neutrophils in a disease context, we obtained neutrophils from IBD patients and performed the protease activity assay. IBD neutrophil protease activity was strongly inhibited by HisBMG-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4i). In contrast, both HisBMG-2 and HisBMG-1Scr failed to suppress the IBD neutrophil protease activity (Supplementary Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 4k).

Together, functional and structural analysis demonstrate that BMG-1 is a potent neutrophil protease inhibitor, which is also reflected in its ability to block neutrophil-induced damage on the colon epithelium in vitro. In addition, we identified the critical region for BMG-1 function, pinpointing the RCL domain at the C terminus of the protein.

BMG-1 specifically inhibits the human neutrophil elastase and pancreatic elastase

We next aimed to identify the specific enzymes in the neutrophil protease contents that are targeted by BMG-1. We tested the 3 main enzymes known to be present in neutrophil granules, Cathepsin G, Proteinase 3, and human neutrophil elastase (HNE) using the cell-free protease assay. BMG-1 (and HisBMG-1) strongly and specifically inhibited HNE, a protease that is known to be linked to IBD pathogenesis (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). This effect was completely abrogated in HisBMG-2 (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 5c) and the scrambled control HisBMG-1Scr (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 5d), suggesting the importance of sequence specificity and the RCL loop in mediating this inhibition of HNE. In contrast, Cathepsin G (CatG) did not show any inhibition in activity by any of the microbial proteins tested (Supplementary Fig. 5e). While there was no effect of BMG-1 on Proteinase 3 (PR3), there was a small inhibition of this protease by both the BMG-2 and scrambled control, with the highest dose of each of these inhibiting PR3 activity (Supplementary Fig. 5f). This data suggest that among neutrophil-derived proteases BMG-1 specifically inhibits HNE.

To examine the effect of BMG-1 on other major elastase enzymes that are present in the intestinal tract, we investigated Chymotrypsin-like elastase 2 A (CELA2A, sometimes referred to as pancreatic elastase) an enzyme that is primarily produced by the pancreas, but also intestinal epithelial cells, and activated in the gut. We tested the effect of BMG-1 on elastase enzymes from the porcine pancreas, using the elastase -specific substrate. Porcine pancreatic elastases share 90% amino acid similarity with CELA2A, but only 50% similarity with HNE. We found that porcine pancreatic elastase activity was inhibited at all doses by both HisBMG-1 and HisBMG-1Scr, but not HisBMG-2 (Supplementary Fig. 5g). This suggests that although BMG-1 inhibited pancreatic elastase, the RCL loop is not critical for this inhibition, unlike HNE. Together, these data demonstrate selective inhibition of neutrophil and digestive proteases by BMG-1, including HNE and pancreatic elastase, but not CatG or PR3. In contrast, BMG-2 has only a small inhibitory effect on PR3.

To more precisely determine the interaction between BMG-1 and HNE, mass spectrometry analysis was performed of the HisBMG-1 and the control HisBMG-1Scr, preincubated with HNE using two different ratios of the microbial protein and HNE (2.5:1 and 10:1) (Fig. 5d, e). While HisBMG-1 and HisBMG-1Scr alone displayed the intact protein spectra only (Supplementary Fig. 5h, k), incubation with HNE resulted in 4 different products: an intact protein and 3 cleaved products with 1.2 kDa, 1.8 kDa and 2.7 kDa loss in mass (Supplementary Fig. 5i, j, l, m). Following HNE incubation, the abundance of the intact product was significantly higher in HisBMG-1Scr control compared to HisBMG-1 (Fig. 5e). In contrast, 1.2 and 2.7 kDa products demonstrated significantly higher abundance in HisBMG-1 interactions with HNE, indicating active cleavage (Fig. 5d–e). These data demonstrate a significantly higher interaction, binding affinity, and cleavage process of HNE for HisBMG-1 compared to the scrambled control, HisBMG-1Scr. Finally, we evaluated the protective effect of HisBMG-1 on differentiated colon epithelial cells specifically against HNE exposure (rather than total neutrophil contents), and demonstrated a dose-dependent protective effect, while HisBMG-1Scr failed to show this effect (Fig. 5f–h). This BMG-1-mediated protection was successfully replicated using organoid cell lines derived from ulcerative colitis patients (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b). Treatment of colonoids with HisBMG-1, but not the scrambled control, significantly reduced epithelial permeability protecting the barrier. We also confirmed that neither BMG-1 nor its scrambled control altered permeability over baseline when treated in the absence of the protease in either UC (Supplementary Fig. 6c), or non-IBD (Supplementary Fig. 6d) cell lines. These data demonstrate a strong interaction between HisBMG-1 and the human neutrophil elastase, a key protease with known barrier-damaging properties in colonic inflammation.

BMG-1 promotes colonic epithelial wound healing and prevents colon epithelial cell death

Given that BMG-1 protects the colonic epithelial barrier integrity from increased permeability, we next examined its capacity to promote colon epithelial repair after damage, a common pathology in IBD. We performed a 2-dimensional colonic epithelial scratch wound assay using both non-IBD and IBD (ulcerative colitis) colonic organoid-derived cells, by introducing the wound and incubating the cells with HNE premixed with HisBMG-1 or HisBMG-1Scr. HNE alone significantly delayed wound healing of non-IBD cells by more than 50% over baseline, but treatment with HisBMG-1 exhibited accelerated healing and 100% complete healing at all doses of HisBMG-1 tested (Fig. 6a). This effect was slightly stronger than that of elafin and untagged BMG-1 (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c). In contrast, HisBMG-1Scr failed to have this potent effect on wound healing at low doses, however, at higher doses it was able to achieve this protective effect suggesting that unlike barrier permeability the impact of BMG-1 on wound healing is not completely due to the anti-protease RCL domain (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Similarly to non-IBD colonic organoid-derived cells, HisBMG-1 induced complete wound repair at all doses in ulcerative colitis colonic organoid-derived cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a). As observed for non-IBD cell lines the scrambled control also had a partly protective effect on wound repair in ulcerative colitis cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 8b), confirming the RCL loop is not critical to the BMG-1 impact on wound repair. Mirroring the result in the permeability assay, we demonstrated that treatment with HisBMG-1 or the scrambled control, in the absence of protease, had no detrimental effect on wound healing, in either non-IBD (Supplementary Fig. 8c) or IBD cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 8d).

Healing of the colonic barrier was tested with HNE at 100 nM. Next, we aimed to test whether HisBMG-1 could impact colonic epithelial cell death when exposed to a higher dose of HNE (250 nM) that could induce clear cell death. We exposed the non-IBD colon epithelial cells to HNE and increasing doses of HisBMG-1 for 24 h, measuring cell confluency and metabolic activity (MTT). HisBMG-1 at 2uM, 1uM and 0.5uM, fully protected against cell death and loss of confluency caused by high dose HNE (Fig. 6c and Supplementary Fig. 8e), which was completely absent in HisBMG-1Scr-treated cells (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 8e). These results were confirmed with the MTT assay, where HisBMG-1 preserved cell viability in a dose-dependent manner and this was again lost with HisBMG-1Scr (Fig. 6e, f). These results clearly demonstrate a strong and specific protective effect of BMG-1 on epithelial repair and viability.

BMG-1 reduces protease driven tight junction transcript upregulation in epithelial cells from colon organoids

Proteases are known to disrupt tight junctions between intestinal epithelial cells at the protein level inducing increased permeability. Therefore, we next explored how BMG-1 mediated barrier protection may also alter transcript levels of tight junction genes in colonic epithelial cells. IBD colon organoids were cultured in ALI and treated with neutrophil lysate and HisBMG-1/HisBMG-1Scr. Neutrophil protease treatment induced significant upregulation of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) (Supplementary Fig. 9a and b), occludin (OCLDN) (Supplementary Fig. 9c, d) and claudin-1 (CLDN1) (Supplementary Fig. 9e and f) gene expression. HisBMG-1 treatment significantly reduced this upregulation in a dose-dependent manner, in ZO-1 (Supplementary Fig. 9a) and OCLDN (Supplementary Fig. 9c), while no significant reduction was observed in CLDN1 (Supplementary Fig. 9e). This effect was completely absent in the scrambled control treated cells (Supplementary Fig. 9b, d, f). Interestingly, treatment with HisBMG-1 or scrambled control in the absence of protease had no effect on either ZO-1 or OCLDN expression (Supplementary Fig. 9a–d), the protein alone induced a small but statistically significant upregulation in CLDN1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 9e and f). We next examined key chemokines released from the colon epithelium that attract immune cells and drive inflammation, CXCL1 and CXCL8. Transcripts of both these chemokine genes were upregulated in response to neutrophil protease stimulation (although CXCL8 was not statistically significant) (Supplementary Fig. 9g–j). Neither BMG-1 nor the scrambled control significantly reduced the chemokine gene expression induced by the neutrophil contents.

BMG-1 reduces colon damage in DSS-induced colitis in vivo

After demonstrating the capacity of HisBMG-1 for HNE inhibition and colon epithelium protection in vitro, we aimed to test its capacity to alter the disease course in a murine model of DSS colitis in vivo. BMG-1 specifically targets human neutrophil elastase, which only shares 71% sequence similarity and identity with mouse neutrophil elastase. Despite this, BMG-1 is still able to inhibit the activity of mouse neutrophil proteases in the cell-free assay against both broad-spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 10a), and elastase-specific substrates (Supplementary Fig. 10b). However, unlike our findings with human neutrophils where the BMG-1 scrambled control lacked activity in the cell-free protease assay (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Fig. 4), we found that the BMG-1 scrambled control demonstrated strong inhibition of mouse neutrophil proteases against both broad-spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 10c), and elastase-specific substrates (Supplementary Fig. 10d). We next compared the effect of BMG-1 on human and mouse neutrophil proteases side-by-side by testing against both the broad-spectrum protease substrate and elastase-specific substrate. BMG-1 had a > 5-fold more potent inhibitory effect on human neutrophil proteases than mouse neutrophil proteases using the broad protease substrate (Supplementary Fig. 10e). The inhibitory effect of BMG-1 on both human and mouse neutrophil proteases was more similar and more pronounced when using the elastase-specific substrate (Supplementary Fig. 10f). There was no inhibition from the scrambled control on human neutrophil proteases using either the broad protease or elastase-specific substrates, however, there was a clear significant inhibition of mouse neutrophil proteases using both the broad and even more strikingly elastase-specific substrate (Supplementary Fig. 10g, h). This data demonstrates the differential effect of BMG-1 on human and mouse neutrophil proteases.

Given these results, we next assessed the impact of BMG-1 using an in vivo model of colitis. Mice were treated with 2% DSS in drinking water, followed by recovery with normal drinking water. HisBMG-1 or vehicle was administered intrarectally every two days (Fig. 7a). We did not observe differences in weight loss between DSS mice treated with vehicle or HisBMG-1 (Fig. 7b). DSS treatment induced a significant increase in the protease activity within the intestinal tract as evidenced by increased faecal protease activity compared to control drinking water groups (Fig. 7c, d). HisBMG-1 administration at least partly abrogated this elevation in fecal protease activity above non-drinking water controls (Fig. 7c, d and Supplementary Fig. 10i). As expected, the DSS-induced mucosal damage and ulceration led to a significant loss of histologically normal colon in DSS-treated mice and a significantly shorter colon length than control drinking water mice (Fig. 7e–g). This colonic damage was reduced in HisBMG-1-treated mice as compared to the vehicle controls, resulting in a significantly greater percentage of normal colon tissue (i.e. less damaged tissue) and greater colon length (Fig. 7e–g).

BMG-1 protein is detected in human fecal samples and is reduced in high disease activity

To determine whether the BMG-1 protein could be detected in its native form naturally occurring in human fecal samples, we analysed samples from non-IBD (n = 20) and IBD (n = 21) patients. Fecal sample protein lysate was run on SDS-PAGE gel, and a molecular weight band was isolated corresponding to our known BMG-1 size. After trypsin digestion, the resulting peptides were mapped to protein sequences translated from our microbiota metatranscriptomic reference, including our known sequence of BMG-1 (see bioinformatic pipeline description in Methods and Results). We defined positive detection as the identification of 2 or more peptide sequences uniquely mapping to the BMG-1 reference as per the standard label-free quantification (LFQ) MaxQuant approach. We identified the BMG-1 protein successfully in 11 of 20 non-IBD samples and 15 of 21 IBD samples (Supplementary Fig. 11a). This demonstrates that the BMG-1 protein exists naturally in human stool, and because of the isolation process through molecular weight-based gel excision, this suggests that it is also relatively stable and does not undergo major degradation into smaller fragments in the harsh fecal environment. We then divided the IBD samples into low and high disease activity and quantified the levels of BMG-1 using the LFQ MaxQuant normalisation method. The results revealed that samples from low disease activity had significantly greater levels of the BMG-1 protein than those of high disease activity (Supplementary Fig. 11a). These results confirm that BMG-1 is a naturally occurring protein in human stool and correlates with disease activity in IBD.

Discussion

Through the design of a new systematic intestinal microbiome metatranscriptomic pipeline and functional screening platform, we identified, synthesised and functionally tested BMG-1, a novel and potent neutrophil elastase inhibitor, with colonic epithelial protective properties. This previously uncharacterised microbial protein showed gene expression that inversely correlated with disease activity, that is, the higher the activity (disease flares), the less expression of the gene. BMG-1 inhibits human neutrophil elastase and neutrophil cell proteolytic activity. Importantly, BMG-1 protects the colonic epithelial barrier from proteolytic damage, promotes colon epithelial wound healing and reduces colon damage in a mouse model of colitis.

Previous studies using bioinformatics to investigate the microbiome in the context of IBD have mainly focused on either taxonomy, metabolites, or broad categories of functional units of genes, such as KEGG categories.36,37 These studies, for the most part, do not investigate specific gene/protein features and their functionality in disease, nor how specific gene features are linked to disease activity and in silico secretion prediction models. To develop novel therapeutic treatments to more effectively tackle unmet clinical needs, we aimed to leverage the microbiome’s role in healthy gut homeostasis, and hypothesised that identifying functional microbial products through transcriptional interrogation would provide novel candidates. Our pipeline was specifically designed to prioritize the activity and functionality of the microbiota by incorporating microbial metatranscriptomic analysis of stool samples to identify actively transcribed protein-coding RNA.

A fundamental aspect of this bioinformatic-based approach involved the construction of personalized genomic references (PGRs), followed by transcriptome mapping to these references. Notably, we observed that ~10–15% of total reads remained unmapped to the reference. This incompleteness was anticipated and arises from several factors, including the abundance of uncharacterised species within the human gut metagenome and the variable degree of completeness of the reference genomes used for mapping. Specifically, the latter has been a point of discussion among researchers, and it is widely accepted that approximately one-third of the microbial genes remain functionally uncharacterised.38,39 These factors primarily reflect the inherent challenges and developmental stage of the evolving field of functionality within microbiome research.

Most bioactive microbiota molecules linked to disease or healthy states, as well as microbiota-derived drug candidates, have been metabolite-based. The utility of protein-based secreted microbial therapeutics has remained largely unexplored. Our study is among the first to identify novel functional microbiota-derived secreted proteins with therapeutic potential for IBD through the metatranscriptomic interrogation of stool samples. We utilised bioassays that were designed to assess the impact on hallmark features of IBD-related pathology, including impaired epithelial integrity and wound repair, for functional validation of our identified microbial protein targets. In our assays, we employed neutrophils to damage the barrier. Elevated neutrophil-derived molecules, such as neutrophil elastase, are abundant in the lumen and strongly associated with IBD diagnosis and disease activity.14,40,41 Our findings suggest that BMG-1 may exert its protective effect by inhibiting neutrophil elastase at the epithelial surface, preventing epithelial damage and subsequent barrier leakage.

Dysregulated protease activity in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract is being increasingly linked to IBD, with elevated protease levels observed in patient faecal samples, even preceding diagnosis.14,42–45 This imbalance can arise from either increased proteolytic enzymes, or due to the impaired inhibition of these enzymes. These enzymes are largely either digestive enzymes derived from the pancreas or produced by immune cells such as mast cells and neutrophils.42 Importantly, proteases derived from neutrophils, such as HNE and MMPs, are strongly correlated with IBD severity.46 Upon inflammatory insult, neutrophils are recruited to eliminate bacteria through phagocytosis, NETs, and ROS production. Apart from ROS, these responses involve the release of proteolytic enzymes that, as a side effect of targeting microbes, also degrade tight junction proteins and compromise barrier integrity. Inhibiting HNE may reduce elastolytic activity of granules and NET-associated enzymes, which may potentially limit the leakiness of barrier function in IBD. In addition to proteases released by infiltrating immune cells, pancreatic digestive proteases also contribute to increased proteolytic activity in the gut lumen and feces in IBD.12 Importantly, this increase is further amplified by antibiotic treatment, which also leads to impaired epithelial barrier and exacerbation of colitis in IL10-/- mice,47 suggesting a microbial role in protease inactivation. We have demonstrated that BMG-1 inhibits pancreatic elastase, although with several caveats. The scrambled version of BMG-1 also exhibited a significant dose-dependent inhibition, suggesting that different regions of the protein are responsible for the inhibition of this enzyme. We used porcine pancreatic elastase as a surrogate for human, since the active form of the human enzyme was not commercially available. Two isoforms of the porcine pancreatic elastase exist in UniProt: CELA1 and CELA2A. They share 70% similarity between them, but have a very high 95% and 90% similarity level to human CELA1 and CELA2A (often referred to as human pancreatic elastase), respectively. Interestingly, Motta et al. have demonstrated that CELA2A is also expressed by human colonocytes and that epithelial cells contribute to increased elastolytic activity in IBD patients,48 which further adds to the significance of our finding.

Recent studies suggest that the gut microbiota may also directly produce detrimental proteases, exacerbating IBD pathology.13,14 Certain bacterial species, such as P. vulgatus and E. faecalis, produce proteases that have been linked to disease activity.13,49 However, an alternative hypothesis suggests that the absence of protease inhibitors, rather than excessive protease production, drives colonic damage and inflammation.50 In homeostasis, the gut maintains protease balance through multiple host inhibitors, including alpha1-antitrypsin (AAT), SLPI, and Elafin. While AAT primarily functions in serum, SLPI and Elafin neutralize neutrophil-derived proteases in the gut mucosa.51,52 Notably, Elafin and SLPI expression are downregulated in mucosal biopsies of IBD patients, potentially compromising epithelial barrier protection.25,53 Studies have also demonstrated that protease inhibition in the gut can alleviate inflammation in mouse models of colitis.54–56 These studies suggest that during chronic inflammation, the epithelial barrier has a dysfunctional host anti-protease/proteolytic balance. However, the focus of these studies has been on synthetic or host-derived protease inhibitors, ignoring the role of microbiota protease inhibition. Our study is one of the first to demonstrate that the gut microbiome can also play a key role in suppressing protease activity in IBD. Identifying a specific microbial protein, BMG-1, that acts as a potent inhibitor of HNE. BMG-1’s expression correlates with disease remission and flares (both at the RNA and protein level), thereby suggesting a role for maintaining the proteolytic balance in the gut. Hence, BMG-1 may also represent a candidate for future therapeutic targeting, potentially enhancing epithelial barrier integrity and repair in IBD. Only four proteins with protease inhibitory properties have been functionally characterised in gut bacteria to date,57 isolated from Bifidobacterium longum,34 Eubacterium sireaum33 and Eubacterium saburreum.32 However, none of these proteins have been investigated in the context of a disease, including no link to IBD. We identified the bacterium that likely expresses the BMG-1 gene in the gut as F. plautii, known as an anaerobic gram-positive member of the healthy human gut microbiome. BMG-1, naturally produced by F. plautii, may neutralise HNE activity in order to perform a symbiotic role for both the host and bacteria to help maintain mucosal barrier integrity and hence maintain the homeostatic niche of this mucosal microbe. The role of F. plautii in IBD has remained relatively unexplored, with only a few studies describing its effects in mouse models.58,59

Gill et al. have provided causal evidence that mouse neutrophil elastase reshapes the broad gut microbiota composition in mice.60 In a mouse Salmonella Typhimurium colitis model, neutrophil recruitment into the intestinal lumen coincided with microbiome shifts. Mechanistically, neutrophil elastase inhibition using the small molecule inhibitor sivelestat during infection prevented the dysbiosis. Conversely, administering purified neutrophil elastase to uninfected mice induced microbiome shifts in Lachnospiraceae and Ruminococcaceae. Together, this demonstrates that neutrophil elastase is not just a marker of inflammation or driver of tissue damage, but is also a driver of microbiota changes in vivo.

As we have shown, BMG-1 gene and protein expression are reduced in IBD patients in states of high disease activity, and given that this clinical index state is directly linked to inflammation, it can be concluded that BMG-1 is reduced in the colon in a high-inflammation environment. Our data also indicates that this altered detection of BMG-1 is likely due to both changes in abundance of Flavonifractor in the colon as well as environmental regulation of BMG-1 gene expression. There is a strong correlation between BMG-1 transcript level (RNA) and Flavonifractor bacterial abundance (DNA), but there are also a large number of patients with lower Flavonifractor and higher BMG-1 expression, as well as the inverse. Perhaps inflamed conditions in the IBD colon lead to reduced Flavonifractor growth and BMG-1 expression, which secondarily exacerbates tissue damage from proteases. Or alternatively, perhaps lower BMG-1 expression/Flavonifractor abundance due to other factors leads to less inhibition of tissue-damaging proteases, and thus contributes to increased inflammation. The presence of higher BMG-1 may protect the host epithelial barrier and hence protect this mucosal microbe’s niche. Furthermore, as BMG-1 is a ~ 45 kDa metabolically expensive protein, it most likely serves an additional beneficial role to the bacteria as well. In homeostatic conditions in the gut, given the abundance of pancreatic elastase, perhaps BMG-1’s inhibition of this protease also promotes survival of this bacterium.

To our knowledge, BMG-1 is the first microbiota-derived protein drug candidate generated through transcriptomic gene expression analysis of the gut microbiome. Our study suggests that the balance of protease activity regulated by both host and microbial factors could be involved as a potential factor in IBD pathogenesis. Our data reveal that the inhibition of HNE by BMG-1 may be a part of the mechanism by which the microbiome contributes to the balance of the gut protease/anti-protease homeostasis. BMG-1 represents a novel candidate for future therapeutic targeting, potentially enhancing epithelial barrier integrity and repair in IBD.

Material and methods

Bioinformatics pipeline

The publicly available datasets utilised in this study were sourced from the Human Microbiome Project 2 (HMP) substudy on IBD.26,27 This extensive longitudinal study encompassed data gathered from diverse cohorts, including 132 participants, comprising individuals with Crohn’s disease (CD) (n = 67), ulcerative colitis (UC) (n = 38), or non-IBD (n = 27). Various analytical techniques, such as metagenomics (MTG) and metatranscriptomics (MTX), were conducted on collected stool samples. This study included bi-weekly sample collection over the one-year follow-up period. A total of 758 stool samples (371 CD, 210 UC, 177 non-IBD) from the dataset had paired MTG and MTX data. Demographic and clinical characteristics were compared across disease groups using Kruskal–Wallis and χ² tests. Age at diagnosis between CD and UC was compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and Fisher’s exact test. Associations between BMG-1 expression, medication use, and outcome variables were evaluated using multiple linear regression (MLR) models including main effects and interaction terms. False discovery rate correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg.

A personal genomic reference scaffold was established for each patient by compiling all genomic references of the taxonomic units identified in the individual, using MetaPhlan2, from the individual’s MTG sequencing data.61 The identified taxa for each individual were collated, and the corresponding reference genomes downloaded from National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) to create a personalised genomic reference (PGR). The accuracy of this approach was confirmed by mapping all MTG reads to the scaffolds on a per sample basis. Following read quality assessment using FastQC, we applied Cutadapt for adapter trimming. MTX reads were then mapped to the genomic reference using Bowtie2.62 Individual (time point) sequenced data with less than 0.5 million read pairs (MTG) or single-end reads (MTX) were excluded. As a quality control of the completeness of the PGRs, unmapped reads from all individuals with more than 10% of unmapped reads were mapped to the global genomic reference (GGR), a reference constructed from all species from all subjects used in the study. Low numbers of unmapped reads on the GGR from PGRs with a moderate or high percentage of unmapped reads indicated incompleteness of the PGR and prompted us to manually expand these specific PGRs with genomes of missing species and strains. For each individual, a ratio was calculated by taking the fraction of unmapped reads that mapped to the GGR and dividing this by the fraction of initially unmapped reads and plotted per individual. For individuals with incomplete personalised genomic references, the cut-off ratio of 0.2 was used for updating the personal reference. All sequencing data from individuals with expanded PGRs were mapped again to the updated PGRs. After this step, all individuals had less than 10% of unmapped reads in MTX and less than 15% in MTG.

To consistently identify putative expressed proteins for all individuals for the quantitation of mapped sequence data, all open reading frames (ORFs) were identified de novo from the GGR using Prokka, a tool designed for finding ORFs in prokaryotes.63 Features such as rRNA, tRNA and tmRNA, that do not encode for protein sequences, were identified using ViennaRNA and were not relevant to our pipeline, excluding them from the annotation of our GGR.64 Then, gene clustering was performed based on amino acid sequence similarity, grouping ORFs into two categories: 100% and 98% amino acid identity to enable the investigation of both unique predicted microbial proteins and those sharing a high degree of similarity but may only differ by 2% of amino acids, perhaps among similar strains of the same species.65

Mapped sequence data were then counted towards these genomic annotations using FeatureCounts.66 A minimum cut-off value of 5 reads per gene was applied to ensure robustness. Multiple mapping reads, of which we kept the top 5 mapping locations, were counted using fractional counts to all their mapping locations. This approach expanded the number of genes with counts for differential expression analysis without disproportionately overrepresenting genes with very similar sequences across multiple species.

For disease diagnosis, two separate contrasts were used in the DE analysis between disease groups: CD vs non-IBD and UC vs non-IBD. To assess differences in microbial gene expression concerning disease activity, the IBD (CD and UC) samples were stratified based on faecal calprotectin (FC) levels, the most clinically common molecular biomarker for disease activity. For this stratification, samples from UC and CD patients were aggregated into two groups: low disease activity and high disease activity, as defined by an FC threshold of 200 μg/g, which is within the recommended range for patient classification.67 Overall,148 samples contained FC data, of which 96 samples were classified as low disease activity, and 52 samples as high.

The statistical models used in the DE analysis, implemented in the R packages DESeq and VariancePartition, have distinct statistical assumptions about the underlying data distributions, leading to different analytical approaches to increase the confidence in any resulting DE gene hits. Their unique assumptions result in model-specific advantages in handling zero-count reads and normalisation.68–70 To be conservative, we applied both statistical models to our DE analysis, ranking the genes based on p-value and fold change. For each contrast (CD vs non-IBD, UC vs non-IBD, and low vs high activity), 5000 top-ranked genes were compared, and overlapping common genes were analysed further. Following the DE analysis, we aimed to prioritise genes that are more likely to be biologically relevant. We hypothesised that gene candidates would be of greater biological interest if they were detected in a substantial portion of the sample population, and we set a presence threshold of 30%. This indicated that the gene had to be detected as being expressed in at least 30% of the samples in at least one of the groups in the contrast. Variance partitioning analysis was also performed to quantify the contribution of treatment-related variables and clinical factors to overall gene expression variability. Inter-individual variability independent of disease activity was modelled as participant, while participant-by-activity effects were captured by the Low/High activity term; Activity represented residual variability not explained by disease activity status. To assess the impact of covariate treatment adjustment. P-values from models with and without treatment covariates were ranked and correlated. The basis of the model and code is included in https://github.com/BioAI-CR/BIO18025-FunMG.git.

Gene candidate selection

Differentially expressed gene lists were further filtered by identifying predicted secreted proteins, as we hypothesised that secreted proteins or extracellular microbial proteins that contained signal peptides would be more likely to exert functional interaction with the host. This filtering step was based on multiple prediction algorithms that identified signal peptides on amino acid sequences, EffectiveDB and SignalP.71,72

Additionally, our selection process for practical laboratory production involved the assessment of gene sizes, considering limitations for the heterologous expression and purification of large proteins in E. coli. Genes larger than 1500 base pairs (~50 kD) were excluded. Next, gene sequences were checked for the presence of restriction enzyme sites that could interfere with cloning. After these filtering steps following DE analysis, a total of 14 genes were chosen. We aimed to purify a panel of ten IBD-linked microbial proteins for screening to identify those that have functional effects on epithelial barrier permeability and repair.

Recombinant protein synthesis

The design of the DNA constructs, in silico cloning and multiple sequence alignments were performed using SnapGene (GSL Biotech LLC). Gene sequences were codon optimised for E. coli and modified by the addition of SapI restriction site to facilitate ligation into an expression vector. The final gene fragments were commercially synthesised (Integrated DNA Technologies), SapI digested and ligated using Electra system according to instructions (Atum). Two rhamnose-inducible expression vectors were utilised: pD861-His or pD861-HisMBP, with N-terminal 6xHis or 6xHisMBP fusion tag, respectively. The correct gene insertion was confirmed using colony PCR and Sanger sequencing (Australian Genome Research Facility). The positive clones were grown to OD600 = 0.4, induced using 4 mM Rhamnose, and cultured overnight at 23 °C for optimal protein expression. The bacterial pellets were freeze-thawed, lysed using the combination of B-PER™ reagent treatment (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and sonication (30% power and pulse for 2 min). The insoluble fraction and debris were removed by centrifugation, and the supernatant containing the recombinant protein was applied to Immobilized Metal Affinity Chromatography (IMAC) columns and purified according to manufacturer’s instructions (Cytiva). The HisMBP tag was cleaved with His-tagged HRV-3C Protease (Sigma) at the specific cleavage site present on the vector and removed along with the protease with a secondary IMAC step. After purification, samples were further processed using Pierce High-Capacity Endotoxin Removal Spin Columns, following manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentration and purity were assessed using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 4–15% Mini-PROTEAN™ TGX Stain-Free SDS-PAGE (BioRad) and Pierce™ Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The presence of His tagged BMG-1 (HisBMG-1) was confirmed by Western Blot using the anti-6xHis Tag Polyclonal Antibody (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Mass spectrometry

For characterisation of the microbiota-derived protein, HisBMG-1, we performed native mass spectrometry. Recombinant HisBMG-1 was buffer exchanged into 100 mM ammonium acetate (pH ~7.5) using Zeba spin desalting columns (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Protein concentration was adjusted to >1.5 µg/mL for all experiments. To assess interactions with human HNE, HisBMG-1 was incubated with HNE at molar ratios ranging from 1:2.5 to 1:10 (HNE:HisBMG-1) for up to 1 h at room temperature prior to MS analysis. Mass spectra were acquired using Q Exactive UHMR Hybrid Quadrupole Orbitrap (Thermo Fisher Scientific), under previously described conditions optimised for native MS.73 Spray voltage was set to 0.8–1.2 kV with the heated capillary entrance being set to 200 °C. All analyses were performed in positive ion mode using a mass range of 2000–6000 m/z. Samples were introduced by direct infusion using static nanospray emitters. Deconvoluted mass spectra were obtained using UniDec, and charge state assignments were manually confirmed based on isotopic spacing and m/z shifts between adjacent charge states. Theoretical masses were calculated from protein sequences and compared to deconvoluted peaks.

Human fecal sample processing for mass spectrometry

Fecal samples were provided by the Digestive Health Biobank from IBD (n = 14 Crohn’s and n = 7 ulcerative colitis) and non-IBD subjects (n = 20) approved by the Hunter New England Health human research ethics committee (2020/ETH03303). Written informed consent was obtained from study subjects. Approximately 500 µl of fecal slurry with DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research) was mixed 1:1 with B-PER to lyse bacterial cells and disrupted (30 Hz for 10 min) with Zirconic beads (0.1 and 0.5 mm) using the TissueLyser II (Qiagen). After disruption, cells were centrifuged at 16,000 x g for 10 min and the supernatant was collected and stored at -80 °C in low protein-binding tubes. Samples were thawed on ice, heated at 95 °C for 5 min, cooled and protein concentration measured by the BCA assay. Samples were diluted to 75 µg/33 µl in sterile water, and Laemmli buffer with 0.1 M DTT added at 1 in 4. Solution was heated for a further 95 °C for 5 min, then cooled, before 2 lanes of a 4–20% Tris-glycine gradient gel (75 µg each) were loaded per sample. Gel was run with ice–cold Tris–Glycine–SDS running buffer (80 V into the start of the resolving gel and then 120 V to travel 3 cm into resolving gel), including the Precision Plus Protein MW Kaleidoscope marker. Recombinant full-length BMG-1 was also run through the gel as a positive control. Gel was rinsed in water three times, and fixed for 15 min in 50% methanol / 10% acetic acid / 40% water to precipitate proteins in the gel, reduce diffusion, and remove detergents. Gel was again rinsed and stained lightly for 1 hour with rocking using Coomassie colloidal stain until bands were visually clear. Gel was then destained (to reduce the background, not the bands) in 2% acetic acid in water, and rinsed in water a further two times. Using the molecular weight markers and BMG-1 standard as a guide, the 37–50 kDa window, which should contain BMG-1, was excised from the gel using a sterile scalpel and diced into ~1mm3 cubes, and then two excised lanes per sample were pooled together into one low protein-binding tube. Gel fragments were destained in 50% acetonitrile/50 mM NH₄HCO₃ twice for 10 min, before being dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile for 5 min followed by speed-vac drying and storage at −80 °C. Post de-staining, 10 mM dithiothreitol was added to the gel pieces for 30 min at 55 °C. The liquid was then discarded, and a solution containing 55 mM chloroacetamide was added for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. Gel pieces were washed with ultrapure water. 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate was added to the gel pieces and left for 30 min at room temperature before being discarded. The gel pieces were then dehydrated with 100% acetonitrile. Once the gels were visibly dehydrated, the acetonitrile was discarded. 100 µL of 2.5 ng/µL sequencing grade modified trypsin (Promega, Australia) in 100 mM ammonium bicarbonate was added to the gel pieces and left 16 h overnight at 37 °C to allow for protease digestion. After overnight digestion, for each sample, the solution on the gel pieces was transferred to a new tube, then 0.1% Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) was added to the gel pieces and left for 30 min at room temperature. The 0.1% TFA was then removed and added to the corresponding tube that contained the original digestion solution. 0.1% TFA in 60% acetonitrile was added to the gel pieces for 30 min then similarly transferred to a tube containing the pooled solution. A final wash of 100% Acetonitrile was added to the gel pieces left for 10 min then transferred to the corresponding pooled solution. These pooled solutions were then dried using a vacuum centrifuge set to 30 °C, and then reconstituted in 20 µL of 0.1% formic acid (FA) and transferred to an autosampler vial.

Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry and fecal protein identification and quantification

Liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) was carried out on a Dionex UltiMate 3000 nanoflow LC coupled to a Thermo Scientific Q Exactive Plus MS. Peptides were analysed in positive ionisation mode with the scan range being set to 350–1500 m/z. All data were acquired in data-dependent acquisition (DDA) TopN mode. An inclusion list containing BMG1 peptides was included, and ions with a 1+ and >4+ charge were excluded from DDA precursor selection. The inclusion list for BMG1 peptides was generated using MS-Digest and verified with the recombinant BMG-1 positive control. Max missed cleavages were set to 2, and the maximum mass of a peptide was increased to 8000. Acetyl (N-term), Carbamidomethyl (C) and Oxidation (M) were included as variable modifications, with the remaining parameters being left to the default settings. For the peptides generated, the m/z of their 2 + , 3+ and 4+ charge states was calculated, and if the m/z value was between the scan range of the MS, it was included in the inclusion list. Collision energy was set to 30 normalised collision energy (NCE) for all MS2 scans. Peptides were separated on an in-house packed fritless nano column (75 µm × 20 cm) containing C18AQ media (1.9 µm, 120 Å Dr Maisch, Ammerbuch-Entringen Germany) maintained at 45 °C. The flow rate was 200 nL/min, with solvent A being comprised of 0.1% FA in ultrapure water and solvent B containing 0.1% FA in 80:20 Acetonitrile: ultrapure water. Peptides were eluted by a gradient elution with a gradient of the following solvent B: 0.00 min 2%, 38.00 min 45%, 39.00 min 80%, 39.50 min 80%, 40.00 min 2%, 50.00 min 2%. LC-MS/MS data files were analysed using MaxQuant V2.7.5.0 for peptide/ protein identification and quantification. The LC-MS/MS data were searched against our microbiota metatranscriptomic reference library FASTA file (see Bioinformatics pipeline method) comprised of genes detected across the fecal metatranscriptomic samples. Minimum peptide length was set to 5, and the minimum unspecific peptide search length was set to 6. The remaining settings were left to their default options. For protein quantification the minimum ratio count was set to 2, and peptides for quantification were set to unique + razor. Label-free quantification (LFQ) was enabled, and MaxQuant LFQ normalisation was performed with 2 being the minimum ratio count and the normalisation type being set to classic.74 Both LFQ and the default quantification results were exported and subjected to further normalisation relative to total protein µg based on the BCA assay.

Human neutrophil enzyme isolation

Blood was collected from healthy volunteers and IBD patients with current active disease as approved by the Hunter New England Health human research ethics committee (2020/ETH03303). Written informed consent was obtained from the study subjects. The whole blood was diluted 2:1 with 3% dextran saline solution to remove the red blood cells. The top layer was diluted with an equal volume of 2% fetal calf serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in PBS. Next, 8 ml mixture was layered on 4 ml LymphoprepTM media, and processed according to instructions (StemCell Technologies). The cell pellet was treated with the red blood cell lysis buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM NaHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA) for 2–5 min. After washing with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific), cells were resuspended in DMEM media (Thermo Fisher Scientific), counted, and purity of neutrophils was assessed using May-Grunwald Giemsa Staining and cytospin (>90%). Neutrophil cell lysate was prepared by one freeze-thaw cycle, sonication (30% power and pulse for 2 min) and a final bead beating step (30 Hz, 5 min). Debris was removed by centrifugation, and cell lysate was aliquoted and stored at −80 °C.

Human neutrophil stimulation in vitro and supernatant collection

Human neutrophils were cultured after isolation from whole blood as described. Cells were seeded in a 96-well culture plate and stimulated with 1 ng/ml TNF-α and 1 μM formyl-Met-Leu-Phe (fMLP), for 30 min before plate was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was removed, and the cells were resuspended in fresh DMEM and incubated for another 2 h. The supernatants were collected after centrifugation and stored at -80 °C.

Murine neutrophil enzyme isolation

Bone marrow was extracted from tibia and femur and pooled in HBSS-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The cell pellet was treated with the red blood cell lysis buffer (150 mM NH4Cl, 10 mM NaHCO3, 0.1 mM EDTA) for 2–5 min. Cells were resuspended, sieved and washed with excess HBSS-EDTA. Cells were then transferred to 5 ml DMEM media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and added to 3 layers of Percoll (Sigma) gradients (78%, 69%, 52%). After separation, the neutrophil fraction was washed and resuspended in DMEM media. Cells were assessed for purity and lysed as described above for human neutrophils.

Culturing of human colonic organoids

Human colonic organoids were provided by the Digestive Health Biobank as approved by the Hunter New England Health human research ethics committee (2020/ETH03303). Written informed consent was obtained from the study subjects. Organoid lines were isolated from colonic biopsies from IBD (ulcerative colitis) and non-IBD patients, expanded and biobanked.75 Organoids were cultured in 24-well plates, in three-dimensional 40 µl dome structures in Matrigel (BD Biosciences) and expanded using our well-established L-WRN stem cell conditioned media (CM) system.23,76–78 For the expansion of organoids, CM was further supplemented with SB 431542 ALK inhibitor (TGFβi, 10 µM, Abcam), Y-27632 dihydrochloride Rho kinase inhibitor (ROCKi, 10 µM, Abcam), SB 203580 p38 MAPK inhibitor (p38i, 5 µM, Abcam) and Primocin (1 mg/ml, InvivoGen).75 Organoids tested negative for Mycoplasma (Lonza MycoAlert).

Intestinal epithelial barrier permeability assay

After organoid culturing, in vitro epithelial barrier permeability assays were conducted by disaggregating the 40 µl organoid domes into single cells using Trypsin-EDTA (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for the formation of differentiated cell layers producing mucous.78 After enzymatic digestion, cells were plated on a 96 well transwell system, containing a 0.4 µm pore polyester membrane (Stem Cell Technologies), in a 1:5 ratio. The cells were incubated with the complete CM, on both basal (120 µl) and apical side (40 µl). Upon reaching confluency, CM was removed from the apical side to create an Air Liquid Interphase (ALI) culture that was differentiated for 2 or 10 days, depending on the experiment. On the final day, the cells were washed with DMEM media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove serum and subsequently treated on the basolateral side for 6 h with the experiment-specific molecules. These included neutrophil lysate (1:2 dilution in DMEM), or recombinant human neutrophil elastase (HNE, 500 nM, Abcam), in combination with or without microbial proteins of interest. Elafin (R&D systems) was used as a positive control. Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (FITC) conjugated Dextran 4000 MW was then added to the apical side, and epithelial permeability was measured in the basolateral side over a 6 h time-course by assessing FITC movement across the layer as an established surrogate of permeability. The fluorescent signal was measured using CLARIOstar microplate reader, and FITC Dextran concentration was calculated using a 4-parameter logistic standard curve.

Wound repair and cell death assay

Our in vitro wound repair assay was conducted on primary human colonic organoid cells. Organoids were passaged as described above and seeded into monolayers in sterile 96-well flat-bottom tissue culture plates at 1:4 ratio and grown to 90% confluency. Cells were then incubated for 24 h before they were mechanically scratched using the Essen Bioscience IncuCyte® WoundMaker - 96-pin wound-making tool (Essen Bioscience Inc.). The cells were then washed three times with DMEM media (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to remove traces of serum and cell debris, and treated with 100 nM HNE premixed with microbiota proteins or controls at various concentrations in differentiation media (advanced DMEM/F-12 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine, 0.5 mg/ml penicillin/streptomycin, 50 ng/ml hEGF and 10 μM Y-27632). Wound healing was monitored at 6 h intervals and analysed over 72 h using the automated IncuCyte® cell migration image capture and analysis software (Essen BioScience, Inc.). For the purpose of the cell death assay, cells were cultured and seeded as described above. Upon reaching 100% confluency, cells were washed twice and treated with 250 nM HNE premixed with microbiota proteins or controls at various concentrations, and cultured in differentiation media for 24 h. Images were taken every 3 h and confluency was measured using the automated IncuCyte® cell confluency image capture and analysis software (Essen BioScience, Inc.). At the 24 h time-point, cell metabolic activity was measured using CellTiter 96® Non-Radioactive Cell Proliferation Assay (MTT), according to manufacturer’s instructions (Promega).

Cell-free protease assay

The protocol was adopted from Mkaouar et al.33 Briefly, proteases (neutrophil lysate, neutrophil supernatant, purified enzymes) and microbiota proteins of interest were mixed in a flat-bottom 96-well plate, followed by substrate addition. Three protease substrates were used: broad-spectrum Mca-RPKPVE-Nval-WRK(Dnp)-NH2 (RnD biosystems), and elastase-specific p-Nitroanilide (Sigma) Suc-AAPF-pNA or MeOSuc-Ala-Ala-Pro-Val-AMC (Cayman Chemical). Substrate was added to a final concentration of 10 µM in assay buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 50 mM NaCl Buffer, pH 8). Absorbance (elastase-specific substrate) or fluorescence (broad-spectrum substrate, excitation 320–15 nm, emission at 405–20 nm) were measured at different timepoints at 37 °C. Raw relative fluorescence unit (RFU) values were normalised to the background control and expressed as percentage enzyme activity.

RT-PCR

Colon epithelial cells were cultured in ALI for 3–5 days, using 24-well plate transwell system, treated with the microbial proteins ± human neutrophil lysate (2x) for 6 h, then lysed in 1 mL TRIzol and stored at -80. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol/Chloroform/Isopropanol method. Briefly, 200 μL chloroform was added, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Aqueous layer was mixed with one volume of isopropanol and 5ug/ul glycogen, and incubated at −20 °C overnight. The pellet was washed in 80% ethanol, RNA was resuspended in 20 μL RNase-free water and quantified using Nanodrop™. One ug of RNA was converted to cDNA using SuperScript III™ reverse transcriptase, according to manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher Scientific). The cDNA was diluted 5x in RNase-free water and stored at −20 °C. RT PCR was carried out using 2 μL of cDNA and PowerUp™ Sybr™ Green Master Mix, according to manufacturer’s instructions (ThermoFisher Scientific), on CFX384 Touch RT PCR Detection System (BioRad). Two reference genes were used for normalisation: GAPDH and RPLP0; the expression levels were analysed using ddCt method, and expressed relative to the untreated group. The list of primers is shown in the Supplementary Table 1.

DSS murine colitis model

C57BL/6 male mice (6 to 8 weeks old) were obtained from Australian BioResources. All mice were kept at room temperature with 12-h light/dark cycles and free access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Animal Care and Ethics Committee at the University of Newcastle. After the one-week acclimatisation period, mice were treated with 2% DSS dissolved in drinking water for 6 days to induce colonic inflammation. Weights were monitored daily. HisBMG-1 (10 mg/kg) or vehicle control was administered intrarectally every 2 days. Mice were lightly anesthetised with isoflurane, and intrarectally injected using a 1.0 mL syringe with 100 μL of recombinant protein solution or vehicle. DSS-treated mice groups were euthanized on day 8, due to standard ethical requirements at 20% weight loss. The vehicle-treated mice group's endpoint was day 10. Faecal samples were collected at endpoint for protease activity measurement. Samples were weighed and dissolved in 1 ml resuspension buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.5) per 100 mg, homogenised by bead beating, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 10 min to remove the debris. Total protein content was quantified in the supernatant, using BCA assay, and samples were diluted to 200 µg/ml. The sample was then mixed with a broad-spectrum protease substrate (RnD biosystems) Mca-RPKPVE-Nval-WRK(Dnp)-NH2 at a final sample dilution 1:50, or elastase-specific substrate MeOSuc-Ala-Ala-Pro-Val-AMC at a final dilution of 1:2; and the protease activity assay was performed as described above. Colon length was measured, and colon tissue was harvested for histological assessment of damage. The DSS-induced epithelial damage was evaluated based on colon histopathology by H&E and PAS. The percentage of normal colon tissue that had no histological mucosal ulceration, no goblet cell loss, or loss of crypt architecture was measured as previously described.79

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software) was used for all statistical analysis. The data from the epithelial permeability assay, wound repair/cell confluency assay and cell-free protease assay were analysed using repeated measures two-way ANOVA or Friedman test and Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. In vivo DSS mouse model data were analysed using ordinary one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s multiple comparison test.
