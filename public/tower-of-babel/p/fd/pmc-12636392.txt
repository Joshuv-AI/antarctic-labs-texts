Multi-omics reveals dynamic succession and correlations between bacterial communities and metabolites in Luoyang mung bean sour during continuous propagation
Authors: Lifei Meng, Xunda Wang, Pengna Li, Lei Zuo, Qiqiong Li, Wentao Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Continuous propagation was employed to maintain the bacterial community of LMBS.•Lactobacillus and Acetobacter were the dominant genera in LMBS.•Tryptophan and arginine metabolism were the primary metabolic pathways in LMBS.•A connection network between metabolites and dominant genera were constructed.Keywords: LMBS, Dominant genus, Metabolomics, Metabolic pathway, Correlation

Introduction

Mung bean sour noodles (MBSN) are a traditional fermented food popular in Henan province, China, renowned for their appealing sour and umami flavors. Originating from the Han Dynasty, MBSN has become a signature dish of Luoyang, with Luoyang mung bean sour (LMBS) serving as the key raw material that imparts its distinctive flavor. The traditional preparation of LMBS involves mixing mung bean powder with distilled water in a specific ratio, filtering out fibers and other insoluble substances through sieving, and fermenting the mixture at room temperature (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Essentially, LMBS is a liquid-fermented mung bean product with both culinary and medicinal value, rich in nutrients such as proteins, dietary fibers, minerals, and vitamins (Wang et al., 2021a). Flavonoids, saponins, and polyphenols are abundant in mung bean seed coat that have significant health benefits caused by their escape from upper gastrointestinal digestion and absorption into blood plasma (Lee et al., 2011; Nirmala Sehrawat and Sharma, 2020). Mung beans also have a high protein content (20–32 %), which helps balance lysine deficiency in cereal grains, and offer antifungal, antibacterial, and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitory activities (Tarahi et al., 2024). However, there is a lack of research on the liquid fermentation of mung beans, particularly regarding the changes in the composition and bioactivity of nutrients, such as flavonoids, polysaccharides, organic acids, and proteins, after fermentation. Previous studies have employed specific strains to ferment mung beans. For instance, Liang et al. (2022) have claimed that the changes of physicochemical properties and volatile profiles of mung bean flour fermented by Lacticaseibacillus casei and Lactococcus lactis. Similarly, Yi and Wu have also demonstrated that fermentation with Lactobacillus. plantarum enhanced the flavor of mung beans by improving fruity and grassy notes while reducing the beany flavor (Tao et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2015; Yi et al., 2021).

Interestingly, soybeans, which have a similar composition to mung beans, have been fermented with beneficial strains (e.g., Pediococcus, Leuconostoc, Lactobacillus) to improve their nutritional and sensory qualities, reducing unpleasant flavors caused by aldehydes and ketones (An et al., 2023; Emkani et al., 2022; Tao et al., 2022). Fermenting raw materials with a single dominant strain offers high controllability and safety while improving flavor, but there is still a big gap comparing with the natural fermentation in flavor and nutrition, as seen in foods like baijiu, suancai, and dark tea, etc (Guan et al., 2020; Liang et al., 2020a). In natural fermentation of mung bean milk, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus coagulans, Bacillus licheniformis, Bacillus subtilis, Bacillus megaterium, Leuconostoc mesenteroides, and Lactobaillus plantarum were isolated using traditional methods, which often overlook the role of less advantageous bacterial genera (John and Olusegun, 2016). High-throughput sequencing has revealed that the predominant genera in Beijing douzhir include Lactococcus, Acetobacter, Streptococcus, and Lactobacillus. (Xu et al., 2018). Li et al. explored the microbial community and volatile compounds in Luoyang mung bean sour liquid, but the changes and correlations between bacteria communities and metabolites at different fermentation stages were not thoroughly examined (Li et al., 2024). Our previous studies identified dominant genera and flavor compounds during LMBS preparation, but lacked a focus on continuous propagation (Wang et al., 2024). High-throughput sequencing has proven valuable in studying traditional fermented foods, offering deeply insights into the succession of microbial community and guiding product development, as seen in kimchi, sour meat, and various fermented teas (Song et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2021). For example, Guan et al. examined microbial dynamics in Chinese Baijiu and Huang jiu during solid-state fermentation using high-throughput sequencing (Guan et al., 2020; Liang et al., 2020b). In addition, metabolomics has provided deeper insights into microbial metabolic activities, as it comprehensively characterizes and quantifies small-molecule metabolites, offering heightened sensitivity for studying metabolic pathways (Wang et al., 2021b; Yan et al., 2024). In fermented foods, metabolites such as free amino acids, organic acids, ketones, and aldehydes play critical roles as flavor precursors, nutrition, and tastes substance. Yan et al. demonstrated that metabolomic analysis improved dairy product quality fermented by Ligilactobacillus salivarius CCFM 1266 (Yan et al., 2024). The evaluation of bitter-tasting Krausen metabolites revealed that polyphenols do not contribute to bitter taste formation during beer fermentation, as demonstrated by UHPLC-Q/TOF-MS-based metabolomics. By combining these findings with data from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database and metabolic profiles, potential metabolic pathways were identified, which are significant for improving the quality of fermented foods. For example, in black tea—a typical fermented tea—the synthesis and degradation of taste substances are primarily associated with the biosynthetic pathways of flavonoids and secondary metabolites (Dong et al., 2024). Metabolites derived from legume fermentation often serve as key precursors to flavor compounds and exhibit enhanced bioactivity. However, the current lack of research on the correlation between dominant microbial communities and metabolites during mung bean fermentation severely impedes the development of fermented mung bean products.

Previous studies have investigated the flavor profile and microbial community of LMBS (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). Therefore, we propose a specific hypothesis that the dominant bacterial community may lead the transformation of metabolites such as organic acids and amino acids. Revealing the dynamics of microbial communities and metabolite profiles during continuous propagation is critical for related product development. Therefore, this study aimed to illustrate the succession and correlations between the bacterial community and metabolites in LMBS during continuous propagation, establishing a theoretical foundation for improving LMBS quality.

Materials and methods

Materials

Mung beans of the same variety and batch were purchased from the Jiu Lihu Cooperative (Baicheng, Jilin Province, China). The mass of 1000 mung bean grains was approximately 60.0 g, with a moisture content of about 8.5 %.

According to the traditional procedure from Luoyang, raw materials and pretreatment methods were followed (Wang et al., 2024). First, LMBS was prepared by mixing 0.6 kg of mung bean powder with 8 L of distilled water, stirring the mixture thoroughly, and filtering it through a sieve with a 0.8 mm mesh. Then, 4 L of LMBS was added as a starter culture to the mixture of 8 L mung bean slurry and distilled water, stirred, and fermented at 25 °C for 72 h. This process was repeated every 72 h, with samples collected on days 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18 during continuous propagation. The steps for continuous propagation of LMBS are illustrated in Fig. 1A. To reduce potential variability caused by natural microbial fluctuations, each sample was composed of mixtures from three randomly selected jars. At each sampling point, 30 mL of the sample was stored at −20 °C for metabolomics. Additionally, 10 mL of each sample was collected for bacterial diversity analysis on days 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, and 18. These samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C (10,000 × g), after which RNase-free PBS (Solarbio, China) was added. The mixture was vortexed for 5 min, centrifuged again, and the resulting sediment was stored at −80 °C for further analysis.

Quantitation of lactic acid bacteria and colonies, and analysis of titratable acidity (TTA) and pH

The pH and TTA of the samples were measured at the designated time points during continuous propagation, as described in previous studies (Yan et al., 2024). The pH was measured at 25 °C using a pH meter (Seven Easy plus S20p, METTLER TOLEDO, Swiss). TTA was determined by neutralizing the diluted sample with NaOH (0.1 mol/L) until the pH reached 8.4, and the volume of NaOH used was recorded. All experiments were conducted at least in triplicate.

To quantify lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and total colonies, the method described by Wang et al. was followed (Wang et al., 2020). LMBS was diluted with sterilized PBS to concentrations of 10−7, 10−8, and 10−9. A 10 μL aliquot of the diluted sample was evenly spread onto MRS (DeMan, Rogosa and Sharpe) medium and PCA (Plate Count Agar) medium in triplicate. All plates were incubated at 28 °C for 48 h for total colony counts, and at 36 °C for LAB enumeration. Colony counts were calculated accordingly.

Bacterial 16S rRNA sequencing and analysis

16S rRNA sequencing and analysis were conducted following the method outlined in a previous study (Wang et al., 2024). Genomic DNA was extracted from 3 replicates of each sample using the TIAN amp DNA Stool Kit (TIANGEN Biotech, catalog #DP328). The 16S V3-V4 region was amplified using universal primers 520F (5′-AY TGGGYDTAAAGNG-3′) and 802R (5′-TACNVGGGTATCTAATCC-3′), and sequencing was performed using the Miseq plarform (Illumina, USA). Amplification and sequencing of the 16S rDNA V4 hyper-variable region were performed by Majorbio Corporation (Shanghai, China).

Metabolomics

Extraction of metabolites

Metabolites were extracted following the method described in previous literature (Wang et al., 2021b). A 25 mg of sample (frozen dried LMBS power) was placed in a centrifuge tube (2 mL) with two abrasive bead (6 mm diameter). Then, 400 µL of extraction solvent (methanol: water = 4:1, v/v) containing the internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine, 0.02 mg/mL) was added. The mixture was processed in a cooling tissue grinder for 6 min (−10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasound extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 KHz). The treated sample was incubated at −20 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged for 15 min (13,000 g, 4 °C). The supernatant was transferred into an injection vial with internal cannula for further analysis (Yan et al., 2024). An equimolar volume of 20 μL from each sample was used to prepare quality control (QC) samples.

LC-MS condition

The samples were analyzed using a UHPLC -Q Exactive HF-X (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China) with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i. d., 1.8 µm; Waters, Milford, USA) for liquid chromatography. A 3 μL injection volume was used for each sample in positive or negative-ionization mode, with mobile phase A (0.1 % formic acid and 5 % acetonitrile in water) and mobile phase B (47.5 % acetonitrile, 47.5 % isopropyl alcohol, and 5 % waters) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. A non-linear gradient was applied as follows: 0–3 min, 0–20 % B; 3–4.5 min, 20–35 % B; 4.5–5 min, 35–100 % B; 5–6.4 min, 100 % B; 6.4–8 min, 0 % B for positive ionization mode. For negative ionization mode, the eluent procedure was: 0–1.2 min, 0–0.3 % B; 1.2–1.5 min, 3.3–5.0 % B; 1.5–2.0 min, 5–10 % B; 2–2.5 min, 10–13 % B; 2.5–4.5 min, 13–30 % B; 4.5–5.0 min, 30–100 % B; 5.0–6.3 min, 100 % B; 6.3–6.4 min, 0–100 %; 6.4–8.0 min, 0 % B. The experiment parameters for UHPLC -Q Exactive HF-X were performed. Samples were ionized by electrospray, and the mass spectrum (MS) signals were collected in both positive and negative ion scanning modes respectively. The MS parameters were as follows: scan type (m/z), 70–1050; sheath gas flow rate (arb), 50; aux gas flow rate (arb), 13; spray voltage (±), 3500 V; capillary temperature, 325 °C; heater temperature, 425 °C; resolution (Full MS), 60,000; resolution (MS2), 7500.

Metabolomics processing and identification

The raw data were imported into the metabolomics processing software Progenesis QI (Waters Corporation, Milford, USA) for line filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, peak alignment, and so on. The resulting data matrix included retention time, mass to charge ratio, and peak intensity. The software was then used to identify characteristic peaks based on the database, combining MS and MS/MS mass spectra with a metabolic database. The mass accuracy for MS was set to less than 10 ppm. Metabolites were identified according to the secondary mass spectrometry matching score. In this study, metabolite identification was performed using a set of rigorously defined parameters to ensure high confidence in annotation. The minimum peak height was set at 5000 counts, while a signal-to-noise ratio of at least 10 was required for peak inclusion. For MS/MS-based identification, a spectral matching score threshold of 0.7 or higher was applied, based on consensus matching of m/z, retention time, and fragmentation patterns against major metabolomic databases including HMDB, MoNA, and NIST. The main databases used included https://www.hmdb.ca, https://metlin.scripps.edu, and a self-built database. Metabolic analyses were conducted using the following software: ropls (R_packages, version1.6.2), KEGG kegg_v20221012 (Release 2017-05-01), scipy (Python, Version1.0.0).

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical significance between samples was determined using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test in Origin 2021. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The network of dominant genera and metabolites was visualized using Cytoscape.

Result and discussion

Changes in biochemical and physicochemical indices

For lactic fermentation foods, low pH and acidity significantly affected flavor, taste, and shelf life (Guan et al., 2020). The continuous propagation process of LMBS, following traditional methods, was shown in Fig. 1A. Fig. 1B illustrated the changes in pH and TTA. From day 3 (D3) to day 18 (D18), both pH and TTA of LMBS showed no significant changes, remaining between 3.60–3.75 and 1.8–1.9, respectively. Overall, pH and TTA exhibited a stable trend. However, some fluctuations were observed, particularly between D3 and D9, which were likely due to variations in microbial composition and metabolic activity during this stage. In Fig. 1C, between days 6 and 12, both the total colony count and LAB count initially increased and then decreased. Meanwhile, the pH on day 6 was significantly lower than on days 3 and 9, which may be related to the consistently high-acidity environment affecting the microbial community. This suggests a major shift in the community composition during this period. On the whole, the total number of LAB and colonies remained stable integrally, suggesting that the microbial community probably stayed consistent throughout the continuous propagation, similar to what was observed in fermented rice wine (Seo et al., 2016). However, the underlying changes in the microbial community was essential to investigate by high-throughput sequencing.

Bacterial community characteristics during the continuous propagation of LMBS

The Chao, ACE, Sob, Shannon, and Simpson indices were used to assess the α-diversity of the bacterial community throughout the continuous propagation of LMBS (Table 1). The ACE, Sob, and Chao indices showed a significant downward trend over the entire propagation period, with a notable decrease from D15 to D18, indicating a clear reduction in bacterial richness and the increasing prominence of dominant bacteria. Similarly, a higher Shannon index or lower Simpson index typically reflected greater microbial flora diversity (Zhong et al., 2021). The declining Shannon index and rising Simpson index in this study were consistent with the trends observed in the ACE and Chao indices, supporting the conclusion that microbial diversity decreased over time. These changes, particularly noticeable at D9 and D15, suggested a continuous decline in bacterial community diversity. This reduction was likely due to the prolonged low pH and the inhibition of harmful strains by antibiotics secreted by LAB (Anyogu et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2015; Sun et al., 2023). As depicted in Supplement-B, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were the absolutely dominant phyla during continuous propagation. Lactobacillus became the dominant genus, accounting for 85 % of the bacterial community, and overwhelmed other genera, such as Leuconostoc (0.20 %), Lactococcus (1.19 %), and Acetobacter (8.01 %) at the genus level (Fig. 2A). For fermented food, lactic acid bacteria play a leading role according to specific succession patterns. At the genus level, Leuconostoc and Weisseria, both heterolactic fermenters, thrived during the early stages of fermentation in a mildly acidic environment, whereas Lactobacillus and Pediococcus, which engage in homolactic fermentation, predominated in the later stages, where acidity was higher. However, LAB did not fully replace other less favorable genera, such as Bacillus, Pseudomonas, Citrobacter, and Klebsiella, in the bacterial community (John and Olusegun, 2016; Wang et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2024). Fig. 2B illustrates the percentage changes of dominant species, including Lactobacillus. plantarum, Leuconostoc. citreum, Pediococcus. pentosaceus, and Acetobacter. orientalis, etc. Lactobacillus. plantarum was the dominant species throughout, increasing from 30.45 % to 79.23 %, and subsequently stabilizing (Supplement-C). Due to the inhibition by dominant strains and the regulation of a long-term highly acidic environment, the percentages of L. citreum and P. pentosaceus significantly decreased, from 29.92 % to 0.02 % and from 4.33 % to 0 %, respectively, during continuous propagation. Notably, the proportion of Acetobacter. orientalis increased from 0 % to 8 %, gradually becoming the second most dominant bacterial group. According to the previous report (Hossain et al., 2023), the symbiotic fermentation between A. orientalis and LAB elevated the content of lactobionic acid in yogurt, proving that these two dominant species could coexist peacefully, which is consistent with our findings. Similar results were observed in other fermented products, such as Shanxi aged vinegar and Laal dahi, where A. orientalis initiated alcohol fermentation, while LAB took over the subsequent stages (Leal Maske et al., 2024; Shangpliang and Tamang, 2023). However, the mechanism by which A. orientalis and L. plantarum dominated fermentation requires further analysis through metabolomics or other methods.

Fig. 2C and 2D represent the principal component analysis (PCA) of samples over time, with PC1 (91.75 %) and PC2 (3.96 %) at the genus level, and PC1 (91.19 %) and PC2 (4.02 %) at the species level. The microbiota composition of D6 and D9 was clearly distinct from that of other periods at the genus level. In contrast, samples from D3, D12, D15, and D18 were close or even overlapped in the PCA, suggesting a similar composition of the bacterial community at the genus level. At the species level, samples from the later stages (D9, D12, D15, and D18) differed from those in the early stages (D3 and D6), but were consistent with each other, indicating that the bacterial community composition became more stable in the later stages of continuous propagation. As the competition among microorganisms continued, Lactobacillus and Acetobacter gradually established dominance and suppressed other less favorable strains, which corresponds to previous studies (Wang et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2020b). However, it is worth noting that the proportion of lactobacillus reached 85 % after continuou propagation, which is far greater than the proportion of 35 % in the preparation stage(Wang et al., 2024). This result suggests that the next isolation and purification of Lactobacillus is of great significance for the preparation of single bacteria starter culture for LMBS. Continous propagation is the responsible approach to keep the quality of LMBS.

Metabolic profiles

Changes in metabolites during continuous propagation

To comprehensively understand the changes in metabolites during continuous propagation, we analyzed the differential metabolites in the LMBS samples (D6, D12, and D18), which effectively represent the entire fermentation process, based on the results of Fig. 1. D6, D12, and D18 represented the primary, middle, and post phases, respectively, during the continuous propagation. In the comparison of D6, D12, and D18 samples, a total of 2379 differential metabolites were identified, with 2170 metabolites common to all samples. The number of unique metabolites in D6, D12, and D18 was 10, 31, and 19, respectively (Fig. 3A). The metabolite profiles of LMBS were analyzed by PCA, which showed PC1 (54.00 %) and PC2 (26.50 %). As shown in Fig. 3B, the distribution of samples in each group was relatively scattered, indicating significant differences among the groups. In the comparison of D6 and D12 samples (Fig. 3C), a total of 2355 metabolites were identified, with 503 up-regulated, 1605 unchanged, and 247 down-regulated, suggesting relatively vigorous metabolism (Wang et al., 2021b). From D6 to D12, peptides were significantly up-regulated, such as Ser-Asn-Thr-Ile-Leu, Lys-Ile-Trp, Gly-Tyr-His, and Arg-Ile-His, which are key taste compounds and are also present in soy sauce, cheese, fermented meats, and bread (Zhao et al., 2016). Down-regulated metabolites included metyrapol, Ile-Val-Val, and N-Acetylmuramoyl-Ala (Fig. 3C and Supplement-D). As shown in Fig. 3D, 2355 metabolites were identified, consisting of 254 up-regulated, 456 down-regulated, and 1645 unchanged in expression between D12 and D18. The number of up-regulated metabolites was significantly lower than the down-regulated ones, suggesting a competitive balance within the bacterial community, a slowdown in metabolic activity, and stabilization of metabolic pathways and the growth environment (Song et al., 2021). According to Supplement D and E, the metabolites with significantly down-regulated VIP scores were primarily peptides (Ph-leu, Ala-Val-Leu, Ile-Val-Val, Valyltyrosine). The up-regulated metabolites mainly included dihydromaleimide-beta-d-glucoside, Pro-Phe-Arg-MCA, homopisatin, and d-Gulono-1, 4-lactone. Whether up-regulated or down-regulated, the most significant changes were observed in peptides, indicating that the dominant metabolic pathway was focused on protein degradation and amino acid metabolism.

The relative content of the top 30 metabolites was analyzed using a heatmap (Fig. 3E), revealing clear differences among the three groups. D3 exhibited the highest number of metabolites, including peptides (gamma-glutamyl-S-methylcysteine, gamma-Glu-Leu), acids (indoleacrylic acid, tricarballylic acid), polysaccharides (neocathamin, neoisoastilbin, chelidonine), and flavonoids (vitexin, isovitexin).Conversely, the levels of these metabolites decreased from D12 to D18. According to previous reports (Zhang et al., 2022), LAB was unabled to synthesize many amino acids, vitamins, and nucleic acids, leading it to degrade proteins to meet its reproductive requirements. The increase in flavonoid content in LMBS after fermentation was consistent with other studies, such as the case of apigenin (Emkani et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). In addition, it is worth noting that noopept, isoleucylproline, lactucin, and phenyllactic acid showed higher content at D12. The characteristic metabolites highlighted several acids, such as methylmalonic acid, trihydroxystearic acid, and phenyllactic acid, which are well known for their ability to inhibit harmful bacteria, particularly phenyllactic acid (Rajanikar et al., 2021). Fig. 3F illustrated the correlation among the 30 top metabolites, showing that compounds of the same type had positive correlations, while those of different types exhibited negative correlations. For instance, flavonoids, such as vitexin and isovitexin, displayed a strong positive correlation within their group and the negative correlation with acids like phenyllactic acid and trihydroxystearic acid. To better understand the relationships and the generative mechanism between these metabolites, further insights from metabolic pathways are essential.

The dominant metabolic pathway of LMBS during continuous propagation

To reveal the metabolic pathways associated with differential metabolites, KEGG enrichment was employed to analyze these metabolites in LMBS across varying fermentation durations. As shown in Fig. 4A, differential metabolites were primarily enriched in metabolic pathways, with notable quantities in amino acid metabolism (39), biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites (34), chemical structure transformation maps (27), lipid metabolism (23), xenobiotics biodegradation and metabolism (19), and carbohydrate metabolism (14) from D6 to D12. Fig. 4B illustrated that during D12 to D18, differential metabolites were mainly enriched in amino acid metabolism (34), biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites (33), chemical structure transformation maps (25), lipid metabolism (21), carbohydrate metabolism (18), and xenobiotics biodegradation and metabolism (15). Overall, during the continuous propagation of LMBS, the dominant metabolic pathways were concentrated in amino acid metabolism and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, consistent with previous studies (Emkani et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024; Zhong et al., 2021).

Pathway enrichment based on KEGG is extensively employed in the analysis of metabolic profiles to gain biological insights into the functional roles of metabolites through KEGG topology analysis (Ma et al., 2019). We selected the top 20 metabolic pathways predominantly enriched for differential metabolites in the D6 VS D12 comparison (Fig. 4C and Supplement G), including tryptophan metabolism, betalain biosynthesis, cutin, suberine and wax biosynthesis, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, and arginine biosynthesis, etc (p < 0.05, and impact value >0.3). Betalains, unique water-soluble pigments, were synthesized and regulated via pathways derived from tyrosine (Zhao et al., 2022). Flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, including the production of quercetin and apigenin, was mediated by Actinomycete Streptomyces (Lou et al., 2021). However, between D12 and D18 (Fig. 4D), three amino acid metabolic pathways—tryptophan metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, and alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism—were observed as the main differential metabolic pathway. Arginine biosynthesis contributed to maintaining high levels of aspartate, which replaced glutamate, enhancing the umami taste(Nüse et al., 2023). Additionally, alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism were enhanced, leading to an increase in citric acid levels and a decrease in L-glutamate, which aligned with previous findings (Qiao et al., 2018).

Correlations between dominant bacteria and metabolic profiles

Based on Pearson’s correlation coefficient, we explored the interaction between dominant bacteria and key metabolites, as shown in Fig. 5 and Supplement F (|r| > 0.7). Notably, Acetobacter and Lactobacillus contributed to the formation of organic acids (e.g., suberic acid, adipic acid, malonic acid, and glutaric acid, etc) while reducing the content of nucleic acids, such as uridine and guanine (Fig. 5A). Acetobacter and Lactobacillus showed negative correlations with isocitric acid, malic acid, and pimelic acid(Fig. 5A), suggesting suppression of the tricarboxylic acid cycle in these bacteria (r < −0.70) (Seo et al., 2016). On the contrary, Pediococcus and Leuconostoc exhibited negative correlations with suberic acid, adipic acid, and glutaric acid (r < −0.70). As shown in Fig. 3B, those bacterial abundance changes indicated that these dominant species were intolerant to harsh acidity, consistent with previous studies (Xu et al., 2024). Additionally, Acetobacter and Lactobacillus were negatively correlated with L-asparagine, L-dopa, citrulline, L-tryptophan, ornithine, and L-histidine, while showing positive correlations with histamine, L-threonine, and putrescine (r > |0.70|), (Fig. 5B). As high-quality protein resources, Glu and L-asparagine are the most abundant amino acids in mung beans and are preferentially utilized during the development of Lactobacillus. Carbohydrate metabolism is a crucial source of energy for microbial growth. In the fermentation of Luoyang mung bean sour (LMBS), the number of dominant microbial genera positively correlated with carbohydrate metabolism includes Lactobacillus (3) and Acetobacter (5). Lactobacillus and Acetobacter showed positive correlations with sucrose and L-ribulose, while they were negatively correlated with N-acetylmannosamine, gluconic acid, and 3-deoxy-D-manno-octulosonate (Fig. 5C). These changes were primarily attributed to polysaccharide hydrolysis, disaccharide accumulation, and monosaccharide consumption (Hu et al., 2020). Mung beans are rich in flavonoids and isoflavone, which have anti-inflammatory, cancer- preventive, and cardiovascular disease, etc (Lee et al., 2011; Wu et al., 2015). A strong negative correlation (r < −0.70) was observed between most flavonoids (e.g., vicenin-2, vitexin, orientin, and neocarthamin) and the dominant strains (Lactobacillus and Acetobacter) (Fig. 5D). This correlation may be because flavonoids are transformed into more biologically active substances, such as antibiotics, which inhibit competing strains. Indeed, antibiotics are critical in microbial competition due to their broad-spectrum activity and low toxicity, making them valuable in medicine and food preservation. Accordingly, Lactobacillus and Acetobacter showed a strong negative correlation with antibiotics like gentamicin C and clavulanate (r < −0.70), but a strong positive correlation with cycloheximide (r > 0.70) (Fig. 5C). As the disadvantaged species, Pediococcus and Leuconostoc were negatively correlated with cycloheximide and positively correlated with clavulanate and gentamicin C. The results indicated that each dominant bacterium employs specific antibiotics for microbial competition. For example, clavulanate and gentamicin C are associated with Pediococcus and Leuconostoc, while cycloheximide is linked to Lactobacillus and Acetobacter. This multivariate analysis revealed relationships between dominant bacteria and metabolites; however, the underlying mechanisms require further investigation using molecular biology techniques. Regarding high concentrations of peptides, in protein-rich fermentation systems such as soy paste and cheese, the abundance of peptides and free amino acids generated through microbial protease-mediated degradation of proteins may indeed alter the ecological niche. Furthermore, in certain fermented environments like kefir and cheese, bacteriocins or other antimicrobial compounds produced by microbial communities are widely recognized as powerful drivers of microbial selection. For instance, bacteriocins such as nisin, produced by lactic acid bacteria, can effectively inhibit the growth of Gram-positive bacteria—including spoilage or pathogenic organisms such as certain Clostridium and Listeria species—thereby profoundly reshaping community structure and creating a micro-environment more favorable to the producers themselves.

Conclusion

This study investigated the changes in bacterial community and metabolic profiles during the continuous propagation of LMBS. The bacterial community evolved over time, with Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc and, Acetobacter, becoming the dominant species, particularly after Day 9. These bacteria suppressed the growth of less favorable species, such as Pediococcus and certain Leuconostoc. For metabolic profiles, the primary pathways involved were protein degradation and amino acid metabolism, leading to an increase in amino acids, peptides, and other metabolites. Notable metabolic pathways included tryptophan metabolism, cutin, suberine, and flavonoid biosynthesis, and arginine biosynthesis, all of which were strongly correlated with the dominant bacterial species. The key metabolites were classified into five groups—organic acids, peptides, flavonoids, antibiotics, and carbohydrates—and showed a strong correlation with and potential regulatory effect on the dominant bacteria. This study provides valuable insights into the changes in bacterial communities and metabolic profiles in LMBS. It should be noted that this study has certain limitations, primarily the lack of necessary control groups, with comparisons made only between experimental samples. This aspect will be addressed in future research. The present work preliminarily elucidates the microbial community and metabolic profile during mung bean fermentation, providing a theoretical foundation for understanding flavor formation in other legume-based fermented foods and developing starter cultures. Furthermore, this study offers deeper insights into the mechanisms of dominant microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria, in legume fermentation, thereby supporting their potential industrial applications.
