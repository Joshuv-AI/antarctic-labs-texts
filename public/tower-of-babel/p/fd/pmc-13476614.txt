The p53–CerS6 interaction on the ER integrates p53 and sphingolipid signaling pathways in metabolic stress response
Authors: Madeline S Childress, Kristen A Jeffries, David A Horita, Sergey A Krupenko, Natalia I Krupenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCeramides, a diverse class of bioactive sphingolipids, play a pivotal role in cellular stress response. We have previously reported a novel mechanism by which a particular member of this class, C16-ceramide, activates death pathways in cancer cells. In this mechanism, C16-ceramide, generated by ceramide synthase 6 (CerS6), binds directly to the tumor suppressor p53 and protects it from MDM2-mediated degradation, thereby promoting a stress response. In the present study, we investigated the mechanism by which p53 acquires ceramide, a highly hydrophobic molecule. Using bimolecular fluorescence complementation, we show that in cells under metabolic stress, p53 is recruited to the cytoplasmic surface of the ER by CerS6, the ER resident enzyme generating C16-ceramide. The direct contact between the two proteins enables the transfer of C16-ceramide from CerS6 to the DNA-binding domain of p53. Domain deletion experiments and pulldown assays with purified recombinant cytoplasmic fragments of CerS6 showed that the p53 physically interacts with the two large cytoplasmic loops of CerS6, forming a complex on the surface of the ER membrane. The interaction between p53 and CerS6 strictly requires the presence of C16-ceramide in the catalytic site of the enzyme. Substitution of the CerS6 lumenal loop, which defines the enzyme’s acyl chain specificity, with the corresponding loop of CerS2, which generates very-long-chain ceramides, abolished the interaction with p53. Our results uncover a previously unknown process, the direct C16-ceramide transfer to the p53 DNA-binding domain at the ER surface, which is a mechanism regulating activation of this tumor suppressor. The findings that this mechanism is triggered upon metabolic stress induction by specific chemotherapeutics, as well as by nutrient and growth factor deprivation, highlight it as a promising target for cancer treatment.Supplementary key words: CerS6, ceramides, endoplasmic reticulum, MTX, p53 activation, sphingolipid signaling

Ceramides, central sphingolipid metabolites, are critical mediators of cellular stress responses playing pivotal roles in the regulation of fundamental cellular processes such as apoptosis, autophagy, and cell cycle arrest (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). They are generated through both de novo and salvage pathways and comprise a structurally diverse family of lipids, defined by distinct sphingoid bases linked via amide bonds to acyl chains of varying lengths and structures (2, 7). Ceramides are synthesized by a family of six endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-resident ceramide synthases (CerS1–6) which transfer acyl chains from acyl-CoAs to sphingoid bases. Each CerS exhibits distinct acyl chain-length preferences (5, 8, 9), with exception of CerS5 and 6, that have overlapping specificities. Ceramides are further metabolized by more than two-dozen enzymes to yield over 200 structurally distinct compounds (2). The biological effects of ceramides are highly context-dependent (2); their accumulation has been linked to diverse cellular stresses, including nutrient deprivation, growth factor withdrawal, and treatment with chemotherapeutics (6, 7, 8, 9). Of note, accumulation of particular ceramides can elicit either pro-survival or pro-death responses, thereby shaping cellular adaptation to metabolic and environmental challenges (6, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14). The exact mechanisms of these perplexing effects are not well understood but are thought to arise from direct interactions of ceramides with specific signaling proteins including the PP2A inhibitor I2PP2A (15), protein kinase C (16, 17, 18), and kinase suppressor of Ras (19). Importantly, different ceramide species regulate distinct cellular pathways, underscoring the high specificity of their signaling response.

We have previously reported a functional link between folate-related metabolic stress—induced by folate withdrawal, overexpression of the folate-regulatory enzyme aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 family member L1, or antifolate drugs—and C16-ceramide signaling (20, 21, 22, 23). Importantly, this mechanism of stress response requires functional p53 (20). The p53 tumor suppressor, a transcription factor regulating cellular proliferation, survival, and death, is commonly activated by metabolic stress (24). In cancer cells, such stress triggered by disruption of one-carbon metabolism led to p53-dependent elevation of CerS6, accumulation of C16-ceramide and apoptosis (20, 21). We have shown that complex mechanism underlying these events is comprised of two key components (22, 23): the transcriptional activation of CerS6 expression by p53, which directly binds to the CerS6 promoter (22); and the activation of p53 itself through direct binding of C16-ceramide, elevated upon increased CerS6. Binding of C16-ceramide induces conformational changes in p53, which prevent the formation of the p53–MDM2 complex, thus protecting p53 from ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation (23). Of note, the CerS6–(C16-ceramide)–p53 axis functions as a self-enhancing loop, amplifying the cellular stress response.

While the above studies expanded our understanding of the ceramide regulatory mechanisms, a fundamental question remained: how does p53, a cytosolic/nuclear protein, gain access to C16-ceramide, a hydrophobic membrane component? We hypothesized that, under stress, p53 directly engages the ER-resident CerS6 on the cytoplasmic leaflet of the ER membrane, enabling the channeling of newly synthesized C16-ceramide from the CerS6 active site directly to p53. This model is supported by our earlier observations that p53 protein, pulled down from stressed cells, carries C16-ceramide bound within the DNA-binding domain (DBD). Furthermore, our published studies have shown that ceramide-dependent activation of p53 is specific to CerS6 since C16-ceramide generated by the homologous enzyme CerS5 does not activate the tumor suppressor (23). Overall, these data strongly suggest the direct ceramide transfer from CerS6 to p53.

Here, we employed bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) approach to demonstrate that p53 and CerS6 directly interact on the cytosolic surface of the ER upon induction of metabolic stress. This interaction is accompanied by reversible ER remodeling causing the formation of discrete ER aggregates. The interaction of p53 specifically with the two largest cytosolic loops of CerS6 was further confirmed by the pull-down of p53 from cell lysates by the purified recombinant Glutathione S-Transferase (GST)-tagged cytosolic fragments of CerS6 on glutathione-agarose. We further combined BiFC with domain mapping/swapping for both CerS6 and p53, as well as site-directed mutagenesis, to identify key structural/functional elements required for the interaction between the two proteins and for the ceramide transfer. Our study provides insight into the novel mechanism of the p53 activation by ceramide upon metabolic stress and raises the possibility of targeting this mechanism for cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

A549 human lung adenocarcinoma (ATCC CCL-185) and PC-3 human prostate adenocarcinoma (ATCC CRL-1435) cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. A549 cells with stable shRNA-mediated knockdown of p53 (A549 p53−/−) were described previously (25). A549 and PC-3 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Cat. #11875-093) supplemented with 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco Cat. #25030-081), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco, Cat. #11360-070), and 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Cat. #26140-079). A549 p53−/− cells were cultured under the same conditions with the addition of 0.5 μg/ml puromycin (Gibco, #A11138-03). All cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell passages 5–20 were used for all experiments.

Drug treatments

Pyridinium C16-ceramide (PyrC16; D-erythro-2-N-[16′-(1′-pyridinium)-hexadecanoyl]-sphingosine bromide) was synthesized by the MUSC Lipidomics Shared Resource/Synthetic Core (http://www.hollingscancercenter.org/research/shared-resources/lipidomics/index.html). A 1 mM stock was prepared in DMSO and stored at −20°C. Unless otherwise indicated, for stress induction, PyrC16 was used at 5 μm for 18 h. Methotrexate (MTX, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. #454126) was dissolved in sterile water (10 mM stock) and applied at 10 nM for 24 h. Lometrexol (LTX, 6R-5,10-Dideazatetrahydrofolate), a generous gift from Dr Joseph Bertino, was dissolved in sterile water and used at 1 μm for 24 h. For serum starvation experiments, cells were maintained in medium containing 0.2% (v/v) FBS for 48 h.

Transient transfection and cloning

Cells were seeded at the density of 1 × 105 cells/well in Nunc Lab-Tek II Chamber Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #154526) and transfected with the corresponding expression vectors after 24 h using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #L3000008) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Unless otherwise stated, 1 μg DNA was used per 1 × 106 cells.

The mCherry-p53 expression construct was generated by PCR amplification of the p53 coding sequence followed by subcloning into a pmCherry-C1 vector (Addgene, Cat. #58476). Primers were designed to introduce 5′ XhoI and 3′ BclI restriction sites for the p53 coding sequence insertion at the 3′-end of mCherry. PCR products and vector were digested with XhoI and BclI, purified, and ligated using T4 DNA ligase. The GFP-CerS6 construct was generated by subcloning the CerS6 coding sequence from pCMV/CerS6 used in our previous study (22) into pEGFP vector (Addgene, Cat. #165830) downstream of GFP. For ER localization studies, GFP-Calreticulin (CalR) was expressed using pCMV6-AC-GFP/CALR vector (OriGene, Cat. #RG203222).

For BiFC assays, equimolar amounts of V1- and V2-fused constructs were cotransfected. BiFC plasmids (pCS2/V1-p53 and pCS2/MDM2-V2) were a generous gift from Prof. Cecilia Rodrigues (26). CerS1-5 coding sequences were PCR-amplified from template vectors pcDNA3.1/CerS2-HA, pcDNA3.1/CerS3-HA, pcDNA3.1/CerS4-HA, pcDNA3.1/CerS5-HA, and pCMV2b/FLAG-CerS1 (generous gifts from Prof. Anthony Futerman). pCMV-CerS6 vector has been described previously (27). Primers (Supplemental Table S1) were designed to introduce 5′ AFlIII and 3′ XhoI restriction sites (excluding the stop codon). PCR products and the recipient vector pCS2/MDM2-V2 were digested with AFlIII and Xhol and ligated using T4 DNA ligase.

Site-directed mutagenesis was performed using the QuikChange Lightning Kit (Agilent Technologies, Cat. #210518) to generate CerS6(H212A), Chim.11, Chim.15, △ 324–384 and △ 354–384, the CerS6–61aa C-terminal deletion and CerS6–33aa C-terminal deletion, p53(1–300), p53(101–393), and p53(101–300) constructs. In the chimeric CerS6 constructs (Chim.11, Chim.15) the third lumenal loop conferring acyl-CoA chain-length specificity was replaced with the corresponding region from CerS2. Chim.11 has an eleven amino-acid substitution based on previous work (28), while Chim.15 has fifteen amino-acids replaced based on alternative prediction of the third lumenal loop (29). Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S1. All constructs were verified by sequencing (Eurofins Genomics).

siRNA transfection

Cells were seeded 24 h prior to transfection with siRNAs targeting CerS5 or CerS6 (Eurofins Genomics), or a nontargeting scrambled siRNA with medium GC content (Invitrogen) as a control, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #L3000008) according to manufacturer’s instructions. siRNA sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S2. siRNAs were used at a final concentration of 20 nM. Cells were incubated for 36 h after transfection, before the further treatments or subsequent transfections. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by immunoblotting (Supplemental Fig. S4A, B).

Fluorescence microscopy and BiFC imaging

Cells were seeded in Nunc Lab-Tek II Chamber Slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #154526) and 24 h later were transfected with indicated vectors. For stress induction, cells were treated with corresponding stressor 12 h post-transfection and fixed 18–24 h after treatment. For nonstressed/untreated conditions, cells were fixed 36 h post-transfection. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (10 min, RT), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (10 min, RT) and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS (60 min, RT). Further, slides were incubated with the following primary antibodies (overnight, 4°C, 1:300: anti-CalR (rabbit polyclonal; Invitrogen, Cat. #PA3-900), anti-Tom20 (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. #SC-17764), anti-Lamin A + C (mouse monoclonal; Novus Biologicals, Cat. #NBP1-97687), anti-p53 (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. #sc-126), anti-FXR1 (mouse monoclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #72105).

After washing with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20, slides were incubated in the dark with corresponding secondary antibodies (60 min, RT, 1:300): donkey anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 (Life Technologies, Cat. #A31572), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 568 (Life Technologies, Cat. #A11031), or chicken anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (Life Technologies, Cat. #A21463). Slides were mounted and nuclei were counterstained with ProLong Diamond Antifade with DAPI (Invitrogen, Cat. #P36962) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus Fluoview FV10i-LIV confocal microscope with the following detection channels: DAPI, Fluo-3 (BiFC signal), Red-Narrow (Alexa Fluor 555), and Far-Red (Alexa Fluor 647). Identical acquisition settings were used for all experimental groups. Images were collected at 60×, 140×, or 160× magnification, as indicated in the corresponding figure legends. Colocalization analysis was performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient calculated with Olympus FV10-ASW software.

Expression and purification of GST-CerS6 fragments

Cytosolic fragments of CerS6 (residues 56–134 or 324–384) were cloned into the pET-42a expression vector to generate N-terminally GST-tagged constructs. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental Table S1. The resulting plasmids were used to transform E. coli BL21(DE3) cells, which then were grown overnight at 37°C on LB agar plates containing 50 μg/ml kanamycin. A single colony was inoculated into 5 ml LB medium supplemented with kanamycin and cultured overnight at 37°C. This starter culture was diluted into 500 ml of LB-kanamycin medium and grown at 37°C until OD600 reached 0.7, at which point protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM IPTG (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and cells were grown for 12 h at 25°C.

Cells were harvested by centrifugation (5,000g, 10 min, 4°C), resuspended in 50 ml of 50 mM NaH2PO4 buffer, pH 8.0, containing 300 mM NaCl, and incubated with 0.2 mg/ml lysozyme for 30 min at 4°C. The suspension was chilled on ice, sonicated, and clarified by centrifugation (10,000g, 40 min, 4°C). The supernatant was applied to a GSTrap FF column (GE Healthcare) connected to ÄKTA Pure chromatography system and equilibrated with 50 mM Tris, 200 mM NaCl, pH 8.0 buffer. After loading, the column was washed with the equilibration buffer, then a 50 mM Tris, 200 mM NaCl, 10 mM glutathione, pH 8.0 buffer was used for protein elution.

GST pull-down assays

A549 cells transfected with the pcDNA3.1-p53 plasmid (WT) were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in IP buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche) for 30 min at 4°C. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (14,000g, 15 min, 4°C), and the supernatants were incubated overnight at 4°C with 5 μg of GST-tagged CerS6 protein (GST alone as control) prebound to Pierce Glutathione Superflow Agarose (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Resin was pelleted by centrifugation and washed twice with cold PBS. Bound proteins were eluted with 10 mM glutathione buffer and analyzed by SDS-PAGE followed by Western blotting. Antibodies used included anti-GST (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. #2624, 1:1000) to confirm fusion protein recovery and anti-p53 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. #sc-126, 1:1000) to detect interacting protein.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA buffer supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. #23227) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Equal protein amounts were loaded for each sample. Protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes for immunoblotting. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-p53 (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. #sc-126, 1:1,000), anti-CalR (rabbit polyclonal; Invitrogen, Cat. #PA3-900, 1:1,000), anti-Lamin A + C (mouse monoclonal; Novus Biologicals, Cat. #NBP1-97687, 1:1,000), anti-GAPDH, (mouse monoclonal; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat. Sc-32233, 1:1,000) anti-GST (rabbit; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. #SAB4301139, 1:1,000), anti-CerS5 (rabbit polyclonal; Abnova, Cat. # PAB13439, 1:1,000), and anti-CerS6 (rabbit; in-house, 1:2,000). Species-specific secondary antibodies used included sheep anti-mouse, HRP-conjugated (GE Healthcare Lifesciences, Cat. #NA931, 1:5,000) and donkey anti-rabbit, HRP-conjugated (GE Healthcare Lifesciences, Cat. #NA934, 1:5,000). For protein band visualization, membranes were incubated with HRP substrate reagent (GE Healthcare), the excess reagent was blotted with filter paper and luminescent bands were detected/quantified using an Odyssey Fc scanner (LI-COR Biosciences). Band intensities were calculated using ImageJ software (NIH). Protein loading was assessed using actin (Abcam, Cat. #ab49900, 1:20,000) or total protein staining (Coomassie; Bio Rad, Cat. #1610786).

Lipidomics analysis

For all sphingolipid measurements, 1 × 107 cells were grown for 24 h, collected, and stored at −80°C. Lipids extraction (the Bligh and Dyer method) and analysis using the LC–MS/MS (Sciex TripleTOF 5600) were performed by the MUSC Lipidomics Shared Resource Analytic core, according to published procedure (30). Quantification of C14 through C26-ceramides was done using internal deuterated standards. Lipid classes and species abundance were normalized to inorganic phosphate content. Ceramide identities were confirmed using retention times and MS/MS fragmentation patterns from analytical standards.

Preparation of cell nuclei for lipidomics analysis

A549 cells were treated with vehicle control, C16-pyridinium ceramide (PyrC16, 5 μm), or MTX, 10 nM for 24 h. Following the treatment, cells were subjected to osmotic shock for 5 min on ice and broken down in a Dounce glass homogenizer. The crude nuclear fraction was obtained by differential centrifugation of cell homogenates followed by three washing rounds. The obtained nuclear fractions were used for lipidomics analysis as described above.

Expression of the chimeric CerS6 proteins in cultured cells

PC-3 and A549 p53−/− cells were transiently transfected with equimolar mixtures of two plasmids, one V1-p53 and the other either CerS6-V2, Chim.11-V2, or Chim.15-V2, using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen). Control cells were mock-transfected (no plasmid). After 24 h, cells were harvested, and ceramide levels measured as above.

Modeling CerS6–p53 interaction

Structural modeling of the CerS6–p53 interaction was performed using UCSF ChimeraX v.1.10.1 (31, 32, 33), with the structure of the N-palmitoyl fumonisin B1/CerS6 complex (PDB ID: 8QZ7 (34)) used as the starting point. The bound N-palmitoyl fumonisin ligand was modified in silico to generate the steric equivalent of a C16-ceramide molecule. Modifications included deletion of fumonisin side chains and truncation of the backbone to match the length of C16-ceramide.

The p53 DNA-binding domain atom coordinates were extracted from PDB ID: 2MEJ (35). The p53 DBD was manually docked to CerS6 such that the previously identified ceramide-binding residues S240 and S241 were in proximity to the cytoplasm-accessible tail of C16-ceramide. Given that the CerS6 structure lacks the C-terminal domain, and that the N-terminal domain may be flexible, no further energy minimization, force-field optimization, or molecular dynamics was performed; rather, the docking was purely manual based on steric and surface complementarity.

Molecular graphics and analyses were performed with UCSF ChimeraX, developed by the Resource for Biocomputing, Visualization, and Informatics at the University of California, San Francisco, with support from NIH R01-GM129325 and the Office of Cyber Infrastructure and Computational Biology, National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases. For more information, see https://www.rbvi.ucsf.edu/chimerax.

Phosphorylation sites predictions

Phosphorylation sites predictions in the 60-amino acid C-terminal sequences of CerS5 and CerS6 were done using NetPhos 3.1 Server (Tech University of Denmark).

Results

p53 localizes to the ER upon ceramide treatment or CerS6 overexpression

To investigate whether p53 can associate with the ER, p53−/− A549 cells were cotransfected with GFP- CalR, an ER marker and mCherry-p53 plasmids (Fig. 1A). Ceramide signaling was directly stimulated using 5 μm PyrC16, which has biological activity similar to natural C16-ceramide but has improved water solubility, faster cellular uptake, and does not form condensed films in aqueous solutions (30). In unstressed cells, mCherry-p53 displayed a predominantly nuclear distribution with no detectable overlap with GFP-CalR (Fig. 1A). Following PyrC16 treatment, however, a time-dependent increase in yellow signal – indicative of red p53 and green ER colocalization – was observed (Fig. 1A). Moderate colocalization was evident after 2-h exposure and became pronounced by 8 h, consistent with stress-induced recruitment of p53 to the ER. To determine whether p53 colocalizes specifically with CerS6 on the ER under stress, A549 cells (p53+/+) were transfected with GFP-CerS6 plasmid. Twenty-four h later, cells were fixed and immuno-stained with p53-Ab, followed by AlexaFluor568-conjugated secondary antibody (red fluorescent dye). Fluorescence imaging demonstrates green staining of the ER (due to expression of the ER-resident CerS6 fused with the GFP) and both nuclear and cytoplasmic staining of p53, with punctate yellow overlap indicating GFP-CerS6-p53 colocalization (Fig. 1B). Consistent with our previous finding that CerS6 overexpression alone is sufficient to induce p53 expression (20), these data suggest that increased intracellular C16-ceramide promotes p53 recruitment to the ER through CerS6. As a complementary approach, GFP-CerS6 and mCherry-p53 fusion proteins were coexpressed in p53−/− A549 cells. Strong ER-localized yellow overlap was observed (Fig. 1C), confirming that CerS6 and p53 can colocalize on the ER.

Metabolic stress induces interaction of p53 and CerS6 on the ER

Our initial experiments established that ceramide treatment and CerS6 overexpression promote p53 recruitment to the ER. To investigate whether p53 directly interacts with CerS6 under metabolic stress conditions, we employed the BiFC approach (26). Two fusion constructs were coexpressed: (i) p53 tagged at the N-terminus with the N-terminal fragment of the Yellow Fluorescent Protein Venus (V1) and (ii) CerS6 tagged at the C-terminus with the C-terminal fragment of the Venus protein (V2). In this approach, the interaction between p53 and CerS6 brings V1 and V2 fragments into close proximity, reconstituting the Venus protein which produces green fluorescence (emission max. 527 nm) (schematically depicted in Fig. 2A). Thus, the presence of green fluorescence indicates complex formation between p53 and CerS6. To avoid interference from endogenous p53 protein, p53-deficient PC-3 cells were used for all BiFC experiments. In these experiments, the interference from endogenous CerS6 can be neglected due to low basal expression of the protein in non-stressed cells (21). Using a range of PyrC16 concentrations (0.5–7 μm), the minimal concentration evoking stress response in transfected cells was determined (Supplemental Fig. S1).

No Venus fluorescence was detected in cotransfected, unstressed PC-3 cells, indicating the lack of p53–CerS6 interaction (Fig. 2B, top panel). In contrast, treatments known to activate p53—including antifolate drug MTX, 10 nM for 24 h, PyrC16 (5 μm for 18 h), or serum starvation (0.2% FBS for 48 h)— produced robust Venus fluorescence (green, Fig. 2B). This fluorescence colocalized with the ER marker CalR (red), producing yellow signal on overlays, indicative of the ER-specific interaction. In contrast, treatment with the antifolate LTX, 1 μm for 24 h, which does not activate p53 (36, 37), failed to produce the BiFC signal, indicating the lack of the p53–CerS6 interaction when p53 is not activated (Fig. 2B).

Notably, stress-induced BiFC signal revealed the formation of discrete ER membrane aggregates (white arrows, Fig. 2B), reminiscent of previously reported stress-induced ER remodeling distinct from the unfolded protein response (38, 39). While the functional significance of such membrane aggregates remains unknown, their reproducible formation in response to various stress stimuli suggests a regulated remodeling event linked to lipid metabolism.

We investigated the reversibility of these structures in a separate experiment by tracking the persistence of BiFC signal following the withdrawal of stress-inducing agents. After formation of the aggregates in response to 24-h treatment with MTX or PyrC16, cells were washed to remove the drugs and cultured in fresh medium for up to 72 h. We observed strong reduction in size and number of the BiFC-positive ER membrane aggregates by 48 h after stressor withdrawal, with the complete lack of such structures by 72 h (Fig. 2C, yellow overlay; full panel - Supplemental Fig. S2). These data indicate that stress-induced ER remodeling, which involves p53–CerS6 complexes, is readily reversible. Although residual BiFC-derived Venus signal persisted throughout recovery, its intensity progressively declined, consistent with the cellular protein turnover. Of note, the BiFC signal intensity increased in a dose-dependent manner with escalating PyrC16 concentrations (Supplemental Fig. S1), suggesting that the p53–CerS6 interaction is tightly coupled to the magnitude of metabolic stress.

Stress-induced p53–CerS6 interaction is confined to the ER

While p53 is a soluble protein primarily observed in the cytoplasm and nuclei, CerS6 is an integral membrane protein predominantly found in the ER (2, 40). Consistent with the CerS6 localization, our data show that the stress-induced p53–CerS6 complexes form exclusively on the ER. BiFC analysis revealed strong colocalization of p53–CerS6 complex with the ER-resident chaperone CalR (Fig. 3). To further confirm that these complexes are not found outside of the ER, BiFC signal was monitored alongside fluorescence labeling of additional cellular compartments, including mitochondria (TOM20, magenta), nuclear envelope (lamins A + C, magenta), and cytosol (FXR1, pseudo-red). No significant colocalization was observed with any of these markers, indicating that the p53–CerS6 complex is spatially confined to the ER (Fig. 3).

Other ceramide synthases do not support biologically relevant interactions with p53 under metabolic stress

To determine whether the ability to interact with p53 is a general property of ceramide synthases, we performed BiFC assays in which V2-fusion constructs of CerS1–5 were coexpressed with V1-p53. In these experiments, metabolic stress was induced by 10 nM MTX. We observed that CerS1-4 did not produce detectable BiFC signal (Fig. 4), pointing to the lack of interaction with p53. Of note, CerS5-V2 showed positive BiFC signal and formation of ER aggregates, suggesting that CerS5 might also interact with p53 (Fig. 4). CerS5 and CerS6 are highly homologous enzymes (29) and both generate C16-ceramide (41). However, in contrast to CerS6, overexpression of CerS5 in cultured cells does not activate p53 (23), suggesting that CerS5-generated C16-ceramide is unavailable to p53 protein. To further clarify whether p53 directly interacts with CerS5, we have transfected PC-3 cells with CerS6- or CerS5-siRNAs for 36 h, then washed, and transfected these cells with pairs of BiFC vectors (specified in figure legends), and 24 h later stressed the cells. Control cells (no siRNA and scrambled siRNA) demonstrated strong CerS6 BiFC signal, indicating that nontargeting siRNA does not interfere with the complex formation (Fig. 5 and Supplemental Fig. S3A–C). CerS6 siRNA treated cells, transfected with CerS6 BiFC plasmid pair, also show BiFC signal, albeit weaker than controls. The weaker signal was due to a residual effect of CerS6 siRNA on the CerS6-V2 message, thus causing lower expression of the construct (Supplemental Figs. S4A) as the RISC complex can persist in cells for 3–7 days after transfection, depending on cells proliferation rate (42). In contrast, cells transfected with CerS5 siRNA and CerS6 BiFC vectors, show strong signal, comparable to controls, indicating that CerS5 is dispensable for CerS6–p53 interaction. Strikingly, CerS6 knockdown completely abolished the CerS5 BiFC signal (Figs. 5, and Supplemental Fig. S3A–C), demonstrating that CerS5 does not interact with p53 in the absence of CerS6, and that the observed BiFC signal for CerS5-V2 was mediated by CerS6. Efficacy of the siRNA knockout and levels of proteins expression were confirmed by Western blot assays (Supplemental Fig. S4A, B).

Of note, while endogenous CerS5 and the depletion of corresponding band with CerS5 siRNA were demonstrated (Supplemental Fig. S4A), we did not observe the CerS5-V2 band in this experiment. We do not believe that CerS6 siRNA prevented the plasmid-driven expression of CerS5-V2 (endogenous CerS5 was not affected), and no CerS6-V2 suppression was caused by CerS5 siRNA. We explain the lack of CerS5-V2 detection on WB by the inability of the commercial CerS5 antibody to access the epitope. Indeed, this antibody was developed against the synthetic peptide corresponding to the last 17 C-terminal amino acids of human CerS5 while in our CerS5-V2 construct the 80-amino acid V2-tag is fused immediately after the Ab epitope. This likely created constrains for the interaction with the antibody. In contrast, our in-house CerS6 antibody generated against the recombinant Cytosolic Domain 1 (CD1) fragment (83 aa long, residues 51–134, near the N-terminus) detected both WT CerS6 and the V2 fusion constructs.

Further evidence for the expression of the CerS5-V2 construct comes from the experiment in which this construct allowed reconstitution of Venus in the presence of endogenous CerS6 and the V1-p53 construct (Fig. 4). Taking all these observations together, we hypothesize that the apparent interaction of p53 with CerS5 in BiFC experiments was due to its heterodimerization with endogenous CerS6. Such heterodimerization, described in the literature (43), could explain the BiFC fluorescence signal seen in the presence of V1-p53 and CerS5-V2 (Fig. 4). Thus, the V1-p53 interacting with the endogenous CerS6 could be placed in close proximity of the V2 fragment of CerS5-V2 construct hetero-oligomerized with CerS6 allowing BiFC. Indeed, siRNA knockdown of CerS6 in these experiments completely abolished the fluorescence complementation of CerS5-V2 and V1-p53 (Fig. 5) providing strong support to our hypothesis. Overall, our findings indicate that CerS5, on its own, does not interact with p53, and that CerS6 is the only ceramide synthase capable of such interaction.

p53-CerS6 interaction upon metabolic stress leads to p53 accumulation in the nuclei

Since the main regulatory action of p53 occurs in the nuclei, with nuclear accumulation of p53 being a hallmark of its activation during adaptation to stress (44), we next investigated the cellular distribution of p53 under metabolic stress. Treatment with MTX (10 nM, 24 h) induced robust nuclear accumulation of p53 in A549 cells (p53+/+), (Fig. 6A). We further evaluated nuclear ceramide content using lipidomics analysis of the nuclear fractions isolated by differential centrifugation from the A549 p53+/+ cells treated with MTX or PyrC16. These cells were selected to evaluate the process associated with endogenous p53 without the input from forced p53 expression. Western blots presented in Supplemental Fig. S5A show strong fraction enrichment with the nuclear markers Lamins A & C, and dramatic reduction of the GAPDH and CalR, biomarkers of cytosol and ER, correspondingly. Lipidomics analysis revealed increased levels of C16-ceramide in nuclear fractions of stressed cells (Supplemental Fig. S5B), coinciding with increased p53 translocation to the nuclei and potentially affecting p53 signaling. Together, these findings establish the ER as a regulatory hub for p53–CerS6 dynamics while highlighting coordinated nuclear accumulation of both p53 and C16-ceramide under metabolic stress and linking ER lipid metabolism to stress-responsive signaling pathways. Interestingly, our experiment revealed that both MTX and PyrC16 had similar effects on nuclear C16-ceramide accumulation, however, only PyrC16 treatment strongly elevated the nuclear sphingosine-1-phosphate, S1P.

Mapping p53 and CerS6 domains required for ER-localized interaction

To gain structural insights into the p53–CerS6 interaction on the ER, we determined, in each protein, specific domains involved in the interaction. First, we generated a series of deletion constructs of p53 fused to V1 at N-terminus. A schematic overview of the p53 domain organization and truncations used is shown in Figure 6B. Three V1-p53 variants were investigated: (i) construct lacking the N-terminal transactivation domain of p53 (△1–100); (ii) construct, lacking the C-terminal regulatory region of p53 (△301–393); and (iii) construct, encoding only the (DBD, △1–100 +△301–393). Each expression construct was cotransfected with CerS6-V2 vector to PC3 cells and ER-associated BiFC signal was monitored in nonstressed cells and cells treated with MTX or PyrC16. BiFC analysis revealed that each of the three constructs supported stress-induced interaction with CerS6 on the ER upon MTX or PyrC16 treatment (yellow, overlay of BiFC and CALR, Fig. 6C; see full panel in Supplemental Fig. S6). These data showed that p53 DBD is responsible for the interaction with CerS6. This conclusion is in agreement with our previous findings demonstrating that C16-ceramide binds directly to p53 DBD at serine residues S240 and S241 (23). Thus, the p53 DBD plays a key role in interaction with CerS6 as well as in acquiring the ceramide.

We next mapped CerS6 regions involved in p53 binding. Based on predicted membrane topology of CerS6 (29), we have focused on two larger cytosolic domains: CD1 (residues 51–134) and Cytosolic Domain 2 (CD2) (residues 324–384) (Fig. 7A). Corresponding protein fragments were expressed in E. coli as N-terminal GST fusions and purified on glutathione resin to near homogeneity (Supplemental Fig. S7A). In vitro pull-down assays with full-length recombinant p53 expressed in A549 cells revealed that both GST-CD1 and GST-CD2 fusions efficiently pulled-down p53 on glutathione agarose (Fig. 7A, purified GST was used as a negative control; full blots can be found in Supplemental Fig. S7A). These findings demonstrate that p53 directly interacts with both large cytosolic domains of CerS6. Importantly, these pull-down assays demonstrating direct physical interaction of the purified cytosolic domains of CerS6 with p53 strongly support conclusions of our BiFC data (Figs. 7B and Supplemental Fig. S7B).

We further investigated the functional role of the CD2 domain using truncated CerS6 constructs in BiFC experiments. Two deletion variants were tested: one lacking the entire C-terminal cytoplasmic domain (61 amino acids, △324–384), and the other lacking only the terminal 33 amino acids (△354–384). Both truncations abolished the stress-induced BiFC signal upon MTX or PyrC16 treatment (no yellow on overlay, Figs. 7B, full panel is in Supplemental Fig. S7B), indicating that the last 33-amino acids of the C-terminus of CerS6 are critical for the interaction with p53. Notably, CD1, while capable of binding p53 in vitro, was insufficient to support BiFC complementation in the absence of CD2. These data can be interpreted as either the critical role of the cytoplasmic C-terminal tail of CerS6, or the requirement for the presence of both CerS6 domains for p53 engagement at the ER membrane.

CerS6 catalytic activity and C16-ceramide are required for the p53-CerS6 interaction

To further define the biochemical requirements for the p53–CerS6 interaction, we assessed the roles of CerS6 enzymatic activity, the presence of C16-ceramide in the catalytic site, and specificity for ceramide acyl chain length. The catalytically inactive H212A CerS6 mutant (mutated residue denoted as “∗” on the CerS6 schematic, Fig. 8A) failed to support BiFC (Fig. 8C, full panel is shown in Supplemental Fig. S8B), demonstrating that both, catalytic activity and the presence of C16-ceramide in the active site, are required for p53 interaction with CerS6.

We next asked whether the requirement for the ceramide presence is limited to C16-ceramide or whether other ceramides could enable the p53 recruitment. To address this question, chimeric CerS6-V2 constructs were engineered to switch substrate preference from palmitate-CoA (C16) to very-long-chain acyl-CoAs (C22-C24) to yield C22-C24-ceramides, accordingly (Fig. 8A). Such approach for switching the ceramide substrate specificity has been reported for CerS5, a close homologue of CerS6 (28). The recently solved cryo-EM structure of human CerS6 defines the enzyme as an integral membrane protein with seven transmembrane domains and four ER lumenal domains, with the final lumenal domain likely to confer the chain-length specificity (Fig. 8A) (28, 34). Based on two independent topology predictions, we constructed two CerS6 chimeras— Chim.11 and Chim.15—in which residues of the last lumenal domain were replaced with those from CerS2, an isoform known to preferentially generate very-long-chain ceramides (C22–C24) (Fig. 8A). Chim.11 includes an 11–amino acid substitution, as in (28), and Chim.15 carries a 15–residue replacement corresponding to the longer lumenal loop identified in (29).

Lipidomic profiling confirmed that these modifications successfully shifted substrate specificity (Fig. 8B, and Supplemental Fig. S8A). Compared to nontransfected cells, C22-, C24- and C24:1- ceramides were elevated about four-fold in chimeras-transfected cells, whereas no elevation for these species occurred upon transfection with WT CerS6. Of note, all transfected cells showed a significant elevation of C16-ceramide, which, in the case of chimera-transfected cells, we ascribe to stress induced by the four-fold increase in very-long-chain-ceramides. Under stress conditions (MTX or PyrC16 treatment), neither Chim.11, nor Chim.15 produced detectable Venus signal, indicating a lack of interaction with p53 (Figs. 8C and Supplemental Fig. S8C). These experiments demonstrate that the p53–CerS6 interaction requires specifically C16-ceramide presence, that cannot be substituted by other ceramide species.

Together, our results establish that both CerS6 catalytic activity and the specific ceramide acyl chain length (C16) are required for ER-localized p53–CerS6 complex formation. Our data support a model in which C16-ceramide, generated by CerS6 within the ER membrane, functions as a lipid mediator or structural facilitator of protein–protein association.

Discussion

Ceramides are increasingly recognized as central signaling molecules involved in the regulation of key processes in the cell, including proliferation, senescence, stress responses, apoptosis, and survival (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6). Despite growing knowledge of their diverse roles, the precise mechanisms by which specific ceramide species orchestrate cellular functions remain poorly understood. We have previously identified a particular ceramide, with C16-acyl chain, as a critical regulator of cellular response to metabolic and nutrient stress (20), and demonstrated that binding of this ceramide to p53 activates p53-dependent stress responses (23). Notably, p53 is not generally known to bind physiological metabolites, although its interaction with NAD+ has been reported (45). This highly soluble dinucleotide, present in cells at high concentrations, binds to p53 with a weak affinity (at millimolar concentrations) and attenuates its DNA binding activity (45). In contrast, we have reported that C16-ceramide, as well as its soluble analog PyrC16, bind p53 with high affinity (∼60 nM) and unparalleled selectivity (23). Because natural ceramides are extremely hydrophobic molecules, they are mostly present in the cells as integral components of membranes or membrane vesicles (2). In contrast, p53 is a soluble cytosolic protein which would not typically associate with cellular membranes (30). Such spatial separation led us to hypothesize that p53 acquires its natural regulatory ligand, C16-ceramide, through the direct interaction with the ER resident CerS6 on the outer leaflet of the ER membrane.

To study this interaction with spatial and functional precision, we initially employed fluorescent fusion constructs of p53 as well as CerS6, in combination with the ER-specific fluorescent markers, to visualize their localization in live cells. These experiments supported the p53 localization to the ER and colocalization of p53 and Cers6 under metabolic stress (Fig. 1). For the demonstration of the p53-CerS6 interaction in cellular space, we selected the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) approach (Fig. 2A), which has been successfully used to visualize protein-protein interactions in living cells (45, 46, 47, 48). In our study, the specificity of the BiFC signal was supported by its confinement to the ER, absence from other cellular compartments, dependence on defined p53 and CerS6 domains, and the lack of detectable signal in nonstressed conditions. To specifically monitor the recruitment of fluorescent p53 construct to CerS6 on the ER and to avoid the interference from endogenous p53, we used p53-null PC-3 cells. We did not consider silencing endogenous CerS6, as its expression is generally very low in unstressed cells (22), while its knockout could interfere with the overall sphingolipid metabolism (46).

Our previous studies have shown that the ceramide-dependent p53 activation is linked to a specific type of metabolic stress (20, 21, 23). In this regard, the antifolate MTX activated such stress while another antifolate LTX did not (36, 37). Similarly, the present study has shown that in contrast to MTX, LTX does not cause the p53 interaction with CerS6 or ER. This not only allowed the application of LTX as an excellent negative control in our experiments but also supported our previous findings by novel mechanistic insight into the molecular mechanism linking metabolic responses, ceramides, and the p53 activation. Our study further suggests that MTX-induced interaction between p53 and CerS6 evokes downstream events happening in the nucleus. Thus, BiFC-positive cells displaying the p53-CerS6 complex formation, demonstrated significant accumulation of p53 in the nuclei, an event likely reflecting the activation of transcriptional response. We also observed elevated levels of C16-ceramide in the isolated nuclei of these cells. Taken together, these findings could be interpreted as potential involvement of the ceramide in transcriptional regulation as an effector bound to p53.

The localization of the p53–CerS6 interaction specifically on the ER has important functional consequences for stress signaling since the ER is the primary site of ceramide synthesis (2, 5). The p53 protein gains access to its natural lipid ligand, C16-ceramide, by targeting CerS6 at the ER in a spatially restricted and highly efficient manner. Previous reports have described association of p53 with the ER at mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) via SERCA, which increased Ca2+ flux to the mitochondria and promoted apoptosis (47, 48). In contrast, the p53–CerS6 interaction observed in our study does not occur in nonstressed cells but is induced either by a particular metabolic stress or by exogenous ceramide, and evokes a distinct ER response, characterized by the formation of atypical ER membrane aggregates. These reversible structures resemble ER remodeling observed in an evolutionarily conserved stress adaptation, not associated with the canonical unfolded protein response (38, 39). The transient nature of this ER remodeling, combined with ceramide’s ability to alter membrane biophysical properties (7, 17, 18, 38, 39), suggests the function for the aggregates as the lipid–protein microdomains stabilizing the p53–CerS6 complex. Such reversibility of the ER aggregation after the stressor withdrawal further suggests that these ER structures are the components of integrated signaling response but not the product of irreversible ER damage mimicking UPR.

Our studies of the structural requirements for p53 to bind CerS6 revealed that the central DBD of p53 is solely responsible for the interaction with CerS6. Notably, we have previously shown that this domain binds C16-ceramide and PyrC16, but not closely related C14- or C18-ceramides (25). Our findings indicate that p53 can engage both major cytosol-exposed regions of CerS6, with the C-terminal peptide being indispensable for maintaining a stable association in situ. It is likely that the C-terminal region, predicted to be disordered (34), not only contributes structural determinants for the interaction but also allows conformational flexibility to accommodate p53 for the complex formation between p53 and CerS6. Importantly, our analysis using the NetPhos 3.1 bioinformatics service (Tech University of Denmark) predicted multiple phosphorylation sites within the 33-amino acid C-terminal region, which are targets for CK1 and 2, PKC, PKA and potentially, additional unidentified kinases (Supplemental Fig. S9B). This suggests the regulation of p53–CerS6 interaction by the cellular kinases, which would be in line with the activation of such signaling by metabolic or nutrient stress. Indeed, reported phosphorylation of yeast CerS homologues (Lac1/Lag1) and mammalian CerS6 by CK2 can modulate enzymatic activity, with phosphorylation mildly enhancing CerS6 function (43, 44).

Remarkably, we have shown that the presence of C16-ceramide in the CerS6 active site is required for p53 binding. Consistent with this, p53 selectively binds C16-ceramide but not ceramides with even slight changes in the acyl chain length, such as C14- or C18- ceramides (25), indicating a uniquely selective signaling circuit. The lack of detectable p53 interactions with four other ceramide synthases (CerS1-4) further suggests a high specificity for the CerS6 recognition by p53. The combination of the requirement for both the unique ceramide (C16) and for the structural features distinct to CerS6, both enable the highly selective interaction of p53 restricted to CerS6. Interestingly, our experiments with the CerS5 BiFC approach indicated that CerS5 might also interact with p53. This was an unexpected result, considering major differences in C-terminal sequences and possible phosphorylation patterns between CerS5 and CerS6 (Supplemental Fig. S9A). However, the highest overall sequence similarity between CerS6 and Cers5, among all ceramide synthases (29), could explain the CerS5-p53 BiFC outcomes. Arguing against the CerS5-p53 interaction, the CerS6 siRNA knockdown eliminated CerS5 BiFC signal indicating that CerS5 by itself, in the absence of CerS6, does not form complex with p53, while CerS6 showed strong BiFC in the absence of CerS5 (Fig. 5). These findings urged us to hypothesize that the CerS5–p53 BiFC signal originated from CerS5 dimerized with CerS6, the phenomenon enabling the complementation between CerS5-V2 and CerS6-bound V1-p53. Of note, reported hetero-oligomerization between CerS5 and CerS6 (43) supports this explanation. Also, consistent with this interpretation, our previous work demonstrated that CerS5 overexpression alone was unable to activate the p53 response in cultured cells (23). Overall, our data support a model in which only C16-ceramide generated by CerS6 can be loaded on p53.

Our findings align well with the recently reported high-resolution cryo-EM structure of the human CerS6 in complex with fumonisin, the inhibitor of ceramide synthases (34). This structure provides explanations for our data from the structural viewpoint (Fig. 9). The structure was determined for the CerS6 homodimer lacking the last 30 C-terminal residues, which were predicted to be disordered. Each protomer in the dimer forms a barrel composed of the TM2-TM7 helices, with a central hydrophobic channel capped at the lumenal side of the membrane by the TM6-TM7 loop. This channel precisely accommodates the C16-acyl chain, transferred from acyl-CoA to the catalytic His211 but excludes longer chains. On the cytosolic side, the channel widens into a 23 Å × 12 Å “vestibule”, providing access to bulky substrates. The synthesized ceramide is believed to be released into the membrane between TM4 and TM7 (34). Our domain-mapping experiments identified the p53 DBD as the domain interacting with CerS6 while CerS6 contributes to this interaction via the CD1 (TM1-TM2 loop) and CD2 (C-terminal cytosolic fragment) regions. This suggests the existence of multiple contact points between the two proteins. To further assess the feasibility of p53 DBD direct binding to CerS6, we modeled p53–CerS6 interactions using the above structure of CerS6. For this in silico model, the C16-ceramide molecule was built in the central tunnel in place of the actual ligand, fumonisin, while the p53 DBD was docked at the cytosolic opening of the channel, the “vestibule” (Fig. 9). In our model, the DBD fits within the cytoplasmic opening of the channel, with the ceramide contact residues Ser240 and Ser241 (23) positioned near the exit from the CerS6 substrate channel. Although this model lacks the final 30 C-terminal residues of CerS6 and does not account for oligomeric organization of each protein, the overall match of the DBD contact surface to the size of CerS6 “vestibule,” together with the location of the p53 ceramide-contacting residues at the channel exit, offer the structural basis for the direct channeling of ceramide from CerS6 to p53.

Consistent with this model, no p53–CerS6 interaction was detected in cells expressing the catalytically inactive H212A CerS6 mutant or the CerS6 chimeras producing very-long-chain ceramides. These findings underscore distinct lipid identity, rather than general hydrophobicity, as the driver of the specificity. Overall, our study supports the substrate channeling model (Fig. 9), in which CerS6 coordinates synthesis, positioning, and direct transfer of ceramide to p53. This model also explains the strict requirement for both catalytic activity and C16-acyl-chain specificity. Notably, the direct lipid acquisition strategy parallels mechanisms used by ER-associated lipid transfer proteins such as CERT and STARD members ORP5/8 and VPS13, which directly extract lipids from active sites of membrane-associated enzymes via defined structural portals (49, 50, 51, 52). In CerS6, coupling product generation to “vestibule” accessibility likely ensures that p53 acquires ceramide only during active, stress-induced biosynthesis, thereby conferring temporal and molecular specificity to the signaling event.

Numerous transcription factors belonging to the nuclear receptor class are known to bind small molecules which modulate their activity (53, 54). This is exemplified by steroid, thyroid, retinoid, and vitamin D receptors as well as PPAR and LXR (55). Our findings highlight a similar mechanism for p53 and C16-ceramide. The DBD’s dual capability to bind DNA and ceramide suggests potential allosteric modulation of the p53 transcriptional activity. Given that C16-ceramide binding enhances p53 stability (23, 56), the p53–CerS6 complex may act as a stress-sensing module that amplifies stress response under metabolic or chemotherapeutic stress. The coupling of ceramide biosynthesis to the activation of the p53 tumor suppressor can be a druggable mechanism with the potential for targeting cancer treatment.

Overall, we identify a novel ER-confined signaling mechanism in which p53 directly engages CerS6 to extract a specific ceramide molecule. This mechanism links stress-induced C16-ceramide synthesis to p53 stabilization and activation. Upon nutrient deprivation, chemotherapeutic challenge, or metabolic exhaustion, p53 binds to CerS6 on the ER surface to allow the direct channeling of C16-ceramide. This interaction is structurally defined by the complementary topology of particular domains in both proteins, requires enzymatically generated C16-ceramide, and is accompanied by the reversible formation of punctate ER membrane aggregates. Our findings reveal a new mode of lipid-mediated signaling in stress adaptation, highlighting the ER as a dynamic scaffold for integrating metabolic cues with tumor suppressor function.
