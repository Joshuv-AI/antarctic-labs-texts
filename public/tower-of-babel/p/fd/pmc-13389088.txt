Emerging XDR and MDR Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains from Oreochromis niloticus reveal high pathogenicity, virulence determinants, antimicrobial resistance genes, and oprL sequence diversity
Authors: Abdelazeem M. Algammal, Mahmoud Mabrok, Bian K. Almessiry, Banan Atwah, Saad Alghamdi, Mohammed B. Alsharif, Madeha O. I. Ghobashy, Maryam Alenize, Enas A. Almanzalawi, Fatma Alzahraa N. Aboelftouh, Reham M. El-Tarabili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pseudomonas aeruginosa represents a considerable public health threat due to its role in severe human infections and a significant economic burden in aquaculture. To assess the prevalence, antibiogram, virulence, and resistance genes, and pathogenicity of P. aeruginosa, 200 Oreochromis niloticus were obtained from commercial fish farms in Port Said, Egypt, and underwent clinical, postmortem, and bacteriological examinations. Herein, the overall prevalence of P. aeruginosa was 20%, and the liver was recognized as the most prominently affected organ. Sequencing of the oprL gene showed that the examined strains shared notable genetic homology with other P. aeruginosa strains from the United Kingdom, USA, India, China, and Japan. The recovered P. aeruginosa strains showed remarkable resistance to tetracycline, amoxicillin, trimethoprim- sulfamethoxazole, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, gentamycin, erythromycin, and cefotaxime. Notably, a 6.4% imipenem resistance rate was observed among the retrieved strains, indicating a carbapenem-resistant phenotype. PCR ascertained that the most common virulence genes inherited by the recovered strains were the oprL (100%), toxA (94.9%), aprA (47.4%), exoS (38.4%), and phzM (35.9%) genes. A total of 64.1% of the tested P. aeruginosa strains were multidrug-resistant (MDR) to seven classes and harbored the blaTEM, sul1, tetA, blaCTX−M1, and aadA1 genes. Additionally, 6.4% of P. aeruginosa strains were extensively drug-resistant (XDR) to eight classes and harbored the blaTEM, sul1, tetA, blaCTX−M1, blaNDM−1, and aadA1 genes. The pathogenicity assay revealed a positive correlation between isolate virulence and the recorded mortality rate. In summary, the detection of XDR and MDR P. aeruginosa in O. niloticus poses a potential public health concern. These strains commonly harbor the resistance genes tetA, sul1, blaTEM, aadA1, blaCTX−M1, and blaNDM−1, alongside the virulence genes oprL, toxA, aprA, exoS, and phzM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-63074-1.

Introduction

Bacterial diseases constitute a critical challenge to the sustainability and productivity of global aquaculture, exerting profound impacts on fish health and economic viability1. Bacterial outbreaks have been estimated to cause substantial losses in cultured fish stocks before market, underscoring the urgent need for robust surveillance and effective disease management strategies2. Among these, Pseudomonads are widely recognized as opportunistic pathogens that can induce severe pathological conditions, including ulcerative syndrome and hemorrhagic septicemia, across diverse aquatic species3,4. Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a widely distributed Gram-negative, aerobic, motile, and non-sporulating bacterium that may exist as part of the normal microbial flora of fish5. Nevertheless, under unfavorable environmental and husbandry conditions, such as overcrowding, nutritional deficiencies, and environmental stress, it can readily shift to a highly virulent opportunistic pathogen. Infected fish typically present with a spectrum of clinical signs, comprising hemorrhagic septicemia, gill damage, abdominal swelling, enlarged spleen, hepatic degeneration, and renal congestion, indicative of systemic infection6,7. The pathogenic potential of P. aeruginosa is underpinned by a complex repertoire of virulence determinants comprising both cell-associated and secreted factors8. In fish, P. aeruginosa pathogenicity is mediated by a similar arsenal of virulence determinants that have been well-characterized in human infections but are increasingly recognized as important in aquatic hosts4. Surface-associated components such as flagella, pili, and lipopolysaccharides are integral to key bacterial processes, including adhesion, motility, host colonization, and evasion of host immune defenses9,10. Concurrently, secreted virulence factors, such as exotoxin A, exotoxin S, and phenazine compounds, are instrumental in disrupting host cellular processes, exacerbating inflammatory responses, and facilitating bacterial invasion and dissemination11,12. Specifically, aprA encodes an alkaline protease that promotes host tissue degradation and facilitates bacterial invasion13; toxA encodes exotoxin A, which disrupts host protein synthesis and induces cell death14,15; phzM is involved in phenazine production, contributing to oxidative stress and subsequent tissue injury12,16; whereas exoS encodes a type III secretion system effector that interferes with host cell signaling pathways and enhances immune evasion17.

Timely and precise detection of Pseudomonas species is crucial for effective diagnosis and disease management in aquaculture systems. However, conventional phenotypic and biochemical approaches, despite their widespread use, are often labor-intensive, time-consuming, and susceptible to variability, which can compromise their reliability18. In contrast, molecular diagnostic approaches, particularly PCR-based assays and 16S rRNA gene sequencing, provide rapid, sensitive, and highly specific tools for pathogen detection and characterization19,20. The application of species-specific genetic markers like the oprL gene has significantly improved the precision of P. aeruginosa identification and facilitated deeper insights into its epidemiology and ecological dynamics21.

P. aeruginosa exhibits extensive antimicrobial resistance mediated by intrinsic and acquired mechanisms, including reduced membrane permeability, efflux pumps, and horizontal gene transfer22,23. Of particular significance is the development of ESBLs, encoded by genes such as blaCTX−M1 and blaTEM, which confer resistance to various β-lactam antibiotics24,25. Additionally, resistance genes such as tetA further exacerbate therapeutic challenges26. The global rise of MDR and XDR P. aeruginosa strains represents a serious and escalating threat to both aquaculture and public health4,27,28. Beyond its impact on aquatic organisms, P. aeruginosa also possesses considerable zoonotic potential29–31.

The propagation of antimicrobial-resistant pathogens and determinants of resistance through the food chain underscores the interconnected nature of aquatic and human health, highlighting the necessity of adopting a One Health framework32–35. Within this context, this study was designed to explore the virulence attributes and resistance patterns of P. aeruginosa recovered from Oreochromis niloticus. This objective was accomplished through bacteriological isolation and identification, antimicrobial susceptibility testing, molecular recognition of virulence and resistance genes, and sequencing analysis of the oprL gene for molecular confirmation.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

The study was conducted in compliance with ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were performed following the relevant guidelines and regulations. The handling of fish and all the experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Review Committee of Suez Canal University, Egypt (Approval No: SCU-VET-AREC–R2026022).

Sampling, clinical, and postmortem examinations

A total of 200 O. niloticus (100 apparently healthy and 100 moribund; average body weight 65 ± 5 g) were collected arbitrarily from private farms in Port Said, Egypt, between August and October 2023. Fish were transferred to the laboratory in iceboxes, where clinical assessment and necropsy findings were carried out3. The fish were euthanized by immersion in an overdose of buffered tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222, 300 mg/L) until opercular movement ceased, following the guidelines established by the American Veterinary Medical Association for the euthanasia of animals. For bacteriological analyses, samples from the spleen, liver, and kidneys were aseptically collected from each fish.

Bacteriological examination

Using a sterile bacteriological loop, a loopful from each sample (liver, spleen, and kidney) was streaked onto cetrimide agar and MacConkey agar (Oxoid, UK), and incubated at 28 °C for 24 h. All suspected colonies were identified based on Gram’s staining, culture characteristics (colony morphology, size, color, and pigment production), motility, and biochemical characteristics, including catalase, Voges-Proskauer, methyl red, urease, oxidase, indole, H2S production, citrate utilization, and gelatin hydrolysis36. Moreover, the identification was molecularly confirmed using a species-specific primer set targeting the oprL gene37.

The oprL gene sequencing

All the retrieved P. aeruginosa strains revealed similar phenotypic traits. Accordingly, the PCR products from two randomly selected P. aeruginosa strains were purified using the PureLink purification kit (Life Technologies, Renfrew, UK). Additionally, the resulting sequences were deposited in GenBank. Likewise, various alignments were performed on the retrieved sequences. The phylogenetic tree was constructed according to the neighbor-joining method implemented in the MEGA 11 software38, using the Kimura 2-parameter model.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

The disc diffusion method was performed on Mueller-Hinton agar (Oxoid, UK) according to the CLSI guidelines39. A panel of 10 antimicrobial agents was involved, comprising amoxicillin (AMX, 30 µg), imipenem (IPM, 10 µg), trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (SXT, 25 µg), cefotaxime (CTX, 30 µg), amoxicillin/clavulanic acid (AMC, 30 µg), erythromycin (E, 15 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg), tetracycline (TE, 30 µg), gentamicin (CN, 10 µg), and colistin sulfate (CT, 10 µg). P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 was used as the quality control strain. P. aeruginosa isolates were categorized as MDR (resistant to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes) and XDR (resistant to at least one agent in all classes except one or two) following previously described criteria40. Also, the multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index was assessed41.

Investigation of virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes using the PCR assay

All 78 recovered P. aeruginosa isolates were individually subjected to PCR analysis to investigate the prevalence of virulence genes (aprA, toxA, phzM, and exoS) and antimicrobial resistance genes (blaTEM, blaCTX−M1, blaNDM−1, tetA, aadA1, and sul1). DNA was extracted using a DNA isolation kit (Norgen Biotek, Ontario, Canada). DNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA), with A260/280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.0 considered acceptable. DNA integrity was verified by electrophoresis on 1% agarose gel. Negative controls (DNA-free) and positive control strains (P. aeruginosa ATCC 27853 for virulence genes; E. coli ATCC BAA-2452 for blaNDM−1 gene; previously molecularly confirmed P. aeruginosa strains from the Animal Health Research Institute (Egypt) were used as positive controls for the remaining antimicrobial resistance genes) were included in each run. The resulting products were separated by electrophoresis on a 1.5% agarose gel and visualized under UV light. The oligonucleotide sequences (Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK) and specific amplicons are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Pathogenicity trial

Approximately 120 healthy-appearing O. niloticus (51 ± 3 g), with no previous disease record, were randomly obtained from a commercial farm in Ismailia, Egypt. Fish were adapted for two weeks in a 1 m³ fiberglass tank supplied with dechlorinated sand-filtered tap water. The fish received a commercial pelleted feed (Skretting, Egypt), administered twice daily to apparent satiation. Only fish exhibiting normal behavior and no visible external lesions were selected for experimentation.

For the challenge trial, 80 fish were randomly allocated to four groups (two replicates per group; 10 fish per 80-L aquarium). The control group (C) was intraperitoneally injected with 100 µL of germ-free phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), whereas the treated groups (T1–T3) received 100 µL of overnight cultures of virulent P. aeruginosa isolates (A–C) at a final concentration of 3 × 107 CFU mL⁻¹ according to Algammal4. The inoculum concentration was selected based on our previous publication and preliminary laboratory pilot studies, which established it as the minimum dose required to produce consistent mortality in O. niloticus over a 14-day observation period. Isolate A carried oprL, toxA, exoS, phzM, and aprA genes; isolate B carried oprL, toxA, aprA, and exoS; and isolate C carried oprL and toxA. Bacterial inocula were prepared by culturing isolates in TSB (Oxoid, UK) at 37 °C for 24 h, followed by adjustment to 0.5 McFarland standard (~ 1 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹) and subsequent dilution to the required concentration. Bacterial concentration was verified using a Helber counting chamber and confirmed by plate counting. Following injection, fish were monitored twice daily for clinical signs and cumulative mortality over two weeks. Moribund and recently dead fish were subjected to bacteriological re-isolation and subsequently euthanized to minimize suffering, in accordance with established ethical guidelines for fish euthanasia (detailed in the ethical statement). Mortality was attributed to the experimental infection only when the inoculated pathogen was successfully re-isolated, in accordance with Koch’s postulates.

Statistical analysis

Sample size was determined using G*Power version 3.1.9.7. An a priori power analysis was performed using the t-test family with the Correlation: Point-biserial model to estimate the minimum sample size required to detect a prespecified effect size for associations involving binary microbiological outcomes. The analysis was conducted using a one-tailed test with an assumed effect size (|ρ|) of 0.30, a significance level (α) of 0.05, and a desired statistical power (1 – β) of 0.95. Based on these parameters, G*Power estimated a minimum total sample size of 111 (df = 109), corresponding to a noncentrality parameter (δ) of 3.3133098, a critical t value of 1.6589535, and an actual statistical power of 0.9503016. The sample size calculation was performed solely to ensure adequate statistical power before data collection and was independent of the subsequent inferential analyses. In addition, a post hoc power analysis was conducted. Statistical analyses were performed using the chi-square test in SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, USA), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients between the presence of resistance genes and the corresponding phenotypic antimicrobial resistance were calculated using R (version 4.0.2; https://www.r-project.org/). For contingency tables, we used adjusted standardized residuals for post-hoc analysis when the overall chi-square test was significant (p < 0.05). To account for multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied by dividing the significance level (α = 0.05) by the number of cells in the contingency table. Cells with adjusted residuals exceeding the Bonferroni-corrected critical z-value were considered to contribute significantly to the observed association. In the challenge trial, cumulative fish survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and significant differences among treatment groups were assessed through pairwise comparisons using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

Results

Clinical and postmortem examinations

Clinical examination revealed that 85% of moribund fish displayed hallmark features of hemorrhagic septicemia, notably multifocal cutaneous hemorrhages across the body surface, with greater prominence in the abdominal region, operculum, caudal peduncle, and perianal area. Additional external signs included fin erosion and scale loss. In some cases, fish exhibited ocular alterations such as cataract formation and/or ocular erythema, along with mild abdominal distension and anal prolapse (Fig. 1). Upon internal examination, 78% of the moribund fish exhibited characteristic pathological changes, including severe congestion of the liver, heart, and kidneys, splenomegaly, and abdominal dropsy marked by the accumulation of serous ascitic fluid (Fig. 2).

Phenotypic characteristics and prevalence of P. aeruginosa

Herein, all the obtained isolates were motile, Gram-negative bacilli. On cetrimide agar, colonies were large and yielded typical yellowish-green fluorescent pigments. On MacConkey’s agar, flat non-lactose-fermenting colonies were observed. Biochemically, the retrieved isolates were positive for oxidase, catalase, citrate utilization, gelatin hydrolysis, and nitrate reduction. In contrast, they tested negative for methyl red, urease, Voges-Proskauer, H2S production, and indole tests. Additionally, all the obtained strains harbored the P. aeruginosa-specific oprL gene, providing the expected amplicon size of 504 bp. In this study, the prevalence of P. aeruginosa in the examined O. niloticus was 20% (40/200), with all 40 positive fish being from the moribund group (40/100, 40%). None of the apparently healthy fish (0/100) yielded P. aeruginosa. Moreover, the liver (35/78, 44.8%) was the most commonly affected organ, then the spleen (25/78, 32.1%) and the kidney (18/78, 23.1%) (Table 1; Fig. 3). No significant variance was observed in the organ distribution of P. aeruginosa between the examined fish (p > 0.05), suggesting that the pathogen does not show a specific predilection for any internal organ, despite its higher recovery rate from the liver.

The oprL gene sequence analyses

The phylogenetic analyses of the two representative P. aeruginosa isolates (Accession No.: PQ767155 and PQ767156) exhibited notable genetic similarity (99.8%) to each other (sequences are almost identical, differing only by one base), as well as to other P. aeruginosa strains from numerous sources and districts (Fig. 4). Specifically, high similarity was observed with the following strains: P. aeruginosa strain JayT (99.6–99.8%), recovered from diseased fish in India (Accession No. ON993361), P. aeruginosa strain LESB58 (99.8–100%), recovered from a human in the United Kingdom (Accession No. FM209186), P. aeruginosa strain SE5416 (99.8–100%), recovered from a human in China (Accession No. CP046404), P. aeruginosa strain NCGM 1984 (99.8–100%), recovered from Japan (Accession No. AP014646), P. aeruginosa strain P15 ABT NVC (99.77–100%), isolated from a dog in India (Accession No. PP436721), P. aeruginosa strain PAO1 (99.6–99.8%), recovered from the USA (Accession No. AE004091), and P. aeruginosa strain C3 ABT NVC (99.55%), recovered from poultry meat in India (Accession No. PP436725).

Antibiogram profile of the recovered P. aeruginosa strains

Herein, the recovered P. aeruginosa strains were resistant to tetracycline, amoxicillin, and trimethoprim- sulfamethoxazole (100% each), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (91.1%), gentamycin (89.7%), erythromycin (88.5%), and cefotaxime (87.2%). Notably, 6.4% of the recovered P. aeruginosa strains were imipenem-resistant (carbapenem-resistant). Remarkably, the tested P. aeruginosa strains were sensitive to ciprofloxacin (100%), imipenem, and colistin sulfate (93.6% each) (Table 2; Fig. 5). Statistically, a significant variance (p < 0.05) was recorded in the susceptibility of the tested P. aeruginosa strains to various antimicrobials. The chi-square test indicated a significant association between antimicrobial agent and susceptibility category (Table 2, X2 = 716.23, df = 18, and p < 0.001). Bonferroni-corrected residual analysis showed that resistance was significant for tetracycline, amoxicillin, and trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, whereas susceptibility for ciprofloxacin, imipenem, and colistin sulfate.

Prevalence of virulence and antimicrobial resistance genes

Herein, the most prominent virulence genes associated with P. aeruginosa isolates were the oprL (100%), toxA (94.9%), aprA (47.4%), exoS (38.4%), and phzM (35.9%) genes. Moreover, the obtained isolates carried the tetA, sul1, blaTEM, aadA1, blaCTX−M1, and blaNDM−1 resistance genes with a prevalence of 100%, 100%, 100%, 89.7%, 87.2%, and 6.4%, respectively (Table 3; Fig. 6). The chi-square test demonstrated a significant association between the resistance gene and its frequency among isolates (Table 3, X2 = 123.68, df = 10, and p < 0.001). Bonferroni-corrected residual analysis indicated that the presence of tetA, sul1, and blaTEM was significantly different, whereas the occurrence of blaNDM−1 and phzM was not significantly different among the tested isolates.

Multidrug-resistance patterns of P. aeruginosa

Among the isolated P. aeruginosa, 64.1% (50/78) were multidrug resistant (MDR) to seven antimicrobial classes and harbored the blaTEM, sul1, tetA, blaCTX−M1, and aadA1 genes. Furthermore, 6.4% (5/78) of P. aeruginosa isolates were XDR to eight classes and harbored the blaTEM, sul1, tetA, blaCTX−M1, blaNDM−1, and aadA1 genes. Additionally, 12.8% (10/78) of P. aeruginosa isolates were MDR to six classes and harbored the genes blaTEM, sul1, tetA, and aadA1, while 6.4% (5/78) were MDR to five classes and harbored the blaTEM, sul1, tetA, blaCTX−M1, and aadA1 genes. Also, 5.1% (4/78) of P. aeruginosa isolates were MDR to six classes and carried the blaTEM, sul1, tetA, and blaCTX−M1 genes (Table 4; Fig. 7). The MAR index values for the XDR and MDR P. aeruginosa isolates ranged from 0.40 to 0.80 (Table 4), all exceeding 0.2, suggesting high antimicrobial exposure. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant strong positive correlations (p < 0.001; r = 1 for each) between the distinguished resistance genes and their corresponding antimicrobials, including blaTEM and AMX, aadA and CN; sul1 and SXT; tetA and TE; blaNDM−1 and IPM; and blaCTX−M1 and CTX.

Pathogenicity trial

Throughout the study, cumulative mortality and survival probability were tracked and recorded in all experimental groups (Fig. 8A and B, respectively). Disease severity and mortality patterns were strongly associated with the virulence gene composition of the challenged strains. Isolates possessing a broader repertoire of virulence-associated genes provoked more severe clinical signs and markedly higher mortality rates compared with those carrying fewer determinants. The gross lesions observed in experimentally infected fish closely resembled those documented in naturally occurring infections, thereby supporting the pathogenicity of the tested P. aeruginosa strains. Mortality increased sharply between days 6 and 8 post-infection, reaching cumulative rates of approximately 90–95% in the T1 and T2 groups challenged with highly virulent strains. In contrast, fish in the T3 group challenged with less virulent isolates exhibited lower cumulative mortality (approximately 75%) and a slower, more prolonged disease progression. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in survival among the treatment groups, as confirmed by the Log-Rank (Mantel–Cox) test (χ² = 15.878, df = 3, p = 0.001). No clinical abnormalities or mortality were observed in the negative control group throughout the experimental period. Re-isolation of the pathogen from the infected organs further satisfied Koch’s postulates, verifying its etiological role in the disease occurrence.

Discussion

The present investigation offers a comprehensive and integrative perspective on the biological behavior, prevalence, and clinical significance of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in Nile tilapia aquaculture, underscoring its position as a highly versatile opportunistic pathogen with far-reaching implications for both fish health and public safety, as widely documented in previous publications42–44. Rather than merely confirming its presence, the study advances current understanding by linking pathogenicity, resistance, and genetic relatedness within a unified framework, consistent with modern integrative approaches to microbial epidemiology27,29.

Clinical examination revealed that 20% (40/200) of O. niloticus were affected, with P. aeruginosa isolated exclusively from moribund fish. A possible explanation for the absence of P. aeruginosa in apparently healthy fish is that bacterial loads in asymptomatic carriers may be below the detection threshold of culture-based methods. Moreover, the exclusive isolation of P. aeruginosa from moribund fish in this study suggests that the pathogen is primarily associated with disease rather than asymptomatic carriage in O. niloticus. The disease pattern associated with P. aeruginosa infection in this study aligns closely with the well-established septicemic profile attributed to this organism in freshwater fish45,46.

Diseased fish exhibited typical signs of hemorrhagic septicemia, along with fin erosion, scale loss, and occasional ocular lesions. Mild abdominal distension and anal prolapse were also observed. Such consistency with earlier reports reinforces the concept that P. aeruginosa employs a conserved pathogenic strategy largely mediated by surface-associated components and an array of extracellular virulence factors, including exotoxins, proteolytic enzymes, and other bioactive molecules47,48. These factors collectively contribute to vascular damage, disruption of cellular integrity, and progressive organ dysfunction49. Differences in prevalence rates observed among various studies can plausibly be attributed to variations in environmental conditions, seasonal fluctuations, management practices, and sampling strategies50.

Regarding the organ-wise distribution of P. aeruginosa, the liver exhibited the highest recovery rate, followed by the spleen and kidney, consistent with a previous report51. The observed difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05), suggesting that P. aeruginosa is systemically distributed without a specific predilection seat. The oprL gene, which encodes an outer membrane lipoprotein, is a widely used species-specific marker for P. aeruginosa identification21. The high sequence similarity (99.6–100%) between our isolates and strains from diverse geographic regions confirms species identity and highlights the strong conservation of oprL across P. aeruginosa populations. This conservation supports its utility as a reliable diagnostic target. However, as a single-locus marker, oprL offers limited epidemiological resolution compared with MLST or WGS. Thus, the observed sequence similarity primarily reflects the conserved nature of the gene rather than recent transmission or direct epidemiological links.

The phylogenetic analysis adds a significant epidemiological dimension by demonstrating a high degree of genetic similarity between the studied isolates and strains derived from diverse hosts and geographic regions, including human clinical cases52. This remark is in line with earlier genomic studies that emphasized the lack of strict host specificity in P. aeruginosa53. Such genetic relatedness suggests that tilapia farming environments may harbor strains that are genetically related to human clinical isolates54. While this study does not confirm reservoir status or zoonotic transmission, it highlights the need for further investigation into the potential role of aquaculture systems in the dissemination of P. aeruginosa strains with public health relevance. This finding carries important public health implications, particularly in regions where fish constitute a major component of the diet and where biosecurity measures may be limited55. A particularly critical aspect of this study is the detailed characterization of antimicrobial resistance patterns, which reflect a concerning trend toward reduced therapeutic efficacy56. The resistance profiles against a variety of antimicrobials are broadly consistent with those reported in earlier investigations28,57,58, indicating that commonly used antimicrobial agents, especially β-lactams and tetracyclines, are increasingly ineffective against P. aeruginosa in aquaculture settings59.

Moreover, resistance to critically important antibiotics such as carbapenems, even at a low percentage (6.4%), raises serious concerns. The detection of carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa carrying blaNDM−1 in fish could minimize the efficacy of last-resort carbapenem antibiotics, underscoring the need for strengthened surveillance and antimicrobial stewardship in aquaculture60. This pattern is widely recognized as a consequence of the prolonged and often indiscriminate use of antibiotics in fish farming61–63. Conversely, the retained susceptibility to certain antimicrobial classes, notably ciprofloxacin as a fluoroquinolone, corroborates previous findings64. The majority of isolates showed remarkable sensitivity to other antibiotics, such as imipenem and colistin sulfate, in agreement with the previous study27. The emergence of such resistance traits signals a potential transition toward more difficult-to-treat infections and emphasizes the urgent need for stricter antimicrobial stewardship and regulatory oversight. Erythromycin resistance was observed in 88.5% of isolates, which is consistent with the intrinsic resistance of P. aeruginosa to macrolides due to its impermeable outer membrane and constitutive expression of efflux pumps. The presence of intermediate resistance in 11.5% of isolates further supports this intrinsic mechanism, as clinical resistance breakpoints for macrolides in P. aeruginosa are not well-established22. The MAR index values for the XDR and MDR P. aeruginosa isolates ranged from 0.40 to 0.80, all exceeding 0.20, suggesting exposure to high-risk contamination sources under excessive antimicrobial pressure. Comparable MAR indices (0.54–0.91) have been reported in a previous investigation28, suggesting that antimicrobial pressure in food-producing sectors is a global concern.

At the molecular level, the study provides valuable insights into the genetic determinants underpinning both virulence and resistance. The widespread occurrence of key virulence-associated genes, particularly those encoding outer membrane proteins and exotoxins, is in strong agreement with prior research65. The detection of additional virulence factors further suggests that P. aeruginosa utilizes a multifactorial infection strategy, whereby multiple genes act synergistically to enhance pathogenic capability66.

Equally noteworthy is the high prevalence of antimicrobial resistance genes, especially those encoding β-lactamases and tetracycline resistance mechanisms. These findings are consistent with a global report67 and provide a molecular explanation for the observed phenotypic resistance patterns. The strong concordance between genotype and phenotype suggests that these genes are likely functional68, although confirmation of expression and protein activity would require further investigation. The perfect correlations (r = 1) observed in this study reflect complete concordance between resistance genes and their corresponding phenotypes within the current isolate collection rather than a statistical artifact, but may not be reproducible in more genetically diverse populations. Moreover, the coexistence of multiple resistance genes within single isolates highlights the genomic pliability of P. aeruginosa and explains the widespread occurrence of MDR and XDR phenotypes69. This co-resistance phenomenon is particularly alarming, as it not only limits available treatment options but also facilitates the dissemination of resistance genes within microbial communities, thereby amplifying the spread of antimicrobial resistance in aquatic environments70.

Furthermore, the pathogenicity trial underscores the functional relevance of virulence gene profiles in determining disease outcomes. Although no reference strain was included, multiple retrieved isolates with distinct virulence gene profiles were examined to explore genotype–pathogenicity relationships. Future inclusion of reference strains would enhance benchmarking and support broader comparability of findings. The observed association between the diversity of virulence determinants and the severity of infection aligns with an earlier experimental study71, supporting the concept that pathogenicity in P. aeruginosa is governed by the cumulative and interactive effects of multiple virulence factors49,72. The present findings are consistent with Devakumar73, who reported widespread degenerative alterations across multiple tissues in crabs infected with P. aeruginosa. Similar observations have been reported in O. niloticus experimentally challenged with P. aeruginosa, showing pronounced clinical signs of septicemia28. These pathological changes are likely attributable to the deleterious effects of bacterial toxins, extracellular enzymes, and other bioactive factors that drive tissue damage and cellular necrosis74.

Study limitations

Sampling was restricted to Port Said fish farms, limiting the broader representation of P. aeruginosa diversity. Future studies should include wider geographic sampling. We acknowledge that MIC determination would provide more accurate resistance classification, particularly for colistin, where disc diffusion is limited. Disc diffusion may also fail to detect low-level imipenem resistance. Future studies should therefore include MIC testing for critically important antimicrobials. The single-locus oprL marker has limited epidemiological resolution compared with MLST or WGS; thus, future studies should use higher-resolution approaches for improved strain discrimination and epidemiological inference.

Conclusion

P. aeruginosa is a recurrent and important emerging pathogen in O. niloticus. The isolates commonly harbor multiple antibiotic-resistance genes conferring resistance to commonly used aquaculture antimicrobials, along with key virulence determinant genes that synergistically contribute to severe infections. The combination of high virulence, widespread antimicrobial resistance, and genetic similarity to human strains suggests that P. aeruginosa in aquaculture represents a potential dual threat to fish health and public health. While direct zoonotic transmission was not demonstrated in this study, the presence of resistance genes and virulence factors in fish isolates raises concerns about the potential for transmission through the food chain. Effective control requires an integrated approach involving routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing and continuous molecular surveillance to manage outbreaks and limit the spread of resistant and virulent strains.
