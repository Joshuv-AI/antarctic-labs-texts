Effectiveness of community-based physical activity models in improving quality of life among elderly: A systematic review
Authors: Indriani, Isna Qadrijati, Agus Kristiyanto, Arsita Eka Prasetyawati, Ahmad Nasrullah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of community-based physical activity models in improving the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of quality of life (QoL) among community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and above.DesignSystematic review.Data sourcesPubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect (January 2020 to July 2025).Selection of studiesEmpirical studies, including randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental, and pre–post designs, focusing on community-based physical activity programs for older adults aged ≥60 years compared to inactive controls, standard care, or non-physical interventions.Data extractionIndependent data extraction and risk of bias assessment using Cochrane RoB 2 and ROBINS-I tools. A systematic narrative synthesis adhering to Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) guidelines was conducted.ResultsThirteen studies involving 2256 older adults were included. Multicomponent programs yielded the most consistent improvements. Significant physical findings included a fall incidence reduction from 51.8% to 31.4%, an 0.81-point increase in Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) scores, and a 7.5-point increase in the Barthel Index. Cognitive outcomes improved, evidenced by a 3-point average increase in Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) scores. Psychologically, resistance and yoga interventions reduced psychological distress by approximately 18%, while high-intensity aerobic exercise yielded an 8.6-point decrease in Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) scores.ConclusionsCommunity-based, multicomponent physical activity interventions sustained for ≥12 weeks at a frequency of 2–5 sessions per week are highly effective in improving physical, cognitive, and psychological outcomes. Integrating culturally adapted practices and digital technologies enhances intervention feasibility, adherence, and long-term engagement.

Introduction

The growing population of older adults is a global phenomenon that demands special attention in terms of health and quality of life. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the proportion of people aged 60 years and older is projected to reach 22% by 2050. With advancing age, older adults tend to experience declines in physical, cognitive, and social functions, all of which can negatively impact their quality of life. Aging leads to a decrease in physical function (such as muscle strength, mobility, and organ health), an increased risk of chronic diseases (e.g., cardiovascular disease, diabetes, osteoarthritis, dementia), and a decline in cognitive and mental functions.1, 2, 3

Physical activity has consistently been shown to improve physical health (including muscle function, mobility, strength, and balance), mental health (by reducing depression and anxiety), cognitive performance, sleep quality, and overall quality of life in older adults.4, 5, 6, 7 It also lowers the risk of chronic diseases, disability, and premature mortality, while slowing down the decline in daily functioning.4, 5, 6, 8 Even light and regular activities, such as walking or gardening, provide significant benefits.9, 10 However, participation rates in physical activity among older adults remain low, especially in developing countries.11, 12 Common barriers include physical limitations, poor health, lack of motivation, fear of injury, unsupportive environments, and limited access to appropriate facilities or programs.11, 13, 14 Positive determinants, on the other hand, include good physical and mental health, social support, safe environments, and positive perceptions of the benefits of physical activity.11, 14

Community-based physical activity interventions have emerged as a promising approach to increasing participation among older adults by leveraging social support, local facilities, and community engagement.15, 16, 17 Community-based physical activity interventions offer a promising, cost-effective, and highly accessible alternative to clinical programs by leveraging local facilities and social engagement. However, existing studies present inconsistent findings regarding their true impact on older adults’ quality of life, largely due to variations in study design, duration, and outcome measurements.16, 18

Community-based physical activity interventions offer a promising, cost-effective, and highly accessible alternative to clinical programs by leveraging local facilities and social engagement. However, existing studies present inconsistent findings regarding their true impact on older adults’ quality of life, largely due to variations in study design, duration, and outcome measurements.19, 20, 21

Therefore, this systematic review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of community-based physical activity interventions in improving the quality of life among older adults. Specifically, this review seeks to: (1) identify the types of community-based models implemented for individuals aged ≥60 years; (2) compare these interventions with inactive or non-physical controls; and (3) assess their multidimensional impact on physical, psychological, and social QoL to provide evidence-based recommendations.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review, registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251122339), followed the PICOS framework to ensure systematic study selection. We included empirical studies (RCTs, quasi-experimental, and pre–post designs) published in English between January 2020 and July 2025. Eligible studies assessed community-based physical activity programs for older adults aged ≥60 years against inactive or non-physical controls. The primary outcome evaluated was the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of quality of life (QoL). A comprehensive search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect utilizing a combination of MeSH terms and keywords (the full search strategy is available in the Appendix BAppendix. Supplementary Material).

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened the articles using Rayyan software to minimize selection bias. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Inter-rater agreement was substantial, with a Cohen's kappa of 0.84 for initial screening and 0.89 for full-text assessment. Data regarding study characteristics, participant demographics, intervention details, and QoL outcomes were extracted independently using a standardized form.

Quality assessment and data synthesis

Methodological quality and risk of bias were evaluated using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool for RCTs and the ROBINS-I tool for non-randomized studies. Due to significant heterogeneity across study designs, participant characteristics, and intervention types, a meta-analysis was not feasible. Instead, a systematic narrative synthesis and a vote-counting approach were conducted, strictly adhering to the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guidelines. Additionally, the risk of reporting bias was qualitatively assessed by comparing published outcomes with the methodologies specified in the respective studies.

Information sources

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across five major electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect. The search was restricted to studies published between January 2020 and July 2025 to ensure the inclusion of the most contemporary evidence regarding community-based physical activity and quality of life.

Results

Study selection

As illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), the initial database searches yielded a total of 2456 records. After the removal of duplicates, 2232 records remained for title and abstract screening. Following the full-text assessment of potentially relevant articles, 13 studies published between 2022 and 2025 met all eligibility criteria and were included in this systematic review. The included studies comprised 11 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and 2 single-group pre–post intervention studies. The study selection process yielded 13 eligible studies involving a total of 2256 older adults (mean age 65–85 years) from diverse geographical locations, as detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). The included studies predominantly evaluated multicomponent programs combining strength, endurance, balance, and flexibility training. Intervention durations varied up to six months, typically with frequencies of 2–5 sessions per week (Table 1). Impact on physical, cognitive, and psychological function

Multicomponent interventions consistently demonstrated the most robust improvements in physical capacity. These programs significantly enhanced mobility, muscle strength, and dynamic balance, directly contributing to substantial reductions in frailty and fall incidence (e.g., falls decreased from 51.8% to 31.4% in an outdoor park intervention). Furthermore, programs integrating traditional cultural exercises (e.g., Wu Qin Xi) or leveraging digital platforms (e.g., Zoom, WhatsApp) proved highly feasible and effective.

Cognitively, the concurrent delivery of physical exercise and cognitive stimulation – such as motor-cognitive training via virtual reality – emerged as a superior strategy, significantly improving global cognition, memory, and executive function. Psychologically, structured interventions provided substantial mental health benefits; yoga and resistance training reduced psychological distress by approximately 18%, while high-intensity aerobic programs yielded even more pronounced reductions in depressive symptoms, underscoring the vital role of regular physical activity in alleviating age-related mental health burdens.

Discussion

The findings of this systematic review affirm the positive impact of community-based physical activity interventions on the physical, cognitive, and psychological dimensions of quality of life (QoL) in older adults. Consistently, multicomponent programs integrating strength, aerobic, balance, and flexibility training yield superior outcomes compared to single-modality exercises.5, 18, 32 From a physical perspective, these models enhance mobility and independence while reducing the risk of falls.24, 30, 31 Cognitively, the combination of physical activity and mental stimulation is proven to improve memory and executive function.23, 24, 34 Regarding psychological well-being, structured interventions effectively alleviate depressive symptoms, with their effectiveness often depending on the program's intensity and frequency (dose-dependent).12, 35

Although the majority of outcomes are positive, some studies reported statistically non-significant improvements in QoL.29, 30, 33 This is likely due to the use of less sensitive generic QoL measurement tools, differences in participant demographics, or settings that are less conducive to social engagement.29, 30, 33 Overall, significant enhancements in QoL are predominantly found in interventions of longer duration (>12 weeks) with a regular frequency (2–5 sessions per week).24, 25, 26, 27, 29, 36, 37

Contextual and cultural factors play a vital role in program success. Interventions that integrate local cultural practices or digital platforms demonstrate higher levels of participation and engagement.16 These findings expand upon previous literature by highlighting the multidimensional impact on QoL as well as the role of modern integration strategies in overcoming adherence barriers.35 However, this review has several limitations, including small sample sizes, intervention heterogeneity, risk of bias in non-randomized designs, and incomplete reporting of adherence in some studies.35

However, this systematic review has several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, there is a potential for selection bias; it is highly possible that the older adults who agreed to participate in these community-based programs were already more motivated or physically less frail than the general older adult population, which could affect the external validity of the findings. Second, the limited duration of the interventions with several studies implementing programs for less than six months may restrict the long-term applicability of the results and the evaluation of sustained adherence. Third, because the literature search was explicitly restricted to studies published from January 2020 onwards to capture the most contemporary evidence, it is probable that some relevant studies published prior to this date were excluded. Finally, other inherent limitations include small sample sizes in certain trials, intervention heterogeneity across different community settings, risk of bias in non-randomized designs, and incomplete reporting of adherence rates in some of the included studies.

Conclusions

Community-based multicomponent physical activity interventions significantly improve physical, cognitive, and psychological quality of life in older adults. Effectiveness is maximized in programs sustained for ≥12 weeks, with a frequency of 2–5 sessions per week, and those incorporating cultural or digital adaptations. These findings provide a strong empirical basis for policymakers and practitioners to develop sustainable health strategies for the aging population.
