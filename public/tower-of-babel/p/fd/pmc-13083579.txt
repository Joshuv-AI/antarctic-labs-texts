Immunotherapy Administration and Survival for Stage IV Melanoma Patients at Minority‐Serving Hospitals
Authors: Mackenzie M Mayhew, Zoe Hemmer, Elizabeth J Olecki, Jacob A Sellers, Nidhi Kuchimanchi, Lynn T Dengel, Craig L Slingluff, Russell G Witt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundImmunotherapy has transformed outcomes in stage IV melanoma, yet disparities in access persist. Minority‐serving hospitals (MSH) disproportionately care for socioeconomically disadvantaged and minority patients, but their impact on immunotherapy use and survival in melanoma is not well defined.MethodsAdults with stage IV melanoma diagnosed from 2012 to 2020 were identified in the National Cancer Database. MSH were defined as the top decile of institutions by proportion of minority patients with melanoma. Outcomes included immunotherapy receipt and overall survival (OS). Logistic regression assessed predictors of immunotherapy receipt. Cox proportional hazards modeling evaluated OS.ResultsAmong 19,469 patients, 1279 (6.6%) were treated at MSH. MSH patients were more often Hispanic (20.6% vs. 1.4%), from lower‐income and less‐educated communities, and had Medicaid or no insurance (all p < 0.001). Immunotherapy was less frequently used at MSH (35.7% vs. 44.7%, p < 0.001). On multivariable analysis, treatment at MSH predicted a lower likelihood of immunotherapy receipt (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.61–0.81, p < 0.001) and worse OS (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.07–1.36, p = 0.003).ConclusionsStage IV melanoma patients treated at MSH were less likely to receive immunotherapy and had worse survival. These findings highlight institutional disparities beyond patient‐level disadvantage and the need for interventions to strengthen access to life‐prolonging therapies at MSH.Keywords: immunotherapy, metastatic melanoma, minority‐serving hospital

Introduction

Melanoma is the fifth most commonly diagnosed cancer in the United States, with an estimated 100,000 new diagnoses annually [1], with approximately 4%–5% presenting with stage IV disease [2, 3]. Prior to the advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors, the predominant form of immunotherapy in current practice, outcomes for metastatic melanoma were poor, with median survival under one year and five‐year survival below 10% [4, 5].

The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors has revolutionized the treatment of advanced melanoma. In 2011, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved ipilimumab, an anti‐CTLA‐4 antibody, followed by pembrolizumab, a PD‐1 inhibitor, in 2014. By enhancing the body's immune response to tumor cells, these therapies provide durable disease control far superior to chemotherapy. Since their introduction, five‐year overall survival has risen to over 50%, leading the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) to recommend immunotherapy as the standard of care for stage IV melanoma [6, 7].

Despite these therapeutic advances, the adoption and application of immunotherapy treatment have varied over time and patient populations. National analyses demonstrate that immunotherapy utilization among patients with stage IV melanoma increased from approximately 10% between 2004 and 2010 to 43.5% by 2015–2016, with more recent estimates suggesting over 60% of patients received immunotherapy by 2019 [8, 9]. However, disparities in access persist. Prior studies suggest that racial and ethnic minority patients, as well as those with lower socioeconomic status or limited access to specialized care, may be less likely to receive guideline‐concordant treatment, including immunotherapy [10, 11, 12]. In addition, immunotherapy is associated with a substantial financial burden, with monthly costs of commonly used immune checkpoint inhibitors ranging from $11,000 to $15,000, often exceeding the average monthly household income in the United States [13, 14]. Although most FDA‐approved immunotherapies are covered by Medicare and private insurance, patients frequently incur out‐of‐pocket expenses such as deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance, which may further contribute to disparities in access to these therapies [15, 16]. Yet, little is known about how treatment patterns and outcomes differ for patients treated specifically at minority‐serving hospitals (MSH), institutions that care for a disproportionately high number of underrepresented minority patients.

This study aimed to assess whether patients with stage IV melanoma treated at minority‐serving hospitals, irrespective of race or socioeconomic status, had equal access to immunotherapy and comparable overall survival to those treated at non‐minority‐serving hospitals. We hypothesized that patients treated at MSH are less likely to receive immunotherapy and experience worse overall survival independent of individual sociodemographic characteristics.

Methods

Data Source

Data were obtained from the National Cancer Database (NCDB), a hospital‐based cancer registry created by the Commission on Cancer (CoC) of the American College of Surgeons and the American Cancer Society (ACS). NCDB captures approximately 70% of all newly diagnosed cancers in the United States and includes data on demographics, pathology, treatment modalities, and survival [17]. All available data are deidentified and compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPA). The CoC and ACS are not responsible for any analytic or statistical methodology used in this study. This study was exempt from Institutional Review Board approval as it utilized de‐identified data from the National Cancer Database.

Patient Selection

We used data from the NCDB melanoma participant user files from 2004 to 2020. Adults aged 18 or older diagnosed with stage IV melanoma at presentation between 2012 and 2020 were included. The 2012 cutoff was selected based on the FDA approval of immunotherapy for metastatic melanoma in 2011 [18]. Eligible histology codes included 8721/3, 8742/3, 8743/3, and 8745/3. Patients were excluded from analysis if data were missing for race, ethnicity, immunotherapy status, vital status, or follow‐up (Figure 1). Since survival data in the NCDB reflect status at last recorded follow‐up, inclusion required available vital status and follow‐up time, with vital status defined as alive at last follow‐up or deceased rather than at a prespecified landmark time.

Flowchart depicting inclusion and exclusion criteria for patients with stage IV melanoma in the National Cancer Database (2012–2020).

Minority‐Serving Hospital Designation

Minority‐serving hospitals were defined as hospitals in the top decile for the proportion of racial and ethnic minority patients treated for metastatic melanoma. All other hospitals outside of the top decile were categorized as non‐MSH. This definition is consistent with that used in several other studies [19, 20]. Minority status included any patient identifying as Hispanic or of a race other than White.

Variable Construction

The primary outcomes of interest were receipt of immunotherapy and overall survival (OS), defined as the time from diagnosis to death or last follow‐up. The NCDB defines immunotherapy broadly, encompassing various systemic immune‐based therapies. For the purposes of this study, we assume that immunotherapy largely represents immune checkpoint inhibitors, including ipilimumab, pembrolizumab, nivolumab, which were the most widely used agents in this category for stage IV melanoma during the study period. The primary independent variable was treatment at MSH. Covariates included demographics and tumor and facility characteristics. Demographic data included age, sex, race, ethnicity, Charlson‐Deyo score [21], median household income, educational attainment, and insurance status. Socioeconomic variables including median household income and educational attainment were derived from U.S. Census data and reported at the zip code level within the NCDB. These variables represent area‐level socioeconomic measures from the location a patient lives rather than individual patient characteristics. Insurance status was categorized according to NCDB definitions as private insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, other government insurance, uninsured, or unknown. Medicare is a federal insurance program primarily covering individuals aged ≥ 65 years or those with qualifying disabilities, whereas Medicaid is a joint federal‐state program that provides coverage for individuals with limited income. Facility type was collected and classified as academic/research, community cancer program, comprehensive community cancer program, and integrated network cancer program. These classifications are based on institutional case volume, available cancer services, and program structure (Table S1). Receipt of immunotherapy (yes or no) was also collected.

Metastatic disease burden was categorized using metastasis‐at‐diagnosis variables according to the hierarchical structure of the AJCC 8th edition M classification: distant lymph node metastases (M1a), lung metastases (M1b), non‐CNS visceral metastases (M1c), and CNS metastases (M1d). Serum LDH values required for further subclassification were not available in the NCDB.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared between groups based on MSH‐status using Chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. Descriptive statistics were expressed as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors associated with receipt of immunotherapy. Univariate survival analysis was performed using Kaplan–Meier method via a log‐rank test and Wilcoxon (Breslow) test which places greater weight on earlier events. Survival was compared between patients treated at MSH and those treated at non‐MSH. Multivariable survival analysis was performed using a Cox proportional hazards model adjusting for age, sex, race, ethnicity, income, education, insurance status, Charlson‐Deyo score, receipt of immunotherapy, MSH status, and year of diagnosis. All statistical tests were two‐sided with a p‐value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.4.1).

Results

Of the 19,469 patients that met inclusion criteria, 1279 (6.6%) were treated at a MSH and 18,190 (93.4%) were treated at a non‐MSH. There were a total of 128 (10.1%) hospitals deemed MSH and 1141 (89.9%) deemed non‐MSH. Compared with non‐MSH facilities, MSH hospitals were commonly located in the South (42.2% vs. 34.4%) and West (30.5% vs. 16.5%), and less frequently located in the Midwest (11.7% vs. 29.4%; p < 0.001). MSH facilities were also more likely to be located in metropolitan areas (93.0% vs. 76.9%, p < 0.001). In addition, a greater proportion of MSH institutions were academic or research institutions compared with non‐MSH institutions (30.5% vs. 15.2%, p = 0.011; Table S2). The distribution of institutions in Medicaid expansion versus non‐expansion states did not differ significantly between MSH and non‐MSH hospitals (p = 0.488).

Patient Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

The distribution of sex (p = 0.54) and comorbidity burden (p = 0.18) was similar between MSH and non‐MSH patients (Table 1). However, MSH patients were significantly more racially and ethnically diverse, with 11.0% non‐White patients compared to 1.6% at non‐MSH facilities (p < 0.001). Most notably, 20.6% of MSH patients were Hispanic versus 1.4% at non‐MSH institutions (p < 0.001). MSH patients faced greater socioeconomic disadvantages across multiple domains. Income disparities were significant overall (p < 0.001), with MSH hospitals serving disproportionately more patients from lower‐income areas. Insurance status differed significantly between the groups (p < 0.001), with more patients treated at MSH on Medicaid (12.8% vs. 7.5%) or uninsured (10.5% vs. 3.6%). Educational disadvantage was striking, with 33.5% of MSH patients residing in areas with high rates of limited education, compared with just 13.1% of non‐MSH patients (p < 0.001).

Receipt of Immunotherapy and Timing

Patients treated at MSH received immunotherapy significantly less than those treated at non‐MSH (35.7% vs. 44.7%, p < 0.001). Among those who received treatment, MSH patients experienced longer delays to initiation of immunotherapy from diagnosis (median 55 days vs. 49 days, p = 0.006). Of those who did not receive immunotherapy, the majority did not receive any systemic therapy regardless of treatment facility (58.6% vs. 58.0%) and approximately 25% of patients treated at each facility type received chemotherapy (p = 0.529, Table S3). Multivariable logistic regression demonstrated that MSH patients were 30% less likely to receive immunotherapy (OR 0.70, 95% CI 0.61–0.81, p < 0.001; Figure 2). Additional factors associated with reduced immunotherapy receipt included lower income (lowest income OR 0.74, 95% CI 0.65–0.84, p < 0.001; lower‐middle income OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.76–0.93, p < 0.001; upper‐middle income OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.81–0.97, p = 0.009), lower education levels (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.75–0.98, p = 0.026), Medicaid insurance (OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.56–0.75, p < 0.001), uninsured status (OR 0.49, 95% CI 0.40–0.58, p < 0.001), and higher comorbidity burden (Charlson‐Deyo score 1 OR 0.82, 95% CI 0.75–0.90, p < 0.001, Charlson‐Deyo score ≥ 2 OR 0.78, 95% CI 0.70–0.88, p < 0.001). Conversely, treatment at academic centers increased immunotherapy likelihood (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.40–1.85, p < 0.001) compared to community programs. Interestingly, Black patients (OR 1.70, 95% CI 1.30–2.24, p < 0.001) were more likely to receive immunotherapy than White patients.

Survival

Among 19,469 included patients, 1451 died within 2 years of diagnosis and 7930 had known 2‐year vital status, defined as death within 24 months or follow‐up of at least 24 months. Median follow‐up by reverse Kaplan–Meier was 12.2 months (95% CI 11.8–12.6). Overall survival was significantly shorter for MSH patients (log‐rank p < 0.001 and Wilcoxon p < 0.001; Figure 3). In multivariable Cox regression adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors, MSH care was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of death (HR 1.20, 95% CI 1.07–1.36, p = 0.003; Figure 4). Immunotherapy was associated with a significant survival benefit (HR 0.82, 95% CI 0.77–0.87, p < 0.001). Other predictors of worse survival included Hispanic ethnicity (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.05–1.52, p = 0.012), Medicaid insurance (HR 1.17, 95% CI 1.02–1.34, p = 0.021), and uninsured status (HR 1.48, 95% CI 1.25–1.75, p < 0.001). Protective factors included treatment at academic centers (HR 0.73, 95% CI 0.64–0.83, p < 0.001) or comprehensive community cancer centers (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.74–0.96, p = 0.011), and diagnosis with nodular (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.74–0.93, p = 0.002) or superficial spreading melanoma (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.66–0.93, p = 0.005). Notably, income and education were not independently associated with survival after adjusting for insurance status and other factors. Metastatic site was also not associated with survival after adjustment.

Effect of MSH Care Across Melanoma Subtypes

There were no statistically significant interactions between MSH status and melanoma histologic subtype (overall interaction p > 0.05). While MSH patients with acral lentiginous melanoma exhibited a trend toward worse survival (HR 2.32, 95% CI 0.69–7.83), this finding was not statistically significant. The adverse survival effect of MSH care appeared consistent across all histologic subtypes. Certain histologic subtypes, including desmoplastic melanoma (OR 1.94, 95% CI 1.33–2.85, p < 0.001), nodular melanoma (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.16–1.48, p < 0.001), and superficial spreading (OR 1.31, 95% CI 1.07–1.60, p = 0.008) were associated with higher immunotherapy receipt.

Discussion

Since the approval of immunotherapy for use in stage IV melanoma, overall survival has markedly improved [7, 22]. Using a national cohort, we evaluated immunotherapy use and survival in patients with stage IV melanoma treated at institutions serving a disproportionately high number of minority patients (MSH) compared with non‐MSH. Although previous studies have documented disparities related to race, insurance status, and socioeconomic status in melanoma, this is the first to evaluate the impact of MSH designation on immunotherapy receipt and survival in advanced melanoma [8, 10, 11, 12, 23]. We found that care at MSH was independently associated with lower receipt of immunotherapy and worse overall survival. These disparities persisted even after adjusting for demographic, clinical, socioeconomic, and facility‐level factors.

Prior work across multiple cancers has shown that care at MSH may be limited by constrained resources, slower adoption of novel therapies, and reduced guideline adherence, leading to worse outcomes [19, 20, 24, 25, 26]. Although the NCCN recommends immune checkpoint inhibitors as first‐line therapy for stage IV melanoma, substantial barriers to access remain [6]. Consistent with earlier studies, we found that patients facing socioeconomic disadvantage, including those with Medicaid or no insurance, lower income, and lower educational attainment, were significantly less likely to receive immunotherapy [13, 27, 28]. Insurance status was one of the strongest predictors of overall survival, with Medicaid and uninsured patients experiencing worse outcomes than those with private coverage. This is not unexpected given that a full course of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy can exceed $250,000, excluding indirect costs such as lost wages and management of immune‐related adverse events [13, 29, 30]. Medicaid expansion under the Affordable Care Act increased eligibility for public insurance coverage among low‐income adults beginning in 2014 and has been associated with improved access to cancer care and systemic therapies [31]. However, despite geographic clustering of MSH in certain regions, we did not observe meaningful differences in the distribution of MSH and non‐MSH institutions between Medicaid expansion and non‐expansion states. Overall, patients treated at MSH were disproportionately concentrated in the lowest income quartile, more likely to be insured by Medicaid or uninsured, and more often from areas with lower educational attainment, factors that may reduce health literacy, limit awareness of treatment options, and complicate navigation of complex care pathways.

Black patients had higher odds of receiving immunotherapy compared with White patients, and adjusted survival analysis showed no significant mortality difference. This finding may reflect differences in referral patterns or treatment settings, as minority patients with advanced melanoma may be more likely to receive care at large academic or research centers where immunotherapy adoption has historically been higher. Alternatively, this association may reflect residual confounding related to disease presentation or unmeasured socioeconomic factors. In contrast, Hispanic patients experienced significantly worse survival, suggesting they represent a particularly vulnerable group within the advanced melanoma population, especially at MSH. These findings differ from prior studies that reported lower immunotherapy use among racial and ethnic minorities [10, 11, 12, 32], indicating that previously observed disparities may, at least in part, reflect systemic and institutional factors rather than inherent differences in treatment allocation or tumor biology. Importantly, treatment at MSH was associated with worse outcomes regardless of race or ethnicity, underscoring the role of institution‐level inequities, such as challenges in timely access and limited resources, in shaping survival. Within this context, Hispanic patients experienced the greatest disadvantage as ethnicity seemed to compound these disadvantages. Language barriers and health literacy may also contribute to differences in treatment utilization. Patients with limited English proficiency may face challenges navigating complex oncology care, including understanding treatment options and accessing specialized therapies. In our cohort, MSH treated a substantially greater proportion of Hispanic patients; however, the NCDB does not capture information on primary language or interpreter services. Differences in language access resources across institutions may therefore represent an additional contributor to disparities in immunotherapy access. These results should be interpreted with caution, given the relatively small number of minority patients with stage IV melanoma and the potential for selection bias among those who reach these treatment centers. Age was not independently associated with overall survival in the adjusted analysis. This may reflect the advanced‐stage nature of the cohort, in which outcomes are largely driven by disease burden and receipt of immunotherapy, as well as adjustment for comorbidity using the Charlson‐Deyo score.

Although treatment at academic and comprehensive cancer centers was associated with improved survival, MSH designation remained an independent predictor of worse outcomes. A slightly greater proportion of MSH patients were treated at community cancer centers, which historically adopt novel therapies at a slower pace and may partly explain these findings. However, inferior outcomes persisted despite a larger proportion of MSH compared to non‐MSH patients also being treated at academic facilities, which are typically associated with improved outcomes in melanoma [8, 33]. This pattern suggests that structural barriers within hospitals disproportionately serving minority and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations may blunt the survival advantage usually conferred by academic centers. Importantly, minority‐serving hospitals are typically underfunded compared with hospitals serving predominantly White populations, having fewer capital resources for infrastructure and service expansion [34, 35]. These financial disparities may limit the ability of MSH to rapidly adopt novel therapies, invest in specialized oncology programs, and support the multidisciplinary care required for advanced melanoma management, thereby contributing to persistent outcome differences despite similar facility designations.

This study shows that treatment at MSH is associated with both lower immunotherapy use and worse survival independent of race, ethnicity, and other patient‐level factors. These findings highlight institution‐level disparities, beyond individual patient characteristics, as a critical but underexplored contributor to inequitable cancer outcomes in the era of immuno‐oncology. Underutilization of immunotherapy at MSH suggests that structural barriers within hospitals play a key role in limiting access to life‐prolonging therapies and driving inferior outcomes. Addressing these structural inequities in access to life‐prolonging therapies will be essential to improving outcomes for patients with advanced melanoma and reducing disparities across cancer care more broadly.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. The NCDB may exhibit selection bias, as it represents only a portion of cancer patients treated in the United States given it is hospital‐based. Our definition of MSH relied on each institution's racial and ethnic distribution of patients with stage IV melanoma, which may not fully reflect the hospital's overall minority‐serving status. Although the use of a large, national database allows analysis of a broad population, the study is limited by the variables captured. Specifically, the immunotherapy variable does not distinguish between drug types, preventing confirmation of which patients received immune checkpoint inhibitors and limiting the ability to attribute outcomes specifically to this therapy class. While the NCDB captures overall survival, it does not record cause‐specific mortality, preventing distinction between melanoma‐related deaths and deaths due to other causes. The follow‐up duration was also relatively limited, which may limit interpretation of longer‐term survival. Additionally, the NCDB does not capture information on primary language spoken at home, measures of health literacy, or access to interpreter services, which may influence healthcare access, communication, and treatment utilization. Resources or funding provided at an institution‐level, rather than insurance or individual‐level income, may also contribute to the findings of this study but were unable to be assessed given the variable limitations.

Conclusions

In this national study of patients with stage IV melanoma, treatment at minority‐serving hospitals was independently associated with lower receipt of immunotherapy and worse overall survival, even after accounting for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical factors. These findings show that disparities in advanced melanoma reflect not only patient factors but also institutional barriers at minority‐serving hospitals. Addressing these barriers will require system‐level interventions targeting resources, supportive services, and care delivery to narrow survival gaps and improve equity.

Data Availability Statement

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the American College of Surgeons' National Cancer Database (NCDB). Restrictions apply to the availability of these data, including an application to obtain the data. Data are available to obtain from the NCDB with the permission of the American College of Surgeons via application.
