Multilayered structure on titanium implants promotes diabetic osseointegration through synergetic regulation of oxidative balance and immune homeostasis in microenvironment
Authors: Yao Xiao, Gaoyang Wang, Maowen Chen, Huan Yang, Xiaohong Zhang, Zhongxue Wang, Qian Chen, Zihui Xia, Liangliang Dai, Chenghu Hu, Zhang Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus (DM) is a metabolic disease characterized by hyperglycemia and chronic inflammation, which is considered a relative contraindication for implantation in clinical practice. In the pathological microenvironment of DM, the endless vicious cycle of oxidative stress-immune disorder-chronic inflammation severely inhibits new bone formation, causing the stagnant healing and poor osseointegration. Herein, we design a multilayered structure composing of 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid modified chitosan (CS-GA) and gelatin (Gel) embedded with trimanganese tetroxide nanozymes (Mn3O4) on the surface of titanium implant through layer-by-layer self-assembly technique (LBL), namely LBL-GA@Mn. In vitro biological tests show that under the action of Mn3O4, LBL-GA@Mn effectively alleviates the intracellular adverse state of oxidative stress and hypoxia while restoring mitochondrial function in macrophages stimulated by simulated DM microenvironment. At the same time, given the immunomodulatory effects of CS-GA, the multilayered structure reprograms macrophages into anti-inflammatory M2 type by activating oxidative phosphorylation and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, effectively ameliorating inflammatory responses and exerting osteogenic cascade effects. Consequently, the conditioned medium collected from macrophages on LBL-GA@Mn significantly accelerates the osteoblast differentiation. More importantly, in a bone defect model of T2DM rat, the LBL-GA@Mn implant can achieve a satisfactory result of anti-inflammation and implant-osseointegration through the synergetic regulation of oxidative balance and immune homeostasis. This study provides an effective surface modification strategy of implant for the synergistic treatment of diabetic bone injury.Keywords: Surface modification, Osseointegration, Macrophages, Anti-oxidative stress, Immunomodulation

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is one of the three major chronic diseases affecting global human health. According to the International Diabetes Federation in 2021, nearly 5.37 billion adults worldwide currently suffer from diabetes, and it is expected that the number of patients will climb to 7.83 billion by 2045 [1]. Recently, the negative impact of diabetes on the skeletal system has attracted increasing attention. In general, the bone turnover rate decreases in diabetes, and the property and microstructure of bone materials are changed, which increases the risk of fractures, becoming a new serious complication of diabetes [2,3]. Clinically, the treatment of diabetic bone injury has always been a huge challenge. Many surgeries involving joint replacement or implantation are affected by delayed and impaired bone regeneration, increasing surgical risks and treatment difficulties.

Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are widely used in the field of orthopedics due to their excellent biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, and good mechanical properties [4]. Clinical practices have demonstrated that some diabetic patients could improve implant success rates by actively controlling blood glucose levels while supplementing with calcium and vitamin D. However, some DM patients still had a relatively high risk of poor bone healing and implant loosening. All in all, DM remains a relative contraindication for implantation in the field of dentistry and orthopedics. In recent years, surface modification strategies have enabled Ti-based materials to further develop into bioactive implants, enhancing their early-stage stability at the implant site, such as coating with related bioactive factors, nanomaterials, or changing the surface roughness. However, these strategies focusing solely on osteogenesis stimulation cannot effectively ameliorate the extreme pathological microenvironments of DM such as local high glucose and oxidative stress that interfere with bone homeostasis and affect the calcium/phosphorus metabolism, thus hindering the implant-osseointegration process. As a consequence, the healing time of bone injury in DM patients will be prolonged, and the risk of bone nonunion and re-dislocation is still high [[5], [6], [7]]. Therefore, it is urgent to develop effective surface modification strategy of titanium implant to improve the pathological microenvironment for the repair of diabetic bone injury.

In addition to the metabolic disorders and chronic inflammation caused by hyperglycemia, the two major factors that seriously hinder bone repair in the diabetic microenvironment are excessive oxidative stress and imbalanced immune homeostasis [8,9]. The accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in diabetic bone injury sites can cause mitochondrial dysfunction, activate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and inhibit the intracellular antioxidant system, thereby resulting in oxidative stress. At the same time, excessive ROS will further damage mitochondria, leading to more serious mitochondrial dysfunction [10]. In addition, oxidative stress induces pro-inflammatory immune cells to continuously infiltrate into bone injury sites, resulting in immune disorders [11,12]. A large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines will aggravate the inflammatory response. Consequently, the endless vicious cycle of oxidative stress-immune disorder-chronic inflammation severely inhibits the physiological function of osteoblastic related cells, thereby hindering the bone repair process [[13], [14], [15]]. Therefore, the development of titanium implants that have antioxidant stress and immunomodulatory function is an effective strategy to comprehensively improve the pathological microenvironment and promote diabetic osseointegration.

In recent years, nanozymes based on the transition metal manganese (Mn) have attracted more and more attention from researchers due to their low cost, multivalence, and excellent catalytic activity. The transformation of multiple valence states enables Mn-based nanozymes (MnO2, Mn3O4, and other Mn-doped nanoparticles) to exhibit excellent antioxidant activity [16]. They can effectively resist oxidative stress damage and are considered as ideal nanomaterials to be applied in the treatment of inflammatory bone diseases. For example, the chondroitin sulfate hydrogel containing Mn3O4 was used to treat osteoarthritis by protecting chondrocyte from oxidative damage caused by uncontrolled inflammatory response by removing excessive ROS in the osteoarthritis microenvironment [17]. Hollow silicon nanoparticles doped with manganese porphyrin (Mn2+) effectively resisted oxidative stress by mimicking the SOD and CAT activity, thus synergistically alleviating the cartilage damage in rheumatoid arthritis [18]. The composite hydrogel containing MnO2 nanosheet coated CaP microspheres could consumes excessive H2O2 in the microenvironment of osteoporotic bone defect, thus reducing oxidative damage and finally accelerating the repair of osteoporotic bone defect [19]. In this study, we attempted to integrate Mn-based nanozymes into the interface of Ti implants and explored its potential to improve diabetic osseointegration by alleviating local oxidative stress and inflammation.

Resistance to oxidative stress alone is often not effective in promoting implant-osseointegration under DM microenvironment, and the remodeling of immune homeostasis should be fully considered. Macrophages play a vital role in osteo-immunomodulation and bone remodeling processes [20]. Macrophages have high plasticity and are influenced by the local bone regeneration microenvironment. They polarize into different phenotypes to perform different functions and adapt to changes in their ecological niche [21]. M1-type macrophages can not only serve as precursors of osteoclasts (OCs) and differentiate into mature OCs but also secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines to promote bone resorption. By comparison, M2 macrophages secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines or growth factors that stimulate the differentiation and mineralization of osteoblast precursors and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells [22]. A large number of studies have shown that the bioactive interface regulated innate immunity by inducing macrophage M2 polarization, which could accelerate bone repair under pathological conditions. For example, the sitagliptin-rich hydrogel coating significantly promoted osseointegration by recruiting more M2-type macrophages to the implant site [23]. Cerium-tannic acid coating could regulate M2 polarization of macrophages, reshapes the immune homeostasis around implants, and effectively promotes diabetic osseointegration [24]. Our recent study showed that the CO gas release interface could not only effectively inhibit bacterial infection, but also activate the expression of antioxidant enzyme (heme oxygenase-1) to promote macrophage M2 polarization, thus inducing osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and ultimately promoting osseointegration under diabetic infection conditions [14,25]. In view of this, endowing the implant with osteo-immunomodulatory function (M2 polarization) provides a new direction for promoting diabetic osseointegration.

The researches on the ‘drug-device combination’ of biomaterials and active ingredients of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) to play an immunomodulatory role and accelerate the tissue repair have become a hot research topic [26,27]. 18β-Glycyrrhetinic acid (GA) is an active ingredient extracted from licorice with a pentacyclic triterpenoid structure, which has immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects, and has no obvious adverse reactions [28]. For example, GA liposomes encapsulated alginate microcapsules could drive M2 macrophage polarization and alleviate inflammatory response through controlled release of GA, thus accelerating the diabetic wound healing [29]. Inspired by this, GA can serve as an immunomodulators with promising application prospects in clinical practice of osteo-immunomodulation. It is worth noting that GA alone may be susceptible to oxidative damage by DM microenvironment, greatly reducing its bioavailability. Therefore, an antioxidant system can be constructed on the implant surface to effectively maintain the biological activity of GA.

Taken together, in this study, we designed a multilayered structure composing of 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid modified chitosan (CS-GA) and gelatin (Gel) embedded with Mn3O4 nanozymes on the surface of titanium implant through layer-by-layer self-assembly technique (LBL), namely LBL-GA@Mn, targeting two key factors in DM microenvironment-excessive oxidative stress and imbalanced immune homeostasis. The abilities of LBL-GA@Mn to exert anti-oxidative stress, immunomodulation, and cascaded osteogenic differentiation were explored in a bone defect model of T2DM rat (Scheme 1). This work provides an insight into the development of implant surface modification strategies for the comprehensive treatment of diabetic bone injury, and highlights the importance of functional surfaces aimed at improving the pathological microenvironment.

Materials and methods

Materials

Manganese acetate, 18β-glycyrrhizin acid (GA), N-Hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), 1-(3-Dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC), and N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) were purchased from Aladdin. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was purchased from Servicebio. Polyethyleneimine (PEI) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Titanium disks and titanium implants were purchased from Northwest Institute of Nonferrous Metals (Shaanxi, China).

Synthesis and characterization of Mn3O4-BSA

0.408 g of manganese acetate was dissolved in 20 mL of anhydrous ethanol, and the solution was magnetically stirred to turn pink, and then the solution was transferred to the reaction kettle, and the reaction was carried out at 120 °C for 24 h. After the reaction was completed, the reaction kettle was cooled to room temperature, and the brown reaction solution was collected by centrifugation (12000 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min), washed with anhydrous ethanol and deionized water for three times, and then placed in anhydrous ethanol for preservation, and the resulting product was Mn3O4 nanoparticles. Mn3O4 (1 mg/mL) was slowly added dropwise to BSA solution (1 mg/mL), and the mixed solution was ultrasonicated in an ice bath for 2 h. Subsequently, the mixed solution was collected and centrifuged (12000 rpm, 4 °C, 10 min) and washed three times with deionized water to remove unbound BSA. Finally, the product was dispersed in appropriate amount of deionized water, and the resultant product was Mn3O4-BSA. The zeta potential of samples was measured by Nanoparticle Size and Zeta Potential Analyzer (Bruker, 90 Plus PALS). The diffraction peaks of samples were measured by the X-ray diffraction pattern (XRD, Bruker, D8 DISCOVER A25). The elemental composition of the samples was measured by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Kratos, Axis Supra). The UV–vis spectra were recorded using a HITACH UV–vis spectrophotometer (UH 5300, Japan).

Synthesis of CS-GA

0.5 g of low molecular weight chitosan powder (CS) was weighed and added to 100 mL of 1 wt% acetic acid solution with magnetic stirring until the solution was clear and transparent. Meanwhile, 60 mg of 18β-glycyrrhizin acid (GA) was weighed and added to 15 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide and stirred magnetically until dissolved. Then, 72 mg of EDC and NHS (mass ratio, EDC/NHS:GA = 1.2:1) were weighed to activate the GA solution and magnetically stirred at room temperature for 4 h until the solution became clear. Next, the activated GA solution (15 mL) was slowly added dropwise to the CS solution (24 mL) and stirred continuously for 72 h at room temperature. During the experiment, the pH of the reaction solution was maintained at 4.5–5.0. Finally, the mixed solution was dialyzed with distilled water for 3 days and then freeze-dried for 24 h to obtain the final product, namely CS-GA. The zeta potential measurement was performed to investigate whether the conjugation of GA would affect the polyelectrolyte property of CS. Specific experimental procedures are as follows: first, 1 mg/mL of CS and CS-GA conjugate solutions (pH = 4.5) were separately prepared; then, the zeta potential was measured using a Malvern Zetasizer (Nano ZS, Malvern, UK). In addition, the chemical compositions of samples were characterized by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (Bruker, Tensor 27), Raman spectrometer (WITec, Alpha300R), and 1H NMR (Bruker, Avance 500), respectively.

Preparation and characterization of LBL-GA@Mn interface

Next, the LBL-GA@Mn interface was fabricated by LBL technique. After immersing Ti in polyethyleneimine (PEI) solution for 30 min, the Ti-based material was washed with distilled water. Thereafter, the samples were alternately immersed in CS-GA solution (2 mg/mL), Mn3O4-BSA NPs (50 μg/mL) and gelatin solution (2 mg/mL) for 10 min each time. After immersion in different solutions, the treated surface was gently rinsed with distilled water to remove the materials that were not integrated onto the interface. After five repetitions, the sample obtained was denoted as LBL-GA@Mn. We performed a test to measure the loading amount of Mn3O4-BSA onto the surface of Ti substrate. In each cycle of LBL, the residual Mn3O4-BSA suspension was collected. In order to facilitate the calculation of the loading amount of Mn3O4-BSA onto the surface of each Ti substrate during each cycle, we collected 40 samples of the residual Mn3O4-BSA suspension together for centrifugating and weighing. Thus, the loading amount onto each Ti substrate could be calculated. After five repetitions, the loading amount of Mn3O4-BSA was approximately 108.75 μg (108.75 ± 15.96 μg).

To obtain the LBL interface, the PEI-modified Ti samples were alternately immersed in CS solution and gelatin solution for 10 min each time. To obtain the LBL-GA interface, the PEI-modified Ti samples were alternately immersed in CS-GA solution and gelatin solution for 10 min each time. To obtain the LBL@Mn interface, the PEI-modified Ti samples were alternately immersed in CS solution, Mn3O4-BSA NPs and gelatin solution for 10 min each time. The surface morphology of the different samples (Ti, LBL, LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, and LBL-GA@Mn) was observed using scanning electron microscopy (FEI, Verios G4) and the elemental composition of the coatings was determined by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FEI, Themis Z). In addition, we reprocessed a batch of samples, including LBL-GA and LBL-GA@Mn, using a titanium foil with a smoother surface to demonstrate the stability of the surface modification of layer-by-layer self-assembly technique. The surface roughness of the materials was observed by atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker, Dimension ICON).

Hydrogen peroxide scavenging activity

Hydrogen peroxide scavenging effect of Mn3O4-BSA NPs was detected using a hydrogen peroxide assay kit. Different concentrations of Mn3O4-BSA NPs (5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 μg/mL) were incubated with 300 mM hydrogen peroxide solution for 30 min, and the supernatant was aspirated and reacted by adding titanium sulfate, and the absorbance was measured at 415 nm. According to the standard curve, the scavenging activity was calculated.

Superoxide anion scavenging activity

The scavenging effect of Mn3O4-BSA NPs on superoxide anion was detected using a total SOD enzyme assay kit (Beyotime). Different concentrations of Mn3O4-BSA NPs (5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 μg/mL) were incubated with WST-8 enzyme/assay working solution for 30 min at room temperature, and then the absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Total antioxidant activity

The total antioxidant activity of Mn3O4-BSA NPs was determined by DPPH method. DPPH contains many hydroxyl radicals (·OH), and its ethanol solution has a characteristic absorption peak at 517 nm. When the free radicals in the solution are reduced by antioxidants, DPPH solution will change from purple to light yellow, and the UV absorbance will decrease. Different concentrations of Mn3O4-BSA NPs (5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 μg/mL) were incubated with DPPH (0.5 mM) at room temperature and protected from light for 30 min. Then the absorbance at 517 nm was measured.

Cell experiments in vitro

Biocompatibility evaluation

RAW264.7 cells (2 × 104 cells) were seeded on different substrates, respectively. After co-culturing for 1, 4, and 7 days, 200 μL of serum-free medium containing 10 % CCK-8 working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Then, the optical absorbance (OD) of the supernatant was measured at 450 nm. Similarly, the cell viabilities of different concentration of Mn3O4-BSA (5, 10, 20, 50, and 100 μg/mL) were evaluated.

Quantification of macrophages adhering onto different interfaces

RAW264.7 cells (2 × 104 cells) were co-incubated with different titanium-based interfaces for 12 h. Then, complete medium containing 25 mM glucose with 100 ng/ml LPS was added to simulate a hyperglycemia inflammatory environment. After culture for another 24 h and aspirating the medium, PBS solution was used to gently wash the macrophages adhered to different interfaces. Then, add 1 mL of 0.25 % trypsin solution and gently pipette to detach the adherent macrophages from the interface. The number of cells was counted using Automated cell counter (Bio-Rad, TC20).

DCFH-DA staining

RAW264.7 cells (2 × 104 cells) were co-incubated with different titanium-based interfaces for 12 h. Then, complete medium containing 25 mM glucose with 100 ng/ml LPS was added to simulate a hyperglycemia inflammatory environment. After culture for another 24 h and aspirating the medium, 200 μl of DCFH-DA solution (10 μM) was added to each well. Cells were incubated with the DCFH-DA solution at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, after washing off the free DCFH-DA, images with the green fluorescence of DCF were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, IX73).

Hypoxia staining

After culture for another 24 h and aspirating the medium, 500 μl of Ru(dpp3) Cl2 fluorescent probe (10 μg/ml) was added to each well. Cells were incubated with the Ru(dpp3) Cl2 solution at 37 °C for 12 h. Finally, after washing off the free [Ru(dpp)3Cl2], images with the red fluorescence of [Ru(dpp)3]2+ were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, IX73).

JC-1 staining

The mitochondrial membrane potential was detected according to the instructions of JC-1 assay Kit (Beyotime). After culture for another 24 h and aspirating the medium, JC-1 staining working solution was mixed with macrophages on different substrates at 37 °C for 20 min, and images were collected by inverted fluorescence microscope (OLYMPUS, IX73). When mitochondrial function is normal, the mitochondrial membrane potential is high and JC-1 forms polymers and emits red fluorescence. In contrast, when there is excess ROS in the mitochondria, the mitochondrial membrane potential decreases and JC-1 forms monomers, which emits green fluorescence.

ATP detection

The ATP level of macrophages on different interfaces was measured by the ATP Detection Kit (Beyotime). Cells were lysed with ATP lysis buffer (200 μL) and centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min. Then, the supernatant (20 μL) was used to mix with the detection working solution (100 μL). After reaction, the luminometer was used to measure the RLU at 562 nm. According to the standard curve, the amount of ATP was calculated.

MDA detection

The MDA level of macrophages on different interfaces was measured by the MDA Detection Kit (Beyotime). Cells were lysed with lysis buffer and centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min. The supernatant (100 μL) was used to mix with the detection working solution (200 μL). After reaction in water bath (100 °C) for 15 min, the 200 μL of mixture was used to measure the absorbance at 532 nm. In addition, the protein concentration was measured by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Seven). According to the standard curve, the amount of MDA was calculated.

SOD activity

The SOD activity of macrophages on different interfaces was measured by the SOD activity test kit (Beyotime). Cells were lysed by sonication with sample preparation solution and centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was incubated with WST-8 enzyme/assay working solution for 30 min at room temperature, and then the absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Immunofluorescence staining

To assess the ability of different titanium-based interfaces to modulate macrophage polarization, the macrophage surface markers CD86 (M1) and CD206 (M2) were labeled by immunofluorescence staining. RAW264.7 cells (1 × 106 cells) were co-incubated with different titanium-based interfaces for 24 h in a hyperglycemia inflammatory environment (25 mM glucose with 100 ng/ml LPS). The cells were gently washed twice with PBS, and then cells were fixed by 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by permeabilization with 0.1 % Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature. After blocking at room temperature for 30 min, the cells were incubated with the corresponding primary antibody (anti-CD86 and anti-CD206 rabbit polyclonal antibody) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, the treated cells were further stained with Cy3-labeled or Alexa Fluor 488-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the samples were blocked with a droplet of fluorescence quenching sealer (containing DAPI), and the images were captured with a Leica TCSSP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Western blot analysis

Western blot was used to detect different protein expression levels. After culture for 24 h, macrophages were collected, and total proteins were extracted using a total protein extraction kit (Sevenbio), and protein concentration was evaluated using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime). After mixing with loading buffer and boiling, 10 μg of protein was loaded onto 10 % SDS-PAGE gels, separated by electrophoresis at 80 V for 30 min. Then, the proteins were further transferred onto 0.22 μm PVDF membranes at 220 mA for 90 min. After blocking with Fast Blocking Western solution (Yeasen) for 10 min, the PVDF membrane with separated proteins was incubated with the corresponding primary antibody (anti-β-actin, anti-Arg-1, and anti-iNOS rabbit polyclonal antibody) at 4 °C overnight. After washing with TBST solution, the membranes were then incubated with goat anti-rabbit horseradish peroxidase-conjugated IgG secondary antibody for 4 h at room temperature. Then, the treated membranes were immersed with SuperSignal HRP ECL work solution, and further were visualized using a gel imaging system. All bands were quantified using Image J software and normalized against β-actin to calculate the relative expression level.

Flow cytometry analysis

After different treatment, the number of CD86-positive and CD206-positive macrophages was further detected by flow cytometry. After culture for 24 h, macrophages were collected into centrifuge tubes using a cell scraper. After centrifugation at 500g for 5 min at 4 °C, the obtained pellets were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and were further blocked with 1 % (w/v) BSA/PBS solution. Then, the cells were incubated with 300 μL of PE-labeled anti-mouse CD86 antibody (1:250) for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. After washing and centrifugation, the pellets were further incubated with 300 μL of Cy5.5-labeled anti-mouse CD206 (MMR) antibody (diluted with a membrane-breaking solution) for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. After washing and centrifugation, the pellets were re-suspended with 500 μL of PBS buffer containing 1 % BSA. Finally, the cell phenotypes were analyzed using flow cytometer (Beckmanm, CytoFLEX).

RT-qPCR

The mRNA expression level of macrophages M1-type marker (TNF-α and iNOS) and M2-type marker (CD206 and Arg-1) was evaluated by RT-qPCR. After culture for 24 h, macrophages were collected and were lysed using TRK lysis buffer. Then, a total RNA extract kit (Omega) was used to extract the total RNA, and PrimeScriptTMRT reagent kit (Takara) was further used to perform the reverse transcription, thereby collecting the cDNA. By adding the cDNA template and the primers, a SYBR Premix EX Taq kit (Takara) was used to monitor the DNA amplification at a Bio-Rad CFX Manager system. The procedure of two-step cycling amplification was: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 39 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 30 s. The relative expression levels were normalized to the GAPDH (housekeeping gene). The primers used in this study were listed in Table S1.

ELISA assay

The levels of inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β) secreted by macrophages were detected using the Simbosun ELISA assay kit. After culture for 24 h, the supernatant media were collected, and according to the manufacturer's instructions, the absorbance values at 450 nm were measured.

Transcriptomic sequencing analysis

The potential mechanism for regulating the physiological functions of macrophages was evaluated by transcriptomic sequencing analysis. After culture for 24 h, DM-stimulated macrophages on Ti and LBL-GA@Mn interfaces were collected, and the total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. The extracted RNA was stored in liquid nitrogen, and IGENEBOOK biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) was commissioned to perform the transcriptomic sequencing and analysis. DESeq2 software was utilized to identify the differential gene expression, and the genes with a p-value <0.05 and fold change >2 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). In addition, R software (version 3.2.0) was further used to achieve the hierarchical clustering analysis of DEGs, thereby displaying the gene expression patterns between different groups and samples. Subsequently, GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs were further performed, and bar charts were drawn using R software to show the screened Top 20 significantly upregulated or downregulated pathways. Finally, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to rank the relative genes.

Collection of the conditioned media from macrophages

RAW264.7 cells (1 × 106 cells) were co-incubated with different titanium-based interfaces for 24 h in a hyperglycemia inflammatory environment (25 mM glucose with 100 ng/ml LPS). Then, the media in different groups were collected as conditioned media. The mixture of conditioned media and new high-glucose DMEM media (1:1, v/v) was used for culture of MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts.

ALP activity evaluation

Osteoblasts (1 × 104 cells) were cultured in the mixture of conditioned media. After 7 days of conditioned culture, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde solution. After washing, the fixed cells were treated using BCIP/NBT Alkaline Phosphatase Staining Kit (Beyotime). Then, stained cells were photographed to observe the representative ALP-positive regions. In addition, after 7 days of conditioned culture, the cells were treated by 1 % Triton X-100 for 30 min. The cell lysate was used to evaluate the ALP activity using the ALP activity detection kit. In addition, the protein concentration was measured by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Seven). According to the manufacturer's instructions, the ALP activity was calculated.

Mineralization level evaluation

Osteoblasts (1 × 104 cells) were cultured in the mixture of conditioned media. After 14 days of conditioned culture, the cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde solution, and were further stained with ARS solution (0.1 %, pH 4.1) for 1 h. After washing, the mineralization nodules of the extracellular matrix were photographed. In addition, the mineralization nodules were dissolved using 500 μL of cetylpyridinium chloride (10 %) for 30 min, followed by detecting the absorbance of the solution at 562 nm.

The expression levels of osteogenic genes

Osteoblasts (1 × 104 cells) were cultured in the mixture of conditioned media. After 14 days of conditioned culture, the total RNA was extracted for subsequent RT-qPCR experiment. The expression levels of osteogenic related genes, including Runx2 and OPN, were evaluated according to the aforementioned method.

Animal experiments in vivo

Fabrication of T2DM rat model

All in vivo animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the approval of the Medical and Experimental Animal Ethics Committee at Northwestern Polytechnical University (Approval No. 202201132) and complied with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory. Six-week-old male SD rats were first acclimatized and fed with a high-sugar and high-fat diet (Research Diets: #D12451) for 4 weeks. After fasting overnight, a one-time intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ) was administered at a dose of 30 mg/kg. One week after the injection, fasting blood glucose was measured by taking blood from the tail vein of the rats, and blood glucose greater than or equal to 16.7 mmol/L was considered as the success of the modelling.

Implantation experiment

30 of T2DM rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (Ti, LBL, LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, LBL-GA@Mn). The surgical instruments were sterilized at high temperature and pressure in advance, and the different implants were sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation. All surgeries were performed in a sterile environment. The body weight of each rat was weighed, and 3 % pentobarbital sodium solution (0.1 mL/100 g) was intraperitoneally injected for anesthesia. Then, the hair on the hind limbs of the rats was shaved, followed by disinfection with iodine. An incision of about 1.0 cm was made in the skin of the knee joint using a sterile scalpel. After carefully incising the muscle, the distal femur was exposed using a surgical forcep. Subsequently, an appropriate implantation hole (approximately 1.5 mm in diameter and 1 cm in length) was drilled along the femur using a surgical drill. Then, different implants (Ti, LBL, LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, LBL-GA@Mn) were carefully inserted into the defect site, and the muscles and skin were sutured. After injection of antibiotics, the rats were kept warm until they were awakened, and they were allowed unrestricted feeding in separate cages.

Preparation of bone tissue sections

After postoperative 7 days and 28 days, T2DM SD rats were euthanized in two batches, respectively, by intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium solution (≥150 mg/kg). The obtained femurs were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and then decalcified using commercial 10 % EDTA solution at room temperature for 4 weeks. Then, implants in different groups were gently removed from the decalcified femurs, and then subjected to gradient dehydration treatment in different concentration of ethanol solution and further xylene infiltration. The treated femurs were performed paraffin embedding and sectioning with approximately 5–8 μm thickness.

Immunofluorescence staining of section

The expression levels of oxidative damage indicator (8-OHdG), hypoxia indicator (HIF-1α), macrophage markers (CD86, CD206, and CD86), and pro-inflammatory factors (IL-6, TNF-α), anti-inflammatory factors (IL-10, TGF-β) were evaluated by immunofluorescence (IF) staining. The sections were treated with dewaxing, antigen repair, permeabilization and blocking respectively. Then, the samples were incubated with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, respectively. After washing, the treated samples were further incubated with Cy3-labeled or Alexa Fluor 488-labeled secondary antibodies for 30 min. Then, the samples were blocked with a droplet of fluorescence quenching sealer (containing DAPI), and the images were captured with a Leica TCSSP8 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Immunohistochemistry staining of section

The expression levels of osteogenic markers (OPN and OCN) were evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining. Then, the pretreated sections were incubated with the corresponding primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, respectively. After washing, the samples were further incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h, followed by treatment with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) detection kit (Gene Tech, GK600505, Shanghai, China). Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, and the images were captured with an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

H&E staining and Masson staining

For H&E staining, the pretreated sections were placed into the hematoxylin dye for 10 min to stain the cell nuclei. After washing, the samples were further placed into eosin dye for 3 min. For Masson staining, the pretreated sections were immersed into the Weigert hematoxylin working solution for 5 min, followed by differentiation in acidic ethanol for 15 s, and were further placed into Masson dye for 5 min. The samples were soaked in Ponceau S dye for 10 min, and then were rinsed with weakly acidic working solution for 1 min. After washing, the samples were treated with 1 % phosphomolybdic acid solution for 5 min, and were rinsed with weakly acidic working solution for 1 min. Finally, the sections were directly stained with aniline blue solution for 2 min. The images of H&E staining and Masson staining were captured with an Olympus optical microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Cytokines detection by ELISA assay

7 days postoperatively, the rat was euthanized, and the upper segment of the femur (including the implant site) was harvested. The implant was removed after crushing, and the bone tissue was accurately weighed and placed in liquid nitrogen. The bone tissue was ground for 5 min using a tissue grinder to achieve a homogenate. The homogenate was then mixed with tissue extract, centrifuged to collect the supernatant, and the levels of pro-inflammatory factors (TNF-α, IL-6) and anti-inflammatory factors (TGF-β, IL-10) in the peri-implant bone tissue were measured using corresponding ELISA kits.

Micro-CT scan and analysis

7 days postoperatively, the femurs in different mice were collected and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. The femurs with different implants were scanned by a micro-CT system (Always Imaging, AX-2000). The multilevel thresholding procedure was set as follow: σ = 0.8, bone threshold = 230 and implant threshold = 700. 3D reconstruction was performed using VG Studio MAX 3.5 correlation analysis software to display the bone formation around the implant. The volume of interest (VOI) was selected as follow: 2 mm below the epiphyseal line and 0.2 mm on each side of the implant. The morphometric parameters of the bone tissues around the implant were analyzed, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabeculae thickness (Tb.Th), bone surface area ratio (BS/BV), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp).

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± standard deviations (n ≥ 3). The number of repeated experiments or the number of experimental samples was indicated in the figure captions. Origin 2022 software was used for the graphics. For statistical analysis, the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's post-hoc test was used to determine the statistical significance among different groups. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 denoted the statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of LBL-GA@Mn

Firstly, Mn3O4 nanoparticles were synthesized by hydrothermal method, followed by surface modification with a layer of bovine serum albumin (BSA) through ultrasound treatment, namely Mn3O4-BSA [30]. As observed in TEM observation (Fig. 1a and b), the morphology of unmodified Mn3O4 nanoparticles still exhibited an aggregated state. In contrast, the Mn3O4-BSA displayed a uniform morphology, showing an improved dispersion with a diameter of approximately 8.41 nm (Fig. 1c). The zeta potentials of Mn3O4, BSA, and Mn3O4-BSA were −2.9 mV, −18.6 mV, and −15.3 mV, respectively. The decrease in zeta potential of Mn3O4 nanozymes after BSA modification suggests that BSA was adsorbed on the surface of Mn3O4 nanozymes (Fig. S1a). Meanwhile, the surface modification of BSA had no obvious effect on the UV–vis spectrum of Mn3O4 (Fig. S1b). The X-ray diffraction pattern (XRD) shown in Fig. 1d indicated that all diffraction peaks of Mn3O4 and Mn3O4-BSA nanoparticles correspond to the standard card of Mn3O4, without additional impurity peaks, confirming that the synthesized material was indeed Mn3O4. The main reason why nanomaterials exhibit the catalytic enzyme-like functions is that the same element exists in different valence states [31]. Therefore, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was further used to analyze the surface properties and valence states of Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes. As shown in Fig. 1e, the presence of C, N, O, and Mn elements in Mn3O4-BSA confirmed the successful synthesis of Mn3O4-BSA. In Fig. 1f, two characteristic peaks were observed at 641.5 eV and 643.21 eV, which were the Mn 2p1/2 and Mn 2p3/2 peaks of Mn3O4-BSA, corresponding to the standard XPS spectrum of Mn3O4. Meanwhile, the Mn 2p3/2 peaks were centered at 641.01 and 642.35 eV, indicating that Mn3O4-BSA had Mn2+/Mn3+ antioxidation sites [30]. Inspired by this characteristic, the ability of Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes to scavenge ROS were tested. As shown in Figs. S2 and 50 μg/ml of Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes exhibited good clearance capabilities for H2O2, ·O2−, and ·OH. With the increase of concentration, the clearance efficiency was further improved. It was worth noting that, when the concentration increased from 50 μg/ml to 100 μg/ml, the cell activity decreased significantly from 83.7 % to 64.7 %, suggesting potential cytotoxicity at high concentrations. Therefore, considering its catalytic efficiency and potential cytotoxicity (Fig. S3), we selected a concentration of 50 μg/ml for the preparation of the multilayered structure on Ti-based interface.

Secondly, chitosan derivative and gelatin (Gel) were employed as polyelectrolytes to construct multilayered structures on titanium surface [32]. As shown in Fig. 1g, we used EDC/NHS as activators to induce an amide reaction between the amino group (-NH2) of chitosan molecules (CS) and the carboxyl group (-COOH) of 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid molecules (GA), thereby synthesizing CS-GA as a cationic polyelectrolyte [33]. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was used to characterize the chemical compositions of CS, GA, and CS-GA. As shown in Fig. 1h, in the molecular structure of GA, a characteristic absorption peak appeared at 1706 cm−1, indicating the stretching vibration of the carboxyl group (-COOH). Meanwhile, a characteristic absorption peak appeared at 1664 cm−1, which attributed to the stretching vibration of the carbonyl group (C=O). In the spectrum of the CS-GA conjugate, the absorption peaks of amide group appeared at 1655 cm−1 and 1600 cm−1, and the characteristic carboxyl group (-COOH) absorption peak of GA at 1706 cm−1 disappeared, indicating the successful synthesis of CS-GA. In addition, Raman spectroscopy was further used to analyze specific groups in CS, GA, and CS-GA molecules. As shown in Fig. S4, due to the C=O stretching vibration of the carboxyl group (-COOH), the Raman spectrum of GA showed a typical characteristic peak at 1667 cm−1. By comparison, in the Raman spectrum of CS-GA, the absorption peak at 1667 cm−1 disappeared, and a new absorption peak appeared at 1645 cm−1, which was due to the C=O stretching vibration in the amide group, proving that GA has been grafted onto CS. The result was consistent with the previous study [33]. As shown in Fig. S5, the zeta potential changed from 1.36 mV of CS to 0.99 mV of CS-GA, indicating that the conjugation of GA reduced the potential of CS, but did not alter its positively charged characteristic. Therefore, the coupling of GA does not affect the polyelectrolyte property of CS. Furthermore, the nuclear magnetic resonance hydrogen spectroscopy (H NMR) of GA and CS-GA was further characterized. As shown in Fig. S6, in the H NMR spectrum of CS-GA, a characteristic peak of the active hydrogen of the amide group was observed at a chemical shift of 6.20, indicating that the reaction between CS and GA formed an amide bond. Meanwhile, the characteristic peaks of the methyl group (-CH3) in GA also appeared in the chemical shift range of 1.0 to 1.5. Therefore, the H NMR results confirmed the successful synthesis of CS-GA. In addition, the hydrogen at a specific position of the glucosamine residues (3.18 ppm) in CS was selected as its quantitative peak, and the C12 olefinic hydrogen of GA (5.38 ppm) was selected as its quantitative peak. The degree of derivatization between the two molecules was directly determined through the ratio of their integral areas. H NMR quantitative results indicated that the derivatization degree of GA in CS-GA was 27.12 %.

Subsequently, a multilayered structure composing of CS-GA and Gel embedded with Mn3O4-BSA was fabricated onto the Ti surface through layer-by-layer self-assembly technique (LBL), namely LBL-GA@Mn. As a control, the different interfaces including LBL, LBL-GA, and LBL@Mn were also prepared. The surface morphology of the different interfaces was observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), as shown in Fig. 1i. The Ti surface showed many scratches caused by sandpaper grinding. In contrast, the surfaces of LBL, LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, and LBL-GA@Mn had relatively fewer scratches after the formation of polyelectrolyte multilayers on Ti surface. Meanwhile, the elemental composition of the LBL-GA@Mn was further analyzed using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 1j). The presence of Mn element indicated that Mn3O4-BSA nanoparticles were successfully deposited on the Ti surface. The surface roughness of different samples was characterized by atomic force microscopy (AFM). As shown in Fig. S7a, the Ra values of Ti disk, LBL-GA, and LBL-GA@Mn were 67.2 nm, 27.8 nm, and 31.4 nm, respectively. In addition, the Ra values of Ti foil, LBL-GA, and LBL-GA@Mn were 11.7 nm, 32.3 nm, and 31.9 nm, respectively (Fig. S7b). The result demonstrated that, whether using rough titanium disk or smooth titanium foil, the Ra value of the LBL-GA@Mn interface remained approximately 30 nm. In addition, the elemental composition in the LBL-GA@Mn surface fabricated using Ti foil also contained the Mn element (Fig. S7c), indicating that Mn3O4-BSA nanoparticles were successfully deposited on the Ti foil surface. These results demonstrated the stability of the surface modification of layer-by-layer self-assembly technique. All the above results indicated that the multilayered structure of LBL-GA@Mn was successfully fabricated.

Antioxidative stress capability of the LBL-GA@Mn interface

The cytocompatibility of designed LBL-GA@Mn interface was studied. As shown in the Fig. S8, after culture for 1, 4, and 7 days, there was no significant difference in the cell viability of macrophages on the LBL-GA@Mn surface compared with the other groups. The results indicated that LBL-GA@Mn has good biocompatibility. In normal physiological conditions, intracellular ROS is decomposed by a series of antioxidant enzymes. However, under DM pathological microenvironment, intracellular ROS induced by hyperglycemia cannot be adequately decomposed, leading to an imbalance between the oxidative and antioxidant systems [34]. Then, we evaluated the antioxidative stress capability of the LBL-GA@Mn interface. The number of macrophages adhering onto different interfaces was firstly evaluated. As shown in Fig. S9, there was no significant difference in the number of macrophages adhering onto different interfaces. In addition, 2,7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) staining was used to detect the ROS level in RAW264.7 cells co-cultured onto different interfaces (Fig. 2a & S10a). The intensity of intracellular green fluorescence could reflect the level of oxidative stress. DM condition obviously activated the oxidative stress of macrophages onto Ti interface. Compared with the Ti and LBL groups, the green fluorescence intensity in the LBL-GA and LBL@Mn was significantly weakened, indicating that the GA molecule or Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes could effectively alleviate oxidative stress, and the clearance ability of Mn3O4-BSA seemed to be stronger. In contrast, the green fluorescence intensity in the LBL-GA@Mn was almost the same as that in the control group, which might be attributed to the synergistic effect of Mn3O4-BSA and GA. As a result, compared with other groups under DM condition, LBL-GA@Mn interface significantly reduced the expression of intracellular 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), which is a biomarker of oxidative DNA damage (Fig. S11) [25]. Subsequently, we further used Ru(dpp)3Cl2 probe to evaluate the intracellular hypoxic conditions. As shown in Fig. 2b & S10b, the trend of hypoxia relief was consistent with the level of intracellular oxidative stress. However, the ability of GA molecules to relieve hypoxia was insufficient, which might be due to the weak CAT activity of GA. These results indicated that LBL-GA@Mn could effectively alleviate the intracellular adverse state of oxidative stress and hypoxia mainly under the action of Mn3O4-BSA.

As one of the important organelles within cells, mitochondria play an important role in participating in metabolism, ATP synthesis, and maintenance of normal physiological functions of cells [35]. We further evaluated the mitochondrial damage of macrophages under the DM condition. The mitochondrial membrane potential evaluated by JC-1 staining could reflect mitochondrial function. The JC-1 forms polymers and emits red fluorescence in normal mitochondria, while forming monomers and emitting green fluorescence in damaged mitochondria [14]. As shown in Fig. 2c & S10c, under DM condition, the green fluorescence was strong and the red fluorescence was weak in the Ti and LBL groups, indicating that the mitochondrial damage and membrane potential of macrophages was significantly reduced. In contrast, in the LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, and LBL-GA@Mn groups, the green fluorescence was reduced and the red fluorescence increased, implying that the recovery of mitochondrial membrane potential was directly related to antioxidant capacity. Therefore, the LBL-GA@Mn interface could restore the abnormal mitochondrial membrane potential to a normal level.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the final products of lipid peroxidation, and the amount of MDA generated can reflect the degree of oxidative damage in mitochondria to a certain extent [24]. With the intensification of oxidative stress, the lipid peroxidation reaction in the mitochondrial membrane is enhanced, and the content of MDA will increase accordingly. At the same time, mitochondria are the main site of ATP synthesis. Mitochondrial damage will directly lead to a decrease of ATP synthesis. As shown in Fig. 2d and e, due to the mitochondrial damage of macrophages in the Ti and LBL groups under DM microenvironment, their MDA content significantly increased, while ATP production decreased significantly. Compared with LBL group, LBL-GA or LBL@Mn treatment effectively alleviated the adverse state, which was attributed to the antioxidant effect of GA molecule or Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes, respectively. Remarkably, the ability of LBL-GA@Mn treatment to reduce MDA content and increase ATP level was significantly better than that of LBL-GA and LBL@Mn groups, demonstrating the synergistic effect of GA molecule and Mn3O4-BSA to ameliorate DM-induced mitochondrial damage. Furthermore, we further evaluated the recovery of intracellular antioxidant system by detecting its SOD activity (Fig. 2f). Macrophages on LBL@Mn group displayed a higher SOD activity compared with LBL group, and LBL-GA@Mn treatment caused the higher SOD activity compared with LBL-GA group, highlighting the favorable function of Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes to restore the intracellular antioxidant system. Indeed, there are two strategies to control and maintain the antioxidative effects of the LBL-GA@Mn interface. Firstly, during each LBL process, by adjusting the input of the Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes onto the multilayered structure, the time of anti-oxidative effects can be controlled. Secondly, the LBL-GA@Mn interface was fabricated by alternately immersing in CS-GA solution, Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes, and gelatin solution for 10 min each time. Therefore, by increasing the number of the LBL process, the time for LBL-GA@Mn interface to exert antioxidative effects can be increased. Therefore, according to the diabetic state, we can properly adjust these two parameters (the input of Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes during each LBL process and the number of the LBL process) to maintain the antioxidative effects of multilayered structure.

Immunomodulatory capability of the LBL-GA@Mn interface

Metabolic disorders in DM patients can affect the metabolism and functional plasticity of macrophages in the bone defect microenvironment. Stimulated by metabolites such as high glucose, inflammation, AGEs, and ROS, the function of macrophages may be impaired, causing them to remain in the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype for a long time and fail to transform into the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype in time, which seriously hinders the normal repair and regeneration of bone tissue [3,36]. We evaluated the ability of the LBL-GA@Mn interface to drive macrophage M2 polarization under simulated DM microenvironment. First, immunofluorescence staining was performed to distinguish the surface biomarkers of M1 (iNOS) or M2 macrophages (CD206). As shown in Fig. 3a, macrophages did not express significant fluorescence signals of iNOS and CD206 in normal condition, indicating that they were in an inactivated state. In contrast, in the DM condition, strong iNOS fluorescence signal was observed in the Ti and LBL groups, indicating that macrophages were polarized into the M1 phenotype, and CS/Gel LBL coating could not alleviate the phenomenon. Interestingly, the iNOS signal in the LBL-GA, LBL@Mn and LBL-GA@Mn groups was significantly attenuated, while the CD206 signal was intuitively enhanced. The result highlighted that the presence of GA molecules or the removal of ROS could effectively transform macrophages from the pro-inflammatory M1 type to the anti-inflammatory M2 type. In addition, based on the WB results in Fig. 3b and S12, compared with Ti and LBL groups, the protein expression of iNOS (M1 marker) was significantly down-regulated in the LBL-GA@Mn group. Meanwhile, DM-treated macrophages on LBL-GA@Mn interface expressed more M2 marker (Arg-1).

To more intuitively evaluate the phenotypic changes of DM-treated macrophages, flow cytometry was further performed to detect the proportions of CD86-positive (M1) and CD206-positive (M2) macrophages. As shown in Fig. 3c and d, CS/Gel LBL coating could not decreased the proportion of M1 macrophages. Compared with the LBL group, LBL-GA or LBL@Mn interface significantly decreased the proportion of CD86-positive macrophages, while significantly increasing the CD206 expression, in line with the result of Fig. 3a. Remarkably, the proportion of M2 macrophages polarized at the LBL-GA@Mn interface was higher than that of LBL-GA and LBL@Mn groups, which further demonstrated that GA molecules and Mn3O4 had synergistic ability to promote macrophage M2 polarization. Subsequently, we further detected the gene expression levels of M1 and M2 type markers in macrophages. As shown in Fig. 3e, compared with the DM + LBL group, the gene expression of Arg-1 in LBL-GA group was significantly up-regulated, while the expression of iNOS and TNF-α was significantly down-regulated. Furthermore, the expression of CD206 and Arg-1 was significantly up-regulated by LBL-GA@Mn compared with LBL@Mn group. These results demonstrated that GA molecules were beneficial to promote macrophage M2 polarization [29].

Given the great anti-oxidative stress and immunomodulatory effect of LBL-GA@Mn interface, the contents of pro-inflammatory (TNF-α, IL-6) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β) secreted by macrophages on different interfaces were evaluated. As shown in Fig. 3f, after induction by LPS and high glucose, activated macrophages in Ti and LBL groups secreted a large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6). In contrast, the contents of TNF-α and IL-6 were significantly decreased in the LBL-GA and LBL@Mn, whereas they displayed an expansionary effect on IL-10 and TGF-β secretion, which was attributed to the potential effect of GA-mediated immunomodulation and Mn3O4 nanozymes-induced anti-oxidative stress. Importantly, the contents of IL-10 and TGF-β were significantly elevated by LBL-GA@Mn compared with LBL-GA and LBL@Mn groups, indicating that LBL-GA@Mn interface could adequately alleviate the proinflammatory response induced by DM pathological microenvironment.

The potential mechanism for regulating the physiological functions of macrophages by transcriptomic sequencing analysis

Through RNA sequencing, we further explored the potential mechanisms by which the LBL-GA@Mn interface reshaped immune homeostasis in DM pathological microenvironment. The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in macrophages pre-treated by high glucose and LPS (DM condition) on the Ti and LBL-GA@Mn interface were detected using RNA sequencing, and the KEGG enrichment analysis on related signaling pathways was performed. As shown in Fig. 4a, there were 4844 DEGs, among which 2536 genes were upregulated and 2308 genes were downregulated, indicating that there was an obvious difference in gene expression of macrophages in DM + Ti and DM + LBL-GA@Mn groups. We further screened the related signaling pathways by using KEGG enrichment analysis.

(b & c) Enrichment analysis of up-regulated and down-regulated signaling pathways in macrophages. (d) Heatmap of differentially expressed genes. As shown in Fig. 4b, compared with the DM + Ti group, DEGs in DM + LBL-GA@Mn group were significantly enriched in signaling pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction, TGF-β signaling pathway, and VEGF signaling pathway. Studies have shown that the main metabolic pathway of M1-type macrophages is glycolysis, while the metabolism of M2-type macrophages mainly involves oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and fatty acid oxidation [37]. Meanwhile, the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway is usually associated with the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses. The activated PI3K-AKT signaling pathway can promote the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 type in various ways [8,38]. For example, it can inhibit inflammation-related signaling pathways, such as the nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway, which typically promotes the polarization of macrophages towards the M1 type and the production of inflammatory factors. Therefore, the activated PI3K-AKT signaling pathway can reduce the production of inflammatory factors (such as TNF-α, IL-1β), thereby creating a favorable anti-inflammatory environment for M2-type polarization. Notably, as shown in Fig. 4c, the DM + Ti group activated many inflammatory related pathways, including the TNF signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, IL-17 signaling pathway, and Th17 signaling pathway, indicating the presence of persistent inflammatory responses in the DM pathological microenvironment. In contrast, the expression of these pro-inflammatory pathway related genes was downregulated in the DM + LBL-GA@Mn group. In addition, the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications was suppressed by DM + LBL-GA@Mn compared with DM + Ti group. Generally, blocking the harmful effects of AGE-RAGE signaling pathway can promote the healing process under DM conditions [39].

Furthermore, we screened the representative DEGs between DM + LBL-GA@Mn and DM + Ti group through a heatmap. As shown in Fig. 4d, compared with the DM + Ti group, the expression of M2-type macrophage marker genes (Arg1, Il10, Tgfbr2), growth factor related genes (Pdgfc, Pdgfa, Vegfb), and antioxidant related genes (Bcl2, Sod2) was significantly up-regulated in DM + LBL-GA@Mn group. In addition, the key genes in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway were also significantly up-regulated (Akt1, Spp1, Csf3, Myc), further indicating that the LBL-GA@Mn interface might affect the function of M1 macrophages by regulating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Meanwhile, compared with DM + Ti group, the expression level of Nod2 gene was significantly down-regulated in the DM + LBL-GA@Mn group. Nod2 activates the NFκB signaling pathway in macrophages by recognizing muramyl dipeptide (MDP) derived from LPS, thus participating in the pro-inflammatory immune response [40]. Consequently, LBL-GA@Mn interface suppressed the expression levels of pro-inflammatory genes (Il17ra, Il6, Tnf, Tlr2, Tlr4, Mmp12). In addition, LBL-GA@Mn interface also alleviated the hypoxia of macrophages caused by DM condition by reducing the expression level of Hif1an.

According to the transcriptomic analysis, we believe that the LBL-GA@Mn interface mainly reprograms macrophages towards M2-type polarization by promoting oxidative phosphorylation and activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby further inhibiting the DM-induced pro-inflammatory response. The oxidative phosphorylation process of macrophages is closely related to mitochondrial function, and the LBL-GA@Mn interface can effectively repair mitochondrial damage and promote ATP synthesis, which indirectly verifies that the LBL-GA@Mn interface can promote the oxidative phosphorylation of macrophages. Subsequently, western blot was performed to evaluate the expression levels of key proteins in the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. As shown in Fig. 4e, compared with the DM + Ti group, the LBL-GA@Mn interface significantly promoted the protein expression of phosphorylated PI3K and phosphorylated AKT in macrophages, activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. These results emphasized that the LBL-GA@Mn interface influenced the physiological functions of macrophages under DM condition, including reprogramming M1 macrophages into M2 type and alleviating the inflammatory response, through regulating the metabolic process (promoting oxidative phosphorylation) and the key pathways (activating the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway).

Evaluation of osteogenic cascade effects by conditioned culture

Macrophages play a crucial role in osteo-immunomodulation and bone remodeling processes [21]. M2-type macrophages are beneficial to bone repair by secreting growth factors that can promote the osteogenic differentiation [41]. Given the fact that LBL-GA@Mn interface elevated the secretion levels of IL-10 and TGF-β in macrophages, we further investigated its osteogenic cascade effects on MC3T3-E1 osteoblasts. The culture media of macrophages co-cultured onto different interfaces were collected and used to evaluate the osteogenic responses (Fig. 5a). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) is an early marker of osteogenic differentiation. After 7 days of conditioned culture, the positive area of ALP staining in the medium from LBL-GA@Mn group was intuitively more than that in the LBL-GA and LBL@Mn groups, and the quantitative detection of ALP activity was also significantly higher than other groups (Fig. 5b and c). Calcification nodules usually appear in the late stage of osteogenic differentiation. After 14 days of conditioned culture, alizarin red (ARS) staining and quantitative mineralization results showed that osteoblasts in the medium of LBL-GA@Mn group had a large number of mineralized calcification nodules, and the degree of mineralization was significantly higher than other groups (Fig. 5b and d). In addition, the expression of osteogenesis-related genes, including Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and osteopontin (OPN), was also detected using qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 5e and f, the gene expression of Runx2 and OPN in the medium of LBL-GA@Mn group was significantly higher than that of other groups. Compared with the LBL group, the expression of osteogenesis-related genes Runx2 and OPN in MC3T3-E1 co-cultured with the conditioned medium collected from the LBL-GA@Mn group was up-regulated by 1.73-fold and 2.33-fold, respectively. The above results indicated that the LBL-GA@Mn interface promotes the polarization of macrophages to the M2 phenotype, which in turn secretes a large amount of anti-inflammatory and pro-generative cytokines, thereby effectively participating in the cascade osteogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization of MC3T3-E1 cells.

In vivo antioxidative stress and anti-inflammatory capability test

The excessive accumulation of ROS in the pathological microenvironment of diabetic bone defects can spread to surrounding bone tissues, causing damage to proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and DNA, leading to cellular oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage, which seriously affects the survival, proliferation, and differentiation of surrounding osteoblasts as well as the osseointegration of implants. A rat model of T2DM was constructed by high-fat and high-sugar feeding and injection of streptozotocin (STZ) to investigate the in vivo antioxidative stress capability of different titanium-based implants [14]. Immunofluorescence staining was performed to mark the oxidative damage marker (8-OHdG) and hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF-1α) in the bone tissue surrounding the different implants. As shown in Fig. 6a and b, the implant sites of the Ti and LBL groups showed strong red fluorescence and green fluorescence, indicating a high degree of oxidative damage and hypoxia under the T2DM pathological microenvironment [10,42]. In contrast, the expression of 8-OHdG and HIF-1α in the LBL@Mn group showed an obvious decreasing trend, implying that the Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes could convert the excessive ROS into O2, thus effectively alleviating the oxidative damage and hypoxic state. Especially, the fluorescence intensity around the LBL-GA@Mn implant showed the lowest level, which might be due to the synergistic effect between GA and Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes. GA could regulate the intracellular antioxidant-related signaling pathways and enhance the activity of antioxidant enzymes, while Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes could directly decompose ROS with their efficient enzyme-like catalytic activity [30,43]. The synergistic action formed a multi-level and all-round antioxidant network, which greatly reduce the oxidative damage to the bone tissue around the implant, thus showing a more adequate antioxidant ability than that of only a single component (GA or Mn3O4-BSA nanoenzyme).

In view of this, we further evaluated the in vivo anti-inflammatory capability. The immunofluorescence staining was performed to mark pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10) in the bone tissue surrounding the different implants (Fig. 6c and d). Due to the excessive ROS and inflammatory infiltration in the T2DM pathological microenvironment, there were a large amount of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) in the implant sites of the Ti and LBL groups, and almost no anti-inflammatory cytokines (TGF-β, IL-10) were observed. Compared with the Ti and LBL groups, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6) displayed varying degrees of decrease in the LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, and LBL-GA@Mn groups, while the expression of anti-inflammatory factors (TGF-β, IL-10) showed an opposite trend. Subsequently, the levels of pro-inflammatory (TNF-α, IL-6) and anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10, TGF-β) were determined by ELISA experiments (Fig. 6e). The contents of TNF-α and IL-6 around LBL@Mn were significantly lower than that of LBL group, which was attributed to the ROS scavenging of Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes. The LBL-GA and LBL@Mn implants were significantly elevated the levels of IL-10 compared to the LBL group, and the content of IL-10 around LBL-GA@Mn was significantly higher than that of LBL-GA and LBL@Mn groups, implying that GA molecules and Mn3O4 nanozymes synergistically endowed the LBL-GA@Mn interface with anti-inflammatory ability. Meanwhile, LBL-GA@Mn interface significantly promoted the expression of TGF-β (a typical regenerative factor) compared with LBL and Ti groups, indicating that it could effectively alleviate the oxidative damage and inflammatory response, providing a more favorable microenvironment for the bone repair around the implant under T2DM condition.

In vivo immunomodulatory capability

Recent studies have demonstrated that functional Ti-based interfaces effectively promoted macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype, thereby accelerating the osseointegration [44,45]. Therefore, immunofluorescence staining of macrophages markers was performed to evaluate the ability of LBL-GA@Mn interface to promote the transformation of macrophages to the M2 type under DM condition. As shown in Fig. 7, we used red and green fluorescence to mark the total macrophage surface marker (CD68) and M1-type macrophages (CD86) or M2-type macrophages (CD206), respectively. A large number of M1-type macrophages (CD86+/CD68+) were observed around the implants of Ti group, while almost no M2-type macrophages (CD206+/CD68+) were observed, indicating that macrophages at the bone defect site were activated into the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype due to hypoxia, ROS accumulation, and excessive inflammation caused by hyperglycemia [23]. LBL coating failed to reverse the immune imbalance. However, compared with the Ti and LBL groups, the number of CD86+/CD68+ positive macrophages around the LBL-GA and LBL@Mn implants displayed a significant decreased trend, while the number of CD206+/CD68+ positive macrophages showed an ascended trend, demonstrating the beneficial effect of GA molecules and Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes to drive macrophage M2 polarization (Fig. 7a and c). Remarkably, quantitative data suggested that LBL-GA@Mn interface outperformed LBL-GA or LBL@Mn in reducing the ratio of CD86+/CD68+ positive macrophages and raising the ratio of CD206+/CD68+ positive macrophages (Fig. 7b and d). The result suggested that the combination of the two components exerts a greater regulatory effect on macrophages M2 polarization than the single-component implant. Collectively, LBL-GA@Mn interface had the ability to reshape macrophage immune homeostasis under DM conditions.

In vivo osseointegration capability

LBL-GA@Mn displayed the synergetic regulation of oxidative balance and immune homeostasis in the DM microenvironment, which provides a good pro-generative condition for subsequent osteogenesis and implant-osseointegration. Four weeks postoperatively, we systematically assessed the newly formed bone tissue surrounding the implants using micro-CT and three-dimensional reconstruction. As shown in Fig. 8a, because they did not have the ability to improve the microenvironment, there is fewer newly formed bone tissues around the Ti and LBL implants. In contrast, the LBL-GA and LBL@Mn groups promoted bone formation to some extent. Compared to the LBL-GA or LBL@Mn groups, the LBL-GA@Mn implants intuitively showed stronger capability of bone regeneration, with a large amount of new bone covering the implant surface. Furthermore, we selected 2 mm below the epiphyseal line and 0.2 mm on each side of the implant as the volume of interest (VOI) for the bone morphometric parameter analysis (Fig. 8b–f), including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), bone surface area ratio (BS/BV), and trabecular separation (Tb.Sp). The BV/TV of new bone tissue around the LBL-GA@Mn implant was significantly higher than that of the Ti, LBL, LBL-GA, and LBL@Mn groups, indicating that under the action of the interface, bone synthesis metabolism was greater than resorption metabolism, leading to increased bone mass. Typically, a higher BS/BV value may indicate a rougher bone surface and poorer bone quality [46]. However, the BS/BV of bone tissue around the LBL-GA@Mn implants was significantly lower than that of Ti implant. Tb.Th, Tb.N, and Tb.Sp are important parameters for evaluating the bone microstructure. Although there was no significant difference, the LBL-GA@Mn group showed the highest Tb.Th value. In addition, the value of Tb.N around the LBL-GA@Mn implant was significantly higher than the other four groups, implying that the interface was conducive to bone formation. Nevertheless, the value of Tb.Sp showed the opposite trend, indicating that LBL-GA@Mn implant could reverse bone loss caused by the DM microenvironment.

As shown in Fig. 8g, H&E staining results showed that there was almost no new bone generated at the edge of the bone defect in the Ti group, and only a small amount of new bone was generated around the LBL implant. In contrast, the LBL-GA, LBL@Mn, and LBL-GA@Mn groups generated a continuous and dense ring of new bone tissue, especially the LBL-GA@Mn group, where the thickness of the new bone was significantly increased, indicating an active bone remodeling capability under DM condition. In addition, the Masson staining shown in Fig. 8h indicated that the newly formed bone around the LBL-GA@Mn implant showed the mature characteristics. A layer of bone collagen fibers was arranged richly and regularly, tightly wrapping the new bone cells and trabeculae, providing good structural support and toughness for the bone tissue [47]. In contrast, almost no collagen matrix was observed in the Ti group, reflecting insufficient synthesis and deposition of bone matrix. In addition, we evaluated the expression levels of osteogenesis-related markers (OPN, OCN) around the different implants using immunohistochemical staining. As shown in Fig. 8i, almost no positive areas of OPN and OCN were observed around the Ti and LBL groups, suggesting that oxidative stress and immune disorders seriously affected the osteogenic signaling. In contrast, certain positive areas were observed in the LBL-GA or LBL@Mn groups, which was attributed to GA molecules or Mn3O4-BSA nanozymes alleviating oxidative stress and increasing the proportion of M2 macrophages. As a result, the improvement of the microenvironment promoted the cascaded differentiation of osteogenic associated cells, thereby inducing the synthesis and mineralization of bone matrix. Remarkably, LBL-GA@Mn implant displayed the highest expressive levels of OCN and OPN at the bone-implant interface, further confirmed that the LBL-GA@Mn implant achieved a satisfactory implant-osseointegration through the synergetic regulation of oxidative balance and immune homeostasis.

Conclusion

In summary, in view of the phenomenon that excessive oxidative stress and imbalanced immune homeostasis in DM microenvironment seriously inhibit the osteogenesis and implant osseointegration, we design a multilayered structure composing of 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid modified chitosan (CS-GA) and gelatin (Gel) embedded with trimanganese tetroxide nanozymes (Mn3O4) on the surface of titanium implant through a simple layer-by-layer self-assembly technique (LBL), namely LBL-GA@Mn, to ameliorate the pathological microenvironment and accelerate the bone repair. With the favorable function of Mn3O4 nanozymes, LBL-GA@Mn interface restores the mitochondrial function and intracellular antioxidant system of macrophages stimulated by DM microenvironment. In addition, given the immunomodulatory effects of CS-GA, LBL-GA@Mn interface displays osteo-immunomodulatory effect by transforming macrophages from pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype into anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. Consequently, in a bone defect model of T2DM rat, LBL-GA@Mn implant effectively suppresses inflammatory responses and shifts the bone immune environment from a pro-inflammatory state to a pro-healing state, thereby achieving a satisfactory result of implant-osseointegration. Taken together, this study provides valuable information to understand the importance of comprehensively addressing oxidative stress and immune disorder for accelerating implant-osseointegration in diabetic microenvironment.
