Comparative Effectiveness of Valoctocogene Roxaparvovec and Efanesoctocog Alfa in the Treatment of Severe Hemophilia A: A Matching-Adjusted Indirect Comparison of Bleeding Frequency
Authors: Thomas G. Douglas, Sandra Santos, David R. Hinds, Anthony J. Hatswell, Kurt Taylor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHemophilia A, an X-linked recessive bleeding disorder, is characterized by reduced factor VIII (FVIII) activity. Hemophilia A can significantly impact a person’s quality of life because of the risk of spontaneous bleeding. Treatment for hemophilia A aims to prevent bleeding from occurring. The innovation of gene therapies for use in hemophilia has the potential to replace prophylaxis treatment by enabling a single treatment infusion that sustains endogenous FVIII production for years. This analysis assessed the comparative effectiveness of valoctocogene roxaparvovec, a gene therapy, versus prophylactic FVIII replacement therapy with efanesoctocog alfa, given the current lack of comparative evidence between these treatments.MethodsComparison between valoctocogene roxaparvovec and efanesoctocog alfa was conducted using matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC). Patient-level data from the phase III GENEr8-1 trial of valoctocogene roxaparvovec were reweighted to align with baseline characteristics of aggregate-level data from XTEND-1, a phase III trial of efanesoctocog alfa. Matching variables included proportion of patients with zero treated bleeds and mean annualized bleed rate (ABR) for treated bleeds prior to initiating therapy. Following reweighting, the proportion of patients who had experienced zero bleeds after 52 weeks was compared, along with mean ABR—each population was assessed for treated bleeds, treated joint bleeds, and treated spontaneous bleeds.ResultsFollowing MAIC weighting, patients treated with valoctocogene roxaparvovec had higher odds of experiencing zero treated bleeds (OR 2.68, 95% CI 1.18–6.14) and zero treated joint bleeds (OR 2.75, 95% CI 1.09–6.86) compared with efanesoctocog alfa. Odds of zero treated spontaneous bleeds were also higher for valoctocogene roxaparvovec, but not statistically significant. Mean ABRs for treated bleeds, treated joint bleeds, and treated spontaneous bleeds were similar between groups, with no statistically significant differences.ConclusionThis MAIC suggests that valoctocogene roxaparvovec provides a greater likelihood of patients experiencing zero treated bleeds compared with efanesoctocog alfa during the first year following treatment initiation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-025-03339-9.

Introduction

Hemophilia A is a recessive X-linked bleeding disorder that affects approximately 1 in every 5,000 males [1, 2]. The clinical phenotype of hemophilia A patients is determined primarily by levels of factor VIII (FVIII) protein—an essential cofactor in the coagulation pathway. The disease is categorized as severe if FVIII activity is less than 1% of that of wild-type (< 1 IU/dL) and moderate if FVIII is between 1 and 5% of wild-type activity [3]. The standard of care for patients with severe hemophilia A is prophylaxis treatment with FVIII clotting-factor concentrates, which are available as either (virally inactive) plasma-derived products, or recombinant products produced using genetically engineered cells [4]. Beyond FVIII products, other hemostatic therapies such as monoclonal antibodies and gene therapies have been developed.

The development of gene therapies for use in hemophilia has the potential to replace prophylaxis treatment by enabling a single treatment infusion that sustains FVIII production for several years [4, 5]. Valoctocogene roxaparvovec (Roctavian®, BioMarin Pharmaceutical Inc.) is an AAV5-based gene therapy that has been approved for the treatment of severe hemophilia A. The phase III, open-label, single-arm GENEr8-1 trial (BMN 270-301; NCT03370913) investigated the efficacy of a single infusion of valoctocogene roxaparvovec in male patients with severe congenital hemophilia A [6–9]. Valoctocogene roxaparvovec treatment was associated with large reductions in annualized bleeding rate (ABR) over the first 4 years of follow-up, along with a reduction in the FVIII infusion rate to less than 5% of the baseline value [6]. In addition to the phase III trial, 7-year data were also published for the prior phase I/II clinical study (BMN 270-201; NCT02576795), further supporting sustained efficacy of valoctocogene roxaparvovec evaluated via both mean ABR and FVIII infusion rate [10].

Subsequent to the approval of valoctocogene roxaparvovec, efanesoctocog alfa has been studied as the first of a new class of FVIII replacement therapies with a 3–4 times longer half-life compared with standard half-life (SHL) and FVIII products [11–14]. The efficacy of prophylactic treatment with efanesoctocog alfa was assessed in the XTEND-1 (NCT04161495) clinical trial, which recruited 159 participants without FVIII inhibitors. Efanesoctocog alfa was found to substantially reduce mean ABR in patients with severe hemophilia A over the 52-week primary efficacy period of XTEND-1, and was statistically superior to pre-study FVIII prophylaxis in mean treated ABR [14]. Despite the demonstrated efficacy of each of valoctocogene roxaparvovec and efanesoctocog alfa individually, the relative efficacy between them has not yet been investigated in a direct or indirect comparison.

The goal of treatment for hemophilia A is to maintain hemostasis and, ideally, to provide patients with a “hemophilia-free” mind—defined as freedom from treatment burden, arthropathy, pain and, most importantly, bleeding [4, 15, 16]. An important achievement for patients is thus the complete avoidance of bleeds, with ABR serving as a useful indicator of residual disease burden (which can be zero) following treatment. This work aimed to assess the comparative effectiveness of a single infusion with valoctocogene roxaparvovec against prophylactic treatment with efanesoctocog alfa in the reduction of bleeding events in patients with severe hemophilia A. Given that there were no head-to-trials available comparing these treatments, indirect treatment comparisons (ITCs) were required. Matching-adjusted indirect comparison (MAIC) was used to compare the treatments [17], which utilized patient-level data (PLD) from one trial (GENEr8-1) weighted to ‘match’ published, aggregate-level evidence from the comparator trial (XTEND-1) [14].

Methods

Analytical Approach

MAIC was selected as the appropriate method for conducting ITCs to evaluate the comparative effectiveness of the two treatments based on the pivotal studies. This method allows for the balancing of characteristics between independent study populations at baseline, with the aim to eliminate the impact of heterogeneity on outcomes, and thus provide a fairer comparison of treatments.

To implement MAIC, variables are identified that are important to equalize between the studies given their effect on outcomes—either directly (referred to as “prognostic” variables) or by affecting the efficacy of treatment (“treatment effect modifiers”). The method attempts to adjust for these variables by calculating a weight for each patient in the PLD such that the aggregate summary statistics match those of the comparator; thereby forming a similar population. Outcomes are then recalculated in the weighted PLD to provide estimates of treatment effect in a population like that of the comparator trial.

A MAIC is appropriate only when there is consistency in inclusion/exclusion criteria between studies and, subsequently, alignment between the patient populations enrolled. When differences are apparent, this can result in extreme patient weights being included in the analysis; often resulting in a substantial reduction in effective sample size (ESS)—a measure of residual information loss following MAIC weighting. For this analysis, study inclusion/exclusion criteria were comparable between GENEr8-1 and XTEND-1, indicating further that MAIC would be appropriate to compare outcomes in these patient populations. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any newer studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Data Sources

GENEr8-1 was a phase III, open-label clinical trial that assessed the efficacy of valoctocogene roxaparvovec in patients with severe hemophilia A who were receiving FVIII concentrate prophylaxis for at least 1 year prior to enrollment [6–9]. The study enrolled 134 patients, who received an infusion of valoctocogene roxaparvovec at a dose of 6 × 1013 vector genomes per kilogram (vg/kg) of body weight at baseline. The primary efficacy period ran from the later of either the beginning of week 5 post-infusion or 3 days after the end of routine FVIII prophylaxis through week 52. During the efficacy period, patients were treated with on-demand FVIII concentrate where necessary (as a treatment for bleeds).

The phase III, multicenter, open-label XTEND-1 trial enrolled 159 patients, of which a subgroup (Group A, n = 133) were receiving prophylaxis with FVIII therapy at baseline. Patients then went on to receive once-weekly prophylaxis with efanesoctocog alfa during the 52-week efficacy period. Given the similarities between treatment regimens at baseline, aggregate-level data from Group A were used to inform the comparison. All patients in XTEND-1 were human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-negative at enrolment, and therefore Group A were compared to HIV-negative patients in GENEr8-1, which were referred to as the modified intention-to-treat (mITT) population within the trial. A total of 4 years of follow-up has now been published for the GENEr8-1 trial [6]. However, to enable a fair comparison, the MAIC is constrained to the quantity of data for XTEND-1, for which only 52-week outcomes are currently reported. These are therefore the outcomes compared between treatments.

Baseline characteristics and outcomes for XTEND-1 Group A were obtained from publicly available information. Definitions of key variables were reviewed and aligned between trials where possible. The definition of bleeding episodes was a key difference between trials, and the definition utilized in XTEND-1 was applied to GENEr8-1 data—both at baseline and during the primary efficacy period. In XTEND-1, treated bleeding episodes were defined as beginning from the first sign of bleeding and ending no more than 72 h after the last injection to treat the bleed. Additionally, if a participant had bleeds in multiple locations treated with a single injection, then these bleeds would be counted as one bleeding episode [14]. However in GENEr8-1, multiple bleeds at different anatomical locations—if treated within 72 h—were regarded as individual treated bleeds, even if all were treated with a single injection [6–9]. This difference necessitated recalculation of bleeds from GENEr8-1 to align with the definition of bleeding episodes in XTEND-1. This realignment adjusted values for both bleeds and target joints (defined as major joints with at least 3 spontaneous bleeding episodes in a consecutive 6-month period [8, 14]). Further information on bleeding definitions from trials is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Matching Variables and Outcomes

Variables available for matching were identified through assessment of all publicly available information for XTEND-1, including the clinical trial publication and protocol, clinicaltrials.gov entry, and regulatory documents [14]. These variables included age, ethnicity, treated ABR, endogenous Von Willebrand factor (VWF), number of target joints, proportion of patients with zero treated bleeds in the 12 months prior to baseline, and proportion of patients with > 10 treated bleeds in the 12 months prior to baseline. The largest differences between studies were in bleeding at baseline, with the GENEr8-1 trial entering the trial with a larger mean ABR. However, matching solely on mean ABR could fail to capture the proportion of values contributing to the mean that are zero. Therefore, in the primary analysis, the variables selected for matching were mean ABR and the proportion of patients with zero bleeds. In addition to the imbalance of bleeding between trials, the decision to match on this small set of variables was made in the interest of model parsimony—i.e., that the simplest model which adequately addresses the research question should be chosen.

In addition to the primary analysis, sensitivity analyses were carried out to assess whether matching on additional variables affected the estimates of comparative effectiveness. The primary outcome for the MAIC analysis was the proportion of patients experiencing zero bleeds, given that this outcome would likely be unaffected by potential inaccuracies arising from bleed rate recalculation. Mean ABR was the secondary outcome, providing an indication of residual disease burden following treatment. Outcomes were presented for treated bleeds, treated joint bleeds and treated spontaneous bleeds.

Statistical Analyses

MAIC was used to balance the differences in mean ABR and proportion with zero treated bleeds in the prior 12 months to baseline between the GENEr8-1 trial, for which PLD were available, and XTEND-1, for which aggregate-level data were available. Baseline characteristics are presented for each population (GENEr8-1 before and after MAIC weighting, and XTEND-1) with standardized mean difference – illustrating the size of differences in characteristics relative to variability observed in each study population.

In XTEND-1, ABRs were calculated using a negative binomial (NB) model, which was also applied to GENEr8-1 data. These models are typically used where a large number of patients have an observation of zero along with varying follow-up times [18, 19]. The NB models used to calculate outcomes included raw number of bleeds as the dependent variable, with a log-transformed offset variable for follow-up time as an independent variable [14], to calculate bleed rates over a 52-week period.

Outcomes of mean ABR and proportion with zero bleeds for each population were calculated from a negative binomial model and are presented with 95% confidence intervals for treated bleeds, treated spontaneous bleed, and treated joint bleeds. Differences in mean ABR between studies were assessed using rate ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Differences in the proportion with zero bleeds between studies were assessed using odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Kish’s ESS is reported alongside outcomes, which indicates the number of non-weighted individuals that would be required to give an estimate with the same precision as the weighted sample estimate [20].

All analyses were conducted in the statistical software package R version 4.3.2 [21] using the maic package version 0.1.4 [22].

Results

Before MAIC weighting, meaningful differences were observed between groups in treated bleeds. Importantly, the baseline treated ABR was considerably higher in the GENEr8-1 trial (5.4 vs. 3.2, p = 0.005). In addition, the proportion of patients experiencing zero treated bleeds at baseline was numerically lower in the GENEr8-1 trial (32.6% vs. 36.1%); however, this difference was not statistically significant.

Following MAIC weighting of the GENEr8-1 mITT population to match the XTEND-1 trial on mean ABR and proportion with zero bleeds at baseline, the ESS was 123—representing 93% of the original sample size. The majority of patient weights were approximately 1, with few extreme weights due to some patients having a large number of bleeds prior to baseline; a histogram of weights is provided in Supplementary Fig. S1.

The most notable difference both before and after MAIC weighting was the proportion of patients of White race (Table 2), which could have been due to the substantial proportion of patients in XTEND-1 for whom race was unknown or not reported. For most of the variables, MAIC weighting brought values closer to those in XTEND-1, with the exception of the proportion of patients with 0 target joints at baseline which deviated further from XTEND-1. Discussion

The aim of this MAIC analysis was to provide an unbiased comparison of a single 6 × 1013 vg/kg infusion of valoctocogene roxaparvovec versus 50 IU/kg of prophylactic efanesoctocog alfa once-weekly. Patients treated with valoctocogene roxaparvovec were found to have higher odds of experiencing zero treated bleeds in the first year following initiation of treatment, along with higher odds of experiencing zero treated joint bleeds, with no statistically significant difference in the odds of experiencing zero spontaneous bleeds. Although efanesoctocog alfa resulted in numerically smaller ABR for treated bleeds and treated joint bleeds, and valoctocogene roxaparvovec resulted in numerically smaller treated joint ABR, these differences were not statistically significant.

Experiencing zero bleeds is a clinically significant outcome for patients with hemophilia A, as it represents disease control and reduces the physical and emotional burden associated with the condition [15, 16]. Bleeds, and particularly joint bleeds, can lead to long-term complications such as chronic pain, joint damage, and reduced mobility, which severely impact quality of life [23]. Achieving zero bleeds minimizes these risks, allowing patients to avoid hospitalizations or additional interventions, and encourages a more active, unrestricted life [15, 16]. In this context, the higher odds of experiencing zero treated bleeds and zero treated joint bleeds with valoctocogene roxaparvovec, paired with the reduction in treatment burden associated with a single infusion of treatment, is therefore relevant to patients. ABR is an important measure of residual bleeding risk in patients who are not yet bleed-free, and similar ABRs between groups indicate that treatment with either valoctocogene roxaparvovec or efanesoctocog alfa could result in patients experiencing fewer than one bleed per year on average. It is important to note that this analysis was limited to a comparison of bleeding outcomes within the first year following initiation of treatment, due to a lack of published long-term data from XTEND-1. As such, long-term comparative effectiveness with respect to bleeding outcomes between valoctocogene roxaparvovec and efanesoctocog alfa could not be assessed in this analysis.

The use of ABR as an endpoint in this analysis presented several challenges. Firstly, the need to align definitions between trials led to a change in the GENEr8-1 values, moving from an ABR of 0.92 to 0.86 during the primary efficacy period, and further exacerbating issues with naïve comparison. Even once definitions are aligned, how results are presented could change their interpretation. For example, a question could be asked as to whether ABR or change from baseline in ABR should be used to compare treatments. Mean ABR favors efanesoctocog alfa (0.71 vs. 0.79), but naïve comparisons of change in ABR from baseline favor valoctocogene roxaparvovec, both in absolute change (− 4.6 vs. − 2.5) and percentage reduction (− 85% vs. − 75%). The proportion of patients with zero bleeds is, however, largely unaffected by these limitations, as these patients would record zero bleeds regardless of the bleeding definition used in data collection.

This was the first analysis to assess comparative effectiveness of valoctocogene roxaparvovec versus efanesoctocog alfa in hemophilia A. The strengths of this analysis include the alignment of bleeding distributions at baseline using MAIC, and accounting for differences in bleeding definitions. Whilst only matching on two variables, results of the primary analysis being consistent with sensitivity analyses—where further population alignment was carried out—provides additional confidence in the unbiasedness of results. The main limitation of the analysis relates to the differences between trial settings. Firstly, information as to how bleeds were collected in XTEND-1 is only available through publicly available documents, and therefore visibility is limited as to how this was implemented in a prophylactic setting. In addition, the nature of treatment administration itself cannot be adjusted for, and therefore the analysis is limited to XTEND-1 bleeding definitions, which may not be relevant to a scenario where FVIII concentrates are administered on-demand. Finally, the large difference in ABR between populations at baseline (ABR of 5.4 in GENEr8-1 vs. ABR of 3.2 in XTEND-1) was approximately equal to the effect size in XTEND-1 itself, which saw a reduction of − 2.5 in ABR from baseline. This could indicate that a population with milder disease were enrolled in XTEND-1, and therefore results may not generalize between studies.

This analysis exclusively compared bleeding frequency between valoctocogene roxaparvovec and efanesoctocog alfa. Therefore, future work should also consider other relevant clinical and safety endpoints and health-related quality-of-life data to compare the benefits of these treatments on overall disease burden for patients with severe hemophilia A.

Conclusion

When balancing the patient bleeding distribution in the 12 months prior to baseline between those treated with valoctocogene roxaparvovec and efanesoctocog alfa, patients treated with valoctocogene roxaparvovec were more likely to have experienced zero treated bleeds and zero treated joint bleeds in the 52 weeks following initiation of treatment. Mean ABRs for treated bleeds and treated spontaneous bleeds numerically favored efanesoctocog alfa, and mean ABR for treated joint bleeds favored valoctocogene roxaparvovec; however, no statistically significant differences were observed in each of these outcomes. While the burden of hemophilia A extends beyond bleeding to features such as pain and arthropathy, the results of this comparison indicate that patients treated with valoctocogene roxaparvovec are more likely to experience a complete reduction in hemophilia-related bleeding within the first year of treatment, thereby alleviating a significant aspect of their disease. Patients who were not bleed-free following treatment show a similar reduction in bleeding frequency with either treatment, as measured by ABR.
