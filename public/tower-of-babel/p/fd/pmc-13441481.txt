N-myristoyltransferase 1, a key gene for protein N-myristoylation, is dispensable for fertility in male mice
Authors: Yang LIU, Yvxia ZHANG, Hailing DING, Jie LI, Tingting GAO, Zongzhuang WEN, Yunshan WANG, Bin WU
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
N-Myristoyltransferase 1 (NMT1), the predominant enzyme catalyzing myristoylation of proteins, is involved in various biological processes, including early embryonic development, immune responses, apoptosis, cellular homeostasis, tumorigenesis, and infection, with therapeutic potential in viral and parasitic infections as well as cancer. Despite the critical functions of NMT1, there have been no reports to date regarding its role in reproduction, especially in spermatogenesis. To investigate the function of NMT1 in this context, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9 technology to create a germ cell-specific Nmt1 knockout mice model for the first time. Surprisingly, male mice lacking NMT1 maintained fertility, exhibiting normal testicular structure and sperm morphology, with no significant differences in spermatogenic tubule structure or germ cell distribution compared to wild-type mice. Additionally, the Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre male mice showed no notable defects in meiosis. These findings suggest that NMT1 is not critical for spermatogenesis or male fertility in mice. However, further studies have shown that the compensatory role of NMT2 may play an unexpected role in maintaining myristoylation levels, which provides a new perspective for understanding the role of myristoylation in spermatogenesis.

Male infertility is a multifactorial pathological condition that affects approximately 7% of the male population [1], yet many pathogenic mechanisms underlying male factors remain unexplored. Spermatogenesis is a complex and tightly regulated process that primarily involves several stages: the proliferation of spermatogonia, differentiation of spermatogonia, meiosis leading to spermatocyte formation, the specialization of round spermatids into mature sperm, and the release of mature sperm. Any abnormalities at these stages can result in impaired sperm production, ultimately leading to male infertility[2,3,4]. To date, at least 2,300 genes have been identified as being expressed in the male germline and involved in spermatogenesis and sperm function [1, 5]. However, the functions of many of these genes remain unclear, which significantly limits our understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms of male infertility and the development of precise diagnostic tools. Continued efforts are needed to discover and elucidate new genes and their pathogenic mechanisms that contribute to male infertility.

N-Myristoyltransferases (NMTs) are indispensable eukaryotic enzymes that catalyze the transfer of myristate from myristoyl coenzyme A to the amino group of amino-terminal glycine, facilitating the N-myristoylation modification of proteins [6,7,8]. As one of the important post-translational modifications, myristoylation derived by NMTs significantly enhances the diversity of proteins and their biological functions within organisms. Myristoylation is ubiquitous in eukaryotes, with over 200 proteins in humans identified as having myristoylation at the N-terminal glycine residue [9,10,11]. As the most predominant N-Myristoyltransferase, NMT1 participates in various biological processes, including early embryonic development [12], immune responses [13, 14], apoptosis [15, 16], tumorigenesis [17,18,19,20], and infection [21, 22]. Additionally, NMT1 has shown therapeutic potential in the treatment of viral and parasitic infections, as well as in cancer [23,24,25,26]. However, despite the critical role of NMT1, there have been no reports to date addressing its function in spermatogenesis.

Our research has demonstrated that the Nmt1 gene is evolutionarily conserved across various mammals and exhibits high expression levels in the testis. Because global Nmt1 knockout mice exhibit embryonic lethality, we generated a germ cell–specific conditional knockout (cKO) model using the Stra8-Cre system to investigate its role in spermatogenesis. The results indicated that male mice lacking NMT1 retained their fertility, with normal testicular structure and sperm morphology. Histological analysis of testicular tissue revealed no significant differences in spermatogenic tubule structure and the distribution of germ cells between wild-type (WT) and Nmt1 knockout (KO) mice. Furthermore, the Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre males did not exhibit any notable defects in meiosis. These findings suggest that NMT1 is not critical for spermatogenesis or male fertility in mice. Interestingly, in this process, elevated expression of the type 2 isozyme NMT2 may act as a compensatory factor to maintain testicular myristoylation and spermatogenesis. The compensatory manner highlights the complexity and evolutionary necessity of the stringent regulation of spermatogenesis.

Materials and Methods

Animals

The Nmt1 gene (NCBI Reference Sequence: NM_008707; Ensembl: ENSMUSG00000020936) is located on mouse chromosome 11 and contains twelve exons, with the ATG start codon in exon 1 and the TAA stop codon in exon 12 (Transcript Nmt1-201: ENSMUST00000021314). Exon 4, which encodes a 119 bp sequence, was selected as the cKO region. Deletion of this region results in the loss of Nmt1 gene function.

Conditional Nmt1 floxed mice (strain name: C57BL/6J-Nmt1em1Cflox/Cya, stock number CKOCMP-18107-Nmt1-B6J-VA) were generated on a C57BL/6J genetic background by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Guangzhou, China) using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated homologous recombination. Homologous arms and the cKO region were amplified from the BAC clone RP24-216B12 and inserted into the targeting vector. Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes and the targeting vector were co-injected into C57BL/6J fertilized eggs. The guide RNA sequences used to insert two loxP sites were designed and validated by Cyagen; detailed sequences and vector design strategies are available from the company upon request (project ID: CKOAIP230522YS1).

The Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre cKO mice were obtained by mating Nmt1flox/+ heterozygous mice with Stra8-Cre transgenic mice (C57BL/6J background; Cyagen Biosciences Inc., Guangzhou, China). Genotyping was performed by PCR using LoxP primers (F1: 5′-GTGATGGGTGTTTGTCATTCAGG-3′, R1: 5′-CAATCTCTCTCCAGTCTTTGGTCC-3′) with expected bands of 139 bp for wild-type and 200 bp for floxed alleles, and Cre primers (F: 5′-CATATTGGCAGAACGAAAACGC-3′, R: 5′-CCTGTTTCACTATCCAGGTTACGG-3′) with an expected Cre-positive band of 413 bp.

PCR was performed using Premix Taq Polymerase (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., P222, Nanjing, China) in a total reaction volume of 25 μl containing 1 μl genomic DNA, 1 μl of each primer (10 μM), 12.5 μl Premix Taq, and 9.5 μlnuclease-free water. The PCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 94°C for 3 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 sec, annealing at 60°C for 35 sec, and extension at 72°C for 35 sec; followed by a final extension at 72°C for 5 min. PCR amplification was carried out using a thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA).

All mice were housed in SPF experimental animal rooms, and all animal experiments were performed according to protocols approved by the Animal Center of the Institute of Genetics and Developmental Biology (IGDB), Chinese Academy of Sciences. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jinan Central Hospital, Approval No. JNCHIACUC2024-44.

Sperm collection and analysis

Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and the epididymides were carefully isolated. Each epididymis was placed in a 1 ml Human Tubal Fluid (HTF) medium (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) in an Eppendorf MicroTest Tube (Shanghai Kegin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) and finely minced using sterile scissors. The samples were then incubated at 37°C for 15 min to allow sperm to swim out. Subsequently, 10 µl of sperm suspension from the cauda epididymis was loaded onto disposable computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) sperm counting chamber slides (CASA10-2; Ascen Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). Sperm concentration, total sperm count, and motility were analyzed using a CASA system (Tsinghua Tongfang Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Histological analysis

Paraffin-embedded testis and epididymis sections were baked at 60°C for 2 h, deparaffinized using an eco-friendly dewaxing and clearing solution (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China), and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%; Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), followed by rinsing in distilled water.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using a modified H&E staining kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water for 5 min, briefly differentiated in 1% hydrochloric acid alcohol for 1–2 sec, washed again in water for 5 min, and counterstained with eosin for 30 s. After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral resin (Solarbio Science & Technology).

Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining was performed using a commercial PAS staining kit (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.; G1280-2 × 50 ml). After deparaffinization and rehydration as described above, sections were oxidized with reagent A for 8 min, rinsed, and stained with Schiff reagent (reagent B) for 15 min in the dark, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining for 3 min. After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with neutral resin and examined under a light microscope.

Immunofluorescence staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were dewaxed using an eco-friendly dewaxing and clearing solution (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by rinsing in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.). Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) by heating. After washing three times with PBS, tissue sections were circled with an immunohistochemistry pen (Boster Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking solution (Kangwei Century Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) at 37°C for 30 min. Subsequently, the primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibody at 37°C for 1 h. DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was applied for nuclear staining, and sections were mounted using an anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime Biotechnology). Images were acquired using a ZEISS LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany) and a fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

The primary antibodies used were anti-γH2A.X (Abcam, Cambridge, UK, ab81299; 1:200) and FITC-conjugated PNA (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, #L7381; 1:50). The secondary antibodies used were as follows: FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China, ZF-0311; 1:50), FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ZSGB-BIO, ZF-0312; 1:50), Rhodamine-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (ZSGB-BIO, ZF-0316; 1:50), and Rhodamine-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ZSGB-BIO, ZF-0313; 1:50).

Immunohistochemical staining

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were baked at 60°C for 2 h, deparaffinized in xylene (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.), and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.) in a microwave oven at high power for 15 min. After natural cooling to room temperature, the sections were rinsed three times with PBS (Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd.).

Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide (ZSGB-BIO) at room temperature for 10 min, followed by three washes with PBS. Non-specific binding was blocked with 5% normal goat serum (ZSGB-BIO) at 37°C for 30 min. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber with a primary antibody against NMT1 (polyclonal anti-NMT1 antibody; Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 11546-1-AP; 1:100). After three washes with PBS, the sections were incubated with the Polymer Detection System for Rabbit Primary Antibody (ZSGB-BIO, PV-9000) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Color development was carried out using a DAB substrate kit (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9017), and the reaction time was monitored under a microscope. Subsequently, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.), differentiated, blued, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). Images were acquired using a light microscope (Olympus Corporation).

Western blot

Western blot (WB) analysis was performed using standard procedures. Protein samples were separated by SDS–PAGE using a vertical electrophoresis system (Beijing Junyi Dongfang Electrophoresis Equipment Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (MilliporeSigma, Billerica, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk prepared in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST; Beyotime Biotechnology).

The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies: anti-NMT1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA, sc-393702; 1:1000), anti-NMT2 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-136005; 1:1000), pan-myristoylation polyclonal antibody (Abbkine, Wuhan, China, ABP57559; 1:500), anti-β-actin (Proteintech, 66009-1-Ig; 1:5000), and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig; 1:5000). All primary antibodies were diluted in antibody dilution buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology).

After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-1; 1:10000) or HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2; 1:10000). Protein bands were visualized using a sensitive enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (Sensitive ECL Detection Kit, Proteintech, PK10002) and captured using a chemiluminescence gel imaging system (ProteinSimple, San Jose, CA, USA).

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from the testes of wild-type (WT) and Nmt1 knockout mice using the SteadyPure Quick RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate Biology, Changsha, China, AG21023) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Evo M-MLV RT Reaction Mix Ver.2 (Accurate Biology, AG11728). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premixed qPCR Kit (Accurate Biology, AG11701) on a Roche LightCycler® 96 detection system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

The primer sequences used were as follows: Nmt2 forward, 5′-TGGGTGGTGATACTATGAAGAA-3′; Nmt2 reverse, 5′-TACAGCTCCTTCAGCACTTCG-3′. Each reaction was performed in triplicate. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, with Gapdh serving as the internal control gene.

Sequence analysis

Amino acid sequences of NMT1 from 10 representative vertebrate species were retrieved from the NCBI database, including mammals (Homo sapiens, Mus musculus, Rattus norvegicus, Bos taurus, Canis lupus familiaris, Macaca mulatta, Pan troglodytes) and non-mammalian vertebrates (Gallus gallus, Danio rerio, Xenopus tropicalis). Sequence alignment was performed using MEGA version 11 software, and sequence conservation was visualized with WebLogo.

Data and statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. The experimental results in this study were obtained from at least two independent replicates. All images were processed with Adobe Illustrator 2022 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) and ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical comparisons were performed using two-tailed Student’s t-tests. Significance was defined as * P < 0.05, ** P < 0.01, *** P < 0.001; ns, not significant.

Nmt1 conditional knockout male mice are fertile

To evaluate spermatogenesis in Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre mice, the morphological characteristics of testicular and epididymal tissues were examined, revealing no significant differences when compared to WT mice (Fig. 2E). Concurrently, the weight of the testis was assessed, with no notable differences found. Therefore, the Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre knockout did not impact the development of reproductive organs in male mice (Figs 2E and G).

For fertility testing, eight sexually mature male mice were selected from 8-week-old littermates. After three months of assessment, normal mating behavior was observed in the knockout males. Female mice produced vaginal plugs following mating, and the number of offspring per litter from matings with adult females was comparable to that of WT mice. The fertility evaluation indicated that Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre male mice were reproductively competent (Fig. 2H).

Subsequently, sperm concentration and motility of spermatozoa in the tail of the epididymis were assessed using the CASA assay. The results indicated that neither sperm concentration nor total sperm motility changed significantly in Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre mice compared to WT mice (Figs. 2I and K). Sperm morphology was evaluated through HE staining, revealing no significant difference in the proportion of abnormal spermatozoa between WT and Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre male mice (Figs. 2J and L).

Histological analyses of the testis, caput epididymis, and cauda epididymis from Nmt1f/f male mice were conducted. H&E staining demonstrated that the Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre knockout mice maintained intact structures of spermatogonial tubules and normal distribution of germ cells (Fig. 2M).

Discussion

Spermatogenesis is a complex process regulated by various genes and non-coding transcripts. Nmt1 is essential for mice development, as pure Nmt1-deficient mice exhibit embryonic lethality between days 3.5 and 7.5 of development [16]. Our research has demonstrated that the Nmt1 gene is evolutionarily conserved across various mammals and exhibits high expression levels in the testis. Consistent with previous reports, Nmt1 is widely expressed in mammalian tissues but shows relatively higher expression in the testis, where abundant N-myristoylated substrates and strong NMT enzymatic activity have been observed in germ cells [27, 28]. To investigate the role of NMT1 in spermatogenesis, we utilized CRISPR/Cas9 technology to create a germ cell-specific Nmt1 knockout mice model for the first time. Notably, 8-week-old Nmt1f/f; Stra8-Cre males were fertile and exhibited no morphological or histological differences in gonads and gametes compared to WT males. This suggests that NMT1 enriched in the testis is dispensable for spermatogenesis, indicating the potential involvement of unknown mechanisms.

Currently, myristoylation's role in regulating mammals' reproductive functions is not well established. However, in plants, impaired myristoylation has been shown to lead to male sterility, underscoring the potential importance of this post-translational modification in maintaining fertility [29, 30]. Additionally, studies on Arabidopsis thaliana have demonstrated that AtNMT shares similarities with NMTs in mammals. Notably, the promoter of AtNMT1 exhibits significantly enhanced activity in pollen grains and stamens. This localized high activity suggests that myristoylation may play a critical role during the developmental stages of male gametophytes [31]. However, our study indicates that the absence of NMT1 does not significantly disrupt spermatogenesis. This finding raises questions about the potential existence of other compensatory mechanisms involved in sperm development.

In mammals, both NMT1 and NMT2 proteins exhibit functional N-Myristoyltransferase activity; however, the activity of NMT2 appears to be approximately four times lower than that of NMT1, contributing minimally to the overall NMT activity in vivo [32]. This observation leads us to hypothesize that NMT1 may play a dominant role in the regulation of fertility. Our research indicates that NMT1 is primarily expressed in round spermatids. Consequently, we utilized the Stra8 Cre system to construct knockout mice and found that NMT1 is dispensable for the meiosis of spermatocytes and the process of spermiogenesis. However, the role of NMT1 in the self-differentiation of spermatogonia remains unclear, and further validation using the vasa-cre approach may be necessary.

In addition, NMT1 and NMT2 have redundant and unique effects on protein processing, apoptosis, and cell proliferation [16]. Previous studies have shown that depletion of NMT1 leads to an 8-fold increase in apoptosis and depletion of NMT2 leads to a 19-fold increase in apoptosis through siRNA-induced selective ablation of NMT1 and NMT2. In contrast to cell proliferation, NMT1 but not NMT2 is involved in cell proliferation [16]. This highlights the subtle differences between these two isoenzymes in regulating biological functions. In our study, following the germ cell-specific knockout of the Nmt1 gene, a significant increase in both protein and mRNA levels of NMT2 was observed in the testis of mice, while N-myristoylation levels remained comparable to those in normal mice. This suggests that NMT2 may play a compensatory role, underscoring the complexity and evolutionary necessity of the stringent regulation of spermatogenesis. The elevated expression of NMT2 could serve as a mechanism to protect the myristoylation status crucial for sperm development and function.

Although the compensatory effect mediated by NMT2 prevented spermatogenic defects in the Nmt1 single-knockout model, this finding itself is of considerable biological significance. It remains unclear whether the upregulation of NMT2 represents a testis-specific compensatory response or a more generalized mechanism occurring in other tissues. The underlying molecular basis of this regulation warrants further investigation and may involve transcriptional, post-transcriptional, or epigenetic mechanisms. Although this hypothesis requires further validation, it provides a plausible framework for understanding how germ cells maintain myristoylation homeostasis through intrinsic feedback regulation. Elucidating how germ cells sense the loss of NMT1 and selectively activate Nmt2 expression will deepen our understanding of the robustness and adaptability of post-translational modification networks. Future transcriptomic analyses may help identify the transcription factors or signaling pathways that play central roles in this compensatory response.

More importantly, to directly assess the essential role of protein myristoylation in germ cells and the compensatory function of NMT2, future work should include the generation and analysis of Nmt1/Nmt2 double conditional knockout (dcKO) mice. In parallel, phenotypic characterization of Nmt2 single-knockout mice would help clarify its independent contribution within the compensatory network. Collectively, our findings reveal a potential functional redundancy and compensation between NMT isoenzymes during spermatogenesis, offering new insights into the regulatory mechanisms of protein myristoylation in male reproduction. Despite the technical challenges, implementing a dual knockout strategy will be essential for elucidating the coordinated regulatory roles of NMT1 and NMT2 in germ cell development.
