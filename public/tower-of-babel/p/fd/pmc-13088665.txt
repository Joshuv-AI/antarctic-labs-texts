Surgical management and outcomes of spinal low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma: a single-center case series and literature review
Authors: Guangjian Bai, Xing Huang, Chenglong Yang, Pan Gao, Baoquan Xin, Xin Gao, Tielong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeLow-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (LGMS) is a rare fibroblastic/myofibroblastic malignancy, predominantly occurring in the head and neck and generally exhibits limited sensitivity to radiotherapy and conventional chemotherapy. Only sporadic cases of spinal LGMS have been reported. The aim of this study was to gain a better understanding of the distinctive features of spinal LGMS and explore its treatment options through retrospective analysis of 7 consecutive patients with spinal LGMS in the context of a literature review.MethodsThe clinical data and prognosis of 7 patients who received surgical treatment at our center between March 2019 and December 2024 were analyzed retrospectively.ResultsThe 7 patients included 2 males and 5 females ranging in age from 24 to 71 years, with a mean age of 42.6 years. The tumors involved the cervical vertebra (n = 1), thoracic vertebra (n = 1), lumbar vertebra (n = 1), and sacrum (n = 4). The mean intraoperative blood loss was 2100 ± 1322.9 ml, the mean operation time was 361.9 ± 152.9 min, and the mean follow-up duration was (19.4 ± 11.7) months. Four of the 7 patients died during the follow-up period despite postoperative adjuvant therapy, including 3 patients who died of in-situ recurrence with distant metastasis, and one patient who died of in-situ recurrence. The remaining 3 patients survived with no sign of disease at the last follow-up visit.ConclusionThe prognosis of spinal LGMS is usually poor. Although evidence suggests that spinal LGMS exhibits limited sensitivity to radiotherapy and chemotherapy, surgical resection is typically pursued as the main treatment modality following pathological confirmation, while recognizing the need for individualized clinical decision-making. Factors affecting the prognosis of spinal LGMS include tumor size, tumor resection methods, tumor location, and the complex anatomies of the spinal cord.

Introduction

Low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (LGMS) was first described as a rare spindle-cell tumor of mesenchymal origin in 1978 [1], accounting for less than 1% of soft tissue sarcomas [2]. In 1988, Mentzel et al. specified the diagnostic criteria for LGMS based on the immunohistochemical and ultrastructural findings in 18 cases [3]. LGMS is a rare fibroblastic/myofibroblastic malignant tumor, which was clearly classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2020 [4]. The cytological features of LGMS cells correspond to myofibroblasts [5], which develop from fibroblasts and express positivity with myospecific actin (MSA), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and vimentin in immunohistochemical staining [6, 7].

LGMS is often located in the soft tissues of the head and neck (especially in the tongue and throat) and limbs [8–9]. Only a handful of cases of LGMS have been reported in the spine. In 2010, Humphries et al. reported a case of a 15-year-old girl with sacral LGMS who underwent surgical resection combined with chemotherapy after experiencing an in-situ recurrence following misdiagnosis [10]. In another reported case of spinal LGMS in the thoracic spine, the first symptom was a progressive decrease in muscle strength in both lower limbs, and no additional adjuvant treatment was performed after surgical treatment [11].

As a low-grade malignant tumor with a high propensity to recur locally, LGMS is unlikely to metastasize distantly [12]. When distant metastasis occurs, the lung is the most common site of involvement [13]. Although LGMS is generally considered a tumor with a low metastatic potential, particularly when arising in the extremities or superficial soft tissues. However, data regarding LGMS of the spine remain extremely limited. Like other soft tissue sarcomas, it is difficult to treat LGMS occurring in the spine with extensive surgical excision because of the complex anatomical structures there [9]. To better understand the distinctive features of spinal LGMS and explore its optimal treatment, we performed this retrospective analysis on seven consecutive patients with spinal LGMS in combination with a literature review. This manuscript adheres to the CARE guidelines for case report.

Materials and methods

Patients

This retrospective study included seven consecutive patients with spinal LGMS who received at least one surgical treatment at our institution between March 2019 and December 2024. General information, clinical manifestations, imaging findings, surgical details, and pathological findings were collected from all patients. The reporting follows the Consensus-based Clinical Case Reporting (CARE) guideline.

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Pathologically confirmed spinal low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma (LGMS); (2) Surgical tumor resection performed; (3) Complete clinical data available. Exclusion criteria: (1) Palliative surgery for spinal LGMS; (2) Coexisting coagulation disorders. The histopathologic criteria for the diagnosis are as follows: (1) microscopically, the tumor tissue showed diffuse infiltrative growth composed of spindle-shaped cells and a prominent hyaline fibrostroma, and most of the spindle cells showed mild to moderate nuclear pleomorphism; and (2) immunohistochemically, the tumor tissue showed positive expression of vimentin and at least one myogenic marker (SMA or desmin) [3].

Imaging assessment and diagnosis

All patients underwent spinal X-ray, CT, and MRI examinations after admission. CTA was performed when necessary to initially determine tumor blood supply and the need for preoperative embolization. Radiological diagnosis was made by two experienced radiologists. In addition to soft tissue mass formation, bone erosion was observed in all lesions, manifesting as osteolytic changes.

Surgical procedures

The whole operation process comprised tumor excision, decompression of the spinal cord, reconstruction and stabilization of the spine. Intraoperative frozen section examination was performed in all 7 cases. In all patients, the initial surgery was performed using a posterior midline approach in the prone position. All patients underwent surgery via a posterior midline approach with the patient in the prone position. A total of seven patients were included, and due to local recurrence or metastatic progression, ten surgical procedures were performed overall. Among these, four procedures were performed for primary tumors, five for local recurrences, and one for distant metastatic disease. For solitary lesions, we preferred total resection, including both piecemeal total resection and en bloc resection. En bloc resection was achieved in two primary cases, whereas piecemeal resection was performed in one primary case due to anatomical constraints and the close proximity of critical neural and vascular structures. In cases of local recurrence or metastatic disease, piecemeal resection was preferentially adopted, aiming at maximal safe tumor debulking and neural decompression while ensuring complete excision of all visible tumor. Importantly, although a piecemeal approach was used, the tumor was resected in separate fragments with the aim of achieving complete removal of all tumor tissue. In addition, five patients received intraoperative platinum-based chemotherapy. Based on the location of the tumor, an appropriate screw rod system was selected for reconstruction. Occipital fusion was performed in patients with C1-4 lesions, and lumbar-iliac fusion was performed in patients with sacral LGMS. The reconstruction materials for bone defects mainly included artificial vertebral bodies, bone cement, and allogeneic bone. Following surgery, all patients underwent a multidisciplinary team assessment to determine the need for adjuvant therapy (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and/or targeted therapy), with treatment decisions made selectively based on tumor recurrence, resection margins, and individual clinical factors.

Follow-up observation

All patients were followed up after surgery, and the progress of their disease (local recurrence, metastasis, or none) was recorded. The final status of the patient was obtained through telephone interviews and classified as no evidence of disease (NED), alive with disease (AWD), or died of disease (DOD). All patients were followed up through regular interviews until the date of patient death or December 2024. (Supplementary File 1)

Literature review

Recognizing that reporting on spinal LGMS is a rare and valuable resource. A PubMed search was conducted with the combination of the “low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma and spine” since 2000 using the advanced search builder. Two articles were found and all included in the current study. Extended literature analysis in the context of related clinical data from our institution was performed by two investigators independently.

Results

General information

This retrospective study included seven patients (2 male and 5 female) with a mean age of 42.6 ± 17.9 years, who underwent a total of 10 surgeries. Four of the seven patients had primary LGMS located in the thoracic and sacral vertebrae, two patients had in situ recurrence in the sacro-lumbar spine, and one patient had metastasis from the lumbar vertebra to the cervical vertebra. The most common symptoms included pain, limb numbness or weakness, bladder, and bowel dysfunction. The Frankel scores before surgery were Grade D in three cases and Grade E in four cases. The Karnofsky performance score (KPS) was 70 in five patients and 80 in the other two patients. The mean maximum tumor diameter was 6.3 ± 1.4 cm (range: 3.5–8 cm), and more than two-thirds of the patients had a tumor diameter of ≥ 5 cm. (Table 1)

Imaging assessment and pathological diagnosis

Bone erosion was observed in all lesions, which manifested as osteolytic changes. MRI was sensitive to the growth mode and scope of the tumor tissue, showing low-signal intensity on T1WI (Fig. 1C), heterogeneous high-signal intensity on T2WI (Fig. 1D-E), and obvious enhancement (Fig. 1F-H). CT contrast-enhanced scan also showed irregular soft tissue mass formation with local cortical bone discontinuity and mild enhancement (Fig. 1I-J). Although histologically similar, LGMS may exhibit different immunophenotypes: VAD (vimentin + SMA+desmin), VD (vimentin+desmin), and VA (vimentin + SMA) [3]. Of the seven cases in this study, VAD type was detected in four, VD type in the other three, and no VA type in any patient. Ki-67 ranged from 5% to 70%. Light microscopy of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections revealed tumor cells arranged in fascicles, showing mild cytological atypia with vesicular nuclei and conspicuous nucleoli (Fig. 2A–C).

Treatment details

All surgical procedures were performed by experienced surgeons in all seven patients, including tumor resection, spinal cord decompression, and reconstruction of spinal stability. Except for the two patients with in situ recurrence, two of the remaining 5 patients with primary tumors refused to undergo fine-needle biopsy for fear of the risk of neurological impairment. The operation time and intraoperative blood loss varied from person to person. The operation time averaged 361.9 ± 152.9 min, and intraoperative blood loss averaged 2100 ± 1322.9 ml. Except for the patient in Case 7 who underwent low sacral resection, all the other patients underwent postoperative reconstruction.

Four of the seven patients received postoperative adjuvant therapy, including chemotherapy and targeted therapy, and none received radiotherapy. Chemotherapy was performed using doxorubicin liposome and ifosfamide in Cases 1 and 3, and in Case 5, using doxorubicin liposome+ifosfamide combined with targeted therapy with erlotinib. In Case 6, targeted therapy with carbotinib was used.

Follow-up observation

The mean follow-up time was 19.4 ± 11.7 months. In situ recurrence occurred in two cases, metastatic recurrence in three cases. Up to the last follow-up, three patients showed no evidence of disease, and the other four patients died of the disease. The patient in Case 5 underwent four surgeries during a 5-year period, including two spinal surgeries in other hospitals. At the last follow-up, there was no sign of disease. Case 2 was a patient with in situ recurrence in the lumbar spine. During the 11 years since the onset of the disease, he received a total of six surgical treatments. After the fourth operation in our spine tumor center, the tumor recurred in situ and distant metastasis of the thoracic spine occurred successively. Unfortunately, even after receiving surgical treatment again, the patient died of multiple systemic metastases 15 months after the last operation. The patients in Cases 1 and 3 also died of abdominal and lung metastases 29 and 13 months after surgery, respectively. The patient in Case 6 experienced poor wound healing and died of in situ recurrence 23 months after surgery. The patient in Case 7 had fluid accumulation in the wound with skin ulceration 2 months after surgery, and the wound healed well after debridement. The result of survival curve for all 7 patients was shown in Fig. 3. The median survival of the seven patients was 23 months.

Discussion

LGMS is a solid infiltrative soft tissue tumor that can occur in different parts of the body. The mean age of LGMS patients is around 40 years, although it can occur in all age groups. There seems to be no gender predilection for LGMS [14]. In this study, we summarized seven cases of spinal LGMS in our center, with a mean age of 42.6 ± 17.9 years, which is consistent with the epidemiological characteristics of common LGMS patients. However, the male to female ratio in our series is 2:5, and this gender difference may be due to the limited sample size.

LGMS grows slowly and presents as a painless mass, which is often mistaken as a benign lesion [15, 16]. The tumor is often buried deep in the soft tissue and occasionally grows in the connective tissue space, muscle, or bone with irregular infiltration [17]. More than two-thirds of the patients in our series had tumors larger than 5 cm, and tumor imaging mostly suggested varying degrees of spinal cord or other important soft tissue invasion, including blood vessels, nerve roots, and even the pleura. Almost all patients in our series reported typical pain symptoms, lower limb numbness, fatigue, or even bladder and bowel dysfunction probably due to the external compression on the spinal cord or the nerve roots of the corresponding segments by the tumor.

Wang et al. [18] analyzed the imaging manifestations of five cases of non-spinal bone LGMS involving the scapula (n = 1), 3 cases of the distal femur (n = 3), and inferior pubic ramus (n = 1). Their X-ray findings suggested that most of the bone LGMS exhibited infiltrative growth with osteolytic realization and calcified tissues in some lung metastases, but no significant enhancement was observed in CT imaging. In our study, the X-ray and CT findings were similar to those described in the literature. In patients with lesions involving the thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, wedge deformations of the vertebral bodies were also present, leading to corresponding symptoms of spinal instability. According to the literature, it is important but difficult to differentiate bone LGMS from osteosarcoma, malignant fibrous histiocytoma, or malignant chondroma simply by imaging findings [18]. In our study, three patients were diagnosed with spinal hemangioma, breast cancer metastasis, and neurogenic tumor respectively by initial preoperative imaging evaluation.

There is no doubt that a preoperative biopsy can facilitate surgery protocol formulation, especially for the solitary lesion. however, in our series, some patients forego it due to concerns about potential neurological injury. LGMS is histopathologically characterized by a bundle-like arrangement of tumor cells with immunoreactivity for myofibroblast differentiation [19]. Mentzel et al. [3] reported that LGMS was a mesenchymal malignant tumor differentiated by myofibroblasts, and the tumor tissue showed positive expression of vimentin and at least one myogenic marker (SMA and desmin). As reported in the literature, the Ki-67 labeling index of LGMS is between 8% and 45%, and its expression is inversely correlated with the prognosis of LGMS patients [20]. It was found in our study that the Ki-67 level was higher than 5% in all seven patients, with the maximum being 70%. In addition, the Ki-67 level after revision surgery was higher than that after the primary surgery (30–70% vs. 5–40%), suggesting that Ki-67 may be positively correlated with the recurrence or metastasis of spinal LGMS. In addition, LGMS should be distinguished from fibrotic hyperplasia, aggressive fibromatosis, and inflammatory myofibroblastic sarcoma in pathological differential diagnosis [21].

Xu et al. [22] analyzed 96 cases of LGMS and found that age greater than 60 years, positive lymph nodes, and no surgical treatment were the independent prognostic factors of LGMS, highlighting that surgical resection is the most effective method for the treatment of LGMS. Some previous case reports concluded that LGMS patients with negative surgical margins showed no evidence of disease recurrence during the follow-up periods [23], while others reported a poor prognosis in three patients with positive margins despite postoperative radiotherapy [24]. No recurrence or metastasis was detected at the last follow-up in two (Case 5 and 7) of the three patients who underwent en-bloc resection in our series. However, the prognosis was poor in the remaining patient (Case 2) who underwent two revision surgeries in other hospitals before en-bloc resection in our center, indicating that primary en-bloc resection has a decisive impact on the prognosis of patients with spinal LGMS. Intraoperative blood loss in our seven patients averaged 2100 ± 1322.9 ml, which also indicates that spinal LGMS has a rich blood supply and therefore preoperative embolization of the tumor vessel may be helpful, though more larger-sample studies are required to verify our suggestion.

Nevertheless, compared with LGMS of the extremities, spinal LGMS is surgically more challenging because of the complex spinal anatomy and the close proximity to critical neural and vascular structures. In extremity lesions, wide resection with adequate margins is often feasible, whereas in the spine, the extent of resection is frequently constrained by the need to preserve neurological function and maintain spinal stability. Goals of surgery for spinal tumors are not only to resect lesions, but also to protect spinal cord and/or nerve roots and reconstruction of spinal stability [25]. As aforementioned, spinal LGMS always invade and erode bone structures and together with a large soft tissue mass besides, and the spinal cord and/or nerve roots are always been enrolled into the tumor mass. Consequently, achieving negative surgical margins in spinal LGMS can be difficult, and limited or piecemeal resection may be unavoidable in some cases. Tumor contamination of the surgical field could hardly be excluded. Thus, intraoperative chemotherapy was used to reduce tumor residual and/or local recurrence by rinsing and soaking the surgical field with cisplatin dissolved by desalted water [26].

Up to now, there is no recommended chemotherapy guideline for LGMS patients, and the effectiveness of adjuvant chemotherapy is undefined [27]. Previous studies have shown that the role of chemotherapy is limited in the treatment of LGMS [9]. However, chemotherapy can be considered in cases with evidence of tumor invasion into adjacent tissues or with the presence of lymphatic and/or blood metastasis [24]. Despite the generally reported limited chemosensitivity of LGMS, adjuvant or intraoperative chemotherapy was applied in selected cases in our series, primarily due to concerns regarding incomplete resection or tumor contamination. In our cohort, patients in Case 1 and 3 received the doxorubicin+ifosfamide chemotherapy regimen, but unfortunately, both patients developed in situ recurrence and distant metastasis. Xu et al. [23] performed a statistical analysis on LGMS patients who received perioperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy through the SEER database, and the results indicated that the role of chemotherapy and radiotherapy in improving the survival of LGMS patients was limited, suggesting that chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy should not be recommended as routine treatment for LGMS patients, especially when their postoperative margins are negative.

LGMS in Case 5 of our series involved the cervical spine. After two surgical treatments in our center, this patient received “Anlotinib+doxorubicin+ifosfamide” chemotherapy in combination with targeted therapy, and no tumor recurrence or metastasis was detected at the last follow-up. The multitargeted tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) anlotinib has exhibited extensive anticancer activity both in vivo and in vitro against a variety of solid tumors [28, 29]. Some recent studies have also suggested that anlotinib could potentially be used to treat bone and soft tissue sarcoma [30]. Yan et al. reported a case of LGMS in a patient with high PI3K/Akt expression and multiple lung and abdominal cavities. After the treatment failure of imatinib, liposome doxorubicin, and ifosfamide, patinib in combination with radiotherapy was applied and achieved a good prognosis [17]. It appears that these treatment strategies provide a new direction for the treatment of LGMS, though further research is needed.

LGMS used to be considered associated with a high risk of recurrence but a low risk of metastasis [12]. However, a high metastatic rate was observed in our case series, in which three patients developed distant metastases from different sites (lung, spine, and abdominal cavity). A recent population-based study from the United States reported 49 cases of LGMS, in whom the five-year overall survival rate was 71.6% [27]. According to some reports in the literature [12], the local recurrence rate of LGMS is about 29.1%, and the advanced metastasis rate is about 17.7%. However, the corresponding figure in our series is 28.6% (2/7) and 42.9% (3/7), and this poor prognosis for spinal LGMS seems to be associated with the tumor location (involving the spinal cord or nerve roots), the large tumor size (making complete resection difficult), and the complex anatomies of the spinal cord (making radical resection difficult). This study is limited by its retrospective design, small sample size, and relatively short follow-up, which preclude statistically robust conclusions. In addition, the extended study period may have introduced heterogeneity owing to advances in surgical techniques and adjuvant therapies over time. Nevertheless, this case series provides preliminary, surgery-focused clinical evidence for the management of spinal low-grade myofibroblastic sarcoma.

Conclusions

Spinal LGMS is a very rare malignancy with a likelihood of misdiagnosis and a poor prognosis. Although radical surgical resection is the optimal treatment option for most solid tumors, it is almost impossible for spinal LGMS due to the complex anatomy of the spinal region and the large size of the tumor. Targeted therapy may provide a potential direction for the treatment of spinal LGMS, but its therapeutic efficacy needs to be confirmed by more multi-center clinical studies.
