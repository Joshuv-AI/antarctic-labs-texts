Notch3/4 Knockdown Inhibits Colon Adenocarcinoma Progression by Suppressing Tumor Cell Activity and Orchestrating VEGFA‐Dependent Tumor Immune Microenvironment
Authors: Wei Liu, Guhang Tang, Ningfu Li, Jiasheng Wang, Han Zhao, Chong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundColon adenocarcinoma (COAD) is a prevalent gastrointestinal malignancy characterized by dysregulation of multiple cellular signaling pathways, including the Notch pathway. However, the specific biological roles of Notch3 and Notch4 in COAD remain incompletely characterized.MethodsWe aimed to investigate the effects of Notch3 and Notch4 downregulation on the tumor immune microenvironment and angiogenesis in COAD. Using a combination of in vitro and in vivo models, we evaluated the impacts of Notch3/4 interference on cell proliferation, migration, immune cell infiltration, and tumor progression.ResultsInterfering with Notch3 and 4 significantly suppressed subcutaneous tumor progression. Notably, this inhibition was not only associated with reduced cell viability, but also with an altered immune environment characterized by enhanced macrophage M1 polarization and T lymphocyte activation, which may be linked to the c‐Myc/VEGFA signaling axis but requires further mechanistic validation. Furthermore, the combination of regorafenib and Notch3/4 knockout (KO) exerted enhanced anti‐tumor effects. More importantly, Mol_2, a candidate compound with predicted binding affinity for both Notch3 and Notch4, was screened out to significantly reduce the protein levels of Notch3 and Notch4 and tumor progression.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that targeting Notch3 and Notch4 receptors may have therapeutic potential for modulating the tumor microenvironment and suppressing tumor progression in COAD.

Introduction

Colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) is a malignant epithelial neoplasm arising in the colon. Along with rectal cancer, it is classified collectively as colorectal cancer (CRC). COAD belongs to the category of malignant tumors in the lower gastrointestinal tract and poses significant risks to one's overall health. Currently, it has become the second leading cause of cancer‐related deaths worldwide [1]. Based on the latest data from the National Cancer Center in early 2022, it is evident that the incidence and mortality rates of CRC in China rank second and fourth, respectively, among malignant tumors. Furthermore, these rates have shown a consistent upward trend from 2000 to 2016 [2]. The pathogenesis of CRC is multifactorial, involving lifestyle patterns, dietary factors, genetic susceptibility, chronic digestive disorders, carcinogen exposure, and gut microbiota dysbiosis [3].

Despite continuous improvements in screening, surgical techniques and systemic therapies, a large proportion of patients still present with advanced/metastatic disease, and most colorectal tumors are mismatch‐repair proficient/microsatellite‐stable (pMMR/MSS), which tend to be immunologically “cold” and respond poorly to immune checkpoint blockade. Recent single‐cell and spatial profiling studies have further revealed that COAD/CRC tumors are organized into coordinated multicellular immune niches and exhibit substantial inter‐patient heterogeneity in immune‐evasion programs, underscoring the need to identify actionable pathways that shape the tumor immune microenvironment [4, 5].

Increasing evidence suggests that the immune microenvironment plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of CRC, as well as in immunotherapy [6]. Immune cells such as T cells, B cells, and macrophages collectively form the complex immune microenvironment of CRC and participate in tumor development [7]. Understanding the infiltration levels and functions of different immune cell types in the CRC immune microenvironment is of great significance for evaluating the prognosis of CRC patients undergoing immunotherapy and identifying potential therapeutic targets [8]. The tumor immune microenvironment is rich in macrophages, which are considered to be one of the most abundant immune cell populations infiltrating solid tumors [9]. Based on immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissue samples from 81 CRC patients, infiltration of M2 macrophages is associated with higher lymphovascular invasion, tumor invasion, TNM staging, and poorer overall survival (OS). Functionally, M2 macrophages can promote tumor invasion and metastasis through various mechanisms [10].

High‐resolution single‐cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses have highlighted that myeloid compartments (including SPP1+ macrophages) and endothelial/stromal programs can form spatially organized hubs that foster immune suppression and tumor progression. Importantly, tumor–stromal crosstalk at invasive fronts can induce immune‐regulatory molecules (e.g., HLA‐G) and reshape macrophage states, providing mechanistic links between tumor invasion, angiogenesis‐related programs, and impaired cytotoxic T‐cell activity [4, 5, 11]. This intercellular communication relies on a repertoire of signaling pathways [12]. Among these, the Notch signaling pathway has been extensively investigated due to its canonical role in mediating communication between adjacent cells [13]. Nonetheless, the distinct roles and cooperative mechanisms of different Notch receptors, particularly Notch3 and Notch4, in this context remain poorly understood, especially in colorectal cancer [14].

In recent years, increasing evidence has shown that the Notch signaling pathway, in addition to its association with tumor cell proliferation and metastasis [15, 16], may also contribute to the formation of a tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment [17, 18, 19], indicating a critical role of Notch signaling in the formation of tumors. Currently, four types of Notch receptors have been discovered in mammals, namely Notch‐1, Notch‐2, Notch‐3, and Notch‐4, which are typically activated by adjacent cells Notch ligands through mutual triggering [20, 21]. Several studies have shown that aberrant activation of Notch‐1 has been shown to initiate CRC, and Notch1‐induced EMT is closely related to CRC invasion and metastasis [22]. In addition, Notch‐2 was also identified as a novel target of β‐catenin dependent Wnt signaling in CRC [20]. However, the role of Notch 3 or 4 in CRC is still unclear. Further exploration of the underlying mechanisms is of great significance to elucidate the role of Notch signaling in the development of CRC.

Beyond intrinsic tumor‐cell oncogenic signaling, Notch pathway activity is increasingly recognized as a critical regulator of immune and vascular components of the TME. For example, tumor‐derived Jagged1 can activate Notch signaling to drive cytokine production, macrophage recruitment, and “education”, and consequent suppression of CD8+ T‐cell proliferation and tumoricidal activity; Notch inhibition combined with PD‐1 blockade has shown enhanced anti‐tumor efficacy in vivo. Moreover, VEGF‐A signaling is a central axis linking angiogenesis to immune evasion, and recent work indicates that VEGF‐targeted interventions can potentiate anti‐tumor immune responses by reprogramming the TME, providing a rationale for exploring Notch–VEGF crosstalk and combination strategies [14, 23, 24, 25].

Collectively, these recent advances suggest that integrating pathway‐level regulation (e.g., Notch3/4 and VEGFA‐related programs) with immune‐contexture remodeling may be essential to overcome immunotherapy resistance and to stratify patients for precision combination regimens [4, 14].

In this study, we used online database to analyze the abnormal expression of Notch3 and Notch4 in COAD patients and the correlation between them and patient survival. By performing double gene knockout of Notch3/4 in CRC cells, we confirmed that the deletion of these two genes could inhibit tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. On the one hand, Notch3/4 knockout directly affects tumor cell proliferation potentially through inhibition of downstream angiogenesis signaling mediated by its ligand Jagged1. On the other hand, reduced VEGFA secretion secondary to Notch3/4 knockout may contribute to enhanced macrophage M1 polarization and T lymphocyte activation, thereby suppressing tumor growth. In addition, Notch3/4 inhibition combined with regorafenib, a multi‐targeted anti‐cancer agent, exerted enhanced anti‐tumor activity. Importantly, Mol_2, a candidate compound predicted to interact with Notch3 and Notch4, was screened from the NPAI natural products database by AI algorithm, which could also inhibit tumor biological activity and improve tumor immune microenvironment. Taken together, these results indicate that Notch3 and 4 can effectively regulate CRC cell growth and tumor immune microenvironment, and we are the first to identify Mol_2 as a candidate natural compound associated with Notch3/4 inhibition anti‐tumor activity.

Material and Methods

TCGA, GEO, GSEA (Gene Set Enrichment Analysis) and Survival Curve Data Analysis

Clinical data and RNA expression data with normalized RSEM values of the colorectal carcinoma datasets (TCGA‐COAD) were retrieved from FireBrowse (http://frebrowse.org). The overall survival of the Notch3/4 was assessed by the TISIDB, a web portal for tumor and immune system interaction, which integrates multiple heterogeneous data types. The genes whose expression was positively associated with Notch3/4 expression in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)‐colorectal carcinoma were picked out using the LinkedOmics browser (http://www.linkedomics.org), which allows the online analysis of TCGA data. Further, we used these genes to do Gene Set Enrichment Analysis. The correlation results were cross‐validation using Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) (http://gepia2.cancer‐pku.cn/#index).

Patients and Tissue Samples

Human COAD tissue samples were obtained from The Dazu Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. All samples were obtained with informed consent, and the study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Chongqing Medical University, as well as the principles expressed in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell Lines and Cell Culture

MC38, CT26, HEK293T, RAW264.7, HUVEC cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). All cell lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination prior to experimentation. The cell lines were cultured in DMEM (Invitrogen/Termo Fisher Scientifc, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S) (Beyotime Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China) at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Lentivirus Production and Infection

Lentiviral particles were packaged using HEK293T cells. Briefly, 5 × 105 cells were seed in a 60 mm dish in complete DMEM culture medium 1 day before transfection. On the day of transfection, 18 μL NED3000 (Western, Chongqing, China), 3 μg of lentiviral construct as well as 1.5 μg of psPAX2 and pMD2. G were pipetted directly into 182 μL serum and antibiotics‐free DMEM medium. Then vortexed and added to HEK293T cells in a drop‐wise manner 15 min later. The virus was collected 48 h later and filtered using non‐pyrogenic filters (0.45 mm, Merck Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Diluted lentivirus and 8 μg/mL polybrene (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) were added to targeting cells. For stable cell selection, puromycin (1 μg/mL) and FCM were used. The lentiviral vector and sgRNA design information are available in (Figure S3 and Figure S4). Single‐cell clones of Notch3/4 knockout (KO) MC38 cells were subsequently generated, and the exact genomic editing sites and corresponding sequence validation of representative single clones are provided in (Figure S5), confirming the successful disruption of the Notch3 and Notch4 loci.

Cell Viability, Invasion, Migration Assays and Colony Formation Assay

Cell proliferation was measured by counting viable cells with a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8, Western, Chongqing, China). The stably transfected MC38 cells and control cells were plated into a 96‐well plate. At the same time each day for 3 consecutive days, the culture medium was changed and replenished with 10 μL CCK‐8 and 100 μL fresh DMEM medium. For counting, the cells were incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h and the plate read by a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific) at 450 nm. The invasion and migration ability of indicated cells were evaluated by transwell assay with or without Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) using 24‐well chemotaxis chambers (Corning cell culture inserts (Corning, NY, USA), 8 μm pore size). For colony formation assays, approximately 50 cells per well were seeded into 24‐well plates and cultured with the same medium for 7 days. Cells were then fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% (w/v) crystal violet solution for 30 min.

Matrigel Tube Formation Assay

Briefly, the inner wells of μ‐Slide Angiogenesis slides (ibidi, Germany; cat. no. 81506) were coated with 10 μL of growth factor‐reduced Matrigel (BD Biosciences; cat. no. 356231) and allowed to polymerize for 30 min at 37°C. Next, 50 μL of HUVEC suspension (1000 cells per well) was added to each well, mixed with conditioned medium from either control or Notch3/4‐KO MC38 cells. After incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 30 min to allow cell attachment, an additional 50 μL of the corresponding conditioned medium was added to each well. Tube formation was imaged at 24 h and 48 h using an ECLIPSE Ti‐s inverted microscope (Nikon, Japan). The number of tubular structures and total branch length were quantified using the “Angiogenesis Analyzer” plugin for ImageJ software.

Allograft Tumor Model

Three distinct in vivo models were established in this study, with all experiments repeated at least three times independently.

Immunodeficient Nude Mouse Xenograft Model

Male BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks of age) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions. Mice were randomly divided into experimental groups (n = 6–8 per group). For tumor inoculation, 1 × 106 MC38 or CT26 cells suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right groin of each mouse. Tumor volume was measured every 3 days using Vernier calipers, calculated as volume = (length × width2) / 2. Mice were euthanized 21 days after inoculation, and tumors were excised and weighed.

Immunocompetent Syngeneic Mouse Model

Male C57BL/6 mice and BALB/c mice (6 weeks of age) were used for syngeneic tumor models. MC38 cells are syngeneic to C57BL/6 mice, and CT26 cells are syngeneic to BALB/c mice. Mice were randomly divided into NC and Notch3/4‐KO groups (n = 6–8 per group). A total of 1 × 106 tumor cells suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right groin. Tumor volume was monitored every 3 days for up to 21 days, at which point mice were euthanized for tumor collection and downstream analyses.

Combination Treatment With Regorafenib

For combination therapy experiments, the MC38/C57BL/6 syngeneic model was used. When subcutaneous tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized into four groups: control, Notch3/4 siRNA, regorafenib, and Notch3/4 siRNA + regorafenib (n = 8 per group). Intratumoral injection of Notch3/4 siRNA (5 nmol per mouse) was administered every 3 days for a total of 5 injections. Regorafenib (3 mg/kg) was administered by gavage once daily for 15 consecutive days, with saline as vehicle control. Blood samples were collected on days 7 and 14 via orbital venous plexus for cytokine detection. At the end of the observation period, all mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, and processed for flow cytometry and histological analyses. The siRNA sequences are listed in (Table S1).

RNA Extraction and RT‐PCR

Total RNA from cells was purified using Trizol reagent (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China). Only the extracted total RNA samples with OD A260/A280 ratio from 1.8 to 2.0, which indicate that the RNA is pure, were subsequently analyzed. The cDNA synthesis was performed with 5 μg RNA per 20 μL using Hifair III 1 st strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix (YEASEN Biotech, Shanghai, China). Real time PCR was carried out on an Applied Biosystems (ABI system) using Hieff UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (YEASEN Biotech, Shanghai, China). Primers were designed with the Primer Express v3.0 Software. The mRNA levels were analyzed using a real‐time PCR assay as previously described. Briefly, the cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation for 10 min at 95°C followed by 40 cycles of denaturation (10 s at 95°C), annealing and elongation (30 s at 60°C). The relative expression was calculated and normalized using the RQ value method relative to GAPDH. The primers used for quantification were listed in (Table S2).

Western Blot

Western blot was performed as previously described. Briefly, cells were lysed on ice for 30 min using cell lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotech, Shanghai, China). Then the lysis was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. The protein concentration of each sample was determined using BCA Protein Assay Kit (TaKaRa, Tokyko, Japan). Samples were then separated by 8%–12% Bis‐Tris PAGE electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, USA) for detection. Western blots were probed overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies in Tris‐Buffered Saline Tween‐20 (TBST) containing 5% skim milk. After washing for 3 times with TBST, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a respective IgG‐HRP labeled second antibody (1:2000) in TBST containing 5% skim milk. Antigens were revealed using ECL Chemiluminescence Solution (Applygen Tech, Beijing, China). Quantification of bands was achieved by densitometry using the TanonImage system (Tanon, Shanghai, China). The antibodies used in WB analysis were listed in (Table S3).

Flow Cytometry Analysis

Cells in vivo and tumors from tumor‐carrying mice were collected. For RAW264.7, Cells collected from all groups were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and washed twice with PBS, and then punched with 0.3% Triton X‐100 for 5 min. After washed twice with PBS, cells were blocked with 1% BSA 200 mL for 1 h. Cells solutions were then centrifuged and the supernatants were discarded. Then, 0.125 μg of CD86/APC (CD206/APC) and 0.25 μg of iNOS/PE‐Cy7 (Arg‐1/PE) (both from eBioscience, CA, USA) were added to label M1 (M2). Respectively, incubated at 40°C for 30 min. Followed by washing twice with PBS, and the cell polarity was measured by flow cytometry. Flow cytometry acquisition was performed on CytoFLEX (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using CytExpert software (2.4.0.28).

Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assays (ELISAs) for IL‐2、IFN‐γ、IL‐4、IL‐10

The mouse IL‐2, IFN‐γ, IL‐4, IL‐10 were determined using a commercial ELISA kit (Jingmei, Jiangsu, China). The protocols were performed as the manufacturers described. In brief, 96‐well plates were coated with the corresponding capture antibody. After incubation with blocking buffer (PBS + 1% bovine serum albumin), a 100 μL aliquot of either standards or serum was added into each well individually, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at room temperature. The detector antibody was added to the wells, the plate was incubated, and unbound detector antibody was removed by washing. The avidin‐peroxidase conjugate was added, the plate was incubated, and unbound conjugate was removed by washing. The enzymatic reaction catalyzed by horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was initiated by adding the substrate, 3, 3, 5, 5 tetramethylbenzidine dihydrochloride (TMB). The absorbance was measured by using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm after the reaction was stopped with 2 M H2SO4.

Immunohistochemistry Staining

Sections were cut at a thickness of 4 μm from routinely prepared, 10% formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded blocks of tumor tissue. The following primary antibodies were used: anti‐CEA antibody (1:100, Affinity, DF6152, USA), anti‐CD57 antibody (1:100, Affinity, AF5125, USA), anti‐CD68 antibody (1:100, Proteintech, 28,058–1‐Ap, China). Paraffinembedded sections were deparaffinized in xylene and dehydrated in graded alcohol. Antigen retrieval was performed in EDTA pH 9.0 for Notch2 and the rest in citrate buffer pH 6.0. The sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with the diluted primary antibodies. Next, the sections were rinsed with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) and incubated with the horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody (Beyotime, China). After rinsing with PBS, the sections were stained with diaminobenzidine (DAB) and counterstained with hematoxylin blue.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical software GraphPad Prism Version 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all statistical analyses. All data were presented as mean ± SDs. For comparisons between two independent groups, Student's t‐test was used. For comparisons among three or more groups, one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was applied. For longitudinal data with repeated measurements over time (e.g., tumor growth curves, time‐course CCK‐8 assays), two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA was used, followed by Sidak's multiple comparisons test for post hoc pairwise comparisons. Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared by the Gehan–Breslow–Wilcoxon test. Correlation analyses were assessed by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Statistical significance was defined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Abnormal High Expression of Notch3 and 4 in Human CRC Tissue

To further clarify the role of Notch3 and 4 in CRC, we used several online databases to analyze and found that they were significantly overexpressed in colon adenocarcinoma (Figure 1A,E), and positively correlated with tumor stage (Figure 1B,F). An independent in‐house reanalysis of the raw TCGA‐COAD dataset corroborated these differential expression findings (Figure S2). Consistently, validation in an independent external patient cohort from the GEO database (GSE39582, n = 566) further confirmed that both Notch3 and Notch4 are highly expressed in COAD tumor tissues compared with normal colon tissues (Figure S2). This independent GEO cohort serves as a cross‐dataset validation complementary to the TCGA‐based analyses shown in Figure 1, reinforcing the robustness of the differential expression findings.

More importantly, online data also showed that Notch3 and 4 expression was negatively correlated with the prognosis of CRC patients, especially overall survival (Figures 1C,D and 1G,H). Finally, the upregulated expression of Notch3 and 4 was further confirmed by real‐time PCR (Figure 1I) and immunohistochemistry (Figure 1J) of clinical specimens collected from patients with CRC.

Notch3 and 4 Knockout Obviously Affect the Biological Behavior of CRC Cell Line

To further investigate the effect of Notch3 and 4 expression on CRC cells, lentivirus and CRISPR/Cas9 system were used to generate Notch3/4 KO CRC cells lines. We found that both single or double knock‐out could significantly inhibit the proliferative activity of MC38 cells (Figure 2A).

Specifically, knockout of Notch3 and/or Notch4 significantly suppressed colony formation (Figure 2B), metastasis (Figure 2C,D) and invasion (Figure 2E,F), and tube formation (Figure 2G–I), while promoting G1/S phase cell cycle arrest (Figure 2J,K). Importantly, these effects were more pronounced in the Notch3/4 double‐KO group, indicating a cooperative functional effect in Notch3/4‐KO cells. In conclusion, these results suggest that Notch3/4 depletion of CRC cell signally affect its basic biological behavior.

Notch3/4‐KO Suppresses Tumor Progression in Both Immunodeficient and Immunocompetent Syngeneic Mouse Models

In order to further investigate the effect of Notch3/4‐KO on tumor progression, we first established subcutaneous xenograft models in immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice and found that Notch3/4‐KO significantly inhibited tumor growth in both MC38 and CT26 cell‐derived tumors (Figure 3A,B). Similar results were observed in CT26 cells (Figure 3A,B). Given that previous studies have shown that the interaction between tumor cells and tumor microenvironment jointly determines tumor fate, we further examined the effect of Notch3/4‐KO in immunocompetent syngeneic mouse models—MC38 cells in C57BL/6 mice and CT26 cells in BALB/c mice. Consistently, Notch3/4‐KO markedly suppressed tumor progression in both syngeneic models. Similarly, Notch3/4‐KO markedly suppressed MC38 or CT26 tumor progression (Figure 3C,D). Notably, the magnitude of tumor growth inhibition was more pronounced in immunocompetent mice than in nude mice, suggesting that immune modulation contributes to the anti‐tumor effects of Notch3/4 depletion.

Notch3/4 Was Closely Related to Tumor Immune Cell Infiltration

The above results show that Notch3/4‐KO seems to have a better tumor suppression effect in immunocompetent mice than in immunodeficient nude mice, which is consistent with a modulatory role of Notch3/4 in the tumor immune microenvironment. We firstly further explore the relationship between Notch3/4 and tumor immunity through the TCGA database. We found that the expression of Notch3 and Notch4 are related to the infiltration of vary immune cells, especially macrophages and T lymphocytes (Figure 4A,B). In addition, the Notch3 and Notch4 expression were positively correlated with the abundance of immune cells in COAD (Figure 4C,P), especially T lymphocytes and macrophages. Taken together, these results suggest that the tumor suppressive effect of Notch3/4‐KO might be related to the tumor immune microenvironment.

To further confirm that Notch3/4‐KO affects the tumor immune microenvironment, we first examined the polarization of macrophages treated with MC38 tumor cells conditioned medium (CM) and found that Notch3/4‐KO significantly increased the proportion of M1‐like macrophages and decreased the proportion of M2‐like macrophages (Figure 5A,B). The comparable results of macrophage polarization were also validated in vivo (Figure 5C). Moreover, the results were also validated in the CT26 mouse tumor model (Figure 5D). And we also found that the expression level of IFNγ in T lymphocytes was significantly increased in Notch3/4‐KO group (Figure 5E).

To further explore how Notch3/4 regulates the tumor immune microenvironment, we first examined its known downstream signaling changes. The results showed that Notch3/4‐KO could significantly reduce the protein levels of Jagged1, c‐Myc, MAPK, and VEGFA (Figure 5F). Previous studies have confirmed that c‐Myc could regulate tumor cell proliferation, and cell angiogenesis through MAPK/VEGFA signaling, which was consistent with the inhibition of tumor proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and angiogenesis in the above experiments. Furthermore, we found that the addition of VEGFA could counteract the Notch3/4‐KO‐mediated M1 polarization of macrophages (Figure 5G) and T lymphocyte activation (Figure 5H), These findings confirm that VEGFA is functionally involved in Notch3/4‐regulated immune remodeling, and suggest that the c‐Myc/VEGFA axis may serve as a potential underlying mechanism, although direct causal evidence for the Notch3/4 → c‐Myc → VEGFA cascade remains to be established through dedicated gain/loss‐of‐function studies of c‐Myc.

Combination of Regorafenib and Notch3/4 Knockdown Exerts Enhanced Inhibitory Effects on Tumor Cell Biological Behaviors

Given that Notch3/4‐KO could not only directly affect CRC cell proliferation but also regulate tumor immunity, it is of great significance to explore the therapeutic strategy based on Notch3/4 signaling pathway for CRC treatment. Previous research has found that the therapeutic effect of regorafenib is directly proportional to the high expression of Notch1 in tumor tissues [26], suggesting that the treatment of regorafenib is closely related to Notch signaling. In vitro CCK‐8 assays demonstrated that the combination of regorafenib and Notch3/4 siRNA knockdown exerted enhanced growth‐inhibitory effects on MC38 and CT26 cells (Figure 6A). And regorafenib could also significantly inhibit the expression of c‐Myc/VEGFA signaling related genes, including Jagged1, cMyc, MAPK1 and VEGFA (Figure 6B–M). Similar reductions in protein levels were also observed (Figure 6N).

Combination of Regorafenib and Notch3/4 Knockout Exerts Enhanced Modulatory Effects on the Tumor Immune Microenvironment and Tumor Progression

To further explore whether regorafenib and Notch3/4‐KO also synergistically regulate tumor immunity and tumor growth, we examined the tumor immune microenvironment after regorafenib and Notch3/4‐KO treatment in vivo (Figure 7A). The results showed that regorafenib and Notch3/4‐KO significantly improved tumor immune cell composition in the MC38 (Figure 7B) and CT26 (Figure 7C) cell‐based subcutaneous xenograft models. Besides, regorafenib cooperated with Notch3/4‐KO to increase the expression of Th1‐related genes (Figure 7D) and inhibited Th2‐related genes (Figure 7E) in the serum of mice. Most importantly, regorafenib and Notch3/4‐KO could further inhibit subcutaneous tumor progression in mice compared with treatment alone (Figure 7F,G).

Mol_2, a Candidate Antitumor Compound Associated With Notch3/4 Inhibition

Given the important role of Notch3 and 4 in the progression of colorectal cancer, it is particularly important to further explore the potential substances that can regulate both of them. We first screened three compounds with predicted binding affinity for Notch3 from the NPAI natural Products database based on the NPAI Engine reverse screening function (Figure 8A, Figure S1A,B). Then molecular docking was performed to predict the affinity of Notch3 and 4 with three natural products, and Mol_2 showed the best affinity for both Notch3 and 4 (Figure 8B,C, Figure S1C–F). Next, we went on to examine the effect of three small molecule drugs on cell proliferative activity. CCK8 assay showed that Mol_2 significantly inhibited the proliferation of MC38 and CT26 cells in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 8D, Figure S1G). However, the effect of the other two small molecule drugs on cell proliferation activity was not obvious (Figure S1H–K). Therefore, we subsequently focused on the effect of Mol_2. Similar to Notch3/4 knockdown, Mol_2 also significantly promoted tumor cell apoptosis (Figure 8E) and cell cycle arrest (Figure 8F), while inhibiting cell migration (Figure 8G). In addition, Mol_2 could significantly repress the protein levels of Notch3, Notch4, c—Myc, MAPK1 and VEGFA in tumor cells (Figure 8H). Mol_2 treated tumor cell CM also obviously inhibited the secretion of pro‐tumor cytokines IL10 and IL4, and promoted the secretion of anti‐tumor cytokines IL2 and IFNγ by macrophages (Figure 8I). In vivo experiments further confirmed Mol_2 could signally improve the tumor immune microenvironment (Figure 8J–N) and inhibit mice tumor progression (Figure 8O–Q).

Discussion

Notch signaling plays an important role in the regulation of CRC cell growth, metastasis, cell cycle and apoptosis [13]. Here, we demonstrated that Notch3 and 4 are abnormally highly expressed in CRC cells and predict poor patient prognosis. Notch3/4‐KO could not only affect the proliferation activity and metastasis of tumor cells, but also modulate key immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment potentially via.

c‐Myc/VEGFA signaling, especially macrophages and T lymphocytes. Moreover, regorafenib, an orally active multi‐kinase inhibitor, exerted enhanced anti‐tumor effects in combination with Notch3/4‐KO in CRC.

Finally, Mol_2, a candidate natural compound associated with reduced Notch3 and 4 protein levels and anti‐tumor activity, was screened, which provides the possibility for further clinical targeting of Notch3 and 4 against tumors.

Recent multi‐omic and spatial profiling has revealed that even early colorectal lesions already contain structured epithelial–myeloid–endothelial niches that prefigure immune exclusion and angiogenic remodeling, highlighting the need to interrogate Notch3/4 functions in a spatially resolved manner [12, 27]. In future work, integrating spatial transcriptomics or imaging mass cytometry with Notch3/4 perturbation could pinpoint which stromal compartments (endothelium vs. fibroblasts vs. myeloid cells) are most responsive to Notch3/4 suppression and help derive clinically actionable biomarkers.

Previous studies have found that aggressive CRC is difficult to treat due to the activation of oncogenic signaling pathways such as Notch signaling [28]. And Notch3 was proven to regulate the tumorigenic characteristics of CRC cells and the infiltration of immunosuppressive cells in the microenvironment [29, 30], but its specific molecular mechanism has not been completely cleared. More interestingly, Zhang et al. found that the role of Notch4 in CRC cells is completely opposite to ours [31], which might be due to the different CRC cell lines we used. Here we demonstrated that the tumorigenic properties of Notch3 and 4 might be associated with enhanced cellular oncogenic activity and immunosuppressive cell infiltration mediated by c‐Myc signaling, suggesting that knockout of Notch3 and 4 might also be an effective treatment strategy for CRC. Therefore, in‐depth exploration of inhibitors or therapeutic antibodies targeting Notch3 and 4 might have good clinical application potential.

From a therapeutic perspective, pan‐Notch blockade has been limited by context‐dependent biology and dose‐limiting toxicities (notably gastrointestinal toxicity), which has accelerated interest in receptor‐ or ligand‐selective strategies and rational combinations [20, 32]. Notably, the feasibility of selectively targeting NOTCH3 has been demonstrated using NOTCH3‐directed antibody–drug conjugates, supporting the clinical tractability of NOTCH3 as a drug target and motivating the development of NOTCH3/4‐biased inhibitors for CRC [33].

Importantly, we found that simultaneous knockout of Notch3 and 4 resulted in a better anti‐tumor effect than knockout of Notch3 or 4 alone, suggesting that the downstream anti‐tumor signals mediated by them are not entirely dependent on c‐Myc signaling. For example, previous studies have shown Notch3 might increase tumor formation via DLL4 signaling [30], and the Notch4‐GATA4‐IRG1 axis in tumor cells might regulate macrophage polarization [34]. Therefore, further exploration of its downstream regulatory signals is extremely important for targeting Notch3 and 4 signaling in the treatment of CRC.

Mechanistically, our observations of reduced VEGFA expression and macrophage infiltration after Notch3/4 knockout align with emerging concepts that vascular abnormality and myeloid immunosuppression are mutually reinforcing. For example, NOTCH3 signaling has been associated with enhanced immunosuppressive cell infiltration and M2‐like macrophage polarization in CRC [29]. Vascular‐normalization approaches—beyond VEGF blockade—such as inhibition of the CD93/IGFBP7 axis, can stabilize tumor vasculature and enhance effector immune‐cell trafficking, thereby sensitizing tumors to immune checkpoint blockade [35, 36]. Together, these data support a working model in which suppressing the Notch3/4–c‐Myc–VEGFA axis may simultaneously restrain tumor cell‐intrinsic growth programs and reprogram the stromal “supply lines” that sustain M2‐like TAM accumulation. Formal validation of this linear cascade will require targeted c‐Myc modulation experiments in future studies.

It is worth pointing out that regorafenib, the first small‐molecule multi‐kinase inhibitor to achieve survival benefits in metastatic colorectal cancer [37, 38], exerted enhanced anti‐tumor activity when combined with Notch3/4‐KO.

Furthermore, regorafenib was found to be used to treat patients who had previously received or were ineligible for anti‐VEGF therapy, which might further confirm that Notch3/4‐KO is anti‐tumor via c‐Myc signaling. Further preclinical trials of Notch3/4 inhibitors combined with regorafenib might achieve better clinical effect. Meanwhile, given the role of Notch3/4‐KO in immune cell infiltration, it seems necessary to explore whether it synergizes with PD1 or CTLA4 monoclonal antibody.

Clinically, a prospective phase 2 study of regorafenib plus nivolumab in pMMR/MSS metastatic CRC reported modest overall activity but responses largely restricted to patients without baseline liver metastases, underscoring organ‐specific immunosuppression and the need for biomarker‐guided combinations [39]. Our finding that Mol_2 reduces pro‐angiogenic and macrophage‐associated signals suggests that Mol_2 could be explored as a TME‐priming partner to regorafenib and PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade, particularly in immune‐excluded settings.

In the end, we first discovered the natural compound Mol_2 could suppress tumor cell biological behaviors, improve the immune microenvironment, and inhibit tumor progression concurrent with downregulation of Notch3 and 4 expression; however, the direct physical interaction between Mol_2 and the two receptors, as well as the exact binding loci, remain to be experimentally validated.

Meanwhile, whether the anti‐tumor mechanism of Mol_2 is the same as Notch3/4 knockdown also needs to be further explored. But it should be pointed out that, the antitumor effect of Mol_2 is clear, no matter it is accomplished by or part of Notch3 and 4.

Conclusion

Overall, we have initially revealed the function and significance of Notch3 and 4 in CRC, and identified a candidate natural compound associated with Notch3/4 inhibition (Mol_2) with anti‐tumor activity. Mechanistically, the c‐Myc/VEGFA axis represents a plausible but not yet fully validated pathway underlying Notch3/4‐mediated tumor progression and immune microenvironment regulation.

Our findings shed light on novel therapeutic strategies for CRC patients based on the Notch signal of tumor cells to regulate autobiologic behavior and tumor immunity.
