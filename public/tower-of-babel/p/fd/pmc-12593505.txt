Diagnostic implication of left atrial stiffness index in patients with SLE with left ventricular diastolic dysfunction
Authors: Xiaofang Zhong, Yingying Pan, Lixiong Liu, Guijuan Peng, Qian Liu, Xiaohua Liu, Yingqi Zheng, Xiaoxuan Lin, Yuanyuan Sheng, Hui Wang, Lixin Chen, Jinfeng Xu, Shuyu Luo, Yingying Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe left atrial stiffness index (LASI) has been proven to be a promising marker for assessing left atrial (LA) and left ventricular diastolic functions. This study aimed to evaluate the implication of LASI in diagnosing left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) in patients with SLE.Methods145 patients diagnosed with SLE were enrolled and subdivided into two groups based on the absence of concomitant lupus nephritis (LN). Additionally, a control group comprising 57 healthy volunteers was recruited. Speckle tracking echocardiography (STE) was performed in all participants. Conventional parameters and the LA strains were obtained, and LASI was calculated.Results(1) Patients with SLE presented significantly increased LA size and reduced function, with a significant increase in LASI, especially in those with concomitant LN. (2) LASI was positively correlated with age, interventricular end-diastolic septal thickness (IVSd) and E/e′ (mitral E peak velocity to e′ peak velocity) average, and negatively correlated with LA total emptying fraction (LATEF), LA passive emptying fraction (LAPEF), glomerular filtration rate (GFR), LA reservoir strain (LASr) and LA conduit strain (LAScd). Multivariate analysis showed that age, IVSd, maximum LA size and SLE activity index were independent determinants of LASI. (3) LASI demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for LVDD compared with E/e′ average, LATEF, LASr and LAScd, with an area under the curve of 0.919.ConclusionsLASI can effectively reflect changes in LA function in patients with SLE and provide superior diagnostic accuracy compared with other parameters and LA strains for LVDD. Therefore, LASI could be a potential marker for the early detection of LVDD in patients with SLE.

Introduction

SLE is an autoimmune disease which can severely damage organs, especially the heart,1 through prolonged inflammation and abnormal immune responses. Cardiac involvement manifests variably, including asymptomatic cases, at any stage of the course.2 Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction (LVDD) is a prevalent, early-onset and persistent complication in patients with SLE.3 4 In patients with LVDD, the left atrial load increases due to the elevated left ventricular (LV) pressure during diastole, which contributes to atrial remodelling and subsequent dysfunction.3 5 Previous studies have demonstrated that LA remodelling and dysfunction, as early and critical pathological alterations, are associated with potential adverse cardiovascular events.6 LA function has been proposed as an accurate and sensitive indicator for disease prognosis.7 However, the non-specific manifestations following cardiac involvement in patients with SLE pose challenges for early and accurate diagnosis that potentially results in cardiogenic death without timely intervention.8 Therefore, assessing LA function provides both diagnostic and prognostic value for patients with SLE.

Speckle tracking echocardiography has been extensively applied as a novel technique for assessing LA function. Researchers have shown that the LA strain can detect changes in LA function earlier than other indicators in patients with SLE.4 However, STE measurement is load-dependent, causing variability under different haemodynamic states. The LA stiffness index, as a novel metric for evaluating LA function under abnormal pressure and volume loads, is calculated as the ratio of LA pressure to peak longitudinal strain. By incorporating LA perfusion pressure and strain, LASI is more accurate and sensitive than traditional indicators.9 To date, LASI has not yet been investigated in patients with SLE. In this study, we sought to investigate the diagnostic implications of LASI in assessing atrial dysfunction and LVDD in patients with SLE.

Methods

Study population

A total of 145 patients with SLE admitted to Shenzhen People’s Hospital between October 2023 and October 2024 were consecutively enrolled, based on the diagnostic criteria published by the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics in 2012.10 Patients with valvular heart disease, diabetes, cardiomyopathy, coronary heart disease, hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism, or congenital heart disease were excluded. Patients with poor image quality were also excluded. Patients with non-SLE-related hypertension were also excluded (online supplemental Figure 1). The patients were subdivided into two groups: lupus nephritis (LNSLE) and non-lupus nephritis (non-LNSLE), according to American College of Rheumatology criteria (persistent proteinuria >0.5 g/day or greater than 3+ by dipstick and/or cellular casts, including red cell, haemoglobin, granular, tubular or mixed).10 The LV diastolic function was graded according to the 2016 American Society of Echocardiography (ASE)/European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) guidelines11: diastolic dysfunction (n=17), uncertain diastolic function (n=13) and normal diastolic function (n=115). 57 healthy age- and sex-matched volunteers formed the control group for comparison. For the control group, all the subjects have normal diastolic function according to the diagnostic criteria outlined in the 2016 ASE/EACVI guidelines.11

Echocardiography

Echocardiographic studies were performed with a commercially available system (Vivid E95 M5Sc 1.4–4.6 MHz transducer, GE Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway). Patients were scanned in the left decubitus position while breathing calmly and connected to a synchronous ECG, and frame rates were set at 51–70 frames/s. Cine loops of the LA were acquired by zooming the LA in both the four-chamber and two-chamber views, using a high frame rate (70–90 frames/s) to accurately capture the rapid motion of the LA walls throughout the cardiac cycle.

Echocardiographic parameters were measured following the 2015 ASE and EACVI recommendations for chamber quantification.12 LV end-diastolic anteroposterior diameter (LVEDD), LV end-diastolic posterior wall thickness (LVPWd), interventricular end-diastolic septal thickness (IVSd), LA anteroposterior diameter (LAD) were measured on the parasternal long-axis view. LV ejection fraction (LVEF) and LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) were calculated using a modified biplane Simpson’s method. E and A peaks of the mitral flow velocity were measured using a pulse Doppler. Septal and lateral mitral annular velocities (Sep. e′ and Lat. e′) from the apical four-chamber view were measured with tissue Doppler imaging. The E peak to e′ ratios were calculated and their average value (E/e′ average) was recorded. Left atrial maximum area (LAarea) was measured in apical four-chamber. Image acquisition and echocardiographic parameter measurements were performed by an experienced echocardiographer (XZ) with over 10 years of experience. LV diastolic function was then evaluated based on the criteria outlined in the 2016 ASE/EACVI algorithms.11

Images were then imported into EchoPAC software (V.204, GE Healthcare, Vingmed Ultrasound, Horten, Norway) for LA volumetric and strain analysis. The following analyses were performed by two trained and experienced echocardiographers (YP and GP) who were blinded to the patients’ medical histories, group assignments and existing risk factors.

The LA volumetric analysis

LA volumes at different phases were calculated using the biplane area-length method. The LA endocardial border was manually traced to obtain the LA maximum volume (LAVmax) at end-systole (the frame just before the mitral valve opens, T wave endpoint), which can be further transformed into LA maximum volume index (LAVImax) corrected by body surface index, the LA minimal volume (LAVmin) at the peak of the R wave (end-diastole phase) and the LA precontraction volume (LAVpre) at the beginning of the P wave. For the measurement, three LA volumetric indicators were derived13:

LA passive ejection fraction (LAPEF)=(LAVmax–LAVpre)/LAVmax×100%.

LA passive ejection fraction (LAPEF)=(LAVmax–LAVpre)/LAVmax×100%.

LA active ejection fraction (LAAEF)=(LAVpre–LAVmin)/LAV pre×100%

The LA strain analysis

Apical four-chamber and two-chamber view cine loops of LA were selected. The R wave of the ECG was used as the reference point of the cardiac cycle. After manually contouring the LA endocardium, the software generated a region of interest (ROI) with an adjustable thickness. The operator adjusted the thickness to ensure that the entire myocardium was encompassed. Following the initiation of strain analysis, the software tracked the myocardial motion within the ROI, thereby generating the LA strain curve (online supplemental Figure 2). The following strains were recorded: left atrial strain during left ventricular systole (LA reservoir strain (LASr)), left atrial strain during early left ventricular diastole (LA conduit strain (LAScd)) and left atrial strain during late left ventricular diastole (LA contractile strain (LASc)).14

Intraobserver and interobserver agreement analysis

20 participants were randomly selected. Two echocardiographers (YP and GP) performed the LA strain analysis and calculated LASI independently. One of the echocardiographers (YP) performed the measurements twice with a day interval, blinded to the patients’ information and medical histories.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD or median (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as a number (%). The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to confirm normal distribution. For two-group comparisons, the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for continuous variables, whereas the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. For multiple-group comparisons, analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis H test was used, followed by post hoc comparisons using Fisher’s least significant difference test or Dunnett’s t-test, respectively. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients. Univariate and multivariate linear regression (stepwise) analysis was performed to identify factors associated with LASI. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was drawn to determine the performance of LASI in diagnosing LVDD. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.25.0 (IBM Corp, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Inter-rater correlation coefficients and Bland-Altman plots were used to examine the intraobserver and interobserver agreement for LA strain and LASI measurements. A two-tailed p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sankey diagrams were constructed using Sankey MATIC (https://sankeymatic.com) to visualise the reclassification of patients in identifying LVDD.

Results

As shown in table 1 and online supplemental Tables 1 and 2, compared with the control group, patients with SLE had significantly higher body mass index (BMI), diastolic pressure and E/e′ average, IVSd and lower A velocity, E/A ratio, Sep. e′ and Lat. e′. Among patients with SLE, the LNSLE group had significantly larger left ventricle size, higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure, pulmonary systolic pressure, and SLE Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI), higher levels of creatinine, uric acid, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), urea nitrogen and 24-hour proteinuria, and lower A velocity, E/A ratio, Sep. e′ and Lat. e′ compared with the non-LNSLE group. No significant differences were found with respect to age or sex.

Correlations among LASI, clinical and echocardiographic parameters

Online supplemental Figure 3 and Table 4 showed that LASI was positively correlated with age (r=0.376, p<0.05), BMI (r=0.218, p<0.05), systolic pressure (r=0.279, p<0.05), IVSd (r=0.363, p<0.05), E/e′ average (r=0.843, p<0.05), creatinine (r=0.287, p<0.05), urea nitrogen (r=0.266,p<0.05) and tricuspid regurgitation (TR) (r=0.231, p<0.05). In contrast, the LASI was negatively correlated with LATEF (r=−0.360, p<0.05), LAPEF (r=−0.395, p<0.05), LASr (r=−0.719, p<0.05), LAScd (r=−0.682, p<0.05), LASc (r=−0.249, p<0.05) and GFR (r=−0.476, p<0.05).

Online supplemental Figure 5 lists univariate linear regression analyses to select the factors potentially related to LASI (variables with p<0.01). A multivariable-adjusted model was constructed using the selected variables stepwise, and the result showed that age (β=0.290), IVSd (β=0.272), LAVmax (β=0.245) and SLEDAI (β=0.206) were independent determinants of LASI.

Performance of LASI in diagnosing LVDD

In patients with SLE, conventional echocardiographic parameters demonstrated significant changes in those with LVDD, with an increase in IVSd, LVEDV, E/e′ average and a decrease in LVEF, A velocity, E/A ratio, Sep.e′ and Lat.e′, compared with patients with normal diastolic function. However, compared with patients with uncertain diastolic function, these changes were not statistically significant in patients with LVDD (online supplemental Table 6).

Among LA-related parameters, there were significant increases in LAD, LAarea and LASI and decreases in LATEF, LAPEF, LASs and LAScd in patients with LVDD compared with those with normal diastolic function. However, when comparing with those with uncertain diastolic function. These changes were only statistically significant for LASI, LATEF and LASr (online supplemental Figure 4 and Table 7).

To determine the performance of LASI in diagnosing LVDD in patients with SLE, an ROC curve was drawn, with the 2016 ASE/EACVI algorithms11 as the standard reference. Patients who fell into the zone of ‘indeterminate diastolic function’ were excluded from the ROC analysis. As shown in figure 1 and online supplemental Table 8, the LASI was superior to all conventional echocardiographic measures of diagnosing LVDD (area under the curve (AUC) 0.919, p<0.0001), compared with E/e′ average (0.822), LATEF (0.721), LASr (0.877) and LAScd (0.906). Additionally, LASI demonstrated the highest discriminatory ability for LVDD with a cut-off of 0.31, providing 80.5% sensitivity and 94.1% specificity, which is superior to LASr and LAScd (AUC 0.877 and 0.906, respectively, both p<0.0001).

Interobserver and intraobserver agreement

Intraobserver and interobserver agreement for each LA strain and LASI measurement showed good repeatability (online supplemental Figures 5 and 6). The intraobserver ICC values for LASI, LASr, LAScd and LASc were 0.979, 0.967, 0.964 and 0.942, respectively, and the interobserver ICC values were 0.975, 0.950, 0.958 and 0.919, respectively.

Discussion

This is the first study to use LASI for evaluating LA function in patients with SLE. The main findings are as follows: (1) LASI was significantly elevated in patients with SLE, with a more pronounced increase in those with LN; (2) LASI was significantly associated with age, septal thickness, LA dilatation and SLEDAI; (3) LA strains and LASI were significantly reduced in patients with uncertain diastolic function, compared to those with normal diastolic function, demonstrating superior performance in diagnosing LVDD than other conventional echocardiographic parameters. This indicated that LASI could be an effective indicator for detecting potential LVDD (figure 3).

LA structural and functional alterations in patients with SLE

Cardiovascular disease is a leading cause of mortality in patients with SLE.15 Early detection and intervention in high-risk individuals can greatly improve patient outcomes. The LA function assessment, including pump, reservoir and conduit functions, provides essential information for clinical practice. Previous studies have shown LA dilatation and LVDD in patients with SLE.16 Similarly, our study found that LA size-related parameters, including LAD, LAarea, LAVmax, LAVpre and LAVmin, were significantly increased in patients with SLE, especially pronounced in those with concomitant LN. These findings suggested that LA remodelling occurred during the course of disease progression. Prolonged inflammation of SLE may induce LA fibrosis and scarring, resulting in elevated LA filling pressure and, consequently, LA dilatation and remodelling.

LA strains, assessed by STE, demonstrated superior performance in assessing LA functions compared with other indicators such as LATEF, LAAPEF and LAAEF, which were more susceptible to various influencing factors.4 It has found that LA functions have changed before structural alterations.4 In our study, the reservoir and conduit strains were significantly reduced in patients with SLE, particularly in the LNSLE group, while the pump function was preserved. These phenomena could be explained as follows. First, the increased LV end-diastolic volume in patients with SLE elevates LV filling pressure, causing blood to accumulate in the LA. This volume overload results in LA dilatation and a reduction in reservoir function. Second, long-term SLE causes damage to myocardial cells, making wall stiffer and harder to relax.17 18 This reduces LV’s ability to aspirate blood from the atrium effectively, resulting in a reduction in LA conduit function. Third, most patients enrolled in our study were at an early stage of LVDD, during which the LA pump function may increase as a compensatory response to increased afterload. This compensatory mechanism may explain why LA contractile function was preserved.

Feasibility of LASI in evaluating LA function in patients with SLE

Katbeh et al19 demonstrated that LASr served as a sensitive marker for detecting heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), but Inoue et al20 argued that the value of LASr in detecting LV filling pressure in patients with HFpEF is limited compared with LASI, which is calculated using LASr and E/e′.21 In addition to being the indicator of early-stage target organ damage of hypertension, LASI also served as a predictor for exercise intolerance and life quality in elderly obese patients with HFpEF. However, the application of LASI in patients with SLE is limited. Our study showed that LASI was significantly increased in patients with SLE, with an even more pronounced increase in those complicated with LN. This result may be attributed to atrial myocyte stretching and fibrosis induced by elevated LA pressure. The abnormal immune status in patients with SLE decreased LV compliance, thereby increasing LV filling pressure and leading to compensatory LA contraction to overcome the elevated LA afterload. The resultant increased LA pressure caused the atrial myocytes, which were thin and pressure-sensitive, to stretch and, eventually, become fibrotic, which contributed to the LA remodelling and stiffness. Additionally, in patients with LNSLE, chronic kidney disease affected chamber size, wall thickness, LV mass and function through metabolite accumulation, electrolyte disturbances and increased preload and afterload, leading to further structural and functional alterations.22 Moreover, nephrotoxicity and long-term pressure and volume overloads caused by LN may trigger myocardial alterations. Metabolic disturbances result in toxic accumulation in myocytes, exacerbating myocardial ischaemia, hypoxia and eventually, fibrosis,23 which is characterised by the significantly elevated LASI.

Correlation analyses showed that LASI was correlated with age, blood pressure, IVSd and GFR. Multivariate regression demonstrated that age, septal thickness, LA size and SLEDAI were independent determinants of LASI. With ageing and the increase in blood pressure, LV compliance decreases, and LA pressure, which is directly influenced by LV pressure, increases simultaneously. The prolonged elevation of LA pressure makes the LA wall thickened, overstretched and eventually dilated. LA dilatation is often accompanied by myocardial fibrosis, which increases LA stiffness, as characterised by elevated LASI, further exacerbating LA functions.24

Considering SLE as a disease with a chronic course and flares, the SLEDAI is recommended as a comprehensive scoring system to evaluate the disease activity. Previous studies have confirmed that SLEDAI correlated well with SLE-induced cardiac injuries, with a higher score indicating severe cardiac involvement.25 During SLE flares, cardiac inflammation infiltration, fibrin degeneration and interfascicular connective tissue swelling become exacerbated,26 thus increasing LA stiffness. Therefore, the prevention of flares and adequate control of disease activity have been recognised as effective strategies to protect LA functions.

The value of LASI in diagnosing LVDD

LVDD is an independent predictor for all-cause mortality in the general population, even in its early stage,27 and it serves as essential evidence for diagnosing HFpEF.28 29 Therefore, accurate evaluation of LVDD plays an important role in clinical practice. Cardiac catheterisation is the gold standard for assessing LV diastolic function and filling pressure, but, as an invasive procedure, it is impractical in routine clinical practice. Given the complexity of diastole itself and its confounding factors, no single echocardiographic indicator could diagnose LVDD in isolation.30 The 2016 ASE/EACVI guidelines11 have recommended evaluating diastolic function using multiple indicators (E/e′ average >14, Sep. e′ <7 cm/s or Lat. e′ <10 cm/s, TR velocity >2.8 m/s, LAVI >34 mL/m²), including Doppler-derived variables (E/e′ average >14, Sep. e′ <7 cm/s or Lat. e′ <10 cm/s, TR velocity >2.8 m/s), which provides instantaneous information on LV diastolic function but is load dependent, as well as LA size indicators (LAVI >34 mL/m²), which offers insights into the severity and chronicity of LVDD. However, the LA size indicators can be confounded by other aetiologies such as atrial myopathies,31 mitral valve disease, atrial fibrillation, bradycardia or high output status.

It has been demonstrated that LA strains were abnormal up to 10 years before LA structural alteration,32 indicating that LA functional measurement was superior to the structural assessment in diagnosing LVDD. LA strains served as a sensitive indicator for detecting early-stage LVDD, especially in those with uncertain diastolic function. Since the reservoir function declines linearly with the progression of LVDD, continuous monitoring of LASr is included in the routine assessment of patients with uncertain diastolic function.30 33 However, LA strains were easily affected by load, patients’ physical status and life quality. Instead, LASI, which combines LA strains with atrial filling pressure, exhibits a more reliable and sensitive performance compared with other LA functional indicators.9 Our study showed similar results that LASI was more effective than E/e′ average, LASr and LAScd alone in diagnosing LVDD, with an AUC of 0.919. Moreover, the latest 2025 ASE guideline34 included LASr in the diagnostic algorithm, thereby acknowledging the added value of combining LASr with other parameters, such as E/e′, in assessing LV diastolic function.

The implementation of stricter diagnostic criteria proposed in the 2016 guideline11 has led to a decrease in the diagnosis of LVDD and a corresponding rise in cases with uncertain LV diastolic function. For patients with uncertain diastolic function, their B-type natriuretic peptide level, cardiovascular event rates and incidence of heart failure were intermediate between those with LVDD and those with normal diastolic function.35 For patients at high risk of LVDD, there may be an underlying diastolic dysfunction, which is considered an early stage of LVDD, even in the absence of more than two positive indicators.33 36 Sanchis37 defined patients with uncertain LV diastolic function as medium-risk for LVDD. Our study revealed that LASI and LA strains were significantly decreased in patients with uncertain LV diastolic function compared with those with normal diastolic function. These findings indicated that LASI could be an effective marker for detecting patients at risk of LVDD. Therefore, when a patient has exactly two positive indicators, classified as uncertain LV diastolic function, further evaluation of LA strains and LASI can provide more accurate information on LV diastolic function. It would be insightful to use LASI to measure early-stage SLE, including its preclinical phase, and explore its potential as a valuable tool for predicting cardiac issues and enhancing long-term patient outcomes.

It should be noted that, despite its accuracy in identifying LVDD, the clinical applicability of LASI is limited by the absence of an established, guideline-recommended normal range. A previous study38 39 proposed a normal range of LASI, which aligns with the range observed in the control group of our study. However, a large-scale study is necessary to establish a more objective and generalised normal range and cut-off values of LASI, thereby providing reliable references for management decision-making.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the 2016 ASE/EACVI algorithms11 do not serve as an independent reference standard for assessing the accuracy of LASI, since E/e′ is incorporated into both the 2016 ASE/EACVI algorithms and the calculation of LASI. Although a blinded assessment strategy was implemented, this overlap may lead to an overestimation of LASI’s accuracy, as potential inaccuracies in Doppler variables, originating from various physiological and haemodynamic influences, were ignored. Second, of the 145 patients with SLE, only 30 had LVDD or uncertain diastolic function. Therefore, further study is required to determine whether the cut-off threshold derived from the current data effectively discriminates LVDD from normal in other cohorts. Third, this is a retrospective single-centre study with a limited sample size, and the optimal threshold of LASI was derived from the same cohort. The absence of follow-up outcome data limits further investigation into its utility in guiding clinical management. The prognostic significance of LASI warrants validation through large-scale, prospective follow-up studies, which will be the focus of our further research.

Conclusions

LASI reflects changes in left atrial function and shows promise as an exploratory early detection marker for LVDD in patients with SLE. However, LASI is currently still in its exploratory stage. Its clinical utility requires validation through larger, multicentre prospective studies.
