Depressive symptoms among migrant Chinese grandparents and intergenerational family conflict: a moderated mediation model
Authors: Huili Su, Yujin Chao, Zhiqing Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAmid China’s rapid urbanization and demographic aging, the population of migrant grandparents who relocate to cities to provide intergenerational care has been steadily expanding. This group is particularly vulnerable to depressive symptoms due to the accumulation of multiple stressors, and they face an elevated risk of intergenerational family conflict. This study aims to examine the relationship between depression among migrant grandparents and intergenerational conflict within families, with a particular focus on the mediating role of hostile attribution bias and the moderating role of collective family efficacy.MethodsUsing the Short Form of the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), the Collective Family Efficacy Questionnaire, the Hostile Attribution Bias Scale, and the Multidimensional Intergenerational Relationship Quality Scale, a questionnaire survey was conducted among 432 representative migrant grandparents in Zhengzhou, China, between March and May 2023. Data analysis included descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and moderated mediation modeling.Results(1) Depressive symptoms were found to be positively and significantly associated with intergenerational family conflict (β = 0.336, 95%CI[0.253,0.418]). Hostile attribution bias demonstrated a significant mediating effect in the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict (β = 0.175, 95%CI[0.097,0.257]). (2) Furthermore, collective family efficacy moderated the mediating role of hostile attribution bias. When collective family efficacy was at a low level, the mediating pathway of hostile attribution bias was significant (β = 0.486, 95%CI[0.374,0.597]); when collective family efficacy was at a high level, the mediating pathway was no longer significant (β = 0.058, 95%CI[-0.063,0.179]).ConclusionWithin the co-residential, multi-generational caregiving arrangements involving migrant grandparents in China, hostile attribution bias mediates the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict, while collective family efficacy moderates the mediating pathway of hostile attribution bias on intergenerational family conflict. The findings provide a theoretical basis for designing targeted preventive interventions. Future interventions are recommended to focus on reducing hostile attribution bias and enhancing collective family efficacy to mitigate the negative relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict, thereby promoting the intergenerational well-being of migrant grandparents.

Introduction

According to the Seventh National Population Census conducted by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, the proportion of the population aged 60 and above has increased by 5.44% compared to the Sixth Census, while the proportion of the urban population has risen by 14.21%. Against the dual backdrop of rapid population aging and urbanization, the population of migrant grandparents who relocate from their original place of residence to their adult children’s cities to assist in caring for their grandchildren has been continuously expanding [1]. These grandparents play a vital role in supporting dual-income urban families [2]. However, they also face multiple stressors, such as the disrupted social networks [3, 4], intensive childcare burdens [5–7], and cultural adaptation challenges [8–10], which increase their susceptibility to depressive symptoms [11]. Some studies further suggest that migrant families are more likely to experience intergenerational conflict [12–14]. Particularly, the challenges of intergenerational family conflict faced by migrant grandparents warrant closer scholarly attention [15]. Although previous studies have examined intergenerational family conflict and depressive symptoms among migrant groups [16–19], the reverse pathway remains underexplored. In particular, limited attention has been given to the potential mediating role of hostile attribution bias in the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict, and how this process might be moderated by protective family factors. Drawing on the Integrated Model of Emotion Processes and Cognition in Social Information Processing (IEPC-SIP) and Social Cognitive Theory, the study examines the mediating role of hostile attribution bias and the protective function of collective family efficacy in the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents. In doing so, it seeks to address key obstacles within the ageing process of migrant grandparents and to promote the psychological well-being of multigenerational co-resident households.

Depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict

The association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict manifests differently across various stages of the life course. Some studies suggest that intergenerational family conflict constitutes a significant source of depressive symptoms, particularly among children and adolescents [20–23]. In contrast, depressive symptoms among adult parents are more strongly influenced by external life stressors, with intergenerational family conflict playing a relatively minor role [24, 25]. Among older adults, research has identified loneliness as a mediator in the relationship between intergenerational family conflict and depressive symptoms [26–28]. However, the direct effect of intergenerational family conflict on depressive symptoms in older parents remains inconclusive. Some studies have reported only weak associations between the two variables [29, 30]. A review by Sharifi and colleague [31] suggests that intergenerational family conflict may be more closely related to older adults’ experiences of loneliness. These differential patterns may reflect developmental and relational dynamics: while adolescents are often unable to escape intergenerational family conflict due to their dependency on parents, older adults can actively reduce contact to avoid such conflict. This confers distinct psychological significance and may account for the varying impact on depressive symptoms.

Furthermore, the relationship between postpartum depression among adult women and intergenerational family conflict is particularly complex. Most studies suggest that, under the context of grandparental childcare support, intergenerational family conflict is not significantly associated with postpartum depressive symptoms. However, over time, excessive grandparental involvement or intergenerational disputes associated with co-residence can increase the risk of depressive symptoms [32, 33]. Furthermore, research on intergenerational co-parenting has found that depressive symptoms in grandparents are more likely to be transferred to parents, thereby undermining intergenerational relationship quality [34]. As previously noted, depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents are the product of cumulative disadvantages and may constitute a key source of intergenerational family conflict. The co-parenting framework posits that caregivers’ depressive symptoms may, through specific psychological pathways, trigger family conflict, creating a dynamic bidirectional reinforcement cycle [35]. Moreover, research has found that in close relationships, depressive symptoms are more predictive of relationship dysfunction than the reverse [36]. Nevertheless, existing research has yet to fully elucidate the socio-psychosocial mechanisms underlying the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict among older adults engaged in co-residential co-parenting.

The Integrated Model of Emotion Processes and Cognition in Social Information Processing (IEPC-SIP) emphasizes the role of emotional context as a critical source of information that influences the processing of social information [37–39]. Depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents constitute an important affective backdrop, which may be closely linked to a hostile attribution bias in the processing of social information during intergenerational interactions, thereby precipitating intergenerational family conflict. Within the constraints of Chinese familial ethics, such conflict rarely manifests as physical violence; instead, it typically takes the form of blame, verbal denigration, control and enforced responsibilities, or covert “silent” aggression [40, 41]. Previous research also indicated that parental depressive symptoms constitute a causal mechanism underpinning the intergenerational continuity of family conflict [42]. Accordingly, depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents may be positively associated with both the frequency and intensity of intergenerational family conflict. In light of this, the present study proposes the following research hypothesis:

H1: Depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents are positively associated with intergenerational family conflict.

The mediating role of hostile attribution bias

Hostile Attribution Bias (HAB) refers to the tendency of individuals to interpret others’ intentions as hostile when social cues are ambiguous or lack clear intent [43, 44]. Individuals with depression are more likely to exhibit HAB than their non-depressed counterparts, and depression has been shown to be associated with HAB independently of trait anger [45, 46]. Although HAB has not yet been systematically conceptualized as a central mechanism in cognitive models of depression, it represents a form of negative interpretive bias consistent with the cognitive distortions’ characteristic of depressive individuals [47–49]. Empirical studies have shown that in family interaction contexts, parents’ hostile attributions toward their children constitute a key factor in the emergence of intergenerational family conflict [50, 51]. In the specific context of migrant grandparents co-residing with their adult children, prior research has indicated that even in generally positive in-law relationships, minor conflicts are difficult to avoid [52]. However, differences in lifestyle or parenting beliefs do not inevitably lead to relational conflict. According to the IEPC-SIP framework, when migrant grandparents are in a depressive emotional state, they are more inclined to misinterpret neutral or ambiguous intergenerational differences (e.g., everyday speech or behaviors) as reflecting hostility or rejection from their adult children. Such hostile attribution bias may contribute to defensive or confrontational responses (e.g., silence, blame, or quarrelling), which are often associated with increased intergenerational family conflict. On the basis of this theoretical and empirical rationale, the present study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2: Hostile Attribution Bias significantly mediates the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents.

The moderating role of collective family efficacy

Social cognitive theory posits that efficacy beliefs are a core foundation of human agency, and in interdependent contexts, success depends critically on perceived collective efficacy within the group [53]. Within family systems, this concept manifests as collective family efficacy, defined as family members’ shared belief in their capacity to collaboratively achieve common goals, which stems from individuals’ confidence in fulfilling their familial roles [54]. High collective family efficacy represents a shared psychological resource that motivates family members to respond to stress and challenges through proactive and cooperative behaviors. However, the environment that migrant grandparents find themselves in often erodes this resource. Their life focus shifting toward their children’s household may alter intergenerational power dynamics and diminish their sense of authority. Simultaneously, intensive childcare and household responsibilities may exceed their capacity, while adult children, constrained by work commitments, may be unable to provide sufficient emotional support [55, 56]. Collectively, these factors may inhibit the formation and maintenance of collective family efficacy. The Convoy Model of Social Relations suggests that efficacy in intergenerational communication can alleviate depressive symptoms [57], and family-based interventions can concurrently improve family relations and depressive outcomes among older migrants [58]. Thus, collective family efficacy may directly moderate the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. More importantly, the protective function of collective family efficacy may operate through cognitive mechanisms as positive family interactions are a crucial source of depression mitigation [59], a process that may be realized through the regulation of hostile attribution bias [60, 61]. More specifically, when collective family efficacy is perceived as high, supportive interactions and shared problem-solving experiences can provide reality testing and positive feedback. These processes help individuals correct hostile attributions toward others, the negative association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. Previous research has emphasized the importance of incorporating collective efficacy into the SIP model of adolescent aggression [62]. Building on this perspective, the present study extends the perspective to migrant grandparents in the context of intergenerational co-residence. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H3: Collective family efficacy directly moderates the strength of the association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict, such that higher levels of collective family efficacy weaken the positive relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. H4: Collective family efficacy moderates the relationship between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias, such that higher levels of collective family efficacy weaken the positive association between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias.

Method

Data and sample

In this study, the term migrant grandparents refers to middle-aged and older adults who have relocate from another region to the city where their adult children reside to assist in caring for their grandchildren. Between March to May 2023, the research team conducted a targeted questionnaire survey on the physical and mental health of migrant grandparents in Zhengzhou, Henan Province, China1. Zhengzhou, located in the central plains region, is a key city in the region and is classified as a “new first-tier city”. From the fifth and seventh national population censuses, the population of permanent residents in Zhengzhou increased from 6,656,759 to 12,600,574, representing an 89.29% increase. During the seventh census, households with three or more generations co-residence accounted for 16.50% of all households, slightly above the national average for the same period. As one of China’s eight ancient capitals and a nationally recognized historical and cultural city, Zhengzhou has a strong tradition of family-centered culture and intergenerational co-residence, making it a particularly suitable setting for studying the phenomenon of grandparental migration and childcare support. For the purpose of defining the study population, survey respondents were required to meet four criteria: (1) having relocated from another locality to Zhengzhou, (2) primarily due to the need to care for grandchildren, (3) currently engaged in such care for at least six consecutive months, and (4) currently co-residing with their children and grandchildren. No restrictions were imposed with respect to age, place of origin, or household registration status2.

Given the difficulty of accurately identifying the distribution and boundaries of migrant grandparent populations in urban areas, this study employed a combination of stratified sampling and convenience sampling. For the stratified sampling, six central districts of Zhengzhou were selected based on geographic administrative divisions, including Zhongyuan District, Erqi District, Jinshui District, Economic and Technological Development Zone (ETDZ), High-tech Industrial Development Zone (HIDZ), and Zhengdong New District. From each district, two communities were randomly selected, yielding a total of 12 communities as the final sampling units. Within each community, 40 questionnaires were collected. For the convenience sampling, community staff introduced eligible participants to the research team, and face-to-face recruitment was conducted. Screening questions were used to strictly verify eligibility before obtaining informed consent and administering the questionnaire.

A pilot study revealed that most of migrant grandparents had an education level of junior high school or below and were unable to complete the questionnaire independently. Therefore, trained interviewers read the questionnaire items aloud face-to-face, ensuring comprehension, and recorded participants’ verbal responses. To mitigate the effects of social desirability among elderly participants, anonymity and confidentiality were emphasized. Each questionnaire took approximately 20 min to complete. A total of 480 questionnaires were distributed and returned. After excluding incomplete or patterned responses, 432 valid questionnaires remained.

Although no official statistical data are available on migrant grandparents, previous studies provide useful benchmarks [63–65]. More specifically, previous studies found that women accounted for 63.0%–69.4% of respondents, compared to 68.5% in this study. In terms of education, 85.0%–97.9% of prior samples had a high school degree or below, compared with 92.8% in this study. The average age in prior studies ranged from 63.29 to 67.7 years, with this study’s mean age at 65.38. With respect to self-rated health, 84.5%–94% of prior participants reported being in good or fair health, compared with 87% in this study (combined proportions of “very good,” “fairly good,” and “acceptable”). Taken together, these figures demonstrate that the demographic characteristics of the present sample are highly consistent with those reported in existing surveys (see Table 1).

Measurements

Depressive symptoms assessment

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [66]. The scale comprises 10 items, with items 5 and 8 reverse coded. Participants were asked to rate the frequency of each symptom (e.g., “I felt depressed”) during the past week on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“rarely or less than 1 day”) to 3 (“5–7 days”). Total scores were calculated by summing all item responses, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 30, where higher scores indicate greater severity of depressive symptoms. The CES-D has been widely applied in assessing depressive symptoms among older adults in Chinese community settings and has demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity [67, 68]. Collective family efficacy

This study employed the Collective Family Efficacy Scale (CFE) developed by Caprara et al. [52]. The questionnaire comprises 20 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). This measure assessed collective family efficacy, encompassing family members’ perceived abilities in managing daily tasks, reaching consensus in decision-making and planning, coping with challenges, fostering mutual negotiation, providing emotional support during difficult times, engaging in joint activities, maintaining relaxation despite multiple commitments, and sustaining positive relations with the community. Higher total scores indicated stronger collective family efficacy. Hostile attribution bias

This study employed the Hostile Attribution Bias Scale [70] to assess the level of hostile attribution bias migrant grandparents directed toward their co-residing adult children over the past month. The scale measures five dimensions: perceived friendliness, intentional harm, aggressive attitude, hostile attitude, and perceived prejudice. The “perceived friendliness” dimension is reverse-scored. Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”), with higher scores indicating a greater tendency toward hostile attribution bias. Given the complexity of affinal and consanguineal ties in Chinese multigenerational co-residence, this study separately assessed migrant grandparents’ hostile attribution bias (HAB) toward sons/daughters-in-law and daughters/sons-in-law. The summed scores for sons and daughters-in-law or daughters and sons-in-law were then calculated to represent the grandparents’ overall HAB toward their co-residing adult children. This approach aligns more closely with the realities of intergenerational family relations in the Chinese cultural context. Intergenerational family conflict

This study used the Intergenerational Multidimensional Relationship Quality Scale [71] to assess intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents, specifically drawing on the intergenerational family conflict dimension. The original scale contains 13 items; however, for this research, items 6, 7, and 8 were extracted to measure the extent of conflicts between migrant grandparents and their co-residing sons/daughters-in-law or daughters/sons-in-law. The total score derived from the responses regarding both sons and daughters (and their spouses) was used to represent the level of intergenerational family conflict with co-residing adult children. The scale adopts a five-point Likert scoring system, ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“always”), with higher total scores indicating greater levels of intergenerational family conflict. Control variables

Analytical strategies

This study employed AMOS 29.0 to conduct confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using the maximum likelihood estimation method. This study employed SPSS 26.0 to perform descriptive statistics, reliability analysis, correlation analysis, mediation analysis, and moderated mediation analysis. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias. The results indicated that 11 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1, accounting for a cumulative variance of 60.11%. The first factor accounted for 23.21% of the total variance, which is below the 40% threshold [72], suggesting that common method bias was not a serious concern. Subsequently, the variables of depressive symptoms, collective family efficacy, hostile attribution bias, intergenerational family conflict, and self-reported physical health were standardized. Mediation and moderated mediation effects were then examined using the PROCESS 3.3 macro in SPSS, with Model 4 employed to test the mediating role of hostile attribution bias and Model 8 to evaluate the moderated mediation effect of collective family efficacy. In both analyses, bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples were used to estimate 95% confidence intervals.

Additionally, age, duration of migration, years of education, number of children, and self-rated physical health were significantly associated with depressive symptoms, collective family efficacy, hostile attribution bias, and intergenerational family conflict. Testing for mediation

Based on the theoretical hypotheses, standardized scores of intergenerational family conflict, depressive symptoms, and hostile attribution bias were treated as the dependent variable, independent variable, and mediator, respectively. Duration of migration, age, number of children, educational attainment, and standardized self-rated physical health were included as control variables. A simple mediation model was tested using Model 4 of the PROCESS 3.3 macro in SPSS, with results presented in Table 3. Bootstrap analysis indicated that, after controlling for covariates, depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents were significantly positively associated with intergenerational family conflict (β = 0.511, 95%CI[0.424, 0.598]), supporting H1. When both depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias were included into the model, the association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict remained significant (β = 0.336, 95%CI[0.253, 0.418]), and hostile attribution bias significantly mediated this relationship (β = 0.175, 95%CI[0.097, 0.257]), accounting for 34.25% of the total effect, thus supporting H2.

Testing for moderated mediation

A moderated mediation analysis was conducted using Model 8 of the PROCESS macro (see Table 4). Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated no concerns, with VIF values ranging from 1.02 to 1.37 and tolerance values from 0.75 to 0.98. In Eq. 2, the interaction between depressive symptoms and collective family efficacy was not significantly associated with intergenerational family conflict (β = −0.067, 95% CI [−0.1342, 0.0002]), indicating that the direct moderating effect of collective family efficacy on the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict was non-significant, and H3 was therefore not supported. In Eq. 1, the interaction between depressive symptoms and collective family efficacy was significantly negatively associated with hostile attribution bias (β = −0.214, 95% CI [−0.2861, −0.1416]), indicating a significant moderating effect of collective family efficacy on the relationship between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias, supporting H4. Overall, the moderated mediation model was supported, with collective family efficacy moderating the first stage of the mediating pathway via hostile attribution bias.

Further analyses examined the conditional indirect effects of depressive symptoms at different levels of collective family efficacy (see Table 5). The conditional indirect effect through hostile attribution bias was also significant: in the low collective family efficacy group (M − 1 SD), hostile attribution bias significantly mediated the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict (β = 0.486, 95%CI[0.374,0.597]), whereas in the high collective family efficacy group (M + 1 SD), this mediating effect was not significant (β = 0.058, 95%CI[−0.063,0.179]).

To further probe the moderating role of collective family efficacy, simple slope analyses were conducted for high and low subgroups (M ± 1 SD), and a simple slopes plot was generated (see Fig. 2). Results showed that when collective family efficacy was low (M − 1 SD), the positive association between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias was stronger (simple slope = 0.486, t = 8.534, p < 0.001). In contrast, when collective family efficacy was high (M + 1 SD), the association was no longer significant (simple slope = 0.058, t = 0.864, p = 0.348).

Discussion

Within the broader process of urbanization in China, tensions between family cultural norms and individual aspirations render intergenerational family conflict a typical dilemma for migrant grandparents. It is therefore necessary to investigate the relationship and mechanisms linking depression symptoms and intergenerational family conflict within co-residential, multi-generational families. Guided by the IEPC-SIP model and social cognitive theory, this study investigated the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents, as well as the mechanisms underlying this association. The findings reveal a significant positive association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. Hostile attribution bias was identified as a significant mediator of this relationship. Furthermore, collective family efficacy moderated the association between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias, which operates in relation to intergenerational family conflict. These results provide a robust empirical basis for developing preventive and intervention strategies that target cognitive processing in intergenerational family interactions. Such strategies may effectively reduce the translation of depressive symptoms into intergenerational family conflict, thereby supporting the well-being of migrant grandparents.

Depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict

This study found that depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents were significantly positively associated with intergenerational family conflict, broadly consistent with previous findings [73]. Understanding this relationship requires situating it within the unique high-risk social context of migration. This population faces three structural vulnerabilities: first, migration disrupts preexisting social networks, increasing susceptibility to depressive symptoms; second, the close contact inherent in multigenerational co-residence amplifies differences in lifestyles and habits across generations [74]; third, the transfer of childcare decision-making authority to adult children undermines grandparents’ household authority, potentially exposing them more to negative intergenerational family conflict environments [75–77]. The present study highlights that, within this high-conflict-risk context, depressive symptoms, hostile attribution bias, and intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents are significantly positively interrelated.

The mediating role of hostile attribution bias

The present study demonstrated that hostile attribution bias (HAB) toward co-resident adult children significantly mediated the association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict, indicating that the positive correlation between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict can be partially accounted for by HAB, consistent with prior empirical evidence [78]. Cognitive behavioural theories of depression posit that exposure to stressful life events amplifies negative interpretive biases, thereby increasing the risk of aggression within close interpersonal relationships [79]. Negativity biases are reflected in the IEPC-SIP model as hostile attribution bias [80], which may increase the likelihood of relationship problems in adulthood [81]. Substantial empirical evidence also indicates that individuals experiencing depressive symptoms tend to interpret ambiguous social information negatively [82, 83].

Within the Chinese context of multigenerational co-residence, this mechanism manifests as migrant grandparents interpreting adult children’s routine behaviours, such as work-related unavailability or autonomous decision-making regarding childcare, as acts of neglect or rejection. This interpretation, in turn, increases hostile attribution bias toward their children. The salience of this cognitive bias is amplified under traditional filial norms and shifting intergenerational power structures. Migrant grandparents, who maintain strong ties to conventional hierarchical family values, are particularly sensitive to identity transitions and role imbalances, resulting in a significant positive correlation between elevated HAB and the frequency of intergenerational family conflict. Survey evidence indicates that approximately 70% of intergenerational family conflicts among Chinese migrant grandparents are closely associated with differences in values and caregiving beliefs [84].

These findings substantiate the theoretical propositions of the IEPC-SIP model and cognitive behavioural theories of depression, demonstrating that the negative cognitive biases accompanying depressive symptoms are closely connected to intergenerational family conflict. In particular, the tendency to interpret ambiguous social information as hostile is both theoretically and empirically linked to such conflict [38, 85]. Within the culturally specific context of Chinese families, which emphasize hierarchical order and relational harmony, the triadic relationship among depressive symptoms, HAB, and intergenerational family conflict forms a negative interrelationship. Cognitive biases and intergenerational power relations interact within this interconnected system, contributing to adverse outcomes in family interactions.

The moderating role of collective family efficacy

The results of this study indicate that collective family efficacy does not exert a direct moderating effect on the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. Instead, it significantly moderates the mediating pathway of hostile attribution bias (HAB) in this relationship. This suggests that collective family efficacy may function less as a broad, non-specific emotional buffer and more as a targeted cognitive protective factor. Specifically, higher levels of collective family efficacy do not significantly moderate the direct association between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. Instead, it moderates the relationship between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias, which is indirectly associated with lower levels of intergenerational family conflict. This mechanism aligns closely with the central proposition of social cognitive theory, which posits that collective family efficacy beliefs primarily underlie how individuals interpret and respond to situational demands rather than directly altering behaviour [86, 87]. It also resonates with the family conflict cognition model, which suggests that individuals with higher efficacy are more confident in their ability to resolve disputes and are more inclined to adopt proactive or cooperative strategies [88]. In other words, the role of collective family efficacy may lie in serving as a “cognitive filter,” promoting more benign interpretations of adult children’s words and actions, thereby indirectly reducing intergenerational family conflict rather than exerting a direct moderating effect. The rejection of Hypothesis 3 thus allows this study to delineate more precisely the boundary conditions of collective family efficacy as a mechanism and offers important implications for future interventions. Rather than solely emphasizing “enhancing family harmony,” interventions may more effectively focus on strengthening collective family efficacy to improve cognitive processing patterns, which can mitigate the negative relationship among depressive symptoms, hostile attribution bias, and intergenerational family conflict.

Taken together, this study demonstrates that depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents are significantly and positively associated with intergenerational family conflict. Hostile attribution bias functions as a key mediator of this relationship, and collective family efficacy moderates the first stage of this relationship. These findings provide robust empirical support for both the IEPC-SIP model and social cognitive theory, while also validating their applicability to the culturally specific context of Chinese migrant grandparents living in multigenerational co-residential caregiving arrangements.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations, which highlight promising directions for future research. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference regarding the relationship among depressive symptoms, hostile attribution bias, and intergenerational family conflict. Longitudinal or experimental designs are necessary to establish causality. Second, the reliance on self-report measures may lead to underreporting of negative family experiences due to cultural norms of emotional moderation in collectivist contexts [89]. Future studies could address this by employing multi-method assessments, such as behavioural observations, family diaries, physiological indicators. Third, the data were collected exclusively from grandparents, thereby neglecting the dyadic nature of conflict. Future research should incorporate data from both generations and apply analytical models such as the actor–partner interdependence model. Fourth, the generalizability of our model, which is specific to migrant grandparents, requires further validation through the inclusion of non-migrant comparison groups. Finally, future investigations should examine the impact of diverse marital arrangements on family functioning within this population.

Conclusion

Despite certain limitations that warrant further investigation, this study holds significant value in understanding the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents living in multigenerational co-residence and caregiving households in China. The findings reveal the relationship between depressive symptoms and intergenerational family conflict. More specifically, depressive symptoms among migrant grandparents were significantly and positively associated with intergenerational family conflict, with hostile attribution bias serving as a mediating pathway. Furthermore, collective family efficacy moderated the association between depressive symptoms and hostile attribution bias, thereby indirectly influencing intergenerational family conflict. Grounded in the IEPC-SIP and Social Cognitive Theory, this study provides a solid theoretical foundation for developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies for intergenerational family conflict in co-resident families.

More significantly, this study argues that intergenerational family conflict among migrant grandparents should not be regarded as a purely private family matter, but rather as a systemic risk emerging from China’s urbanization and demographic aging processes. The research therefore carries important practical implications. Firstly, mental health considerations should be systematically integrated into urbanization and aging policy. Municipal government could incorporate mental health risk assessments for migrant grandparents within the basic public health services delivered by community health centres. This might be achieved by supplementing the annual health check-ups offered to all senior residents with brief depression screenings and evaluations of family relationship quality. Secondly, targeted cognitive behavioural interventions should be implemented at both community and clinical level. Such interventions could help grandparents identify and challenge hostile attributions of ambiguous family interactions, practice generating alternative and more benign interpretations, and develop constructive communication skills to clarify intentions and resolve misunderstandings. Finally, collective family efficacy should be activated and strengthened through structured family-level initiatives. Community social workers could facilitate collective family efficacy building programs that guide multi-generational families. For instance, convening regular family meetings to openly discuss challenges, facilitating collaborative problem-solving, and co-creating family agreements that acknowledge grandparents’ contributions. By implementing these targeted measures at multiple levels, the mental health risks associated with grandparental migration will be effectively mitigated, transforming systemic challenges into opportunities for fostering resilient and harmonious multi-generational families.
