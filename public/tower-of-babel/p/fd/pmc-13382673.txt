Residual-Gas-Induced Oxygen Accumulation as an Analytical Bias in XPS of Sputter-Treated Metal Surfaces
Authors: Jingyun Chen, Bo Feng, Yunshan Shang, Xingye Huang, Congyun Liu, Linge Ma, Zhenglong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Trace oxygen-containing residual gases in X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) chambers can adsorb on or react with sputter-treated metal surfaces, introducing uncertainty into oxygen-related XPS analysis. However, such residual-gas effects remain insufficiently clarified. In this work, ten representative elemental metal films were examined under identical XPS chamber conditions, and the primary comparison focused on seven films showing lower apparent initial oxygen levels after Ar+ sputtering. Time-resolved O 1s measurements revealed a common two-stage behavior, with an initial rapid increase followed by a slower approach to a quasi-steady state. The apparent transition to the slower stage was material-dependent; several metals entered this stage within the early measurement period, whereas Al showed a prolonged rapid accumulation stage of approximately 2000 s. Among the seven films, the early stage O 1s accumulation rates were relatively high for W, Mo, and Mn, intermediate for Al, and lower for Fe, Co, and Ni. Kinetic modeling further indicated two distinct oxygen-accumulation behaviors, with Avrami-type and pseudo-second-order (PSO) models providing the preferred descriptions. Measurable O 1s growth despite nearly unchanged metal core-level spectra indicates that metal-peak stability alone is insufficient to rule out the presence of ultrathin oxygen-containing overlayers. High-resolution O 1s fitting revealed that the accumulated oxygen was mainly present as chemically bound M–O and −OH species rather than only molecularly adsorbed H2O. These findings improve the understanding of residual-gas interactions with sputter-treated metal surfaces and provide practical guidance for optimizing oxygen-related XPS measurement protocols to reduce analytical bias.

Introduction

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) is a key technique for surface analysis. It enables highly sensitive characterization of surface chemical composition and elemental chemical states under vacuum conditions that strongly suppress gas-phase scattering and surface contamination and has been widely used in studies of metal oxidation, coating interfaces, and corrosion. Although vacuum conditions in XPS analysis chambers greatly reduce the collision frequency between gas-phase molecules and the sample surface, trace reactive residual gases in the analysis chamber (e.g., H2O and O2) can still adsorb on or react with metal surfaces. Such processes may cause the acquired spectra to deviate from the intrinsic surface state, thereby reducing the accuracy of both the quantitative and qualitative XPS analyses. However, their influence is often overlooked or underestimated in routine data interpretation.

In XPS sputter-depth profiling of metals and their alloys, it is commonly observed that, after nominal removal of the surface oxide layer by sputtering, the oxygen concentration does not drop to zero but instead remains at a low and relatively stable level over a certain depth, forming an “oxygen plateau” often at a level of a few to ∼14 at. % in many reported studies. − This phenomenon has been reported for many alloy systems and is often attributed to bulk oxidation, dissolved oxygen in solid solution, interfacial reactions, or organic contamination. However, the possible contribution from residual-gas-induced oxygen accumulation is frequently neglected.

In recent years, more rigorous efforts have been made to distinguish intrinsic oxygen from oxygen introduced during the experimental process. For example, abnormal oxygen signals observed during depth profiling of CoCrMo coating interfaces and Au–Al and Cu–In alloy thin films have been attributed to adsorption of residual gases in the vacuum chamber. − Beyss et al., Liu et al., and León et al. further demonstrated that adsorption of chamber gases and/or reoxidation under vacuum can be an important origin of the oxygen plateau. In particular, León et al. showed that an oxygen plateau of the 8–14 atom % formed after sputtering an electrodeposited chromium coating is an analytical artifact caused by spontaneous reoxidation of the highly reactive Cr surface in the presence of trace oxygen/water vapor. These findings indicate that residual-gas adsorption can substantially influence oxygen-related XPS signals. Moreover, the response to residual gases varies markedly among metals: oxygen accumulation on Au and Ag surfaces is limited, whereas metals such as Mg and Al show a strong tendency toward adsorption and oxidation.

Collectively, the oxygen plateau commonly observed in XPS depth profiling may originate either from intrinsic oxygen within the material or from surface oxygen accumulation driven by residual-gas adsorption after sputtering. If the latter contribution is misinterpreted as an intrinsic signal, the analysis of key information, such as interfacial structure, oxide-layer thickness, and elemental chemical states, may be incorrectly evaluated. Recent work by Shallenberger et al. further demonstrated that vacuum exposure can measurably influence oxygen-related XPS signals in reactive materials, highlighting the importance of exposure history in oxygen-related XPS measurements of reactive materials. In this work, ten representative metal films of common alloying elements (Al, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Nb, Mo, and W) were examined under the same analysis-chamber conditions. Using continuous, time-resolved measurements, we monitored the evolution of the O 1s peak area together with the corresponding metal core-level peaks after Ar+ sputtering, enabling analysis of the kinetics and chemical-state evolution of residual-gas-induced oxygen accumulation. Specifically, this study aims to (1) compare oxygen-accumulation behaviors among different sputter-treated metal-film surfaces under the same chamber background conditions, evaluate initial O 1s accumulation rates and the apparent end times of the rapid accumulation stage, and classify their responses to residual gases; (2) combine changes in metal core-level peak line shape, binding energy, and full width at half-maximum (FWHM) with O 1s peak fitting to evaluate the apparent contributions of oxygen-containing components, including oxide-like M–O, hydroxyl/hydroxide-related M–OH/–OH, and adsorbed or bound H2O species; and (3) propose practical XPS measurement procedures and acquisition sequences to minimize residual-gas effects and improve the reliability and comparability of XPS data. These results provide a systematic framework for identifying and minimizing residual-gas-induced analytical bias in oxygen-related XPS measurements of sputter-treated metal surfaces.

Experimental Section

Sample Preparation

The ten selected elemental metals (purity ≥ 99.9%) were deposited on precleaned single-crystal silicon wafers by magnetron sputtering to form uniform metal films with a thickness of ∼200 nm. The coated wafers were then cut into specimens with dimensions of 8 mm × 8 mm × 1 mm for XPS analysis.

Instrumentation and Measurement Conditions

XPS measurements were carried out on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer equipped with a monochromated Al Kα X-ray source (hν = 1486.6 eV; spot size: 650 μm; emission angle, θ = 0°). All measurements were performed using the same XPS system, sputtering conditions, and acquisition sequence under nominally identical chamber background conditions. The analysis chamber pressure was approximately 1.5 × 10–9 mbar before sputtering, increased to approximately 1.0 × 10–8 mbar after Ar+ sputtering, and was approximately 1.2 × 10–8 mbar during the subsequent XPS measurement sequence. These values correspond to total chamber pressure readings recorded during the measurement workflow. The residual-gas background was analyzed using a residual gas analyzer (RGA; Pfeiffer Vacuum PrismaPRO) over a mass-to-charge ratio range of m/z = 1–100.

Before XPS analysis, all metal-film samples were sputter-cleaned with an Ar+ ion gun to reduce surface contamination and native oxide contributions. The sputtering was performed at an ion energy of 2 keV over an etching area of 2 mm × 2 mm, and the same sputtering parameters were used for all metal-film samples.

Measurement Procedure

Time-Resolved O 1s Acquisition (Oxygen-Accumulation Kinetics)

After Ar+ sputtering, the analyzer pass energy was set to 100 eV to improve the acquisition efficiency and signal counting stability. O 1s and C 1s spectra were acquired sequentially in cyclic mode for a total of 70 repeated cycles with an interval of 80 s between consecutive cycles.

High-Resolution Acquisition (Chemical-State Evolution)

After Ar+ sputtering, the analyzer pass energy was set to 30 eV to improve the energy resolution. High-resolution spectra of O 1s and the characteristic core levels of the corresponding metals were collected repeatedly in cyclic scans.

Data Processing

All samples were sufficiently conductive; therefore, no charge correction was applied. The O 1s peak areas were obtained after background subtraction using the Smart method, and peak fitting was performed by using Avantage software.

Results and Discussion

Residual Gas Analysis

To characterize the representative residual-gas background in the present experiments, the gas composition in the analysis chamber was measured using the RGA connected to the XPS system. The dominant residual species were identified as H2, H2O, CO/N2, CO2, and a small amount of O2. Among the oxygen-containing species, H2O showed the highest partial pressure, with a representative value of approximately 9.3 × 10–10 mbar, whereas the O2 partial pressure was approximately 8.9 × 10–11 mbar. This composition is consistent with the typical residual-gas background commonly reported for turbomolecular-pumped XPS analysis chambers. ,− These results confirmed the presence of trace oxygen-containing residual gases in the analysis chamber. In particular, H2O and O2 may serve as potential oxygen sources for adsorption and/or reaction on the postsputtered metal-film surfaces, which could contribute to the time-dependent increase in the O 1s signal discussed below.

Oxygen Accumulation on Sputter-Treated Metal-Film Surfaces

Analysis of Time-Resolved O 1s Spectra

Following the measurement protocol, 71 O 1s spectra were acquired for each metal-film sample. By integrating the O 1s peak area, the time-dependent evolution of the apparent surface oxygen signal was tracked. Minor nonmonotonic variations at a few time points (e.g., local decreases) may arise from counting statistics, minor fluctuations in background subtraction or peak-area integration, or short-term instrumental drift, and do not affect the analysis of the overall oxygen-accumulation trend. For consistency with the exposure estimate and to avoid including the transient Ar-related pressure increase after sputtering, the exposure was estimated using the base pressure before sputtering. At a base pressure of ≤1.5 × 10–9 mbar, the first O 1s acquisition, which lasted approximately 40 s and was defined as the nominal t = 0 s spectrum (t 0), corresponds to an estimated residual-gas exposure of approximately 0.045 L, and each subsequent 80 s interval corresponds to an additional exposure of approximately 0.090 L. This exposure range is comparable to that commonly encountered during XPS sputter-depth profiling, typically ∼0.01–1.0 L. The t 0 O 1s spectra showed measurable residual O 1s signals for all of the metal films. The apparent O atomic percentages at this initial state are summarized in Table S1, ranging from 1.58 atom % for Al to 32.61 atom % for Cr. These values indicate that the sputter-treated surfaces were not oxygen-free. Therefore, the t 0 O level was used as an experimental baseline for evaluating subsequent oxygen accumulation rather than as a direct measure of the bulk oxygen content of the metal films. The initial O 1s signal may include contributions from pre-existing oxygen incorporated in the films, residual oxygen-containing species remaining after Ar+ sputtering, and rapid adsorption/reaction of residual gases during the short interval before or during the first O 1s acquisition. Because Cr, Ti, and Nb showed relatively high apparent initial O levels after sputtering, they were not used for the primary comparison. Their results are provided and discussed separately in Text S2.

Despite the different initial oxygen levels, the O 1s peak area increased to varying degrees for the remaining seven metal films during the measurement sequence (Figure ), indicating continued adsorption or reaction of trace residual gases on the sputter-treated metal surfaces. A common two-stage evolution behavior was observed with an initial rapid increase followed by a slower approach to a quasi-steady state. On the basis of two-segment piecewise linear regression, W, Mo, and Mn showed relatively early transitions from the rapid stage to the slower stage, whereas Co, Fe, and Ni showed later or less distinct transitions. In contrast, Al exhibited a clearly prolonged rapid accumulation stage with the transition occurring at approximately 2000 s. This prolonged response is consistent with previous reports of water-vapor-induced oxidation/hydroxylation of Al, which provide useful surface-chemistry context for the observed O 1s increase. Representative apparent O atomic percentages at selected stages are summarized in Table S2. These results indicate that residual-gas-induced oxygen accumulation relative to the t 0 baseline is material-dependent under the same chamber background and acquisition conditions.

Initial Oxygen Accumulation Rate

To compare the apparent early stage oxygen accumulation rates, the O 1s peak areas during the first five time points (0–320 s) were fitted by linear regression, and the resulting slopes were taken as the initial accumulation rate. During this early period, surface reactive sites were relatively abundant, and the O 1s response was less affected by later-stage site depletion, overlayer growth, or passivation effects; therefore, the slope was used as a semiquantitative indicator of the early stage O 1s response relative to the t 0 baseline. As shown in Figure , the initial slopes varied substantially among the seven selected metal films: W, Mo, and Mn exhibited relatively high accumulation rates (>100 au s–1), Al showed an intermediate response (50–100 au s–1), whereas Fe, Ni, and Co showed relatively low accumulation rates (<50 au s–1). Because the fitted slopes may show modest run-to-run variability due to counting statistics, background subtraction, and short-term instrumental drift, the slope-based grouping is intended as a semiquantitative comparison within the present experimental workflow, rather than as a strict ranking of intrinsic metal reactivity. Although direct comparison with previous studies is limited by differences in surface preparation, chamber background, and acquisition conditions, metal-dependent oxygen accumulation during vacuum exposure has also been reported by Steinberger et al.

Kinetic Analysis of Oxygen Accumulation

To compare the apparent oxygen-accumulation kinetics on different metal-film surfaces, the net O 1s peak-area increment relative to the initial postsputtering spectrum was defined as q(t) = A O1s(t) – A O1s(0), where A O1s(t) is the integrated O 1s peak area at time t. The q(t)-time curves were fitted using four commonly used phenomenological kinetic models: pseudo-first-order (PFO), pseudo-second-order (PSO), Elovich, and Avrami models. − Model performance was evaluated primarily using the Akaike information criterion (AIC), with RMSE and R 2 used as supporting indicators. As shown in Table , Al, Fe, Co, and Ni were better described by the Avrami model. This result suggests that oxygen accumulation on these surfaces may involve the progressive development of oxygen-containing surface species such as nucleation/growth-like evolution or gradual overlayer formation. In contrast, Mn, Mo, and W were better described by the PSO model, indicating that their oxygen accumulation may be more strongly affected by reactive site availability and coverage-dependent surface reactions.

Together with the time-resolved O 1s evolution, the kinetic-fitting results are consistent with a two-stage oxygen-accumulation behavior. The initial rapid increase can be regarded as a site-limited adsorption/reaction stage, during which residual oxygen-containing species rapidly interact with postsputtering metal-film surfaces. As the most reactive sites are progressively occupied and an ultrathin oxygen-containing layer forms, the process gradually enters a slower stage, which may be influenced by reactive-site depletion, surface heterogeneity, and further growth of, or transport through, the surface layer. , The prolonged rapid stage observed for Al may be related to its high oxygen affinity and its ability to sustain oxide/hydroxide formation before effective surface passivation is reached. ,, Overall, the early stage accumulation rate, apparent rapid stage duration, and preferred kinetic description all reflect material-dependent differences under the present chamber background and acquisition conditions.

Evolution of Surface Chemical States on Metal Films

Analysis of Metal Core-Level Spectra

To determine whether the accumulation of oxygen signals was accompanied by changes in the metal chemical states, we monitored the time-dependent evolution of the metal core-level line shape, peak position, and full width at half-maximum (FWHM). The core-level spectra of Fe, Co, Ni, Mo, and W remained essentially unchanged throughout the measurement period, showing stable line shapes and binding energies, with only minor FWHM fluctuations and no discernible temporal trend (ΔFWHM <0.06 eV). This indicates that, within the time scale of the present experiment, the continued increase in O 1s intensity for these films was not accompanied by a resolvable change in the main metal core-level profiles.

In contrast, Al and Mn exhibited more evident chemical-state evolution during the early measurement cycles. For Al 2p, an additional component associated with Al–O/Al–OH species at higher binding energies was already detectable by the third measurement cycle (∼160 s) (Figure a). The Mn 2p spectrum evolved even more rapidly, with distinct line-shape broadening evident by the second measurement cycle (Figure b). These observations indicate that Al and Mn surfaces more readily adsorbed or reacted with residual gases in the chamber background, leading to the formation of surface oxygen-containing species.

Representative early stage metal core-level spectral evolution after Ar+ sputtering: (a) Al 2p spectra acquired at cycles 0, 3, and 6; (b) Mn 2p spectra acquired at cycles 0, 1, and 2.

It should be noted that the apparent stability of metal core-level peaks does not necessarily imply the absence of oxygen-containing species on the surface. Given the typical nanometer-scale information depth of XPS (<10 nm), the metal core-level spectra are dominated by the metallic component. Therefore, even an ultrathin oxygen-containing overlayer can induce a pronounced increase in the O 1s signal, while the corresponding oxidized metal contribution may remain weak or unresolved in the much stronger underlying metallic signal. To estimate the apparent thickness of the additional oxygen-containing overlayer responsible for attenuating the substrate metal signal, we employed the standard exponential attenuation relationship for a planar overlayer: , I/I0=exp[−dapp/(λcos⁡θ)] where I 0 is the integrated peak area of the metal core-level signal measured at the nominal t = 0 s postsputtering state, and I is the integrated peak area of the same metal core-level signal measured after oxygen accumulation, here taken from the quasi-steady-state spectrum. d app is the apparent overlayer thickness, and λ cos θ is the effective attenuation length projected along the photoelectron emission direction. This approach is widely used for estimating overlayer thickness in XPS when λ is treated as the effective attenuation length (EAL). , For an order-of-magnitude estimate, we adopted λ cos θ = 2 nm as a representative effective attenuation length. Because the EAL depends on the substrate composition, electron kinetic energy, the selected core level, and emission angle, the calculated d app should be interpreted as an approximate thickness. This assumed value is consistent with reported EALs of ∼1.8 ± 0.2 nm for photoelectrons with a kinetic energy of ∼1 keV. In this study, the attenuation of the metal core-level signals was only 1.55–11.03%, corresponding to d app ≈ 0.03–0.23 nm (Table S5). These values indicate that the apparent additional oxygen-containing layer was ultrathin, likely in the submonolayer-to-ultrathin regime. Such a layer could markedly enhance the O 1s signal, yet for many metals, the corresponding oxidized component may remain weak or unresolved in the metal core-level spectra and may not induce pronounced shifts in binding energy or FWHM within the present resolution. Thus, the thickness estimates support the spectroscopic observation that the O 1s intensity increased continuously even when many metal core-level spectra showed little obvious change.

Analysis of Oxygen-Containing Species

To further identify the apparent oxygen-containing species accumulated on the metal-film surfaces and their evolution, the high-resolution O 1s spectra acquired at t 0 and the quasi-steady state t ss were fitted using a component model, where applicable (Figure ). Based on literature values, the O 1s components were assigned to oxide-like M–O/lattice oxygen-related species, hydroxyl/hydroxide-related M–OH/–OH species, and adsorbed or bound H2O, with binding-energy ranges of 529.7–530.7 eV, 531.0–532.2 eV, and 532.9–533.7 eV, respectively. − During fitting, the FWHM values were maintained within physically reasonable ranges and kept comparable among overlapping components in the same spectrum to avoid overfitting. The detailed fitted binding energies, FWHM values, areas, and RSF values are summarized in Tables S6 and S7.

At t 0, the O 1s signals on Al and W surfaces were extremely weak. For Mo, only a weak M–O component was detected at t 0. For Fe, Co, and Ni, three resolvable oxygen-containing components were already observed at t 0: M–O, M–OH/–OH, and adsorbed/bound H2O. For Mn, only M–O and M–OH/–OH components were resolved, and no distinct H2O component was detected throughout the measurement period. At t ss, the O 1s spectra of all seven metal films except Mn contained the three oxygen-related components.

Although previous studies involving intentional O2/H2O dosing or near-ambient water-vapor conditions are not directly comparable with the present residual-gas exposure experiments in terms of oxidation rate, they provide useful qualitative surface-chemistry context. Water adsorption on metal surfaces is known to involve molecular adsorption, partial dissociation, −OH formation, and −OH/H2O coadsorption, depending on the metal, surface structure, oxygen coverage, and temperature. , Similar qualitative trends, including hydroxyl formation and −OH/H2O coexistence, have also been reported in in situ XPS studies under water-vapor environments. Therefore, the predominance of ΔR(M–O/Me) and ΔR(OH/Me) over ΔR(H2O/Me) is consistent with the interpretation that, under the chamber background conditions, residual H2O and/or other oxygen-containing species are more likely associated with chemically bound oxide-like and hydroxyl species than with purely molecular water accumulation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that trace oxygen-containing residual gases in the XPS analysis chamber can interact with sputter-treated metal-film surfaces and contribute to the measured oxygen signal during analysis. Therefore, the oxygen signal detected after Ar+ sputtering is not determined solely by the pre-existing oxygen content of the material but may also depend on the exposure history of the freshly generated surface during the measurement sequence. Ten representative elemental metal films, including Al, Ti, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Nb, Mo, and W, were examined under the same chamber background and acquisition conditions. Among them, seven metal films with lower apparent initial O levels were used for the primary comparison, whereas Cr, Ti, and Nb, which showed relatively high initial oxygen levels after sputtering, are discussed separately in the Supporting Information. This arrangement allows the main comparison to focus on films with more comparable initial postsputtering states while retaining the high-initial-oxygen samples as supplementary data sets.

Time-resolved O 1s measurements revealed a common two-stage accumulation process, characterized by a rapid initial increase followed by a slower approach to a quasi-steady state. Within this common evolution pattern, the seven films retained for the main comparison showed clear material-dependent differences: W, Mo, and Mn exhibited relatively high early stage O 1s accumulation rates, Al showed an intermediate but prolonged response, and Fe, Co, and Ni showed lower early stage rates. Kinetic fitting further indicated that the O 1s accumulation profiles could be described preferentially by either Avrami-type or pseudo-second-order models, reflecting different apparent accumulation behaviors under the same chamber background and acquisition conditions. The combined analysis of O 1s kinetics, metal core-level spectra, and high-resolution O 1s chemical-state fitting showed that the accumulated oxygen was not attributable solely to weakly or molecularly adsorbed H2O. Instead, it was mainly associated with chemically bound oxygen-containing species, particularly M–O and M–OH/–OH species, with the relative increases in M–O- or M–OH/–OH-related components varying among metals. Importantly, measurable O 1s growth occurred even when many of the corresponding metal core-level spectra remained nearly unchanged, indicating that ultrathin oxygen-containing overlayers can affect the near-surface chemical state while remaining difficult to resolve from the dominant metallic signal.

These findings provide further insight into the interpretation of low-level oxygen signals in XPS sputter-depth profiling. Even after nominal removal of the surface oxide layer by sputtering, the remaining oxygen signal may include a measurement-induced contribution from residual-gas adsorption and reaction on the postsputtering surface. Because this contribution is both time- and material-dependent, oxygen plateau signals should be interpreted with caution, especially when assessing oxide-layer thickness, interface position, chemical-state evolution, or low-concentration oxygen signals after sputtering.

Based on these results, several practical strategies are recommended to reduce oxygen-related analytical bias in XPS:(1)The chamber vacuum conditions should be optimized as the primary measure. The base pressure should be improved as much as practically possible, and the partial pressures of reactive oxygen-containing residual gases, especially H2O and O2, should be minimized. When comparing different samples or measurement batches, the chamber pressure and residual gas composition should be monitored and reported to ensure that the observed oxygen signals are not dominated by changes in the vacuum background.(2)The interval between Ar+ sputtering and spectral acquisition should be minimized and standardized. This is especially important for comparative measurements among different metals, samples, and depth positions.(3)High-resolution O 1s spectra should be acquired early in the measurement sequence to establish the postsputtering oxygen baseline before further residual-gas exposure occurs.(4)For metals that are highly sensitive to residual gases, the acquisition of O 1s and the corresponding metal core-level spectra should be prioritized together to reduce the time-dependent bias in chemical-state interpretation.
