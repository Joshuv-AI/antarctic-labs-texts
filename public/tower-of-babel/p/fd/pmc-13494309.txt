Single-center, retrospective cohort study of perioperative outcomes in robotic-assisted, laparoscopic, and open distal gastrectomy: A propensity score matching analysis
Authors: Yinpeng Qiang, Vetshev Fedor, Sergey Osminin, Ildar Bilyalov, Evgeniya Panina, Natalia Petukhova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRobotic-assisted distal gastrectomy (RADG) and laparoscopic distal gastrectomy (LDG) serve as minimally invasive alternatives to open distal gastrectomy (ODG); however, the magnitude of their perioperative benefits remains controversial. This study aimed to compare the advantages and disadvantages of these minimally invasive approaches with conventional open surgery, identify the potential superiorities of RADG for the treatment of distal gastric cancer, and explore its clinical application value.MethodsThis single-center, retrospective cohort study enrolled patients diagnosed with gastric cancer (GC) who underwent distal gastrectomy between January 2020 and June 2026. All patients were divided into three groups according to the surgical procedure performed: the RADG group, the LDG group, and the ODG group. Propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was applied to mitigate confounding differences, and perioperative outcomes of RADG were separately compared with those of LDG and ODG. The primary outcomes included operative time, intraoperative blood loss (IBL), lymph node (LN) harvest number, and complication rates. Secondary outcomes consisted of R0 resection rates and postoperative hospital stays.ResultsAfter PSM, 41 patients in the RADG group were matched with 41 patients in the LDG group, and 40 patients in the RADG group were matched with 40 patients in the ODG group. Compared with LDG and ODG, RADG was associated with significantly less intraoperative blood loss (P=0.029 vs. LDG; P<0.001 vs. ODG), greater lymph node harvest yields (P=0.006 vs. LDG; P<0.001 vs. ODG), and shorter postoperative hospital stays (P=0.042 vs. LDG; P<0.001 vs. ODG). No significant differences in operative time were observed between RADG and LDG (P=0.605) or between RADG and ODG (P=0.418). All groups exhibited comparable R0 resection rates and severe complication rates.ConclusionsRADG may yield favorable perioperative outcomes, including reduced IBL, improved LN retrieval, and shortened postoperative recovery time, without significantly increasing the incidence of severe perioperative complications.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is among the most prevalent malignancies globally. The latest 2022 global cancer statistics indicate that GC accounts for 4.9% of all cancer cases in both sexes, ranking fifth in overall cancer incidence, and contributes to 6.8% of all cancer-related deaths, retaining the same ranking. Notably, the incidence of GC is markedly higher in males than in females and presents significant regional disparities; for example, East Asia, particularly Mongolia, has the highest incidence rates worldwide. 1

Surgical intervention remains the cornerstone of GC treatment. An analysis of surgeon survey data from 2014 to 2020 demonstrates that although open surgery remains the predominant approach for the management of advanced GC, the adoption rate of minimally invasive surgical techniques has increased substantially, rising from 9% in 2014 (when 91% of procedures were performed via open surgery) to 39% in 2020.2,3 Laparoscopic surgery, first introduced for GC treatment by Kitano et al. in 1994, 4 has been comprehensively evaluated in multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs), including the CLASS-01 trial from China, the JCOG0912 and JLSSG0901 trials from Japan, and the KLASS-01 and KLASS-02 trials from Korea. These studies have confirmed that laparoscopic distal gastrectomy (LDG) yields superior perioperative outcomes compared with open distal gastrectomy (ODG). The specific advantages include reduced intraoperative blood loss (IBL), shortened hospital stay, alleviated postoperative pain, and accelerated recovery of gastrointestinal function.5–8 Furthermore, LDG has been proven non-inferior to open surgery in terms of long-term oncological outcomes.9–14 Accordingly, laparoscopic surgery has emerged as a safe and feasible alternative to conventional open surgery. Nevertheless, the laparoscopic approach has inherent limitations, including two-dimensional imaging, the fulcrum effect of instrument manipulation, limited degrees of freedom, and unavoidable physiological hand tremors. Such technical constraints, particularly the lack of three-dimensional spatial visualization and haptic feedback, significantly prolong the learning curve for surgeons performing laparoscopic gastrectomy, thereby hindering the widespread application of this technique in GC surgery.

To overcome the limitations of laparoscopic technology, robotic surgical systems have been developed. Since Hashizume et al. first reported the application of robotic surgery in 2002, 15 the use of robotic systems in distal gastrectomy has expanded progressively. Robotic systems provide high-definition three-dimensional imaging, simulate the dexterity of the human wrist, and filter out hand tremors, which substantially improves the precision and controllability of minimally invasive surgical procedures. Existing research has demonstrated that robotic surgery offers significant perioperative benefits, particularly in reducing IBL, improving lymph node (LN) retrieval, and lowering the incidence of postoperative complications.16–20

Although the advantages of LDG over ODG have been well established, data comparing all three surgical approaches (RADG, LDG, and ODG) within a single patient cohort remain sparse. Furthermore, existing evidence is predominantly derived from East Asian populations, and its applicability to populations from other geographical regions remains unvalidated. Therefore, continuous evaluation and comparison of the short-term outcomes of these three surgical approaches are essential to optimize clinical decision-making. This study adopted a single-center retrospective design to systematically assess the perioperative outcomes of RADG, LDG, and ODG, aiming to clarify the relative strengths of RADG among the three techniques. Uniquely, this study enrolled a cohort of Russian patients, thereby broadening the geographical scope of relevant research and providing more generalizable evidence for the clinical application of RADG. We hypothesized that RADG would yield superior perioperative outcomes, including reduced intraoperative blood loss, higher lymph node yield, and shorter postoperative hospital stay, compared with LDG and ODG, without increasing the incidence of severe complications.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975, revised 2024). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Sechenov University (approval number: 08-25; approval date: 10 April 2025). Written informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study. All patient data were fully anonymized and de-identified prior to analysis to ensure complete patient privacy. All consecutive patients who underwent distal gastrectomy for gastric cancer at the Department of Faculty Surgery No. 1, Sechenov University between January 2020 and June 2026 were screened for eligibility, and all eligible patients were included in the final analysis. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines for observational studies. 21 This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study. The sample size was determined based on the number of consecutive eligible patients who received distal gastrectomy during the study period; no a priori sample size calculation was performed due to the retrospective study design. A total of 192 patients were enrolled, including 97 males (50.52%) and 95 females (49.48%), all of whom underwent distal gastrectomy at the First Department of Surgery, Sechenov University. Postoperative follow-up data were collected from electronic health records and outpatient follow-up visits. All patients were followed up until hospital discharge, and 30-day postoperative outcomes, including mortality and readmission events, were systematically recorded. All surgical procedures were performed by a surgical team proficient in both laparoscopic and robot-assisted surgical techniques. The patients were stratified into three groups:(1) Robot-assisted distal gastrectomy group (RADG group, n=45)(2) Laparoscopic distal gastrectomy group (LDG group, n=61)(3) Open distal gastrectomy group (ODG group, n=86)

Baseline characteristics and clinical data were systematically collected and analyzed from the hospital’s electronic health records. The number of LNs harvested was determined by pathologists who performed routine lymph node sorting and pathological examination of the postoperative specimens.

Surgical team and perioperative management

All surgical procedures included in this study were performed by the same surgical team, consisting of three gastrointestinal surgeons. Each held independent operating qualifications and had accrued over 10 years of experience in gastric cancer surgery. Prior to the study, the surgeons had surpassed the learning curve for laparoscopic procedures, with each having performed over 100 independent laparoscopic gastric cancer surgeries. For robotic procedures, all attending surgeons had completed systematic training and obtained certification in robotic surgery. Procedures conducted before January 2020 were part of the learning curve phase and thus excluded from this study. Only cases conducted after the team achieved technical proficiency were included in the final analysis. Throughout the study period, our institution adhered to a standardized enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathway. 22 The perioperative management protocols, including anesthesia, analgesia, nutritional support, and postoperative care, remained consistent with no significant changes. During the study period, the three surgical approaches were performed in parallel. The choice of surgical approach was determined jointly by the surgeon and patient based on the following factors: (1) economic status, as robot-assisted surgery involves additional out-of-pocket costs that are not fully covered by insurance; (2) tumor-related factors, including tumor location, depth of invasion, and extent of lymph node involvement; (3) patient anatomical characteristics and surgical history, such as abdominal wall thickness, BMI, and prior abdominal surgery history; (4) comorbidities that affect cardiopulmonary function and patient tolerance of pneumoperitoneum; and (5) intraoperative surgeon judgment regarding anatomical conditions and tumor adherence, with no temporal variations in institutional clinical practice. At our institution, all patients who received neoadjuvant chemotherapy were administered the FLOT regimen (docetaxel, oxaliplatin, leucovorin, and 5-fluorouracil).

Preoperative examination

The preoperative assessment included standard laboratory tests, esophagogastroduodenoscopy, endoscopic ultrasonography, and imaging via contrast-enhanced CT for patients without an iodine allergy, or MRI for those with an iodine allergy. All enrolled patients subsequently underwent distal gastrectomy.

Technical details

ODG instruments and techniques

After a midline laparotomy, an inspection of the abdominal organs was performed. The omental bursa was accessed through the gastrocolic ligament, and the greater omentum was completely mobilized. Subsequently, the duodenal bulb was circumferentially mobilized. The right gastroepiploic artery was ligated and transected at its origin, enabling the retraction of the fatty tissue and the sixth group of LNs towards the stomach.

The distal transection line was established 1-2 cm below the pylorus, where the duodenum was divided using a 60 mm linear stapler. This step was followed by the peritonealization (Vicryl 3-0). Subsequently, the stomach was freed along the lesser curvature from the hepatoduodenal ligament to the abdominal segment of the esophagus, along with the surrounding fatty tissue and LNs.

Both the right and left gastric arteries were ligated and transected at their respective bases. The dissection continued along the greater curvature up to the left gastroepiploic artery, where similar ligation and transection were executed. The proximal transection line was established by connecting two points: 2 cm below the lesser curvature and the level at the left gastroepiploic artery along the greater curvature. The stomach was then divided using a linear stapler. The mechanical sutures were subsequently peritonized (Vicryl 3-0).

LDG instruments and techniques

The number, size, and placement of the trocars for LDG are depicted in Figure 1. The surgical instruments primarily used include endoscopic equipment from Karl Storz, Germany, and Arthrex, USA. The lead surgeon operates from between the patient’s legs.Figure 1.Schematic diagram of trocar placement for laparoscopic distal gastrectomy.

Schematic diagram of trocar placement for laparoscopic distal gastrectomy.

First, an optical trocar (10-12 mm) is installed using an open technique. The ports are then inserted under visual control. Carbon dioxide gas is introduced to a pressure of 12 mmHg, and a 30-degree laparoscope is used to perform the laparoscopic examination and inspect the abdominal organs.

Next, to appropriately position the main and auxiliary ports, the surgical table is adjusted to Fowler’s position based on the patient’s individual measurements. The main steps and sequence of laparoscopic surgery are not notably different from those of traditional surgery, with the core difference being the instruments and techniques used. Sutures (size 0-0) can be used for fixation, sequentially passing through the anterior abdominal wall, the medial leaf of the right diaphragm, and finally passing from the interior through the abdominal wall to the exterior, positioned lateral to the ligamentum teres of the liver.

During tissue dissection and hemostasis, laparoscopic ultrasonic scissors are commonly employed. Ligation of blood vessels, lymphatics, and bile ducts is typically performed using titanium or polymer clips, with the size of the clips determined by the diameter of the structure being clamped. The surgery begins at the greater curvature of the stomach, entering the omental sac, starting at the lower third of the stomach body, and positioned at least 4 cm away from the right gastroepiploic artery. The specimen is extracted through a periumbilical incision, with the incision extended 2 cm towards the caudal and cranial sides to allow for extracorporeal intestinal anastomosis through the same incision if necessary, thus restoring the gastrointestinal tract. The specimen is then extracted through either a periumbilical or a small incision above the pubic bone.

RADG instruments and technology

The number, size, and placement of the trocars for RADG are depicted in Figure 2. During the robot-assisted surgery, the “da Vinci Si” Intuitive Surgical System was used. The positioning of the patient on the operating table and the arrangement of the trocars were the same as in laparoscopic surgery. A 12 mm trocar was inserted next to the umbilicus. Given the use of three robotic manipulators and their diameters, two specialized tool trocars with a diameter of 8 mm were placed 3 to 4 cm above the umbilicus, along the midclavicular line on both sides. In the right upper abdomen, a 12 mm auxiliary trocar was installed to introduce sutures, surgical gauze, and a linear stapler.Figure 2.Schematic diagram of trocar placement for robot-assisted distal gastrectomy.

Schematic diagram of trocar placement for robot-assisted distal gastrectomy.

The da Vinci Si tools utilized during the procedure included ProGrasp Forceps (with extended jaws), Mega SutureCut Needle Driver (with a cutting wire device), Harmonic Curved Shears (ultrasonic surgical scissors), and Fenestrated Bipolar Forceps.

PSM analysis

PSM was performed using a logistic regression model to calculate propensity scores based on the following variables: age, sex, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, 24 receipt of neoadjuvant therapy, tumor differentiation degree, pathological T stage, pathological N stage, and pathological TNM stage. 25 Based on these variables, two independent 1:1 pairwise matching analyses were performed for the comparisons of RADG vs. LDG and RADG vs. ODG, respectively. For each comparison, a nearest-neighbor matching algorithm with a caliper width of 0.2 times the standard deviation of the logit propensity score was independently applied to the original unmatched cohorts. Specifically, RADG patients were first matched to LDG patients, followed by a separate matching procedure to match the same pre-matched RADG patients to ODG patients. Consequently, the two matched RADG subgroups partially overlapped but were not identical, as they were generated via two independent runs of the matching algorithm. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), whereby an SMD<0.1 was defined as acceptable balance.

Statistical analysis

This study utilized a retrospective design to assess and compare surgical parameters and clinical outcomes across three distinct patient cohorts. The data processing was executed using SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) on a Windows platform. Continuous variables adhering to a normal distribution were depicted as the mean±standard deviation, while those not normally distributed were presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were characterized by frequencies and percentages. The continuous variables analyzed included age, BMI, operative time, IBL, number of LNs harvested, and length of hospital stay. Categorical variables comprised sex, ECOG performance status, ASA physical status classification, clinical T stage, clinical N stage, tumor location, pathological T stage, pathological N stage, pathological TNM stage, degree of differentiation, received neoadjuvant chemotherapy, lymphadenectomy, resection margin status, and postoperative severe complications. For continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution, unpaired Student’s t-test was used for analysis, whereas non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was employed to analyze categorical variables, as appropriate. All tests were two-sided, and a P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In terms of operative metrics, the mean operative time showed no significant differences after matching between RADG and LDG (236.95±55.44 min vs. 229.88±67.25 min, P=0.605) or between RADG and ODG (235.88±53.74 min vs. 225.12±63.95 min, P=0.418). For intraoperative blood loss, RADG resulted in significantly lower blood loss than both LDG (152.68±49.25 mL vs. 179.51±59.66 mL, P=0.029) and ODG (151.00±47.87 mL vs. 218.75±60.09 mL, P<0.001). Regarding postoperative recovery, the RADG group had a significantly shorter mean hospital stay than the LDG group (12.20±4.82 days vs. 14.29±4.35 days, P=0.042) and the ODG group (12.12±4.87 days vs. 17.05±5.48 days, P<0.001).

Furthermore, all pairwise comparisons after PSM demonstrated high R0 resection rates, with no significant differences between RADG and LDG (97.56% vs. 97.56%, P=1.000) or between RADG and ODG (97.50% vs. 95.00%, P=0.556).

Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the overall rates of severe complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III) between RADG and LDG (9.76% vs. 12.20%, P=0.724) or between RADG and ODG (10.00% vs. 7.50%, P=0.692). The severe complications were detailed as follows: anastomotic leakage occurred in 1 patient (2.5%) in the RADG group, 1 patient (2.5%) in the LDG group, and 1 patient (2.5%) in the ODG group. Peritoneal infection was observed in 1 patient (2.5%) in the RADG and LDG groups respectively, and in 2 patients (5.0%) in the ODG group. Abdominal bleeding occurred in 1 patient (2.5%) in the RADG group and 2 patients (5.0%) in the LDG group, with no cases recorded in the ODG group. Pancreatic fistula occurred in 1 patient (2.5%) in the LDG group only, with no cases in the RADG or ODG groups. Intestinal obstruction occurred in 1 patient (2.5%) in the RADG group, with no cases in the other two groups. All severe complications were successfully managed with appropriate treatment, and no perioperative mortality was reported. The 30-day mortality rate was 0% across all groups. The 30-day readmission rate was 0% in the RADG group and 2.5% (1 patient each) in both the LDG and ODG groups.

Discussion

This study evaluated the perioperative outcomes of three distinct surgical methodologies. The results suggest that RADG may offer advantages over conventional ODG in reducing surgical trauma, improving the efficiency of LN retrieval, and facilitating expedited postoperative recovery. Nevertheless, the three approaches demonstrated equivalent oncological thoroughness, as reflected by comparable R0 resection rates and overall safety profiles, with no significant disparities in severe complication rates. 26

A comprehensive meta-analysis encompassing 68,755 patients with GC indicated that robotic surgery enables the retrieval of more LNs compared with laparoscopic surgery. 27 Our findings support this observation, providing additional evidence for the significant advantages of the robotic platform in achieving more extensive LN dissection. We propose that this advantage may derive from the system’s proprietary stereoscopic vision and enhanced image magnification, which clearly delineate anatomical boundaries among fascial spaces, lymphatic adipose tissue, and neurovascular bundles. This superior visualization assists surgeons in precisely identifying and localizing vascular-adjacent LNs, including tiny nodes that are frequently undetectable in conventional laparoscopic procedures. Moreover, the robotic system’s articulated instruments, which possess seven degrees of freedom similar to the human wrist, afford a wider range of motion than the human hand. This characteristic allows precise, multi-angled dissection in confined anatomical regions, such as the splenic hilum, peripyloric No. 6 LNs, and No. 7 and 8a LNs, thereby facilitating more comprehensive en bloc resection of lymphatic adipose tissue. Additionally, the robotic platform’s tremor filtration function and enhanced operational stability substantially reduce the risk of LN capsular rupture and residual tissue retention caused by instrument vibration, thereby preserving the integrity of lymphadenectomy.

Notably, despite the additional time required for the setup and docking of the robotic surgical system, no statistically significant difference was observed in total operative time between robotic-assisted distal gastrectomy and laparoscopic distal gastrectomy in this study (p=0.605). This observation contradicts previous reports. The proficiency of an experienced robotic surgical team, particularly during critical steps such as lymph node dissection and digestive tract reconstruction, may enable operative times comparable to or even shorter than those of laparoscopic procedures. Such efficiency appears to offset the initial time required for robotic system setup and docking. Supporting this assertion, the study by Pan Hua Feng et al. found no significant difference in actual console time (excluding docking and undocking time) between robotic and laparoscopic groups (p=0.717). 28 Similarly, Francesco Vigo et al. demonstrated that after structured interdisciplinary team training, which enhanced team proficiency, the mean docking time for robotic procedures significantly decreased from 45.3 minutes to 27.3 minutes (p<0.001), and the mean total operative time was reduced from 235 minutes to 179 minutes (p=0.0071). 29 These findings suggest that the precision advantages of robotic surgery do not necessarily lead to longer operative times when performed by skilled teams. Therefore, we propose that increased team proficiency in robotic surgery is a key factor contributing to the comparable operative times between RADG and LDG in this study.

Regarding secondary outcome measures, the study revealed a notably shorter postoperative hospital stay for the RADG group compared to both the LDG (p=0.042) and ODG groups (p<0.001). Postoperative hospital stay is a critical clinical indicator of overall patient recovery post-surgery. The shortened duration typically suggests a more favorable recovery trajectory. It is hypothesized that the accelerated recovery in RADG patients may be attributed to the intraoperative benefits of robotic surgery, such as precise dissection and superior tissue preservation, which likely contribute to reduced surgical trauma, effective control of IBL, and minimized disruption of gastrointestinal function. Collectively, these factors enhance postoperative recovery. This outcome further underscores the clinical value of robotic gastrectomy in terms of perioperative recovery.

The results of this study are highly consistent with previous investigations conducted at leading research institutions in East Asia, which suggest that robotic surgery offers significant enhancements in LN dissection, minimizes IBL, and reduces the duration of hospital stays. However, the distinctive contribution of our research stems from the fact that all surgeries were performed at a single institution, where surgical teams adhered to uniform treatment protocols. This approach markedly diminishes systematic biases that may arise from variations in hospital workflows, nursing standards, and postoperative care strategies. As a result, it provides a more precise assessment of the effects of surgical techniques on patient outcomes. Despite variations in individual surgical methodologies, the core composition of the surgical team, anaesthesiology support, and the philosophy of perioperative management were consistently maintained. This consistency effectively mitigates potential confounding variables related to surgeons’ technical proficiency, experiential biases, or disparities in their learning trajectories, thereby rendering the comparative analysis more indicative of the intrinsic differences between the technological platforms. Furthermore, this study is pioneering in validating these benefits in a patient cohort representative of an Eastern European demographic, specifically individuals from Russia. In contrast to prior studies that predominantly included East Asian participants, our cohort displays characteristics typical of a European population, such as a higher average BMI and potentially divergent tumor biological traits. We postulate that the technical superiorities of robotic surgery, namely its 3D high-definition visual field and enhanced manipulation precision, are particularly advantageous in managing the complex intra-abdominal anatomy often present in patients with elevated BMI. This could be a critical factor underlying the well-controlled blood loss observed in our study. Additionally, despite variances in the distribution of disease stages among the examined cases, the robotic group consistently showed higher LN yields. Thus, our findings not only reaffirm the conclusions drawn from major East Asian studies but also generate the hypothesis that robotic gastrectomy may be feasible and potentially beneficial for Eastern European Caucasian populations. These observations add to the growing body of literature on robotic gastric surgery, and their clinical relevance deserves further investigation.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Firstly, as a single-center retrospective study, it is inherently susceptible to selection bias and allocation bias. Although post-PSM analysis demonstrated no significant intergroup differences in baseline characteristics, unmeasured confounding factors may still exist, which could introduce bias to the study results. In addition, tumor size, specific cardiopulmonary comorbidities, a history of previous abdominal surgery, and patient preference could not be fully adjusted for due to incomplete documentation. These unmeasured factors may have influenced both the selection of surgical approach and postoperative outcomes, thereby constituting residual confounding. In addition, although the initial learning curve for robotic procedures was excluded by restricting enrollment to cases performed after 2020, and all procedures adopted the same da Vinci Si platform throughout the study period, subtle learning effects within the RADG cohort during clinical practice cannot be entirely ruled out. Secondly, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect significant differences in short-term severe complication rates. Accordingly, although no statistically significant intergroup differences in complication rates were observed, this finding should be interpreted with caution, as the sample size may have been insufficient to identify genuine differences or confirm equivalent safety profiles. The absence of evidence does not equate to evidence of equivalence. Moreover, although unified operations performed by expert surgical teams at a single medical center facilitated technical standardization, the study results may be affected by the specific expertise and preferences of the study team, which may limit generalizability. Finally, this study mainly focused on short-term perioperative outcomes and lacked long-term oncological data, including overall survival and disease-free survival. The retrospective design and potential selection bias may lead to the overestimation of the clinical benefits of RADG. However, PSM with a caliper width of 0.2 was applied to minimize measurable confounding, and the residual bias derived from unmeasured confounders is likely modest. Future studies should adopt multicenter, large-sample, prospective RCTs with prolonged follow-up periods to clarify the association between the minimally invasive advantages of robotic surgery and long-term patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This observational study suggests that RADG may offer favorable perioperative outcomes compared with LDG and ODG, including reduced IBL, increased LN yield, and accelerated postoperative recovery, without increasing perioperative complication risks. Considering the observational design and limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted with caution and further validated by large-scale prospective studies with long-term oncological follow-up.
