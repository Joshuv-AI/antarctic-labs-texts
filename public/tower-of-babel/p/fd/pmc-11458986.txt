Strong metal‒support interaction modulates catalytic activity of Ru nanoparticles on Gd2O3 for efficient ammonia decomposition
Authors: Xiaohua Ju, Lin Liu, Xiaoyan Xu, Jiemin Wang, Teng He, Ping Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryExploration of efficient Ru-based catalysts is significant for advancing the production of hydrogen from ammonia decomposition, which depends predominantly on the rational regulation of the metal-support interaction of Ru-based catalysts. Herein, highly dispersed Ru nanoparticles (NPs) on Gd2O3 (Ru/Gd2O3) are developed and applied for the NH3 decomposition reaction. By varying the reduction temperature, the activity of the Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst can be remarkably improved. Characterization results reveal that the enhanced activity is associated with the strong metal‒support interaction (SMSI) induced formation of Gd2O3 overlayer on the Ru NPs. With a relatively low reduction temperature, the exposed Ru NPs possess relatively low activity toward NH3 decomposition. After high temperature activation treatment, the Gd2O3 overlayer coated Ru NPs show much enhanced intrinsic activity than that of the exposed Ru NPs. This study brings a facile strategy to modulate the catalytic performance of oxide supported Ru catalysts by regulating the SMSI of catalysts.Subject areas: Chemistry, catalysis

Introduction

Hydrogen is one of the most promising energy carriers for building a sustainable society. However, storage of H2 in an economic and sustainable way is still a challenge and hampers its promising application in different fields. Among the different materials for storage of H2, ammonia attracts increasing attention as an H2 carrier for its high content of hydrogen (17.7 wt %) and high density of energy (ca. 3 kWh kg−1), and billion tons of annual production.1,2,3 NH3 decomposition is a critical step for the utilization of NH3 as H2 feedstock. Usually, a high temperature is required for the production of H2 from NH3. Thus, the development of efficient catalysts for NH3 decomposition, especially performing well at relatively low temperatures, is highly expected. During the past several decades, great efforts have been made to develop explore highly active metal catalysts for NH3 decomposition.1,2,3

Amoand ng the different kinds of NH3 decomposition catalysts, it is particularly true that Ru-based catalysts, frequently dispersed in the form of Ru NPs on metal oxides and carbon supports, have attracted extensive attention for their excellent catalytic performances.4,5,6,7 Previous studies also show that the catalytic performances of oxide supported Ru catalysts in NH3 decomposition can be greatly influenced by the properties of supports. Oxide supports have been actively explored. A variety of oxide supports such as SiO2,8,9 TiO2,5 zeolite,9 Al2O3,10,11 MgO,5,12,13,14 ZrO2,15 CeO2,16,17,18 La2O3,19 Sm2O3,20 Y2O3,21 and Pr2O322 have been used as supports for Ru-based catalysts. To advance the design of efficient Ru-based catalysts for NH3 decomposition reaction, exploring new kinds of oxide supports that are able to effectively disperse and stabilize the Ru NPs is still highly desired.

The role of oxide supports is not only to disperse and stabilize metal particles but also to generate interface phenomena through metal‒support interaction, which are both highly related to the catalytic performances of Ru NPs on oxide supports. It is well documented that the strong metal-support interaction (SMSI) plays a critical role in regulating the catalytic performance (activity/selectivity/stability) of oxide supported metal catalysts.23,24 Since the first report about the suppressed chemisorption of CO and H2 over TiO2-supported platinum-group metals after high temperature reduction, SMSI has been recognized as a wide effect in a variety of heterogeneous oxide supported metal catalysts. Typically, the SMSI effect in oxide supported metal catalysts can induce the formation of an oxide overlayer on the surface of metal particles, which can significantly modulate the catalytic activities of supported metal particles on the oxides. SMSI frequently induces the formation of an overlayer of oxides (e.g., TiO2 and Nb2O5) during high temperature reduction, which decorates the metal nanoparticles and greatly improves the catalytic performance of Co-based catalysts for Fischer-Tropsch (FT) reaction.23,24 The oxides overlayer was thought to facilitate the reduction of cobalt oxide to the active metal state or altered electronic properties of metallic Co species and hence accelerate the FT reaction rate. Rare earth oxides such as La2O3 and CeO2 have been widely used as supports for Ru-based catalysts in ammonia synthesis. Especially, SMSI on reducible oxide-supported Ru catalysts can occur in a Ru/La0.5Ce0.5O1.75 catalyst, where partially reduced La0.5Ce0.5O1.75 overlayer can be formed and can further influence the catalytic ammonia synthesis process.25 Meanwhile, rare earth oxides have also shown great promise as support for Ru-based catalysts in the NH3 decomposition reaction in recent years.16,19,20,21,22,26 Thus, the intrinsic role of SMSI in tuning the activity of rare earth oxides supported Ru catalysts is also worth studying in depth. However, the SMSI effect on the catalytic activities of rare earth oxide supported Ru catalysts for NH3 decomposition is less studied and not well understood up to now.

In this work, we report a facile precipitation route for the synthesis of Gd2O3 supported Ru NPs (Ru/Gd2O3) catalysts for NH3 decomposition. By varying the reduction temperature, SMSI on Ru/Gd2O3 can be modulated without changing the particle size of Ru NPs, which further plays a crucial role in tuning the activity of catalysts. With a reduction treatment at 300°C, bare Ru NPs on the catalyst surface only show relatively low activity. After the catalyst undergoes a high temperature reduction activation treatment at 500°C, Ru NPs are encapsulated by reduced Gd2O3 species, which can significantly enhance the activity by 1 order of magnitude under identical conditions. Detailed characterization results demonstrate that the remarkable activity enhancement is associated with the formation of reduced Gd2O3 layers on Ru NPs, which can improve the activity and intrinsic activity of Ru NPs toward NH3 decomposition by regulating the electronic structure of the Ru NPs. As a result, we show that the modulation of the strong metal‒support interaction is a promising strategy to greatly improve the catalytic performance of Ru-based catalysts supported on reducible oxides.

Results

Reduction treatment effect on the activity of Ru/Gd2O3

Chosen as a model catalyst system, Ru/Gd2O3 samples with 1.5 wt % Ru and 3 wt % Ru loading were prepared by a facile precipitation method. The actual Ru mass loading of the Ru/Gd2O3 samples was determined by ICP-OES analysis (Table S1). As expected, the experimental values of Ru content are much closer to those of the theoretical values of Ru in the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 and 3% Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts. The 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 and 3% Ru/Gd2O3 sample was reduced in 5% H2/Ar flow at 300°C (defined as 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R), and then tested for NH3 decomposition reaction. As shown in Figure 1A, the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalysts were inactive at relatively low reaction temperature (<350°C) even after reduction. Obvious activity can only be observed at temperatures above 375°C on 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalysts. At 425°C, only a very low NH3 conversion of less than 20% can be observed for the 3% Ru/Gd2O3-R catalyst. By contrast, remarkable enhancement of activity can be obtained for the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 and 3% Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts activated in a NH3 flow at a temperature of 500°C for 2 h (defined as 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts). The activity of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst at 400°C was found to be 45.9%, which is much higher than that of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R (3.8%) (Figure 1A). The NH3 conversion at 400°C shows the activity increased by more than 12.1-fold after high temperature reduction treatment (Figure 1B), and is comparable to many other Ru-based catalysts reported in the literature (Table 1). As a very important characteristic, the wide applicability of the high temperature treatment method was also examined for different Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts. As shown in Figure 1A, after treating 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 in the NH3 stream at 500°C, remarkably enhanced NH3 conversion can also be observed in 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A in comparison with that of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R (Figure 1A). The activity of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst at 400°C was found to be 15.0%, much higher than that of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R (1.7%). Accordingly, the NH3 conversion of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A at 400°C shows that the activity increased by more than 8.8-fold after high temperature reduction treatment (Figure 1B).

NH3 conversion and H2 formation rate of typical supported Ru catalysts at 450°C

A comparison of the activity of Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst with those of the other efficient Ru-based NH3 decomposition catalysts reported in the literature is performed. The NH3 conversion of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A is higher than many of the unmodified supported Ru catalysts in literatures (Table 1). Based on the weight of the Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst, the H2 formation rate of the catalyst per gram can be calculated. Not only the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A but also the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst possesses a higher H2 formation rate than many typical efficient Ru-based catalysts under similar reaction conditions. For example, the H2 formation rate of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst is also higher than many typical efficient Ru-based catalysts such as Ru/Al2O3, Ru/SiO2, Ru/CNTs, Ru/MgO, Ru/La2O3 and Ru/Sm2O3 under similar reaction conditions (Table 1; Figure S1). Considering the amount of Ru content in 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst, the H2 formation rate of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst per gram Ru is as high as 1578.4 mmol gRu−1 min−1 at 450°C, which is 3.2 times that of typical efficient 5% Ru/MgO catalyst under same reaction conditions. The catalytic behavior of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A was further examined at various GHSV values. Figure S2 depicts the effect of GHSV on the catalytic performance of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst at 450°C. It can be seen that NH3 conversion decreases remarkably with the increase of GHSV because of the increase in the residence time of the reactants. At 450°C, nearly complete conversion can be obtained with a GHSV as high as 10000 mL gcat−1 h−1. The H2 formation rate of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst at 450°C was also calculated. With the GHSV value increases from 10000 to 70000 mL gcat−1 h−1, the H2 formation rate of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst at 450°C shows a remarkable increase from 613.5 to 2224.9 mmol gcat−1 h−1. Besides activity, stability is another crucial parameter for the evaluation of the catalytic performance of the catalyst in NH3 decomposition. As shown in Figure 2, the activity of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst can be well maintained during the following long isothermal test under NH3 decomposition reaction conditions. Such an activated 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst remained stable and did not show an obvious decrease in its activity (∼83.6%) over 100 h on stream at 450°C, evidencing the excellent stability of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst for NH3 decomposition reaction.

Structure characterization of Ru/Gd2O3

Even though the Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalyst has been reduced before the activity test, the Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalyst still needs to be activated at optimized high temperature. The activation temperature is critical for the activation of the Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts, which may be related to the structure evolution of Ru/Gd2O3 at the high reduction temperature. To examine the structure evolution of catalysts, XRD was applied to characterize the structure of Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts. For the Gd2O3 support, diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 27.42, 31.59, 39.45, 45.67, and 53.83° can be observed in the XRD pattern, which match well with the diffractions from the (222), (400), (431), (440) and (622) crystal planes of Gd2O3 phase (JCPDS 00-011-0608) (Figure 3). Loading Ru results in no obvious change of the structure of Gd2O3 support and no characteristic Ru peaks were observed for the Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples, demonstrating the high dispersion of Ru NPs on the surface of Gd2O3 support (Figure 3). Similar to that of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples, there are no obvious diffraction features attributable to metallic Ru can be found in the XRD patterns of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples.

XRD patterns of Gd2O3, 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R, 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A, 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R, and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts

It is well known that the surface structure and chemical state of Ru NPs determine the catalytic performance of supported Ru catalyst in NH3 decomposition reaction. Thus, a comparison of the H2 formation turnover frequency (TOFH2) value on each exposed Ru atom is very useful for evaluating the intrinsic activity of Ru NPs in different catalysts. TOFH2, calculated by normalizing the observed H2 formation rate (mole formed, H2 s−1 gcat−1) to the number of exposed Ru surface atoms per gram of catalyst, is calculated to understand the enhanced catalytic performance of Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts. In comparison with that of the Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalysts, an obvious enhanced TOFH2 value can be observed for Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts (Figure S3), in line with the catalytic activity results. At 400°C, the TOFH2 value of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A is 1.96 s−1, while the TOFH2 value of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R is only 0.16 s−1. The TOFH2 value of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A at 400°C is 12.3 times higher than that of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R, clearly demonstrating the greatly enhanced intrinsic activity of Ru NPs in the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst for NH3 decomposition. At 450°C, the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample gives a TOFH2 value of 3.58 s−1, which is comparable with that of the typical efficient Ru/MgO catalysts (3.0 s−1)14 under identical reaction conditions, revealing the good intrinsic activity of Ru NPs in 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample. Figure 4 also shows the TOFH2 value of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts at 400°C and 450°C, respectively. A TOFH2 value of 1.21 s−1 at 400°C can be observed for 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A, which is 8.6 times higher than that of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R (0.14 s−1) sample under identical reaction conditions. Upon increasing the temperature from 400°C to 450°C, the TOFH2 value of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A increased by about 3.8 times to 4.59 s−1. The TOFH2 value of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A (4.59 s−1) at 450°C is not only higher than that of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R (2.29 s−1) but also higher than that of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A (3.58 s−1) at the same temperature. Compared with that of the Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples, the increased TOFH2 value of Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample implies that the decomposition of NH3 molecules can proceed much facilely and efficiently over the Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples.

Strong metal-support interaction in Ru/Gd2O3

The reduction behavior of the as-prepared 3% Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst was then acquired by an H2-TPR experiment. As shown in Figure 5, two main reduction peaks centered at about 127°C and 212°C can be observed, which are attributed to the reduction of free standing RuO2 NPs to Ru NPs. For the Gd2O3 support, two broad and weak reduction peaks centered at 300°C and 580°C can be observed in the H2-TPR profile. Different from that of the Gd2O3 support, two small peaks at around 116°C and 146°C and two strong broad peaks centered at around 308°C and 364°C can be observed for the 3% Ru/Gd2O3 sample. The presence of two reduction peaks indicates that Ru NP precursor exists in two major different states in 3% Ru/Gd2O3 sample. The reduction of Gd2O3 in the 3% Ru/Gd2O3 sample catalyst, which can be confirmed by the presence of broad reduction peaks at 320°C and 530°C. Similar to that of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3 sample, two small peaks at around 121°C and 152°C and two strong broad peaks centered at around 330°C and 375°C can be observed for the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 sample. Benefiting from the interaction between the RuO2 NPs and Gd2O3 support, the reduction of Gd2O3 in 3% Ru/Gd2O3 is greatly facilitated in comparison with that of pure Gd2O3 support (at 671°C) due to the facile hydrogen spillover. Loading Ru NPs on the Gd2O3 significantly facilitates the reduction of support. Compared with the Gd2O3 support, the reduction of surface oxygen of Gd2O3 support can be greatly enhanced by the presence of the highly dispersed Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts. The larger hydrogen consumption peak as compared with that of Gd2O3 support suggests surface oxygen vacancies should be formed on the Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst. As the reduction of surface oxygen species may overlap with the reduction of Ru species, thus directly assigning each peak in the H2-TPR profile of Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts is a very difficult task. Quantitative H2-TPR results for the Gd2O3, 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3, and 3% Ru/Gd2O3 samples are given in Table S2. The H2 consumption of the Ru/Gd2O3 samples was analyzed, and the theoretical value of H2 consumption of Ru in Ru/Gd2O3 samples was calculated based on the assumption of RuO2 formation from Ru content from ICP results. This theoretical value was compared to the experimental H2-consumption measured by H2-TPR results. The H2 consumption amount of bare Gd2O3 support is 123.4 μmol gcat−1. By contrast, the actual H2 consumption amount of 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 is 3691.0 μmol gcat−1, much higher than the theoretical value of 217.7 μmol gcat−1 for the reduction of Ru NPs in the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst. In the case of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst, the actual H2 consumption amount of 3% Ru/Gd2O3 (3859.8 μmol gcat−1) is also higher than the theoretical value of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst of (548.1 μmol gcat−1). At the same time, the H2 consumption values of Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts are also higher than that of the theoretical value for the reduction of Ru NPs and bare Gd2O3. Thus, we can propose that the large discrepancy between the theoretical and experimental H2 consumption values of Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts implies significant amount of the surface oxygen near Ru NPs has been reduced along with the reduction of Ru NPs. Furthermore, it is also important to note that the reduction degree of Gd2O3 support in the Ru/Gd2O3 sample was supposed to be improved with the increase of the reduction temperature.

Here, the dramatic activity enhancement of the Ru/Gd2O3 is closely related to the structural change of the catalyst after high temperature reduction treatment. TEM characterization was employed to characterize the morphology of Ru NPs in different Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. As shown in Figure 6, Ru NPs are uniformly dispersed on the Gd2O3 support for Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts. Here, the high temperature reduction treatment has little effect on the particle size of Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample. As shown in Figure 6, highly dispersed Ru NPs are uniformly dispersed on Gd2O3 in the HRTEM image of Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. Based on the Ru NPs in the images of different Ru/Gd2O3 samples, the average particle sizes of the Ru NPs can be preliminary estimated (Figure S4). The average particle size of the Ru NPs for the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A from statistical analysis is about 2.7 and 2.9 nm, respectively. Meanwhile, the average particle size of Ru NPs in 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A are 2.4 and 2.9 nm, respectively, similar to that of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. In addition to highly dispersed Ru NPs, an amorphous overlayer can be observed on the Ru NPs in the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample, revealing the formation Gd2O3 layer on the Ru NPs after activation treatment. Similar to that of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample, an amorphous overlayer can also be observed on the Ru NPs in the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample (Figure 6).

Generally, structural factors including the dispersion, size, and morphology of Ru NPs, interaction between Ru NPs and support, and surface property of support can influence the intrinsic activity of supported Ru NPs. Thus, the remarkably different TOFH2 values of similar Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts indicate that another key factor is highly related to the greatly enhanced intrinsic activity of Ru NPs rather than the particle size of Ru NPs. As a powerful tool for discerning highly dispersed Ru NPs, the structure of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst was further investigated by HAADF‒STEM. As shown in Figure 7A, an overlayer with much different bright-field image contrast can be observed in the HAADF-STEM image of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample. The presence of a Gd2O3-x overlayer is also evident in the Ru and Gd line scanning profiles across the Ru NPs (Figure 7B). Line-scan profile of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A shows that the Gd2O3-x overlayer was located on the surface of Ru NPs. EDX scan analysis indicates that the inner NPs are composed mainly of Ru element, whereas the high-contrast species on the surface of NPs are composed mainly of Gd element (Figure 7B). Clearly, upon high temperature activation treatment, the Ru NPs of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R experienced a drastic transformation from bare NPs to Gd2O3-x overlayer partially covered NPs without changing their size and morphology. The formation of Gd2O3-x overlayer on the surface of Ru NP catalysts while maintaining the high dispersion of Ru NPs on Gd2O3 support.

CO-DRIFT spectroscopy was applied to further confirm the structure of Ru species on 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples due to the sensitivity and accuracy of this technique in determining the electronic properties of Ru NPs. Figure 8A shows the DRIFTS spectra of adsorbed CO on the different catalysts. The 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalyst displays three bands, i.e., 2180 cm−1, 2039–2062 cm−1, and 1900–2025 cm−1, similar to those reported in the previous literature. According to the literature results, the bands at about 2050 and 1974∼1982 cm−1 are ascribed to the linearly adsorbed CO and bridge bonded CO on the low-coordinated surface Ru atoms located on metallic Ru NPs,32,33,34,35 while the band at 2180 cm−1 is usually assigned to the multi-carbonyl species on the partially oxidized Ru sites [Ruδ+(CO)x] on the oxide supports.36,37 Meanwhile, it can be seen that the band at about 1900–2025 cm−1 of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A (1982 cm−1) and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A (1982 cm−1) samples display a blue-shift in comparison with that of the same band of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples (1974 cm−1). The blue-shift of the band at about 1974∼1982 cm−1 in the Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples may have a direct relation with the formation of an overlayer of Gd2O3-x layer on the Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. Due to the formation of an overlayer of the Gd2O3 layer on the Ru NPs, the active sites on Ru NPs in 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples may be partially blocked. Here, a CO chemisorption experiment was conducted to investigate the accessibility of the CO molecules on the Ru NPs in 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. According to the CO chemisorption results, similar values can be observed over the different 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R (0.130 mmol g−1) and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A (0.129 mmol g−1) samples (Figure 8B). Quantity analysis chemisorbed CO species on the coated Ru NPs is similar to that of the bare Ru NPs, indicating the partial coating Gd2O3 layer on the Ru NPs does not obviously decrease the accessibility of CO molecules active sites in 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. As expected, the Ru NPs in 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples possess similar metal dispersion values based on the CO chemisorption result. Based on CO pulse chemisorption results, the Ru dispersion of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples possess similar values (Table S1). Accordingly, the Ru particle size of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples estimated by CO pulse chemisorption is 2.7 and 2.9 nm, respectively, in line with the TEM results.

NH3 decomposition over Ru-based catalyst has been recognized as a structurally sensitive reaction.38,39,40 The B5 site on the surface of metallic Ru NPs, an active site for the decomposition of NH3 molecules during the NH3 decomposition reaction process, shows high dependence on the particle size of Ru NPs.40 Previous studies reveal that the catalytic performances of Ru-based catalysts depend sensitively on the surface structure of Ru particles, which are closely related with the metal dispersion, size, and shape of Ru particles.38,39,40 Here, the metal dispersion, size, and morphology of Ru NPs are all nearly identical for the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. Thus, the remarkably different TOFH2 values over the two 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples indicate that another key factor plays an important role in modulating the intrinsic activity of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples rather than the particle size and metal dispersion of Ru NPs. TEM and HAADF-STEM results clearly show the formation of overlayer on the surface of Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. Thus, we propose that the remarkable difference in the activity and intrinsic activity may arise from the difference in the electronic structure in different 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples.

A renowned example of support effects occurs when reducible metal oxide supports (TiO2, Nb2O5, CeO2, and so forth) are partially reduced by hydrogen spillover from the metal nanoparticle, i.e., the so-called strong metal–support interaction (SMSI).23,24 Moreover, suboxide species are generated from the support and may migrate onto the metal nanoparticles surface to form an oxide layer upon the reduction treatment. SMSI may occur in TiO2 supported catalysts. SMSI in oxide supported metal catalysts can partially or even totally encapsulate metal particles and may affect the catalytic performance of oxide supported metal catalysts. It is widely accepted that a reduced support could transfer electrons to metal nanoparticles since partially reduced TiO2 with oxygen vacancies and interstitial titanium cations usually has a higher Fermi level than that of transition metals, leading to an electron-rich state of the metal nanoparticles.23,24 Here, an amorphous Gd2O3 overlayer on the surface of Ru NPs is clearly observed from HRTEM images of Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalyst. In contrast, there is no such overlayer on the surface of Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalyst. Thus, XPS was performed to investigate the chemical state of Ru NPs in different catalysts. The XPS analysis on the reduced and activated Ru/Gd2O3 catalyst was performed. Based on the survey XPS spectra of the Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts, the presence of N element can be excluded due to the absence of XPS peak associated with the N element in the XPS spectra of the reduced and activated Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts (Figure S5). The chemical states of the Ru, Gd, and O elements on the catalyst surface were analyzed by XPS, and the results are shown in Figure 9, Figure 1, Figure 2, Figure 3, Figure 4, Figure 5, Figure 6, Figure 7, Figure 8, Figure 9and S7, respectively. It can be seen that nearly identical Gd 4days XPS spectra can be observed for the different Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts, implying the well preservation of the structure of Gd2O3 support in different samples (Figure S6). At the same time, obvious peak at 531.8 eV in the O 1s XPS spectra of Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples may be signed to surface weakly bound oxygen species such as -OH groups and chemisorbed oxygen species (Figure S7).41,42 As shown in Figure 9, Ru 3d5/2 peak at 280.4 and 280.3 eV can be observed in the XPS spectrum of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples. The Ru 3d5/2 peak for 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R is similar to that of the highly dispersed metallic Ru NPs.12,43 By contrast, the Ru 3d5/2 binding energy of 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample is 280.5 eV, little higher than that of the 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R sample. Here, the Ru NPs are nearly identical in the Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. The little difference between the binding energy of Ru 3d5/2 peak in the Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples may be attributed to the identical Ru NPs in different electronic states, which is believed to originate from the formation of overlayer on the surface of Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. Besides the peak associated with the metallic Ru species at 280.3–280.5 eV, a broad peak at about 281.0 eV can also be observed for the Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. As the Ru NPs in all the samples have been fully reduced, the much different binding energy value of Ru 3d5/2 peak at about 280.3 and 281.0 eV can be attributed to the metallic Ru species in different electronic environment states. In particular, the Ru 3d5/2 peak at about 281.0 eV may be associated with the Ruδ+ species located at the interface between the Ru NPs and Gd2O3 support. This result demonstrates that the electronic environment of Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒A sample is much different from that of the Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples. Based on the Ru 3days XPS spectra (Figure 9), the concentration of Ruδ+ species in total surface Ru species on the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples are estimated to be about 31.6% and 41.8%. Compared to the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R samples, the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A show a relatively lower surface Ruδ+ fraction of 6.5% and 17.5% respectively. Here, substantial differences in chemical structure can be observed over the Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples even with nearly identical Ru NPs. Considering the similar Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples, the relatively lower surface Ruδ+ fraction in Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples is supposed to be caused by the formation of Gd2O3-x overlayer on the Ru NPs, which may be beneficial for the catalytic performances in NH3 decomposition.

Ru 3days core level XPS spectra of the 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒R, 1.5% Ru/Gd2O3‒A, 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒R, and 3% Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts

From all the above results, the role of the Gd2O3 overlayer on Ru NPs during the NH3 decomposition process is preliminarily analyzed. It is widely known that the size and shape of Ru NPs play a crucial role in tuning the catalytic performances of Ru-based catalysts in NH3 decomposition reaction, which can be attributed to the change of concentration of active B5 sites on the surface of Ru particles. Here, our results prove that remarkably enhanced activity and intrinsic activity for NH3 decomposition can be observed over the Gd2O3 overlayer coated Ru NPs on Gd2O3 with respect to the bare Ru NPs with similar particle sizes on Gd2O3. As a result, the formation of Gd2O3 overlayer on the Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts should be responsible for the excellent catalytic performance. For the NH3 decomposition reaction, adsorption, and activation of NH3 on the Ru-based catalysts are critical for the decomposition of NH3 and formation of H2 and N2. It is worth mentioning that the formation of a Gd2O3 overlayer upon high temperature reduction treatment will inevitably induce the creation of a high density of oxygen vacancies in the Gd2O3 overlayer in Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts. The XPS results suggested that the Gd2O3 overlayer on Ru NPs can regulate the electron state of Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. Based on all the above results, we conclude that the formation of a Gd2O3 overlayer can not only regulate the electronic structure of Ru NPs but also facilitate the adsorption and activation of NH3 molecules, which is beneficial for the decomposition of NH3 molecules on the Ru NPs with the presence of Gd2O3 overlayer. Agreeing well with the previous reports, our results demonstrate that the Gd2O3 overlayer partially covered Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts possess much enhanced activity and intrinsic activity with respect to the bare Ru NPs in Ru/Gd2O3‒R catalysts.

Conclusions

In summary, Gd2O3 supported Ru catalysts with different Ru content have been constructed by a facile precipitation method. Catalytic test results show that the activation of Ru/Gd2O3 at 500°C in NH3 stream can greatly enhance the activity of Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts for NH3 decomposition reaction. Detailed characterization results reveal that the formation of a partially covered Gd2O3-x overlayer on the surface of Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts occurred through a strong metal‒support interaction effect. In comparison with that of the bare Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3-R samples with similar particle size, the Gd2O3-x overlayer covered Ru NPs in the Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts exhibit 5–12-fold activity enhancement for the decomposition of NH3 under identical conditions. Detailed characterization results reveal that the Gd2O3-x overlayer on the Ru NPs induced by strong metal‒support interaction can modulate the electronic structure of the Ru NPs and facilitate the activation and decomposition of NH3 on the Ru NPs, thus greatly enhancing the activity and intrinsic activity of Ru/Gd2O3-A catalysts in NH3 decomposition. In view of the excellent activity and robust stability achieved by the Ru/Gd2O3‒A catalysts, this work offers a facile and efficient approach to enhance catalytic performances of reducible oxide supported Ru catalysts by tuning their strong metal‒support interaction.

Limitations of the study

Ammonia is a gas with a strong irritant odor and is toxic. Its hazards to the human body mainly include skin erosion, eye irritation, respiratory system irritation, central nervous system irritation, and damage to the liver and kidneys. In order to avoid the toxic effects of ammonia, we conducted a comprehensive inspection of the airtightness of the reaction system in the laboratory to prevent gas leakage. Due to the high activity of our catalyst, the conversion of ammonia is also very high under reaction conditions. However, due to thermodynamic limitations, the product inevitably contains slightly cool ammonia. Thus, we adopt a scheme of absorbing trace amounts of ammonia in the exhaust gas with dilute sulfuric acid before discharging it, to avoid the residual ammonia in the product from causing harm to the environment and human health.

Method details

Preparation of Gd2O3 support

Gd2O3 support was synthesized by hydrothermal method according to the previous literature. Briefly, Gd(NO3)3·6H2O (0.01 mol) was dissolved in 20 mL of deionized water. Subsequently, the pH value of solution was adjusted to about 13 by using KOH solution (1.0 M), then the white product was formed immediately. After stirring for 15 min, the mixture was transferred into a Teflon-lined stainless autoclave and treated at 180°C for 12 h. The Gd(OH)3 product was obtained via filtration, washing and drying at 80°C for about 12 h. Finally, Gd2O3 support was obtained by calcining Gd(OH)3 product at 600°C for 2 h under air atmosphere in a muffle furnace.

Preparation of Ru/Gd2O3 catalysts

Ru/Gd2O3 sample with a Ru mass loading of 1.5 wt % and 3 wt % were prepared by the precipitation of aqueous solutions of RuCl3 with K2CO3 solution. In a typical synthesis, 0.30 g of Gd2O3 was dispersed into 30 mL of water containing 0.09 g of K2CO3. After stirring for 30 min, 10 mL of RuCl3 solution (0.015 M) was slowly added to above solution under vigorous stirring. After stirring at room temperature for 2 h, the precipitates were filtered, washed with water, and dried at 80°C for 6 h. The dried catalysts (20–40 mesh) were reduced in 5% H2/Ar stream at 300°C for 2 h, and the obtained sample was denoted as x% Ru/Gd2O3‒R where x corresponding to the mass loading of Ru in Ru/Gd2O3 samples. For a comparison, the dried catalysts (20–40 mesh) were reduced in NH3 stream at 500°C for 2 h, and the obtained sample was denoted as x% Ru/Gd2O3‒A where x corresponding to the mass loading of Ru in Ru/Gd2O3 samples.

Catalyst characterization

The Ru content was determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) analysis (Optima 7300DV, PerkinElmer). Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were collected using an X’Pert Pro (PANAlytical) X-ray diffractometer. Nitrogen adsorption and desorption measurements were performed on an using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyser at −196°C. The specific surface area was calculated by the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted on a JEOL 2100X microscope. The high angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscope (HAADF-STEM) images were taken on a JEOL ARM-200F field-emission transmission electron microscope operated at 200 kV equipped with a CEOS Cs corrector on the illumination system. Compositional maps were obtained with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) using four large-solid-angle symmetrical Si drift detectors. H2 temperature-programmed reduction (H2-TPR) was carried out in a fixed-bed reactor equipped with a gas chromatograph. In each run, 50 mg of catalyst was treated in a flowing stream of 5% H2/Ar stream with a flow rate of 30 mL min−1 and the programming temperature was ramped from room temperature to 800°C with a ramping rate of 10°C min−1. The dispersion of Ru was calculated from CO chemisorption by using pulses of a gas mixture containing 10% CO in He at room temperature on an AutoChem II 2920 instrument (Micrometrics). The dried sample (100 mg) was reduced for 1 h in 10% H2/Ar (30 mL min−1) at 400°C and 500°C respectively, the different reduction temperature was used to represent the different state of Ru/Gd2O3‒R and Ru/Gd2O3‒A samples. After reduction treatment, then the sample was cooled down to 20°C and purged with He (30 mL min−1) for 20 min. A given amount of CO (50 μL) was introduced in a pulse every 5 min until the intensity of the peak reached a constant value. The Ru dispersion was calculated by assuming a CO:Ru stoichiometry of 1:1. The Quasi in situ X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) measurements for reduced samples were carried out ESCALAB250 Xi system of Thermo Fisher Scientific equipped with monochromatic Al Ka (1486.6 eV) X-ray source. The fresh sample was pressed into a clean aluminum plate and used for test. Typically, the fresh sample was heated to the set temperature of 300°C and 450°C with a ramping rate of 10°C min−1 in atmosphere and kept at the desired temperature for 2 h. After activation, the spectra of the activated sample at the Gd 4days, Ru 3days and O 1s XPS regions were collected. The calibration of all binding energies was performed by referencing C 1s to 284.8 eV. Diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) of CO adsorption measurements were performed on a Bruker TENSOR II. Prior to the measurement, the catalyst disk was exposed to 5% CO/Ar at room temperature for 5 min. Before measuring the absorbance spectrum, the cell was then purged with Ar (50 mL min−1) for 5 min to remove gaseous CO. Then, the DRIFTS spectrum of the chemisorbed CO was recorded at room temperature.

Catalytic activity test

Catalytic tests were carried out on a continuous fixed-bed flow quartz reactor. All the activity tests of catalysts were conducted under atmospheric pressure with different gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) values in NH3 stream. Product gas composition was analyzed by on-line gas chromatograph (GC-7890A, Agilent) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector and Porapak N column. The tested reaction temperature was in the range of 300∼500°C.
