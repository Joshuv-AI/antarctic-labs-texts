Evolving Trends in Outpatient Hand Surgery Utilization Across Ambulatory Surgical Centers and Hospital-Based Outpatient Departments
Authors: Troy B Amen, Omar Shareef, Kaveh A Torabian, Mitchell A Johnson, Adriana P Liimakka, Daniel A Osei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeDisparities within the field of hand surgery have been extensively described; however, little is known regarding how these disparities have evolved alongside the growth of freestanding ambulatory surgical centers (ASCs). This study sought to examine utilization trends for four common hand procedures and provide a nuanced breakdown of where disparities in the hand surgery delivery model may be arising.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted using the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project New York State Ambulatory Database from 2015 to 2022. Differences in utilization rates for four hand procedures were assessed and evaluated over time by race/ethnicity for both hospital-based outpatient departments (HOPDs) and ASCs. Poisson and logistic regressions were used to evaluate the utilization rates for these procedures and their associations with various demographic variables.ResultsBlack, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander patients were less likely to undergo all four common hand procedures at both ASCs and HOPDs compared to White patients. Similarly, patients with Medicaid insurance and those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds were less likely to be treated at ASCs. However, disparities in utilization rates decreased over time among both Black and Hispanic patients for several procedures in HOPDs when compared to White patients.ConclusionsDespite identifying differences in ASC and HOPD utilization among Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients, there were several trends that suggested these disparities are improving, particularly in HOPDs. These improvements, however, should be considered alongside persistent variability in access to ASCs for minorities, patients with Medicare and Medicaid insurance, and those of lower socioeconomic status.Clinical relevanceAs the use of ASCs for hand surgery continues to grow, surgeons should explore strategies and health care policies that expand access to these high-volume surgical centers for minority patients and individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds. This may help improve disproportionately higher patient costs incurred between facility types and overall access to surgical care for these patients.Key words: Ambulatory surgical centers (ASC), Hand surgery, Hospital outpatient departments (HOPD), Racial and ethnic disparities, Surgical Utilization

Disparities in health care utilization and outcomes based on race, ethnicity, and insurance status are well documented and persistent across various fields of medicine, including hand surgery.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 The field of hand surgery, specifically, is no exception to these trends.6,7 Prior studies have demonstrated that Black and Hispanic patients not only exhibit lower rates of surgical utilization but also experience longer wait times from initial consultation to surgery and poorer functional outcomes following certain hand surgery procedures.6, 7, 8, 9, 10 These findings are thought to be due to a complex array of factors including a patient’s direct geographical access to care,11 their socioeconomic status,6,12 social support networks,4 perceptions regarding surgery,13 as well as their surgeons’ treatment recommendation.7

To date, there is limited data on how these disparities in care have evolved alongside the rapid growth of freestanding ambulatory surgical centers (ASCs) and whether restrictive patterns of access to these facilities exist based on various sociodemographic variables such as race and insurance type. Consistent with other surgical subspecialties,14 a growing proportion of hand surgeries are taking place in freestanding ASCs in place of hospital-based outpatient departments (HOPDs).15 This transition is supported by numerous studies demonstrating that common hand procedures can be safely performed at freestanding ASCs and provide cost-effective care by decreasing patient length of stay and increasing operational surgical efficiency.16,17 Additionally, legislative changes in Medicare reimbursements have also further incentivized ASC utilization.18 While the reduced cost of care associated with ASCs carries a clear potential to help eliminate barriers to care for underserved populations, no studies have previously assessed how these trends have evolved in patients undergoing hand surgery.

Thus, the purpose of this study was to (1) analyze rates of ASC utilization for four common outpatient hand procedures, including open carpal tunnel release (CTR), endoscopic CTR, open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the distal radius, and trigger finger release (TFR), by race and insurance status and (2) assess how differences in ASC utilization have evolved over time across racial and ethnic groups and insurance groups. We hypothesized that (1) Black, Hispanic, and Asian/Pacific Islander patients would be less likely to undergo one of the aforementioned hand procedures at both ASCs and HOPD compared with White patients, and (2) increased utilization of ASCs among minority patients would lead to decreased disparities over time.

Methods

Data source

A retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project New York State Ambulatory Surgery and Services Database (NYSASD) from the years 2015–2022. The NYSASD is one of the largest outpatient databases with encounter-level data for all hospital and privately owned ambulatory centers in New York state. This database has been successfully used in numerous prior studies and contains over 90 patient and hospital related variables per patient index procedure.14,19

Patients who underwent four common outpatient hand procedures including open CTR, endoscopic CTR, TFR, and distal radius ORIF were queried using their respective common procedural terminology codes. Encounters were further stratified by outpatient service type and categorized as occurring at a freestanding ASC or HOPD. Demographic patient data were collected, including race (White, Black, Asian/Pacific Islander, Other), ethnicity (non-Hispanic, Hispanic), age, sex, insurance type (Medicare, Medicaid, Private), median household income by quartile, and urban-rural designation of residence (using National Center for Health Statistics designation schema).20 Patients who underwent multiple procedures during the index encounter were excluded from the study. These four procedures were selected because they collectively represent some of the most commonly performed hand procedures in the ambulatory setting, are routinely performed in both ASCs and HOPDs, and are reliably identified by common procedural terminology codes in administrative data. Although distal radius ORIF is performed in the setting of acute trauma, the surgery itself is overwhelmingly performed in a scheduled outpatient setting and is frequently performed at freestanding ASCs. This focused approach preserved adequate sample sizes for stratified and multivariable comparisons and minimized the clinical and coding heterogeneity that would accompany a larger set of procedures.

Disparities and utilization rate analysis

Differences in demographic and surgical center characteristics (ie, ASC vs HOPD) were compared by race and ethnicity. Utilization rates for four common hand surgeries were standardized across racial groups by dividing yearly population estimates from the NYSASD by yearly New York census data estimates for each group.21 This approach yields population-based utilization rates that account for the underlying differences in racial composition of the New York State population. Standardized utilization rates were then plotted over time, and trends were assessed using Poisson regression. The model included an interaction term between race and calendar year and adjusted for age, sex, insurance, socioeconomic status, and Charlson comorbidity scores, yielding adjusted rate ratios (ARRs), as performed in previous studies.14,22

Adjusted demographics analysis

A multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between patient and demographic factors and the likelihood of being treated at a freestanding ASC compared to an HOPD. The analysis was adjusted for sex, age group, race, primary payer, income quartile of the patient's zip code, National Center for Health Statistics urban-rural classification, and Charlson comorbidity score. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each independent variable to determine the likelihood of receiving treatment at a freestanding ASC. Statistical significance was defined as P <.05. This study was exempt from institutional review board approval given its use of publicly available deidentified data.

Results

A total of 345,265 patients were included in this study (Table 1). A comprehensive summary of patient demographics broken down by ASC and HOPD utilization can be found in Table 1. After adjusting for several demographic variables including age, sex, insurance status, socioeconomic status, comorbidities and urbanicity, we found several disparities in ASC utilization. Black patients were significantly less likely to receive their care at an ASC compared with White patients (OR: 0.66, 95% CI: 0.64−0.79, P <.001). Similar trends were also found for Hispanic (OR: 0.45, 95% CI: 0.45−0.48, P <.001), and Asian patients (OR: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.47−0.54, P <.001; Table 2). Private pay (OR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.34−1.41, P <.001) and self-pay patients (OR: 2.75, 95% CI: 2.60−2.91, P <.001) were also significantly more likely to be treated at ASCs compared to patients on Medicare (Table 2). Finally, patients with higher median incomes were more likely to receive their care at an ASC compared to patients from the lowest median income bracket (2nd income quartile OR 1.05 (1.02−1.08), and 3rd income quartile OR 1.27 (1.23−1.30), P <.001 for both). Temporal trend assessing adjusted utilization rates

Between 2015 and 2022, Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients were less likely to undergo all four common hand procedures at freestanding ASCs compared to White patients (ARR < 1.0 for Black, Hispanic, and Asian patients vs White patients across the entire study period). Temporal trend analysis demonstrated that most disparities in ASC utilization were relatively stable over time, even after adjusting for patient demographics. However, there was evidence of modest change for Hispanic patients undergoing endoscopic CTR (Ptrend =.048) and Asian patients undergoing TFR (Ptrend =.037), although underutilization persisted even in 2022. Discussion

Racial and ethnic disparities have been well documented throughout the orthopedic1,2,4,5,23 and hand surgery literature6 with specific examples, including differences in utilization and/or outcomes after distal radius fracture surgical fixation,8 CTR,7 total elbow arthroplasty,24 and digit replantation.25 However, less is known regarding how these disparities have evolved in conjunction with the growth of freestanding ASCs. This study evaluated over 300,000 patients undergoing one of four common hand procedures between 2015 and 2022 and found that significant disparities are still present in the relative utilization of hand procedures performed at both HOPD and ASC. In this study, we found that Black and Hispanic patients were significantly less likely to have their hand surgeries performed at ASCs (vs HOPDs) compared to their White counterparts. Similarly, patients with Medicare and Medicaid insurance and patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds were also less likely to be treated at ASCs. Although Black and Hispanic patients had lower standardized utilization rates for all hand surgeries performed at both ASCs and HOPDs, there were encouraging improvements in disparities for minority patients being treated at HOPDs over time. Additional research is needed to better understand the factors that are influencing ASC utilization and the referral patterns for patients who are receiving care at these freestanding high-volume surgical centers.

To our knowledge, this is the first study in hand surgery to investigate disparities in ASC utilization and provide a nuanced breakdown of where disparities in the hand surgery delivery model may be arising. Given increasing utilization of ASCs in the field of hand surgery,15 understanding differential access to care in this emerging setting may be critical in helping guide future policy and targeting disparities in care. In this study, we found that overall utilization rates at ASCs were lower for Black and Hispanic patients, and that these patients were also less likely to be treated at ASCs (vs HOPDs) than their White counterparts. Despite these disparities, we found that the standardized utilization rates of four common hand procedures by Black and Hispanic patients at ASCs were in fact increasing over time. These trends likely speak to the results of several initiatives designed to expand access to ASCs in New York State.14,26 Chief among these was New York’s 2015 release of “A Path Toward Value-Based Payment: Roadmap for Medicaid Payment Reform,” which aimed to transition 80% to 90% of Medicaid managed care payments from fee-for-service to value-based models by 2020.27 This initiative included the Delivery System Reform Incentive Payment program, which funded projects to expand outpatient infrastructure, enhance care coordination, and improve service delivery to safety-net populations. By restructuring financial incentives to reward quality and equity-focused outcomes, this roadmap may have encouraged health systems to refer more Medicaid beneficiaries, including Black and Hispanic patients, to efficient, lower-cost settings such as ASCs, thereby promoting more equitable access to ambulatory surgical care.27

In this study, we also found that disparities in surgical utilization at HOPDs were improving for Black and Hispanic patients over time when compared to White patients. This pattern was particularly striking for endoscopic CTR, where the year-over-year ARR for Black patients more than doubled from 0.20 in 2015 to 0.50 in 2022 (Ptrend = .006), with similarly meaningful trajectories observed for Hispanic and Asian patients. These findings are some of the first highlighting improvements in disparities in the field of hand surgery and build on several prior studies in other surgical subspecialties that have suggested improvements in racial and ethnic disparities in care.14,28 One possible explanation is that HOPDs are often embedded within larger hospital-based systems, including academic medical centers, safety-net hospitals, emergency departments, and Medicaid-serving referral networks, which may allow them to serve a broader and more socioeconomically diverse patient population. Conversely, freestanding ASCs may remain more sensitive to geographic distribution, insurance mix, referral patterns, and facility-level patient selection. Although these mechanisms could not be directly evaluated in the present study, they may help explain why disparities narrowed for select procedures in HOPDs while remaining more persistent in ASCs.29, 30, 31 Additional work is needed to understand the forces fueling these improvements in HOPD care in hopes of trying to mirror these improvements at freestanding ASCs.

Several additional barriers likely play a role in helping determine access to ambulatory surgical care such as insurance and socioeconomic status. In this study, we found that patients with Medicare and Medicaid insurance and patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds were less likely to be treated at ASCs. These findings have been corroborated in several other surgical subspecialties and highlight the importance of insurance and socioeconomic status in dictating facilities to which patients have access.11,28,32, 33, 34, 35 These findings may be due to the fact that ASC construction has been favored in wealthier neighborhoods and in more affluent counties in New York State, possibly making some of these facilities geographically inaccessible to lower-resourced patients.11 It may also point to bias in patient selection from physician groups that privately own ASCs and have a financial incentive to treat patients from higher socioeconomic backgrounds.36 Regardless of these underlying causes, expansionary policies that incentivize physician groups to treat patients from a broad spectrum of socioeconomic and insurance backgrounds may help increase access to hand surgery centers and minimize bias in patient selection and facility referrals.37,38

While these disparities have several implications for our overall health care-delivery model, it is also important to understand the direct implications as they relate to patient experience and payment. Benefits of undergoing surgery at ASCs may include improved efficiency,39 decreased case time,40 high safety profile,41 and theoretically decreased cost of care.42 However, it should also be noted that the cost of surgical care for patients varies based on facility type, type of procedure, and insurance type. For all procedures evaluated in this study, the patient would be expected to pay, on average, a lower amount if receiving surgical care at an ASC compared to a HOPD according to Medicare procedure price listings.43 For example, a patient with Medicare insurance would pay almost 40% less out of pocket if undergoing CTR at an ASC compared to a HOPD ($266 at ASC vs $454 at HOPD).43 These findings highlight important financial implications for minority patients and patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds who may already be at higher risk of experiencing financial stress from their medical/surgical expenses.44,45

While strengths of this study should be noted, including a high-quality data set encompassing one of the largest outpatient databases with both freestanding ASC and HOPD level information, several limitations should also be discussed. First, despite adjusting for comorbidities using the Charlson comorbidity index, administrative data sets often lack the full granular data necessary to account for the complex medical profiles which may inherently limit ASC eligibility. Consequently, unmeasured confounding may persist, particularly among patients with lower socioeconomic status who often carry a higher burden of disease.46,47 Second, this analysis relies on realized surgical utilization and does not account for any upstream barriers that may prevent patients with operative indications from ever reaching surgical evaluation. Third, this database only includes patients in New York State and thus may not be generalizable to other regions with varying demographics or regulatory landscapes. Fourth, our analysis was limited to ambulatory surgical settings and could not directly compare utilization against inpatient or office-based settings. While this limitation was mitigated by calculating population-based utilization ratios, the growing shift of select hand procedures to office-based settings represents an important and evolving gap in the literature which should be explored. Fifth, as is inherent to utilization-based disparity research, this study could not differentiate between underutilization by minority patients and over-utilization by White patients. Sixth, the procedures included in this study differ in indication and acuity. Although CTR, endoscopic CTR, and TFR are generally elective, distal radius ORIF is commonly performed after an acute injury and may be shaped by distinct referral patterns, timing constraints, injury severity, emergency department access, and trauma-care pathways. As a result, disparities in distal radius ORIF utilization may reflect different access mechanisms than those observed for elective hand procedures. However, the directional consistency of our findings across all four procedures suggests that procedural heterogeneity is unlikely to fully account for the observed disparities. Last, the clinical importance of the small absolute rate differences in this study reported on a yearly basis should be interpreted cautiously. Relative and cumulative changes over the study period and potential population-level consequences are likely meaningful but further work may be needed to link changes in utilization to patient-level outcomes.

In conclusion, this study finds that while disparities exist regarding utilization of outpatient hand/wrist surgical care based on race, these disparities may be lessening over time. We note that differences in utilization for HOPDs for Black and Hispanic patients were declining over our study period, specifically in patients undergoing endoscopic CTR, with additional improvements observed for Asian/Pacific Islander patients undergoing open CTR. Interestingly, these trends were less apparent in patients undergoing outpatient surgery at free standing ambulatory surgery centers, where disparities remained more persistent. Further evaluation into barriers to access for outpatient surgical care at both freestanding and hospital-based outpatient surgical care facilities is warranted.
