Young Adults’ Knowledge and Perceptions of Permanent Noise-Induced Tinnitus and its Influence on Behavioural Intentions
Authors: Lucy Runciman, Christine Johnson
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objective:Young adults’ music-listening behaviours may put them at risk of developing permanent hearing loss and tinnitus. This study aimed to assess knowledge of permanent tinnitus and whether this knowledge may influence listening behaviours to a greater degree than knowledge of hearing loss.Materials and Methods:A two-group (between subjects), randomised post-test only, single-factor experimental design was used to determine the effects of health message focus (permanent tinnitus vs. permanent hearing loss) on the main outcome measure of protective behavioural intention. The sample included 109 male and 287 female adults, aged 18 to 25 years, living in the United Kingdom. The young adults’ existing knowledge of either permanent hearing loss or permanent tinnitus was measured, and after exposure to a health message, their perceptions and resultant behavioural intentions were assessed and compared. The pooled data were used to test a proposed model of factors influencing hearing protective behavioural intention using path analysis.Results:Whilst 83% knew about the link with hearing loss, only 75% knew that loud music may cause permanent tinnitus. The participants viewed tinnitus as closer temporally than hearing loss, perceived themselves as equally susceptible to both, but perceived hearing loss as more severe. There was no significant difference in behavioural intention between the groups.Conclusion:Perceived susceptibility and severity have a positive effect on behavioural intentions. In light of these results, recommendations for future noise-damage prevention campaigns are made.

INTRODUCTION

Tinnitus, defined as the perception of a sound in the absence of an external sound source,[1] can be experienced either permanently, if it is continuous or intermittent, or temporarily, if it does not recur.[23] The phenotype of tinnitus is thought to be influenced by environmental, genetic and lifestyle factors.[4] Similar to hearing loss, exposure to noise is a significant risk factor for developing tinnitus.[567] Young adults who expose themselves to unsafe music intensities are at risk of developing tinnitus.[789101112]

Permanent tinnitus (PT) can be a distressing condition and has many negative physical and psychological effects. Among young people with PT, 62% report difficulty with concentration,[13] and the condition has been linked to anxiety, depression, reduced sleep and missed work days.[14] PT has also been associated with increased thoughts of suicide,[9] poorer central auditory processing[15] and increased listening effort.[16] As young adults are often in education or beginning of their careers, the consequences of these effects may be far-reaching.

Tinnitus is an incurable condition, and management strategies and counseling/therapies are used with varying success rates.[17] Referrals to the National Health Service (NHS) in the United Kingdom for bothersome tinnitus are increasing annually,[18] and the current estimated annual cost to the NHS for tinnitus therapies is £750 million per year.[17] Prevention of noise-induced tinnitus is crucial due to its potential negative impact on quality of life and the difficulty and cost of effective treatment. The frequency of temporary tinnitus (TT) increases with increasing music exposure at concerts/clubs, use of personal listening devices (PLDs)[19] and increasing lifetime leisure noise exposure.[20] Transient noise-induced tinnitus is considered a precursor to chronic or permanent noise-induced tinnitus. The reported prevalence of PT in young adults varies between studies, ranging from 4.2% to 14.8%,[62122232425] which is higher than the prevalence of permanent hearing loss in this age group.[19]

Protective behaviours such as reducing the frequency of noise exposure, reducing the volume of personal listening devices (PLDs), wearing hearing protection devices like earplugs in loud music venues and taking ‘regeneration breaks’ in loud environments can help prevent permanent noise-induced tinnitus in the same way as permanent noise-induced hearing loss.[26] Although there have been many noise-damage prevention campaigns that focus on permanent hearing loss (PHL) prevention, their effectiveness is often limited,[1027] and despite high levels of knowledge of PHL among young adults, few engage in protective behaviours.[28293031]

Many studies on preventing noise-induced damage have focused primarily on PHL and the need to avoid it, often overlooking awareness and avoidance of tinnitus (PT) as a secondary or warning symptom.[71129323334] However, some researchers suggest that since tinnitus is experienced more frequently by young adults and may be more noticeable than temporary hearing loss, health messages emphasizing tinnitus prevention may have a greater impact than those focusing solely on PHL.[203335] Despite their knowledge of PHL, young adults still lack engagement with hearing protective behaviours, which highlights the need for investigating the level of awareness of PT in this population and whether increased awareness may encourage better listening behaviours.

To date, there has been no research conducted to determine the extent of young adults’ knowledge regarding the potential permanent condition caused by noise-induced tinnitus (TT), and therefore, it is unclear whether this knowledge alone would influence their listening behaviours. However, several studies have examined how experience, fear and information about tinnitus impact attitudes towards noise and listening behaviours.[1122243336373839404142] From reviewing this literature, it appears that experiencing or fearing PT can prompt changes in behaviour. However, most individuals with TT are unaware of the potential risk of developing a permanent condition. Despite this, there is still a lack of quantitative research measuring young adults’ awareness of PT, and there is no quantitative evidence indicating whether knowledge of PT would lead to greater changes in listening behaviour than knowledge of PHL.

To determine whether knowledge of PT might have a different effect on behaviour compared to knowledge of PHL, we can refer to predictive models of health behaviour. In the Health Belief Model it is stated that the decision to engage in a preventative behaviour is influenced by perceived severity of the condition, perceived susceptibility to the condition, benefits of the behaviour, barriers to the behaviour and external cues to action.[43] Protection Motivation theory predicts that the likelihood of performing a protective behaviour is increased by perceived severity of the threat, perceived susceptibility to the threat, perceived response-efficacy and perceived self-efficacy.[44] An expanded version also includes the concept that the likelihood is decreased by perceived rewards of the maladaptive behaviour and perceived barriers or costs.[45] Both models have been used to explore and explain noise-damage prevention behaviours,[32333446474849] although none of the studies specifically address PT as the condition to be avoided. As the actions or behaviour required to prevent PT and PHL are the same, it might be expected that perceived self-efficacy, perceived response-efficacy and benefits, rewards of the maladaptive behaviour and perceived benefits, barriers or costs will not differ depending on which condition a person is aiming to prevent. However, the perceived severity of and susceptibility to the respective condition may differ. Evidence suggests that perceived susceptibility to PHL in young adults is low, which has been used to explain the low uptake of protective behaviours despite widespread knowledge of PHL.[4647]

There are two concepts that can potentially explain why individuals may feel more susceptible to PT than PHL. Previous experience of a condition has been shown to influence perceived susceptibility,[505152] and young adults seem to experience temporary tinnitus more frequently than permanent hearing loss.[192142] Additionally, perceived psychological or temporal distance is another construct that can affect perceived susceptibility.[53] Adolescents perceive tinnitus as more likely to affect them in the short-term than hearing loss.[54] As hearing loss is often viewed as a condition that only affects older adults, it is perceived to be temporally distant and therefore less likely to occur.[55] It has been recommended that preventative campaigns focus on symptoms that are viewed as temporally proximal to increase the effectiveness of the message.[56] In a study examining the impact of hearing health messages on protective behavioural intention, exposure to a tinnitus and hypersensitivity message resulted in a significant increase in intention, while there was no change for a hearing loss message.[54] However, the study did not distinguish between permanent and temporary tinnitus or assess the number of participants who knew that temporary tinnitus may become permanent. It is possible that exposure to a permanent tinnitus message may further influence behaviour due to the likely perception of permanent tinnitus being more severe than temporary tinnitus.

Current study and hypotheses

This study aims to investigate young adults’ awareness of permanent noise-induced tinnitus (PT) and explore how health messages focused on PT versus permanent noise-induced hearing loss (PHL) influence their intention to adopt noise-damage prevention behaviours. The study tests a theoretical model derived from health behaviour models and the above literature [Figure 1]. One group will receive a PT message, while another group will receive a PHL message. The study tests several hypotheses related to knowledge of PT, factors influencing perceived susceptibility and behavioural intention and health message effects. Understanding young adults’ perceptions of PT and PHL can guide the development of effective educational campaigns and reminder messages in risk areas.

Proposed model of perceived susceptibility and perceived severity influences on hearing protective behavioural intention.

The following hypotheses are tested:

H1: Knowledge of PT. Fewer young adults are aware of permanent noise-induced tinnitus than are aware of permanent noise-induced hearing loss.

H2: Factors influencing perceived susceptibility. H2a: Closer (higher) perceived temporal distance will lead to higher perceived susceptibility. H2b: More frequent previous experience of the condition will lead to higher perceived susceptibility.

H3: Factors influencing behavioural intention. H3a: Higher perceived susceptibility will lead to greater behavioural intention. H3b: Higher perceived severity will lead to greater behavioural intention.

H4: Health message effects. Young adults considering PT will exhibit shorter perceived temporal distance to the condition (H4a), have more frequent previous experience of the transient symptom (H4b), and show greater perceived susceptibility (H4c) but equal perceived severity (H4d) to the condition, than those considering PHL. This will result in greater protective behavioural intentions (H4e) in those exposed to the PT message than those exposed to the PHL message.

METHODS

Procedure and measurement

A two-group (between-subjects) experimental design with a random assignment to either a PT or PHL focus was used. The main outcome measure was protective behavioural intention, which was assessed through two online questionnaires. The study was conducted with the approval of Queen Margaret University and all participants gave consent as part of data collection. Participants first provided demographic information and were asked to indicate their pre-existing knowledge of the focus condition. The health message was then presented followed by measurement of the five outcome variables: perceived temporal distance, previous experience of the temporary version of the condition, perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, and protective behavioural intention. At the end of the questionnaire, participants’ knowledge of the alternative condition was assessed. The pooled knowledge data were used to test hypothesis H1, while the pooled outcome measures were used to test hypotheses 2 and 3 (H2 and H3) based on the proposed concept model. Finally, to test hypothesis H4, the outcome measures were compared between the PT and PHL focus groups to assess the effect of the message focus on the outcome variables.

Participants

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants for the study. Recruitment efforts included internal postings at the host University and postings on social media platforms, such as Facebook, targeting young adults between the ages of 18 and 25 who resided in the United Kingdom. Participants were self-selected and assigned to either the PT or PHL group by clicking on a single link in the study advertisement, which randomly redirected them to one of the two questionnaires. Of the 580 initial participant responses received, 184 were excluded due to various factors (pre-existing PT and/or PHL; non-disclosure of gender; non-participation in the behavioural outcomes being assessed; study of a subject which may increase knowledge of the conditions above that of the general population, e.g., audiology, medicine), resulting in a total of 396 valid responses being retained for data analysis. Of the participants, 109 (27.5%) were males and 287 (72.5%) were females. The PT group consisted of 197 participants, with 56 (28.8%) being males, and the PHL group consisted of 199 participants, with 53 (26.6%) being males.

Online questionnaires and health messages

As no validated form existed for measuring the specific combination of constructs in this study, questions which have been used to measure these concepts separately, were gathered from the literature. To minimize mono-method bias, several questions were used to measure each construct wherever possible. The selected questions focused on both PHL and PT and used the same wording in both questionnaires. A 7-point Likert scale was used to measure each answer to the construct measure questions, where 4 represented a neutral response, and a higher number indicated a greater, higher, or more immediate measure of the perception. Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to compare the knowledge of permanent hearing loss and permanent tinnitus. Both groups of participants were asked about their knowledge of both conditions, and a related samples McNemar test was conducted using IBM SPSS v23 statistics vs 23, to examine if the difference in the proportion of those who knew about tinnitus was significantly different from those who knew about hearing loss.[60] The independent variable was the condition (permanent tinnitus vs. permanent hearing loss), and the dependent variable was knowledge (yes vs. no).

Path analysis of the pooled data from both groups was undertaken using IBM SPSS Amos, to test Hypotheses 2 and 3 (H2 and H3) (i.e., the proposed concept model). Path analysis is an extension of multiple regression which can be used to test models where there are chains of effects.[61] Exogenous variables in the analysis were perceived temporal distance, perceived experience of the condition, and perceived severity. Endogenous variables were perceived susceptibility and behavioural intention. Coefficients of each path represented by the hypotheses were calculated and their significance assessed. The coefficient of determination (r2) was calculated for each endogenous variable to assess to what degree variances in the contributing variables explained the variance in the scores. Model fit indices, including normed fit index (NFI), comparative fit index (CFI), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA), goodness of fit index (GFI) and Akaike’s information criterion (AIC), were examined to assess how well the data fits the model.[62] The indices were also used to test a modified model suggested by the IBM SPSS Amos software. Although path analysis cannot establish causality, it can be used to establish whether the data fits the concept model.[61]

To test hypothesis H4, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted with the independent variable MESSAGE FOCUS (with two groups: permanent tinnitus vs. permanent hearing loss) to determine if the differences in the mean group scores for perceptions and behavioural intentions were statistically significant. Wilks’ Lambda was used to test the statistical significance of the differences between the PT and PHL groups at the multivariate level. Univariate analysis was then performed in the form of one-way ANOVAs on each dependent variable to investigate hypotheses H4a/H4b/H4c/H4d/H4e, and a Bonferroni correction was applied.

RESULTS

Most participants were aware of the link between loud music and both permanent tinnitus (95.7%) and temporary hearing loss (94.9%). 183, or 92.8% of the 197 participants in PT group had experienced TT after exposure to loud music, whereas in the PHL group only 135, or 67.8% of the 199 participants had any experience of temporary hearing loss.

Hypothesis H1: Knowledge of permanent tinnitus

For hypothesis H1, 24.4% of participants did not know about permanent tinnitus and 17.1% did not know about permanent hearing loss. A McNemar’s test with continuity correction and a confidence level of 0.95 showed that significantly fewer 18–25-year-olds knew about permanent tinnitus than permanent hearing loss (χ2(1) = 4.762, asymptotic P = 0.001), leading to acceptance of hypothesis H1.

Hypotheses

Proposed concept model

Path analysis was carried out on the pooled data, with exogenous variables being perceived temporal distance, perceived experience of condition and perceived severity, and endogenous variables being perceived susceptibility and behavioural intention. Results (3df) indicated an oversaturated and therefore statistically significant model, with perceived temporal distance having a positive effect on susceptibility, but previous experience not having a significant effect. Analysis revealed that perceived temporal distance had a significant positive effect on susceptibility, β = 0.502, SE = 0.044, P < 0.0005, but previous experience score did not have a significant effect on susceptibility, β = 0.064, SE= 0.31, P = 0.159. Perceived susceptibility had a significant positive effect on behavioural intention, β = 0.314, S.E. = 0.053, P < 0.0005, as did perceived severity, β = 0.153, S.E. = 0.063, P = 0.001. Despite these results, the coefficient of determination for behavioural intention was small, R2 = 0.12, suggesting that the model accounted for only 12% of the variance in behavioural intention. The coefficient of determination for perceived susceptibility was larger (R2 = 0.28). Model fit indices were mixed between an acceptable and unacceptable fit (χ2 = 21.891, df = 3, P < 0.0005, CFI = 0.923, NFI = 0.914, TLI = 0.743, RMSEA = 0.126. AIC 45.891, GFI = 0.979).[58] Results are shown in Figure 2.

Path analysis model showing standardised path coefficients on each path and coefficients of determination for the endogenous variables.

On running path analysis, an alteration to the concept model that resulted in a better model fit was highlighted. Previous experience of the condition was unlinked from perceived susceptibility, and instead a direct path leading to behavioural intention was found. Previous experience was then found to have a significant negative effect on behavioural intention, β = −0.111, SE= 0.37, P = 0.020, and the model fit indices improved, with a mix between good and unacceptable fit (χ2 = 18.645, df = 3, P < 0.0005, CFI = 0.936, NFI = 0.972, TLI = 0.787, RMSEA = 0.115. AIC 42.645, GFI = 0.982). The coefficient of determination for behavioural intention was also higher, R2 = 0.14, with 14% of the variance now explained by the model. Results from this altered model are shown in Figure 3.

In summary, the hypothesis H2a that closed perceived temporal distance leads to higher perceived susceptibility was accepted, as were hypotheses H3a and H3b which stated that higher perceived severity and higher perceived susceptibility lead to higher behavioural intention. However, hypothesis H2b was not accepted, as more experience of the condition did not lead to significantly higher perceived susceptibility. Analysis of the variables indicated that more experience of the condition actually led to lower behavioural intention.

Hypothesis 4: Health message effects

Hypothesis 4 was tested using a a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA), which found a significant difference between the conditions on the combined dependent variables (F(5, 390) = 31.261, P < 0.0005; Wilks’ Λ = 0.714; partial η2 = 0.286). Univariate post-hoc analysis, in the form of one-way ANOVA, was conducted on each dependent variable to investigate hypotheses H4a/H4b/H4c/H4d/H4e.

Results revealed that participants perceived PHL to be significantly more temporally distant than PT (Mtinnitus =3.579, SD = 1.211 vs. Mhearing loss = 3.111, SD =1.170, F(1, 394) = 15.302, P <.0005; partial η2 = 0.037), and participants in the tinnitus group had experienced the symptom significantly more frequently than those in the hearing loss group (Mtinnitus = 3.868, SD = 1.610 vs. Mhearing loss = 2.538, SD = 1.543, F(1, 394) = 70.437, P < 0.0005; partial η2 = 152). Participants in the PT group reported greater perceived susceptibility than those in the PHL group, but the difference was not statistically significant (Mtinnitus = 5.154, SD = 1.150 vs. Mhearing loss = 4.971, SD = 1.217, F(1, 394) = 2.350, P = 0.126; partial η2 = 0.006). Perceived severity was significantly higher in the PHL group than in the PT group (Mtinnitus = 5.155, SD = 0.9933 vs. Mhearing loss = 5.913, SD = 0.873, F(1, 394) = 64.947, P < 0.0005; partial η2 = 0.142). Despite PHL being viewed as more severe than PT, participants exposed to the PT health message reported greater intention to change their behaviour than those in the PHL group, but this difference was not statistically significant (Mtinnitus = 3.599, SD = 1.396 vs. Mhearing loss = 3.512, SD = 1.302, F(1, 394) = 0.415, P = 0.520; partial η2 = 0.001).

In summary, participants in the PT group exhibited closer perceived temporal distance and greater previous experience of the condition, so hypotheses H4a and H4b were accepted. However, they exhibited lower perceived severity and there was no significant difference between the groups in perceived susceptibility or behavioural intention following the interventions. Hypotheses H4c, H4d, and H4e could not therefore be accepted.

DISCUSSION AND RECOMMENDATIONS

Knowledge of PT

Although a previous study on British students found that almost all (90%) knew that loud music can cause PHL,[30] in the current study this figure was lower at only 82.9%. The previous study had a higher percentage of medical students (78%), who may have been more knowledgeable about the risks of noise damage to hearing, which could explain the higher levels of knowledge. The current study showed that fewer young adults were aware of PT, indicating the need for further education about the risks of both PHL and PT in this age group. Nevertheless, the majority of young adults in the sample (75.6%) were aware that loud music can cause PT, which is a positive development. This contrasts with Hunter’s qualitative study, which found that "many" young adults were not aware of PT.[55] It is possible that knowledge about PT has become more widespread in recent years.

Proposed concept model

Consistent with previous research on behavioural models and hearing loss prevention,[3348] both perceived susceptibility and severity had a positive impact on behavioural intention, as hypothesised in the current study. However, these findings contrast with those of Keppler et al.[10] who found no significant effect of perceived susceptibility and severity on earplug use. This discrepancy may be due to the actual correlation coefficient calculated in the current study, as opposed to the grouping method employed by Keppler et al., as well as the wider range of behavioural intention outcomes used.

Notably, perceived susceptibility had a larger effect size than severity in the current study (β = 0.314 vs. β = 0.153), suggesting that campaigns aimed at promoting protective behaviour should focus more on emphasising individual susceptibility than the severity of the condition. This finding is unique, as previous studies did not report results in terms of effect size. Perceived temporal distance also had a significant effect on susceptibility, emphasising the importance of selecting symptoms that are perceived as being more temporally proximal. Campaigns could also highlight the fact that PT and hearing loss can occur at any age, not just in old age, by featuring younger people with these conditions or by informing young adults of the high prevalence in their age group.

Although the current study did not attempt to manipulate perceived temporal distance, it is an important construct to consider in future research. Perhaps a study featuring a temporal aspect in the health message could investigate whether this influences behavioural intention to a greater degree. However, temporal distance only accounted for 28% of the variability of perceived susceptibility, indicating the need for further research to explore other factors that may be influencing this construct. As perceived temporal distance is difficult to manipulate, identifying other constructs that are more amenable to education could be helpful.

The results of this study did not indicate a significant relationship between previous experience of the condition and susceptibility. This finding is consistent with previous research in the field.[3341] However, it was expected that informing participants about the risk of tinnitus becoming permanent would change their perception of the transient condition, and thus alter their susceptibility. Unfortunately, this was not the case, and the modified concept model showed that previous experience of tinnitus or hearing loss actually had a negative impact on behavioural intention. One possible explanation for this unexpected result is that people who enjoy loud music may attend events more frequently and therefore experience the transient symptom more often. These individuals may be less likely to change their behaviour if music is seen as their ‘passion’. Additionally, those who are inclined towards risk-taking may experience these symptoms more frequently and are less likely to engage in protective behaviours. Qualitative investigations have also suggested that the symptoms of noise damage are often associated with a good time, indicating "sensation seeking behavior." [37] This type of behaviour has been documented in relation to loud music, as well as in other health fields.[42]

The low coefficient of determination for behavioural intention in both the original and altered model, which explained only 12% and 14% of the variance, suggests that factors beyond the model had a substantial impact on intention. Societal norms, perceived barriers, perceived response efficacy and self-efficacy, costs, and the rewards of maladaptive behaviour are just a few examples of additional factors that may have played a significant role in the participants’ decision-making, as these factors have been shown to be influential in health behaviour models. A meta-analysis of the health belief model (HBM) found that perceived barriers and benefits had a greater impact than perceived susceptibility and severity.[63] However, even when all of the constructs in a model are included, models typically do not account for all of the variance. For instance, Vogel et al. found that even with all of the constructs of the PMT model included, only 25.7% of the variance in intention was accounted for.[47] In this study, the rewards of maladaptive behaviour, perceived response efficacy and self-efficacy were found to be more strongly correlated with behavioural intention than perceived susceptibility and severity. Using a proposed model of hearing protective behaviours based on the Theory of Planned Behaviour rather than HBM or PMT, Widén found that societal norms and barriers were the strongest predictors of intention, although this study focused only on earplug use.[41] In the same group of students, Landälv et al. found that risk-taking, social norms and risk judgement explained 32.1% in the variance of the teenagers’ attitudes towards noise, showing that these factors are also important contributors to young people’s behaviour,[40] and both Hunter and Gilles et al. found that peer influence had a considerable effect on earplug use.[2237]

Perceptions and health message effects

Although the participants in this study perceived PT to be closer in time and had more experience with the temporary condition, they did not exhibit significantly greater behavioural intention towards the tinnitus message than the hearing loss message. This may be because they viewed hearing loss as a more severe condition compared to tinnitus. The health messages in this study did not aim to influence the participants’ pre-existing perceived severity of each condition, but rather highlighted that neither condition could be cured. To prompt more behaviour change, future health messages could emphasise the consequences of PT, such as increased risk of anxiety and depression, increased listening effort, and difficulty concentrating and sleeping, thus increasing young adults’ perceived severity of tinnitus.

The lack of significant difference in intention may also be attributed to the influence of other constructs not included in the concept model investigated in this study. These external factors may have a greater impact on behaviour, regardless of whether the health message focuses on tinnitus or hearing loss, nullifying the minor impact of differences in perceived susceptibility and severity. Nevertheless, it is encouraging that the mean scores for both PT and PHL were higher than a neutral 4, indicating that this group of young adults recognizes their susceptibility to the conditions and views them as relatively severe. Past studies have expressed concern that adolescents and young adults may not fully appreciate the severity of the consequences of noise damage or perceive themselves as vulnerable to damage or both.[33464864]

Although the young adults in the study demonstrated an understanding of the severity and personal susceptibility to PT and hearing loss, their mean score for behavioural intention was below neutral, with 3.60 in the tinnitus group and 3.1 in the hearing loss group. Interestingly, the mean score was lowered by their reluctance to wear earplugs, with a mean score of 2.3. However, the participants were willing to perform other protective behaviours, such as standing in quieter areas and taking breaks, with mean scores of 4.19 and 4.21, respectively. Although earplug use is crucial, it may be more heavily influenced by peer pressure and perceived aesthetics, making it a less desirable behaviour to engage in. However, promoting earplugs through social media influencers and musicians could help overcome this barrier. Widén et al. found that earplug use in concerts was much higher in Sweden than in the United States, and they speculate that this is due to a longer history of education and campaigns in Sweden.[65] It could be that once earplug use reaches a certain level, a cultural acceptance ‘tipping point’ occurs, lessening the peer pressure effect. Therefore, promotion of earplugs by influencers may help reach this tipping point more quickly in the United Kingdom. This speculation is supported by recent qualitative research into ear plug use by adults in the United Kingdom.[26] The researchers reported that people who did wear earplugs had higher exposure to social influences, such as friends and peers, and had more positive beliefs about the consequences of using earplugs compared to those who had never worn them. Emotion and reinforcement tactics, such as fear of losing the ability to listen to music and creating habits, were also mentioned by wearers but not by non-wearers. Both groups identified lack of environmental context and resources, such as prompts and cues, and challenges with memory, attention, and decision-making processes as barriers to earplug use. Therefore, education campaigns could focus on promoting a range of protective behaviours, as described above, in addition to building a more positive culture around earplug use.

Study limitations

As no existing, validated scale was available to assess perceptions of tinnitus, a scale was developed. Additionally, only behavioural intentions were measured, and so despite participants indicating that they were willing to change some behaviours, this may not translate to actual behavioural change. However, for the purposes of the experimental comparison, intentions were viewed as being an acceptable outcome to assess.

There was also a bias towards female participants in the study, and as protective behaviours have been found to vary with gender,[61] if there had been an equal male to female split, then absolute outcomes may have differed. However, there was no significant difference in the proportion of male to female in each group, so for the purposes of the experimental design this was acceptable.

The vast majority of the study participants were students, and although they were from a wide range of degree subjects and different universities, it does mean that the results cannot be generalised to the entire population of young adults. The value of this research could be enhanced by capturing more specific lifestyle information. Many of the papers studied in the literature review also sample only students, so there is a need to widen noise-damage prevention research to a wider demographic.
