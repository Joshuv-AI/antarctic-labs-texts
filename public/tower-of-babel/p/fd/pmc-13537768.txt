Prehospital Practices and Clinical Profile of the Patients with Snake Envenomation Admitted in a Tertiary Care Hospital: A Case Series
Authors: Dishani Dey, Lakshmi Ramamoorthy, S. Manu Ayyan, H. T. Lalthanthuami, Swetha Ramesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Snake envenomation is a common life-threatening medical emergency, especially in rural areas where limited access to healthcare and a lack of awareness about proper first-aid procedures often lead victims to rely on traditional healers before seeking modern treatment. These practices, such as the use of tourniquets, pressure immobilization, and native concoctions, might result in adverse outcomes and cause delays in seeking medical assistance.Methodology:This Case series design was conducted to identify the prehospital practices among patients with snake envenomation who presented to the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital. The study included 100 adult participants with definite characteristics of snake envenomation. Consecutive sampling was used to select participants, and data collection focused on clinico-epidemiological profiles and prehospital practices, including traditional remedies and first aid measures. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median, and associations were assessed using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test.Results:Participants had a mean age of 45.5 ± 15.6 years, with male predominance (71%) and the highest incidence in the 31–45 age group (33%). Most were from rural areas (94%). The median time from the bite to hospital admission was 90 minutes (IQR 4-180 minutes). 56% of participants used various methods before reaching the hospital. Washing the bite site with water and soap was the most common (19%), followed by topical native concoctions (17%) and pressure immobilization (17%).Conclusion:The study showed that various prehospital practices among patients were associated with threefold higher odds of delay in arriving at the hospital. The public should be educated to prevent unnecessary delays in transporting patients to the hospital because of these practices.

INTRODUCTION

Snake envenomation is a common medical emergency in tropical areas, causing an extent of morbidity and mortality globally, where the lack of accessible medical facilities leads many victims to rely on traditional practices before seeking modern medical care.[1]

In 2017, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared snakebites a priority within the neglected tropical diseases category, which predominantly affect impoverished populations. The WHO estimates that approximately 5.4 million people are bitten by snakes each year, with between 1.8 and 2.7 million cases resulting in envenomation. This leads to an estimated 81,410 to 137,880 deaths annually worldwide, and around 400,000 survivors are left with amputations and other permanent disabilities.[2] Snake envenomation is a significant public health issue in tropical and subtropical regions, especially in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, where venomous snakes are common and access to medical care and antivenom is limited.[3]

In Asia, particularly in India, snakebite deaths and disabilities are common, largely due to highly venomous species like the Indian cobra and Russel’s viper.[4] Prior studies in India estimate that 58000 fatalities occur annually due to snake envenomation, with 0.77 to 1.24 million cases reported each year. India has identified 236 snake species, of which 13 are known to be venomous.[5]

In rural areas across the developing world, the absence of accessible medical facilities compels snakebite victims to consult traditional healers before seeking modern medical care. Lack of awareness about proper first-aid procedures often leads to the adoption of traditional practices such as applying tourniquets, cutting the bite wound, attempting to suck out the venom, using electric shock or black stones, applying pressure immobilization bandages or relying on herbal remedies.[6]

Individuals at higher risk include those engaged in rural occupations such as farming, animal herding, fishing, and hunting, as well as children who work and people living in poorly constructed homes or areas with limited access to education and healthcare.[78] Young individuals and children are particularly vulnerable, experiencing higher rates of morbidity and mortality, with children being at an even greater risk of death from snake bites.[2]

This study was carried out to assess the identify the prehospital practices implemented among patients with snake envenomation and clinico-demographical characteristics admitted to the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

A case series design was carried out; 100 adult participants with a history of snake envenomation admitted to the Emergency Department of a tertiary care hospital in southern India between August 2023 and May 2024 were included in the study.

Sample size calculation and sampling

A consecutive sampling method was used to select participants. The sample size was calculated based on a single proportion. By expecting 80% of the adult population would practice some form of first aid and prehospital measures before reaching the hospitals, with 7% of absolute precision and 90% of the confidence interval, it is estimated to be 89, with a 10% attrition rate, the sample size is rounded off to 100.[910] The study included participants aged ≥18 years with a history of snake envenomation.

Ethical consideration

The study received approval from the institute’s Research Monitoring Committee (CON/NRMC/M.Sc/2022/MSN/5) and the Ethical Committee for Human Studies (CON/IEC/M.Sc./2022/MSN/5), in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines and the 2013 revision of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were included after obtaining informed consent from their legally authorized representatives. They were informed prior to data collection about the confidentiality of their data, their anonymity, and their right to withdraw from the study at any point.

Data collection

The study collected data in three sections: Section I covered participants’ demographic details, including age, gender, domicile, and personal habits. Section II focused on prehospital practices and other details related to snake bites. Section III collected clinical variables at admission, including the time from the bite to hospital arrival, initial treatment, snakebite severity score, vital signs, physical and neurological examinations (including the Glasgow Coma Scale), as well as investigation reports and any biochemical and hematologic abnormalities.

Patients meeting the study criteria were identified. Consent was obtained from participants or their legally authorized representatives as soon as possible after admission. Baseline characteristics, including demographic data and details of the snakebite, were recorded. A comprehensive history and physical examination were conducted to assess snakebite severity, vital signs, and other clinical parameters. Hematologic and biochemical investigations were performed upon admission and as needed. Clinical manifestations and complications were monitored through laboratory reports. Follow-up was conducted every day until discharge.

Operational definitions

Prehospital practices is Prehospital practice is defined as measures undertaken prior to arrival at the hospital, including application or ingestion of topical native concoctions, tourniquet use, electric shock, cryotherapy, black stone, washing the area with water or soap, pressure immobilization, and incision of the affected limb with or without suction or any other sort of practice performed before coming to the healthcare department and Prehospital delay occurs when a patient seeks healthcare more than 4 hours after a snakebite.

Data analysis

The data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 25.0. Categorical variables were presented with frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on the normality of the data distribution. Variables with skewed distributions, including the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and Severity Score, were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). The associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were performed at a 5% significance level. Univariable logistic regression was conducted for each prehospital variable to quantify the magnitude of the association with hospital delay and mortality, whenever applicable. Variables that were independent predictors of delayed hospitalization (P < 0.20) were carried forward into the final multivariable logistic regression model. No imputation for missing data was performed, as none were present in the clinical variables analyzed. Participants unable to identify snake species were categorized as “unknown species” and analyzed as a separate category.

Patient partnership strategy

Since this manuscript is part of an academic project, patients were not involved in the study design; however, informed consent was obtained from patients before enrollment. Based on the overall results, patients were educated to avoid unnecessary actions that could delay their presentation to the hospital.

RESULTS

Among the 100 participants, a male preponderance (71%) was noted, and most were from rural areas (94%). The mean age of the participants was 45.5 ± 15.6 years.

Of the participants recruited, 31% could not identify the type of snake. Among the identified species, the Russell’s viper was the commonest (30%), followed by the common krait (15%). 38% of them had snakebites while working in the field, followed by another common location, the participant’s house (21%). The highest frequency of snakebites occurs in winter (34%), followed by summer (29%). Most of them were bitten in their lower extremity (53%), and fang marks were present in most of the participants (74%) [Table 1a].

Among the snakebite patients, 56% of participants implemented some type of prehospital practices before seeking medical care. Washing the bite site with water and soap was the most common (19%), followed by applying topical native concoctions and using pressure immobilization (17% each). Only 1% sought traditional healing methods. Most of them (79%) visited a healthcare center. The median time from the bite to the hospital was 90 minutes (IQR: 4-180 minutes). Of the 100 patients, 18 experienced delayed presentation, and 96% received antivenom, with a median time to ASV administration from the time of bite of 170 minutes (interquartile range: 90–241 minutes) [Table 1b].

Among 18 patients with delayed hospital presentation, 14 used some form of prehospital care, suggesting that prehospital care was associated with delayed hospital presentation (P = 0.040). Among prehospital practices, topical application of native concoctions and washing the bite site with water and soap were significantly associated with threefold and fivefold higher odds of delayed presentation, respectively. The two significant predictors on univariable analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. After controlling for other prehospital practices, washing the bite site with soap and water was the strongest independent predictor of delayed hospital presentation, with the odds increasing to 5.87-fold. No significant associations were found for ingesting native concoctions, tourniquet use, pressure immobilization, incision with/without suction, or traditional healing with delayed presentation [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Since snakebites are more common in rural areas, the true extent of morbidity and mortality is probably underreported. This underreporting results from several factors: limited access to healthcare, a shortage of antivenom, and victims often seeking care from traditional healers instead of medical facilities.[910] There are 13 known venomous snakes, and among these, four are particularly lethal: the common cobra (Naja naja), Russel’s viper (Daboia russelia), saw-scaled viper (Echis carinatus), and common krait (Bungarus caeruleus).[5] In this study, the most frequent type of snakebite was due to the Russell’s viper (30%), followed by the common krait (15%), although most patients were unaware of the snake species involved.

In the present study, the participants’ mean age was 45.5 ± 15.6 years, with male predominance (71%) and the highest snakebite incident observed in the 3-45 year age group (33%). Most participants were from rural areas (94%). These findings are similar to those previously reported in the literature.[185101112] The majority of victims were bitten outdoors, mainly in the fields (38%), during daytime (38%), and primarily on the lower limbs (53%). Our findings align with previous studies.[1591012] Fang marks were present in 74% of participants. Additionally, we found that most bites occurred during the winter season (34%); in contrast, some similar studies reported the highest number of snakebites from July to September or during the monsoon season.[5121314]

The present study showed that 96% of participants received antivenom serum, while 8%, 20%, and 33% needed inotropic support, renal replacement therapy, and blood transfusions, respectively. A study conducted at a tertiary care center on snakebite envenomation found that patients requiring ionotropic support, renal replacement therapy, and blood transfusions had a statistically significant association with poor prognostic outcomes. Conversely, the total dose of antivenom serum (ASV) administered did not significantly correlate with patient outcomes.[14] Notably, the participants with snake envenomation exhibited various symptoms, including cellulitis (55%), ecchymosis (51%), blisters (25%), bleeding from the bite site (40%), mild to moderate swelling (86%), ocular symptoms such as blurred vision (29%), diplopia, ptosis, neurological symptoms (31%)—mainly headache and dizziness—and death occurred in six participants. A previous study stated that 82.56% of participants had cellulitis, 40.21% had bleeding tendencies, 76 participants had neuroparalysis, and there is considerable variability in reported mortality rates associated with snakebite envenomation, with one study indicating no deaths or critical care admissions, while another reported a mortality rate as high as 39%.[1516] Other studies have reported varying percentages of clinical presentations, such as nausea/vomiting, bleeding, swelling, pain, cellulitis, ecchymosis, blisters, ulceration, external bleeding (epistaxis, gastrointestinal bleeding, intracranial bleeding, etc.), and mortality, among patients with snake envenomation.[101117] In the current study, the median hospitalization duration was 6 days. The mortality rate was 6%.

In low- and middle-income countries, prehospital management of snakebite is strongly influenced by limited healthcare access, poor transport facilities, financial constraints, and deeply rooted cultural beliefs. As a result, many victims initially rely on self-administered remedies or traditional practices before reaching definitive care. Such delays may increase venom absorption, worsen tissue injury, and contribute to complications such as coagulopathy, acute kidney injury, and neurotoxicity.[911] In the present study, more than half of the participants reported adopting at least one prehospital measure, highlighting the substantial gap between recommended first-aid practices and community-level management of snakebite. Although washing the bite site was the most common practice, potentially harmful interventions such as tourniquet application, pressure immobilization, and topical native concoctions were also frequently reported.[1214] The continued use of these practices suggests inadequate public awareness regarding evidence-based snakebite first aid and underscores the need for targeted community education programs, especially in rural agricultural populations where occupational exposure is high.[9101618]

Interestingly, our study did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between prehospital practices and complications or mortality. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because the sample size may have limited the ability to detect clinically meaningful differences. Previous evidence suggests that inappropriate tourniquet application can aggravate local tissue ischemia and increase the risk of limb complications by prolonging tissue hypoxia.[610] The role of pressure immobilization remains controversial in viper bites commonly encountered in India. While pressure immobilization may delay systemic venom spread and has shown benefit mainly in neurotoxic elapid envenomation, its improper application or prolonged use in hemotoxic snakebites may potentially worsen local tissue damage.[6] Therefore, the clinical significance of pressure immobilization likely depends on the snake species, type of envenomation, timing, and correctness of application. These findings emphasize the importance of developing context-specific first-aid recommendations and strengthening training of both healthcare workers and the public regarding safe and evidence-based snakebite management. Early transport to healthcare facilities and prompt administration of antivenom remain the most critical determinants of favorable outcomes.[6911]

Limitations of the study

The study has the following limitations. Firstly, the sample size was smaller than anticipated, primarily due to a limited data-collection period, which limited the number of participants. Additionally, the research was confined to a single tertiary care hospital. Another potential limitation is that patients admitted to the hospital may differ significantly in health-seeking behavior, condition severity, or other factors from individuals who do not seek hospital care. Moreover, in June and July, participant enrollment was prevented due to the restricted data collection period.

CONCLUSION

Snakebite remains a significant public health problem, particularly in rural agricultural communities. In the present study, a considerable proportion of participants practiced various prehospital measures before reaching healthcare facilities, including the use of tourniquets, pressure immobilization, topical native remedies, and washing the bite site. Although most participants reached the hospital within a few hours, these practices may contribute to delays in accessing definitive treatment. The findings highlight the continued need for community-based education on appropriate first-aid measures, early hospital referral, and timely administration of antivenom to improve outcomes following snake envenomation.
