Structural determinants and health-related quality of life among people living with HIV: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Jason M. Lo Hog Tian, Branden Ngo, Divya Parmar, Aditi Chiney, Lori Rosman, Kaitlyn Atkins, Amrita Rao, Stefan Baral
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntiretroviral therapy has increased life expectancy of people living with HIV, though estimates of quality of life remain consistently lower than that of the general population. Research on determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV has predominantly focused on clinical factors, with limited attention to upstream structural determinants, or the economic, political, and social systems shaping resource distribution and health outcomes. Moreover, structural and social determinants are frequently conflated in the literature, challenging both conceptual clarity and intervention design. Eligible studies will quantitatively examine associations between structural determinants (including policy and legal environment, culture, social class, socioeconomic position, stigma and discrimination, built environment, and material living conditions) and health-related quality of life measured using validated instruments among people living with HIV. Two reviewers will independently screen studies, extract data, and assess risk of bias using Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools. Subgroup analyses will examine variation by geographic region, gender, race, and structural determinant category.DiscussionThis review will systematically synthesize evidence on structural determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV. Findings will identify modifiable upstream factors associated with quality of life, informing prioritization of structural interventions alongside biomedical approaches to HIV care. Background

The dynamics among both the HIV pandemic and people living with HIV have evolved significantly over the past two decades. The number of people living with HIV is projected to peak at 44.4 million by 2039, and HIV-related mortality has declined by nearly 40% since 2010 [1]. Advances in antiretroviral therapy (ART) have enabled people living with HIV who initiate and maintain treatment to have effectively normal life expectancy [2, 3]. This epidemiological shift has repositioned HIV in settings with broad access to ART from an acute, life-threatening condition to a chronic, manageable condition, fundamentally changing the priorities of HIV programs and research.

Despite these improvements in survival, people living with HIV continue to report lower health-related quality of life compared to the wider population [4, 5]. Health-related quality of life refers to individuals’ subjective assessment of how their health status affects their physical, psychological, and social functioning and overall wellbeing [6, 7]. Moreover, quality of life has emerged as a critical outcome in HIV care, reflecting dimensions of health that matter to patients beyond laboratory markers [8, 9]. As people living with HIV age, optimizing long-term wellbeing has continued to emerge as a program and policy priority [9]. Describing and quantifying the factors that shape health-related quality of life is therefore essential for improving comprehensive HIV care and ensuring that people living with HIV can thrive while managing a chronic condition.

Research on determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV has predominantly focused on individual-level clinical factors, including CD4 + T-cell count, viral load, HIV symptoms, and comorbidities [10, 11]. While clinical predictors are important, focusing exclusively on biomedical factors may overlook the broader conditions that shape health outcomes. Increasingly, researchers and policymakers have called attention to the social and structural determinants of health as critical influences on quality of life [12, 13]. However, these terms are frequently used interchangeably or inconsistently in literature, creating conceptual confusion that makes it challenging for effective evidence synthesis, intervention design, and policy translation [14].

Conceptual framework

The term “social determinants of health” gained prominence through the World Health Organization’s Commission on Social Determinants of Health (CSDH), which defined them broadly as the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, shaped by the distribution of money, power, and resources [12]. The CSDH conceptual framework distinguishes between two levels of determinants: structural and intermediary. Structural determinants of health refer to the socioeconomic and political context, including governance, policies, and cultural values, which together generate and maintain social stratification. Intermediary determinants are the factors through which structural determinants influence health including material circumstances, psychosocial factors, and health behaviors.

Despite this framework, considerable confusion persists regarding what constitutes a structural determinant, with many studies conflating structural and social determinants or neglecting to provide a definition at all [14]. Recent theoretical work has sought to improve this conceptual confusion as well as apply these concepts to HIV [15–17]. In a framework developed for HIV transmission dynamics modelling, Stannah et al. distinguished between distal structural determinants, or macro-level conditions such as laws, policies, cultural norms, and systemic discrimination that affect entire populations, and proximal structural determinants, or individual-level socioeconomic positions such as education, income, and employment that result from these broader conditions [17].

For this review, we define structural determinants as economic, political, and social systems shaping the distribution of power and resources, as well as the socioeconomic positions and material conditions resulting from these systems. Distal structural determinants are macro-level factors including laws, policies, cultural values, and systemic discrimination that affect populations and shape the distribution of resources and opportunities across social groups. Proximal structural determinants are individual-level circumstances including social class, education, employment, income, stigma and discrimination, built environment, and material living conditions directly resulting from these distal structural determinants. These proximal determinants in turn influence downstream social and behavioral determinants of health, ultimately affecting health-related quality of life among people living with HIV.

People living with HIV have historically faced, and continue to face, substantial structural barriers [18]. In many countries, laws criminalize behaviors associated with populations disproportionately affected by HIV exacerbating risks including among gay men and other men who have sex with men, people who inject drugs, and sex workers [18, 19]. HIV-specific criminal laws in some jurisdictions penalize HIV non-disclosure, exposure, or transmission, creating legal risks for people living with HIV [20]. Stigma and discrimination affecting people living with HIV remain pervasive across healthcare settings, workplaces, and communities [21–23]. These structural factors may reduce access to healthcare services, undermine engagement in care, and directly affect psychological wellbeing and quality of life.

Several systematic reviews have examined relationships between structural factors and HIV-related outcomes, documenting associations between policing practices and HIV burden among sex workers [24], immigration status and HIV outcomes among migrant sex workers [25], social and structural factors and HIV disease progression among people who use drugs [26], and structural determinants of HIV among female sex workers [27]. However, these reviews focused on HIV acquisition, transmission, and disease progression rather than quality of life outcomes. Some systematic reviews have examined different determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV including clinical, demographic, and psychosocial factors [10], and social and demographic determinants [11, 28].

A comprehensive synthesis of evidence on structural determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV is needed to identify modifiable upstream factors, inform structural intervention design, and guide policy priorities. This systematic review aims to address this need by identifying, synthesizing, and quantitatively pooling available evidence on associations between structural determinants and health-related quality of life among people living with HIV.

Methods

Registration and reporting

This protocol is registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD420261373190; 22 April 2026) and is in accordance with the guidelines specified in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses statement for protocols (PRISMA-P) [29]. The completed PRISMA-P checklist is provided in Additional File 1. Any amendments to the protocol will be documented and updated in the PROSPERO registration record.

Information sources

The following electronic databases were searched: PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, PsycINFO, PAIS, Sociological Abstracts, and Scopus. This combination of databases was selected to capture biomedical, nursing, psychological, public policy, and social science literature relevant to the research questions. We will not search grey literature, trial registries, or contact experts for unpublished studies, as our focus is on peer-reviewed evidence. The searches covered studies published from database inception to February 1, 2026. Following full-text screening and identification of included studies, we will conduct citation searching to identify additional relevant studies not captured by database searches. We will review the reference lists of all included studies (backward citation searching) and identify studies that have cited the included studies (forward citation searching). Identified studies will be screened by two reviewers using the same eligibility criteria applied to database-retrieved studies.

Search strategy

The search strategy was developed in consultation with a health sciences information specialist at Johns Hopkins University. The search combined three core concepts: HIV, health-related quality of life, and structural determinants of health. Controlled vocabulary terms (e.g., MeSH or Emtree) and free-text keywords were used. Search strategies were adapted for each database. The complete search strategies for all databases are provided in Additional File 2.

No minimum sample size will be required for inclusion. No language restrictions will be applied. For non-English language studies that meet inclusion criteria, we will use translation software to translate full texts for screening and data extraction. If translation software is insufficient for accurate assessment, we will seek assistance from native speakers or professional translation services.

Study selection

All search results will be imported into Covidence systematic review management software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for screening and data management [31]. Duplicate records will be removed automatically and manually verified. Title and abstract screening will be conducted independently by two reviewers. Studies will be classified as “include,” “exclude,” or “maybe.” Full texts of potentially eligible studies (i.e., those classified as “include” or “maybe” during screening) will then be independently reviewed by two reviewers. Conflicts will be resolved through discussion or by consultation with a senior reviewer if the conflict cannot be resolved. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be documented and reported. The study selection process will be documented using a PRISMA flow diagram [29].

For studies with missing or unclear data essential for inclusion determination or meta-analysis (e.g., missing effect estimates, standard errors, or sample sizes), we will contact corresponding authors via email. Up to two follow-up emails will be sent over a four-week period. Studies will be included in narrative synthesis if essential quantitative data remain unavailable after author contact but will be excluded from meta-analysis for that specific outcome.

Risk of bias assessment

Risk of bias will be assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools, with the specific checklist selected based on study design [32]. Two reviewers will independently assess risk of bias for each included study. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. Each checklist item will be rated as “yes” (criterion met, low risk), “no” (criterion not met, high risk), “unclear” (insufficient information), or “not applicable”. Studies will be categorized into overall risk of bias categories based on the proportion of applicable items rated “yes” with ≥ 70% being categorized as low risk of bias, 50–69% being categorized as moderate risk of bias, and < 50% being categorized as high risk of bias. Studies will not be excluded based on risk of bias assessment.

Data synthesis

A narrative synthesis will summarize the characteristics and findings of included studies. We will describe study characteristics, populations examined, structural determinant exposures, health-related quality of life outcomes, and key findings. Where sufficient data are available, meta-analyses will be conducted. Effect estimates will be extracted as odds ratios, risk ratios, beta coefficients, or correlation coefficients with corresponding confidence intervals. To standardize effect estimates for pooling, effect sizes will be converted to standardized mean differences (SMD) and 95% confidence intervals for continuous outcomes and odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals for categorical outcomes. Random-effects meta-analysis will be used for all analyses to account for anticipated between-study heterogeneity in populations, settings, exposures, and methods [33]. Where sufficient data are available, subgroup analyses will be conducted based on gender, race, geographic region, type of structural determinant, health-related quality of life instrument. Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of results, including excluding studies with high risk of bias, excluding studies with extreme effect estimates, and comparing fixed-effects and random-effects models. Where sufficient studies are available, meta-regression will be conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity. Potential moderators may include publication year, age, gender, and country. All analyses will be conducted in R version 4.5.1 [34].

Assessment of heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity will be evaluated using Cochran’s Q test and the I2 statistic. A Cochran’s Q p-value less than 0.10 and an I2 greater than 50% will be considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. When substantial heterogeneity is present, we will explore potential sources through subgroup analyses and meta-regression. If heterogeneity cannot be adequately explained and is judged too substantial for meaningful pooling, we will present results narratively with forest plots displaying individual study estimates without a pooled summary.

Assessment of publication bias

Publication bias will be assessed visually using funnel plots where at least ten studies are included in a meta-analysis. Egger’s regression test will be conducted to evaluate funnel plot asymmetry [35]. In the presence of asymmetry, we will investigate possible explanatory factors including publication bias, selective outcome reporting, small-study effects due to lower methodological quality, and true heterogeneity related to study size.

Assessment of certainty of evidence

We will provide a narrative assessment of our confidence in the synthesized findings. This assessment will consider the overall risk of bias across studies (using JBI Critical Appraisal ratings), the consistency of associations in direction and magnitude, and the precision of effect estimates based on sample sizes and confidence intervals. Where sufficient studies are available, we will also consider evidence of publication bias from funnel plots and statistical tests. We will describe our overall confidence in each set of findings, assigning higher confidence when multiple studies at low risk of bias show consistent associations and lower confidence when findings are inconsistent or based primarily on studies at high risk of bias.

Discussion

This systematic review will comprehensively synthesize evidence on structural determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV. By examining upstream factors including policy and legal environments, socioeconomic position, stigma and discrimination, and material living conditions, this review will identify which structural factors are most strongly associated with wellbeing and inform priorities for intervention beyond biomedical approaches. Additionally, by applying distinguishing distal and proximal structural determinants from social determinants, this review will contribute to conceptual clarity in a field where these terms are frequently conflated, providing a model for future research examining structural influences on health.

Several methodological features strengthen this review. Our comprehensive search strategy spans seven databases covering biomedical, psychological, public policy, and social science literature, maximizing identification of relevant studies across disciplines. Our structural determinants framework, adapted from established conceptual models [12, 17], provides clear operational definitions and enables precise synthesis and interpretation of findings. Prespecified subgroup analyses will examine effect modification by geographic region, sex and gender, race and ethnicity, and structural determinant category, providing nuanced evidence relevant to diverse populations and contexts.

We anticipate several challenges in conducting this review. Substantial heterogeneity is expected across studies in how structural determinants are defined, measured, and operationalized, as well as in health-related quality of life instruments and populations studied. We will employ random-effects models and detailed effect measure standardization protocols to address this. Where heterogeneity precludes meaningful pooling, we will provide comprehensive narrative synthesis. Our preliminary searches suggest that distal structural determinants such as policy environments, legal frameworks, and cultural norms are examined less frequently than proximal determinants such as socioeconomic position and stigma. This may limit our ability to draw conclusions about upstream factors, though systematically documenting these evidence gaps is itself a valuable contribution. The predominance of cross-sectional study designs in this literature will preclude causal inference. We will interpret findings as associations and explicitly discuss temporality limitations when drawing conclusions. The broad scope of structural determinants examined may result in sparse evidence for specific determinant–outcome combinations and unmeasured confounding in observational studies may bias observed associations.

Despite these limitations, this review will generate evidence relevant to multiple stakeholders. For policymakers, funders, and program planners, findings will identify structural conditions most strongly associated with quality of life among people living with HIV, informing prioritization of structural interventions alongside biomedical approaches. Evidence on policy and legal environment effects in particular may inform advocacy for legal and policy reforms affecting people living with HIV. For researchers, the review will systematically map the current evidence landscape, identify methodological gaps, and highlight under-researched structural determinants and populations requiring further investigation. For clinicians and healthcare systems, findings may inform screening practices to identify structural barriers affecting patients and guide referrals to social services and support programs. For communities and advocates, the review will provide evidence to support advocacy for structural interventions, such as anti-discrimination legislation, housing programs, and economic support, and to hold systems accountable for addressing conditions that undermine quality of life for people living with HIV.

In conclusion, this review will synthesize evidence on structural determinants of health-related quality of life among people living with HIV, applying a conceptual framework to identify modifiable upstream factors. By examining conditions beyond viral suppression that shape wellbeing, this review supports the broader goal of ensuring that people living with HIV can thrive in the era of effective antiretroviral therapy.
