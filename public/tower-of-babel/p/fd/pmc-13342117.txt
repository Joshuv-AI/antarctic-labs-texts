Deep learning-based prediction of 24-2 visual field from fundus photographs in glaucoma
Authors: Keunheung Park, Hwayeong Kim, Sangwoo Moon, Jiwoong Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo develop a deep learning architecture for predicting visual field (VF) maps from fundus images.MethodsFundus images and VF test results were obtained from two tertiary hospitals. The training dataset comprised 31,443 fundus photographs and 27,364 VF examinations, whereas the test dataset comprised 6,436 fundus photographs and 5,647 VF examinations. A novel deep learning architecture, TDV-Net, was developed to predict VF maps and assess glaucoma progression from input fundus images. The model performance was evaluated using the pointwise mean absolute error (MAE) between the predicted and actual VF. Additionally, the ability of the model to predict glaucoma progression was compared with that of human experts.ResultsThe overall prediction error (MAE) of TDV-Net was 3.91 dB across all glaucoma stages. According to glaucoma severity, the MAEs were 3.09, 5.66, and 9.15 dB for mild, moderate, and severe glaucoma, respectively. In predicting glaucoma progression, TDV-Net achieved an accuracy of 76.3%, sensitivity of 73.4%, and specificity of 77.1%, outperforming human experts.ConclusionTDV-Net demonstrated robust performance in predicting the VFs from fundus photographs and outperformed human experts in predicting glaucoma progression. TDV-Net has the potential to enhance accessibility to VF assessment, particularly in settings where standard perimetric testing is not readily available, such as routine health checkups or primary care environments.Keywords: Glaucoma, Deep learning, Visual field, Fundus photo

Introduction

Glaucoma is one of the three leading causes of blindness, along with diabetic retinopathy and age-related macular degeneration [1]. It is characterized by irreversible, progressive loss of retinal ganglion cells and their axons. The visual field (VF) in patients with glaucoma progressively narrows, which may eventually lead to complete loss of vision [2]. Detecting and monitoring the progression of glaucoma is important to preserve the patient’s vision. However, the VF test contains considerable errors and fluctuations and is difficult for many patients to perform [3]. It is time-consuming, and many patients—such as older adults, individuals with cognitive impairments, physical disabilities, or those who are bedridden—may have difficulty completing the test. Many clinics, especially those outside of ophthalmology departments, may be unable to afford expensive VF testing equipment such as the Humphrey Field Analyzer.

Previous attempts have been made to develop an alternative method for predicting VF using optical coherence tomography (OCT) and deep learning [4, 5]. Unlike VF test, OCT examination requires less time to complete and demonstrates excellent intervisit repeatability [6–8]. Among ocular imaging modalities such as stereo-disc photography, ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM), or confocal scanning laser tomograph, OCT achieved the highest reliability [7]. Our first deep learning method successfully predicted the 24 − 2 VF from OCT images, achieving a prediction error of 4.79 ± 2.56 dB [4]. In a follow up study, it was also able to predict 10 − 2 VF from swept-source OCT images with a lower prediction error of 3.10 ± 2.40 dB [9]. Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated that deep learning methods can effectively estimate VF from OCT [10–13], suggesting that OCT may serve as a viable alternative for VF assessment when VF testing is unavailable.

However, several limitations hinder the widespread adoption of OCT as a substitute for VF. Commercial OCT devices cost as much as $180,000, occupy approximately 1m2 of floor space, and weigh approximately 30 kg [14]. OCT systems typically require a large tabletop setup and a certain level of examiner expertise to acquire high-quality images. Consequently, OCT is primarily used in ophthalmology clinics, and is often unavailable in general hospitals [15]. In contrast, fundus cameras are less expensive than OCT devices, allow image acquisition within a few seconds, and are easier to operate. Furthermore, portable and handheld fundus cameras are now widely available, making it possible to obtain the retinal images of bedridden patients. If a deep learning method could accurately predict the VF from fundus photographs, the clinical benefits would be substantial.

The purpose of this study was to develop a novel deep learning architecture called TDV-Net to predict 24 − 2 VF from fundus photographs. In addition to evaluating the performance of the model in predicting a single VF from a single fundus image, the authors investigated its ability to predict glaucoma progression using a longitudinal series of fundus photographs. Furthermore, a comparative analysis was conducted between deep learning methods and human experts.

Methods

All training and test data were obtained from patients who visited glaucoma clinics at two tertiary hospitals, Pusan National University Hospital (PNUH) and Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital (YPNUH), between June 2004 and April 2022. The datasets from PNUH and YPNUH were used as the training and test sets, respectively. There was no overlap of patients between the training and test datasets. The requirement for patient consent was waived by the institutional review board because of the retrospective study design. This study was performed according to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of each hospital (Pusan National University Hospital (Approval No.: 2408-006-141) and Pusan National University Yangsan Hospital (Approval No.: 55-2025-049)).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) ocular diseases other than glaucoma; (2) VFs with respect to the vertical meridian, suggestive of a brain lesion; and (3) fundus photographs with poor image quality, rendering it difficult to identify glaucomatous features, such as vertical cup-to-disc ratio (VCDR), retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) defects, disc hemorrhages, laminar dot sign, bayonetting of disc vessels, and other characteristic findings. If both eyes were eligible, one eye was randomly selected. The quality of fundus images was classified by the JWL according to the following criteria: (1) good: the optic nerve head (ONH) is clearly visible, and the VCDR and RNFL defects can be identified; (2) average: slightly unclear but ONH and RNFL defects can still be identified without difficulty; (3) bad: unclear with only partial identification of ONH features; and (4) very bad: ONH and RNFL features cannot be identified.

A subset of the test set containing five or more fundus photographs per eye was selected to compare the performance of TDV-Net to that of human experts in detecting glaucoma progression. The ground truth for the progression status was determined by the author (JWL). Two human experts, who are members of the Korean Glaucoma Society (KGS), participated in the evaluation. Only the original fundus images and date on which each image was captured were provided to the experts. Each series of fundus photographs was organized into separate numbered folders. The experts independently reviewed the images in each folder and determined whether glaucomatous progression had occurred.

Visual field examination

Standard automated perimetry (SAP) was performed using the Humphrey Visual Field Analyzer 750i (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA) with the Swedish interactive threshold algorithm 24 − 2 or 30 − 2. When VF was measured using the 30 − 2 test pattern, 54 test points (including two points of physiological blind spots) overlapping with the 24 − 2 test pattern were used. Glaucomatous VFs met at least one of the following criteria: glaucoma hemifield test results outside the normal limits and/or pattern standard deviation (PSD) probability outside 95% of the normal population. The reliability criteria applied were a fixation loss (FL) of less than 33%, a false-positive (FP) rate of less than 33%, and a false negative (FN) rate of less than 33%.

Deep learning architecture

We designed a deep-learning architecture called TDV-Net using the EfficientNet B3 [16] model as the backbone feature extractor (Fig. 1). The resolution of the input image was (896, 896) pixels, and automatic cropping, resizing, and padding were used to fit the original fundus image to the input size. Following the Efficient Net B3 backbone, three linear layers were added, each using the SiLU activation function. The last linear layer outputs predicted 54 total deviation values (TDVs) without scaling. To mitigate the effects of class imbalance, a cost-sensitive weighted MSE (mean squared error) loss function was utilized. Given the skewed distribution of the training data—which favors early glaucoma over moderate and severe stages—we applied differential weights based on disease severity. Specifically, the loss calculation was scaled by a factor of 2 for cases with an average TDV ranging from − 6 to − 12 dB, and by a factor of 3 for advanced cases with a TDV below − 12 dB.

Deep learning model training

An adaptive moment estimation optimizer (Adam) (learning rate = 0.001) was used to train the TDV-Net model, and the mean square error was used for its loss function. The batch size was nine, and an early stopping function with a patience of 20 was used to stop the training automatically. The TDV-Net model was developed using Python 3.12 libraries including PyTorch 2.2.2, CUDA version, NumPy 1.26.3, and SciPy 1.13.1.

Results

The training dataset consists of 31,443 fundus photographs and 27,364 VF exams from 13,497 eyes of 7,499 subjects. The mean age at first visit was 53.4 ± 18.0 years, and the mean follow-up duration was 388.8 ± 671.8 days (1.06 ± 1.84 years). The average interval between fundus photograph and VF exams was 20.8 ± 48.5 days (Table 1). A total of 31,443 training fundus images were randomly split into training and validation datasets in a ratio of 9:1. The validation data were used to monitor the neural network’s performance during training and help prevent overfitting.

Globally, MAE was 3.91 ± 0.03 dB across all stages of glaucoma and 3.09 ± 0.03, 5.66 ± 0.06, and 9.15 ± 0.09 dB in mild, moderate, and severe glaucoma, respectively. In mild glaucoma, the prediction error was the largest in the superonasal sector and the lowest in the central sector. However, in severe glaucoma, the central sector exhibited the largest prediction error, whereas the superior sector exhibited the smallest.

The cosine similarity between the actual and predicted VFs increased with glaucoma severity. The value was 0.486 for mild glaucoma, substantially increased to 0.829 for moderate glaucoma, and reached 0.908 for severe glaucoma. In other words, although the absolute prediction error increased with glaucoma severity, the spatial pattern of scotoma was more accurately predicted. Figure 4 illustrates the trend in the prediction error (mean signed error) according to glaucoma severity. In early glaucoma, the TDV-Net tended to produce relatively negative errors, whereas in more advanced stages, it showed increasingly positive errors. This indicates that TDV-Net tends to underestimate the intensity of scotoma, particularly in the advanced stages of glaucoma.

Discussion

TDV-Net demonstrated good performance in predicting VF tests from fundus images, achieving an MAE of 3.914 dB. As glaucoma progressed, the prediction error increased from 3.089 dB in mild glaucoma to 9.152 dB in severe glaucoma. The image quality also influenced the prediction accuracy, with MAEs of 3.348 dB for high-quality images and 6.006 dB for poor-quality images. When evaluating glaucoma progression using a series of fundus images, TDV-Net outperformed the human experts. The progression detection accuracy of TDV-Net was 76.3%, compared to 69.2% and 75.0% for the two human experts.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to predict the VF from fundus photographs using a deep learning approach. Therefore, the performance of TDV-Net cannot be directly compared with that of previous studies that primarily used OCT data. Hemelings et al. [13] developed a deep learning regression model based on Xception architecture to predict 24 − 2 VF from OCT images, achieving a best prediction error (MAE) of 4.82 dB. Asano et al. [12] conducted a similar study to predict the 10 − 2 VF from OCT images, reporting a prediction error (MAE) of 9.4 ± 9.3 dB. The authors have previously conducted related studies using OCT. In our first study, a deep learning method predicted 24 − 2 VF from OCT images with a prediction error (root mean squared error, RMSE) of 4.79 ± 2.56 dB [4]. In our subsequent study using swept-source OCT, the best prediction error (RMSE) was 4.44 ± 2.09 dB [5]. In the present study, TDV-Net achieved an MAE of 3.914 dB, which improved to 3.348 dB when limited to good quality fundus images. Notably, TDV-Net offers a practical advantage over existing OCT-based approaches, as it relies on relatively inexpensive fundus photographs while maintaining comparable or superior predictive performance.

The performance of TDV-Net declined with increasing glaucoma severity, as reflected by an increase in the prediction error (MAE) from 3.089 dB in mild glaucoma to 9.152 dB in severe glaucoma. However, despite the higher prediction error, the cosine similarity between the predicted and actual VFs increased from 0.486 to 0.908, indicating an improved accuracy in capturing the overall pattern of VF loss as glaucoma progressed. Cosine similarity reflects the degree of similarity in the scotoma patterns between the actual and predicted VFs. A high cosine similarity indicated that even if TDV-Net did not accurately predict the overall intensity of VF loss, it was still effective in identifying the spatial distribution of scotomas as glaucoma progressed. As shown in Fig. 3, representative examples demonstrate that the predicted VFs closely resemble the actual scotoma distribution patterns even in cases with a relatively high prediction error (MAE). As shown in Fig. 4, the TDV-Net tended to underestimate the intensity of scotomas in advanced glaucoma. The authors attributed this to an imbalance in the training dataset, which included 20,625 fundus images from normal to mild glaucoma but only 5,538 from severe glaucoma. This imbalance led TDV-Net to favor predictions resembling milder stages of glaucoma.

The association between RNFL thickness and VF sensitivity becomes highly nonlinear in late-stage glaucoma, largely due to a biological floor effect where non-neuronal tissues persist despite severe functional decline [18]. This makes it difficult to estimate the exact intensity of VF loss from fundus images alone. However, our study found that cosine similarity between predicted and actual VFs increased markedly as glaucoma progressed, indicating that the spatial pattern of the scotoma was well-reproduced even if the absolute depth was underestimated. Collectively, these results suggest that while predicting precise sensitivity levels in advanced glaucoma is limited, model-based pattern recognition remains highly reliable. This capability is clinically valuable for providing longitudinal alerts and triage-oriented support in environments where conventional VF testing is inaccessible.

In a comparative study, TDV-Net demonstrated a higher accuracy in predicting glaucoma progression than that of human experts. Although human experts exhibited higher specificity than TDV-Net, their sensitivity was noticeably lower. Previous studies have shown that human experts have limited accuracy in grading glaucomatous ONH [19]. Another study reported that clinicians tended to either underestimate or overestimate glaucomatous signs when evaluating optic disc photographs [20]. Among human experts, interobserver agreement in assessing VCDR and glaucomatous damage from fundus photographs has been reported to be moderate [21]. Furthermore, expert assessment of the CDR showed the weakest correlation with the VF MD and PSD, and human performance in distinguishing eyes with glaucomatous VF defects was the lowest [22].

These studies collectively suggest that the evaluation of fundus photographs by human experts is limited in accuracy, with no universally accepted standard, contributing to low sensitivity in detecting glaucoma progression. In contrast, TDV-Net was trained using a machine-to-machine paired dataset, where the input fundus photographs were acquired by a fundus camera, and the ground truth was derived from VF data obtained by an automated perimeter. This machine-to-machine training approach eliminates the need for human labeling, resulting in a more objective ground truth that is free from human subjectivity, including inter-observer variability. Additionally, it enables the rapid preparation of large-scale training datasets, further enhancing model robustness.

While SAP has historically served as the primary functional endpoint for glaucoma management, its clinical utility is limited by substantial test–retest variability, particularly when detecting progression over short follow-up periods [23]. Indeed, a previous study demonstrated that even in rapidly progressing glaucoma, approximately 20% of cases may remain undetected despite six SAP examinations over two years, highlighting the intrinsic limitations of SAP-based progression assessment [24]. In contemporary clinical practice, this limitation has contributed to an increasing reliance on structural imaging modalities such as OCT for monitoring disease progression. In this context, deep learning–based prediction of VF loss from fundus photographs may offer a complementary approach that is less susceptible to the test–retest variability inherent to SAP, while preserving clinically interpretable functional information.

This study has several limitations. First, there is an issue of data imbalance. The training and test datasets contain a much larger proportion of early-stage glaucoma patients compared to those with moderate or advanced stages. As a result, the model may have been trained to be more suited to early-stage glaucoma. Second, although the performance in predicting glaucoma progression was generally superior to that of human clinicians, the specificity (78.1%) was low. This can lead to a high rate of false positives in general clinics where progressive glaucoma is relatively rare. Even if the specificity isn’t perfect, the potential for some patients to undergo further testing due to false positives is acceptable. Rather, it’s more important to improve sensitivity and negative predictive value to prevent missing patients who actually progress. Recent studies have suggested that response delay is closely associated with glaucomatous damage [25, 26]. It is reported that a novel perimetry method, which measures both sensitivity and response delay, exhibits superior diagnostic accuracy and lower test-retest variability [26]. In future research, utilizing these advanced VF metrics can be considered to enhance the performance of deep learning models.

In conclusion, TDV-Net demonstrated a strong performance in predicting VFs from fundus photographs and outperformed human experts in predicting glaucoma progression. As TDV-Net requires only a fundus photograph as input, it offers a practical and accessible alternative to VF testing. TDV-Net could be particularly valuable in real-world settings, where VF testing is difficult to perform. The TDV-Net has the potential to make VF assessment more accessible, particularly in settings such as routine health checkups, where it could serve as a valuable screening tool for glaucoma progression.
