Small Cell Lung Cancer Establishes a Metabolic Autocrine Mechanism Through Active Extracellular ATP Transport
Authors: Takeshi Tsuruda, Manabu Kodama, Keiichi I Nakayama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSmall cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive malignancy with a 5‐year survival rate of less than 7%. SCLC is characterized by accelerated de novo purine nucleotide biosynthesis, which fuels its rapid proliferation. While ATP serves as an essential metabolic substrate for nucleotide polymer synthesis and as the universal energy currency that fuels essential biological processes, it can also act as a potent extracellular signaling molecule. Here, we identify an autocrine mechanism in which SCLC actively exports ATP to the extracellular space through pannexin 1 (PANX1) channels, thereby promoting its own proliferation via purinergic signaling. Marked elevation of extracellular ATP was observed in SCLC cells. Clinical meta‐analysis revealed that high PANX1 expression is significantly associated with poor survival in SCLC patients. Pharmacological inhibition or genetic knockdown of PANX1 suppressed extracellular ATP levels and markedly reduced SCLC cell proliferation, whereas PANX1 overexpression increased extracellular ATP and accelerated growth. This ATP efflux is driven by calcium‐dependent activation of PANX1, with the calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII)–TRPA1 axis identified as a key upstream regulator. Moreover, blockade of the P2RX7 receptor abrogated ATP‐induced proliferation, indicating that SCLC establishes a metabolic autocrine loop through ATP release and P2RX7 activation. In mouse xenograft models, PANX1 knockdown suppressed, whereas PANX1 overexpression enhanced, tumor growth in vivo. These findings indicate that SCLC exploits a PANX1‐dependent ATP release mechanism to engage P2RX7‐mediated autocrine signaling and suggest that targeting this axis may represent a potential therapeutic opportunity for this lethal cancer.Keywords: adenosine triphosphate (ATP), ligand‐gated ion channel 7 (P2RX7), metabolic autocrine, pannexin 1 (PANX1), purinergic receptor P2X, small cell lung cancer (SCLC)

Introduction

Small cell lung cancer (SCLC) accounts for approximately 15% of all lung cancer cases and represents one of the most aggressive and lethal tumor subtypes [1, 2, 3, 4]. Most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, with rapid tumor progression and early metastatic dissemination, resulting in an extremely poor prognosis [5, 6, 7]. The current standard therapy for SCLC consists of platinum‐based chemotherapy combined with radiotherapy, which provides only transient tumor shrinkage, and nearly all cases eventually relapse [8, 9, 10]. Consequently, the 5‐year survival rate remains as low as 5%–7%, and the median overall survival is less than two years [11, 12].

Recent studies have highlighted the metabolic reprogramming of SCLC as a hallmark of its malignant behavior. In particular, the de novo purine nucleotide biosynthesis pathway is markedly upregulated to meet the high proliferative demand of SCLC cells [13, 14, 15, 16]. Phosphoribosyl pyrophosphate amidotransferase (PPAT), the rate‐limiting enzyme in this pathway, has been identified as a strong prognostic marker, with high PPAT expression correlating with increased mortality risk in SCLC patients. ATP, one of the major end products of PPAT‐mediated purine synthesis, serves not only as an essential substrate for nucleotide polymerization and energy metabolism but also as an extracellular signaling molecule acting through purinergic receptors such as P2X and P2Y [17, 18, 19]. Although ATP metabolism has long been implicated in tumor growth and progression, the precise role of ATP dynamics in SCLC pathogenesis has not been fully elucidated [16].

ATP can be exported from cells through several mechanisms, including ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporters, pannexin 1 (PANX1) channels, and connexin hemichannels [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. PANX1 is best known as a channel activated during apoptosis via caspase‐3 and caspase‐7 cleavage of its autoinhibitory COOH‐terminal tail, resulting in channel opening and ATP release [25, 26, 27, 28]. PANX1‐mediated ATP release has also been reported in non‐apoptotic contexts, such as hypoxia in human erythrocytes and TLR stimulation in macrophages [20, 29]. Moreover, extracellular ATP can activate the purinergic receptor P2X7 (P2RX7), triggering PI3K–Akt signaling to promote tumor proliferation and invasion [30, 31, 32, 33]. However, it remains unclear whether viable cancer cells can actively secrete ATP under steady‐state conditions to sustain their own growth.

In this study, we demonstrate that SCLC cells actively transport ATP to the extracellular space via PANX1 channels in a Ca2+‐dependent manner. Pharmacological inhibition of the TRPA1–CaMKII–PANX1 axis, or genetic knockdown of PANX1, markedly reduced extracellular ATP levels and suppressed tumor cell proliferation, whereas PANX1 overexpression enhanced ATP release and accelerated growth. Furthermore, ATP‐induced proliferation was blocked by a P2RX7 inhibitor, indicating the establishment of a metabolic autocrine signaling loop. Xenograft experiments confirmed that PANX1 expression levels directly affect tumor growth in vivo.

Our findings suggest that SCLC may couple metabolic output with autocrine signaling through a PANX1–ATP–P2RX7 axis to support malignant proliferation. Targeting this axis may therefore represent a potential therapeutic approach for SCLC.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

Human cancer cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 using Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell line authentication was performed by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and all lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination by PCR‐based testing prior to use.

For retroviral infection, cells were first transduced with the mouse ecotropic retrovirus receptor (EcoVR) with the use of amphotropic retrovirus produced by HEK293T packaging cells co‐transfected with pCX4‐EcoVR and pGP/pE‐ampho (Takara Bio). To generate recombinant retrovirus encoding human PANX1, pCX4 carrying human PANX1 cDNA was co‐transfected with pGP/pE‐eco (Takara Bio) into HEK293T cells. Target cells expressing EcoVR were then infected with the PANX1‐expressing retrovirus and selected in medium containing puromycin (2 μg/mL).

RNA Interference and Stable Cell Line Generation

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting PANX1 were designed and cloned into the pCX4 retroviral vector. The target sequences were:

shRNA #4: 5′‐TTAGATTTCATCTTGTTAACTC‐3′.

shRNA #5: 5′‐TATTGTTTAAGTAAAATCCTAT‐3′.

Recombinant retroviruses were produced by transfection into HEK293T cells and used to infect SCLC cell lines. Cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) to establish stable knockdown lines. Unless otherwise indicated, experiments were performed using shRNA #4 and #5.

PANX1 Overexpression

Full‐length human PANX1 cDNA was subcloned into the retroviral expression vector pCX4. Retroviral transduction and selection were performed as described above to generate stable PANX1‐overexpressing SCLC cell lines. Empty vector‐infected cells were used as controls.

Animal Experiments

Female athymic nude mice (Balb/c‐nu/nu, 6–8 weeks old) were purchased from CLEA Japan. Mice were housed in specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions under a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle with free access to chow and water. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Institute of Science Tokyo (approval code A2023‐228C) and performed in accordance with institutional guidelines.

For tumor xenografts, 2.5 × 106 SCLC cells (control, PANX1 knockdown, or PANX1 overexpression) suspended in 100 μL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of nude mice. Tumor size was measured every 4 days using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as:

Mice were sacrificed at the defined endpoint, and tumors were excised and weighed.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP‐40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche). Protein concentrations were determined by the BCA assay (Thermo Fisher). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 μg) were separated by SDS–PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat dry milk in TBST (TBS + 0.1% Tween‐20) and probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C.

Primary antibodies used were:

anti‐PANX1 (Proteintech, 12595‐1‐AP; 1:1000).

anti‐Hsp90 (BD Transduction Laboratories, 610419; 1:10000).

After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (GE Healthcare) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using ECL reagents (Cytiva) and detected on the ImageQuant LAS‐4000 system (GE Healthcare). Band intensities were quantified using ImageQuant TL software.

ATP Quantification

Intracellular (iATP) and extracellular (eATP) concentrations were measured using the CellTiter‐Glo 2.0 Assay (Promega, G9242) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were seeded in 24‐well plates at 2.5 × 104 cells/well. After 24 or 48 h incubation, culture supernatants were collected for eATP measurement, and adherent cells were lysed for iATP analysis. Both samples were adjusted to 100 μL volume and mixed with 10 μL CellTiter‐Glo reagent. After gentle mixing and incubation for 5 min at room temperature, luminescence was measured using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices). To calculate ATP levels on a per‐cell basis, an ATP standard curve was generated using serial dilutions of ATP standards. Luminescence values obtained from each sample were converted to relative ATP levels based on the standard curve and subsequently normalized to cell number. All measurements were performed in triplicate, and background signals were subtracted using blank wells.

Glucose Uptake Assay

Glucose uptake was quantified using the Glucose Uptake Assay Kit‐Green (Dojindo, 347‐09821). Cells were incubated in medium containing Glucose Uptake Probe‐Green for 15 or 30 min, washed three times with ice‐cold WI solution (1×), and resuspended in WI solution for measurement with a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3).

Pharmacological Inhibitors

TRPA1 inhibitor: HC‐030031 (FUJIFILM Wako, 086‐09961, 222‐00781, 135‐18311).

PANX1 inhibitor: Carbenoxolone (CBX; Sigma, C4790).

P2RX7 inhibitor: A‐740003 (Selleck Chemicals, S0826).

Cells were treated with inhibitors at indicated concentrations for 24 h unless otherwise specified.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were independently performed at least three times unless otherwise stated. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.) or standard error of the mean (s.e.m.) as indicated. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using the paired, two‐tailed Student's t‐test. Survival curves were analyzed using the log‐rank test, and hazard ratios were calculated using Cox proportional hazards regression. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

High PANX1 Expression Correlates With Poor Prognosis in SCLC Patients

To investigate the clinical significance of purine metabolism‐related genes in SCLC, we analyzed transcriptomic datasets from 35 patients (Figure 1A,B). Consistent with previous findings [13], patients with high PPAT expression showed significantly poorer survival than those with low expression, whereas no clear prognostic association was detected for other enzymes involved in de novo purine synthesis or the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Figure 1B,C). Notably, high expression of PANX1, which encodes an ATP release channel, was also significantly associated with poor prognosis (Figure 1D). Meta‐analysis integrating multiple patient datasets revealed that PANX1 was the only gene whose 95% confidence interval exceeded 1 and whose median hazard ratio was greater than 2 (Figure 1E). PPAT also showed a trend toward poorer prognosis, with a median hazard ratio > 2, consistent with previous studies. PANX1 expression tended to increase in advanced‐stage tumors (Figure 1F), although this difference was not statistically significant. Together, these results suggest that both upregulated de novo purine synthesis (PPAT) and ATP export (PANX1) are associated with worse outcomes in SCLC patients.

SCLC Cells Actively Secrete ATP Through PANX1 Channels in a Ca2+‐Dependent Manner

To evaluate ATP dynamics in vitro, we quantified intracellular (iATP) and extracellular ATP (eATP) in a panel of 10 SCLC and 18 other types of cancer cell lines. iATP levels did not differ between the two groups, whereas eATP levels were significantly elevated in SCLC cells but barely detectable in other cell lines (Figure 2A). Immunoblot analysis revealed markedly higher PANX1 protein levels in SCLC than in other cell lines (Figure 2B,C), implicating PANX1 in ATP efflux.

Although PANX1 is classically activated during apoptosis via caspase‐3/7‐mediated cleavage, recent studies have also implicated Ca2+‐dependent phosphorylation in PANX1 channel opening [26, 34]. Analysis of SCLC patient samples showed no difference in CASP3 or CASP7 expression between normal and tumor tissues, but a significant increase in CaMKIIβ expression in tumors (Figure 2D). High CaMKIIβ expression was also associated with poorer survival (Figure 2E). Among various calcium influx channels, including transient receptor potential ankyrin 1 (TRPA1), ORAI calcium release‐activated calcium modulator 1 (ORAI1), and voltage‐gated Ca2+ channels (CACNA1S, CACNA1C, CACNA1D), only TRPA1 was significantly upregulated in SCLC tumors compared with normal tissues (Figure 2F). Pharmacological inhibition of TRPA1 with HC‐030031 significantly reduced extracellular ATP levels in SCLC cells (Figure 2G). These results indicate that SCLC cells, even in the absence of apoptosis, actively transport ATP extracellularly through PANX1 channels activated by TRPA1–CaMKII‐dependent signaling.

Pharmacological Inhibition or Knockdown of PANX1 Suppresses Extracellular ATP Release and Cell Proliferation

To determine the functional relevance of PANX1‐mediated ATP efflux, we pharmacologically inhibited PANX1 using carbenoxolone (CBX). CBX treatment caused a dose‐dependent reduction in extracellular ATP levels across multiple SCLC cell lines (Figure 3A), accompanied by significant growth inhibition in STC‐1 and H446 cells (Figure 3B).

To further validate these findings, we established stable PANX1 knockdown (KD) SCLC lines using shRNAs (Figure 3C,D). PANX1 depletion increased iATP levels but significantly reduced eATP (Figure 3E) and resulted in impaired cell proliferation compared with control cells (Figure 3F). Importantly, reintroduction of a shRNA‐resistant PANX1 cDNA (OE) rescued both extracellular ATP levels and cell proliferation (Figure 3E,F). Collectively, these data indicate that PANX1 is essential for maintaining extracellular ATP levels and promoting proliferation in SCLC cells.

PANX1 Overexpression Enhances ATP Efflux and Drives Proliferation Through P2RX7 Signaling

We next examined whether increased PANX1 expression could further promote ATP efflux and proliferation. PANX1‐overexpressing (OE) STC‐1 cells displayed reduced iATP and significantly increased eATP compared with control cells (Figure 4A,B). These cells also showed accelerated proliferation (Figure 4C) and enhanced glucose uptake (Figure 4D), consistent with increased metabolic activity.

(B) Intracellular and extracellular ATP levels in control and PANX1‐OE cells. (C) Proliferation curves of control and PANX1‐OE cells. (D) Glucose uptake assay in control and PANX1‐OE cells at 0, 15, and 30 min. (E) Cell proliferation upon exogenous ATP addition (0, 1, 10 μM). (F) Proliferation of STC‐1 cells stimulated with ATP (10 μM) with or without P2RX7 inhibition (A‐740003, 10 μM). *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 (Student's t‐test).

Exogenous addition of ATP to the culture medium enhanced STC‐1 cell proliferation (Figure 4E). Moreover, treatment with the P2RX7 inhibitor A‐740003 abrogated ATP‐induced proliferation (Figure 4F). These findings support a model in which PANX1‐mediated ATP efflux activates P2RX7‐dependent autocrine signaling, driving metabolic activity and proliferation in SCLC.

PANX1 Expression Regulates Tumor Growth In Vivo

To assess the impact of PANX1 on tumor growth in vivo, we performed xenograft experiments using nude mice (Figure 5A). Subcutaneous implantation of PANX1‐knockdown STC‐1 cells resulted in significantly reduced tumor growth (Figure 5B–D) and weight compared with control tumors (Figure 5E). Conversely, overexpression of PANX1 markedly accelerated tumor growth and increased tumor mass (Figure 6A–E). These results demonstrate that PANX1 expression levels directly modulate SCLC tumor growth in vivo.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a previously unrecognized metabolic autocrine mechanism in which SCLC actively transports ATP to the extracellular space through PANX1 channels and promotes its own proliferation via P2RX7 signaling (Figure 7). While ATP release has traditionally been considered a passive event associated with apoptosis or inflammatory stimuli [26, 28, 35, 36], our findings demonstrate that viable SCLC cells export ATP under steady‐state conditions through TRPA1–CaMKII‐dependent PANX1 activation. This ATP efflux sustains proliferative signaling, linking purine metabolism directly to oncogenic growth.

PANX1‐driven autocrine signaling via extracellular ATP transport. Inhibition of PANX1 by CBX and blockade of P2RX7 by A‐740003 suppressed the proliferation of SCLC cells, revealing the presence of an autocrine mechanism in which SCLC cells actively release ATP through PANX1 channels and promote their own proliferation via ATP binding to P2RX7 receptors.

ATP release is classically triggered by caspase‐mediated cleavage of PANX1 during apoptosis, or by stress signals such as hypoxia or viral mimicry in nonmalignant cells [20, 26, 28, 29]. In contrast, our results reveal a mechanism by which tumor cells themselves exploit PANX1 activation in the absence of apoptosis. This behavior aligns with the hyperactive metabolic state of SCLC, which is characterized by accelerated de novo purine synthesis to meet the high biosynthetic demands of rapid cell proliferation [16, 37]. ATP produced through PPAT‐mediated pathways is not only consumed intracellularly but is selectively released to the extracellular space, where it functions as a potent autocrine signaling molecule.

Despite a marked increase in glycolytic activity upon PANX1 overexpression, intracellular ATP levels remained unchanged. This finding is consistent with the concept of metabolic homeostasis in rapidly proliferating cancer cells. Enhanced PANX1 expression significantly promoted cell proliferation, which is accompanied by increased ATP demand for biosynthetic pathways, ion transport, cytoskeletal dynamics, and membrane remodeling. Under such conditions, additional ATP generated through elevated glycolysis is likely consumed immediately to sustain these ATP‐intensive processes rather than accumulating within the intracellular pool.

Furthermore, PANX1 activation facilitates ATP release and purinergic signaling, which may further accelerate ATP turnover and consumption. Together, these observations suggest that intracellular ATP concentration is maintained at a steady‐state level through tight coupling between ATP production and utilization, even in the context of enhanced glycolytic flux. This metabolic balance may enable PANX1‐overexpressing cells to support sustained proliferation while simultaneously engaging extracellular ATP‐dependent signaling.

A key finding of this work is the identification of TRPA1–CaMKII–PANX1 signaling as the upstream regulator of ATP efflux in SCLC. While PANX1 activation by CaMKII has been reported in immune cells [34], its role in cancer has remained largely unexplored. Previous studies have shown that ATP–P2X/P2Y receptor signaling promotes proliferation or inflammation in immune cells and the tumor microenvironment [38, 39, 40, 41]. However, direct evidence that tumor cells actively export ATP to support their own growth was lacking. The significant upregulation of TRPA1 and CaMKIIβ in SCLC tumors, coupled with their association with poor prognosis, indicates that this pathway is constitutively engaged to support tumor growth. Pharmacological inhibition of TRPA1 or PANX1 markedly reduced extracellular ATP levels and suppressed proliferation, providing direct functional evidence.

Extracellular ATP signals through purinergic receptors, and among these, P2RX7 has been implicated in tumor growth, invasion, and immune modulation [42, 43]. Our results demonstrate that P2RX7 mediates ATP‐driven proliferation in SCLC and that its inhibition effectively abrogates this effect. Together, these findings support a model in which SCLC establishes a self‐reinforcing loop: accelerated purine synthesis → ATP efflux through PANX1 → P2RX7 activation → enhanced proliferation and metabolic activity.

This mechanism has several important implications. First, it represents a direct coupling between metabolic output and growth signaling, highlighting how cancer cells can co‐opt metabolic intermediates as autocrine signals to sustain malignancy. Second, this pathway may contribute to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, as extracellular ATP is rapidly converted to adenosine by CD39/CD73, leading to immune evasion. Third, the PANX1–ATP–P2RX7 axis constitutes a druggable vulnerability. Inhibition of PANX1 or P2RX7 could suppress tumor growth and potentially synergize with immune checkpoint blockade by limiting adenosine‐mediated immune suppression.

Nevertheless, several questions remain. The precise molecular mechanism of CaMKII‐dependent PANX1 phosphorylation in SCLC requires further elucidation, including its upstream regulatory signals and potential cross‐talk with other metabolic pathways. Moreover, this study was based primarily on cell line and xenograft models; the role of the ATP–P2RX7 axis in the native tumor microenvironment and its impact on immune and stromal components warrant further investigation. Finally, other purinergic receptors and metabolic pathways may cooperate with P2RX7 signaling, and their contributions should be systematically evaluated.

In summary, we demonstrate that SCLC establishes a metabolic autocrine loop through PANX1‐mediated ATP release and P2RX7 activation, integrating purine metabolism with proliferative signaling. Targeting this pathway offers a promising therapeutic strategy for SCLC, a malignancy with historically poor outcomes and limited treatment options. Further exploration of PANX1 and purinergic signaling may pave the way for novel metabolism‐directed and immune‐modulating therapies in this aggressive cancer.
