Quantum secure direct communication in Internet of Vehicles
Authors: Wei Zhao, Guangyao Li, Peng Zhang, Fuqiang Wang, Xinchao Ruan, Jinjing Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe Internet of Vehicles (IoVs) is one of the most popular techniques among the applications of Internet of Things. The existing IoVs are mainly protected by public key cryptographic systems, which provide identity authentication and information security. Nevertheless, using the proposed Shor’s algorithm, the security of all classical cryptographic schemes will be exposed to future quantum computer technologies. Fortunately, based on the unconditional security property of quantum mechanics, direct communication protocols can resist quantum computer attacks and deliver secret messages directly without the requirement of pre-shared keys. For IoV networks, we present a continuous-variable quantum secure direct communication (CV-QSDC) protocol in which the orbital angular momentum (OAM) is used as the information carrier. For one thing, the field of continuous-variable quantum communication system is compatible with the standard telecommunication technology; for another, orbital angular momentum eigenstates for coding possess higher information capacity.

Introduction

As a pioneering adaptation of smart cities, Internet of Vehicles (IoVs) has made the transportation sectors revolutionized. Its communication is concentrated on the vehicle-to-vehicle or vehicle-to-infrastructure pattern. Specifically, IoV networks need to perceive and collect traffic data to ensure their smooth execution in predicting traffic flow, easing traffic congestion and reducing traffic accident rate. Because of the public transmission channels, the massive amount of information puts forward higher requirements for the security of IoVs.

Currently, most of the deployed cryptographic algorithms rely on integer factorization and discrete logarithm problem, adopting asymmetric cryptography to provide information security and user privacy. With the proposal of Shor’s algorithm,1 asymmetric cryptograph, such as RSA and ECC, has been proved to be completely insecure in theory. Compared with symmetric cryptography, which can also use longer key sizes to resist quantum computer attacks, asymmetric cryptography must be rebuilt using a new approach. Fortunately, the unconditional security property of quantum mechanics provides a theoretical foundation for information security, since it can resist quantum computer attacks. In particular, for Internet of Things (IoT) networks, Meng et al. proposed a key provisioning mechanism and provided a quantum-key-distribution-enhanced architecture,2 and Al-Mohammed et al. designed a defense mechanism with quantum key distribution.3 However, it is too expensive to deploy quantum-based mechanisms and quantum channels, which is overweight for IoT devices. These will further limit the application of the quantum technology in IoT networks.

For IoVs, quantum secure direct communication (QSDC) is a promising paradigm for transmitting data, which allows trusted communicators to deliver secret messages directly without pre-sharing a key. Unlike quantum key distribution4,5,6 and semi-quantum,7,8,9,10,11 QSDC changes the structure of secure communication modes from a dual-channel structure with key distribution and cipher transmission to a single-channel structure with one quantum direct communication channel, which reduces the process of information leakage, eliminates security vulnerabilities in key storage, distribution, and transmission, as well as further improves security. Based on the quantum no-cloning theorem12 and the Heisenberg uncertainty principle,13 the QSDC protocol offers imperceptibility and intrinsic security. Therefore, the QSDC protocol may be very important in some specific environments of the IoV networks, e.g., emergency communication and transmission routes are compromised or monitored.

In 2003, Deng et al. utilized the photon-pair blocks to present QSDC with a two-step protocol,14 who later employed single photons to propose another QSDC with a one-time pad protocol in 2004.15 It is worth mentioning that Deng et al.16 proposed four requirements that QSDC must meet: (1) secret messages can be delivered and read without additional classical information; (2) no useful information about the ciphertext is available to anyone other than the legitimate users; (3) trusted communicators can detect eavesdroppers before encoding secret messages; (4) technology of block transmission is used to send and receive quantum states. Since then, researchers have paid more attention to QSDC and made breakthroughs in terms of protocol content as well as security assurance methods.

The security of the protocols mentioned earlier relies on an assumption that the measuring devices are ideal. However, loopholes in practical QSDC systems, especially defects in measurement devices, give eavesdroppers the potential to steal secret information. To solve this problem, the measurement-device-independent (MDI) structure is proposed by several groups,17,18,19 which can further eliminate the security vulnerabilities related to measurement devices. Nevertheless, the topology of MDI structures generally exhibits a lower coding rate. Subsequently, high-dimensional encoding is proposed to overcome disadvantages of MDI-QSDC protocols, including the low communication efficiency. In addition, several important experimental demonstrations have been realized. In 2016, the DL04 protocol was experimentally realized by Hu et al. using single photons and frequency coding.20 In 2017, the two-step protocol was experimentally demonstrated by Zhang et al., in which atomic ensembles were employed for storage.21 Subsequently, the first long-distance experiment was reported by Zhu et al., where the transmission could reach 0.5km fiber with 50bps.22 In 2021, Qi et al. demonstrated a 15-user QSDC network.23 Recently, the group of Long achieved a system of over 100km fiber24 and adopted it in quantum networks.25

All the QSDC protocols mentioned earlier are in the field of discrete-variable (DV) quantum communication system, in which single-photon detectors are taken as receivers. As an alternative, the continuous-variable (CV) quantum communication system takes continuous light and a homodyne detector as the receivers.26 These differences enable the existing optical fibers to be directly employed in the CV quantum communication system for a better compatibility and availability. Since Pirandola et al. presented a protocol in the field of CV in 2008,27 great progress has been made in CV-QSDC.28,29,30 Especially, Cao et al. proposed a Gaussian mapping protocol and implemented it experimentally in 2023.31 However, existing CV-QSDC protocols mainly focus on the fiber channels while they do not involve free space, so that the application scenarios are limited.

In this work, we present a QSDC protocol for the CV communication system, which can be applied in the vehicle-to-vehicle or vehicle-to-infrastructure pattern. The proposed protocol may be very important in some specific environments, such as emergency communication where transmission routes are compromised or monitored. Moreover, we explore the feasibility of employing orbital angular momentum (OAM) to encode secret information in the proposed protocol. Light-carrying OAM exhibits an exp(ilθ) vortex phase structure. Since the topological charge number l could theoretically be any infinite integer, the Hilbert space can be extended to infinite dimensions.32,33,34,35 As a consequence, utilizing OAM eigenstates for quantum coding opens up new possibilities for high-capacity quantum communication. Besides, a new information block mapping method is proposed, which makes the distribution of OAM eigenstates uniform while avoiding potential security threats caused by the excessive proportion of some eigenstate distributions.

The rest of the paper can be outlined as follows. In Section preliminaries, we introduce some preliminaries about vortex beams. In Section system model, we describe the specific implementation of the CV-QSDC protocol; in particular, approaches for information block mapping and OAM quantum coding are presented. In Section results, we first analyze the upper bounds of effective information and the maximum transmission distance in the asymptotic regime through atmospheric turbulence channels. Then considering that the exchanged blocks have a certain length instead of infinitely signals, we recalculate the two indicators of the finite-size regime to get closer to the actual situation. The conclusion will be given in Section discussion.

Step 1 (preparation beams stage)

Alice first prepares a momentum-squeezed vacuum state(Equation 7)xˆ1=erxˆ1(0),pˆ1=e−rpˆ1(0),

To generate two-mode squeezed beams S1 (detection light) and S2 (signal light), Alice mixes the above two vacuum states, where beams S1 and S2 are given by41(Equation 9)xˆ1′=(erxˆ1(0)+e−rxˆ2(0))/2,pˆ1′=(e−rpˆ1(0)+erpˆ2(0))/2,

and(Equation 10)xˆ2′=(erxˆ1(0)−e−rxˆ2(0))/2,pˆ2′=(e−rpˆ1(0)−erpˆ2(0))/2,

respectively. Subsequently, Alice sends beam S1 to Bob through atmospheric turbulence channel and retains beam S2 for herself.

Step 2 (channel detection stage)

Alice randomly selects a part of the time window and measures two quadratures of beam S2 at the selected positions. Then, she informs Bob of the measurement results XS2′ and PS2′ at the corresponding positions. According to the information disclosed by Alice, Bob measures two quadratures in the corresponding position and records the measured results as XS1′ and PS1′. If results satisfy the inseparability criterion,42 namely(Equation 11)⟨[Δ(XS1′±XS2′2)]2⟩+⟨[Δ(PS1′∓PS2′2)]2⟩<2,

it indicates that the entanglement relationship of the entangled states on the corresponding time window are not destroyed by eavesdropper. In this stage, the communication efficiency is described with the concept of effective information, which will be analyzed in Section results. If the effective information is higher than the security threshold, the protocol continues.

Here, {l1,l2,l3,l4} denotes four OAM eigenstates, and {N1,N2,⋯,N8}={15,30,45,60,75,90,105,120} represents different lengths of information blocks.

Step 3 (authentication stage)

The direction of communication in this stage is exactly the opposite of Step 2. In particular, Bob randomly selects a part of the time window, measures two quadratures, and informs Alice of the measurement results. According to the inseparability criterion, if the effective information is greater than the security threshold, Alice can determine the identity of Bob and continues. Otherwise, the protocol stops. The two stages of Steps 2 and 3 correspond to the modules of checking eavesdropping (CE1,2) in Figure 4.

Step 4 (information mapping stage)

Alice divides the secret information composed of 0 and 1 into several blocks of information and transforms them into random sequences composed of elements 0, 1, and 2 after mapping. Taking one of the information blocks as an example, sequence is denoted as M1–1=[m1,m2,m3,⋯,mn−1,mn]. After the first mapping, sequence transforms into M1–2=[m1′,m2′,m3′,⋯,mn−1′,mn′], where(Equation 12)M1−2:{mi′=mi+mi+1,mi′=mn,i=1,2,⋯,n−1i=n.

Subsequently, sequence M1–2 is replaced by 2-bit elements. For instance, the elements 00 and 11 are selected to replace the “0” and “2” elements, respectively. Besides, the code elements in {01,10} are randomly selected to replace the “1” element. After coding conversion, sequence M1–2 is converted into M1–3=[k1,k2,k3,⋯,k2n−1,k2n]. So far, 0 and 1 elements in sequence M1–3 have been uniform. According to the length of the information, Alice selects the detection bits and error-correcting bits to insert and finally gets the sequence M1–4=[k1′,k2′,k3′,⋯,k4n−1′,k4n′], where 2n is the total number of detection bits and error-correcting bits.

Step 5 (LG beam modulation stage)

On the basis of sequence M1–4, Alice prepares the LG beam for OAM eigenstates. In particular, by controlling the electrical signal, beam S2 is passed into an LC-SLM loaded with different phase bases. We denote the set l∈{l1,l–1,l3,l–3} as the OAM standard basis throughout this protocol, where l1=1, l–1=−1, l3=3, and l–3=−3. Reasons for choosing these four bases have been described in Section preliminaries. For instance, if the information blocks k2i−1k2i in M1–4 are 00,01,10,11, the OAM eigenstates of LG beam are selected as |l1⟩,|l–1⟩,|l3⟩,|l–3⟩, respectively. We simulate the probability density distribution of four different OAM eigenstates, as depicted in Figure 5, where {N1,N2,⋯,N8}={15,30,45,60,75,90,105,120} represents different lengths of information blocks. It is obvious that as the length of information block increases, the probability densities of four OAM eigenstates are nearly equal. Since the four eigenstates satisfy uniform distribution, the information block mapping method described in Step 4 is effective, avoiding the potential security threat caused by the large proportion of one-eigenstate distribution. Finite-size security

The effective information in the asymptotic region, mentioned in the previous section, is calculated on the assumption that an infinite number of signals are exchanged. Nevertheless, the effective information and transmission distance in asymptotic regime is a theoretical value, and its upper bound cannot be achieved in practice. In what follows, we will take the finite-size regime into account where Alice and Bob exchange blocks of a certain length instead of infinitely many signals. Suppose that the total number of signals is N, where ζ signals are utilized to extract effective information and the remaining m=N−ζ signals are utilized for parameter estimation. and ϵ¯ denotes the smoothing parameter of private key amplification processes. Compared with the effective information in asymptotic regime, there are two differences in Equation 25. On the one hand, there is an additional prefactor ζ/N, considering the length of the blocks is finite. Discussion

To sum up, we have presented a CV-QSDC protocol for IoV networks, which can deliver secret messages directly. The proposed protocol is compatible with the standard telecommunication technology, offering imperceptibility and intrinsic security. For quantum coding, we employ OAM eigenstates as information carriers, which are suitable for wireless communication and possess a higher information capacity. Specifically, the protocol consists of two stages, security detection and message transmission. In the stage of security detection, we calculate the effective information and the maximum transmission distance in the asymptotic and finite-size regime, respectively, and the performance of the latter is closer to the reality. In the stage of message transmission, we adopt the method of information block mapping, avoiding potential security threats caused by the large proportion of one-eigenstate distribution. The protocol may be very important in some specific environments, such as emergency communication where transmission routes are compromised or monitored.

Limitations of the study

Although the QSDC network is not involved in this paper, the subsystem can be simplified to a point-to-point QSDC regardless of the topology of the QSDC network. All in all, the proposed protocol provided a theoretical foundation for further QSDC network.
