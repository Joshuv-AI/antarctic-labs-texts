Treatment of chronic nonbacterial prostatitis by modifying gut microbiota: a step forward, a long way to go
Authors: Zhiguo Zhu, Junwen Zhang, Zesong Jiang, Fuding Lu
Published: 2024

To the editor,

Chronic nonbacterial prostatitis (CNP) is also known as chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS), which is characterized by its high prevalence, recurrence, and diverse symptoms. The fluctuation and complexity of symptoms often leave patients feeling helpless, impairing their daily life and mental well-being. Similar to other challenging conditions, the intricate and uncertain causes of CNP hinder the ability to achieve favorable results with existing treatment approaches. Since the discovery that gut bacteria influence the brain and behavior (microbiota-brain axis), research has focused on whether similar regulatory relationships exist in the gut microbiota and other organs. Given the anatomical and functional links, there may be a potential regulatory axis between gut microbiota and the prostate.

In 2016, Shoskes et al. [1] demonstrated for the first time significant alterations in the gut microbiota of CNP patients through MiSeq sequencing. However, most cohorts cannot rule out the influence of environment and diet on the microbiome. Subsequent researches have not produced consistent results regarding the characteristic changes in the gut microbiota of patients with CNP. Mendelian randomization analysis can use the summary data of Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as instrumental variables to statistically deduce causality from exposure to outcome, while overcoming the methodological limitations of traditional observational studies and making the results more robust and reliable. In 2024, a two-sample Mendelian randomization study indicated a causal relationship between gut microbiota and CNP [2]. However, the diversity and dynamics of the microbiome far exceed those of the human genome. The gut microbiome is affected by many factors, including living environment, eating habits, time, medication. Despite the expert consensus advising caution in antibiotic treatment for patients with CNP, many patients have taken antibiotics either under the guidance of a doctor or on their own initiative. Prolonged use of antibiotics can alter the gut microbiota (including abundance and composition), and these changes are difficult to reverse after stopping antibiotic therapy. Moreover, we are not sure whether all the gut microbial species have been identified. These interferences pose a huge challenge to our understanding of the causal relationship between gut microbiota and CNP. At the same time, we should also recognize that gut microbiota is not typically the direct cause of CNP, implying that there may not be a direct causal relationship between them. The reality may be that in the presence of other pathogenic factors, dysregulation of gut microbial function disrupts the body’s homeostasis, contributing to the development of CNP. Restoring the composition and function of gut microbiota may enhance the body’s biological defenses, thereby reversing prostatic inflammation.

Although rigorous mechanistic studies have yet provided a clear rationale for therapeutic intervention, researchers have boldly begun to attempt to treat CNP by regulating intestinal microbes. Through a search in the PubMed database, we identified eight relevant studies (Table 1). Most of these studies have been published within the past five years, all of which utilized animal models (rat or mouse). Notably, six of these studies (75%) were conducted by Chinese researchers. The research subjects included berberine hydrochloride. berberine hydrochloride, astaxanthin, rapeseed bee pollen, poria cocos polysaccharide, whey protein-derived early glycation products, and galactoglucomannan-rich hemicellulose extract from Norway spruce. All of the studies evaluated the therapeutic effects of the aforementioned substances on prostatic inflammation and assessed changes in gut microbiota in CNP animal models after intervention using 16 S rRNA sequencing. However, five of these studies (aiticle 3, 5–8 in Table 1) prematurely inferred that the interventions alleviated CNP by modifying gut microbiota based solely on these findings. It is evident that these researchers have committed the logical fallacy of “False cause fallacy”. They failed to provide sufficient evidence to demonstrate that the gut microbiota played a role in alleviating prostatic inflammation. Fecal microbiome transplantation (FMT) is an effective and reliable method for studying the relationship between gut microbiota and specific diseases. Additionally, evidence has demonstrated that FMT in adults is feasible, scalable, and safe. The studies by Tian [3] and Liu [4] highlight the mediating role of gut microbiota in the anti-prostatitis effects through FMT. Yu et al. [5] employed the metabolites of poria cocos polysaccharide fermented by intestinal bacteria as an intervention to treat the CNP rat. They employed this approach to demonstrate that the “Gut-prostate axis” plays an important role in alleviating CNP.

Although these studies are preliminary, they highlight a promising new avenue for treating CNP: microbiota modulation therapy. A critical challenge lies in translating laboratory findings into clinical practice. Double-blind randomized clinical trials are yet to be conducted. How can we ensure the continuity, reliability, and standardization of microbial sources used in FMT? How can we identify patient populations most likely to benefit from microbiome modulation therapy and predict treatment outcomes? Clearly, there is still a long way to go in overcoming these hurdles.
