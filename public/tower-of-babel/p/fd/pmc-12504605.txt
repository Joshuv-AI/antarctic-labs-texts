Accurate velocity estimation from surface-consistent residual statics
Authors: Ahmad Muhammad, Paul Edigbue, Sherif Hanafy, Umair bin Waheed, Abdullatif Al-Shuhail
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a novel methodology for estimating accurate Normal Moveout (NMO) velocities by utilizing residual time shifts resulting from imperfect NMO corrections. The proposed method uses the residual static component that results from using incorrect NMO velocity to estimate a better velocity that minimizes this component of the statics solution. The proposed algorithm is tested on synthetic and real seismic datasets demonstrating its robustness and high accuracy. When compared with the best velocity obtained by an experienced processor, the velocity estimated via the proposed method shows only a 0.05% deviation from the true model velocity. Furthermore, application to real 2D seismic data from East Texas resulted in an estimated NMO velocity value that differed by only 0.6% from the value derived by an experienced processor. The algorithm minimizes the effects of human error, enhances seismic event flattening, and ultimately improves the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), making it a valuable tool for subsurface imaging in complex geological environments.Keywords: Normal moveout (NMO) velocity, Seismic data processing, Residual static correction, Velocity estimationSubject terms: Geophysics, Seismology

Introduction

Velocity analysis serves as a fundamental component in the processing of seismic data, offering vital insights for the delineation of subsurface structures through the estimation of layer thicknesses and depths1. Conventional velocity analysis typically employs a hyperbolic approximation, which characterizes the two-way travel time (TWT) of seismic waves as a hyperbolic function of the source–receiver offset. This method is effective for horizontally stratified or gently dipping layers at short offsets; however, it faces considerable limitations when applied to greater offsets, steeply dipping reflectors, or complex near-surface conditions. Additionally, reliance on conventional velocity estimates for normal moveout (NMO) correction may result in stretching effects in shallow seismic events, which can artificially increase wavelengths and compromise the quality of the stacked data2.

Moreover, previous studies have investigated the effects of non-hyperbolic and anisotropic phenomena. For instance, Alkhalifah & Tsvankin (1995) illustrated that the traveltime characteristics in transversely isotropic (VTI) media can be articulated using a limited set of parameters, which includes an anellipticity parameter η alongside NMO velocity3,4. This framework facilitates enhanced velocity analysis and imaging in shale-type anisotropic environments3,5,6. Blias (2007; 2009) conducted a thorough assessment of various NMO approximations and introduced novel long-offset expressions that occasionally explicitly include average velocity; these alternatives yield better fits for certain layered models and offset ranges, demonstrating that the selection of moveout law significantly influences the estimated interval and RMS velocities at larger offsets7–9. Our methodology connects residual statics with velocity estimation through surface-consistent corrections, indicating that biases arising from an insufficient moveout law (such as the assumption of a simple hyperbolic form in the presence of non-hyperbolic behavior) can affect joint statics and velocity estimates. The approach presented in this study can be adapted to include non-hyperbolic or anisotropy-aware traveltime models as necessary.

To increase the accuracy of velocity estimation, sophisticated techniques such as velocity spectrum analysis and constant velocity stacks (CVS) have been introduced10,11. Velocity spectrum analysis involves the transformation of time-offset (T-X) data into a velocity-spectrum domain to pinpoint stacking velocities that optimize semblance, whereas CVS employs iterative constant velocity corrections on common midpoint (CMP) gathers to align reflectors and improve coherence12. Despite their effectiveness, these methodologies exhibit high sensitivity to near-surface irregularities, such as fluctuations in the weathering layer velocity and undulating topography, highlighting the essential need for precise velocity analysis.

Corrections for statics are essential for addressing time delays that arise from near-surface heterogeneities, including variations in topography and velocities of weathered strata. Elevation statics are employed to adjust for differences in the elevations of seismic sources and receivers, whereas refraction statics utilize first-break analysis to identify and correct long-wavelength anomalies13. Additionally, residual statics enhance the accuracy of seismic data by compensating for short-wavelength variations that result from lateral velocity discrepancies within the weathering layer14. The conventional approach to estimating residual statics involves breaking down the total static time shift into components related to the source, receiver, structure, and normal moveout (NMO) correction15,16.

Existing methodologies often consider residual statics and NMO corrections as distinct, iterative processes, neglecting to account for their interdependence17. This separation can result in cumulative errors, particularly in complex subsurface environments where near-surface layers exhibit significant heterogeneity. Such inaccuracies are commonly linked to the initial NMO velocity estimates obtained from traditional velocity analysis, which are subsequently applied to perform static corrections in challenging near-surface conditions18.

This study proposes a novel approach that directly integrates residual statics with the NMO correction process to address these limitations, enabling accurate velocity estimation. By developing a linear inversion framework that explicitly correlates residual statics with the imperfect NMO component, this approach establishes a strong relationship for accurately determining NMO velocities. The application of the correct NMO velocity obtained through this method significantly mitigates the static effects linked to velocity inaccuracies, thereby effectively reducing the overall error in the seismic imaging process. The efficacy of the proposed approach is demonstrated through validation with both synthetic and actual 2D datasets, demonstrating its ability to address challenges posed by near-surface complexities and irregular reflection times.

Synthetic and real datasets used to test the method

Synthetic seismic data

The model used in this study represents a two-dimensional elastic seismic dataset generated via conventional 2D seismic data acquisition techniques. This model extends over a profile length of 5 km and includes a dune situated in the middle (Fig. 2). The dune reaches a maximum elevation of approximately 100 m above the flat boundary of the uppermost layer, creating considerable statics due to the elevation and velocity differences of sources and receivers on the dune.

The spatial arrangement of sources and receivers in the acquisition setup is characterized by spacings of 1 m and 20 m, respectively. To reduce the impact of surface-related ghost reflections, all sources and receivers are positioned 15 m beneath the surface. The P-wave velocity at the sources and receivers on the dune is the average velocity from the surface to the depth of the source or receiver. According to Eq. (9), the vertical one-way travel time from the surface to a depth of 15 m is calculated to be 0.0197 s21. As a result, the minimum P-wave velocity is estimated to be approximately 761 m/s, which is subsequently rounded to 700 m/s. Assuming that the S-wave velocity is half that of the P-wave velocity, the minimum S-wave velocity is determined to be 350 m/s. The maximum P-wave velocity in the model is that of the second layer, which is 5000 m/s. The density of all the layers, including the dune, is assumed to be constant at 2000 kg/m³. A 10 Hz zero-phase Ricker wavelet is employed for the simulation, yielding a maximum usable frequency of 25 Hz.

Grid cell dimensions of 2.8 m, rounded to 2.5 m, are utilized for both the x- and z-axes, which are calculated from the minimum velocity (350 m/s) and the maximum usable frequency (25 Hz) and requiring at least 5 grid cells in the minimum wavelength. To satisfy the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) stability criterion, the simulation time step is calculated from the grid cell size (2.5 m) and maximum velocity (5000 m/s) to be 0.000355 s, which is rounded to 0.0003 s.

The synthetic dataset was processed via a conventional 2D seismic data processing workflow. Amplitude gain was performed to mitigate the impacts of geometrical spreading. The ground roll was suppressed via frequency-wavenumber (FK) filtering techniques. Deconvolution improved the vertical resolution, sharpened the data, and normalized the amplitudes to maintain consistent reflection energy throughout the dataset. The data was subsequently sorted into the CMP domain, and a single CMP near the dune was selected for further analysis.

Real seismic data

The proposed method was evaluated also via a 2D seismic land dataset from East Texas, USA, which was previously presented in Mousa and Al-Shuhail22. It consists of 18 shot gathers, each with 33 channels, with varying shot spacing and a consistent receiver spacing of 67 m. In total, the dataset comprises 594 traces, with each trace containing 1501 samples recorded at a 2 ms sampling interval. The processing included amplitude gain, spiking deconvolution, band-pass filtering, amplitude balancing, and CMP sorting. A CMP was selected then to test the proposed method.

Results and discussion

Results from synthetic data

The impact of the dune on the synthetic seismic dataset is most prominently observed in CMP gathers near the dune. As a result, the CMP at X = 2000 m (i.e., CMP 2000) located at the edge of the dune was chosen to test the proposed method. The impact of the dune extends beyond direct arrivals; it also alters sections of both P-wave and S-wave events, leading to a significant decrease in the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) in the central area of CMP 2000 (Fig. 3). This central zone, which is directly influenced by the dune, shows a significant decline in data quality compared with the adjacent flanks of the CMP, which remain largely unaffected. The seismic events on these flanks maintain a higher quality, offering a clearer representation of subsurface reflections. For further analysis, the P-wave reflection event was selected as the primary focus because of its relative clarity in comparison to the S-wave reflections. Subsequently, normal moveout (NMO) correction was applied to the dataset using an intentionally incorrect velocity of 4000 m/s. This erroneous velocity resulted in P-wave reflection, which has a zero-offset travel time (0) of 1.0 s, displaying a distinct concave-down curvature instead of the anticipated horizontal alignment, as shown in Fig. 4.

Results from real seismic data

The real seismic dataset used in this study underwent a series of preprocessing steps, culminating in CMP sorting. CMP 225 (Fig. 8) was subsequently selected as the focus for implementing the proposed methodology aimed at estimating the appropriate NMO velocity from residual static corrections. The selection of CMP 225 was based on its relatively greater fold, indicating a greater number of traces per CMP, and its superior SNR compared with other CMPs in the dataset, thereby facilitating a more reliable analysis. The analysis within CMP 225 concentrated on a significant hyperbolic reflection event with an apex at about 1.9 s, as shown in Fig. 8b. This particular event was selected to enable a thorough assessment of the proposed algorithm. The observed (i.e., “true”) NMO velocity value for this reflector was 9581 ft/s, which was determined by an experienced processor using the velocity spectrum method based on the semblance and flattening criteria (Fig. 9). Conclusions

This study introduces an effective methodology for accurately estimating the NMO velocity using the residual static component associated with using an incorrect velocity for NMO correction. The proposed approach has been rigorously validated using both synthetic and real 2D seismic datasets, demonstrating its robustness and accuracy. The synthetic data was generated from a velocity model that included two horizontal layers overlain by a sand dune exhibiting a complex velocity profile. We observed that the estimated NMO velocities by the proposed method were within 10% of the true NMO velocity of the layer in the velocity model with an average NMO velocity that is only 0.01% from the model value.

The application of this methodology to real 2D seismic data from East Texas further substantiates its practical applicability. The NMO velocities estimated by the proposed method were confined within 1% range from the true NMO velocity of the selected reflector as derived from traditional velocity analysis performed by an experienced processor. Furthermore, the average of the estimated NMO velocities was only 0.6% from this true NMO velocity value. These tests of the method indicate that it can significantly reduce the effects of picking a wrong velocity value for NMO correction by the processor by employing its influence on residual statics.

Despite the manual picking of the total residual statics used in the illustration of the proposed method on synthetic and real data sets, standard automatic methods for picking residual statics can be used such as the pilot-trace method12.
