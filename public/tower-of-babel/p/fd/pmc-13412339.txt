Relative Superiority: A framework for military and civilian trauma care based on special operations warfare
Authors: Kanwal Singh, Pierre-Marc Dion, Verity Tulloch, Andrew Beckett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT:The foundational principles in trauma care align with the operational strategies of Special Forces. Here we present a novel perspective on trauma care drawn from the book “Spec Ops—Case Studies in Special Operations Warfare: Theory and Practice” (1995) by Admiral (Ret’d) William H. McRaven. The book introduces the concept of Relative Superiority (RS), defined as a point in time when a smaller, well-trained, and well-prepared force gains a decisive advantage over a larger adversary. Achieving and maintaining RS requires precise timing and coordinated action. As time progresses, the opportunity to gain or retain this advantage diminishes. In trauma care, RS can be compared with the critical window for achieving hemorrhage control, timely resuscitation, and rapid transition to damage control surgery. Delays in these interventions decrease survival probability. If hemorrhage control is achieved but not sustained, re-establishing it becomes increasingly difficult and often fatal. This framework is illustrated through two trauma case studies. In Case Study I, timely interventions result in sustained control and survival. In Case Study II, delays led to only temporary control, which was subsequently lost, contributing to patient death. Within the RS framework, rapid surgical access can shift the advantage by attenuating hemorrhage and mitigating shock; however, speed alone does not guarantee survival. Achieving and maintaining RS requires sustained hemostasis, access to resources, and delivery of high-quality care. Applying the RS framework to trauma care offers a structured approach to evaluating the timing and effectiveness of interventions during critical phases of care. Its integration into Morbidity and Mortality discussions may help identify missed opportunities, guide performance improvement, and inform system-level changes. This conceptual model may support trauma teams in understanding how small gains at key moments influence overall outcomes in high-acuity clinical scenarios. RELATIVE SUPERIORITY IN SPECIAL OPERATIONS AND TRAUMA CARE

Modern special operations warfare and trauma care share fundamental similarities, as success in both realms is bound by timely execution. Whether it is a special operations mission or trauma management, both involve high-stakes outcomes where specific objectives or interventions must be achieved with precision and timeliness to achieve positive outcomes. In the book, “Spec Ops—Case Studies in Special Operations Warfare: Theory and Practice” (1995), retired United States Navy Admiral and Navy SEAL William H. McRaven analyzed special operation missions to develop the concept of “Relative Superiority” as a framework for how success is achieved in special operation missions.1

In the context of special operations, McRaven defines Relative Superiority (RS) whereby a generally smaller attacking force gains a decisive advantage over a larger or well-defended enemy force.1 RS is governed by three general properties:RS is achieved at the pivotal moment in an engagement. This is considered the threshold point, whereby the probability of success outweighs the probability of failure (ie, overcoming the last line of defense).1 Once RS is achieved, it must be sustained to guarantee victory. That is, continued suppression of the enemy.1 If RS is lost, it is difficult to regain. Inherently special forces lack firepower relative to a large conventional force.1

The RS framework can be depicted as a graph (Fig. 1A), with the x-axis representing time and the y-axis representing the probability of mission completion.1 The point of vulnerability marks the start of the mission, the RS line is the pivotal threshold, and the area of vulnerability (AV) is a function of mission completion over time.1 As the engagement prolongs, the inherent challenges of war make the likelihood of mission success increasingly difficult.1

Translating this framework to trauma care, immediate hemorrhage control, airway and respiratory management, and resuscitation using blood products serves to reduce the AV (Fig. 1B).1–3 The pivotal threshold of RS is ultimately achieved with definitive surgical hemostasis, which in most trauma cases occurs in the operating room (OR; Fig. 1B).3,4 Although many surrogates have been extensively studied and used to define mortality benchmark timelines to predict trauma outcomes;5 however, rapid achievement of these benchmarks creates an opportunity for success but does not guarantee it. The decisive moment occurs when surgical hemostasis is achieved, which is analogous to attaining RS during Special Forces operations. In trauma, “victory” may be defined by what happens next in the OR, where definitive hemorrhage control must be achieved and whether it can be sustained to preserve end-organ perfusion. As in special operations, RS must be secured quickly and sustained; once hemorrhage control is lost, irreversible hemorrhagic shock follows, and the opportunity for recovery dramatically decreases.

In addition to the three properties of RS, special operations are guided by six principles: simplicity, security, repetition, surprise, speed, and purpose.1 Ideally, a special operation mission consists of a simple plan, carefully concealed, repeatedly and realistically rehearsed, and executed with surprise, speed, and purpose.1 These principles can be similarly applied to trauma care. The medical team led by the surgeon must have a simple plan, carefully constructed, repeated and realistically practiced, and executed with precision, speed, and purpose.

Nonetheless, the concept of RS can be meaningfully applied to trauma as a dynamic model, whereby survival depends on timely intervention, resuscitation, and sustained comprehensive critical care to mitigate shock. To demonstrate RS in trauma care, we present the following two case studies. The first case exemplifies the rapid achievement and sustainment of RS, while the second case exemplifies how RS can be tenuously achieved and lost. We aim to provide a unique perspective of trauma care through the lens of McRaven’s RS framework to guide the future of trauma care.

CASE STUDIES: TRAUMA SCENARIOS AND RELATIVE SUPERIORITY

Case Study 1

This case involves an individual who was stabbed in the heart (Injury Severity Score: 29). Despite this life-threatening injury, RS was achieved and sustained. The prompt response of Emergency Medical Service and patient transfer to a level 1 trauma center within 37 minutes, combined with the rapid activation of trauma and OR teams and the provision of necessary resources before the patient arrived, led to a favorable outcome (Fig. 1C, Table 1).

Initial patient assessment revealed pericardial tamponade and signs of hemorrhagic shock. The patient underwent intubation, arterial line placement, initiation of massive hemorrhage protocol (MHP), and a thoracotomy in the trauma bay. The patient experienced two separate episodes of asystole, followed by a successful return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC). The second ROSC was achieved after a cardiac massage. RS was achieved when the patient was transferred to the OR for surgical hemostasis 18 minutes after sustained ROSC. The prompt transfer to the OR increased the likelihood of successful surgical hemostasis, involving trauma laparotomy, trauma thoracotomy, cardiac and diaphragm repairs, and ligation of the distal left anterior descending artery, alongside ongoing MHP. Following successful hemostasis, the patient was transferred to the ICU 212 minutes after the point of injury.

Collectively, the immediate transfer to a trauma center, early activation of the trauma and OR teams, adequate resource availability, and prompt transfer to the OR after sustained ROSC allowed for RS to be achieved, thereby optimizing the odds of survival. These efforts by the medical team led to successful resuscitation and surgical hemostasis, despite the life-threatening nature of the injury and two episodes of asystole. This case mirrors special warfare concepts in achieving victory through swift and decisive action.

Case Study 2

This case involves an individual who was stabbed multiple times in the abdomen (Injury Severity Score: 9). In contrast to the first case, here, RS was tenuously achieved and lost during attempts at surgical hemostasis. Emergency Medical Service promptly transported the patient to a level 1 trauma center within 32 minutes. Upon arrival, the patient displayed signs of hemorrhagic shock and had a traumatic arrest. CPR was initiated, and a thoracotomy was performed with cardiac massage. The patient was intubated, and MHP was activated. ROSC was achieved 16 minutes later (Fig. 1D, Table 2).

However, concurrent ongoing operative cases resulted in a delayed transfer to the OR, requiring an additional OR team to be called from home. In conjunction with MHP, a resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta was deployed as a temporary measure. The patient was taken to the OR 55 minutes after sustained ROSC for surgical hemostasis. The prolonged OR delay and profound blood loss resulted in only tenuous RS being achieved. Despite efforts at surgical hemostasis, including trauma laparotomy, trauma thoracotomy, and repair of hepatic vessels and liver, alongside ongoing MHP, the likelihood of success was decreased. The patient was transferred to the ICU with a temporary abdominal closure, on-going﻿ MHP, on three high-dose vasopressors, and remained acidotic and hypothermic. The patient was pronounced dead 392 minutes after the point of injury.

This second case exemplifies the importance of achieving RS early and sustaining RS until the task is completed to increase the likelihood of successful outcomes. The delay to the OR due to resource constraints, coupled with significant blood loss evident by asystole upon arrival, and the need for resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta, was likely beyond the capabilities of MHP and surgical intervention. In line with the third property of RS, once hemostasis control was lost, hemostasis was difficult to regain and was ultimately never regained.

APPLICATION OF RELATIVE SUPERIORITY IN MORBIDITY AND MORTALITY

Morbidity and Mortality (M&M) conferences serve to identify system and provider factors that contribute to adverse events or outcomes with the goal of elevating the standard of care.6 The concept of RS offers a novel framework for evaluating trauma care in M&M conferences. In trauma care, the effectiveness of early interventions to attain hemostasis through damage control resuscitation and surgery directly impacts patient survival rates within the 3- to 6-hour window.7,8 This critical window of time is analogous to special operations, emphasizing the critical nature of swift and effective action in both settings﻿. The RS model in trauma M&M conferences provides a framework to detail whether interventions were timely and effective, paralleling military tactics where success hinges on gaining an advantage at a pivotal moment.1 In the context of hemorrhage, we identify this critical moment as achieving RS when surgical hemostasis is initiated amid ongoing MHP.

Furthermore, the RS framework serves as a visual tool for identifying areas of improvement within trauma care delivery, aiming to decrease the AV to improve patient outcomes. By identifying delays and inefficiencies, health care professionals can target specific areas for training enhancements, simulation training, protocol adjustments, and system improvements, such as adequate staffing, eliminating barriers to rapid OR access, standardized MHP processes, and streamlined transfer of accountability. In addition, implementing prehospital blood products, such as whole blood or dried plasma, could prove beneficial,9,10 as evident in the second case.

CONCLUSIONS

Previous trajectory-mapping of trauma patients has demonstrated that the physiologic window for meaningful intervention to alter mortality was between 20 and 40 minutes after injury compared with the traditional “golden hour.”5 This critical window varies according to mechanism of injury, severity, and physiologic state, with penetrating and hypotensive trauma showing the most compressed timelines.11 Within the RS framework, this window represents a transient physiologic threshold, whereby initiating surgical hemostasis may shift the advantage by attenuating hemorrhage and mitigating shock. However, speed to OR alone is insufficient and does not guarantee survival, given that most severely injured patients often reach the OR earliest yet exhibit high mortality.11 Accordingly, achieving RS in trauma underscores that trauma patients not only require rapid surgical access but also effective prehospital care and early blood-based resuscitation to meaningfully improve survival.

Implementing the RS framework to trauma care demonstrates the value of integrating military strategic concepts into medical practice. The principles of simplicity, speed, and purpose, essential to achieving RS in the military setting, are equally pertinent to the successful management of trauma cases. Trauma survivability follows a “logarithmic” decline; without timely intervention, patients progress toward irreversible hemorrhagic shock. Early hemostasis and blood resuscitation can minimize the downward trajectory; however, sustaining the slope with comprehensive critical care achieves success. Therefore, “victory” requires more than timely achievement of RS; it depends on deliberate preparation, access to appropriate resources, and the delivery of high-quality care, which together can be the difference between life and death.
