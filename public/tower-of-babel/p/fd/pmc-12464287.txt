Ground settlement induced by NATM tunneling and surface loads in Shiraz metro station
Authors: Arif Khan, Yong Fang, Sulaiman Khan, Basit Ali, Yifan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Urban tunneling, especially in the construction of shallow metro stations, can lead to significant ground movements that may affect the safety and integrity of nearby surface structures. This research explores how surface settlements develop during NATM-based tunnel excavation, focusing particularly on the role of ground surface loads from traffic and buildings. The study centers on a real-world subway station in Shiraz, Iran, and employs the FLAC3D v9 software to simulate ground responses during excavation using the finite volume method. Initial modeling, carried out in the absence of surface loading, showed a maximum ground settlement of 62.3 mm. Subsequently, variable surface loads, including traffic and building weights, were applied to the ground surface above the metro station. The results demonstrated a linear increase in surface settlement with increasing surface loads. For instance, the load of a 3-storey building elevated the settlement to 73.3 mm, while that of a 9-storey building further increased it to 93.4 mm (21.5% increase). Similarly, traffic loads exhibited proportional effects on settlement. In a final analysis mimicking the conditions at Esteghlal Station, a combined surface load consisting of 20 kPa traffic load and a 3-storey building load was applied. The findings revealed that the lateral stress imposed by side buildings mitigated vertical stresses on the station, thereby reducing surface settlement. The lateral load from buildings above the metro station decreased maximum surface settlement by approximately 5 to 10 mm (6.5–14.8% reduction). These results suggest that moderate lateral loads from side surface structures can positively influence settlement behavior during urban tunneling projects.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of urban areas and the scarcity of available surface space have necessitated the increased construction of underground structures in recent decades. Among these, underground transportation networks in metropolitan regions have emerged as effective solutions for mitigating traffic congestion and reducing associated costs. Consequently, the assessment of risks associated with urban tunneling has gained significant importance. The development of subway stations, in particular, has progressed extensively across major cities worldwide. However, the construction of subway stations in shallow-buried, soft ground conditions often induces disturbances in the surrounding soil, leading to surface settlement1. Furthermore, the impact of ground movements resulting from the construction of subway systems on nearby surface structures and utilities has been extensively studied2. Excessive surface settlement beyond permissible thresholds can result in significant damage to surrounding buildings, infrastructure, and surface facilities3–17. Numerous recent examples highlight collapses and accidents associated with tunneling project construction. Therefore, it is imperative to identify the underlying sources of ground movements and to accurately estimate their magnitude and spatial extent prior to the commencement of tunneling operations18.

Numerous studies have addressed the challenges and methodologies for analyzing and predicting surface settlement during metro station construction, which can be broadly grouped into three main themes:

Excavation methods and their influence on settlement

Several works have examined how excavation approach and sequencing affect ground deformation. Kavvadas19 analyzed NATM excavation for the Athens underground metro station and showed that settlement was highly sensitive to soil properties and excavation sequence, with increased excavation steps and denser temporary supports significantly reducing deformation. Liu et al.4 introduced the tunnel-column method for the Beijing metro station, achieving good agreement between numerical analyses and in situ measurements. Farias et al.20 highlighted the role of excavation speed, face distance, and lining in controlling settlement. Walter21 used time-dependent 3D modeling for Vienna’s Taborstraße station, evaluating different excavation techniques. Fang et al.11 applied the shallow tunneling method (STM) for 19 Beijing stations, correlating field data with numerical results. Tang et al.22 linked excessive settlement to groundwater leakage, stressing its importance in shallow tunneling. Zhang et al.23 combined dual-shield tunneling with the cavern-pile method for two-storey stations, while Cao et al.24 optimized settlement control through shield tunneling and arch excavation.

Support systems and settlement control measures

Various structural systems have been proposed to maintain stability during excavation. Sadaghiani and Dadizadeh25 developed the Concrete-Arch Pre-Supporting System (CAPS) to enhance stability and reduce settlement. Valizadeh Kivi et al.26 applied the Central Beam–Column (CBC) method in Tehran Metro for settlement control. Liu et al.27 used 3D finite-difference modeling to show that approximately 80% of settlement originated from lateral and middle pilot tunnel excavation, highlighting the importance of support arrangement. Li and Wang28 assessed the effect of drift excavation sequence and support type on settlement. Xu et al.29 demonstrated how the pile-beam-arch (PBA) method could reduce space requirements and control loads, while Guo et al.30 noted that pilot tunnels and arches played key roles in settlement mitigation.

Surface load effects and soil-structure interaction

Although most research has focused on tunneling rather than stations, some studies have addressed the effect of surface structures. Liu et al.31 examined settlement under building loads, while Yu et al.32 found that settlement beneath strip foundations was roughly twice that of pile foundations, also noting the influence of soil, groundwater, and construction sequence. Other investigations33–41 explored tunneling-induced settlement under various surface loading conditions but primarily considered tunnels along metro lines, not shallow buried stations.

These studies collectively highlight significant progress in excavation and support techniques but also reveal a gap in understanding the combined effects of building and traffic loads on shallow NATM constructed metro stations a gap addressed in the present work.

Although several studies have examined surface settlements induced by NATM excavations, limited attention has been paid to the combined influence of surface building and traffic loads on surface ground displacements, particularly in shallow-buried metro stations. This study addresses this gap by employing numerical simulations using an advanced soil constitutive model to evaluate the interactive effects of these surface loads on settlement behavior. In this research, FLAC3D v9, a finite volume method (FVM) software, was employed to simulate surface settlements induced by subway station excavation. The research begins with an introduction to the case study and a detailed description of the geological characteristics of the region. Subsequently, surface settlement resulting from traffic loads of 20, 30, and 40 kPa, as well as from building loads corresponding to 3-, 6-, and 9-storey structures, was analyzed. All data used in this study were calibrated and validated using field measurements from the Esteghlal Metro Station in Shiraz.

Project description

Fars Province, located in the southwest of Iran, has Shiraz as its capital, one of the most populous cities in the country. The Shiraz metro system, designed to span a total length of 90 km, consists of six planned lines. Line 1 has been completed, while Line 2 is currently under construction and partially operational. Shiraz Metro Line 2, approximately 15 km in length and consisting of 13 stations, extends from Shokofeh station in the southwest to Saadi station in the northeast. The alignment of Line 2 and the location of Esteghlal station are shown in (Fig. 1).

Shiraz metro stations are designed and constructed at an average depth of 17 m below ground level. Esteghlal station, located 7.7 km along Line 2 at the intersection of Enghelab and Moshir streets, lies between Amirkabir and 15 Khordad stations. This station spans 147 m in length and 26.10 m in width, and comprises two floors. The geotechnical characteristics of the station are presented in (Fig. 2).

The selection of appropriate constitutive soil models is critical for accurate numerical modeling of the tunneling process and for predicting lining pressures. A constant stiffness approximation is inadequate for analyzing surface settlements across varying shear strain levels42. To address this, shear and volumetric hardening are incorporated into the plastic hardening (PH) soil model for soil behavior simulation. This model is characterized by a hyperbolic stress-strain relationship under drained axial compression, with elastic unloading/reloading, and stress dependency governed by a power law. It also integrates shear and volumetric hardening rules alongside the Mohr-Coulomb (MC) failure criterion43. NATM tunneling

Various construction methods are employed in metro project development, including the open-cut method, shield tunneling, the New Austrian Tunneling Method (NATM), shallow tunneling, and the drill-and-blast technique. Among these, the open-cut method, also referred to as the cut-and-cover technique, is a traditional approach that was extensively employed during the early stages of metro system development. This method involves excavating a long trench, constructing the metro tunnel within it, and subsequently backfilling it with soil. In earlier applications, brick-and-mortar construction was common, while modern projects predominantly utilize concrete. A variation of the open-cut method, known as top-down construction technique, involves the early placement the concrete top deck early in the construction process. This approach allows surface activities to resume without disruption while underground construction continues. Shield tunneling, a highly mechanized and widely adopted method globally, provides a modern and efficient alternative for metro construction. Additionally, the NATM, or related sprayed-concrete-lining techniques, is another prominent approach. NATM employs stepwise partial excavation combined with continuous monitoring and control to optimize the use of wall and roof support materials, tailored to site-specific geological conditions encountered during tunneling45.

The New Austrian Tunneling Method (NATM) was developed by Rabcewicz, Pacher, and Müller between 1957 and 196546. NATM encompasses a comprehensive set of measures implemented during and after tunnel excavation. These measures are based on a precise understanding of the geotechnical properties of the site, the timely application of appropriate retaining systems, and objective measurements. A fundamental aspect of NATM is the continuous monitoring of ground behavior which helps optimize the load-bearing capacity of the surrounding soil as serving as a key component of the tunnel support and stability system.

NATM does not represent a specific excavation or lining technique; rather, it integrates a combination of excavation and tunneling methods. Its primary distinction from other tunneling approaches lies in the systematic monitoring of ground behavior and the iterative adjustment of the lining system to ensure an economical and stable tunnel lining design. Methodology

FLAC3D 9.1 as geotechnical software

Although experimental, analytical, and statistical methods are commonly used to estimate ground surface settlement resulting from tunneling activities, these approaches often depend on empirical parameters and coefficients, that can be challenging to determine accurately. In contrast, numerical methods provide greater efficiency and flexibility, as they can accommodate complex geometries, advanced constitutive models, and detailed construction processes with higher precision48.

FLAC3D is widely recognized as a reliable and validated geotechnical software for simulating complex soil-structure interactions and analyzing geotechnical engineering problems with high accuracy. FLAC3D 9.1 utilizing explicit FVM (Finite Volume Method) codes are specifically designed to address a wide range of geotechnical problems using advanced constitutive material models and analysis techniques. This software incorporates various failure criteria, including Mohr-Coulomb (MC), Modified Mohr-Coulomb (MMC), Mohr-Coulomb-Tension (MCT), Hoek-Brown, Drucker-Prager, Plastic Hardening (PH), Cam-Clay, Soft Soil, CHSoil, CYSoil, and others. Additionally, User-Defined Constitutive Models (UDMs) can be defined and implemented alongside the existing predefined models within the software. FLAC3D supports both two-dimensional (2D) and three-dimensional (3D) modeling, making it a versatile tool for simulating and analyzing complex geotechnical conditions49.

Numerical simulation of the NATM metro station

In this research, Finite Volume Method (FVM) by FLAC3D 9.1 was employed to simulate the station and investigate the impact of surface loads on ground surface settlement. The excavation and construction of the station were carried out in a stepwise manner using the New Austrian Tunneling Method (NATM). This method involves sequential tunnel excavation along different fronts and at various times, with temporary soil columns providing stability. After each section is constructed, temporary and initial supports and linings are dismantled, and the remaining soil column is excavated, leading to the completion of the station body44.

The dimensions of the model utilized in this simulation were carefully optimized through sensitivity analysis to minimize the influence of side and bottom boundary effects. The model dimensions were selected to avoid overly small dimensions that could distort simulation outcomes, as well as excessively large dimensions that would increase computational complexity. Thus, the model has a width of 200 m in the X direction and a height of 70 m in the Y direction. The upper layer is 12 m high, while the lower layer extends to a height of 58 m, with a soil overburden of approximately 7.2 m above the station crown, considering the construction of two underground floors. Additionally, the groundwater level is positioned at a depth of 20 m below the ground surface (see Fig. 4). In the numerical modeling process, groundwater was incorporated by applying hydrostatic pore water pressure. Furthermore, the thickness of the third dimension (Z) in the model was set to one meter. Specific boundary conditions were applied to enable a two-dimensional plane-strain analysis of the zero and one-meter-thick Z-plates. This approach ensured an accurate simulation of the behavior and interactions within the modeled sections while maintaining computational efficiency.

The soil was modeled using a nonlinear elastoplastic Plastic Hardening (PH) constitutive model, while the tunnel lining was represented by an isotropic linear elastic constitutive model. For the initial support structures, shell elements were used, with the temporary shotcrete and piles modeled as solid elements, with a thickness of 300 mm and a diameter of 500 mm, respectively. The final tunnel lining was also simulated using solid elements. During the installation of the final lining, the temporary and initial linings were removed, and the thickness of the final lining was incorporated into the numerical model by combining the thicknesses of the initial and final linings.

The density and Poisson’s ratio of the initial, temporary, and final linings were assumed to be 2500 kg/m2 and 0.2, respectively, in the numerical calculations. The compressive strength values for the initial and temporary linings were set at 25 MPa, while the final lining was assigned a compressive strength of 35 MPa. Standard boundary conditions were applied: the upper boundary was free, the side boundaries were constrained horizontally while allowing vertical movement, and the lower boundary was fixed in both horizontal and vertical directions. In describing the excavation process, it is important to emphasize that all modeling stages are based on actual construction procedures, as illustrated in Figs. 4 and 5, and Fig. 6, using the NATM tunneling method. The process begins with the excavation of the central section of the upper floor, followed by the installation of temporary support and central piles to ensure interim stability and act as a fulcrum for subsequent stages. Subsequently, the side sections (Sects. 2 and 3) of the upper floor are excavated in four stages, with temporary support installed in each section. Upon completing the upper floor excavation, selected portions of the temporary support are removed, while the initial support is retained along the outer edges and the final support is installed. A similar sequence is followed for the basement level, where each section is temporarily excavated and supported before the final support is applied. As previously noted, in this numerical modeling, the final support is simulated using solid elements, with each construction stage modeled as a distinct element, enabling a sequential representation of tunnel excavation and support installation in the numerical framework.

Investigating the effect of building load

To evaluate the impact of building loads on surface settlement, 3-, 6-, and 9-storey buildings were placed across the width of the station, and the station was subsequently excavated. The resulting surface settlement curves for the no-load condition, as well as for the scenarios with 3-, 6-, and 9-storey buildings, are shown in Fig. 8. These curves demonstrate a progressive increase in surface settlement as building loads increase.

Under the load of a 3-storey building, a maximum surface settlement of 73.3 mm was observed, compared to 62.3 mm for the free surface condition with no load. Similarly, the application of a 9-storey building load resulted in an increased maximum surface settlement of 93.4 mm. Additionally, the effect of the building’s surface loads decreases with distance from the tunnel’s center, and at a distance of 60 m from the station, the building loads no longer affect the maximum ground surface settlement. The maximum surface settlement as a function of increasing building weight is illustrated in Fig. 9, where a load of 50 kPa per three-storey unit was considered. The relationship is linear, indicating that maximum surface settlement increases proportionally with the weight of the building.

Simultaneous of the effect of Building and traffic loads

In this scenario, consistent with the conditions at Esteghlal station, a central traffic load of 20 kPa with a width of 40 m is applied along the station’s axis and its asymptotes on both sides, while a 3-storey building is simulated. The results, illustrated in Fig. 12, compare cases with no surface load, only the surface load from the side building, and the combined surface load of traffic along the station axis. The findings reveal that the load from the side building introduces lateral stress due to Poisson ratio mechanics, which mitigates the vertical stress on the station axis. Consequently, this interaction reduces the impact of the traffic load and the associated surface settlement.

Conclusion

In this study, the excavation and construction process of the Esteghlal metro station in Shiraz was simulated using the finite volume method (FVM) in FLAC3D v9, following the principles of the NATM method. The sequential excavation of different cross-section parts and the associated stress relaxation were modeled in plane strain, considering the influence of building loads, traffic loads, and their combined effects on surface settlement.

Validation of numerical results: The maximum surface settlements predicted by the model were compared with in situ monitoring data obtained during the actual construction of the Esteghlal station. The difference between numerical predictions and field measurements was within 13.5%, which we consider satisfactory for urban tunneling applications. Furthermore, the overall settlement patterns and magnitudes were consistent with trends reported in similar NATM station studies (e.g.31,32, providing further confidence in the robustness of the results.

Mechanism interpretation: The simulations confirmed that surface settlement increases approximately linearly with increasing building and traffic loads. However, when these loads are applied simultaneously, the lateral stresses generated by side buildings reduce vertical stress concentrations on the tunnel axis, leading to a settlement reduction of about 5–10 mm (6.5–14.8%). This effect can be explained by the confining influence of the building load, which alters the stress redistribution pattern around the excavation. Additionally, differences in stiffness between the upper and lower soil layers affect the load transfer path, with the softer layers amplifying the influence of surface loads.

Engineering implications and limitations: The linear load–settlement relationships obtained in this study can be used in preliminary design to estimate settlement under various surface loading scenarios in shallow-buried NATM metro stations. The findings also highlight the potential role of adjacent structures in mitigating traffic-induced settlement, which could influence construction staging and risk management strategies. Nevertheless, the study has certain limitations: (1) the use of a plane-strain condition, which captures the three-dimensional effects of excavation and load distribution with relaxation stress; and (2) the assumption of uniform soil properties within each modeled layer. Future work should incorporate full 3D analyses and account for spatial variability in soil parameters to further refine predictions.
