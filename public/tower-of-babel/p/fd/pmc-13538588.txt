Sequential mediation linking perceived government autonomy support and public-sphere water conservation intentions via autonomy satisfaction and intrinsic motivation in karst regions
Authors: Zhenglin Liang, Yi Zou, Chao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKarst regions render groundwater both invisible and vulnerable, raising the question of how autonomy-supportive governance relates to community stewardship. Grounded in Self-Determination Theory, we examined whether Perceived Government Autonomy Support is associated with Public-Sphere Water Conservation Intentions indirectly via sequential associations with Autonomy Satisfaction and Intrinsic Motivation, and whether Self-Referencing conditions these associations. We conducted a cross-sectional survey in karst communities in Guangxi, China (N = 406) and estimated a moderated serial mediation model. Results showed a positive direct association between Perceived Government Autonomy Support and Public-Sphere Water Conservation Intentions and indirect associations through Autonomy Satisfaction and Intrinsic Motivation, consistent with the sequential path from Perceived Government Autonomy Support to Autonomy Satisfaction to Intrinsic Motivation to Public-Sphere Water Conservation Intentions. Self-Referencing positively moderated the association between Perceived Government Autonomy Support and Autonomy Satisfaction, such that the association was stronger at higher levels of Self-Referencing, and the corresponding conditional indirect associations through Autonomy Satisfaction, and through Autonomy Satisfaction followed by Intrinsic Motivation, increased with Self-Referencing. These findings extend Self-Determination Theory to the governance of invisible groundwater and suggest that Self-Referencing may function as a boundary condition for autonomy-supportive communication. Practically, although the cross-sectional design and the use of self-reported measures limit conclusions about sustained water-conservation practices, autonomy-supportive, person-centred communication that signals choice and respect and leverages locally resonant narratives may support community participation and help foster water stewardship intentions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60270-x.Keywords: Public-sphere water conservation intentions, Perceived government autonomy support, Self-determination theory, Autonomy satisfaction, Intrinsic motivation, Self-referencingSubject terms: Development studies, Environmental social sciences, Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

The global mismatch between freshwater supply and demand is widening1. Sustainable Development Goal 6 sets a clear expectation that water must be managed sustainably, with particular attention to communities at the sharp end of vulnerability. In this wider frame, groundwater operates as the quiet backbone of urban–rural and agricultural security. It is hidden from view and, for that very reason, hard to govern well. Recent global evidence in Nature points to accelerating declines in most major aquifers, with recovery confined to a small minority of basins, a pattern that argues for prevention rather than repair as the organising principle of policy2. Karst systems amplify the challenge. Their fracture–cavity–conduit architecture moves water and contaminants quickly and silently. Shocks travel fast, uncertainty grows and the difficulty of ensuring fair access at community level rises sharply3. The stakes are highest for households whose livelihoods are entangled with groundwater across peak-cluster depressions, karst plateaus and tower-karst plains. Natural variability in water availability is compounded by institutional barriers, including thin information, opaque participation and high costs of engagement. These residents become water-vulnerable groups who most need mobilising and are most easily overlooked.

Guangxi sits within the heart of South China’s karst belt, one of the world’s most extensive continuous karst regions4. Hidden aquifers react sharply to pulses of pollution and drought, which pushes up both supply vulnerability and managerial uncertainty5. In China’s karst regions, water security is shaped not only by hydrogeological vulnerability but also by management-related pressures, including water quality, engineering water shortage, flood and drought losses, and uneven capacities for groundwater development and utilisation6. Against this backdrop, a governance style built mainly on post-hoc fixes and hard constraints rarely settles into a steady state. A different tack is needed. When decision processes are experienced as fair, transparent and inclusive, trust and voluntary compliance increase markedly7. Put simply, policy communication that signals choice and voice offers the right cues for perceived government autonomy support (PGAS). It strengthens residents’ sense of autonomy and identification with policy, shifting them from passive targets to willing participants and raising the intrinsic acceptance of water-saving measures8,9. Converting high-level policy into steady, day-to-day protective action therefore depends on igniting intrinsic motivation and supporting the internalisation of policy information. Demand-side approaches and behavioural change in water-use practices are proven ways to build resilience in water systems10. Intervention-oriented water research also indicates that information provision, feedback, and social-comparison strategies can improve conservation outcomes, although evidence on long-term persistence remains less extensive than evidence on short-term response11–13. Our study asks how governmental messages can be internalised by individuals in ways that travel through communities as workable public-sphere water-conservation intentions.

We adopt the usual distinction between private and public domains of environmental behaviour. Public-sphere acts comprise participation, advocacy and oversight in relation to collective water resources, rather than household conservation alone14,15. Here, public-sphere water conservation intentions (PWCI) refer to residents’ voluntary engagement in policy support, collective action and public participation grounded in environmental citizenship. Recent work argues that public-sphere determinants have been downplayed and that mapping internalisation routes which policy can influence is highly valuable for design and delivery16. Such a distinction becomes particularly salient in karst regions, where the protection of a shared but largely invisible resource depends not only on private restraint at home but also on residents’ willingness to support public policy, respond to local mobilisation, and participate in collective water stewardship. This is the immediate motivation for our inquiry.

Two questions follow. How does perceived government autonomy support (PGAS) shape PWCI through cognitive internalisation? And does Self-Referencing (SR) strengthen this pathway? To unpack how policy gets under the skin of public-sphere water-conservation intentions, we bring together Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and Narrative Persuasion Theory (NPT) in a water-governance setting. SDT suggests that social contexts can facilitate internalisation when they support basic psychological needs, including autonomy, competence, and relatedness17. Within this broader SDT framework, because PGAS is theorised as residents’ perception that government communication and policy rollout provide choice, respect, and room for self-endorsed participation, the present model focuses on autonomy satisfaction as the need-satisfaction mechanism most directly aligned with this governance perception. In practice, autonomy-supportive governance sustains intention better than control-oriented approaches in water contexts18. Meta-analytic research across domains further supports a robust pathway linking autonomy support, need satisfaction, and more self-determined motivation19,20. This theoretical choice also resonates with a broader literature on public communication and policy design. Research drawing on Self-Determination Theory has argued that public messages are more likely to elicit high-quality self-regulation when they minimise coercion and support autonomy rather than rely solely on pressure or compliance cues21. Related work in environmental communication further shows that public campaigns can be more effective when they are designed around psychologically grounded mechanisms such as efficacy, norms, emotion, and perceived relevance, rather than information transmission alone (de Vries, 2020)22. Related evidence from narrative communication research further suggests that stories can heighten perceived environmental risk and intention to act23. More broadly, recent research also shows that context and experience reshape the psychology of conservation. Experiencing extreme weather, for instance, can tighten or loosen the link between attitude and intention, indicating that internalisation unfolds within situationally salient contexts24. Taken together, these lines of research suggest that theory-informed governmental communication can shape not only what citizens know, but also how they interpret policy, whether they feel addressed by it, and how ready they are to participate in collective environmental action. These findings support our focus on autonomy-supportive communication and on Self-Referencing as a perceived message characteristic in water governance.

Literature review

Self-determination theory and water conservation intentions

SDT asks a simple question with wide reach. What turns compliance into conviction? The answer is not a switch but a spectrum that runs from amotivation through qualitatively different forms of extrinsic motivation and, at the far end, to intrinsic motivation, where people act because the activity is interesting or satisfying in itself17. Three basic psychological needs sit at the heart of this spectrum. Autonomy is the felt sense of choice. Competence is the confidence that one can cope well with challenges. Relatedness is the experience of connection with others25. When the social environment consistently supports these needs, regulation becomes more internalised and more autonomously regulated. That logic has travelled well. Beyond classrooms, clinics and workplaces26–28, SDT now informs explanations of pro-environmental action, where supportive contexts reliably predict more durable and self-regulated actions such as saving energy, joining recycling schemes and choosing low-impact travel29–33. Water conservation, however, raises the bar. It is a public good and, in many settings, a hidden one. The resource is out of sight and the feedback from individual efforts arrives slowly, which makes it harder to kindle more autonomous motivation. That is precisely why water conservation is a strong test of SDT.

A second move is overdue. Much SDT work still looks closely at micro-level interactions in homes and schools. The next step is to lift the lens to the governance arena and ask how government, as a builder of context, shapes motivational processes34. The theory is clear that contextual factors can either feed or frustrate need satisfaction, which in turn steers internalisation35. Early studies showed that a supportive climate crafted by researchers or programme teams helps people set environmental goals and stick to them36. The governance translation came with Lavergne et al.8, who argued and demonstrated that citizens’ perceived government style matters for environmental motivation. The key is not only what a policy says but how it is carried out. An autonomy-supportive style speaks to people’s psychology more directly and opens up a convincing route from policy to practice.

Even so, gaps remain that blunt progress. First, applications often cluster around highly visible practices such as energy saving and waste sorting, where outcomes are easy to see on the electricity bill or in a tidy street37. Groundwater is different. It is an invisible resource. Its degradation can be concealed and the payoff from protective actions is delayed, a combination that dampens perceived competence and complicates motivational internalisation38. Second, existing research on water conservation has focused disproportionately on household and residential contexts, while the micro-level motivational processes of residents in resource-dependent and environmentally distinctive settings, such as karst regions, remain less fully understood12,39. These limits converge on a sharper question. In the governance of invisible resources, can autonomy-supportive government still foster internalisation when risks are weakly visible and feedback from protective action is delayed. Meeting this challenge does more than broaden the map of applications. It stress-tests SDT in the kind of complex environmental problems where it needs to prove its worth.

Perceived government autonomy support in water governance

At its core, PGAS concerns how policy is experienced by residents, including whether they perceive latitude, respect, and a meaningful opportunity to have a say8. This sits squarely against a controlling governance style built on orders and penalties. Control can deliver quick compliance, but it does so by side-stepping basic psychological needs and, in the longer run, it chips away at identification with policy and can even provoke pushback40. The public administration literature has been clear on one point: when decisions are experienced as fair, transparent and inclusive, trust rises and so does willing compliance7,41. In environmental settings, PGAS is where procedural justice becomes felt, not just stated. Choice and voice invite residents to internalise policy, to see it as their own, and to move from being managed to choosing to participate. The result is a more deeply rooted acceptance of water-saving measures and a warmer emotional stake in their success42. Soft influence of this kind is more likely to outlast hard constraints, because fear tends to fade whereas identification endures.

Evidence on the ground points in the same direction. When local campaigns are perceived as autonomy-supportive, residents may be more likely to internalise the value of green behaviour and to engage in it for more self-endorsed reasons8. Likewise, in studies of waste sorting in China, stronger policy identification and a felt sense of autonomy go hand in hand with higher and more stable participation, reinforcing the view that PGAS functions as a durable psychological engine rather than a fleeting nudge43.

Autonomy satisfaction and intrinsic motivation

Our starting point is straightforward. If the context signals autonomy, people are more likely to make the cause their own. In SDT terms, PGAS is expected to nurture autonomy satisfaction, which then supports intrinsic motivation and, in turn, more stable public-sphere water conservation intentions.

The first step in this chain is the link from PGAS to autonomy satisfaction, because choice, clear communication and respect all matter. When residents sense that government offers room to decide and listens in good faith, they report a stronger feeling of volition in what they do8. Reviews of decision processes reach the same conclusion. Where procedures are experienced as fair, transparent and inclusive, trust grows and so does the inner willingness to comply45. Being respected and being heard are therefore not decorative features of governance but central to autonomy-need satisfaction. In water governance, a government that is widely seen as autonomy-supportive helps residents feel like agents of protection rather than mere executors, which lifts the chances that they will internalise policy goals.

Once autonomy needs are met, motivation changes accordingly, and Organismic Integration Theory helps explain why. People who feel they act out of genuine choice are more inclined to take in the value of the action and to cultivate intrinsic motivation that endures27. Evidence also backs this mechanism. Autonomy satisfaction reliably boosts intrinsic motivation, and the effect is pronounced where feedback is slow, as in groundwater protection, compared with quicker-win domains like energy saving46.

Finally, Intrinsic motivation helps explain whether people keep going when no one is watching. In practice, intrinsic motives tend to outperform rewards or sanctions, promoting both the uptake and the stability of conservation practices more effectively than extrinsic incentives47. A likely reason is qualitative. Intrinsic motivation carries eudaimonic well-being rather than fleeting utility, which helps actions feel aligned with one’s values and sense of self48. The result is more durable public-sphere water-conservation intentions and a deeper moral connection to water protection.

On this foundation, and drawing on prior evidence8,46, we articulate a sequential mediation model at the centre of our study, with hypotheses H2a to H2d shown in Fig. 1. This framework allows a full test of the route from autonomy-supportive governance, through autonomy satisfaction and internalised motivation, to the intentions that communities can sustain.

Self-referencing as a moderator of autonomy-support effects

NPT posits that when a narrative captures attention, fires the imagination, and stirs emotion, audiences enter a state of “transportation,” becoming more receptive to its message49. In that state, they do something crucial. They link the story to themselves. This process, known as SR, makes distant issues feel close and abstract ideas feel concrete. In doing so, it sharpens both emotional and cognitive responses, making attitudes and motivations more malleable50.

Building on this foundation, we argue that SR does not merely accompany autonomy-supportive communication; it actively strengthens it. Residents who habitually map policy messages onto their own lives interpret supportive cues from the government as personally salient. When presented with relatable models of water saving or the humane logic behind a measure, they are more likely to experience PGAS as something meant for them, which in turn better satisfies their need for autonomy. Supporting evidence confirms this direction. When information arrives in narrative form and Self-Referencing is high, people perceive autonomy-supportive signals more readily and report stronger need satisfaction51. Accordingly, this study introduces SR as a moderator within the SDT framework to illuminate why individuals exhibit differing psychological and intention-related responses under the same policy-supportive communications. On that basis, we advance our next set of hypotheses, H3a to H3c, which are displayed in Fig. 1.

H3a

SR positively moderates the association between PGAS and AS. Specifically, this association is stronger at higher levels of SR.

H3b

SR positively moderates the indirect association between PGAS and PWCI through AS. Specifically, this indirect association is stronger at higher levels of SR.

H3c

SR positively moderates the sequential indirect association from PGAS to PWCI through AS and IM. Specifically, this sequential indirect association is stronger at higher levels of SR.

Methodology

Research area and sample description

We conducted a cross-sectional survey in the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region in southern China. Guangxi is a classic karst region. Its distinctive hydrological makeup, where surface and subterranean water systems are tightly coupled, renders local groundwater exceptionally vulnerable to anthropogenic pressures52. To anchor respondents in broadly comparable exposure to water conservation concerns, we followed the province’s geomorphic ladder and drew our sample from five cities that typify its key settings. Guilin, Hechi, Baise, Nanning and Chongzuo together capture peak-cluster depressions, karst plateaus and tower-karst plains. The region’s subtropical monsoon climate sets the stage. Rain falls mainly between April and September, with a dry season from October to March. Groundwater supplies only 2.1 per cent of all water used, a small share that, in a sharply seasonal regime, signals a system vulnerable to extremes53.

The final dataset contains 406 valid cases, which is adequate for SEM based on established guidance54. The gender split is balanced and mirrors the regional census. Men number 212, or 52.2%, and women 194, or 47.8%, close to the male share reported in the Seventh National Population Census for Guangxi. The modal age band is 26 to 45 years at 56.8%. Younger adults aged 18 to 25 years account for 20.2%, while those aged 46 to 59 years account for 22.9%, which basically covers the main age segments of the local adult population. Upper secondary or below accounts for 72.2%, and tertiary education and above accounts for 27.8%. Education levels also align with the census profile55.

Measures and questionnaire design

We measured demographics and five focal constructs using seven-point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree). All instruments were adapted from validated sources to fit the study context; items were bilingual back-translated and vetted by domain experts. A pilot study (N = 263) supported scale adequacy (α = 0.815–0.857, KMO = 0.704, p < 0.001).

Procedure

Ethical clearance was granted by the Jiangnan University Research Ethics Committee (REC) under approval number JNU202506RB028. Our data were collected in the field from June to July 2025. The researchers visited designated local communities, invited residents who met the criteria to take part and secured informed consent. Participants needed to be at least 18 years old and to have lived in the community for six months or more. They were told what the study involved and reminded that participation was voluntary, responses were anonymous and the information would be used only for academic work.

Ethical clearance was granted by the Jiangnan University Research Ethics Committee (REC) under approval number JNU202506RB028. Our data were collected in the field from June to July 2025. The researchers visited designated local communities, invited residents who met the criteria to take part and secured informed consent. Participants needed to be at least 18 years old and to have lived in the community for six months or more. They were told what the study involved and reminded that participation was voluntary, responses were anonymous and the information would be used only for academic work.

The procedure was simple and consistent across sites. Each participant first viewed three posters on local groundwater protection. The posters addressed groundwater as an invisible water resource, everyday individual protection, and community collaboration in safeguarding shared water sources. Each poster featured a narrative structure comprising three interconnected yet distinct illustrated scenes. These scenes depicted either the positive impacts of water conservation or the potential negative consequences of neglecting it, accompanied by concise captions that called for personal or community action. Visually, the posters adopted a traditional Chinese picture-story layout with three vertically arranged panels. The posters served as standardized contextual visual materials and provided a light-touch thematic cue to place respondents in a shared context of karst groundwater protection before they completed the questionnaire. English-translated examples are provided in the Supplementary Materials as Figures S1–S3. Participants viewed the posters independently, without researcher-led discussion or additional prompts, and then completed the paper questionnaire on their own. If someone struggled with reading, a researcher read the items aloud in a neutral manner. Returned questionnaires were screened and cases with missing data were excluded. This yielded 406 valid cases for analysis.

SEM and hypothesis testing

The structural model exhibited good fit (χ²/df = 1.066, CFI = 0.998, TLI = 0.998, RMSEA = 0.013, SRMR = 0.022). These indices meet the conventional cutoffs (CFI/TLI ≥ 0.95; RMSEA ≤ 0.05; SRMR ≤ 0.08), indicating an acceptable model–data correspondence60,61.

Discussion

Governments set the tone for water governance, yet the leap from high-level policy to everyday conservation is not automatic, especially when the resource lies out of sight in karst aquifers. Our evidence points to three key psychological patterns. One is a pattern of sequential associations, consistent with SDT, where higher AS is linked to higher IM, which in turn is associated with greater engagement in PWCI. Another is the pattern that emerges when people hear policy in an autonomy-supportive register. The associations appear to be twofold, with a direct link to PWCI and an indirect link running through internal states. The final piece is SR. When residents map messages onto their own lives, SR strengthens the link from PGAS to AS and, in doing so, magnifies the indirect association linking PGAS to PWCI through AS and then IM. Taken together, these patterns offer a usable social-psychological account of the relationship between macro policy intent and micro-level public-sphere water-conservation intentions. In what follows, we draw out the theoretical contributions and the policy moves that flow from this pattern.

Theoretical contribution

Our first contribution is to extend the application of SDT to the challenging domain of public environmental governance, specifically in relation to an ‘invisible’ resource such as karst groundwater. We introduce PGAS as a key psychosocial antecedent, validating SDT’s central tenet regarding internalisation17. The theory holds that when a social context provides cues that support basic psychological needs, external regulations are more readily transformed into genuinely self-determined actions7. Our findings are consistent with this claim. Karst groundwater is hidden from view, the benefits are slow to register, and residents often feel distant from the problem. The motivational burden of such a resource is distinctive: residents receive few sensory cues, feedback from protective action is delayed or absent, and the problem remains psychologically distant in everyday life67,68. Under these conditions, policy internalisation requires more than simple rule transmission. Communication that signals respect and choice may therefore be especially important for supporting autonomy satisfaction. Against this backdrop, higher PGAS is associated with a different psychological pattern. When communication carries signals of respect and choice, people begin to read policy as support rather than command. Autonomy satisfaction is more likely to increase, intrinsic motivation is more likely to strengthen, and the likelihood of public-sphere engagement may improve, even when there is little immediate personal payoff. In this sense, the present work pushes SDT beyond its usual haunts in classrooms and workplaces and shows that, in China’s mixed urban–rural communities, autonomy-supportive communication may help reduce the psychological distance that invisible resources typically create.

A second step is to explain why the same message does not land the same way for everyone. SR provides that key. Individuals who habitually connect messages to their own lives show a stronger association between PGAS and felt autonomy; this stronger association is, in turn, linked to a lift in intrinsic motivation and, eventually, PWCI. This is not a cosmetic effect of storytelling. This suggests that Self-Referencing may function as a psychological factor that corresponds to a more efficient statistical path from governance cues through need satisfaction to motivational internalisation. The result is a clearer account of heterogeneity in policy responses under the same conditions.

We also show that these mechanisms hold across mixed urban–rural populations and in marginalised neighbourhoods. That matters for theory as well as practice. Prior studies have rightly underlined personal responsibility as a driver of environmental action69. Our findings forge a direct link between macro-level governance strategies and micro-level psychological workings through the moderating force of Self-Referencing. The results suggest that motivation driven by perceived justice and personal connection can foster public-sphere water-conservation intentions even without the promise of immediate rewards. This provides psychological support for a broader shift in governance thinking, moving away from a traditional top-down logic of compliance towards a collaborative model built on supported autonomy and internalized commitment. This is theoretically notable because the pattern emerges in a context where autonomy-supportive governance might, under conventional expectations, be assumed to matter less than in many Western settings. The present findings therefore suggest that procedural fairness and autonomy-supportive governance may remain psychologically consequential even under social and cultural conditions that might otherwise be presumed to dampen their relevance.

Practical and policy implications

How people take up policy depends on more than the policy itself. It also hinges on whether they see themselves in the message. When residents habitually map governmental communication onto their own lives, they read Perceived Government Autonomy Support rather than control, and they may be more inclined to act. One practical implication is that not only content but also communicative form matters, especially when it invites psychological resonance. Respectful, tailored communication that sparks self-connection can bring more voices into the room and move water governance towards a process that feels fair and within reach.

To this end, local water authorities and grassroots leaders could usefully pivot to more personalised and contextualised communication strategies. One practical starting point is to begin with the audience rather than the announcement by segmenting residents according to meaningful traits and matching each segment with a communication style that fits. When explaining rules on pollution control, lean on local examples and everyday phrasing. Speak in a register that links the collective to the personal, as in you and our hometown, so that macro concerns meet household stakes. Make the procedure visible. Spell out how people can participate, how feedback will be handled and how decisions are made. These are the cues of procedural justice that build legitimacy and trust. A further step may be to work with communities near key water sources to co-create stewardship through co-governance agreements, volunteer programmes and local media storytelling.

Visual narratives can carry much of this load in conservation education. Rather than relying on traditional campaigns limited to directive slogans, practitioners can utilise visual formats that combine illustration with accessible descriptive captions. As illustrated by the materials used in our study, by vividly depicting either the positive impacts of conservation or the potential negative consequences of neglecting it, these visual narrative designs help residents relate otherwise invisible groundwater resources to familiar routines. Because SR moderates impact, design should be adaptive. For groups high in SR, emphasise character immersion and emotional cadence. For groups lower in SR, prioritise clear causal pathways and visible evidence. In practice, narrative-rich and autonomy-supportive soft tools belong alongside the hard levers of infrastructure and regulation. Together they may help deepen public engagement and support the resilience needed for water sustainability.

Nevertheless, these implications should be read as design-oriented inferences rather than as evidence of durable change in water-stewardship practices, given the cross-sectional and self-report nature of the present data. Encouraging public engagement may be an important first step, but translating such engagement into sustained, long-term water stewardship will require ongoing community support and future longitudinal validation.

Limitations and future research

Our evidence comes from Guangxi’s karst heartland. That is both a strength and a constraint. The region’s distinctive hydrogeology, its particular mix of social development and its local history and culture may narrow what travels elsewhere. The next step is replication. Try the model in the Chinese provinces of Yunnan and Guizhou; take it to karst communities in South-East Asia and Europe; map where it holds and where it bends, so that the boundary conditions for wider use become clear. One more caution is in order. We sampled across several communities yet did not model, in a structured way, how differences in community development might shape how residents read autonomy-supportive governance. That gap is a promising target for future work.

Time is another limitation. Our cross-sectional design provides a valuable snapshot of the associations between these variables, but it cannot establish causality or the temporal precedence of these relationships. In addition, the present study examines associations among self-reported perceptions and public-sphere water conservation intentions, rather than observed water-stewardship practices under experimental conditions. Accordingly, the findings should not be interpreted as evidence of durable causal effects on actual long-term water-conservation practices. While our findings are consistent with the theoretical model we proposed, the dynamic interplay of these variables over time remains to be tested. The procedure involving visual materials marks an additional boundary. The posters were used as standardized contextual visual materials to provide a shared karst groundwater-protection context before questionnaire completion, and their role is best understood as contextual framing within the survey procedure. Future research could use controlled designs with explicit source cues and transition instructions to examine how contextual materials shape interpretations of government communication and, in turn, SR and PGAS. To build on our findings, future work could employ longitudinal designs or field experiments. Tracking communities across pre-intervention, post-intervention, and maintenance phases would allow for a much more robust validation of the temporal ordering and potential causal structure of these relationships.

Finally, PGAS remained directly associated with PWCI, which implies the presence of additional mediating variables beyond the serial pathway tested here. Future research could examine other psychological mechanisms through which autonomy-supportive governance is translated into public-sphere water conservation intentions. Given that SR alters effect strength, future studies could conduct contextualised experiments that manipulate narrative ingredients such as point of view and plot tension, and vary the salience of procedural justice cues, to observe how these changes affect SR, motivational internalisation and, ultimately, public-sphere water conservation intentions.

Conclusion

Drawing on SDT and NPT, we identify a plausible psychological pathway linking policy communication to public-sphere water stewardship. When residents read governmental messages as respectful and procedurally fair, autonomy satisfaction is more likely to increase, intrinsic motivation is more likely to be strengthened, and public-sphere stewardship may become a more sustainable orientation. When policy messages feel personally relevant, this pattern appears stronger. Self-Referencing acts as the boundary condition that magnifies the journey from perception to motivation to public-sphere water-conservation intentions. Our findings indicate that this pathway is present in China’s mixed urban–rural communities, thereby extending the practical reach of SDT. The implications align with Sustainable Development Goal 6. Water management is more likely to become fairer and more accessible when institutions support intrinsic motivation and widen community participation, helping ensure that progress includes those who might otherwise be left behind.
