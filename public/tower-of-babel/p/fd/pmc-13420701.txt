Platypnea-Orthodeoxia Syndrome
Authors: Tomohiro Kaneko, Eiichiro Sato, Nobuyuki Kagiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: bubble contrast echocardiogram, patent foramen ovale, platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome, right-to-left shunting

Platypnea-orthodeoxia syndrome (POS) has long been described as a condition characterized by dyspnea and arterial desaturation that worsen in the upright position and improve when supine.1,2 Although well described, it remains under-recognized in clinical practice and is often overlooked as a cause of hypoxemia. Traditionally, its pathophysiology has been attributed to the interplay between an interatrial communication, most commonly a patent foramen ovale (PFO), and additional anatomical or functional factors that promote right-to-left shunting (RLS). These include structural conditions affecting the heart, lungs, vasculature, or thoracic anatomy, as well as dynamic factors such as changes in preload, venous return, and body position that influence intracardiac flow.3,4

The accompanying case report in this issue of JACC: Case Reports presents a well-characterized patient with suspected POS.5 Despite only modest postural changes in oxygenation, the authors combined physiological maneuvers with invasive assessment to demonstrate RLS and to characterize its dynamic behavior under varying physiological conditions. This case is clinically informative, but it also raises an important question: how strictly should POS be defined? In this patient, the degree of orthodeoxia is modest. Even when commonly cited thresholds, such as a decrease in oxygen saturation of >5% or a reduction in PaO2 of >4 mm Hg,3 are applied, the present case might not clearly fulfill these criteria. Sitting at the margin of these definitions, it underscores a fundamental issue: POS lacks a universally accepted quantitative definition.1,6 In practice, it is often recognized as a clinical pattern rather than diagnosed according to fixed physiological cutoffs.

From this perspective, the present case illustrates that a congenital interatrial communication, such as a PFO, may remain clinically silent for decades but become clinically relevant later in life when combined with age-related anatomical remodeling and dynamic physiological perturbations. Therefore, substantial desaturation in the supine position should not exclude POS-related physiology; orthodeoxia may represent positional worsening superimposed on an already significant baseline RLS. This interpretation shifts the focus from whether the patient strictly crosses a predefined orthodeoxia threshold to whether multiple anatomical and physiological factors converge to produce clinically meaningful, dynamic RLS.

These considerations suggest that rigid categorization may be less informative than a more integrative conceptual approach. In this framework, the clinical presentation reflects not a single abnormality but the interaction of several contributing elements, including their relative magnitude and interdependence.7 Anatomical features such as PFO or atrial septal aneurysm, alterations in venous flow direction, distortion of thoracic or aortic anatomy,8 and dynamic changes in preload or intrathoracic pressure may each be clinically silent in isolation. When present together, however, they can produce a wide range of manifestations, from minimal shunting to clinically significant, posture-dependent hypoxemia.

This perspective helps explain several observations encountered in practice. Some patients with large PFOs remain asymptomatic, whereas others with relatively minor anatomical findings develop pronounced hypoxemia. It also provides a framework for understanding cases in which no clear precipitating factor is identified, as well as those demonstrating atypical or even paradoxical positional patterns.9 Notably, contemporary series have reported that a substantial proportion of patients lack an identifiable trigger, suggesting that the traditional 2-hit model may not fully capture the heterogeneity of this syndrome.

Important implications also arise for diagnostic evaluation. In many settings, assessment relies on relatively static comparisons, such as supine vs upright oxygen saturation or resting bubble studies. As illustrated by the present case, such approaches may underestimate dynamic RLS. Provocative maneuvers that alter venous return, including Valsalva or abdominal compression, can help unmask clinically relevant shunting.10,11 More physiological stressors, such as exercise, may also enhance diagnostic sensitivity in selected patients; however, their effects are likely to depend on the underlying hemodynamic profile and may not be consistent across individuals.

From a management standpoint, the benefit of closing an interatrial communication, whether percutaneously or surgically, reinforces its role in symptom generation.1 At the same time, decisions regarding intervention should not rely solely on whether a patient fulfills strict diagnostic criteria for POS. Rather, they should be guided by the overall clinical context, including the severity of hypoxemia, the relative contribution of RLS, and the likelihood of meaningful improvement after closure.

Importantly, this case highlights the limitations of current definitions and the variability inherent in its clinical expression. POS is best viewed not as a condition defined by a single numerical threshold but as a continuum shaped by the interaction of multiple factors (Figure 1). Accordingly, dynamic RLS should remain an important consideration in patients with otherwise unexplained hypoxemia, even in the absence of classical features.

POS Arises From the Interaction of Anatomic Substrates, Hemodynamic Conditions, Structural Distortion, and Physiological Maneuvers That Promote Dynamic Right-to-Left Shunting Across an Interatrial Communication

The magnitude of shunting may vary with posture and provocative conditions, producing a broad clinical spectrum from mild or subclinical oxygenation changes to classically defined POS with marked orthodeoxia. Dynamic evaluation and individualized clinical judgment are important because clinically relevant shunting may be present even when conventional diagnostic thresholds are not fully met. Ultimately, the more relevant question may not be whether a patient strictly fulfills the definition of POS but whether the combination of underlying factors is sufficient to produce clinically meaningful hypoxemia. Viewed in this way, the present case serves not only as an illustration of a syndrome but also as a reminder of the importance of interpreting physiology beyond diagnostic labels.
