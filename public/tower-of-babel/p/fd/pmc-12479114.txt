Disseminated herpes zoster infection in a varicella zoster–seropositive kidney transplant recipient
Authors: Takaaki Nawano, Hayato Nishida, Norihiko Tsuchiya
Published: 2025

A 73-year-old man on immunosuppressive therapy after renal transplantation presented to our emergency department with fever, weakness, nausea, and vomiting. Before transplantation, he was seropositive for varicella zoster virus (VZV), which suggested immunity. On presentation, his physical examination was nonspecific. His laboratory results were notable for a leukocyte count of 19.9 (reference range 33.0–86.0) × 109/L (neutrophils 97% [reference range 37.9%–76.8%], lymphocytes 1.0% [reference range 15.4%–50.9%]), C-reactive protein level of 9.7 (reference range 0.0–0.14) mg/dL, and elevated liver enzymes. Computed tomography showed granular and interstitial shadows in the lungs (Figure 1A and Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.250498/tab-related-content). We diagnosed pneumonia, started intravenous ceftriaxone, and reduced his immunosuppressant dosage. The next day, we observed vesicles and erythematous papules on the patient’s trunk and limbs (Figure 1B). We sent de-roofed vesicular fluid for VZV rapid antigen testing, which confirmed disseminated herpes zoster (HZ). We initiated intravenous acyclovir (5 mg/kg, 3 times daily). He became delirious and his level of consciousness decreased. Magnetic resonance imaging of the head was normal. Polymerase chain reaction testing of cerebrospinal fluid was positive for VZV, suggesting HZ-related encephalitis. We doubled the acyclovir dosage and the patient’s level of consciousness gradually improved. We discontinued the acyclovir 24 days after the initial HZ diagnosis, and he was discharged.

(A) Chest computed tomography scan of a 73-year-old male renal transplant recipient on immunosuppressive therapy, showing granular shadows in the lungs (arrows). (B) Vesicles and erythematous papules (5–10 mm in diameter) on the skin of the patient’s trunk.

Patients undergoing transplantation are screened for VZV immunity and vaccinated if results are seronegative. Live attenuated vaccination is contraindicated after immunosuppression.1–4 Post-transplantation, VZV-seronegative patients exposed to VZV or HZ should receive post-exposure prophylaxis with VZV immunoglobulin within 10 days.3,4 Regardless of the patient’s serostatus, disseminated VZV infection should be suspected if the recipient develops a rash not limited to a dermatomal distribution. Prompt diagnosis, early treatment that continues until lesion crusting, and reduction of immunosuppression (as appropriate) are critical. Oral antiviral agents can be used for uncomplicated varicella.3,4 Non-live recombinant HZ vaccine can be administered to immunocompromised patients to decrease infection risk.1,2 Disseminated HZ infection is rare in seropositive recipients but can become severe regardless of the patient’s serostatus.
