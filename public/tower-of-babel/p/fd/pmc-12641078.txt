A Fast Path from Innovation to Safe and Effective Medicines
Authors: Peter Arlett, Falk Ehmann, Ralf Herold, Evdokia Korakianiti, Pierpaolo Moscariello, Liese Barbier, Iordanis Gravanis, Frank Pétavy, Emmanuel Cormier, Andrew Thomson, Spiros Vamvakas, Steffen Thirstrup
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Consistent progress in medicines development has allowed both for de novo treatment options and for the refinement of existing products that improve effectiveness or reduce harm. Nonetheless, unmet medical needs persist, particularly in rare diseases, pediatrics and underserved populations. The many promises of pharmacological, technological, and regulatory innovation spark new hope for patients and doctors searching for treatments, and it is therefore essential that progress made translates into the availability of new and improved medicines. We here present our vision for accelerating the path from innovation to safe and effective medicines. Our vision is driven by the continued and transparent collaboration between regulators, academia, industry, patients, and healthcare professionals. Through concrete examples and strategic initiatives, we illustrate how the EMA works with stakeholders to support continuous evolution of regulatory science, contribute to patient‐focused refinements in evidence generation and medicines assessments, and engage in forward‐looking initiatives that monitor and examine innovative developments. The ultimate goal is to future‐proof the regulatory ecosystem and ensure that scientific progress translates into meaningful health outcomes.

At a time when medicines development is increasingly costly, 1 and the pace of development does not yet address the burden experienced by patients for whom treatment is insufficiently effective or even unavailable, innovations in medicines development, trial methodology, and novel regulatory approaches may provide new hope. 2 , 3 , 4

For new medicines to be granted a marketing authorization, there needs to be proof of efficacy and quality, and they need to convincingly show that their benefits outweigh their risks. Achieving this relies on the quality of the evidence collected. Clinical trials need to be relevant and robust for a successful marketing authorization application and reimbursement decisions, and post‐authorization monitoring of data on medicine safety and real‐world effectiveness are key to safeguard their benefits for public health. Continued improvements in the EU healthcare landscape, such as new legislation 5 and technological developments in the use of real‐world data (RWD), 6 drive continued innovation in evidence‐generation methodology, to the benefit of patients whose disease may be so rare, or assessment of treatment effects so complex, that traditional trial methodologies are insufficient.

Once data on a medicine’s quality, safety and efficacy have been collected by developers, this evidence is often the basis for both the scientific evaluation by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) that serves as the basis of an EU marketing authorization and the Health Technology Assessment (HTA) done by HTA bodies in each Member State, even though these assessments differ in scope (see section A Patient‐Centered Path: Optimizing Medicines Development and Evaluation). Efforts in evidence generation therefore need to be supported by regulatory efforts to facilitate medicines evaluation and the path to market. Using the COVID‐19 pandemic as an example, EMA’s rolling review of vaccine data 7 and the close collaboration between regulators globally 8 accelerated the path from innovation to vaccines roll‐out, and these developments have provided lessons for improvements beyond the pandemic. 9 , 10

New platform technologies, such as mRNA delivery systems in COVID‐19 vaccines 11 , 12 and vector‐ and CRISPR‐based gene therapy medicinal products, 13 , 14 have already shown their impact in preventing and treating diseases, and promising benefits in terms of rapid development, tailored therapy and treatment potential for patients with rare and difficult‐to‐treat conditions.

On the contrary, the implementation of a platform technology framework also introduces regulatory challenges (see Box 1), and highlights that the promise of innovation in medicines development and manufacturing needs to be supported by innovation in regulatory science to ensure that it translates into new medicines that are safe and effective and provide tangible benefits to public health. The development, evaluation, and monitoring of medicinal products is therefore underpinned by regulatory science, providing the rigorous scientific and technical frameworks necessary to assess their safety, efficacy, and quality. 15 , 16

Benefits and challenges of regulating platform technologies in medicine

In pharmaceutical development, the term platform technologies is not yet defined by legislation. It refers to standardized systems, methods, or technological frameworks that can be repeatedly applied across multiple therapeutic products or indications.

These platforms allow for a modular approach to innovation, enabling faster and more efficient development of new medicines by building on a proven and accepted technological base. The use of platform technologies thereby enables acceleration of product development timelines, reduces uncertainty in early‐stage research, and supports rapid scaling (e.g., in response to emerging health threats, as demonstrated by the swift deployment of COVID‐19 vaccines using mRNA platforms). Such platforms also facilitate iterative improvement and cost efficiency, as knowledge, infrastructure, and regulatory learnings can be reused across product pipelines.

But as exposure and knowledge about platforms are gained in areas like personalized medicine, oncology, and rare diseases, regulators and developers must learn and collaborate to establish a flexible, science‐based framework ensuring the safety of these platforms while fostering innovation.

Established evidence‐generating methods like extrapolation, 85 and modeling and simulation, 86 and the experience from developing and regulating biosimilar medicinal products 87 will inform and influence how regulators successfully implement platform technologies.

Innovation in medicine development is inherently progressive, aiming to address unmet clinical needs through novel approaches. For developers and regulators to keep up, it is essential they look ahead and work together, so they are prepared to seize the opportunities provided by progress in technology and regulatory tools. Across the spectrum of medicines development, innovation demands a dynamic, collaborative, and responsive approach to ensure that it leads to patients having timely access to better medicines.

In this article, we present an EMA perspective on the regulatory framework for medicines innovation, current efforts into advancing medicines development, authorization and safety monitoring, and how we work toward a continued dialogue between all stakeholders to ensure that we have a fast path from innovation to safe and effective medicines.

A SCIENTIFIC PATH: REGULATORY SCIENCE AS A FRAMEWORK FOR MEDICINES DEVELOPMENT

Regulatory science is a collection of scientific disciplines applied to both medicines research and development and the regulation of these products. All involved in medicines development, regulators, industry, academic developers, health professionals and patients, operate within the framework of regulatory science, ensuring that all therapeutic interventions meet the necessary standards before reaching patients. Regulatory science informs decision making and contributes to the continued development of regulatory standards and tools.

Research on regulatory science aims to advance medicines development and evaluation. This ensures that the medicines regulatory framework remains resilient and adaptable to the rapid advancements in medical technology and evolving healthcare needs. 15 A forward‐thinking approach is important for addressing emerging challenges and, by stimulating dynamism, ensures that the regulatory environment stays both protective and progressive, capable of adapting to the changing landscape of medical science maintaining scientific rigor in medicines assessment. Therefore, to future‐proof the EU medicines innovation ecosystem, European regulators have stated clear strategic objectives that highlight the importance of continued support to medicines innovation. 17

In order to achieve these objectives, it is essential that the medicines regulatory framework evolves in step with medical innovation and progress. To continuously improve regulatory science, research in this area encompasses a broad range of methods, including wet laboratory (e.g., generate scientific evidence on the safety and efficacy as determined by different delivery systems and routes of administration; perform quantitative analysis of how and which changes in platform technologies could impact results of non‐clinical and clinical testing of medicinal products) and desk‐based research (e.g., mapping of clinical trials with decentralized elements and perform trend analysis to describe challenges and opportunities; research into efficiency of innovative clinical trial designs, see ref. [18]), as well as diverse scientific disciplines (e.g., pharmacology, 19 toxicology, 20 biostatistics, 21 , 22 and mathematical and social sciences). By doing so, regulatory science research addresses new and recurring cross‐cutting issues in the development and evaluation of medical products, such as challenges in clinical trial design, data analysis and post‐market surveillance, which ultimately helps to improve the overall efficiency and effectiveness of medicines development and regulatory processes.

Moreover, regulatory science research aims to bridge gaps that hinder regulatory decision making. These gaps can arise from a lack of access to data and information on previous drug developments, scientific knowledge, as well as outdated regulatory frameworks or insufficient collaboration among stakeholders. By conducting targeted research, regulatory scientists can develop innovative solutions to overcome these barriers and enhance the regulatory system. 16 EMA has identified several regulatory science research needs, focusing on (1) the development of new methodologies and tools to enhance the design and conduct of clinical trials; (2) adapting to changing scientific and technological landscapes, emphasizing the importance of developing flexible and responsive regulatory frameworks that can accommodate new innovations and challenges; (3) generating evidence to facilitate the integration of new technologies into the healthcare system; and (4) advancing regulation of veterinary medicines. 16

Regulatory science research takes place in the academic sector, in for‐profit organizations and regulatory agencies. Academia is increasingly involved in providing foundational research that informs policy decisions and ensures that regulations are based on solid scientific evidence. As academic researchers act in the pre‐competitive space, where they are not driven by commercial interests, they are uniquely positioned to tackle complex regulatory challenges through research, explore high‐risk or long‐term scientific questions that can be shared across sectors and serve as neutral conveners in multi‐stakeholder collaborations. Many important advances and disruptive innovations in medicines development and clinical practices also start in non‐commercial settings, but alignment of these developments with regulatory requirements is limited, which hinders progress and translation, and needs to be strengthened through dialogue and education. 23 , 24

In 2025, EMA and the Heads of Medicines Agencies (HMA) launched the European Platform for Regulatory Science Research as a mechanism for regular engagement between researchers and regulators, to foster collaboration in identifying and tackling pressing needs in regulatory science and develop collaborations to address these needs in important areas of regulatory science. 15

There is a need to accelerate the translation of regulatory science research outputs into practical applications, ultimately benefiting patients with timely access to safe and effective medicines. For researchers and research consortia working on medicine development and regulatory science challenges, it will be beneficial to improve preparedness through regulatory strategies (such as structured plans that ensure outputs are developed in alignment with regulatory standards and can be used in regulatory decision making), embedded earlier in projects, with change management approaches incorporated to ensure proactive engagement with regulators and continual regulatory awareness. To accomplish this, and to achieve solutions that can be translated into advancements in regulatory science by the multiple stakeholders concerned, a collaborative approach is essential. 25 , 26

This involves engaging not only academic researchers, but also industry leaders, regulators, policymakers, healthcare professionals, and patients. Creating a fertile ground for collaboration ensures that regulatory science is comprehensive, inclusive and responsive to the needs of society. 27 The European Platform for Regulatory Science Research reflects EMA’s and HMA’s commitment to this goal, raising awareness among researchers and stakeholders, promoting structured exchange, encouraging collaboration across the research lifecycle, and building a larger and stronger research community for regulatory science. 26

Additionally, regulatory sandboxes (see Box 2) may serve as valuable instruments for testing and applying regulatory science solutions emerging from these interactions. They offer a confined and controlled setting in which conventional regulatory frameworks can be critically examined, while new needs, technologies, and innovative approaches are explored and validated.

Regulatory sandboxes

Regulatory sandboxes in pharma are a developing tool allowing innovators to test novel technologies, products, or regulatory approaches under the supervision of regulators for a limited period of time.

The concept, inspired by work done in financial technology, is gaining momentum in the EU’s life sciences landscape. Sandboxes can reduce uncertainty for developers, accelerate learning for regulators, and foster early dialogue around evidence requirements, safety considerations, and policy adaptation.

These frameworks aim to support the safe exploration of complex innovations—such as advanced therapies, platform technologies, digital health solutions, and AI‐driven tools—by providing a space for real‐world experimentation without immediately meeting all standard regulatory requirements.

Though still evolving, EU regulatory sandboxes complement initiatives like EMA’s Innovation Task Force 30 , 88 and Scientific Advice, 43 , 89 , 90 aligning with broader goals of adaptive regulation, patient‐centricity, and technological leadership. As Europe seeks to enhance its global competitiveness in pharmaceutical innovation, regulatory sandboxes represent a promising pathway to balance agility with oversight.

In order to ensure access to new medicines for patients, it is essential to have a regulatory environment that understands and facilitates innovation. The EMA and the HMA support the development of innovative methodologies by fostering greater collaboration, as outlined as a strategic goal in the medicines agencies network strategy 2028. 17

The EU Innovation Network (EU‐IN) was established to strengthen the collaboration between national competent authorities (NCAs) and the EMA on regulatory matters relating to emerging therapies and technologies. 28 EU‐IN aims to facilitate the development of innovative medicines and associated technologies by providing a platform to, among other activities, 29 share opinions and good practices, harmonize and increase efficiency of scientific regulatory advice and innovation support, strengthen engagement with innovators, and support the translation of strategic goals into tangible outcomes. It is comprised of representatives from national innovation offices and the EMA’s Innovation Task Force (ITF), 30 which acts as early point‐of‐contact for innovators and developers who are seeking guidance to bring their innovative therapy or technology forward (see also section: A Forward‐Looking Path: Seizing Opportunities).

In conclusion, in some areas of medicines development, for instance for products for the treatment of rare diseases or products intended for use in pediatric populations, progress is still not fast enough to provide the needed solutions even with significant efforts. Here regulators have a particular opportunity and obligation to actively support and stimulate advancements and, over time, their role has undergone a profound transformation from acting as gatekeepers to enabling innovation through development of regulatory tools that refine methodology, provide development incentives, and offer platforms where developers, regulators, academia, and patient representatives can collaborate to foster an environment conducive to innovations in medicines development. In other domains, opportunities resulting from public health challenges (e.g., diseases in the elderly and obesity) and technological and scientific advances have accelerated innovation of medicines, and here, regulators are compelled to adapt swiftly to facilitate the transition of innovation to medicines that are safe and provide meaningful benefits to increasingly large patient populations.

A PATIENT‐CENTERED PATH: OPTIMIZING MEDICINES DEVELOPMENT AND EVALUATION

Better evidence

The core of a successful marketing authorization application is excellent evidence, and efforts to streamline the evidence‐generation process. Increasing the decisional value of evidence will significantly contribute to expediting the path from innovation to safe and effective medicines for patients.

While clinical evidence remains the main driver of the benefit–risk evaluation that determines whether a medicine is authorized, there are many other factors driving change. These include both push incentives, such as the slow speed and high cost of product development, and pull incentives, such as the continued burden of unmet medical need, opportunities from greater healthcare data access, better study methods and technological advances like artificial intelligence (AI).

The EU regulatory Network has set out a vision for clinical evidence generation. 31 It places the patient voice at the center of evidence generation (Figure 1 ), which is planned and guided by data, knowledge and expertise. In this vision, the research question drives the evidence choice and embraces the spectrum of data and methods. Clinical trial data remain the core of the application, but trials are smarter, better, and faster. In addition, real‐world evidence is enabled and its value established. 32 , 33 , 34 This vision also foresees high levels of transparency as a driver of societal trust.

Patient’s voice is at the center of clinical evidence generation. The generation of evidence can be seen as a cycle, with the patient voice guiding every step. Patients have trusted all stakeholders by transparently sharing their medical needs, health outcome priorities and preferences to direct research questions, evidence generation and decision making. Their willingness and trust in these steps in medicines regulation are underpinned by the need for high levels of stakeholder transparency. In this way, the patient voice drives excellence in evidence generation to ensure that evidence standards can support impactful decisions (adapted from Arlett et al. 31 ).

By delivering on this vision, stakeholders should work together to bring innovative, better medicines to patients faster. This will increase the efficiency and effectiveness of research and development, target medicines to those who will benefit most, engage patients, enable better decision making and meet the needs of multiple decision makers.

Key guiding principles will enable this vision to be realized. Firstly, we need to acknowledge the problems we face. Secondly, we need to be guided by stakeholders, with the patient at the center of our decision making. Thirdly, we need to plan for better evidence and replan based on feedback. Fourthly, we need to harness the resources we need, be these people, data, technology, or knowledge. Fifthly, we should take a global perspective, converging or where possible harmonizing with other regulatory frameworks. Lastly, we should always remain focused on benefits, particularly benefits for patients.

The European regulatory network has worked for many years to ensure the patient voice is heard. Patients are members of EMA committees, provide expertise, advise on guidance, labelling changes, policy, and key strategic initiatives. 35 , 36 EMA will soon be consulting on a reflection paper on patient experience data and, going forward, we consider these data will be central for patient listening and play a bigger role in medicines evaluation. 37 Examples of international efforts to improve standards for end points related to patient‐reported outcomes and quality of life, such as the work done by SISAQOL‐IMI, 38 further increase the role of patient data in medicines development and evaluation.

Key to designing excellent clinical evidence is learning from prior experience and taking advice from experts in the field. The provision of data and information from previous product development, including clinical trials and observational studies, is therefore critical. To address this, EU regulators have worked hard to ensure very high levels of transparency for clinical trials and observational study data. 39 , 40 , 41 In addition, the EMA can provide scientific advice at different stages of medicines development, and there is a strong correlation between following advice from regulators on the design of clinical development programs and a successful subsequent application for marketing authorization. 42 This provides strong evidence that medicines developers should work closely with regulators at key development milestones. 43

In the area of clinical trials, the EU regulatory system is evolving, and the Clinical Trials Regulation is now in force. The Regulation enables developers to submit a single application for a clinical trial in up to 30 countries and use one clinical trial application collaboration and publication portal known as the Clinical Trials Information System. 44 We remain in the early days of operation of the new regulation, and stakeholders have highlighted areas for further improvement in the oversight of clinical trials in the European Union. 45 , 46 There are specific activities in place to further streamline the work of the national competent authorities, ethics bodies and the interface with medical devices, including the COLLABORATE and COMBINE initiatives. 47 , 48

Importantly, the new regulatory framework is being complemented by a transformation program known as Accelerating Clinical Trials in the EU (ACT EU). 49 , 50 This transformation program aims to address the complexities associated with the clinical trial authorization process in the EU, which has historically been the responsibility of each individual Member State. ACT EU has a multi‐stakeholder platform at its heart to ensure that clinical trial stakeholders are identifying the key priorities for change and helping to implement that change. Examples of improvements include a pilot on combining clinical trial authorization advice with marketing authorization evidence advice, 51 an interactive map that enables patients to identify recruiting clinical trials in their local area 52 (Figure 2 ) and dedicated support to academic clinical trial sponsors on the application for a clinical trial authorization. 53 The EMA will continue to work to optimize clinical trials, making them better, smarter and faster and we have set key performance indicators for this work to measure our success (study manuscript in development).

In the area of real‐world evidence, we have made major strides to increase accessibility of healthcare data and to optimize the study methods applied. The flagship initiative in the European Union is known as the Data Analysis and Real‐World Interrogation Network (DARWIN EU), 54 , 55 which currently gives access to data from 180,000,000 patients across Europe. Through the use of a common data model, DARWIN EU allows rapid, scalable analytics. As an example, a phenotype generation framework was built in the DARWIN EU Network and has recently been used to produce reliable and reproducible disease phenotypes for systemic lupus erythematosus and pancreatic cancer, among others. 56 In 2025, EMA initiates 100 studies through DARWIN EU, and use cases are contributing to decision making across the life cycle of medicinal products from phase II and phase III clinical development to authorization and post authorization monitoring. Furthermore, DARWIN EU is acting as a pathfinder for the European Health Data Space, 57 demonstrating both the practicalities and the benefits of accessing and analyzing large healthcare datasets.

Considering for a moment special populations, it is clear that a multi‐modal approach will be needed to ensure adequate evidence for decision making. Whether it concerns small populations of people with rare diseases, populations not normally represented in clinical trials based on age (notably young children and the very elderly) or pregnant or breastfeeding people, a combination of clinical trials, real‐world evidence (including registries), modeling and simulation efforts, extrapolation and use of synthetic data may play a role in ensuring that robust regulatory decision making continues to be based on sufficient evidence.

In this way, through careful planning based on existing data, information, knowledge, and expertise, guided by the patient and ensuring that it is the research question that guides the choice of data and methods, we can ensure that excellent clinical evidence is efficiently generated to optimize the path from innovation to regulatory submission and beyond.

Optimizing assessment

Once a marketing authorization application has been submitted and validated, the centralized procedure for assessing the evidence follows a defined timetable that takes 210 active days, interrupted by one or two clock stops where applicants prepare the materials to address questions raised by EMA’s Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use (CHMP). 58 For medicines that are expected to be of major public health interest, particularly from the point of view of therapeutic innovation, developers can request an accelerated assessment where the duration of CHMP’s evaluation is reduced to 150 days. In 2024, the average duration of a standard evaluation (including CHMP’s evaluation, clock stops and the decision process by the European Commission) was 434 days, compared with 217 days for an application reviewed under accelerated assessment. 59

Importantly, over the last 3 years, the average time needed by developers to address CHMP’s questions in the clock stop periods was almost the same as the time spent by CHMP on the assessment, and 10–20 applications are withdrawn each year before CHMP can complete its evaluation, 59 often because major objections cannot sufficiently be addressed within a reasonable time period. This indicates that developers could benefit from regulatory support to optimize their development plans and submission package. For small‐ and medium‐sized enterprises (SMEs), often the major drivers of innovation in medicines development, 60 issues with the submitted evidence lead to major objections that threaten the success of their applications. 61

Such support is available in the form of scientific advice (SA), through which developers receive recommendations from regulators on the most appropriate ways to generate robust evidence on a medicine’s benefits and risks. Interestingly, one of the incentives EMA provides to SMEs is that SA is free for medicines with an orphan designation and available at a reduced fee for other medicines. EMA also provides SA fee reductions for certain not‐for‐profit entities to encourage researchers and developers from the academic sector. 62 Medicines that target an unmet medical need are eligible for additional support under EMA’s Priority Medicines (PRIME) scheme, which allows enhanced interaction and early dialogue between EMA experts and developers to optimize development plans and speed up evaluation so these medicines can reach patients earlier. 63 Medicines granted PRIME status benefit from an early multidisciplinary meeting, termed “kick‐off meeting,” that is intended to establish a platform for tailored development support that helps define and plan the technical and scientific assistance needed. The first analyses of medicines developed under PRIME suggest that the duration of clock stops is shorter and the number of major objections lower compared with non‐PRIME medicines, in particular for products developed by SMEs. 64

Facilitating patient access

Obtaining a marketing authorization is only the first step toward patient access to new medicines. EU medicines regulations place all decisions on a medicine’s use, pricing and reimbursement in the hands of the individual Member States, based on HTA that ensure the quality and sustainability of national healthcare systems. EMA collaborates closely with HTA bodies but, until recently, HTA assessments were conducted exclusively at a national level. Because requirements for the assessments may differ between Member States, 65 medicines access may differ for patients depending on their country of residence. With the EU regulation on HTA entering into force in January 2025, 66 this has changed for new medicines for oncology as well as advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs; e.g., cell and gene therapies). In parallel with the EMA’s evaluation of the marketing authorization application, these products will now undergo a Joint Clinical Assessment (JCA) carried out by the HTA Coordination Group (HTA‐CG) 67 consisting of Member State experts. A JCA will not result in decisions on pricing and reimbursement, but it is intended to create a pan‐EU HTA basis that supports Member States in their national decisions, thereby facilitating faster and more uniform access to these products across the European Union. From 2028, the legislation will also cover medicinal products with orphan designations, and from 2030 all new medicinal products are subject to JCA. EMA has been working closely with the HTA‐CG to implement rules that will facilitate the JCA, as the parallel assessments differ in their scope; EMA’s evaluation establishes that the medicine meets the requirements for quality, safety and efficacy, and that the benefit–risk balance is positive, whereas the HTA‐CG focuses on the comparison with the current standard of care and potential added benefits over existing therapies.

In addition to centralizing HTAs, the new HTA legislation also allows medicines developers to seek scientific advice on the HTA requirements. This platform, termed Joint Scientific Consultation (JSC), allows developers an early opportunity for dialogue with HTA bodies where they can request advice and receive nonbinding feedback on their development plans. As mentioned previously, the EMA scientific advice procedure has shown that such advice improves the outcome of marketing authorization applications, 42 and it can therefore be anticipated that increased dialogue between developers and HTA bodies will better align development plans with the evidentiary needs required for JCAs. The new regulation also offers developers a joint scientific advice, termed the parallel Joint Scientific Consultation (pJSC), which allows developers to request scientific advice from EMA in parallel with scientific advice provided by the HTA‐CG. Such parallel advice mechanisms are not new, they have been in place since 2010, but under the new regulation pJSCs are expected to increase cross‐fertilization if not alignment between regulatory and HTA requirements toward facilitation of development plans. This will address possible differences in evidentiary requirements noted by stakeholders, 68 and will eventually result in faster patient access to new medicines. Under the HTA regulation, the first pJSC has started in September 2025.

Taken together, to support the therapeutic opportunities for patients stemming from innovations in medicines development and regulatory science, the EMA has put in place a clear strategic vision for the generation of clinical and real‐world evidence and support structures to aid developers in the design of their studies and reduce the time from regulatory submission to approval. Together with recent legislative changes that are intended to facilitate patient access to innovative medicines after approval, this will help expedite the path from early innovation to better treatment options benefiting public health.

A FORWARD‐LOOKING PATH: SEIZING OPPORTUNITIES

Innovation allows for opportunities to enhance medicines development and assessment, ultimately facilitating patient access to better medicines. As presented above, EU regulators have put in place the mechanisms to support development and evaluation of innovative new medicines. However, regulators need to stay abreast of technological developments and need to create and maintain platforms for collaboration to ensure that the opportunities provided can be leveraged. Here, we present ongoing efforts that facilitate integration of innovation into medicines regulation, platforms that enable early‐stage exploration and discussion of innovative approaches, and the potential of AI for medicines development and regulations.

Horizon scanning

In an increasingly dynamic and competitive environment, it is essential to identify emerging trends, technologies, products, and scientific developments that could impact the future of healthcare and medicines regulation. Such horizon scanning helps regulators, policymakers, and health systems stay ahead of the curve. It informs risk‐based decision making, anticipates regulatory needs, guides resource allocation, and enhances early dialogue with innovators.

The EMA, together with the EU‐IN, has conducted horizon scanning activities for many years. 69 By systematically reviewing innovation pipelines, research landscapes, and global R&D movements, this has supported early identification of transformative changes from novel drug modalities, such as mRNA and cell therapies to digital therapeutics, AI‐enabled drug discovery, and synthetic biology. A key output is Horizon Scanning Reports 70 on priority areas, including recommendations on how to enable better drug development and patient treatment in the respective field. Recent report topics include new approach methodologies (i.e., novel methods that aid in replacing, reducing, and refining animal use in medicines testing), 71 Alzheimer’s disease, nanotechnology‐based medicinal products for human use, and fecal microbiota transplantation.

To be effective, horizon scanning requires a multidisciplinary, collaborative approach. In May 2025, the EMA conducted a webinar on Horizon Scanning methodologies with relevant stakeholders, including the World Health Organization (WHO), EU Policy Lab, and Joint Research Centre of the European Commission (EC), European Food Safety Agency (EFSA), and National Institute for Health Research (NIHR, Newcastle University) to discuss various strategies for performing an effective Horizon Scanning and possibly identify synergies for future collaborations. As innovation accelerates, this tool and a collaborative approach are becoming central to shaping agile, future‐ready healthcare, and regulatory ecosystems.

Collaborating to build and leverage knowledge and expertise

Harnessing scientific excellence, comprehensive experience, and openness for innovation are challenges that the EMA embraces for all its regulatory activities. Most regulators are regulatory scientists employed full‐time by agencies, often recruited from academic institutions and, at times, from commercial entities. Agencies support their scientists through various activities to keep connected with their field of interest. Such activities (e.g., lecturing in university and other educational contexts, undertaking research on questions of regulatory interest, participating in scientific congresses, and co‐authoring scientific articles, and representing their agencies in research consortia) ensure that regulators stay connected to the latest developments and the expert scientists involved.

Regarding scientific consortia, public–private partnerships (PPPs) offer important opportunities for exchange and mutual learning between regulators and involved partners, typically scientists from the for‐profit and academic sectors, as well as patient and research organizations. Importantly, although some participants in a PPP may be active in the for‐profit sector and many of the solutions developed for horizontal issues within a PPP are expected to subsequently be used by commercial developers, PPPs in which regulators are involved operate exclusively in the non‐competitive area. The multiple stakeholders involved in PPPs, including regulators, learn to speak a common language and learn how other stakeholders operate, which in turn helps to de‐risk and accelerate innovation. 27

The need to accelerate learning continues to accompany innovations and change in health systems. As an example of a unique approach to learning, the EU regulatory Network is working to establish a clinical trials’ learning ecosystem for academia and SMEs as part of ACT EU Priority action 10. 72 The aim is to provide bidirectional offerings (regulators learning from non‐regulators, and non‐regulators learning from regulators) and to provide offerings that meet the educational material criteria of regulators and non‐regulators.

Beyond the steps already mentioned, regulators should further deepen their engagement with science and academia. This includes new ways of collaboration between regulatory scientists and academic researchers in relevant fields, enabling more fluent training and timely learning from recent cases, and through the openness of relevant stakeholders to engage in meaningful scientific discussions to learn from each other.

Enabling platforms for discussion

The previous section already highlighted the synergism of information exchange between stakeholders, where scientists from different backgrounds share their viewpoints and explore existing knowledge gaps and how to address them. Within the EU‐IN, two groups are specifically tailored to discuss potential problems and regulatory approaches for innovative medicines.

EMA’s Innovation Task Force (ITF)

The ITF, part of the EU‐IN, is a multidisciplinary group consisting of European regulators and EU Regulatory Network experts. The ITF has interacted with developers through the so‐called ITF briefing meetings since 2017. Advice is free of charge, and provided through informal, 90‐minute brainstorming sessions where applicants can ask questions and receive initial guidance on the development of their innovative products. Briefing meetings are not restricted to innovative medicines but are also available for developers of novel methodologies (see Table 1 ) and biomarkers, and evidence shows that these meetings cover a very wide range of topics (Figure 3 ).

Enabling technologies in the area of real‐world data analysis include use of wearables to collect patient data and use of AI to analyze them; use of target trial and estimand frameworks to improve the quality of real‐world evidence in regulatory submissions; and use of IT tools (e.g., blockchain and AI) to enable better analysis of real‐world evidence. Technologies in the area of novel end points include video/AI‐assisted electronic Clinical Outcome Assessment (eCOA); in vitro methods to replace, reduce and refine (3Rs) animal use; and prediction models for understanding a placebo effect.

Briefing meetings serve to help developers in the transition from the early stages of development to, in the case of medicinal products, the marketing authorization application, informing them of available support (e.g., regulatory services), possible requirements for methodological approaches utilized in their preclinical or clinical development plans (e.g., qualification of novel methodologies), and the requirements for the regulatory package. By providing an early point of contact for developers, ITF briefing meetings therefore facilitate and enable innovation in medicines and help strengthen the EU innovation system. 30

EMA’s Quality Innovation Group

The EU Regulatory Network supports the application of novel manufacturing technologies and analytical techniques to the design, manufacture and quality control of medicines. This support is not only focused on producing new medicines but also on improving the supply of existing ones and facilitating the adoption of more sustainable practices with a reduced environmental footprint.

New technologies can bring manufacturing closer to patients, right to the point of care. There are examples of personalized medicines being made for patients directly in hospitals. 73 Several projects are testing the potential of portable on‐demand production units, which can be transported wherever there is a need. For regulators, the challenge has always been to find solutions to allow patients to benefit from these developments.

To stay informed, the EMA conducts regular industry surveys on technologies expected to shape medicines development and manufacturing over the next 5 years. Findings have indicated a growing interest in many areas, including digital tools (e.g., AI, automation, process modeling, and digital twins), platform technologies, and continuous (CM) and decentralized manufacturing (DM – see Box 3).

Regulatory considerations for continuous and decentralized manufacturing

As both the pharmaceutical industry and medicines regulators are looking for technological innovations that can improve sustainability of medicines manufacturing, continuous manufacturing (CM) may offer benefits over traditional batch manufacturing medicines production. In CM systems, input material(s) are continuously fed into the process and subsequently transformed, and the processed output is continuously removed from the system. ICH guideline Q13 on CM came into effect in 2023, 91 and the EU has developed additional guidelines and supporting materials that, although not specific to CM, may further guide the use of this technology and manage its risks.

As CM technology continues to evolve, EU regulators recognize outstanding challenges that need to be addressed for CM to reach its full potential. Such challenges, including the absence of analytical tools to provide timely quality attributes, require innovative solutions, and it is important to provide a regulatory framework within which such solutions can be developed. The Quality Innovation Group (QIG) provides a platform for stakeholders to share challenges, so they can identify solutions and ensure there are no regulatory barriers.

Another innovation in medicines manufacturing concerns the shift from a centralized manufacturing system, which offers an economy of scale at the cost of environmental sustainability and potential disruption of supply chains, toward decentralized manufacturing (DM). In a DM approach, medicinal products are made by many facilities spread over many geographical locations. This can offer resilience of the supply chain (e.g., in a modular manufacturing set up and potable‐on‐demand units) and address challenges for medicines with short shelf‐lives (e.g., in point‐of‐care production). Although DC can offer benefits, such as allowing tailored treatments for underserved patients (e.g., pediatric patients or those with rare diseases) and improving sustainability through on‐demand manufacturing, there are major regulatory challenges compared with centralized manufacturing systems.

As an example, the complexity of site and product comparability studies increases manyfold for medicines under DM, as does the planning of effective regulatory oversight of production facilities. These challenges may require adapting existing regulatory tools used for large‐scale production, and many authorities are evaluating the best approach. To support alignment, ICMRA hosted a workshop to share insights and experiences fostering convergence and enabling faster implementation of such alternative manufacturing and supply models globally. 92

To keep pace with this rapid growth, the EMA established the Quality Innovation Group (QIG) in 2023, with the aim to provide continued support to developers from an early stage of implementation of a novel manufacturing technology (i.e., from proof‐of‐concept and throughout a product’s lifecycle). It is a multidisciplinary group of quality assessors with chemical and biological expertise (including ATMPs) and good manufacturing practice inspectors. QIG is responsible for developing the EU Network’s thinking around the challenges of production of high‐quality innovative medicines. Its focus areas are defined through industry surveys, pipeline and technology meetings and horizon scanning activities, and are outlined in its 3‐year workplan. 74 Recognizing the global dimension of medicines development and manufacturing, the QIG holds regular meetings with international partners to share knowledge and align on regulatory thinking and participates in international regulatory fora.

The QIG offers an open and agile forum for informal discussions through listen‐and‐learn Focus Group meetings (LLFGs), where developers, academics, vendors, and learned societies present practical cases to explore the challenges they face and find solutions. All meetings are attended by international partners, including the US FDA, PMDA (Japan), TGA (Australia), and Swiss Medic. Since its establishment, the QIG has held five LLFGs; meeting reports highlight the key challenges as identified during the discussions, and proposed solutions as well as the actions for the QIG. These are published on the EMA’s website. 75

A key example of the QIG’s agile engagement is its work on process models. Following an LLFG meeting in October 2023, developers sought regulatory clarity on the topic. QIG issued preliminary considerations in February 2024 and engaged further with more detailed questions in a second LLFG later that year. The QIG is now revising the guidance and aiming to publish an updated version in 2025. 76 This iterative approach, informed by parallel discussions with international partners as well as product‐specific cases, has been very important in shaping regulatory expectations in a key area for digitalizing medicines manufacture and control, and sharing them with developers in a timely fashion.

In addition to engaging with stakeholders via LLFGs, the QIG also provides product‐specific support to developers. Depending on the phase of introduction of a technology in the product lifecycle and maturity of the technology, QIG experts lead or contribute to product discussions, scientific advice and evaluation procedures offering a “PRIME‐like” continuity in regulatory product support. Developers can request confidential one‐on‐one meetings for early‐stage technical discussions or regulatory guidance on the regulatory introduction for emerging technologies approaching full operational implementation. Additional experts and international partners may participate upon request. QIG also conducts site visits to assess GMP aspects of novel technologies, often observed by international partners and local supervisory authorities to support convergence and continuity in the advice given.

Since its establishment, QIG has contributed to 33 ITF meetings, 17 closed one‐on‐one sessions, three manufacturing site visits (one with international partners), and five scientific advice assessments on novel manufacturing technologies, including new‐generation sequencing, bacteriophages, robotic aseptic filling, and neoantigens.

Overall, the evolving landscape of pharmaceutical manufacturing innovation necessitates an adaptive regulatory approach that keeps pace with rapid advancements. The EMA plays a central role in coordinating efforts to support innovation from its earliest stages through the entire medicine lifecycle. By leveraging established regulatory tools and support mechanisms, and creating new tools and procedures, the EMA ensures that novel technologies can be effectively integrated into medicines development and manufacture, ensuring timely access to high‐quality, cutting‐edge treatments for patients.

Artificial intelligence

The expectations and promises of AI are immense, and these also hold true for the life cycle of medicines. AI and Large Language Models (LLMs) will not only facilitate the early stages of medicines development, from molecular identification, toxicological testing, and early pharmacology, but will also have a significant impact on later stages of drug development in terms of prediction, accuracy, and speed.

Industry and regulators are expecting AI to increase efficiency and productivity by automating processes and taking over routine tasks, as well as providing personal assistance in the preparation of documents and dossiers for regulatory review, significantly reducing the time from clinical trial data lock to submission for regulatory review.

AI can already predict protein folding (e.g., Google Deep Mind Alphafold) and potentially target binding, enabling early predictions of pharmaco‐dynamics and pharmaco‐kinetics in silico, and work is being carried out to use AI models to identify alternative methods to animal testing, 77 fundamentally changing early drug identification and development. AI‐assisted search engines are facilitating faster and more to‐the‐point information for planning and decision making, and AI‐supported LLMs can assist in transforming text data into structured data and support regulatory review of text and data. Moreover, AI is becoming an integrated part of many medical devices used both in daily clinical practice and in clinical trials. A recent example of this is the EMA/CHMP qualification opinion for an AI‐based measurement of non‐alcoholic steatohepatitis (AIM‐NASH) histology in liver biopsies to determine disease activity in NASH/MASH. 78

AI has already found its way into many para‐clinical disciplines like radiology and pathology, increasing accuracy in daily practice 79 , 80 and such developments are likely to also facilitate end‐point assessment in clinical trials. Planning, execution, data collection, and analysis of clinical trials will increasingly be guided by AI. This may include predictive modeling (in silico trials), providing synthetic control arms for otherwise uncontrolled studies based on analysis of RWD, and adjusting for confounding, heterogeneity, etc. in the final dataset.

From a regulatory perspective, the use of any AI tool in medicines development by developers will be assessed by regulatory agencies, including EMA, as any other methodology being applied during the life cycle of a medicinal product. Developers will be responsible for ensuring that fit‐for‐purpose algorithms and datasets are being used in accordance with current ethical, technical, scientific, and regulatory standards described in regulatory guidance documents and GxP guidelines. AI tools can and will be inspected, and regulators are expecting to be provided with sufficient scientific and technical detail to allow for a comprehensive assessment, like for any other method applied during development.

Given the very rapid advancements in AI, EMA, and its scientific committees for human (CHMP) and veterinary (CVMP) medicines drafted a reflection paper on the use of AI throughout the medicine life cycle in 2023; it has since been subjected to public consultation and further discussions as part of a multi‐stakeholder workshop, 81 and has now been published on EMA’s website. 82 Given the pace of development, EMA foresees regular updates to this document as part of the Agency’s multi‐annual workplan on AI. 83 An AI focus group between EU regulators and experts from the pharmaceutical industry has recently been established, underpinning EMA’s collaborative stakeholder‐driven approach to innovation.

Regulatory authorities are increasingly experimenting and applying AI and LLMs to automate processes, support regulatory review, and focus human resources and brain power on challenging parts of dossiers that are not considered prior knowledge. Although out of scope for this paper, it is important to mention the potential of AI to support post‐authorization pharmacovigilance activities in signal detection using large datasets of RWD. 84

CONCLUSION

Innovations in medicines development require innovative advances in regulatory science to ensure their promise translates into patient benefit. To this end, the EMA employs and adapts the regulatory tools to efficiently evaluate the quality, benefits and risks of new medicines, while collaborating with experts in regulatory sciences across the stakeholder spectrum to ensure these tools continue to evolve. Because the evaluation of medicines depends on the robustness of the data submitted, we have a clear vision on the type of evidence required, and we work closely with stakeholders to ensure a shared understanding of how such evidence can best be generated to optimize the journey from early development to patient access. Finally, for scientific advances to translate into the timely development and marketing of better medicines, we need to continue looking ahead for new developments and foster strong collaborations among all stakeholders. This will help ensure that opportunities are identified, shared and leveraged to improve public health.

In this way, we can ensure a fast path from innovation to safe and effective medicines.
