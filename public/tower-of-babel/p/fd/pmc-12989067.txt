Durvalumab combined with concurrent chemoradiotherapy in patients with limited‐stage small cell lung cancer: A prospective, single‐arm, phase 2 clinical trial
Authors: Chunyang Song, Xiaohan Zhao, Shuguang Li, Xuehan Guo, Xueyuan Zhang, Xiaobin Wang, Youmei Li, Ke Yan, Jingwei Su, Jinrui Xu, Shuchai Zhu, Wenbin Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current standard treatment for limited‐stage small cell lung cancer (LS‐SCLC) is concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT) plus consolidation immunotherapy, with or without prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI). However, it remains unknown whether administering immunotherapy concurrently with chemoradiotherapy confers additional benefit. This clinical trial is designed to investigate the efficacy and safety using durvalumab with chemoradiotherapy for LS‐SCLC.MethodsIn this single‐arm phase 2 study, patients with LS‐SCLC received three (1–4) cycles of etoposide, cisplatin, or carboplatin, and durvalumab every 3 weeks, following by thoracic radiotherapy of 60.0 Gy to the gross tumor volume and 54.0 Gy to the planning target volume in 30 once‐daily fractions and chemoimmunotherapy. After cCRT plus durvalumab, patients received durvalumab consolidation therapy every 3 weeks for a minimum of 1 year. PCI was recommended to patients with partial or complete response when chemoradiotherapy completed. Efficacy and adverse events were assessed.ResultsOverall, 51 patients were enrolled from March 1, 2021, to April 30, 2024, with a median follow‐up of 32.0 months. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year progression‐free survival (PFS) rates were 56.9%, 35.9%, and 28.2%, respectively, and the median PFS was 17.0 months. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year overall survival (OS) rates were 88.1%, 68.6%, and 49.6%, respectively, and the median OS was 32.0 months. There were 17 (33.4%) patients with grade 3 or 4 adverse events, 7 of which were immune‑related.ConclusionsThese results suggest that the addition of durvalumab to chemoradiotherapy was well tolerated and showed encouraging efficacy, supporting further investigation in patients with LS‐SCLC eligible for radical chemoradiotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

Small‐cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a lung cancer subtype affecting 13%–15% of all lung cancer patients. 1 , 2 SCLC is divided into limited‐stage SCLC (LS‐SCLC) and extensive‐stage SCLC (ES‐SCLC). SCLC is characterized by a rapid tumor cell doubling time, early diffusion, and high response rates to antitumor therapies, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Before this year, the standard treatment for LS‐SCLC involved chemotherapy combined with concurrent thoracic radiotherapy, followed by prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI) in patients who responded to therapy. 3 With the profound transformation brought by immunotherapy to the oncology treatment landscape, the current standard of care for LS‐SCLC is concurrent chemoradiotherapy (cCRT) plus consolidation immunotherapy, with or without PCI. 4

Would advancing the administration of immunotherapy to the cCRT phase, or even the induction chemotherapy phase, for LS‐SCLC patients yield better therapeutic outcomes? According to the latest findings from the randomized phase 3 international clinical trial, the NRG‐LU005 study, 5 incorporating immunotherapy during cCRT may not represent the optimal approach. However, results from small‐sample clinical trials have demonstrated promising efficacy with the combination of cCRT and immunotherapy. 6 Our trial will further investigate the efficacy and safety of combining cCRT with durvalumab in patients with LS‐SCLC. The rationale for this study design is based on the hypothesized biological synergy between radiotherapy and immunotherapy, supported by encouraging early‐phase data in LS‐SCLC (e.g., SINCE‐01). Although the concurrent approach did not improve outcomes in NRG‐LU005, 5 variations in radiotherapy dose and fractionation may influence both safety and efficacy. This phase 2 trial evaluates a specific regimen combining durvalumab with a distinct radiotherapy schedule (60/54 Gy in 30 fractions) to contribute additional data to this evolving field.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

This single‐arm, phase 2 prospective study was conducted at the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University. Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years) with newly diagnosed, histologically/cytologically confirmed LS‐SCLC, an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1, 7 and at least one measurable lesion. Staging was performed using chest/upper abdomen computed tomography (CT), brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and bone emission computed tomography (ECT) (18F fluorodeoxyglucose‐positron emission tomography [PET/CT] was optional) per International Cancer Control (UICC)/American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines. 8 Key exclusion criteria included prior antitumor therapy, history of autoimmune/inflammatory diseases, active infections (e.g., tuberculosis, hepatitis B/C), or other malignancies within the past 5 years. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee (no. 2022015) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06371482).

Chemotherapy

Chemotherapy consisted of etoposide (100 mg/m2/day on days 1–3) plus cisplatin (75 mg/m2 on day 1) or carboplatin (Area Under the Curve (AUC) 5–6 on day 1), administered concurrently with durvalumab (10 mg/kg on day 1) every 3 weeks. Patients were planned to receive one to four cycles of induction chemoimmunotherapy before radiotherapy. The goal was to achieve early disease control and identify patients suitable for definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy. Patients who showed no disease progression after one to four cycles proceeded to the concurrent phase, during which thoracic radiotherapy was initiated with the chemoimmunotherapy. The protocol planned for a maximum of six total cycles of chemoimmunotherapy. In practice, the actual number of cycles administered was individualized based on patient tolerance and treatment response, ranging from four to six cycles. This exploratory design aimed to provide a prolonged window for immune activation during the concurrent phase, aligning with the principle of flexible chemotherapy duration established in clinical practice—as exemplified by the CASPIAN study in extensive‐stage SCLC, which permitted up to six cycles of chemotherapy. Routine antiemetic pretreatment was given during chemotherapy, and steroid and anti‐inflammatory drugs were used with caution.

Radiotherapy

Intensity‐modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) was mandatory. Digitized CT simulation images were transferred to the treatment‐planning system (ADAC Pinnacle3 8.0 m, Philips). The gross tumor volume (GTV) was delineated on post‐induction chemotherapy chest CT and included the residual primary tumor and all clinically involved nodal regions; nodal regions that fully resolved after induction were still encompassed based on pre‐chemotherapy CT. Elective nodal irradiation was omitted. The clinical target volume (CTV) was generated by adding 5–7 mm to the GTV, and the planning target volume (PTV) by expanding the CTV by 6–8 mm. Thoracic radiotherapy was prescribed as 60 Gy to the GTV and 54 Gy to the PTV in 30 once‐daily fractions (five fractions/week). Linac parameters were optimized to minimize normal‐tissue exposure. Organs at risk (OARs) constraints were: mean lung dose <20 Gy, lung V5 <60%, V20 < 30%, V30 < 20%; mean heart dose <30 Gy, heart V30 < 40%, V40 < 30%; esophagus V35 < 50%; and spinal cord Dmax ≤45 Gy. 9 , 10 Patients with complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) who consented after a detailed discussion of benefits and risks were offered PCI 3–4 weeks after completing chemoimmunotherapy and thoracic radiotherapy (25 Gy in 10 fractions); hippocampus‐sparing IMRT was used when indicated. PCI timing did not alter consolidation immunotherapy scheduling.

Immunotherapy

Durvalumab (10 mg/kg, every 3 weeks) and chemotherapy were administered simultaneously for four to six cycles. Thereafter, immunotherapy alone was administered for at least 1 year, or until the patient showed progression, intolerance, or automatic withdrawal.

Study objectives

The primary study end point was OS, defined as the time interval between the date of study initiation and the date of all‐cause death. The secondary end points were: progress‐free survival (PFS), defined as the time interval between the date of study initiation and the date of disease progression or all‐cause mortality; local progression‐free survival (LPFS), defined as the time interval between the date of study initiation and the date of first clinical evidence of progressive disease at the primary site or death; distant metastasis‐free survival (DMFS), defined as the time interval between the date of study initiation and the date of first clinical evidence of distant metastasis or death; objective response rate (ORR), calculated using the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumours, Version 1.1 (RECIST 1.1): CR, complete disappearance of imaging signs; PR, target lesion shrunk by ≥30%; stable disease (SD), target lesion changed by ±20%; and progressive disease (PD), target lesion increased by ≥20%. Side effects (SE) were recorded following the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, Version 4.

Sample size

This study used PASS software (version 15.05, NCSS, LLC., Kaysville, Utah) to estimate the required sample size. Based on our study’s aim, we set the two‐sided α to 0.05, the power to 0.80, the duration of enrollment (Ta) to 3 years, and the follow‐up time (Tf) to 3 years. We expected the 2‐year OS rate to increase from 51% (the 2‐year OS rate of the once‐daily group in the CONVERT trial 7 ) to 66%, the hazard ratio (HR) to 0.7, and set the k parameter to the system default value of 1. Based on the calculations, a sample of 81 cases was needed to complete this study. This calculation was based on survival data from the chemoradiotherapy‐only era (CONVERT trial), because when the study was designed (March 1, 2021) consolidation immunotherapy with durvalumab after cCRT had not yet been established as standard of care; the practice‐changing results of the ADRIATIC trial were reported later.

Statistical analysis

Final data were collected on March 1, 2025. Median follow‐up was 32.0 months. Survival was compared by the log‐rank test and HRs estimated with Cox proportional hazards models. Categorical variables were compared by χ2 test. p < .05 indicated statistical significance. Analyses were performed in R v4.3.

Because this was a single‐arm study, efficacy and safety end points were analyzed descriptively for the enrolled cohort. An exploratory comparison with institutional historical data was performed and is detailed in the Supporting Information S1.

Treatment delivery and tumor response

By the study’s follow‐up end date, 22 patients completed chemoimmunotherapy and radiotherapy with subsequent maintenance immunotherapy, four were still under maintenance immunotherapy, five discontinued immunotherapy due to immune‐related adverse events (AEs), and 20 stopped immunotherapy due to disease progression during treatment. The 51 patients received induction chemotherapy with a median of 3 (range, 1–4) cycles, including four patients who received one induction chemotherapy cycle, 15 who received two cycles, 13 who received three cycles, and 19 who received four cycles. The objective response assessment after induction chemoimmunotherapy (before radiotherapy) showed that two patients achieved CR, 40 achieved PR, and nine achieved SD, yielding an ORR of 82.4%. The objective responses after radical radiotherapy and four to six chemoimmunotherapy cycles showed that 10 patients achieved CR, 37 achieved PR, three achieved SD, and one patient showed PD. The ORR for the entire group was 92.2%, and the DCR was 98.0% (Figure 1).

Survival outcomes

The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year OS rates for the 51 enrolled patients were 88.1%, 68.6%, and 49.6%, respectively, with a median OS of 32.0 (27.0–NA (Not Reached)) months. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year PFS rates were 56.9%, 35.9%, and 28.2%, respectively, with a median PFS of 17.0 (11.4–25.3) months. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year LPFS rates were 78.3%, 57.1%, and 40.8%, respectively, with a median LPFS of 27.0 (21.0–NA) months. The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year DMFS rates were 60.8%, 46.1%, and 33.4%, respectively, with a median DMFS of 19.6 (12.0–38.5) months (Figure 2).

The overall survival, progression‐free survival, local progression‐free survival, and distant metastasis‐free survival of the 51 patients with limited‐stage small cell lung cancer.

Given the single‐arm design of this study, a formal comparative analysis with a concurrent control group was not performed. An exploratory descriptive comparison of survival outcomes with a historical cohort of patients treated with chemoradiotherapy alone at our institution is provided in the Supporting Information S1.

Impact of prophylactic cranial irradiation

Among the 47 patients who achieved CR or PR after radical chemoradiotherapy, 21 received PCI. The brain metastasis incidence in the PCI group (4 of 21, 19.0%) was significantly lower than in the no‐PCI group (16 of 26, 61.5%; χ2 = 6.930; p = .008). The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year OS rates in the no‐PCI group were 84.3%, 56.2%, and 30.9%, with a median OS of 27.0 (19.5–NA) months. The respective values in the PCI group were 95.2%, 85.7%, and 73.5%, with the median OS not reached. Compared to the no‐PCI group, the PCI treatment benefited patient OS (HR, 0.27; 95% CI, 0.10–0.74; p = .007). The 1‐, 2‐, and 3‐year PFS rates in the no‐PCI group were 38.5%, 24.4%, and 14.7%, with a median PFS of 10.1 (7.7–20.3) months. The respective values in the PCI group were 81.0%, 57.1%, and 50.8%, with the median PFS not reached. Compared to the no‐PCI group, the PCI treatment benefited patient PFS (HR, 0.29; 95% CI, 0.14–0.62; p = .001; Figure 3)

Treatment‐related adverse events

The treatment‐related adverse reactions in our study included grade 1 in seven cases (13.8%), grade 2 in 15 cases (29.4%), grade 3 in 10 cases (19.6%), and grade 4 in seven (13.8%) cases. Among the patients with grade 3 and 4 treatment‐related adverse reactions, seven were associated with immunotherapy (four had immune‐related pneumonia, one of which resulted in death, and one led to a reduction in the radiotherapy dose to 54 Gy; two had immune‐related myocarditis; and one had immune‐related colitis). A total of six cases of myelosuppression occurred during radiotherapy and chemotherapy, including one who had concurrent radiation pneumonitis, leading to a reduction in the radiotherapy dose to 50 Gy. There were six cases of radiation pneumonia and one of chemotherapy‐related nausea and vomiting. A total of 17 adverse reactions (grades 1–2) were immune‐related, accounting for eight cases of pneumonia, four each of thyroid dysfunction and cardiac rhythm abnormalities, two of colitis, and one of hypophysitis. The 11 chemotherapy‐related adverse reactions included nine myelosuppression and four nausea and vomiting. The eight radiation‐related adverse reactions included six radiation pneumonia and two radiation esophagitis. All four patients with PCI‐related adverse reactions had nausea and vomiting. Patterns of treatment failure

Of the 51 patients, nine developed local recurrence alone, 19 developed distant metastasis alone, and eight developed both. Among the distant metastatic events, the most common site was the brain (20 cases), followed by bone (eight cases), adrenal glands and liver (three cases each), and the spinal cord and pleura (one case each).

DISCUSSION

Cancer treatment has fully entered the era of immunotherapy. Similarly, the addition of immunotherapy has led to substantial improvements in survival for patients with SCLC, including both limited‐stage and extensive‐stage disease. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 In LS‐SCLC, it has been shown that cCRT followed by consolidation immunotherapy, with or without PCI, yields a clear survival advantage compared with cCRT alone. 14 This raises the question of whether advancing immunotherapy to be administered concurrently with chemoradiotherapy could confer further survival benefits. Combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy is considered a promising treatment. Studies 15 , 16 suggested that radiotherapy and immunotherapy could produce synergistic effects by modulating the tumor microenvironment, thereby improving outcomes. Studies suggest that the synergistic effects between radiotherapy and immunotherapy mainly focus on these aspects: (1) spatial cooperation, (2) enhanced cytotoxicity, (3) biological cooperation, (4) temporal regulation, and (5) protection of normal tissues. 17 , 18 Combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy can enhance antitumor efficacy through several molecular mechanisms. 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 The dynamics of cellular and tumor responses to radiation offer unique opportunities for beneficial interplay with immunotherapy.

Studies on combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy in LS‐SCLC are limited. The STIMULI study, 23 a randomized phase 2 trial, evaluated the safety and efficacy of consolidative treatment with nivolumab plus ipilimumab after cCRT in patients with LS‐SCLC. Their observation and combination therapy groups showed similar PFS (10.7 vs. 14.5 months; HR, 1.02) and OS (not reached vs. 32.1 months; HR, 0.95). Grade 3 or higher treatment‐related AEs occurred in 62% of patients in the combination therapy group and 25% of patients in the observation group, with four and one treatment‐related deaths, respectively. The study suggested that treatment interruptions related to toxicity might have impacted treatment efficacy and led to failure to meet the primary end point. This result made the prospect for exploring combined radiotherapy and consolidative immunotherapy in LS‐SCLC less clear. Two years later, the ADRIATIC trial, 14 a global, multicenter, phase 3 clinical study, included 730 patients with LS‐SCLC who had no disease progression after cCRT. These patients were randomly assigned to receive durvalumab with or without tremelimumab (N = 264) or a placebo (N = 266). Compared to the placebo, the durvalumab treatment provided benefits in OS (HR, 0.73; median OS, 55.9 vs. 33.4 month; 2‐year OS rate, 68.0% vs. 58.5%) and PFS (HR, 0.76; median PFS, 16.6 vs. 9.2 month; 2‐year PFS rate, 46.2% vs. 34.2%). The grade 3/4 all‐cause AE rates in the durvalumab and placebo groups were 24.3% vs 24.2%, leading to treatment discontinuation in 16.3% and 10.6% of the patients and to the death of 2.7% and 1.9% of the patients, respectively. Although the two clinical studies were similarly designed, unlike the STIMULI study, the ADRIATIC trial was a multicenter phase 3 trial and had a larger sample, possessing stronger persuasive power and demonstrating the important role of immunotherapy as a consolidation treatment after chemoradiotherapy in LS‐SCLC.

To investigate whether advancing immunotherapy to the chemoradiotherapy stage could further improve the therapeutic efficacy, the SINCE‐01 study, 24 a single‐arm phase 2 clinical trial, evaluated the safety and efficacy of combined sintilimab and cCRT in patients with LS‐SCLC. The study enrolled 20 patients, and the median follow‐up was 24 months. Neither median PFS nor OS was reached. The 1‐ and 2‐year PFS rates were 75.0% and 59.1%, respectively, whereas the 1‐ and 2‐year OS rates were 90.0% and 69.3%. The ORR was 95%, including six patients achieving CR, 13 achieving PR, and one achieving SD. Grade 3 or higher AEs occurred in 45% of patients, with immune‐related AEs reported in 35% (seven patients), and radiotherapy‐related AEs reported in 30% of patients. The results of the SINCE‐01 study demonstrated encouraging efficacy and acceptable safety. Similarly, durvalumab was involved in treating LS‐SCLC in our study, with an increased treatment intensity. The planned use of up to six cycles of chemoimmunotherapy represented an exploratory strategy to maximize the duration of immune checkpoint inhibition during the induction and concurrent phases. The flexible execution (four to six cycles) reflects real‐world clinical practice and balances treatment intensity with individual patient tolerability. Furthermore, the treatment‐related adverse reactions were manageable and within an acceptable safety range. The rate of grade 3/4 AEs was 33.4%. Although higher than in the ADRIATIC study (24.3%), it was significantly lower than in the SINCE‐01 study (45.0%). The initial design of our study aimed to improve the 2‐year OS rate achieved in the CONVERT trial 25 by 15% to 66%. The 2‐year OS rate for the enrolled patients in the present study was 68.6%, exceeding the predefined target and closely aligning with the 2‐year OS rate in the ADRIATIC study (68%). In an exploratory comparison with institutional historical data (Supporting Information S1), the survival outcomes observed in our study appeared numerically favorable compared to those achieved with chemoradiotherapy alone in a previous era. However, this indirect comparison is limited by inherent biases. A phase 2 clinical study 6 with a similar study design to ours that combined durvalumab and chemoradiotherapy throughout treatment for LS‐SCLC achieved consistent promising outcomes (ORR, 96.0%; 2‐year OS, 67.8%; immune‐related grade 3/4 AEs, 12.0%).

However, a recent study indicated that adding immunotherapy to cCRT for LS‐SCLC did not improve survival outcomes. The NRG‐LU005 study, 5 a randomized phase 3 international clinical trial, included 544 patients with LS‐SCLC. All patients received one cycle of chemotherapy before being randomly assigned at a 1:1 ratio to the cCRT group or the cCRT plus atezolizumab (cCRT + atezo) group. The median OS was 39.5 months in the cCRT group and 33.1 months in the cCRT + atezo group (HR, 1.11). The median PFS was 11.5 months for the cCRT group and 12.0 months for the cCRT + Atezo group (HR, 1.00). The incidence of pneumonia at a grade greater than 3 was 3.1% in the cCRT group and 5.6% in the cCRT + atezo group.

The potential benefit of incorporating immunotherapy concurrently with chemoradiotherapy in LS‐SCLC remains investigational, with conflicting evidence from clinical trials. Although studies such as STIMULI and NRG‐LU005 failed to demonstrate a significant PFS or OS advantage for the concurrent approach, other early‐phase trials (e.g., SINCE‐01) have reported promising signals. These discrepancies may stem from variations in trial design, patient populations, radiotherapy protocols, and immunotherapeutic agents. Currently, no definitive phase 3 evidence supports the survival benefit of adding concurrent durvalumab to CCRT followed by consolidation in LS‐SCLC, and based on a comprehensive review of public trial registries and the literature, no phase 3 trial with this specific design is currently ongoing. This landscape highlights the relevance of our phase 2 findings. Our trial was motivated by the biologic rationale for radio‐immunotherapy synergy and encouraging early data. We evaluated a specific regimen of durvalumab combined with a different radiotherapy schedule (60 Gy to GTV and 54 Gy to PTV in 30 fractions), distinct from the protocols used in negative trials. Although our study shared a similar design with NRG‐LU005, the outcomes differed. Notably, although NRG‐LU005 (using atezolizumab) did not meet its primary end point, our study employed a different immunotherapy agent (durvalumab) and a lower radiotherapy dose intensity (60/54 Gy/30 fractions vs. predominantly 66 Gy/33 fractions). A key distinction lies in the radiotherapy intensity: NRG‐LU005 primarily used 66 Gy in 33 fractions (2 Gy/fraction), whereas our regimen employed a lower total dose. This reduced intensity may have mitigated the toxicity of combined modality therapy, potentially contributing to the observed survival benefit. Notably, within the NRG‐LU005 trial itself, patients receiving an accelerated twice‐daily schedule (45 Gy in 30 fractions) exhibited superior survival compared to the once‐daily group, supporting the notion that radiotherapy dose and fractionation significantly influence outcomes when combined with immunotherapy. Prior studies corroborate that high‐dose radiotherapy can exacerbate treatment‐related AEs, thereby impairing efficacy and prognosis. 26 , 27 These results indicate that future studies of chemoradiotherapy combined with immunotherapy should prioritize optimization of radiotherapy dose and fractionation. Multiple clinical trials on combined chemoradiotherapy and concurrent or consolidation immunotherapy for LS‐SCLC are currently underway (e.g., NCT06095583, NCT05623267, NCT04691063, NCT05353257, and NCT04624204). We look forward to the announcement of their trial results.

SCLC has a high incidence of brain metastasis, with approximately 50% of patients developing brain metastases after completing the initial treatment. 28 PCI is considered the standard treatment for patients with LS‐SCLC who responded well to the initial therapy. Even in the era of immunotherapy, the role of PCI has not been supplanted. 29 However, several retrospective analyses had found that patients staged by MRI or PET/CT before PCI did not have a reduced incidence of brain metastases or improved prognosis. 30 , 31 Additionally, the study reported the intracranial antitumor effects of immunotherapy, raising questions about the role of PCI in LS‐SCLC in the era of immunotherapy. 32 Our study showed that PCI reduced the incidence of brain metastases and significantly improved the patient’s prognosis, with no specific neurotoxicity observed in the context of immunotherapy. Despite the expanding role of immunotherapy in LS‐SCLC, these data suggest that PCI remains an effective intervention for reducing brain metastases in patients who respond to initial treatment. This finding is consistent with recent reports from the ADRIATIC trial and other contemporary cohorts, 29 , 33 supporting the continued value of PCI even in the era of immunotherapy. This may be attributed to systemic immune activation clearing radiographically occult micrometastases, thereby preventing and eradicating brain metastases. 34 , 35

The proportion of never‐smokers in our cohort (31.4%) was consistent with our institution’s historical data (33.0%) but notably higher than the global estimate that over 90% of SCLC cases are smoking‐related. 36 This discrepancy may reflect regional epidemiological characteristics, as similar cohorts of SCLC in East Asia also report a substantial proportion of never‐smokers. 37 , 38 Therefore, our findings may be most directly relevant to similar regional populations. Importantly, subgroup analyses from the PACIFIC trial in non–small cell lung cancer demonstrated that the survival benefit of durvalumab was independent of smoking status, 39 which indirectly supports the broader applicability of our results. Definitive assessment of the impact of smoking status on the efficacy of concurrent immunotherapy in LS‐SCLC will require future prospective studies or detailed subgroup analyses of trials such as ADRIATIC.

This study has several limitations. First, its single‐arm design and limited sample size preclude definitive conclusions, necessitating validation in larger cohorts. Second, the comparison with historical controls, despite propensity score matching, is inherently susceptible to residual confounding and temporal biases, lacking the robustness of a prospective randomized trial. Third, exploratory biomarker analyses (e.g., programmed death‐ligand 1 expression) were not performed, which could have provided insights into potential predictors of treatment response. Fourth, the variable number of induction chemoimmunotherapy cycles (one to four) introduces heterogeneity, making it challenging to isolate the specific contribution of the induction phase. Future studies should consider a standardized induction period. Future prospective, randomized Phase 3 trials are warranted to confirm these findings and to identify patient subgroups most likely to benefit from this combined modality approach.

In conclusion, our study showed promising clinical efficacy and tolerable safety profiles for combined durvalumab and cCRT in patients with LS‐SCLC. We anticipate positive results from the ongoing phase 3 trials based on these positive data. Furthermore, we intend to perform a phase 3 trial to obtain more convincing data after completing the current study.
