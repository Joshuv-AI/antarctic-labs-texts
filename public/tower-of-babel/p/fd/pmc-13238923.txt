Laryngopharyngeal Trauma in Grappling Martial Arts: A Mixed‐Methods Study
Authors: Kyle W. Singerman, Cole L. Bird, SiYung Cho, Hannah L. Kavookjian, Shannon M. Kraft
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the prevalence and cultural attitudes surrounding laryngopharyngeal injuries in Brazilian Jiu‐Jitsu (BJJ) and Mixed Martial Arts (MMA) athletes.Study DesignCross‐sectional, mixed‐methods study.SettingCommunity‐based survey and interviews of BJJ/MMA practitioners.MethodsBJJ/MMA practitioners were recruited over a 6‐week period via social media and targeted email distribution. The 44‐item survey collected data on the awareness and prevalence of laryngopharyngeal symptoms and injuries, care‐seeking behaviors, and clinical outcomes related to chokeholds. 13 respondents participated in semi‐structured interviews to further explore these themes.ResultsOf 160 survey respondents, 88% reported laryngopharyngeal symptoms following chokeholds, most commonly sore throat (79%) and odynophagia (66%). Most symptoms resolved within 1 week (68%), though 15% reported permanent voice change. 87% of practitioners continued training while symptomatic, and 78% indicated that permanent voice changes would not deter them from training. 50% of respondents indicated they would not seek care if symptoms lasted fewer than 2 weeks. Only 11% sought medical care (n = 18), of whom nearly half (n = 8) were diagnosed with a hyolaryngeal fracture. Interviews highlighted limited awareness of laryngopharyngeal injury risks and a prevailing “push through the pain” cultural ethos.ConclusionsDespite chokeholds being a foundational technique in BJJ/MMA, there is a paucity of data on laryngopharyngeal injury in these sports. Laryngopharyngeal symptoms are common among athletes yet often disregarded, with athletes frequently continuing to train despite symptoms and seldom seeking care. These findings underscore a gap in awareness and highlight the need for further research and education regarding laryngeal safety in combat sports.

Brazilian Jiu‐Jitsu (BJJ) is a grappling martial art in which a primary objective is to “submit” an opponent using chokehold techniques. BJJ techniques are also foundational to Mixed Martial Arts (MMA), in which fighters combine grappling and striking skills during matches. Over the past 2 decades, BJJ has expanded from a niche practice into a global sport with millions of practitioners. MMA promotions, such as the Ultimate Fighting Championship (UFC), have experienced record‐breaking viewership and multi‐billion‐dollar market valuations. 1 , 2 This exponential growth in popularity has generated a large population of recreational athletes who are regularly exposed to potentially injurious chokehold techniques.

Chokeholds are typically categorized as either “blood” or “air” chokes. Blood chokes, such as the rear‐naked choke and triangle choke ( Figure 1A‐B ), decrease cerebral perfusion pressure via compression of the carotid artery and jugular vein, and possibly through vagal stimulation. 3 Numerous case reports and series describe acute neurologic sequelae secondary to neurovascular injuries related to blood chokes. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Demonstration of 2 common types of blood chokes. (A) Demonstration of rear naked choke; (B) Demonstration of triangle choke.

By contrast, air chokes such as the guillotine and Ezekiel choke (Figure 2A and B), compress the laryngotracheal complex against the vertebral column, restricting airflow and inflicting pain. Despite widespread use, injuries from air chokes are not well studied. One high‐profile example, however, underscores their potential morbidity. In a widely televised UFC event in 2025, a fighter sustained chokehold‐induced laryngeal fracture that was initially missed during hospital workup. The athlete was later admitted to the intensive care unit with a tension pneumothorax secondary to the fracture. 9

Demonstration of 2 common types of air chokes. (A) Demonstration of guillotine choke; (B) Demonstration of Ezekiel choke.

Much of our understanding of laryngotracheal injuries is derived from experience with blunt cervical trauma from motor vehicle collisions, 10 , 11 sporting injuries, or strangulation injuries. Among sporting injuries, ice hockey has been shown to be a common culprit, constituting nearly half of laryngeal fractures in 1 series. 10 , 12 Strangulation‐related laryngotracheal injuries have also been well characterized, with hyoid and/or thyroid cartilage fractures observed in 20% and 40% of postmortem strangulation‐related cases, respectively. 13 BJJ/MMA‐related laryngotracheal injuries occupy a distinct middle ground, combining the high‐force impact of sports‐related trauma with the sustained compression of strangulation.

This study aims to better elucidate the prevalence of and characterize attitudes toward laryngopharyngeal injuries among BJJ and MMA athletes.

Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Kansas Medical Center (Study00161077). A 44‐item questionnaire was designed in a REDCap database hosted at the University of Kansas Medical Center (CTSA Award # UL1TR002366) to capture demographic data, knowledge of chokehold mechanisms, laryngopharyngeal symptoms or loss of consciousness (LOC) during or after chokehold practice, care‐seeking behaviors and any reported injuries or sequelae. Participants were given the option to provide contact information to volunteer for a follow‐up semi‐structured interview. English‐speaking BJJ/MMA practitioners aged 18 years or older we eligible for the study.

A combination of convenience and snowball sampling was used for recruitment. A subreddit dedicated to BJJ (www.reddit.com/r/bjj), the largest online grappling forum with over 800,000 members at the time of the study, served as the primary recruitment platform. 14 Multiple posts invited BJJ and MMA practitioners to participate anonymously. Additional outreach involved snowball sampling of Reddit posts containing terms such as “larynx,” “throat,” or “trachea” in combination with “injury,” “pain,” or “hurts.” Over 30 relevant posts describing anterior neck trauma and sequelae (eg, dysphagia, dysphonia, odynophagia) were identified, and authors and respondents were privately invited to participate. The survey was also disseminated via BJJ/MMA gym owners and coaches through email, text, and Instagram. The survey was open for a 6‐week period in late 2024.

GraphPad Prism (v10.5.0) was used for statistical analysis. Chi‐square and independent t‐tests were used to evaluate associations between training variables and injury outcomes. A P < .05 was established a priori as statistically significant.

Semi‐Structured Interviews

Interested respondents were purposively sampled for interviews. Participants received personalized invitations via email or phone. Interviews were conducted via video calls, lasting 30 to 45 minutes, and audio‐recorded with consent. Recordings were transcribed using Trint (Trint Ltd.; www.trint.com), a web‐based platform with validated accuracy comparable to professional transcription. 15

Interviews explored training history, motivation, injury experiences, symptoms, care‐seeking behaviors, perceived voice changes, and injury prevention. Transcripts were imported into Dedoose v9.0 (Sociocultural Research Consultants, LLC; www.dedoose.com) for qualitative analysis. 16 Two investigators independently developed an inductive codebook, reconciling discrepancies through discussion and refining operational definitions. A third senior researcher adjudicated conflicts. Thematic saturation was defined as 3 consecutive interviews yielding no new codes. Dedoose frequency functions were used to summarize the prevalence of themes and subthemes. Representative quotations were extracted verbatim from source audio files to ensure accuracy.

Results

Quantitative Findings

Awareness of Chokes and Laryngopharyngeal Injury

Most (94%) were confident in their ability to differentiate between blood and air chokes, and 91% indicated that air chokes are more likely to cause laryngopharyngeal injury. Nearly one‐quarter (22%) indicated that, at some point, they have been told—either by a coach, peer, or both—that they can “hold out” on air chokes prior to tapping.

Experience With LOC

LOC from a chokehold was reported by 35% of participants, half of whom reported experiencing LOC on 2 or more occasions. Most LOC events (82%) occurred during routine training rather than competition. The majority (66%) of those who had LOC history attributed it to lapel‐based chokes, which are a type of submission maneuver that involves using the fabric of one's own gi (martial arts kimono) material—either the lapel or the sleeve—to apply focal pressure to the neck (Figure 3).

Thirty‐eight percent reported symptom resolution within 0 to 3 days, 31% within 3 to 7 days, and 13% within 1 to 2 weeks. 18 percent reported symptoms lasting greater than 2 weeks. Thirty‐six percent of symptomatic respondents reported experiencing symptoms on 3 or more occasions. The guillotine choke (40%) and rear‐naked choke (34%) were most associated with laryngopharyngeal symptoms (Figure 4). Eighty‐seven percent of those who experienced symptoms continued to train while symptomatic.

Chokeholds associated with laryngopharyngeal symptoms.

Fifteen percent reported permanent voice changes related to their practice. Permanent voice change was independently associated with years in sport (P = .021). Permanent voice change was also statistically more likely to be seen in respondents who reported any LOC history (P = .016) and respondents who had symptoms last longer than 2 weeks (P = .027). Most practitioners (77%) indicated that a permanent voice change would not stop them from training.

Medical Attention and Seeking Care

Eighty‐seven percent of symptomatic respondents did not seek medical attention, with half indicating that they would only seek care if their symptoms lasted longer than 2 weeks. Most (89%) indicated that they would seek medical attention if they experienced hemoptysis or difficulty breathing (80%). A minority indicated that they would seek medical attention for globus sensation (33%), pain with swallowing (20%), hoarseness (10%), and sore throat (3%).

Twenty‐two percent indicated that either they or an associate had been diagnosed with laryngopharyngeal injury. Of the 18 participants in this cohort that sought medical attention, 39% were seen by an otolaryngologist, 33% by a primary care provider, 28% by an emergency provider and 17% by an urgent care provider. More than half (56%) underwent laryngoscopy. Of those that sought medical attention, 83% indicated that they received a diagnosis, with laryngeal fractures reportedly identified in 47% of cases.

Semi‐Structured Interviews

Thirteen participants were interviewed, and several key themes emerged. (Supplemental Tables S1‐S3, available online).

Perceived Risk and Decision to Tap

Participants often commented on (n = 111 occurrences, 100% of respondents) their awareness—or lack thereof—of injury risk during training, particularly in relation to air chokes. They reflected on the conflict between pushing through discomfort to progress versus recognizing signs that indicated the potential for serious harm: “…the guy that I rolled with just had a different style that I wasn't used to, and I think I was just trying to grit it out too much. Or maybe I had a lot of adrenaline going just because I was having a blast and didn't notice how much it hurt or something.”

“…the guy that I rolled with just had a different style that I wasn't used to, and I think I was just trying to grit it out too much. Or maybe I had a lot of adrenaline going just because I was having a blast and didn't notice how much it hurt or something.”

Cultural Minimization of Injury Risk

Cultural minimization of injury risk surfaced as a common theme (n = 68 occurrences, 92% of respondents). Participants discussed the culture within the BJJ and MMA communities, where pushing through pain or discomfort is often normalized, and injuries are viewed as an inevitable part of training: “They use [injuries] as a badge of honor…I've heard on occasion for example, the coach just pointed at someone one day, saying this guy—he's been injured, but he's never stopped training …there's a culture of…why weren't you here? Why weren't you training?”

“They use [injuries] as a badge of honor…I've heard on occasion for example, the coach just pointed at someone one day, saying this guy—he's been injured, but he's never stopped training …there's a culture of…why weren't you here? Why weren't you training?”

Mechanics of Dangerous Chokes

The mechanics of specific chokes was a recurring topic (n = 126 occurrences, 100% of respondents). Many participants detailed how certain choke variations were more likely to cause discomfort or injury, especially when applied more rapidly than expected: “I was taught that you pretend your forearm is piano wire on chokes and pretend you're an assassin. That's a slicing motion across the throat. And it's bone on throat. And they're teaching that and letting guys practice that over and over.”

“I was taught that you pretend your forearm is piano wire on chokes and pretend you're an assassin. That's a slicing motion across the throat. And it's bone on throat. And they're teaching that and letting guys practice that over and over.”

Mismatch in Training Partner

Another theme that emerged was the mismatch in training partners (n = 58 occurrences, 85% of respondents), particularly regarding size, experience and training intensity: “I think the fact that it didn't work for the first four minutes, and he kept trying it, I think a more experienced guy would not do that.”

“I think the fact that it didn't work for the first four minutes, and he kept trying it, I think a more experienced guy would not do that.”

Distrust or Disillusionment With Healthcare

A small but notable group of participants (n = 28 occurrences, 62% of respondents) expressed distrust or disillusionment with the healthcare system, citing past negative experiences with medical professionals or a general belief that healthcare providers might not understand the nature of combat sports injuries: “I worked with my [PCP] for a couple of times and he was like, I don't know, dude, go to the VA.”

“I worked with my [PCP] for a couple of times and he was like, I don't know, dude, go to the VA.”

Barriers to Seeking Care

Barriers to seeking medical care (n = 31 occurrences, 77% of respondents) included time constraints, financial concerns, fear of being sidelined from training, and a lack of understanding of the severity of their symptoms: “It really wasn't affecting my life any more than just when I'm eating, and I eat something dry, I have to cough it back up. So I've just had to deal with that.”

“It really wasn't affecting my life any more than just when I'm eating, and I eat something dry, I have to cough it back up. So I've just had to deal with that.”

Gaps in Education on Airway Safety

Every participant noted a significant gap in education about airway safety in BJJ/MMA (n = 247 occurrences, 100% of respondents). Athletes indicated that while they receive some instruction on general safety and techniques, specific education about the risks of laryngopharyngeal injuries is sparse. This gap was particularly notable among newer practitioners: “There was no emphasis put on the risks associated with certain types of chokes. So that was something that I was more or less ignorant of until I was, you know, it was too late.”

“There was no emphasis put on the risks associated with certain types of chokes. So that was something that I was more or less ignorant of until I was, you know, it was too late.”

Discussion

This study is the first to characterize both the prevalence and cultural perceptions of laryngopharyngeal injury among BJJ and MMA athletes. Symptoms consistent with laryngopharyngeal trauma appear to be a routine part of training for many athletes, raising concern that clinically meaningful injuries may be normalized or missed. Despite this, the qualitative data suggest that symptoms are frequently normalized, with widespread gaps in airway safety education and practical barriers to seeking care—including a sense for some that healthcare providers had little understanding of what grappling martial arts entail.

Notably, the subset who did seek care reported clinically significant diagnoses, suggesting that when athletes present, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion even if symptoms are minimized. Unrecognized symptoms of injury, delayed care, and underestimation of injury severity by both athletes and clinicians can negatively impact outcomes and result in a chronic functional deficit. Reports of permanent voice change raise concern for unrecognized (and therefore, untreated) or cumulative sub‐threshold laryngopharyngeal injury, given the significant association between years and sport and permanent voice change.

Existing literature on chokehold safety has focused primarily on blood chokes, illustrating over a dozen examples of often devastating cerebrovascular injury that may occur when blood chokes are applied incorrectly or in the face of pre‐existing risk factors. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 17 LOC appears to be a normalized experience in this population, and often attributable to a distinct set of chokehold techniques (eg, lapel‐based chokes). Instructor oversight is advisable for those practicing these techniques, especially given that most LOC events occur during regular, unsupervised sparring in the gym, and not in competition, where referees trained to mitigate injury risk and medical personnel are readily available. Although professional martial arts competitions are subject to oversight by state athletic commissions and federal legislation, 18 these regulatory frameworks do not extend to day‐to‐day training sessions, where most chokehold exposure occurs.

Air chokes have received significantly less attention in the literature. Our study highlights the potential morbidity of air chokes, ranging from chronic functional issues (odynophagia and dysphonia) to fractures requiring surgery. Few of the athletes in this study were formally taught how to apply these techniques safely or recognize associated injuries. Paradoxically, some respondents described being encouraged to “hold out” longer on tapping to air chokes or to apply these chokes in a particularly vicious fashion to inflict laryngeal pain. Participants consistently described limited formal instruction on airway safety and recognition of laryngopharyngeal injury.

Voice symptoms illustrate that air chokes may cause not only acute laryngopharyngeal trauma, but chronic, “sub‐threshold” injuries that, when repeated over months to years of training, permanently alter participants' voices. A loss of higher vocal ranges was commonly noted among interview subjects. Yet most athletes reported continued training, expressing little concern about the symptoms. No participants reported awareness that chokehold practice may incur a risk to vocal quality, and several respondents who sung both recreationally and semi‐professionally reported diminished vocal performance. Both recreational and professional voice users should be aware of this risk should they choose to participate in BJJ/MMA.

Qualitative interviews identified a pervasive “push through the pain” mindset in BJJ and MMA. Seeking medical care, while not explicitly stigmatized, is often eschewed. Several interviewees even reported a sense of pride in being able to “train through” injuries, and this practice was occasionally lauded by coaches. Several athletes feared losing training time, citing the almost addictive nature of the sport as an impediment to seeking care. A sizeable minority of respondents expressed skepticism that healthcare providers understood the nature of combat sports injuries, a barrier that may further reduce care‐seeking behavior. For clinicians, this highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for laryngopharyngeal injury in athletes presenting with laryngopharyngeal symptoms—even when patients minimize their complaints.

Increased awareness among primary care providers, emergency physicians, and otolaryngologists is critical. Laryngoscopy should be considered in BJJ/MMA athletes with voice changes, odynophagia, or dyspnea after chokehold practice. Concerning history or exam findings (crepitus, palpable anterior neck irregularities) in the absence of laryngoscopic abnormalities should prompt consideration for high‐resolution computed tomography of the neck, as even minimally displaced laryngeal fractures may impact both function and stability of the laryngeal framework. Importantly, laryngeal fractures may be radiographically underdiagnosed in younger athletes, as non‐displaced injuries of non‐ossified cartilage is more difficult to visualize on CT. 19

Timely diagnosis is critical. Multiple studies emphasize that laryngeal fractures requiring surgical repair should ideally be treated within 48 hours—and no later than 2 weeks—to optimize outcomes. 20 , 21 , 22 Beyond that window, scar formation and distortion of soft tissue planes may render repair either suboptimal or no longer feasible. Many athletes indicated they would delay evaluation beyond this critical window. Healthcare providers should have both realistic expectations and frank discussions with these patients about the inherent risks of grappling martial arts, the importance of early symptom reporting, and safe return‐to‐sport guidelines that can reduce long‐term morbidity.

Ultimately, injury prevention efforts must begin in the gym. Coaches, instructors, and fellow athletes should emphasize airway safety alongside existing injury prevention curricula. Technical refinements—such as gradually tightening chokes, avoiding motions that rapidly compress the airway, and encouraging early tapping—may help reduce the risk of laryngeal trauma. The guillotine choke may represent a critical area of focus for airway safety. A useful parallel exists in joint locks, such as the heel hook: when applied improperly or hastily, heel hooks can cause devastating knee injuries. As a result, they are banned from competition at lower levels and taught in an extremely conservative fashion.

At the community level, BJJ and MMA organizations could also consider incorporating airway safety modules into belt promotion criteria or instructor certifications. Broader dissemination of these findings to state athletic commissions and national martial arts organizations may further support these efforts in facilitating development of standardized educational materials on airway safety, early symptom recognition, and safe return‐to‐training recommendations following suspected laryngopharyngeal injury. While formal regulation of independent gym training may be logistically challenging, promoting evidence‐informed safety guidance at the state and organizational level represents a feasible step toward reducing preventable injury.

This study has limitations. Convenience sampling via online platforms may have introduced selection bias, particularly toward athletes more willing to report injuries. As such, our sample may have overrepresented symptomatic participants and/or participants with laryngopharyngeal injury history. Data were self‐reported and therefore subject to both recall bias and the inability to clinically validate clinical data. Despite these limitations, our findings show that air choke‐related laryngopharyngeal injuries are both common and underrecognized in both the athlete and medical communities. Integrating airway safety into combat sports culture and increasing clinician awareness may help reduce preventable injuries and improve athlete outcomes.

Conclusion

Laryngopharyngeal symptoms suggestive of injury are prevalent among BJJ and MMA athletes. Care seeking is rare and persistent symptoms are not uncommon. Cultural attitudes, educational gaps, and lack of clinician awareness may contribute to decreased injury recognition and undertreatment. Increased educational efforts within the combat sports community, as well as improved collaboration between grappling athletes and healthcare providers is needed to promote airway safety and protect athletes as these sports continue to expand.
