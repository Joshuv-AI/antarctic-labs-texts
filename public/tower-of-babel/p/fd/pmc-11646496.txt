Hypertension Severity as Quantified by Hypertension Daily Dose and Blood Pressure With Risk of Stroke in REGARDS
Authors: Ying K. Loo, Katherine Wilkinson, Tyler Harkness, George Howard, Virginia J. Howard, Suzanne E. Judd, Neil A. Zakai, Paul Muntner, Lillian Min, Suzanne Oparil, Timothy B. Plante
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is unknown how blood pressure (BP) relates to stroke risk across levels of hypertension daily dose (HDD)‐quantified antihypertensive medication intensity.Methods and ResultsThe REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study enrolled 30 239 participants from the 48 contiguous US states in 2003 to 2007 with in‐person follow‐up in 2013 to 2016 (Visit 2). We included those without prior stroke at Visit 2, treating this visit as T0. Biannual phone calls and medical record review ascertained incident stroke events. Cox proportional hazard models estimated the hazard ratio (HR) of incident stroke by treatment intensity defined by systolic BP stages and HDD groupings. There were 344 stroke events over a median 5.5 years. Relative to systolic BP <120 mm Hg and no antihypertensive medications, the stroke HR was 2.86 (95% CI, 1.68–4.85) for systolic BP 140 to 159 mm Hg and HDD tertile 2, 2.33 (1.37–3.97) for systolic BP 140 to 159 mm Hg and HDD tertile 3, 3.08 (1.20–7.88) for systolic BP ≥160 mm Hg and HDD tertile 2, and 3.66 (1.61–8.30) for systolic BP ≥160 mm Hg and HDD tertile 3. Stroke risk was similar across HDD levels for people with systolic BP <140 mm Hg.ConclusionsAmong adults without prior stroke, systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg and HDD tertile ≥2 was associated with greater stroke risk. For adults with BP <140 mm Hg, stroke risk was similar despite cumulative dose of antihypertensive medications used. These findings support the practice of BP‐lowering medications to mitigate stroke risk.

Hypertension is one of the most prevalent health conditions in the United States, with an estimated prevalence of 47% among US adults. 1 , 2 Hypertension is a modifiable risk factor for stroke, coronary heart disease, kidney disease, and cognitive decline, which makes blood pressure (BP) an important target for multiple disease prevention. 3 Among the various sequelae of hypertension, one especially detrimental complication of hypertension is stroke. Stroke is the fifth leading cause of death in the United States and the second most common cause of mortality worldwide. 4 , 5 In adults with hypertension, antihypertensive medications are the most effective approach to reducing the risk of first or recurrent strokes. 6 Residual risks of stroke persists even if BP is controlled with antihypertensive medication, and stroke risk appears to increase with more intensive hypertension treatment regimens defined by number of unique antihypertensive classes. 7 However, using only counts of antihypertensive drug classes fails to account for increasing doses of individual medications.

Hypertension daily dose (HDD) 8 quantifies an aggregated dose‐intensity of individual antihypertensive medications across multiple classes of antihypertensive agents. A standardized HDD unit of 1 is defined as 50% of the dose of an antihypertensive medication needed to provide a fully beneficial effect. For example, amlodipine's full beneficial dose is 10 mg per day, a dose of amlodipine 5 mg per day equals an HDD of 1; lisinopril's full beneficial dose is 40 mg per day, a dose of lisinopril 20 mg per day equals an HDD of 1, with combinations of lesser doses of multiple agents (eg, lisinopril 10 mg per day plus amlodipine 2.5 mg per day equals an HDD of 1; lisinopril 40 mg per day plus amlodipine 10 mg per day equals an HDD of 4). Using an antihypertensive treatment intensity metric might more precisely quantify medication treatment than medication counts alone. The association between this metric of total antihypertensive dose‐intensity and stroke risk is unknown.

The objective of this study is to estimate the risk of incident stroke by systolic BP and level of HDD among adults free from antecedent stroke. We hypothesized that stroke risk would be greater among individuals with both higher systolic BP and higher HDD.

Methods

Data Sharing Statement

REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) data, including those that were used in the present analysis, are available to investigators with an approved study proposal. Policies and procedures about submitting study proposals can be found at https://www.uab.edu/soph/regardsstudy/researchers.

REGARDS Design

REGARDS enrolled 30 239 Black and White adults aged ≥45 years from the 48 continuous US states in 2003 to 2007. It oversampled Black participants and those living in the Stroke Belt, a cluster of Southeastern states with high stroke mortality. 9 Briefly, participants were recruited by mail and telephone, using a computer‐assisted telephone interview, and provided verbal informed consent, basic demographics, and medical history information. An in‐home visit (Visit 1) was conducted approximately 3 to 4 weeks later, where a brief physical exam was conducted that included height and weight, BP measurements, medication inventory, blood and urine samples, an ECG, and written informed consent. The BP measurement protocol consisted of measuring systolic BP and diastolic BP by a trained technician using a standard protocol, where participants were first seated for 5 minutes, then a sphygmomanometer was placed on the left upper arm whenever possible and was inflated to a pressure just until the pulse disappears and then inflated another 20 mm Hg above this pulse obliteration pressure, before slowly deflating to obtain specific BP values. This BP taking process was performed twice for each participant at each in‐home visit, and the average of the 2 measurements were used. A second in‐home exam (Visit 2) was conducted in 2013to 2016 and was similar in design to Visit 1. One important difference between the visits is in completion of the medication logs. Medication logs from Visit 1 collected only medication name. Visit 2 included medication name, dosage, and frequency of administration. For this reason, we used BP measurements and calculated HDD values from Visit 2 as this time point contains the complete medication inventory needed to determine HDD. Visit 1 BP measurements and BP medication inventories were not used. Institutional review boards of the participating institutions approved the REGARDS protocol.

Analytical Population

Participants were included if they attended Visit 2 with in‐home assessment. Participants were excluded if they had a stroke before Visit 2, missing stroke details at Visit 2, no follow‐up beyond Visit 2, or if systolic BP was not measured at Visit 2.

HDD Metric and HDD Groupings

The medication inventory for each participant was synthesized and converted into a numeric HDD value based on the HDD metric. 8 First, an HDD was calculated for each individual medication by incorporating dosage and frequency details. The overall HDD score included the HDD for each individual antihypertensive medication that is commonly used for BP lowering (hereafter, “HDD” refers to this overall HDD score). Specifically, HDD included angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, thiazides or thiazide‐like diuretics, potassium‐sparing diuretics, β‐blockers, centrally acting α‐agonists, vasodilators, and direct renin inhibitors. As in the HDD validation article, 8 we excluded the use of loop diuretics, nitrates, and α‐antagonists because, although these agents have BP‐lowering properties, their primary indications are typically not essential hypertension (eg, loop diuretics and nitrates for heart failure, α‐antagonists for benign prostatic hyperplasia). 10 Moreover, these agents are often given on an “as needed” basis and thus may falsely elevate or decrease true HDD values for individuals who are taking them.

HDD scores were stratified into 4 categories: the first category encompasses all participants who were not on any hypertension medication (HDD=0), and the remainder were distributed into tertiles.

Systolic BP Groupings

Participants were also stratified into 4 systolic BP categories using the Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC 7) 11 classification: <120, 120 to 139, 140 to 159, and ≥160 mm Hg. In a secondary analysis, we used the 2017 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) guidelines 12 and stratified the systolic BP into <120, 120 to 129, 130 to 139, and ≥140 mm Hg.

Stroke Events

The outcome of interest was incident stroke event following Visit 2. Stroke was defined as (1) World Health Organization stroke—focal neurological deficit lasting ≥24 hours, confirmed with medical records, or (2) clinical stroke—focal or nonfocal neurological deficit with positive imaging, including those lasting <24 hours, confirmed with medical records. Participants included in this analysis were followed for stroke events via computer‐assisted telephone interview at 6‐month intervals, with medical records retrieved on suspected events that required hospitalization. These medical records were adjudicated for stroke outcomes by a physician panel. The follow‐up for incident stroke events occurred through September 30, 2020.

Statistical Analysis

Visit 2 was considered “baseline” (index time) for this analysis because it was the only visit collecting sufficient medication details to calculate HDD. BP measurements and a complete medication inventory were obtained for each participant and were used to stratify participants based on systolic BP categories and dose intensity of antihypertensive regimen.

Cumulative incidence plots demonstrated stroke events during follow‐up by the groups of participants defined by their BP and treatment intensity. Cox proportional hazards models estimated hazard ratios (HRs) for incident stroke. We adjusted for age, race, sex, age‐by‐race interaction, the deviation from the mean systolic BP level for the category, and the deviation from the mean HDD for the tertile. The age‐by‐race interaction adjustment was included based on prior studies showing this interaction to be a statistically significant confounding factor. 13 , 14 The systolic BP deviation is the difference between each participant's systolic BP and the mean of all participants' systolic BP in their respective systolic BP stratum. This was used to remove potential residual confounding from differences in mean systolic BP levels within each systolic BP stratum. Similarly, the deviation from mean HDD within each tertile was adjusted to remove any residual confounding that can arise from these continuous variables. In an additional sensitivity analysis, we adjusted for covariates in the prior model as well as additional covariates based on the Framingham Stroke Risk Profile (FSRP) including presence of diabetes, smoking status, history of myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, and left ventricular hypertrophy. The primary statistical analysis was performed with JNC 7 systolic BP groupings, then a secondary analysis with AHA/ACC 2017 systolic BP groupings, then an additional sensitivity analysis with JNC 7 systolic BP groupings adding FSRP risk factors.

We considered P < 0.05 to be statistically significant. Analyses used R version 4.1.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing) and Stata v17 (StataCorp, College Station, TX).

Of the 30 239 unique participants in the REGARDS study, 14 448 completed Visit 2. Of those, 553 was excluded due to stroke before Visit 2, 41 due to missing stroke data at Visit 2, 568 had no follow‐up beyond Visit 2, and 21 for missing systolic BP data (Figure 1). These exclusions led to a final population of 13 265 participants who were followed for incident stroke events after Visit 2.

HR for Stroke by HDD and Systolic BP Groupings

For participants with systolic BP <120 and 120 to 139 mm Hg, risk of incident stroke was similar across HDD groupings (Figure 3) whereas those in a higher systolic BP strata of ≥140 mm Hg had a greater HR for stroke across higher HDD. This pattern achieved statistical significance for the 140 to 159 mm Hg group (11% of population; P = 0.009) but not for the ≥160 mm Hg group (2% of population; P = 0.15).

When comparing stroke risk by HDD groupings, there was no statistical significance in stroke risk across systolic BP groupings if not on BP medications or in HDD tertile 1. There was greater stroke risk across systolic BP groupings for those in HDD tertile 2 (P = 0.006) and HDD tertile 3 (P = 0.001).

Secondary Analysis Using the ACC/AHA 2017 Systolic BP Groupings

In the secondary analysis that applied systolic BP groupings from the 2017 ACC/AHA BP guideline, cumulative stroke events were similar for the 3 lowest groupings (<120 mm Hg, 120–129 mm Hg, and 130–139 mm Hg; Figure S1). Those with systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg had nearly a doubling of observed stroke events relative to the other groups. As is shown within systolic BP groupings in Table S1 and Figure S2, the number of stroke events and HR for stroke were similar across HDD groupings for those with systolic BP <120 mm Hg, 120 to 129 mm Hg, and 130 to 139 mm Hg. For those with systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg, most observed strokes occurred in HDD tertiles 2 and 3. Within HDD groupings, the numbers of stroke events across systolic BP groupings for those not on BP meds and in HDD tertile 1 were similar. The most stroke events occurred in HDD tertiles 2 and 3 for those with systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg.

Sensitivity Analysis Using the JNC 7 Systolic BP Groupings and Adjusting for Framingham Stroke Risk Profile

We performed a sensitivity analysis of the primary analysis with additional adjustments for covariates based on the FSRP. When adjusted for presence of diabetes, smoking status, history of myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation, and left ventricular hypertrophy, once again the HR for stroke was similar across HDD groupings for those with systolic BP <140 mm Hg (Table S2). In the systolic BP 140to 159 mm Hg and tertile 3 as well as the ≥160 mm Hg and tertile 2 groupings, the HR for stroke is no longer statistically significant when FSRP are adjusted for. However, there continues to be a statistically significant trend in HR for stroke in tertiles 2 and 3 when going from systolic BP <120 mm Hg to ≥160 mm Hg.

Discussion

In a large US‐based cohort study of Black and White adults without prevalent stroke, the risk of incident stroke was similar among those with systolic BP <140 mm Hg, regardless of use of BP‐lowering medications or intensity of antihypertensive therapy. Among those with systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg, stroke risk was higher among those HDD >1. Because an HDD of 1 is equivalent to half the maximum beneficial dose of a single medication (or multiple agents that when combined are equivalent to half the maximum beneficial dose of a single medication), individuals with uncontrolled hypertension on at least moderate doses of antihypertensives were observed to have an elevated stroke risk. Stroke risk was similar across systolic BP groupings <140 mm Hg, including the updated ACC/AHA hypertension target of <130 mm Hg relative to 130 to 139 mm Hg and with the additional sensitivity analysis incorporating FSRP. Adjusting for these additional stroke risk factors showed that stroke risk was highest among those with systolic BP ≥160 mm Hg and with HDD ≥2.51, indicating the detrimental effects experienced by this treatment‐resistant group.

Elevated BP is the most prevalent modifiable risk factor for stroke. Data from meta‐analyses studying the correlation between BP and stroke risk have shown that each 10 mm Hg decrease in systolic BP down to 115/75 mm Hg is associated with a 33% lower stroke risk in those aged 60 to 79 years. 15 High BP can cause stroke by several mechanisms. Over time, the mechanical stresses that high BP exerts on arteries throughout the body cause smooth muscle cells in systemic and cerebral arteries to undergo hypertrophy and remodeling, leading to narrowing of the vessels and resultant ischemia. In addition, endothelial damage of the vasculature due to high BP creates a favorable environment for atherosclerotic plaque formation. These plaques can break off and embolize to the brain, causing ischemic strokes. 16 Optimal BP control is a mainstay strategy in the prevention of stroke events, although the extent to which antihypertensive medication‐driven BP lowering reduces hypertension's stroke risk is unclear.

A 2015 REGARDS analysis that stratified its sample population by level of observed systolic BP and count of BP medications showed that adults with hypertension and a larger number of BP medications had greater stroke risk than those treated with a lower number of BP medications, despite having similar BP. 7 Participants were divided into 4 BP categories: normotensive, prehypertensive, stage 1 hypertension, and stage 2 hypertension; for those in the 3 higher systolic BP strata, stroke risk was higher for those on more classes of antihypertensive medications. 7 When stratified by the number of classes of antihypertensive medication participants were taking, the study found that those not taking any medication were at a higher risk of stroke at each successive systolic BP stratum. Those on 1 or 2 classes of antihypertensive medications had a higher stroke risk at higher BP levels, but the increase was smaller compared with those not on medication. Counting the number of BP medication classes may misclassify the intensity of BP‐lowering medical therapy, because individuals on low doses of multiple classes of medications may have lower overall antihypertensive intensity than individuals on a maximal dose of a single agent.

Our analysis incorporated the HDD metric, a validated metric that aggregates cumulative does of BP‐lowering medications over multiple drug classes, to quantify the intensity of BP‐lowering therapy across multiple agents. We observed that low‐ or high dose‐intensity of antihypertensive medication treatment yielded similar stroke risks when systolic BP was <140 mm Hg. We did not observe residual stroke risk among those with systolic BP <140 mm Hg who required medical therapy, regardless of intensity. Therefore, intensive BP‐lowering with medical therapy may attenuate stroke risk if BP levels are brought below a systolic BP threshold of 140 mm Hg. This is in contrast with the prior REGARDS analysis that observed residual stroke risk among those on more classes of BP medication, regardless of systolic BP strata. 7

The present findings advance scientific knowledge in several ways. Current hypertension guidelines put forth by the ACC and AHA recommend a systolic BP treatment goal of <130 mm Hg across a wide spectrum of risks and comorbidities, citing individual studies and meta‐analyses that have reported progressively higher CVD risk as systolic BP increased from <120 mm Hg, to 120 to 129 mm Hg, to 130 to 139 mm Hg. 12 These updated hypertension guidelines have not been universally accepted. 17 , 18 In the present analysis, we did not observe differential stroke risk mediated by lower systolic BP groupings (ie, stroke risk was not lower for those with systolic BP <120 mm Hg in comparison with 130–139 mm Hg). These findings support systolic BP targets <140 mm Hg for stroke reduction and are consistent with trials such as the SPRINT (Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial) that reduced BP to below <120 mmHg but that had less relative reduction of stroke compared with the reduction of acute heart failure. 6 One possible explanation is that this analysis focuses only on the risk of stroke at various systolic BP and HDD levels, whereas the AHA/ACC guidelines referenced studies such as the SPRINT trial with primary outcomes of myocardial infarction, heart failure, and acute coronary syndromes, in addition to stroke. 6

Interestingly, this analysis also shows that those in the systolic BP 140 to 159 mm Hg and ≥160 mm Hg ranges and who are on no medication or low amounts of medication (HDD 0 and 1), are not at increased risk of stroke. This population may represent “white coat hypertension,” a phenomenon where an individual's BP readings are falsely elevated due to the environment or situation where these measurements are taken, typically because they are taken in a clinic or by a health care professional. This would mean that these participants may be misclassified into a higher systolic BP category due to these transiently elevated BP readings, but that their bodies are not subject to the same chronic mechanical stress from consistently elevated BP and therefore do not experience the same sequelae in the form of higher stroke risk. Another possible explanation for this group may be that Visit 2 was the first time they were diagnosed with hypertension, and they subsequently received antihypertensive treatment to control systolic BP to <140 mm Hg, which would effectively change them to the <140 mm Hg category and continue to support our conclusion that risk of stroke is similar across BP groups <140 mm Hg regardless of amount of antihypertensive use.

This analysis has strengths and limitations. Strengths include the use of a large sample size and a consistent protocol for recording BP data and adjudicating incident stroke. Another strength of this study is the precision by which hypertension therapy was quantified using the summary HDD metric, which included dose intensity and frequency to assess the intensity of hypertension treatment more precisely. Limitations of this study include unknown medication adherence by the study participants. It is possible that third tertile HDD groups were given high doses but not taken. As this study captures each individual's BP and antihypertensive regimen as a snapshot in time, it does not reflect the history of potential cumulative vascular stress and injury before this time point that can increase risk of thrombus formation and weaken cerebral vessel walls. In addition, the REGARDS cohort study used the measured average of 2 BP values during a single encounter (at Visit 2) for the determination of BP in this analysis, as opposed to average of 2 BP values made on 2 separate encounters, which is the clinical standard for diagnosis of hypertension. Because the data to calculate the HDD were not available until Visit 2 in REGARDS, the follow‐up for incident events had to begin at Visit 2. This implies that the incident stroke events occurring between the Visit 1 and Visit 2 could not be used in this analysis. This led to a reduction in the number of stroke events from 860 events in the prior report assessing the number of classes of medications 7 to the 344 available for the current analysis. This reduction in the number of stroke events is associated with a decrease in power, making it more possible that failing to detect an association with HDD level for select systolic BP strata was simply a Type 2 error (ie, failed to detect an association that is truly present). Further, the systolic BP ≥160 mm Hg stratum had proportionally fewer participants than other strata and failed to reach a level of statistical significance. The magnitude of association with HDD was similar to the 140 to 159 mm Hg strata, however. Finally, there was incomplete follow‐up between Visits 1 and 2 because of death and study withdrawal. The analytical population of this study is likely healthier than the REGARDS population at baseline.

Conclusions

In conclusion, in a large prospective cohort of Black and White US adults without prevalent stroke, those with systolic BP <140 mm Hg had similar stroke risk, regardless of HDD‐quantified BP medication intensity. Those with systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg, and particularly those with BP medication HDD >1, had increased risk of incident stroke. We did not observe lower stroke risk in those with systolic BP <130 mm Hg relative to the JNC 7 systolic hypertension threshold of 140 mm Hg.
