Temporary intravascular shunting using a carotid shunt for traumatic femoral artery and vein injury in a hemodynamically unstable patient
Authors: Nicola M. Habash, Fari Fall, Jin Park, Babak Abai, Michael Nooromid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Vascular trauma involving the peripheral arteries of the extremities can lead to limb loss and, in severe cases, death. The use of intravascular shunts can help to temporarily perfuse the limb, while allowing for resuscitation of a critically injured patient. Here we present a case of a concomitant complex injury of the common femoral artery and common femoral vein, and the complementary vascular strategies used to achieve limb salvage.

Vascular trauma involving the peripheral arteries of the extremities can lead to limb loss and, in severe cases, death. These injuries pose a dual threat of hemorrhage and ischemia.1 Temporary intravascular shunts are indicated for use in vascular injuries affecting the proximal portion of extremity vessels, providing a means of maintaining perfusion until definitive repair can be performed.1 The use of vascular shunts for temporary arterial revascularization of the proximal extremity has been well-described in wartime settings2 and has been increasingly adopted in civilian trauma centers.3,4 More recently, venous repair, rather than routine ligation, has gained favor, although reports of concomitant venous shunting, particularly of the femoral vein (FV), remain uncommon.5 This report describes the integration of complementary vascular strategies, temporary arterial and venous shunting, and autologous spiral vein grafting, applied sequentially within a damage control framework to achieve limb salvage after complex femoral vessel trauma. The patient provided consent for case publication.

Case report

A 20-year-old man presented as a level 1 trauma after sustaining a gunshot wound to the left groin, with an exit wound in the left gluteus. He was unknown to the institution, and no prior medical history was available. He was hemodynamically unstable upon arrival, with a heart rate fluctuating between 70 and 130 beats per minute and systolic pressures ranging from the 60s to 110s mm Hg. The primary survey noted absent palpable pulses in the left lower extremity and an expanding hematoma in the left groin. Neurological assessment of the limb could not be performed owing to intubation. A Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma examination was negative, but plain abdominal radiography showed a possible metallic foreign body; therefore, the trauma team performed an emergent exploratory laparotomy for rapid triage and potential iliac vessel control. No intra-abdominal hemorrhage was identified, prompting a focus on the left lower extremity.

Exploration revealed a large anteromedial thigh hematoma with active hemorrhage. Both the femoral artery and vein sustained blast injuries and were transected at the level of the common femoral artery (CFA) bifurcation, where the CFA divides into the superficial femoral artery (SFA) and profunda femoris artery (PFA), and where the deep femoral vein (DFV) and FV converge to form the common FV (CFV). A massive transfusion protocol was initiated. Vascular surgery was consulted intraoperatively and, on arrival, the trauma team had clamped the SFA. Control was sequentially established over the CFA, PFA (Fig 1, A), CFV, FV, and DFV (Fig 1, B). The proximal great saphenous vein (GSV) was transected by the gunshot wound and ligated. At this point, the patient received 24 U of blood products (2 U whole blood, 9 U packed red blood cells, 9 U fresh frozen plasma, 2 U platelets, and 2 U cryoprecipitate). Given the presence of acidosis (lactate 9.0 mmol/L, pH 7.03), coagulopathy, and hypothermia (35.4 °C), and episodic hypotension requiring pressor support, definitive reconstruction was deferred in favor of temporary intravascular shunting. A 3 × 5 mm Sundt internal carotid endarterectomy shunt (Integra Neurosciences) was used to connect the CFV to the FV and restore venous drainage. This was followed by a CFA-to-SFA shunt to reestablish arterial perfusion. Shunts were secured with 2-0 silk ties and the PFA was oversewn (Fig 1, C). Owing to prolonged ischemia, prophylactic four-compartment lower leg and two-compartment thigh fasciotomies were performed. The patient remained intubated and was transferred to the intensive care unit for resuscitation, rewarming, and continued heparin infusion targeting a partial thromboplastin time of 60 to 80 seconds until return to the operating room.

After resuscitation, he was hemodynamically stable but preoperative vein mapping did not identify a suitable contralateral GSV. He developed marked venous swelling and leg edema, prompting return to the operating room for definitive reconstruction (6 hours and 48 minutes later). The shunt was intended to provide temporary outflow during early stabilization and was expected to last several hours. Although its small caliber contributed to venous engorgement, it remained patent and served its purpose. Owing to size mismatch between the native FV and the available GSV, a spiral vein graft was fashioned using a 15-cm segment of the transected ipsilateral GSV, wrapped around a 32F chest tube and sutured in a running fashion with 6-0 Prolene, yielding a 6-cm graft of the appropriate caliber (Fig 2). We recommend using an appropriately sized thin-walled chest tube or metal dilator as a mold when constructing the spiral vein graft. This ensures a consistent luminal diameter and facilitates a uniform suture line, which is critical for maintaining laminar flow and preventing kinking, redundancy, or narrowing, especially in longer segments.Fig 2Spiral vein fashioning. Venous repair began by suturing the back walls of the FV and DFV together with 5-0 Prolene to create a common channel to serve as a single outflow tract. This newly constructed confluence was then anastomosed end-to-end to the distal end of the spiral graft, with the proximal end of the graft subsequently anastomosed to the CFV (Fig 3). Arterial repair was performed using a 7 mm × 29 cm cryopreserved saphenous vein graft owing to inadequate autologous conduit. The main graft was first cut to appropriate length, and a 3-cm segment of the discarded segment was repurposed to create a side limb. To recreate the native bifurcation of the CFA, a graftotomy was made in the main graft and the 3-cm segment was anastomosed to the main conduit in end-to-side fashion with 6-0 Prolene. The main graft's proximal end was sewn to the CFA using 5-0 Prolene, and the distal end to the SFA with 6-0 Prolene. The side limb was then joined to the PFA to restore arterial continuity (Fig 4). After repair, the patient had a palpable dorsalis pedis pulse and a posterior tibial signal detectable by Doppler ultrasound examination, confirming successful limb reperfusion. Venous swelling resolved promptly after reconstruction. All fasciotomy wounds were closed on postoperative day (POD) 2, once edema had resolved sufficiently to allow tension-free closure. He remained on a heparin drip, transitioned to oral anticoagulation with rivaroxaban and aspirin by POD 6, and was discharged on POD 9. By approximately 4 months postoperatively, he had regained full mobility with physical therapy, with independent ambulation and return to baseline function. At 15 months, he returned with short distance claudication. Angiography revealed high-grade stenosis of the CFA, SFA, and PFA, and he underwent successful drug-coated balloon angioplasty with transition to dual antiplatelet therapy owing to financial barriers to long-term anticoagulation. At the 29-month follow-up, the ankle-brachial index was 1.1. Most important, his leg was saved.

Discussion

By pairing temporary shunting to secure immediate perfusion with spiral vein grafting to reestablish durable venous outflow, this case illustrates a coordinated pathway from damage control to definitive reconstruction. The sequence prioritizes rapid stabilization of hemorrhage and ischemia, preserves arterial inflow by maintaining outflow, and then uses an autologous conduit to rebuild the venous system once physiology permits. The emphasis is on versatile, resource-aware decision-making guided by the patient's condition, operative bandwidth, and graft availability at each step. This framework is directly applicable to trauma and acute care practice, where instability, limited personnel, and variable conduit options are common, and it offers a practical template—restore inflow, preserve outflow, and defer complex reconstruction until resuscitation allows—for achieving limb salvage under real-world constraints.

Most published studies on temporary vascular shunting describe its use in settings without on-site vascular surgery expertise, where it serves as a bridge to definitive care at higher-level centers.6 These reports predominantly focus on arterial shunting, with venous repair gaining more attention only in recent years.5 Although venous repair is generally straightforward in isolated injuries, decision-making becomes more nuanced in the context of polytrauma, hemorrhagic shock, and hemodynamic instability.5 Current studies advocate for immediate venous repair or ligation.7,8 Although practice may vary by anatomical location, repair is generally favored in junctional venous injuries, such as those involving the FV.

Venous repair has been shown to reduce the risk of compartment syndrome and contractures.1,9 There is also a limb salvage benefit when venous repair accompanies arterial repair in combined injuries.10 Although arterial shunting has well-documented outcomes, evidence supporting venous shunting remains limited. Concerns regarding thrombosis and patency have raised questions about the morbidity of delayed venous repair, particularly in the absence of clear data showing improved limb outcomes.2,6 However, thrombosis of venous shunts is infrequent and rarely results in limb loss.5 In this case, the FV was shunted first to preserve outflow and mitigate the risk of arterial shunt thrombosis. In complex trauma and hemodynamic compromise, when immediate repair may not be possible, current recommendations support venous shunting as an alternative to routine ligation.11

Conduit selection for venous reconstruction depends on appropriate size matching to the outflow vessel.1 The CFV averages 10.6 mm in diameter and is typically larger in men (averaging 12.9 mm).12,13 The proximal FV averages 7.1 mm in diameter.13 In this case, only 3 × 5mm shunts were available, which posed a limitation given the size of the injured FVs. Although the shunt remained patent, it provided inadequate outflow, contributing to venous congestion and limb swelling. Based on this experience, we recommend using larger-caliber devices, such as 16F to 24F thoracostomy tubes, for temporary shunting of large peripheral veins to ensure sufficient outflow.14 Subramanian et al15 successfully reported using smaller 14F Argyle shunts for FV injuries in a series of patients. Although suboptimal, such small diameter shunting conduit remains a viable salvage option when limb loss is the likely alternative, particularly in young trauma patients.

At the time of definitive repair, autologous conduit was preferred over cryopreserved FV given the potentially contaminated field.16 Because the ipsilateral GSV was already transected proximally and would otherwise thrombose, it was harvested to fashion a conduit. However, there was a large size mismatch between the lumens of the CFV and GSV, so it was opened longitudinally and wrapped around a 32F chest tube to create a spiral vein graft approximately 11 mm in diameter. This technique has been described previously in the setting of oncological and trauma-related pelvic vessel reconstructions, where its flexibility in sizing and autologous nature make it suitable for complex venous repair.17 Recognizing early uncertainty regarding whether the available autologous vein would suffice for the entire length of the vein reconstruction, we simultaneously thawed a cryopreserved GSV graft as a contingency.

For trauma centers without access to cryopreserved grafts, autologous options such as contralateral GSV, DFV, or external jugular vein may be considered. However, in our case, these alternatives were not feasible owing to the need for additional dissection and exposure, which would have significantly prolonged operative time in a critically ill patient. Arterial reconstruction had to follow venous repair, and parallel dissection was not possible given that no additional surgical team or fellow was available. Furthermore, it is difficult to evaluate the quality and diameter of vein available for harvest in a patient with clamped vessels and ongoing hemorrhagic shock. Although the contralateral GSV could have served as an alternative to the cryopreserved conduit, it could not be reliably assessed or accessed.

Ultimately, sufficient autologous vein was available for venous repair, and rather than discarding the now-defrosted cryopreserved graft, it was used for arterial reconstruction. Use of cryopreserved conduit allowed arterial graft preparation to proceed in parallel with a time-consuming spiral venous reconstruction. Although a cryopreserved femoral artery may have been preferable for arterial repair, it would have required additional thawing time, something that could only begin after confirming the adequacy of autologous vein, thus delaying revascularization even further. The anastomotic stenoses observed at the 15-month follow-up were likely related to the use of cryopreserved saphenous vein. This approach demonstrates that combining sequential strategies with consideration of available resources can maximize operative efficiency, reinforcing the role of adaptability as a core strength of vascular surgery in managing complex, time-sensitive extremity trauma.

Conclusions

This case illustrates the strategic use of temporary shunting, size mismatch management, spiral vein grafting, and cryopreserved conduit in the staged management of complex femoral vessel trauma. In the setting of physiological instability and the operative constraints typical of damage control, resource-conscious decision-making enabled timely revascularization and successful limb salvage.
