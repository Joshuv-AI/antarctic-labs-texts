Social Isolation and Incidence of Chest Pain and Mortality in Older Adults of the United States Population: A Cross‐Sectional Study From NHANES 2001–2018
Authors: Xiaozhe Zheng, Shaorui Niu, Jiayi liang, Andi Lu, Yuhao Zhou, Kangming Chen, Xingwang Huang, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundOur study aimed to investigate the association between social isolation and chest pain, and to evaluate the relationship between social isolation and all‐cause mortality stratified by the presence or absence of chest pain among participants.MethodsThis study incorporated data from the 2001–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from the whole of the United States, adopting a hybrid design integrating cross‐sectional and longitudinal cohort methodologies. Weighted logistic regression modeling was employed to examine the association between social isolation and chest pain. Additionally, a weighted Cox proportional hazards model was utilized to estimate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for all‐cause mortality.ResultsAnalysis revealed a notable association between social isolation scores and chest pain. Following covariate adjustment, individuals with social isolation scores of 3 or 4 exhibited a 52% higher likelihood of reporting chest pain compared to the reference category. Compared with the socially integrated group, the socially isolated group had an approximately 31% increased risk of chest pain. Multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that, after adjusting for all covariates, the risk‐adjusted ratio (HR) for death was 2.17 for participants with social isolation scores of 3 to 4 and 2.06 for those with chest pain. Compared with the socially integrated group, the risk‐adjusted ratio for death in the socially isolated group was 1.65 among all participants and 1.48 among those with chest pain and 2.43 in the group without chest pain. High social isolation was associated with an increased risk of death, regardless of the presence of chest pain.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrate a significant association between social isolation and increased prevalence of chest pain, identifying specific high‐risk subgroups. Importantly, social isolation was linked to elevated all‐cause mortality in both individuals with and without chest pain, highlighting its independent prognostic significance across these clinical subgroups.

Introduction

Chest pain ranks among the most prevalent causes prompting patients to seek medical attention in the emergency department [1]. It comes second only to trauma, accounting for over 4 million emergency department visits annually [2]. Most patients with chest pain require further evaluation and treatment [2, 3, 4], and a missed diagnosis of chest pain can have serious consequences [5]. Chest pain is a highly prevalent health issue in the US population, with a lifetime prevalence rate spanning from 20% to 40% [6]. A significant number of individuals present to hospitals with chest pain and suspected myocardial infarction. Notably, chest pain has been identified as the predominant reason for hospital admissions in the United Kingdom, comprising approximately 5% of all emergency admissions during the 2017–2018 period [7]. The causes of chest pain are diverse and can range from relatively benign conditions to those that are potentially life‐threatening. These encompass various categories such as cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, and psychological factors contributing to chest pain [3, 8, 9, 10] And cardiovascular disease (CVD) being one of the most common and serious causes [11]. Chest pain can severely impact patients' quality of life and even pose a threat to their life and health [12, 13]. As a crucial early warning sign of CVD, pinpointing the risk factors associated with chest pain holds significant public health importance and clinical value.

A substantial body of literature demonstrates associations between psychological factors and various disease conditions, particularly life‐threatening illnesses [14]. Loneliness refers to the subjective sense of lacking meaningful social connections or a desired partner, whereas social isolation denotes an objective condition characterized by limited social interactions. Both conditions are linked to adverse health outcomes and increased mortality [15]. In the United States, approximately one‐third of adults aged 45 and above report experiencing loneliness, and nearly a quarter of those aged 65 and older are classified as socially isolated [16]. Existing research indicates that social isolation is linked to an elevated risk of cardiovascular events, including stroke, heart failure, coronary heart disease, and myocardial infarction [17, 18, 19, 20]. Moreover, significant associations have been observed between social isolation and both cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality, underscoring its role as a critical prognostic factor in cardiovascular health [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. However, the relationship between social isolation and chest pain, a common cardiovascular symptom, has not been reported in the literature. Whether social isolation has an impact on health through the early symptoms of chest pain has not been clearly established. Based on this research gap, this study aims to fill it.

Thus, this investigation sought to utilize data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine the association between social isolation and chest pain, while additionally assessing the potential link between social isolation and mortality outcomes in both individuals with and without chest pain.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

The NHANES is a large, systematic epidemiological survey sponsored by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Since 1960, NHANES has collected a wide range of health, nutrition, and lifestyle data through multistage sampling of a representative population in the United States. The survey covers the individual's physiological indicators (such as blood pressure, blood sugar, etc.), laboratory test results, dietary habits, disease history, and behavioral patterns. Data were obtained through interviews, physical examinations and laboratory tests to provide country‐level health assessment and risk factor analysis. NHANES data are widely used in public health, epidemiology and clinical research, and provide a solid scientific basis for exploring the epidemic trend, risk factors and health intervention strategies.

A total of 91,351 participants participated in the 2001–2018 NHANES cycle, and the remaining sample was analyzed by excluding participants with no social isolation data (marriage, living alone, two social activities), chest pain diagnosis data (16,837), follow‐up data, and covariate data (Figure 1).

Exposure and Outcome

The exposure data is social isolation. The social isolation indicator we chose was created by Nadia T. Saif and his team on the basis of the existing Social Isolation Index and first applied in their research [26, 27, 28], which measures social isolation as a structure and incorporates the existing NHANESA questions. Areas such as marital status [27, 29], living alone [30] and participation in social activities [27, 28, 30] are covered.

The measure of social isolation consisted of four variables: marital status, living alone, and two elements of social participation. Specific scoring criteria are as follows: In marital status, widowed, divorced, separated or unmarried people get 1 point; Of the total number of people in a household, only one person scores 1 for the person living alone; In Physical Functioning (PFQ), for the two activities of “going out shopping, watching movies or participating in sports activities” and “participating in social activities (visiting friends, attending clubs or meetings, attending parties)”, If the individual has “some difficulty”, “very difficulty”, “cannot do” or “does not participate” in these activities, one point is awarded for each. Social isolation scores ranged from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater social isolation.

In this study, social isolation scores were divided into two types of groupings. The first category classifies individuals with scores of 0–2 as the “socially integrated group”, and individuals with scores of 3–4 as the “socially isolated group”. This is consistent with previous work [31, 32, 33]. The second group grouped scores into 0, 1, 2, and 3–4, where the two groups were combined into the “most socially isolated group” due to the small number of individuals scoring 3 and 4. Therefore, social isolation groups were divided into four groups (30–31): 0, 1, 2, and 3–4.

Chest pain is a common symptom of CVD, and its occurrence is closely related to the severity of CVD. Previous studies have confirmed a link between social isolation and the onset and development of CVD. In order to more closely fit the purpose of this study, and to exclude chest pain caused by non‐cardiovascular disease to the extent possible, we selected chest pain with a duration greater than 30 min as the primary outcome variable. Specifically, the presence of chest pain was determined by answering the following questions: “Have {you/she/he} ever had a severe pain across the front of {your/her/his} chest lasting for half an hour or more?” If the answer is “yes”, chest pain is considered present. The selection of this criterion helped ensure that the study focused on the type of chest pain associated with CVD, maximizing the specificity of the outcome variable and the scientific nature of the study. Through December 31, 2019, before the national death index of public access (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/data-linkage/mortality.htm) to determine the end of the participants and the subsequent information, we use “MORTSTAT” variable as a state of death, The “PERMTH_EXM” variable was used as the follow‐up time.

Covariate Data

Basic information about all participants, including age, sex, race, education level, household poverty‐to‐income ratio, and smoking status. All were collected by questionnaire survey. Body mass index (BMI) is calculated by dividing weight in kg by height in m² [34]. Participants provide serum samples when they visit the mobile screening center, and laboratory test results are analyzed by the National Centre for Environmental Health and stored in appropriate freezing conditions until the time of shipment. The diagnosis of CVD was based on self‐reports and previous medical records. Congestive heart failure was diagnosed when participants answered “yes” to the following question: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have congestive heart failure?” Similarly, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and stroke were diagnosed according to the following question: Coronary heart disease: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have coronary heart disease?” Angina: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you have angina?” Heart attack: “Ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had a heart attack?” Stroke: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had a stroke?” Participants were considered to have CVD if they met any of the above diagnostic criteria for CVD. Hypertension is diagnosed if one of the following criteria is met: (1) Systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg; (2) Have been told by a doctor or other health professional that they have high blood pressure; (3) Currently taking antihypertensive drugs [35]. How participants met any of the following criteria to be diagnosed with diabetes: (1) Hemoglobin A1C concentration ≥ 6.5%; (2) Fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL; (3) Self‐reported use of antidiabetic drugs; (4) Doctor informed of diagnosis of diabetes [36].

Statistical Analysis

This study was a cross‐sectional design to investigate the relationship between social isolation and chest pain. Data came from the NHANES from 2001 to 2018, and the study included adults ≥ 18 years of age and participants with complete data on social isolation and chest pain. Because NHANES uses a complex stratified sampling design, all analyses are sample weighted to ensure the representativeness of the results.

The baseline features were measured by descriptive statistics, continuous variables were represented by mean ± standard deviation (SD), and inter‐group comparisons were performed by t‐test or Wilcoxon rank sum test, depending on the circumstances. Categorical variables were represented by weighted counts, and differences between groups were compared by Person Chi‐square test.

The association between social isolation and chest pain was assessed using weighted univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, with results presented as odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Multivariate regression analysis was divided into three models: Model 1 was the baseline model without adjusting for confounding factors; Model 2 adjusts for gender, age, and race/ethnicity; Model 3 further adjusted for education level, household income ratio (PIR), smoking, and cardiovascular disease‐related factors (hypertension, heart failure, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction, stroke, diabetes). To investigate the relationship between social isolation and chest pain in different subgroups, subgroup analysis and interaction analysis were performed, focusing on the effects of age, sex, family PIR, BMI, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease. For participants with or without chest pain, the relationship between social isolation and mortality was evaluated using weighted univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards models in the form of hazard ratios (HR) and their 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Kaplan‐Meier curve was used to show mortality in different socially isolated groups, and the logrank test was used to compare between groups.

Our study utilized data from the publicly accessible NHANES database, which was approved by the institutional ethics committee, with all participants providing written informed consent prior to inclusion. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2), incorporating the complex weighted design of NHANES to ensure representativeness of the national population.

Correlation Between Social Isolation Score and Chest Pain

Analysis across all participants revealed a significant association between social isolation scores and chest pain. Following adjustment for potential confounders, individuals with social isolation scores of 3 or 4 demonstrated a 52% higher likelihood of reporting chest pain compared to the reference category (score = 0), with statistical significance (p < 0.001). Moreover, compared with the socially integrated group (social isolation scores of 0, 1, and 2), the socially isolated group (social isolation scores of 3 and 4) had an approximately 31% increased risk of chest pain (p = 0.011). Correlation Between Social Isolation Score and All‐Cause Mortality

Of all the participants, 1623 (29.68%) died. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve showed that the higher the social isolation score, regardless of the presence of chest pain, the higher the all‐cause mortality for all participants (Figure 4). Multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that after covariate adjustment, participants with social isolation scores of 3–4 exhibited a significantly elevated hazard ratio (HR) for all‐cause mortality compared to the reference group (score = 0), with an adjusted HR of 2.17 (95% CI: 1.76–2.67) (Table 3). Stratified analysis revealed that this association persisted in both subgroups: among individuals with chest pain, the HR was 2.06 (95% CI: 1.35–3.16), and among those without chest pain, the HR was 2.27 (95% CI: 1.80–2.82), highlighting the independent prognostic impact of social isolation across clinical subgroups. Compared with the socially integrated group (social isolation score 0–2), the risk‐adjusted ratio for death in the socially isolated group (social isolation score 3–4) was 1.65 (95% CI: 1.41–1.94) among all participants and 1.48 (95% CI: 1.48) among those with chest pain. High social isolation was associated with an increased risk of death, regardless of the presence of chest pain.

Discussion

Our investigation leveraged 2001–2018 NHANES data to elucidate the relationship between social isolation scores and chest pain symptoms, as well as the association between social isolation and all‐cause mortality in participants with or without chest pain. Findings revealed that higher social isolation scores were significantly associated with an increased risk of reporting chest pain. Notably, the impact of social isolation on mortality was not confined to individuals with chest pain but was also evident in those without, underscoring its broad prognostic relevance across clinical subgroups.

Growing evidence indicates that social isolation, beyond its established role in mental health disparities, is closely linked to the pathogenesis and progression of multiple CVDs. Many studies have reported that social isolation is closely associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular events [17, 18]. Numerous investigations based on the UK Biobank have demonstrated that social isolation is associated with an elevated risk of CVD [37, 38]. Using the Mendelian randomization method, it was shown that genetic prediction of social isolation and loneliness susceptibility was significantly associated with increased risk of angina pectoris [39]. Collectively, these findings from diverse methodologies reinforce social isolation as a robust risk factor for adverse cardiovascular outcomes, spanning acute events, chronic complications, and genetically influenced clinical phenotypes.

Chest pain is a common symptom of CVD, yet existing research on the relationship between social isolation and chest pain remains scarce. Our study has demonstrated a significant correlation between social isolation scores and the occurrence of chest pain. Specifically, individuals with the highest social isolation scores were found to be at a substantially greater risk of experiencing chest pain when compared to those with the lowest social isolation scores. Comparable findings were also observed in another group within our study.

Subgroup analyses revealed that elevated social isolation was significantly associated with increased chest pain risk among women, individuals under 65 years, those with PIR values between 1.30 and 3.50, and participants without preexisting CVD. Notably, CVD status significantly modified the association between social isolation and chest pain (interaction p < 0.05). These findings align with prior research demonstrating that women are more vulnerable to emotional stressors linked to social isolation, potentially amplifying physiological responses that contribute to chest pain pathogenesis.

This phenomenon is underpinned by multiple biological and neuroendocrine mechanisms. First, sex‐specific differences in hormonal milieu, particularly involving estrogen and cortisol regulation, may modulate physiological responses to social stressors. Second, structural and functional disparities in key brain regions associated with emotional processing, such as the amygdala and prefrontal cortex, have been documented between sexes, influencing stress perception and cardiovascular reactivity [40, 41]. Furthermore, women exhibit distinct stress response profiles, including heightened hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis reactivity and autonomic nervous system activation, when exposed to social isolation [42]. Collectively, these sex‐related differences likely amplify the association between social isolation and chest pain in female populations, underscoring the need for gender‐specific considerations in understanding psychosocial contributors to cardiovascular symptoms.

Moreover, women often exhibit more subtle early manifestations of CVD, with atypical presentations of chest pain that may be underrecognized or misattributed [43, 44]. This clinical nuance may amplify the observed association between social isolation and chest pain in female populations. In our study, participants were at least 40 years old a demographic typically experiencing heightened social stressors and more dynamic social networks compared to older age groups [45]. Social stress and unstable social networks, two interrelated factors prevalent in midlife, often interact synergistically to exacerbate psychological and physiological vulnerabilities [46, 47]. As a consequence, this age group tends to be more psychologically and physiologically sensitive to the adverse impacts of social isolation. In contrast, while older age groups can also experience social isolation, age‐related adaptive changes in the cardiovascular system render them less sensitive to chest pain [48]. In subgroups with PIR values between 1.30 and 3.50 (a range often characterized by financial strain and marginal social support) both economic stressors and inadequate social resources are thought to exacerbate the effect of social isolation on chest pain [45, 46, 47]. A significant interaction was observed between preexisting CVD and social isolation in predicting chest pain. Among individuals with established CVD, chest pain incidence was more strongly linked to disease‐related mechanisms, with social isolation exerting a relatively modest incremental impact. In contrast, among those without CVD, social isolation emerged as a primary driver of chest pain, underscoring its role as a key psychosocial determinant in this subgroup.

Previous research has consistently linked social isolation to elevated mortality risk across diverse populations. While prior investigations have primarily examined associations with all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality, there is evidence that social isolation was significantly associated with increased all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality across both Black and White American subgroups. This finding underscores the universal prognostic significance of social isolation, transcending racial and ethnic boundaries, and aligns with growing evidence of its role as a fundamental determinant of population health [49]. Using data from NHANES III and data from the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing, we found that social isolation significantly predicted mortality [24, 50, 51]. A Finnish cohort study also showed that social isolation independently predicted mortality, even when controlling for loneliness and other demographic and clinical covariates [25]. Our study further establishes a robust association between social isolation and mortality. Importantly, subgroup analysis revealed that social isolation remained independently associated with increased mortality risk even among individuals without chest pain, highlighting the potential prognostic utility of social isolation assessments in predicting outcomes across non‐cardiovascular populations. These findings extend the clinical relevance of social isolation beyond cardiovascular contexts, underscoring its role as a transdiagnostic risk factor with broad implications for population health prognostication.

Emerging evidence indicates that social isolation influences chest pain susceptibility and mortality risk through interconnected psychophysiological pathways. Firstly, the neuroendocrine system plays a pivotal role. Socially isolated individuals frequently exhibit activation of the HPA axis [52, 53]. Prolonged activation of the HPA axis can lead to excessive cortisol secretion. Cortisol affects chest pain and cardiovascular health by inducing physiological changes such as hypertension, inflammatory responses, and altered immune function [52]. A sustained elevation in cortisol levels over an extended period can also diminish insulin sensitivity and induce insulin resistance. Socially isolated individuals experience chronic stress over a long period of time, which further leads to decreased function of glucocorticoid receptors and reduced efficiency of glucocorticoid signal transduction [54]. This process may lead to excessive inflammation and overactivation of corticotropin‐releasing hormone and sympathetic nervous system (SNS) pathways [55], contributing to chest pain and even death. Secondly, social isolation influences health outcomes by disrupting autonomic nervous system homeostasis. Emerging evidence indicates that positive social engagement promotes an elevation in high‐frequency heart rate variability (HF‐HRV), a key biomarker of parasympathetic nervous system activity and cardiovascular regulation [56]. A prospective cohort study involving 2066 British male civil servants demonstrated that lower social integration was significantly associated with reduced HF‐HRV [57]. Lower HF‐HRV was strongly associated with cognitive decline, depression, CVD, and increased all‐cause mortality [56, 58, 59, 60]. Moreover, social isolation is linked to a chronic low‐grade inflammatory state, a key pathological pathway underlying its adverse health effects. Multiple epidemiological studies have demonstrated that social disconnection is associated with elevated circulating levels of proinflammatory biomarkers, including C‐reactive protein and fibrinogen [61, 62, 63]. Chronic low‐grade inflammation, a hallmark of social isolation, may heighten chest pain risk through vascular endothelial dysfunction and atherosclerotic progression. Additionally, social isolation is closely associated with deleterious lifestyle behaviors, including reduced physical activity, poor sleep quality, and tobacco use, modifiable risk factors with well‐documented links to cardiovascular morbidity [61, 64, 65]. Preclinical studies in rodent models have corroborated these associations: male rodents exposed to social isolation exhibit significant declines in physical activity and disrupted sleep architecture, characterized by reduced slow‐wave sleep duration [52]. These changes further heighten the risk of developing CVD such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. Consequently, these health issues contribute to the development of chest pain.

This study's strengths include its utilization of large‐scale cross‐sectional data from the NHANES database, which ensures strong representativeness and broad generalizability across diverse populations. Through rigorous subgroup analyses, the research systematically explored potential heterogeneity in the association between social isolation and chest pain, thereby enhancing the depth and applicability of its findings. These results align with prior evidence demonstrating that social isolation may elevate CVD risk, consistent with numerous studies linking social disconnection to increased CVD incidence. By integrating robust data sources with nuanced subgroup examinations, the study not only reinforces the established role of social isolation in cardiovascular pathogenesis but also provides novel insights into its differential effects across population subgroups, enriching the translational relevance of its conclusions. This study further demonstrates that social isolation extends beyond its established association with CVD incidence to exert a significant influence on the development of chest pain. Through the discovery of the relationship between social isolation and chest pain, we offer novel evidence for comprehending the role of social isolation in the pathogenesis of CVD. Proactive identification of social isolation, particularly in high‐risk subgroups, and the implementation of targeted interventions may represent a viable strategy to mitigate chest pain prevalence and improve related health outcomes.

Some important limitations of this study merit consideration. The cross‐sectional design employed herein, while providing valuable insights into associations, does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships between social isolation and chest pain. Secondly, while we have adjusted for possible confounding factors, there may still be potential confounding factors that are not controlled for, affecting the accuracy of the results. Thirdly, although the social isolation index we used is based on validated measures, it does not cover all key aspects of social isolation, which can lead to exposure classification errors. The upper threshold of social isolation defined by our index may reflect moderate rather than severe social disconnection when compared to more comprehensive, multidimensional measurement scales. Fourthly, we mainly explored the relationship between social isolation and the duration of chest pain exceeding 30 min. Due to the inherent limitations of the NHANES database questionnaire, it is difficult to distinguish the cause and nature of chest pain, although we believe that chest pain lasting longer than 30 min may be more closely associated with cardiogenic chest pain. In the future, the impacts of social isolation on different subtypes of chest pain can be further investigated. Fifthly, because the NHANES database specifically collects information about the U.S. population, our study focused primarily on the U.S. population and may not account for externalities and potential differences between different populations. Moreover, due to data limitations, loneliness was not incorporated into our analysis. The last limitation is that the conceptual framework wherein CVD, rather than social isolation is seen as a moderator. It should be emphasized that this study focused exclusively on the objective dimension of social isolation, without incorporating loneliness or other psychological variables. While this design facilitates isolation of the independent effects of structural social deficits, future research should integrate both objective and subjective measures to comprehensively elucidate the complex relationships between social factors and health outcomes.

Conclusion

This study revealed that social isolation was strongly linked to a higher prevalence of chest pain and pinpointed specific high‐risk subgroups. Importantly, social isolation was associated with increased all‐cause mortality not only among individuals with chest pain but also among those without such symptoms, indicating its role as a generalized risk factor across diverse populations.
