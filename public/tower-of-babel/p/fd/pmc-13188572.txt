Performance of FTIR-Based IR Biotyper for Identification and Strain Discrimination of Cryptococcus Species
Authors: Eun Jeong Won, Yukyung Jeon, Tae Yeul Kim, Yong-Sun Bahn, Heungsup Sung, Mi-Na Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe IR Biotyper is a commercialized real-time typing tool based on a Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy system. To date, it has not been evaluated for use with pathogenic Cryptococcus species complexes. This study aimed to assess the utility of the IR Biotyper for strain typing of pathogenic Cryptococcus species isolated from both environmental and clinical sources. We analyzed 51 isolates of Cryptococcus species, including clinical samples from two university hospitals, environmental isolates, and reference strains. Using a modified protocol optimized for Cryptococcus, we generated a total of 765 spectra that demonstrated high intra-isolate consistency. When the spectrum of Cryptococcus neoformans isolates was analyzed, distinct clusters were formed, clearly separating them from Cryptococcus species other than C. neoformans (Cryptococcus gattii, Cryptococcus deuterogattii, and Cryptococcus deneoformans). However, spectra from C. deuterogattii and C. gattii overlapped, indicating their close relatedness. Spectral discrimination was not clear between environmental and clinical isolates of C. neoformans. A dendrogram analysis grouped the 51 isolates into 10 clusters at a cutoff value of 6.245, which could discriminate four sequence types of C. neoformans. The IR Biotyper is useful for the identification and strain-level discrimination of C. neoformans, but its resolution is insufficient for reliable discrimination within the C. gattii complex. This initial study demonstrates further validation with larger, more diverse training sets is promising.

Introduction

The genus Cryptococcus comprises haploid, encapsulated yeasts within the division Basidiomycota, some of which can cause life-threatening infections in immunocompromised individuals [1]. Cryptococcus neoformans (previously C. neoformans var. grubii), which is ranked as the top fungal pathogen on the WHO fungal priority pathogens list, is a globally distributed opportunistic pathogen that causes life-threatening fungal meningoencephalitis [2]. Phylogenetic analyses using multilocus sequence typing (MLST) or whole-genome sequencing (WGS) are typically required to assess whether different sequence types correlate with distinct virulence profiles or antifungal susceptibility patterns. However, these approaches are both time-consuming and costly. To address this limitation, phenotypic methods based on Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) spectroscopy have been introduced as alternative typing strategies [3]. FT-IR is a technique based on the absorption of infrared radiation by the sample analyzed, and it can characterize the chemical composition of a complex sample [4]. The different components of the microorganism cell (proteins, fatty acids, polysaccharides, etc.) absorb light and produce specific absorption profiles. Previously, it faced clinical adoption hurdel due to lack of standardization of sample preparation preventing spectral variability [5]. In 2017, the IR Biotyper (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany), an automated FT-IR-based instrument, was commercialized as a tool for microbial identification and typing [6]. The IR Biotyper has been applied to a range of microorganisms, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Enterobacter cloacae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Acinetobacter baumannii [7–12] and Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococcus species [7,13–15], Candida parapsilosis [16], and Candida auris [17,18], respectively. Although Lemmer et al. suggested the utility of FT-IR spectroscopy distinguishing C. neoformans and Cryptococcus gattii [19], it is still lacking data describing the performance of the IR Biotyper for Cryptococcus species. In this study, we evaluated the IR Biotyper for species-level identification and strain typing of Cryptococcus isolates obtained from both clinical and environmental samples.

Materials and methods

Collection of the isolates, species identification and MLST typing

A total of 51 Cryptococcus isolates were evaluated using the IR-Biotyper, comprising 40 clinical isolates including 38 C. neoformans and 2 Cryptococcus deuterogattii, 6 environmental C. neoformans isolates, and 5 reference strains (2 C. gattii, 2 Cryptococcus deneoformans, and 1 C. neoformans). All clinical isolated were collected between 2020 and 2023 from two university hospitals in Korea, which have 2137 and 2764 beds, respectively. The only the first isolate obtained from each patient was included in this study. Species identification was performed using the MALDI Biotyper sirius (Bruker Daltonics GmbH, Bremen, Germany). Internal transcribed spacer (ITS) and D1/D2 sequencing were performed to distinguish C. neoformans from C. gattii. All sequence types (STs) were determined according to the ISHAM consensus MLST scheme for the Cryptococcus species complexes (http://mlst.mycologylab.org/) [20].

IR-biotyper assay for Cryptococcus species

For IR Biotyper (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) analysis, all isolates were grown at 30 °C on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) plates for 48 h. With a slight modification to previous protocol for Candida species [16,17], two heavily inoculated 1 µL loops of yeast colonies were suspended in 50 µL of 70% (v/v) ethanol in a suspension vial, vortexed, and mixed with 50 µL of deionized water. A 10 µL aliquot of each suspension was spotted onto a silicon sample plate. For technical replicates, five spots were prepared per isolate, and biological replicates were obtained on three different days with secondary subculture from the same thawed stock on consecutive three days. Each run included the IR Test Standard for quality control, as recommended by the manufacturer.

Data analysis and statistics

After measurement, distance between each IR spectra was analyzed within the same isolate and between different isolates, respectively. Differences between mean distances of each spectra were evaluated for statistical significance using various tests, including Kruskal–Wallis test and Mann–Whitney test. Data were analyzed with GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). p < .05 was considered a statistically significant difference. Then, IR spectra were analyzed by hierarchical clustering for dendrogram generation. Primarily, strain typing was performed using the splicing method with a default wavenumber range of 1300–800 cm−1. In addition, the amide region (1800–1500 cm−1) and CH region (3000–2800 cm−1) were analyzed for further clarification. Clustering was carried out using Euclidean distance and the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean algorithm. Spectral acquisition and data processing were performed using OPUS v.8.2 and IR Biotyper Client v.4.0 software (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG). During OPUS analysis, available metadata such as source, species, and ST were included. For additional data interpretation, two- and three-dimensional scatter plots were generated by LDA.

Results and discussion

In total, 765 spectra were generated from these 51 isolates. For ensuring reproducibility of each replicates, we compared the intra-isolate distances and inter-isolate distances in clinical or environmental isolate groups (Table 1). Overall mean (SD) value of intra-isolate distance was 5.2 (1.22), and there was no statistical difference of mean value of intra-isolate distance between two groups (clinical isolates vs. environmental isolates, 5.1 vs. 6.1, p = .537 by Mann–Whitney statistic). Overall mean (SD) value of inter-isolate distance was 15.5 (5.0), and there was no significant statistical difference of mean value of inter-isolate distance between two groups (clinical isolates vs. environmental isolates, 14.0 vs. 16.5, p = .081 by Mann–Whitney statistic). Overall mean value of inter-isolate distances was significantly higher than those of intra-isolate distances (inter-isolate vs. intra-isolate, 15.5 vs. 5.2, p < .001 by Mann–Whitney statistic). When we compared the mean values of the distance among the three replicates, there were no statistical significance between each trial of replicates (1st vs 2nd vs. 3rd, 15.4 vs. 14.6 vs. 14.8, p = .260 by Kruskal–Wallis statistic). Therefore, IR-Biotyper could generate reproducible and discriminative spectra for each isolate.

To visualize species-level discrimination, Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) was performed. A scatter plot showed that a cluster of C. neoformans was distinguished from those of C. gattii, C. deuterogattii, and C. deneoformans (Figure 1). This ability of IR Biotyper differentiating C. neoformans from C. gattii complexes seemed to be similar to the performance of Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption Ionization–Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) system [21]. Both methods are based on phenotypic fingerprints, but differ in scope: the IR Biotyper generates comprehensive biochemical fingerprints, whereas MALDI-TOF MS focuses on protein mass spectra. On the other hand, C. deuterogattii and C. gattii isolates formed too closely related clusters to distinguish from each other, indicating relatively lower discriminative power of the IR-Biotyper within the C. gattii complex. Even with further analysis of the amide region (1800–1500 cm−1) and CH region (3000–2800 cm−1), the isolates of C. gattii complex were indistinguishable at the species level (Supplemental Figure 2). The previous MALDI-TOF MS study reported similar findings that species descrimination within the C. gattii complex was not clear unlike species resolution of the C. neoformans complex [21]. This might be partly explained based on biological differences between them such as capsule composition, melanization, or serotype variation. As previously reported, C. gattii isolates often produced capsules larger than those of C. neoformans isolates, resulting the failure of identification by MALDI-TOF MS [22]. In addition to capsule abundance, differences in capsule architecture, or glycan branching between C. neoformans and C. gattii complex isolates could drive separation along carbohydrate-related spectral regions [23]. However, within the C. gattii complex, C. gattii (VGI) and C. deuterogattii (VGII) are closely related species sharing a high degree of genetic similarity (>99.99% in some cases). Previously, a considerable amount of heterogeneity in capsule thickness and cell diameter were observed within the C. gattii complex [24]. Although we could not resolve ambiguity of spectra within C. gattii complex in this study, it can be helpful to implement protocols for thinning/removing the capsule at the pre-FTIR stage, or to test approaches to increasing the wave amplitude of inorganic compounds [25]. In addition, we found that environmental isolates were not significantly distinguishable from clinical isolates (Supplemental Figure 1). This lack of distinction may indicate an overlap or close relationship between the two populations, which has implications for understanding the transmission of cryptococcal infections and the environment reservoirs that contribute to disease in hospital and community setting.

A dendrogram and distance matrix derived from the IR Biotyper classified the 51 isolates into 10 groups at a cutoff value of 6.245 (Figure 2). Within C. neoformans, four sequence types (ST5, ST15, ST2, and ST707) were distinguished, in addition to the well-differentiated clusters of four Cryptococcus species (C. neoformans, C. gattii, C. deuterogattii, and C. deneoformans). Although we did not directly compare them with WGS data, this data suggested the potential of the IR Biotyper generating clustering results comparable to sequence typing for Cryptococcus species. Using a cutoff value of 6.245, the major sequence type in South Korea (ST5) was distinguished from the other sequence types (ST2, ST15, and ST707) [26]. The absence of standardized clustering cutoff values has been a common limitation across studies using the IR-Biotyper. For example, reported cutoffs include ≥0.9 for C. parapsilosis [16], 0.160 for C. auris [17], 0.106–0.111 for vancomycin resistant Enterococcus faecium [15], and a variable ranges (0.15–0.3) for K. pneumoniae according to different researchers [8,9]. Even allowing for species-specific variation, our cutoff value of 6.245 appears relatively high, suggesting that the IR-Biotyper may distinguish sub-strains with the same genotype or at a sub-species level. This may reflect the fact that our clinical isolates were collected over a 4-year period from two university hospitals and were unrelated to any our break. In addition to biological heterogeneity within Cryptococcus, replicate inflation, or potential overfitting during OPUS-based threshold computation might partly affect. Inclusion of additional environmental isolates or serial clinical isolates from individual patients could help clarify this observation.

Although the IR Biotyper has been introduced as a real-time strain typing tool in clinical laboratories, it has not previously been evaluated for Cryptococcus species. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report describing the performance of the IR Biotyper for pathogenic Cryptococcus species complex. Previous studies have shown that the IR Biotyper offers advantages for rapid screening and typing for various pathogens in hospital settings, with results concordant with WGS [7,15]. However, limitations have also been noted, particularly the influence of pre-analytical and pre-processing variables, along with the lack of standardized protocols [8,13]. FT-IR spectra can reflect minor variations in culture conditions, such as culture age, medium composition, pH, temperature, humidity, and storage conditions. Therefore, standardization of pre-processing steps, including culture medium used and incubation time, is essential to ensure consistent results. In this study, we tried to suggest a proper protocol with modifying the manufacturer’s protocol proposed to Candida species and obtained reproducible spectra for Cryptococcus species under optimized conditions.

There may be several limitations in this study. First of all, this is the proof-of-concept study, thus further validation of cut off value with a larger and diverse set of isolates would be necessary to establish as a diagnostic modality. During the validation step, the IR Biotyper of the Artificial Intelligence tools, such as Classifiers, can be helpful to practival use of identification of Cryptococcus species. Second, we could not include Cryptococcus bacillisporus (VGIII, AFLP5), Cryptococcus tetragattii (VGIV, AFLP7), and Cryptococcus decagattii (VGVI and VGIIIc, AFLP10).

In conclusion, the IR Biotyper, with a Cryptococcus-adapted protocol, produced reproducible spectra and it can be helpful for the identification and strain-level discrimination of C. neoformans. Yet, its resolution is insufficient for reliable discrimination within the C. gattii complex. Further validation with larger, diverse training sets would be necessary.
