Comparative machinability and neural network-driven optimization of AISI D2 steel using chamfered and conventional tooling
Authors: Muhammad Sana, Muhammad Umar Farooq, Saqib Anwar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dry turning of AISI D2 steel requires a balance between productivity, surface integrity, thermal loading, and energy demand. This study compares the machinability of a chamfered Xcel insert and a conventional carbide insert during dry turning of AISI D2 steel using a 24 full-factorial design with cutting speed (VCS: 100–150 m/min), feed rate (FR: 0.2–0.3 mm/rev), and depth of cut (DOC: 0.5–1.0 mm). Volumetric material removal rate (VMR), microhardness (MH), turning-zone temperature (TTZ), and power consumption (PC) were measured and analysed using ANOVA, ANN modelling, and NSGA-II optimization. The results showed that the Xcel insert consistently outperformed the conventional carbide insert within the investigated range. The highest measured VMR and MH were 1341.57 cm3 and 177.78 HV, respectively, whereas the lowest TTZ and PC were 272 °C and 420.28 W under conservative cutting conditions. ANOVA showed that FR was the dominant factor for VMR (27.69%), while VCS dominated MH (74.51%) and TTZ (49.36%); DOC was the strongest contributor to PC (29.55%). Artificial neural network (ANN) models were used as local-response surrogates, and Non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm (NSGA-II) identified an optimum at 149.84 m/min, 0.3 mm/rev, 0.997 mm, and Xcel insert. The study shows that the Xcel geometry is beneficial for increasing productivity in dry turning of AISI D2 without a disproportionate increase in thermal and energy penalties.

Introduction

Contemporary manufacturing innovation prioritizes enhanced surface topology, microstructural integrity, and mechanical performance optimization. Traditional processing of hardened alloys like AISI D2 steel necessitated sequential operations: preliminary machining, thermal hardening treatments, and abrasive finishing to attain required surface specifications1,2. The ecological burden of coolant-led grinding and conventional turning has motivated sustainable alternatives. Hard turning now offers a consolidated solution, eliminating finishing abrasion stages and cutting fluid dependency. This integrated approach demonstrates superior resource efficiency through reduced thermal distortion, shortened production cycles, and diminished environmental impact3,4. The machining process of turning plays a vital role in shaping materials into precise designs, especially when working with tough materials like AISI D2 steel. This steel, characterized by its high carbon and chromium content, stands out for its remarkable hardness, resistance to wear, and durability5,6. These qualities make it a preferred material for creating components such as molds, dies, punches, cutting tools, and shear blades, all of which require high precision and excellent surface finishes. Turning offers an efficient solution for meeting these stringent requirements, providing advantages over traditional grinding methods7,8. It allows for more adaptable tool use, customized cutting techniques, and reduced production times and expenses9. Specifically, in the case of AISI D2 steel, turning streamlines the process by eliminating the need for multiple finishing steps, ensuring the components’ reliability and robustness in demanding applications10,11. This approach supports industries like automotive, aerospace, and heavy machinery by meeting their exacting standards for accuracy and performance12.

Turning involves machining hardened materials, typically within the 30–45 HRC range, such as cast iron and tool steel, using a single-point cutting tool. AISI D2 steel, a high-carbon, high-chromium tool steel, is further strengthened by the addition of alloying elements like molybdenum and vanadium. Through heat treatment, AISI D2 steel achieves exceptional hardness, strength, and wear resistance13,14. Turning is increasingly favored over traditional cylindrical grinding due to its benefits, including faster MRR, shorter machining times, and greater adaptability15. Additionally, components machined through turning often exhibit a fatigue life twice as long as those with comparable finishes achieved through grinding16. Conducted without the use of coolant, this method is considered environmentally friendly, eliminating the complexities of managing and recycling cutting fluids17.

Dias et al.18 focused on assessing the performance of a PCBN tool when machining quenched and tempered AISI D2 steel under both continuous and interrupted cutting conditions during the finishing stage with dry machining. Key factors analyzed included surface irregularities metrics (Ra, Rt, and Rz), the wear patterns on the cutting tools, the mechanisms behind this wear, and the morphological characteristics of the resulting chips. The findings revealed that in continuous cutting, crater and flank wear were the most prominent issues, whereas interrupted cutting showed signs of tool chipping and severe failure. Kara et al.19 focused on hard turning of AISI D2 cold work tool steel that underwent deep cryogenic treatment followed by tempering, exploring its impact on surface finish and tool wear. Two types of cutting tools were tested during the experiments: an untreated alumina ceramic tool with an Al2O3 + TiC matrix (AB30) and a TiN-coated ceramic tool with the same matrix composition (AB2010). The findings revealed that the DCTT-36 sample provided the most favorable outcomes in terms of both Ra and tool wear. Agebo et al.20 engaged on the optimizing multiple responses during the turning of AISI D2 steel samples under both wet and cryogenic machining conditions. The authors employed a Taguchi L16 mixed design orthogonal array to analyze the effects of varying spindle speeds, FR, DOC, and types of cutting fluids. Validation tests showed that the errors in predicting MRR, Ra, and the Grey Relational Grade (GRG) were 4.33%, 9.09%, and 1.29%, respectively, compared to the experimental values.

Srivathsa et al.21 investigated the machining performance of AISI D2 steel using minimum quantity lubrication (MQL) enhanced with sub-micron sillimanite (Al2SiO5) particles combined with eco-friendly rice bran oil. Cutting zone temperatures reached 371.8 °C with SiO2, 352.4 °C with Al2O3, and 341 °C with Al2SiO5 as additives in the MQL system. The use of Al2SiO5 also resulted in the lowest maximum flank wear at 49.9 µm, which was significantly less compared to SiO2 and Al2O3 additives. Senthilkumar et al.22 focused on optimizing turning parameters for machining AISI D2 steel using both coated and uncoated cemented carbide cutting inserts. A hybrid multi-objective approach combining Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) and Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied. The output metrics, including tool wear (TW), Ra, and MRR, were analyzed using grey relational coefficients, leading to the calculation of a Multi-Response Performance Index (MRPI) through PCA. According to the ANOVA, the coated cutting insert contributed 72.87% to the MRPI, while the FR accounted for 17.15%. Khan et al.17 introduced two variations of Xcel inserts designed for chamfering during the turning of AISI D2 steel for key performance metrics, including tool life, wear mechanisms, MRR, PC, Ra, MH, and microstructure. The results indicated taht Xcel inserts demonstrated superior performance in MRR and achieved a Ra of 0.38 μm, which was either comparable to or better than that of conventional and wiper inserts.

Recent machining research has moved beyond single-response machinability studies toward structured design-of-experiments, sustainability-oriented assessment, and data-driven multi-objective optimization. Experimental studies on difficult-to-machine alloys have shown that eco-friendly process variants and carefully planned test matrices can strongly affect chip morphology, surface integrity, and machining efficiency, as seen in water-vapour-assisted and green machining studies on Inconel 718 and Ti-6Al-4 V (Pawade et al.23 ; Kadam and Pawade24,25; Kadam et al.26). In parallel, hybrid predictive frameworks based on ANN, GEP, MCDM, NSGA-II, TLBO, GA, and PSO are increasingly being used to model and optimize multi-response manufacturing behavior in WEDM, EDM, and sustainable turning (Biswas et al.27–30; Debnath et al.31; Sen et al.32). More recent studies have further extended this direction toward Industry 4.0-enabled wear monitoring, sustainability ranking, technoeconomic–environmental assessment, and computer-vision-assisted microstructural analytics (Sen et al.33–35). However, these advances are concentrated mainly on superalloys, non-traditional machining, or sustainability-assisted cutting environments; comparable integrated studies remain limited for dry hard turning of AISI D2 steel, particularly for direct comparison of advanced insert geometry against conventional tooling while jointly considering productivity, surface integrity, thermal load, and power demand.

Previous studies on hard turning of AISI D2 steel have mainly focused on individual machinability indicators, alternative lubrication strategies, or conventional multi-response optimization routes such as Taguchi-GRA/PCA. Our earlier study examined the wear behavior and machinability of modified Xcel insert designs during dry turning of D2 steel, but it did not explicitly model the turning-zone temperature nor formulate a simultaneous ANN–NSGA-II optimization of VMR, MH, TTZ, and PC for the Xcel versus conventional insert comparison. Therefore, the novelty of the present work is not the use of ANN or NSGA-II; rather, it lies in integrating: a direct insert-geometry comparison under the same dry turning window,simultaneous consideration of productivity, surface integrity, thermal load, and energy demand, andconfirmation experiments for the ANN-guided Pareto-optimal settings. Materials and methods

AISI D2 steel was selected because it is a widely used cold-work tool steel for dies, punches, molds, shear blades, and other wear-critical tooling components. Its high carbon and chromium contents produce a hard, carbide-rich microstructure that offers excellent wear resistance and dimensional stability in service, but the same characteristics also make it difficult to machine efficiently. The material used for the workpieces comprised two cylindrical bars of AISI D2 tool steel, each with a diameter of 150 mm and a length of 175 mm. This study employed two distinct Sandvik Coromant cutting inserts for the AISI D2 dry turning trials: a modified Xcel type (featuring a chamfered nose geometry) and a standard round-nosed carbide insert. The Xcel insert corresponded to Grade S05F with a rhombic 80° shape and 4.762 mm thickness. To further boost fracture toughness and prolong TLIFE at elevated VCS, this insert received an TiCrN + Al2O3 + TiN CVD coating and features a specific chamfer geometry (45° major cutting edge angle, 6° minor cutting edge angle, 1mm chamfer). The Xcel’s minimal chamfer dimensions and absence of a traditional nose radius contribute to reduced cutting forces, lower temperatures, diminished vibration, and consequently, improved surface finish. Initial machining trials on 30 HRC AISI D2 employed manufacturer-recommended parameters: VCS = 160 m/min, FR = 0.40 mm/rev, and DOC = 0.5 mm. These conditions yielded suboptimal results: Ra reached 3.10 µm, exceeding the acceptable threshold of 1.60 µm for hardened turning6, while TLIFE was limited to 3 min with flank wear measuring 364 µm. Catastrophic tool failure occurred via tip fracture after machining 147 mm of spiral length. Recognizing the direct correlation between FR and Ra, FR was reduced to 0.20 mm/rev while maintaining other parameters. This adjustment improved performance: flank wear decreased to 296 µm, Ra dropped to 1.90 µm (approaching acceptability), and TLIFE extended to 4 min. However, irregular groove formation on the tool tip prompted further refinement. Reducing FR by an additional 0.20 mm/rev and DOC by 0.50 mm achieved stable tool wear progression, a TLIFE of 6.80 min, and a significantly improved Ra of 0.90 µm.

This study used a 2^4 full-factorial design comprising four two-level factors: VCS (100, 150 m/min), FR (0.2, 0.3 mm/rev), DOC (0.5, 1.0 mm), and insert type (Xcel , conventional carbide), resulting in 16 experimental combinations. The factorial design was selected to screen the main effects and interaction tendencies across a tightly bounded industrially relevant operating window identified during preliminary trials. Because the design contained one observation per treatment combination, it should be interpreted as a structured comparative dataset rather than a large training dataset. Accordingly, the ANN models were used as local-response surrogates within the investigated factor space, and the conclusions are restricted to interpolation inside this design window. Future work will expand the dataset with replicated and intermediate-level runs to support higher-order modelling and stronger statistical generalization. Since the data is not large, cross validation is carried out by changing data splitting strategy to reflect that model truly learns rather than memorizes any single data-split. Machining trials employed a dry turning configuration on an Emcomat-17D horizontal precision lathe (5.3 kW spindle power, 3000 rpm maximum speed). Tool wear progression was monitored according to ISO-3685, terminating each test upon reaching either 300 µm flank wear or observable tooltip damage. Literature indicates wear thresholds for hardened steels vary considerably (110–400 µm)36,37, influenced by required Ra and integrity targets. While turning studies occasionally report wear limits up to 400 µm, this typically compromises surface quality (Ra > 1.60 µm). Tool life was terminated when average flank wear reached 300 µm or when visible edge damage occurred. This threshold was selected as a conservative compromise between productivity and surface integrity. Previous hard-turning literature reports end-of-life criteria over a broad range, typically depending on the work material, insert material, and required surface finish. For hardened tool steels, higher wear limits can still permit continued cutting, but they are frequently accompanied by deteriorated surface finish, higher thermal loading, and greater risk of white-layer formation. Therefore, a 300 µm criterion was adopted in the present study to avoid severe edge degradation while maintaining a finish-compatible dry-turning condition6. Surface microhardness was measured using a Vickers microhardness tester under a load of 100 gf and a dwell time of 5s38. Flank wear dimensions were quantified using a CE450DV coordinate measuring machine (CMM), capturing data at 70 × magnification and processed via Quadra-Check software. Worn insert degradation mechanisms underwent detailed characterization using an FEI Inspect S50 scanning electron microscope (SEM). Machining zone thermal profiles were acquired with a Testo 868 thermal imager, utilizing its integrated IFOV warning for automatic focus adjustment within 0.5 m. Temperature measurements along the workpiece length were recorded for both fresh and worn tool conditions during each run. Machine tool power consumption (PC) during cutting was measured directly at the supply using connected voltmeter and ammeter instrumentation. Total power for individual experiments was computed using Eq. 1, incorporating a power factor (pf = 0.74). The ANN models were developed to approximate the response surfaces of responses within the experimentally investigated domain. Inputs were VCS, FR, DOC, and insert type, and each output was modelled separately using a single-hidden-layer feed-forward backpropagation network. Before training, input and output variables were normalized to [0,1]. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training, validation, and testing subsets using a 70% and 15% each split and three fixed random seeds. The hidden-layer neuron count was varied from 3 to 10, and the learning rate was varied from 10–4 to 10–1. The hidden and output activation functions were default, and the networks were trained using the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. To reduce overfitting, model complexity was intentionally restricted, early stopping was activated using a validation-failure limit of six, and the final architecture for each response was selected based on validation/testing performance rather than training fit alone. In addition, because only 16 factorial runs (with repetitions) were available, the ANN should be interpreted as a local surrogate for optimization rather than a universal predictive model.

Volumetric material removal

VMR increased mainly with FR and DOC because both parameters directly increase the uncut chip area39 and therefore the volume of material removed per unit time as shown in Fig. 2a–d. VCS produced a secondary increase by raising the effective machining rate, but its benefit depended strongly on tool-condition stability. The Xcel insert maintained higher VMR than the conventional carbide insert because its chamfered edge resisted wear and preserved chip formation more effectively under the abrasive cutting conditions of AISI D2. In contrast, progressive edge degradation in the conventional insert increased ploughing and irregular chip formation, which reduced the effective material removal efficiency at the higher cutting settings.Fig. 2VMR’s 3D plots across parametric combinations: (a, c) Xcel insert; (b, d) Conventional carbide.

VMR’s 3D plots across parametric combinations: (a, c) Xcel insert; (b, d) Conventional carbide.

The Xcel configuration maintains near-theoretical removal efficiency because of its cutting edge geometry minimizes plowing effects that dissipate energy non-uniformly, while the TiCrN + Al2O3 + TiN coating’s thermal barrier properties preserve edge geometry against abrasive carbide particles in the workpiece microstructure40. Conventional carbide tools exhibit progressive edge recession under identical conditions, primarily through micro-chipping at the tool-workpiece interface caused by cyclic stress concentrations. Elevating FR to 0.3 mm/rev proportionally increases VMR through enhanced material engagement per revolution. Xcel inserts sustain VMR linearity through three material science advantages: the substrate provides exceptional fracture resistance against interrupted cutting forces; the proprietary chip breaker geometry regulates thermal flux distribution; and the coating’s stable adhesion prevents diffusion wear below critical temperatures41,42.

The VMR during AISI D2 turning exhibits direct proportionality to VCS, as evidenced in Fig. 2. At minimal VCS (100 m/min), constrained material shear rates inherently limit VMR for both tool types. Conversely, conventional carbide tools experience progressive flank wear through micro-fracture of tungsten carbide grains at the secondary shear zone. Elevating VCS to 150 m/min proportionally increases VMR by reducing the material’s shear strength through thermal softening43. Conventional carbides, however, demonstrate nonlinear VMR decay beyond 120 m/min due to three synergistic effects: binder phase depletion accelerates grain pull-out at elevated temperatures; crater wear develops from diffusion-mediated dissolution of WC in ferrous chips; and thermo-mechanical stress induces notch propagation at DOC boundaries. These degradation mechanisms cause intermittent chip adhesion, disrupting continuous material excision. At minimal DOC engagement (0.5 mm), constrained tool-workpiece interaction inherently limits VMR for both insert types44. This degradation manifests as inconsistent chip thickness and gradual VMR reduction over time. Elevating DOC to 1.0 mm amplifies VMR proportionally through increased material engagement, yet conventional carbides exhibit non-linear performance decay characterized by thermally-assisted plastic deformation at cutting edges and accelerated notch wear at depth transition boundaries45.

Comparative wear morphology analysis across parametric extremes (Fig. 3a–d) reveals distinct degradation pathways between insert types. Conservative machining regimes (diminished VCS, DOC, FR) generate attenuated thermo-mechanical loading that suppresses dislocation-based wear mechanisms in both tools (Fig. 3b, d). Conversely, Xcel inserts maintain structural integrity through Al2O3 passivation limiting oxidative diffusion, multi-directional stress redistribution via multi-layer coating architecture, and delayed crack nucleation from compressive residual stresses.Fig. 3Flank wear morphology evolution at parametric extremes: (a,c) Conservative machining regime; (b,d) Aggressive cutting conditions.

Microhardness

The MH evolution in machined AISI D2 surfaces demonstrates pronounced sensitivity to VCS, as established in Fig. 6a–d, with thermal-strain interactions governing subsurface alterations. At 100 m/min, limited frictional heating promotes dislocation-driven strengthening where Xcel inserts maintain homogeneous strain distribution through acute edge geometry, yielding consistent MH values. Conventional carbide tools conversely induce heterogeneous hardening patterns from progressive edge deterioration that disrupts uniform plastic deformation17. Elevating VCS to 150 m/min activates dual competing mechanisms: thermal softening potential is counteracted by chromium carbide dissolution during cooling cycles, and high VCS also reduces the workpiece and tool interaction19.Fig. 6MH’s 3D plots across parametric combinations: (a, c) Xcel insert; (b, d) Conventional carbide.

MH’s 3D plots across parametric combinations: (a, c) Xcel insert; (b, d) Conventional carbide.

MH distribution in machined AISI D2 surfaces demonstrates critical dependence on FR and insert configuration, where competing thermo-mechanical mechanisms govern subsurface alterations. At minimal FR (0.2 mm/rev), reduced specific cutting pressure limits dislocation density accumulation, yielding moderate MH elevation through constrained strain hardening. Xcel inserts achieve homogeneous hardness distribution via their acute cutting geometry which maintains consistent shear plane stability, effectively containing plastic deformation within a controlled subsurface layer17. MH changes in machined AISI D2 surfaces exhibit significant dependence on DOC and insert characteristics, governed by competing thermo-mechanical phenomena. At shallow DOC (0.5 mm) engagement, constrained material deformation generates limited dislocation density near the surface. This facilitates moderate hardening through restricted plastic work accumulation46.

TTZ evolution during AISI D2 machining exhibits pronounced dependence on FR and insert characteristics, governed by interfacial friction dynamics. At minimal FR (0.2 mm/rev), reduced chip thickness maintains predominantly boundary friction conditions, limiting frictional work conversion to thermal energy. The Xcel system exploits this regime through its multi-layer coating architecture, which suppresses adhesive junction formation while promoting stable tribofilm development at asperity contacts. Conventional carbide tools conversely experience progressive binder phase depletion at crystalline boundaries, accelerating microscale adhesive wear that elevates real contact area and associated frictional heating over time. Elevating FR to 0.3 mm/rev transitions interfacial conditions toward mixed seizure phenomena, substantially increasing plastic deformation work. At minimal DOC (0.5 mm) engagement, constrained material deformation maintains predominantly boundary friction conditions. The Xcel system leverages this regime through its engineered coating architecture, which disrupts adhesive junction formation while redirecting thermal conduction pathways away from critical edge regions. Elevating DOC (1.0 mm) transitions the interface toward seizure-dominated friction through intensified material confinement.

The conventional insert showed higher local temperatures, consistent with its greater wear, larger real contact area, and higher frictional heat generation. Power consumption

Multi-objective optimization through NSGA-II

The NSGA-II implements an evolutionary optimization framework through generational population refinement. Its computational architecture operates by hierarchically classifying solution candidates according to Pareto dominance principles, where non-dominated individuals constitute the primary optimality frontier. A spatial distribution metric quantifies solution density within objective hyperspace, preferentially conserving diverse candidates during selection. The reproductive phase combines rank-biased selection with stochastic variation operators (crossover and mutation) to generate descendant solutions. Subsequent generational transitions merge parental and offspring populations, implementing elitist retention of high-fidelity candidates while preserving solution diversity. This dual-strategy approach maintains equilibrium between convergence pressure and exploratory coverage within the design space.
