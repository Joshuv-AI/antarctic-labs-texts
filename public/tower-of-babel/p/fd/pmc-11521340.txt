Corticotropin-releasing hormone and vasopressin challenge affects metabolic, hematologic, and rumen fermentation parameters of growing beef steers
Authors: Meagan D Geeslin, John T Richeson, Jennifer A Hernandez Gifford, Sergio A Soto-Navarro, Nicole C Burdick Sanchez, Kendall L Samuelson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study was to evaluate the impacts of stress challenge duration on metabolic, hematologic, and rumen fermentation parameters of beef steers. Thirty steers (416 ± 19 kg) were used in a randomized complete block design with 2 blocks and 3 treatments. Treatments were intravenous injection of (1) saline at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h (n = 10; control); (2) corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and vasopressin (VP) at 0 h and saline at 24, 48, and 72 h (n = 10; acute); or (3) CRH and VP at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h (n = 10; chronic). Serum samples were collected at various time points for analysis of serum chemistry and nonesterified fatty acids (NEFA). Whole blood was collected for analysis of complete blood count, and ruminal fluid was collected via oral lavage to evaluate volatile fatty acid (VFA) composition. Serum cortisol was greater (treatment × hour; P ≤ 0.01) for cattle receiving acute and chronic than control at 1 h (P < 0.01) and greater for chronic than acute and control at 25, 26, 49, 50, 73, and 74 h (P < 0.01). Similarly, there was a treatment × hour interaction (P < 0.01) for serum glucose concentrations such that cattle receiving acute and chronic had greater glucose at 1 h than control (P < 0.01), and cattle receiving chronic had greater glucose at 25, 49, 73, and 74 h than acute and control (P ≤ 0.04). Serum insulin concentrations were greater (treatment × hour P < 0.01) in chronic and acute than control at 1 h (P < 0.01) and greater for chronic compared to acute and control at 25, 49, and 73 h (P ≤ 0.01). Serum NEFA tended (P = 0.09) to be greater in stressed cattle compared to control. There was a treatment × hour interaction (P = 0.003) for total white blood cell count such that chronic had greater concentration than control at 72 h (P < 0.01). Conversely, monocyte concentration was less (treatment × hour interaction P < 0.01) for chronic than acute and control at 144 h (P < 0.01) and eosinophil concentration was greater (treatment × hour interaction P = 0.02) for chronic than control steers at 48 h (P = 0.02) and greater for chronic than acute at 72 and 144 h (P ≤ 0.03). Minimal differences were observed in VFA concentrations with the exception of acetate (treatment × hour interaction P = 0.05). These results demonstrate that administration of CRH and VP affects complete blood count and serum chemistry, and longer duration of treatment exposure prolongs the physiological responses to a stress challenge.

Introduction

Stress occurs as a reaction to a threat or stimuli and involves a complex interaction between the hypothalamic pituitary adrenal (HPA) axis, the central nervous system, and the sympathetic nervous system to disrupt homeostasis (Minton, 1994). The biological changes that occur in response to a stressor can alter immune status and/or immune response, feed intake and metabolism, respiration rate, reproductive function, and endocrine secretion patterns (Burdick et al., 2011). Depending on the scenario, activation of the stress response can be either beneficial or detrimental and is highly influenced by the magnitude, frequency, and duration of stress as well as individual variations in temperament, sex, genetics, and a number of other factors (Hughes et al., 2014; Burdick Sanchez et al., 2016). For example, acute stress is experienced for only a short length of time (less than 24 h) and is thought to repartition nutrients, limit digestive function, and prime the immune system to support the flight-or-fight response in reaction to a stressor (Carroll and Burdick Sanchez, 2014). In contrast, chronic stress is experienced for a longer length of time (greater than 24 h) and stimulates a prolonged response that is more likely to negatively impact the animal long term, such as catabolism of body tissues and suppression of immune function (Carroll and Burdick Sanchez, 2014).

Cattle have the ability to cope with stressful situations as an evolutionary-derived response designed to promote survival (Dragos and Tanasescu, 2010). However, multiple stressors cattle experience during the transition from the ranch of origin to the feedlot during the marketing process can create a compounding effect that promotes chronic stress. Because there is an association between stress, nutrition, and animal health in cattle (Galyean et al., 1999), it is important to understand how the duration of stress affects the physiological response, as this may, in turn, influence cattle health and performance. Our hypothesis was that increasing the duration of time cattle are stressed (i.e., chronically stressed cattle) would compound the negative effects of stress as evidenced by differential changes in the biological markers of nutritional and immunological status. Therefore, the objective of this study was to determine the effect of an acute or chronic stress challenge on serum metabolites, complete blood count, and fermentation profiles of growing beef steers.

Materials and Methods

All experimental procedures used in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at West Texas A&M University (WTAMU, Canyon, TX; Approval #02-02-18).

Study Design and Experimental Treatments

For this study, 30 crossbred Angus steers from the WTAMU Nance Ranch were received at the WTAMU Research Feedlot. Before initiation of the study, steers were halter trained and acclimated to human contact, restraint, and individual animal isolation for 35 days. The study used a 28-day experimental period that consisted of 14 days for adaptation and a 14-day collection period. On day 15 and day 14, steers [n = 30; initial body weight (BW) = 416 ± 20 kg] were weighed and stratified by BW into 2 blocks to accommodate limited availability of individual feeding pens. Therefore, this study used a randomized complete block design consisting of 30 steers in 2 blocks with 3 treatments (10 animals per treatment).

Within each block, steers (n = 15) were randomly assigned to 1 of 3 treatments administered intravenously in the jugular vein from 0 to 72 h: 1) saline (3 mL) administered at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h (control); 2) 0.3 µg/kg of BW corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH; Bachem Ag, Bubendorf, Switzerland) and 1.0 µg/kg of BW arginine vasopressin (VP; Sigma-Aldrich, Co., St. Louis, MO) on h 0, followed by saline (3 mL) at 24, 48, and 72 h (acute); or 3) 0.3 µg/kg of BW of CRH and 1.0 µg/kg of BW VP at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h (chronic). Before administration each day, CRH and VP were mixed separately with saline into individual stock solutions, and each steer received a total injection volume of 2 to 3 mL based on BW to deliver the correct dose of each hormone. In contrast, cattle receiving control received 3 mL of saline only. Thus, the saline volume received by control steers was designed to represent the highest possible injection volume received by steers on either the acute or chronic treatments. The challenge dose and duration were selected based on previous research conducted by Buntyn et al. (2016), Hughes et al. (2017), and Seiver et al. (2019). Steers were individually housed, fed, and watered throughout the 28-d experimental period. Steers were fed equal parts of a common receiving diet (Table 1) twice daily at approximately 1.75% of BW from days −14 to 6. Steers were limit-fed during the collection period to minimize feed refusals and mimic the low dry matter intake (DMI) typically observed in stressed, newly received calves (Richeson et al., 2019). From days 7 to 14, feed delivery was increased by 0.227 kg/d on a DM basis if all feed was consumed from the previous day to achieve ad libitum intake during the postchallenge period. Water intake was determined using a digital display water meter (Fill-Rite 820, Fort Wayne, IN) fitted with a water hose on one end and an open and closed water valve on the opposite end to measure the amount of water supplied each day.

Sample Collection and Analysis

Blood was collected via jugular venipuncture into a 10-mL evacuated tube containing no additive (BD Vacutainer SST; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ) at 0, 1, 2, 24, 25, 26, 48, 49, 50, 72, 73, 74, 144, and 336 h respective to the initial administration of treatments for serum chemistry analysis. Samples were placed into an insulated cooler with ice packs, transported to the WTAMU Animal Health Laboratory, and centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 20 min at 20 °C. Once centrifugation was complete, serum was extracted and aliquoted into 3 labeled microtubes that were stored at −20 °C until further analysis. Before analysis, serum was thawed overnight in a laboratory refrigerator at 4 °C and an automated chemistry analyzer (Vet Scan VS2, Abaxis, Union City, CA) was used to measure serum chemistry variables including glucose, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), calcium (Ca), creatine, albumin, phosphorus (P), sodium (Na), potassium (K), and chloride (Cl) concentrations.

Serum insulin was analyzed on the same set of samples using a commercially available bovine-specific insulin enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Alpco Diagnostics, 80-INSBO-EO1, Salem, NH). The minimum detectible concentration was 0.1 ng/mL of insulin, and the intra- and interassay coefficients of variation were 6.6% and 5.0%, respectively. Serum NEFA concentrations were analyzed using a commercially available enzymatic assay kit (HR Series NEFA HR (2); Wako Diagnostics, Richmond, VA), as described by Buntyn et al. (2016). The minimum concentration detectable was 0.0014 mmol/L, and the intra- and interassay coefficients of variation were 5.6% and 9.5%, respectively. Serum cortisol concentrations were analyzed by the endocrinology laboratory at New Mexico State University (Las Cruces, NM) using radioimmunoassay as described by Yates et al. (2010). The intra- and interassay coefficients of variation were 2.9% and 5.6%, respectively.

Rectal temperature was recorded at 0, 1, 2, 24, 25, 26, 48, 49, 50, 72, 73, 74, 144, and 336 h using a digital thermometer (GLA Agriculture Electronics, San Luis Obispo, CA). Blood was also collected via jugular venipuncture into a 4-mL evacuated tube containing ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (BD Vacutainer K2EDTA; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ) at 0, 24, 48, 72, 144, and 336 h for complete blood count. After collection, samples were transported to the WTAMU Animal Health Laboratory and immediately analyzed using an automated hematology analyzer (Idexx, ProCyte Dx Hematology Analyzer, Westbrook, ME). Variables derived and analyzed from the complete blood count test were red blood cells (RBC), reticular disc width (RDW), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), and concentrations of platelets (PLT), white blood cells (WBC), neutrophils (NEU), lymphocytes (LYMPH), monocytes (MONO), eosinophils (EOS), basophils (BASO), and finally, neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (N:L).

Ruminal fluid samples were collected from each animal via oral lavage with a suction strainer and filter flask at 0, 72, 114, and 336 h. The collection apparatus was rinsed with deionized water between each steer, and the first 150 mL of ruminal fluid from each animal was discarded to limit saliva contamination. At sampling, 50 mL of ruminal fluid was collected from each steer and transferred to a beaker to measure pH using a handheld pH meter (VWR International, Radnor, PA). After recording pH, 100 mL of ruminal fluid was collected into individual conical tubes, temporarily placed on ice to halt fermentation, and frozen at −20 °C until analysis. Concentrations of ammonia in the ruminal fluid were analyzed as described by Broderick and Kang (1980), and volatile fatty acids (VFA) were analyzed in the ruminant nutrition laboratory at the University of Florida using gas chromatography (Goetsch and Galyean, 1983).

Before the morning feeding on day 0, steers were fed 400 g of Co-EDTA and 120 g of Yb-labeled diet. The labeled feeds were prepared according to the methods described by Waggoner et al. (2009a) and were provided to each animal approximately 1.5 h before the administration of the initial CRH and VP challenge. To determine both liquid and solid passage rates, 250 g of manure was collected from the rectum of each steer 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h after feeding the labeled diets. Concentrations of Co and Yb were determined using atomic absorption spectrometry in the ruminant nutrition laboratory at New Mexico State University. Liquid and solid passage rates were then determined using the one-compartment model explained by Ellis et al. (1982).

Statistical Analysis

The MIXED procedure of SAS 9.3 (SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC) with repeated measures was used to analyze blood parameters, rectal temperature, and fermentation end products. Individual steer was used as the experimental unit. Before analysis, the UNIVARIATE procedure was used to test distribution, and variables that were determined to have non-normal distribution (RBC, HGB, WBC, MONO, and NH3) were log2-transformed before statistical analysis. The model included the effects of treatment, hour, and treatment × hour, and both block and steer were used as random effects. For repeated measures analysis, the repeated statement was hour and the subject was steer within block. Akaike’s information criterion was used to determine the best-fit covariance structure for each dependent variable. For EOS, statistical differences were observed among treatments at hour 0. Therefore, hour 0 was removed from the analysis of these variables and used as a covariate for the remainder of the time points evaluated. A contrast statement was used to evaluate the effects of stress treatments (acute and chronic) compared to the no-stress treatment (control). Statistical significance was established if the P-value was ≤ 0.05, and a tendency was reported if the P-value was between 0.06 and 0.10.

Results and Discussion

Serum Chemistry

A treatment × hour interaction (P < 0.01; Fig. 1) was observed for cortisol where cattle receiving acute and chronic had greater cortisol concentrations at 1 h compared to control (P < 0.01), and chronic had greater serum cortisol concentrations than acute or control at 25, 26, 49, 50, 73, and 74 h (P < 0.01). Greater cortisol concentrations for acute and chronic validate that the HPA axis was indeed stimulated in response to the CRH and VP stress challenge. The serum cortisol concentration of steers receiving the chronic treatment continued to peak near concentrations of 40 ng/mL within 1 to 2 h of CRH and VP administration for all 4 days of the stress challenge, and indicates that the duration of challenge application did not attenuate the magnitude of the serum cortisol response observed over time. Injection of CRH, both with and without VP, has been reported to increase serum cortisol concentrations in cattle within 0.5 to 1 h of administration (Cooke and Bohnert, 2011; Hulbert et al., 2013; Buntyn et al., 2016). However, the return to baseline evidenced by samples collected before administration of the challenge each day (24, 28, and 72 h) suggests that cortisol concentrations were regulated by the negative feedback mechanism of the HPA axis and returned to homeostasis within 24 h. Similar to CRH and VP, using LPS to induce an inflammatory challenge also results in increased serum cortisol, although the magnitude of the response is greater, with peak cortisol values reported from 60 to 80 ng/mL (Waggoner et al., 2009b; Samuelson et al., 2014; Löest et al., 2018). Natural stress challenge models have also demonstrated increased serum cortisol at a concentration similar to CRH and VP challenge models (Carroll and Forsberg 2007; Falkenberg et al., 2013; Cooke et al., 2013).

Serum glucose concentrations were greater for steers administered acute and chronic compared to control 1 h post-challenge (P < 0.01), and greater for chronic steers at 25, 49, 73, and 74 h (P ≤ 0.04) compared to either acute or control steers (treatment × hour; P < 0.01; Fig 1). At 50 h, glucose concentration was greatest for chronic, intermediate for acute, and least for control (P ≤ 0.04). The glucose results agree with previous research conducted using CRH and VP (Carroll and Burdick Sanchez, 2014; Buntyn et al. 2016), dexamethasone (DEX; Burdick-Sanchez et al., 2020), LPS (Löest et al., 2018), and transport stress (Browning and Leite-Browning, 2013), which also increased serum glucose concentrations. This observation was expected because cortisol is a glucocorticoid that stimulates gluconeogenesis in the liver by mobilizing fat and protein into glycerol and amino acids that are converted to glucose (Long et al., 1940; Burdick Sanchez et al., 2016). The initial increase and subsequent return to baseline in serum glucose concentrations observed for acute steers suggests that stressed cattle respond quickly and can then regulate glucose to achieve homeostasis once relieved of acute stress. Similar to cortisol, the response observed for serum glucose concentration for chronic steers suggests that continued application of a stress challenge impacted the duration but not the magnitude of glucose mobilization, as the cattle responded comparably each day of the challenge.

A treatment × hour interaction (P < 0.01; Fig 1) was also observed for insulin such that chronic and acute steers had greater concentrations of insulin 1 h after the challenge compared to control steers (P < 0.01), and chronic had greater insulin than acute and control steers at 25, 49, 73, and 74 h (P ≤ 0.01). Similarly, greater serum insulin was reported in heifers 2 h after administration of CRH and VP (Buntyn et al., 2016). Insulin potentiates the use of glucose from either food consumed or body energy stores (Kovacs and Ojeda, 2011). Therefore, the increased serum glucose concentrations for stressed steers in the present study explain the resulting changes in insulin concentrations, as insulin mediates glucose uptake and promotes return to homeostasis (Ostrowska et al., 2015).

In the current study, NEFA concentrations tended (P = 0.09; Table 2) to be greater for steers receiving stress vs. control. Cortisol acts as a lipolytic agent during both stress and inflammation, which explains the increased serum NEFA concentrations for steers exposed to stress. Changes in NEFA in response to CRH and VP administration have not been extensively studied in cattle. However, Burdick-Sanchez et al. 2020 reported that cattle subjected to a chronic stress challenge via DEX had a greater change in NEFA compared to cattle that were acutely challenged or a nonchallenged control. This differs slightly from the results of the current study, where either duration of treatment (whether chronic or acute) resulted in increased NEFA. Greater serum NEFA concentrations have also been observed in cattle challenged with LPS (Lippolis et al., 2017) and transport stress (Marques et al., 2012). There was a treatment × hour interaction for BUN (P ≤ 0.01); however, no meaningful treatment differences were observed within an hour.

A treatment × hour interaction was observed for Ca (P < 0.01; Fig 2) where concentrations were least for acute, intermediate for chronic, and greatest for control steers at 1 h (P ≤ 0.02), less for acute and chronic steers than control at 26 h (P = 0.03), and greater for acute and control steers than chronic at 73 h (P ≤ 0.03). In dairy heifers, decreased blood Ca was observed within 30 min of injection of 250 IU of adrenocorticotropic hormone and continued to decrease throughout the 24-h collection period (Wegner and Stott, 1972). A tendency for a treatment × hour interaction (P = 0.08; Table 2) existed for albumin concentrations such that acute was greatest, chronic was intermediate, and control was least at 1 and 48 h. This disagrees with the 7% decrease in plasma albumin 24 h after transportation of young bulls reported by Buckham Sporer et al. (2008). Phosphorus (treatment × hour interaction; P < 0.01; Fig 2) was less for chronic than acute at 25 and 26 h (P < 0.01), less for chronic than control at 49 h (P = 0.03), and less for chronic than control and acute at 50, 73, and 74 h (P ≤ 0.03).

A treatment × hour interaction was also observed for Na (P < 0.01; Fig 2) where steers administered the acute treatment had less Na compared to control at 1 h (P < 0.01), and less than chronic and control at 2 h (P < 0.01). Sodium concentrations for chronic steers were greater at 48 h compared to control (P = 0.04), less than both acute and control at 49 h (P ≤ 0.02), and less than acute at 73 h (P = 0.04). Potassium concentrations (treatment × hour interaction; P < 0.01; Fig 2) were less for acute and chronic at 1, 2, and 24 h compared to control (P < 0.01), and less for chronic at 25, 26, 49, 50, 73, and 74 h compared to acute and control (P < 0.01). This agrees with results reported by Wegner and Stott (1972), where Na increased and K decreased 30 min after adrenocorticotropic hormone administration.

The decline in K and increase in Na in the current study indicate that these minerals respond to cortisol and likely the subsequent aldosterone effect. Aldosterone causes renal stimulation and excretion of K and absorption of Na (Wegner and Stott, 1972). In sheep infused with cortisol, researchers observed an increase in urine output and decreased urinary Na (Parker et al., 2003) following infusion. Parker et al. (2003) attributed the changes in urinary output to cortisol-inducing diuresis. In the current study, the diuretic effect of cortisol was probably attributed to the differences in electrolytes and minerals; however, urine output and composition were not quantified. A treatment × hour tendency (P = 0.09; Table 2) was also observed for Cl, where Cl concentrations tended to be greatest for chronic, intermediate for acute, and least for control at 144 and 336 h.

Rectal Temperature

There was no treatment × hour interaction (P = 0.98) or treatment effect (P = 0.18) for rectal temperature, but there was a tendency (P = 0.07; Table 3) for cattle receiving either stress treatment to have greater rectal temperatures compared to control. This agrees with previous research where heifers challenged using CRH and VP exhibited increased vaginal temperature within 25 min of administration (Buntyn et al., 2016) and CRH alone resulted in increased rectal temperatures in steers from 1 to 8 h after administration (Cooke and Bohnert, 2011). Increased body temperature, or febrile response, is typical after stress and immune challenges, but the magnitude can vary due to environmental factors, breed, and sex (Carroll and Burdick Sanchez, 2014). In the current study, a single rectal temperature value was collected at each time point instead of continuous measurement, which limited our ability to detect intermediate changes and temporal patterns in rectal temperature.

Complete Blood Count

Concentrations of RBC, HGB, HCT, MCV, MCHC, and MCH did not differ (P ≥ 0.13; Table 3) among treatments. There was a treatment × hour interaction for RDW (P < 0.01); however, evaluation of the within hour means revealed no difference among treatments. The lack of difference disagrees with Hudson et al. (2020), where exposing calves to procedures designed to mimic marketing and transportation stress greatly influenced erythrocyte characteristics. These authors attributed potential differences in red blood cell components to reduced water consumption and DMI. However, this did not occur in the current study, as DMI and water consumption did not differ among treatments (Table 4), which could explain the discrepancy in results. A treatment × hour interaction was observed for PLT (P = 0.02; Fig 3) where PLT was less for acute than control at 144 h (P = 0.02). In the study conducted by Hudson et al. (2020), PLT were reduced 3 d after a natural stress challenge, but no difference in PLT were observed on days 5, 7, 14, or 21, which differs from the current study where less PLT were observed on day 6.

There was a treatment × hour interaction for WBC (P < 0.01; Fig 3); WBC was greater for chronic than control 72 h after the initial CRH and VP treatment (P < 0.01). Greater WBC could indicate an activated immune system following administration of CRH and VP or alternatively, stress-associated effects on leukocyte translocation. Hughes et al. (2017) conducted a similar study where 0.5 mg/kg of BW of DEX was administered on day 0 (acute), day 3 through 0 (chronic), or no DEX (control) and observed increased WBC in chronic steers compared to control through day 1. In a CRH and VP challenge using the same dose as the current study, greater WBC were observed from 2 to 8 h after administration of the challenge (Buntyn et al., 2016). Increased WBC concentrations have also been reported in natural stress challenge models. For example, when steers were transported for 4 h, greater total WBC concentration was observed compared to nontransported cattle (Browning and Leite-Browning, 2013). The delayed increase in WBC concentrations reported in the present study may have occurred because of the time needed for the immune system to respond postchallenge. More pronounced differences in WBC in the current study may have been observed if blood samples were collected more frequently after challenge administration.

While there was a treatment × hour interaction for LYMPH (P = 0.04; data not shown in figure), no differences among treatment means within hour were noted. This is in contrast to where LYMPH increased 2 h post CRH and VP challenge and remained increased for 8 h and Hughes et al. (2017) where LYMPH decreased in chronic steers immediately after DEX administration compared to both acute and control treatments. In cattle transported for 4 h, there was a decrease in LYMPH count that returned to basal concentrations the day after transport (Browning and Leite-Browning, 2013), which could explain the lack of differences observed in the current study when samples were collected 24 h after the challenge.

There was also a treatment × hour interaction for MONO concentrations (P < 0.01; Fig 3) where MONO concentrations did not differ among treatment from 0 to 72 h (P ≥ 0.25) but were less in chronic steers compared to acute and control steers at 144 h (P < 0.01). The sharp decline in MONO from 72 to 144 h follows a pattern similar to WBC count, as MONO are a component of the measurement of total WBC concentrations. Kelly et al. (2016) indicated increased MONO concentrations were observed for up to 6.5 h after administration of CRH. In contrast, Hudson et al. (2020) reported MONO concentrations of cattle exposed to 3 d of marketing and transport stress were less than control cattle from 0 to 7 d after arrival. The long-term effects of a stress challenge on MONO in cattle have not been extensively evaluated, so the pattern observed in the current study is difficult to explain. However, the decline in MONO after ceasing the stress challenge in chronic cattle is interesting and suggests suppression of MONO associated with removal of a chronic stressor.

There was a treatment × hour interaction for EOS concentrations (P = 0.02; Fig. 3) where EOS concentrations were greater for chronic than control steers at 48 h (P = 0.02) and greater for chronic than acute at 72 and 144 h (P ≤ 0.03). In disagreement with these results, exposure to glucocorticoids has been shown to decrease EOS concentrations in previous research (Roth, 1985; Anderson et al., 1999; Hughes et al., 2017). However, a rebound effect where EOS initially decreases followed by a marked increase has also been observed in naturally stressed calves (Hudson et al. 2020). Remaining complete blood count variables such as NEU, BASO, and N:L were not different (P ≤ 0.20) among treatments. No difference in NEU disagrees with previous research, as neutrophilia is considered to be a classic response to stress (Hulbert et al., 2013; Carroll and Burdick Sanchez, 2014; Buntyn et al., 2016). However, in the majority of these studies NEU returned to baseline values within 8 h of the challenge.

Dry Matter Intake and Water Delivery

No differences were observed among treatments for DMI or water intake for any time period measured during the experiment (P ≥ 0.17; Table 4). Previous research suggests that exposure to stress and inflammation decreases DMI (Waggoner et al., 2009b; Lippolis et al., 2017). However, in an LPS challenge, Lippolis et al. (2017) also reported the negative effects on DMI are alleviated within 24 h. Therefore, the effects of stress on DMI are likely transient and may have not been large enough to generate differences across the 7-d observation period as the stress challenge was only applied on days 0, 1, 2, and 3. Furthermore, in the current study, steers were limit-fed to 1.75% of BW to limit-refusals and mimic DMI of newly received calves, thus limiting the potential for a difference in DMI. Because DMI can influence fermentation characteristics and passage rate (Owens et al., 1986) limit-feeding steers to a similar DMI allowed us to investigate the impacts of exogenous stress hormone administration on fermentation and digestion characteristics independent of changes in DMI, which has not been extensively explored in the literature.

Fermentation and Digestion Characteristics

Ruminal pH, VFA, and passage rate data are presented in Table 5. No differences (P ≥ 0.15) were observed for ruminal pH, total VFA, or molar proportions of propionate, isobutyrate, butyrate, isovalerate, and caprioc acid. However, acetate (mol/100 mol) was least for chronic, intermediate for control, and greatest for acute at 72 h (treatment × hour interaction; P = 0.05; Fig. 4). Similarly, a treatment × hour interaction was observed (P = 0.05) for the molar proportion of valerate, but no treatment differences within hour were noted. The acetate to propionate ratio (P = 0.90) and ruminal concentration of ammonia (P = 0.24) did not differ. In contrast, the liquid passage rate (%/h) was least (P = 0.05) for chronic, intermediate for acute, and greatest for control (5.7%, 6.4%, and 7.6% per h, respectively), while the solid passage rate did not differ (P = 0.70).

The enteric nervous system regulates the supply of stress hormones in the gastrointestinal tract (Verbrugghe et al, 2012). Introduction of stress hormones into the gut has the potential to alter microbial populations (Freestone and Lyte, 2009), and changes in rumen microbial growth, species, and population may impact the rumen environment and fermentation characteristics. Samuelson et al. (2016) infused increasing concentrations of cortisol into continuous culture fermenters in artificial saliva and found that increasing cortisol from 0 to 3 ng/mL increased total VFA and isobutyrate (mol/100 mol) while decreasing the molar proportion of butyrate. Löest et al. (2018) challenged growing calves with LPS and observed increased acetate and decreased butyrate concentrations after inflammation. In contrast, Lopez et al. (2017) did not observe a difference in total and individual VFA in LPS challenged cattle. It is important to note the complexity of the biological response to stress comprises a cascade of numerous endocrine secretions that may differentially influence gut function. Therefore, discrepancies between previously reported research and the results of the current study may be attributed to differences in the animal’s biological response to stress or could have occurred simply from individual animal variability in fermentation patterns.

Similarly, Samuelson et al. (2016) evaluated changes in fermentation patterns for a 3-d recovery period postinfusion of cortisol and no differences in fermentation indices existed. These results suggest exposure to stress may only have short-term influence on rumen fermentation patterns. Stress hormones such as cortisol can also impact overall digestive tract function. In the current study, CRH and VP reduced liquid passage rate, with the greatest impact observed in chronically stressed steers. To the author’s knowledge, the impacts of an exogenous stress hormone challenge on digestion and fermentation characteristics have not been reported in cattle. However, in agreement with these results, Waggoner et al. (2009b), Lippolis et al. (2017), and Loest et al. (2018) indicated that administration of LPS to growing cattle reduced liquid passage rates. Waggoner et al. (2009b) and Loest et al. (2018) reported cattle that received LPS had slower solid passage rate, but the changes in passage rate were concomitant with reduced DMI. Because DMI did not differ among treatments in the current study, this could explain no difference in solid passage rate as DMI is a physical regulator of rumen digestibility, motility, and rate of passage. In contrast, the reduced liquid passage rate implies that factors other than DMI could influence the liquid dilution rate in response to stress.

Conclusions

In this experiment, CRH and VP administration increased serum cortisol, which can be attributed to stimulation of the HPA axis and validates the stress challenge model. The cortisol, glucose, and insulin responses observed in steers receiving the chronic treatment suggests that the biological response to chronic stress is not attenuated across multiple challenge events. Additionally, the changes in serum chemistry values indicate that activation of the stress response primarily influences energy and fat metabolism. Decreased electrolyte concentrations in the serum of stress challenged steers could have resulted from increased urination, but urine output was not measured. Repeated administration of CRH and VP altered some blood leukocyte populations, specifically WBC, MONO, and EOS in chronically challenged steers. Further research comparing type, dose, and duration of stress hormones is warranted to improve our understanding of how stress impacts health and nutrition of cattle.
