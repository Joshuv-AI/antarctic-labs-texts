The relationship between insomnia and non-suicidal self-injury(NSSI) among college students: parallel mediation effect of subjective well-being and rumination
Authors: Hanqing Wang, Jiale Wang, Zhiwei Li, Hongli Sun, Tingwei Zhong, Jiale Zheng, Zihan Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the relationship between insomnia and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) among college students, and to examine the mediating roles of subjective well-being and rumination.MethodsA questionnaire survey was conducted with 667 university students from Jiangsu Province using the Athens Insomnia Scale, Adolescent Self-Injury Scale, World Health Organization Well-Being Scale, and Ruminative Responses Scale.ResultsThe prevalence of NSSI among university students was 34.03% (227/667). Insomnia was significantly positively correlated with rumination and NSSI(r = 0.686, 0.400, P < 0.001), and negatively correlated with subjective well-being (r =-0.331, P < 0.001). Rumination was significantly positively correlated with NSSI (r = 0.410, P < 0.001) and negatively correlated with subjective well-being (r =-0.271, P < 0.001). Subjective well-being was significantly negatively correlated with NSSI (r =-0.350, P < 0.001).ConclusionsInsomnia is a positive predictor of NSSI, and it indirectly influences NSSI through its effects on subjective well-being and rumination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03335-2.Keywords: Insomnia, Rumination, NSSI, Subjective well-being, College students

Introduction

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) refers to the intentional and repeated infliction of mild or moderate bodily harm on oneself without suicidal intent. It is socially unacceptable and characterized by repetitiveness, intentionality, and intermittency. Common forms of self-injury include cuts, strikes, scratches, needlesticks, abrasions, burns, and actions that interfere with wound healing, such as deliberately removing scabs [1, 2]. Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-V (DSM-5) classifies NSSI as a distinct category warranting further attention and research [3]. A review indicates that the lifetime prevalence of NSSI among Chinese adolescents is notably high, reaching 24.7% [4]. Individuals with long-term recurrent non-suicidal self-injurious behaviors are at increased risk for suicide [5], with a comorbidity rate of 7.7% [6]. Given its high prevalence and strong association with future suicidal behaviors, further research is essential to understand the underlying mechanisms of NSSI and develop effective interventions tailored to this population.

The issue of insomnia has become a global concern. Research indicates that the prevalence of insomnia symptoms among college students has been steadily increasing, with reported rates ranging from 13.0 to 30.3% [7]. College students experiencing sleep problems may face a variety of physical and psychological challenges. Rising academic and life pressures, coupled with excessive nocturnal use of electronic devices and irregular sleep patterns, contribute to an increased risk of insomnia [8]. Furthermore, sleep disturbances are linked to mental health disorders such as anxiety and depression [9]. The Sleep and Emotional Processing Theory [10] suggests that sleep plays a crucial role in emotional regulation by facilitating the adaptive processing of emotional experiences. Sleep deprivation, particularly a lack of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, weakens the prefrontal cortex’s regulatory control over the amygdala, leading to heightened emotional reactivity and impaired emotional regulation. This dysregulation may contribute to increased rumination, as individuals struggle to process negative emotions effectively and become trapped in repetitive negative thought patterns. Empirical research supports this framework, indicating that poor sleep quality is associated with heightened rumination and a reduced ability to disengage from distressing thoughts [11]. In addition to emotional dysregulation, neurobiological mechanisms such as hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation may further explain the link between insomnia and NSSI. Chronic sleep disturbances can lead to heightened HPA axis activity, resulting in increased cortisol secretion and prolonged physiological stress responses [12]. Dysregulated cortisol rhythms have been associated with greater emotional instability, impulsivity, and maladaptive coping behaviors, which are commonly observed in individuals who engage in NSSI [13]. Moreover, excessive HPA axis activation may weaken the body’s ability to regulate stress, further exacerbating emotional distress and increasing the likelihood of self-injurious behaviors [14]. Recent studies have shown that individuals with heightened HPA axis reactivity tend to experience greater difficulties in emotion regulation, which may contribute to their engagement in NSSI as a maladaptive strategy to relieve psychological distress [15]. Empirical studies provide support for these theoretical perspectives, indicating that chronically sleep-deprived adolescents may engage in NSSI as a way of coping with emotional distress [16, 17]. Given the interplay between sleep disturbances, emotional dysregulation, and stress-related physiological mechanisms, insomnia may serve as a critical risk factor for NSSI among college students.

Subjective well-being refers to an individual’s evaluation of their life satisfaction and emotional experiences [18], encompassing the overall balance of positive and negative emotions. According to self-determination theory, the fulfillment of basic needs—namely relatedness, competence, and autonomy—can significantly enhance subjective well-being [19]. Research has shown that college students with difficulties in emotion regulation are more likely to engage in NSSI, while effective emotion regulation can improve subjective well-being and reduce the likelihood of NSSI [20, 21]. Insomnia, characterized by inadequate sleep quality or duration, may disrupt the balance of sleep needs, thereby diminishing well-being [22]. According to the four-function model, NSSI serves as an emotion regulation mechanism, where individuals with low subjective well-being may resort to NSSI to alleviate negative emotional states [23]. Based on this, the present study proposes Hypothesis 1: subjective well-being mediates the relationship between insomnia and NSSI.

Rumination refers to the tendency of individuals to repeatedly focus on their negative emotions and the perceived negative consequences of related events, without actively attempting to resolve the underlying issues [24]. According to Response Style Theory, rumination is considered a coping mechanism for negative events; however, this approach has limitations [25]. It emphasizes passive thinking while lacking necessary action, which not only fails to effectively address the problem but may also exacerbate the individual’s situation. The relationship between insomnia and rumination is bidirectional. While excessive rumination can contribute to sleep disturbances, insomnia itself can further heighten rumination. The Cognitive Model of Insomnia [25] suggests that individuals with insomnia tend to engage in maladaptive cognitive processes, including excessive worry about sleep difficulties, leading to heightened physiological arousal and emotional distress. This, in turn, fuels further rumination, creating a self-perpetuating cycle. The 3-P Model of Insomnia (predisposing, precipitating, and perpetuating factors) [26] further explains how pre-existing vulnerabilities (e.g., trait-level rumination) and acute stressors (e.g., academic pressure) interact with poor sleep to sustain chronic insomnia. Beyond these cognitive frameworks, the Emotional Cascade Model (ECM) provides a theoretical basis for understanding how rumination intensifies negative emotions and contributes to maladaptive behaviors like NSSI [27]. According to ECM, individuals who experience intense negative affect engage in persistent rumination, which amplifies emotional distress and leads to emotional dysregulation. This cycle may increase the likelihood of engaging in NSSI as a maladaptive coping mechanism to disrupt or escape from overwhelming distress [28]. From a neuroscientific perspective, rumination is associated with hyperactivity in the default mode network (DMN), particularly in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) and posterior cingulate cortex (PCC)—brain regions linked to self-referential thinking and emotion regulation [29]. Research suggests that insomnia disrupts functional connectivity within these networks, increasing cognitive rigidity and reinforcing repetitive negative thought patterns [30]. Additionally, sleep deprivation impairs the prefrontal cortex’s regulatory control over the amygdala, heightening emotional reactivity and making it more difficult to disengage from distressing thoughts [31]. These neural mechanisms further explain why chronic sleep disturbances may intensify rumination and contribute to NSSI as an emotion regulation strategy. Given this theoretical and neurobiological foundation, it is plausible that rumination serves as a key mechanism linking insomnia to NSSI. Chronic sleep disturbances may increase susceptibility to excessive, repetitive negative thinking, which heightens emotional distress and, in turn, increases the likelihood of engaging in NSSI as a means of affect regulation. Therefore, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: Rumination mediates the relationship between insomnia and NSSI.

Methods and materials

Participants

This research involved a sample of 680 college students from two universities in Nanjing, China. A stratified cluster sampling method was employed to ensure a balanced representation across academic years. Specifically, 10 classes were selected from each academic year (first-year to senior students), and within each selected class, approximately half of the students were randomly chosen to participate. This approach accounts for the observed class size discrepancy, as the study did not survey entire classes but rather sampled students proportionally. The research employed a double-blind design, with all procedures conducted by a single researcher. Importantly, informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to the administration of any assessments. Of the 680 distributed questionnaires, 667 (98.09%) were completed and deemed valid for analysis. The sample comprised 426 males (63.87%) and 241 females (36.13%). Geographically, 362 participants (54.27%) were from urban areas, while 305 (45.73%) were from rural regions. Furthermore, 402 participants (60.27%) were only children, and 265 (39.73%) had siblings. The participants’ ages ranged from 17 to 24 years, with an average age of 19.81 years (SD = 1.31).

Measures

Athens insomnia scale

The scale, developed by Dan Sedmark [32], consists of 8 items, with higher scores indicating greater insomnia severity. It demonstrates robust internal consistency, as indicated by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.862. The AIS has been widely used and validated in Chinese populations, demonstrating good internal consistency and reliability in previous studies [33].

Adolescent self-injury scale

The scale, developed by Feng Yu and Zheng Ying [34, 35], consists of 19 items. The NSSI score was calculated as the product of the frequency and severity scores, where frequency was rated from 0 (never) to 3 (≥ 5 times), and severity was rated from 0 (none) to 4 (very severe). This scoring method ensures that the total score reflects both the frequency and severity of the behavior. Higher total scores indicate greater severity of NSSI. The scale exhibits robust internal consistency, as indicated by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.931. The Chinese version of the Adolescent Self-Injury Scale has been validated in prior studies, showing good psychometric properties [36].

World health organization well-being scale

The scale, developed by the World Health Organization [37], is intended to measure subjective well-being. It comprises five items, with higher scores indicative of better subjective well-being. The scale demonstrates strong internal consistency, as evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.909. The Chinese version of the World Health Organization Well-Being Scale has been validated in prior studies, showing good psychometric properties [38].

Ruminative responses scale

The scale, developed by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema and Juan Yang [39, 40], consists of 21 items, with higher scores indicative of greater levels of rumination. The scale exhibits strong internal consistency, as indicated by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.968. The Chinese version of the Ruminative Responses Scale has been validated in prior studies, showing good psychometric properties [41].

Procedure

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sanda University (approval number: 2023006). All participants provided voluntary informed consent after being thoroughly briefed on the study’s purpose, the terms of anonymity, and the procedures for ensuring data confidentiality and security. The study sample comprised college students from a university in Jiangsu Province, with individuals exhibiting suicidal tendencies excluded from participation. Completion of each questionnaire required approximately 10 to 15 min.

In order to address the potential impact of Common Method Bias on the results, Harman’s single-factor test was utilized. This analysis involved performing an exploratory factor analysis on 53 items across three distinct scales, without applying rotation. The outcomes indicated that the eigenvalues for seven factors surpassed 1, and the first factor explained only a modest proportion of the total variance (18.75%), which is well below the 40% threshold typically used to indicate substantial bias. These results suggest that Common Method Bias had a negligible effect on the data obtained from the questionnaire.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 to conduct descriptive statistics, independent samples t-tests, and correlation analyses. To investigate the mediating role of rumination in the relationship between insomnia and NSSI, a bootstrap analysis was conducted utilizing the Hayes Process 4.3 plug-in (Model 4) [42]. While PROCESS is a widely accepted approach for mediation analysis, we acknowledge that Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) could provide a more comprehensive assessment of model fit. However, given our study’s focus and sample size, PROCESS was deemed appropriate. Future research could employ SEM to explore more complex relationships and confirm model robustness.

Discussion

Direct associations between insomnia and NSSI

The findings of this study revealed a significant positive association between insomnia and NSSI, consistent with previous research [43]. This result is in line with the cognitive model of insomnia, which posits that individuals experiencing insomnia may show diminished emotional functioning and impulse control, potentially increasing vulnerability to self-injurious behaviors [44]. Two possible explanations may help account for this observed association. First, impulsivity has been identified as a correlate of NSSI [45], and prior studies suggest that sleep difficulties may be linked to heightened impulsivity over time [46]. Second, sleep deprivation has been associated with impaired emotion regulation, which may co-occur with increased risk of NSSI [47]. Additionally, our findings indicated that female participants reported a higher incidence of NSSI than male participants. This aligns with literature suggesting that females may experience greater emotional vulnerability and more frequent difficulties in stress regulation and problem-solving [48, 49]. Interestingly, although previous research has found that females tend to ruminate more than males, our study did not detect a significant gender difference in rumination levels. This inconsistency may be attributable to differences in sample composition, measurement instruments, or cultural context, and should be explored further in future studies. Given the cross-sectional design of this study, these associations should be interpreted with caution. The findings do not imply causal relationships, and longitudinal research is needed to better understand the temporal and directional nature of these links.

The mediating role of rumination between insomnia and NSSI

Our findings indicate that rumination may play a mediating role in the association between insomnia and non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI). Specifically, higher levels of insomnia symptoms were associated with greater tendencies to ruminate, which were in turn linked to increased reports of NSSI. The cognitive correlates of insomnia offer a possible explanation for this pattern. Prior research suggests that individuals with insomnia often experience heightened pre-sleep cognitive activity, characterized by persistent worry, repetitive negative thinking, and difficulty disengaging from distressing thoughts [44]. Compared to those with good sleep quality, college students experiencing insomnia symptoms may be more prone to excessive worry and obsessive thinking at bedtime. These patterns of thinking may involve ruminating on past events and analyzing negative emotions, potentially reinforcing maladaptive cognitive styles [50]. Although our model statistically examined rumination as a mediator between insomnia and NSSI, we acknowledge that the relationship between insomnia and rumination may be bidirectional. Individuals who engage in frequent rumination may also experience elevated physiological and cognitive arousal, which could contribute to difficulties falling or staying asleep. Therefore, rumination may function both as a correlate and a potential antecedent of insomnia. Given the cross-sectional nature of our study, we cannot draw conclusions about causality or the directionality of effects. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are warranted to further explore the temporal and reciprocal dynamics between these variables.

According to Response Style Theory, rumination may prolong and amplify negative emotional experiences, diminish problem-solving abilities, and reinforce maladaptive responses to stress [51]. In this context, increased rumination may function as a maladaptive cognitive strategy that contributes to heightened emotional distress and impaired emotion regulation capacities [52]. Emotional Cascade Theory also posits that intense rumination may trigger a cascade of negative affect, potentially leading individuals to engage in NSSI as a means of alleviating emotional distress [53]. This is consistent with the Experiential Avoidance Model of NSSI, which suggests that individuals experiencing persistent and aversive emotional states may use self-injurious behaviors as a strategy to escape or manage these emotions [54]. In summary, our findings suggest that insomnia and rumination are significantly associated with NSSI, and rumination may be one cognitive process that helps explain this association. However, further research using longitudinal designs is needed to clarify the directionality and underlying mechanisms involved.

The mediating role of subjective well-being between insomnia and NSSI

Our findings suggest that subjective well-being may function as a mediating factor in the association between insomnia and NSSI. Specifically, higher levels of insomnia symptoms were associated with lower subjective well-being, which in turn was linked to higher levels of NSSI. Prior research has indicated that insomnia is negatively associated with subjective well-being, as it reflects dissatisfaction with sleep duration or quality [55]. This perceived mismatch between sleep needs and actual sleep experience may contribute to a decline in well-being among college students [56]. Poor sleep quality and insufficient rest have been associated with increased psychological distress, greater emotional instability, and reduced life satisfaction—factors that constitute core dimensions of subjective well-being. According to the Experiential Avoidance Model, individuals with lower subjective well-being may be more likely to engage in maladaptive avoidance behaviors, including NSSI, as a means of managing distressing emotional states [54]. In this context, self-injury may serve as a short-term coping mechanism to escape or blunt negative affect, especially in the absence of effective emotional regulation strategies. Over time, individuals experiencing chronic sleep disturbances may become increasingly vulnerable to negative emotionality and cognitive distortions, further impairing their capacity for emotional regulation and possibly increasing the likelihood of engaging in NSSI. While our findings highlight the potential role of subjective well-being in explaining the association between insomnia and NSSI, the cross-sectional nature of the study prevents us from determining causal relationships. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify the directionality and mechanisms underlying these associations. These results underscore the importance of subjective well-being as a possible psychological buffer. Interventions aimed at improving sleep quality and enhancing overall well-being may be valuable for reducing the risk of self-injurious behaviors among college students.

Theoretical and practical contributions

This study examined the associations between insomnia and NSSI among college students and explored the potential mediating roles of subjective well-being and rumination. Our findings contribute to the theoretical understanding of the links between sleep difficulties and self-injurious behaviors in several ways: (1) They are consistent with and extend emotion regulation theory and stress-coping theory by suggesting that insomnia may be associated with increased NSSI through cognitive-emotional mechanisms such as heightened rumination and reduced subjective well-being. (2) They underscore the potential importance of psychological processes in understanding the relationship between insomnia and NSSI, offering directions for future research on cognitive and affective mediators. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, the findings should be interpreted with caution and cannot establish causal relationships. However, they may inform mental health promotion strategies in university settings. First, the observed associations suggest that addressing sleep problems could be an important component of efforts to reduce NSSI risk. Universities might consider integrating evidence-informed sleep interventions, such as Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I), into their mental health services. CBT-I techniques—including stimulus control, sleep restriction, and cognitive restructuring—have been shown in prior research to alleviate insomnia symptoms and may help reduce emotional distress. Second, our findings highlight the potential benefits of promoting subjective well-being and reducing rumination in university students. Positive psychology interventions (e.g., gratitude exercises, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy) and evidence-based cognitive therapies (e.g., traditional CBT, rumination-focused CBT, and acceptance and commitment therapy) may support students in improving emotional regulation and adaptive coping. While our study cannot test the effectiveness of these interventions directly, our results suggest that targeting these factors might be useful in future intervention trials. From a policy perspective, the findings align with national mental health initiatives in China, such as the National Mental Health Work Plan (2023–2030), which emphasizes the importance of strengthening psychological support systems in universities. Promoting sleep and mental health literacy, offering psychoeducation, and facilitating access to peer support and counseling services may help students better understand and manage sleep-related emotional challenges. Cross-sector collaboration between educators, mental health professionals, and policymakers may further enhance the development of integrated, student-centered intervention frameworks.

In conclusion, while our study is limited by its cross-sectional design, it provides preliminary evidence for potential cognitive and emotional pathways linking sleep difficulties to NSSI in university populations. These insights can inform the development of future longitudinal studies and evidence-based interventions aimed at improving student well-being.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that warrant cautious interpretation of the findings. Most importantly, the cross-sectional design precludes any conclusions about causal relationships among insomnia, subjective well-being, rumination, and NSSI. While we observed associations between these variables, we cannot determine the directionality of effects. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to examine the temporal and causal pathways more definitively. Second, all variables were measured through self-report instruments, which may be susceptible to biases such as common method variance and social desirability. To enhance the validity of future research, multi-method approaches—such as behavioral tasks, structured clinical interviews, or objective physiological and sleep-based indicators (e.g., actigraphy)—should be considered. Third, although we examined rumination as a mediator, we treated it as a unidimensional construct. Future studies could dissect the subcomponents of the Ruminative Responses Scale (RRS)—including depressive rumination, reflective pondering, and brooding—as separate mediators to explore their unique roles in the relationship between insomnia and NSSI. Fourth, this study did not perform confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to test the measurement models of the core constructs, which limits the ability to rigorously validate the scales used. Incorporating CFA in future research would strengthen the psychometric foundations of the study. Fifth, while our theoretical framework posits links between insomnia and rumination, existing literature suggests a potential bidirectional relationship. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify these temporal dynamics and understand whether insomnia leads to increased rumination, or vice versa, and how these patterns contribute to NSSI risk over time. Sixth, the sample had a gender imbalance, with a higher proportion of male participants. This may restrict the generalizability of findings related to gender differences. Future research should strive for more balanced gender representation to ensure more robust gender-related analyses. Finally, the study was conducted within a specific cultural and educational context—Chinese university students—which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. Cross-cultural and cross-regional studies are recommended to examine whether similar patterns hold in different sociocultural environments.

In summary, while this study sheds light on the psychological mechanisms linking insomnia and NSSI, the limitations—particularly the cross-sectional design—call for cautious interpretation. Future research employing longitudinal, experimental, and cross-cultural designs will be crucial for advancing understanding and informing more effective prevention strategies.

Conclusion

This study investigated the associations between insomnia and NSSI among college students, with a focus on the potential mediating roles of subjective well-being and rumination. The results showed that insomnia was positively associated with NSSI and that subjective well-being and rumination were significant mediators in this association. Specifically, higher levels of insomnia were linked to lower subjective well-being and higher rumination, both of which were in turn related to increased NSSI engagement. These findings suggest that sleep difficulties, reduced well-being, and maladaptive cognitive patterns may co-occur with self-injurious behaviors in this population. However, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, caution is warranted in interpreting these relationships as causal. Future research employing longitudinal or experimental designs is needed to clarify the directionality of these associations and to further elucidate the psychological mechanisms underlying NSSI.
