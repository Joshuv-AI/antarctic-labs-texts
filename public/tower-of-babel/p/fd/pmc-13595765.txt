Prehospital suicide mortality in the Lake Van basin: rural–urban disparities from three-year EMS dispatch surveillance in eastern Türkiye
Authors: Mehmet Tatlı, Kerem Dost Bilmez, İsmail Altıntop
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEastern Türkiye records high suicide mortality; however, prehospital emergency medical services (EMS) dispatch records from this region have not been systematically evaluated as a surveillance source. We aimed to describe EMS-attended suicide and self-harm cases in Van Province and to quantify the association between rural residence and prehospital suicide mortality.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective population-based surveillance study of all ‘suicide’-coded records in the Van Province 112 EMS database (ASOS) from January 2023 to December 2025. After excluding duplicates and miscoded entries, 848 physically enacted cases formed the primary sample; 41 ideation/threat records were described separately and excluded from the primary mortality model. Prehospital death was defined as death recorded before hospital handover. Associations were estimated using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and method lethality, with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) as a sensitivity analysis. Daily meteorological associations were examined using Poisson regression with harmonic seasonality controls.ResultsAmong 848 physical-act cases (57.7% female; mean age 28.7 ± 12.7 years), the estimated crude annual EMS-attended rate was 25.7 per 100,000 population. Drug/chemical ingestion predominated (52.6%), whereas hanging accounted for 62.1% of prehospital deaths. The rural prehospital death rate was 24.7% compared with 10.8% in urban areas (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 2.18, 95% CI 1.38–3.44, p < 0.001). Females constituted 33.9% of prehospital deaths (national average: 26.7%). Temperature showed a nominally significant but practically small association (incidence rate ratio [IRR] 1.006/°C, p = 0.022) that did not survive Bonferroni correction. No association with lunar phase was detected.ConclusionsIn this dispatch-based surveillance study, rural residence was associated with approximately twofold higher odds of prehospital suicide mortality even after adjustment for age, sex, and method lethality. The findings support the value of 112 dispatch data for prehospital surveillance and generate hypotheses regarding geographic access, delayed discovery, EMS resource constraints, and method lethality that require confirmation through linked dispatch-hospital data and studies with measured response intervals.

Background

Suicide claims more than 727,000 lives annually and ranks as the third leading cause of death among persons aged 15–29 years worldwide [1]. Mortality statistics capture only the fatal endpoint of a broader continuum that extends from ideation through non-fatal self-harm to suicide death, and each level of this continuum may have distinct epidemiological and service-use patterns [2, 3].

In Türkiye, suicide mortality has increased substantially, with 4,460 deaths reported in 2024 (crude rate 5.22 per 100,000), compared with 2,301 deaths in 2002 despite a smaller increase in population size [4]. A nationwide analysis of suicide-related mortality from 2009 to 2022 reported an increasing male trend [5], while long-term national analyses have consistently identified Eastern Anatolia as one of the regions with the highest crude suicide rates [6, 7]. Because Van Province is characterised by marked seasonal and climatic variation, environmental patterns also require local evaluation rather than extrapolation from other settings [8]. These regional differences suggest that local surveillance is needed rather than relying solely on national estimates.

Eastern Türkiye also presents an epidemiological and sociocultural context that may differ from the patterns described in many high-income settings. Several studies from eastern and southeastern Türkiye have reported a relatively high burden of suicidal behaviour among young women and have described patterns that challenge the conventional gender paradox in suicide epidemiology [9–11]. Related regional literature has also described gendered patterns of highly lethal self-harm, including self-immolation, in neighbouring contexts [12]. However, the extent to which EMS-attended prehospital events reflect these patterns remains insufficiently described.

Rural-urban disparity is a second central dimension. Meta-analytic evidence indicates that rural residence is associated with increased suicide death risk, and a recent umbrella review identified rurality among relevant social determinants of suicide-related outcomes [13, 14]. Proposed mechanisms include geographic isolation, delayed discovery, longer transport distances, reduced access to mental health services, social isolation, and differential availability or use of high-lethality methods [15, 16]. In Türkiye, one prehospital study reported longer rural ambulance response times than urban response times [17]; however, no study has quantified rural-urban differences in prehospital suicide mortality in eastern provinces.

Seasonality and meteorological exposures are additional issues that require local evaluation. International evidence suggests short-term associations between meteorological conditions and suicide-related outcomes, but reported effects vary by region, climate, study design, and modelling strategy [8]. Van Province has an extreme continental climate and dispersed high-altitude settlements, making it a relevant setting in which to describe seasonal and meteorological patterns while avoiding overinterpretation of exploratory environmental associations.

Prehospital EMS dispatch records may provide a practical surveillance source in settings where hospital or mortality registries incompletely capture the prehospital phase. Ambulance-based surveillance systems in Australia and Scotland have demonstrated the value of coded EMS records for monitoring self-harm and mental-health-related emergencies [18, 19]. In Türkiye, 112 is the national emergency number, and 112 Emergency Health Services (EHS) records operational data through ASOS (Acil Sağlık Operasyon Sistemi, Emergency Health Operations System). Existing Turkish dispatch-based suicide research is limited to non-eastern provinces [17, 20]. Prior studies in Van have been hospital-based or medicolegal [9, 21], and EMS dispatch data have not been used to examine prehospital suicide mortality. Therefore, this study analysed 112 EMS-attended suicide and self-harm records in Van Province, with a primary focus on the association between rural residence and prehospital mortality and secondary descriptive analyses of sex, method, temporal, and meteorological patterns.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective, population-based, cross-sectional surveillance study conducted in Van Province, Eastern Anatolia (area 19,069 km2; population approximately 1.1 million). The provincial 112 EHS system operates through a single command and control centre and records dispatch-level data in ASOS. ASOS is the national electronic emergency health operations system used by Turkish 112 EHS services and includes operational fields such as dispatch code, date and time, location, ambulance station, demographic information recorded for the patient, method or event description when documented, and prehospital disposition/outcome as entered in the dispatch and EMS record. The analytic extract used in this study did not include linked hospital outcomes, detailed psychiatric history, socioeconomic variables, or reliably analysable response-time intervals. This report conforms to the STROBE statement for cross-sectional studies and the RECORD extension for routinely collected health data [22].

Data source and case identification

All ASOS records assigned a ‘suicide’ or suicide-related dispatch code between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2025, were extracted (n = 1,115). The dispatch code was based on the routine operational classification recorded by 112 dispatch personnel and, when available, information subsequently documented by EMS teams. Records were screened to identify physically enacted suicide/self-harm events, ideation or threat without a documented physical act, duplicate records, and miscoded entries. Duplicate records were excluded when the same event appeared more than once based on dispatch identifiers, date-time information, location, and case narrative. Miscoded entries were excluded when the available ASOS fields or narrative indicated that the event was not suicide-related or reflected an administrative coding error. After exclusion of duplicates (n = 185) and miscoded entries (n = 41), 889 eligible records remained. Of these, 848 records with a documented physical act formed the primary analytic sample, while 41 ideation/threat records were retained as a secondary descriptive group but were not included in the primary prehospital mortality model (Fig. 1).

Variable definitions

Setting classification. Cases were classified as rural or urban according to the ambulance dispatch location and the operational catchment of ambulance stations. Settlements served by Van city centre ambulance stations (Van Merkez, Ipekyolu, Edremit, and Tusba districts) were defined as urban; all other peripheral districts, including Bahcesaray, Catak, Baskale, Gevas, Ercis, Ozalp, Saray, Muradiye, Gurpinar, and Caldiran, were classified as rural. This pragmatic classification reflects operational ambulance accessibility rather than census-based administrative boundaries.

Outcome definitions. Prehospital death was defined as death recorded in ASOS before hospital arrival or before formal handover to hospital care. Cases documented as alive at EMS handover or transported alive were classified as alive at EMS handover; this category should not be interpreted as survival to hospital discharge because in-hospital outcomes were not available. Deliberate self-harm and interrupted attempt categories were retained as recorded in the operational ASOS classification and supporting case narrative.

Method lethality. Self-harm methods were classified a priori as high-lethality (hanging, firearms, jumping from height, and self-immolation) or low-lethality (drug/chemical ingestion, sharp instrument, and other/mixed methods). This classification was based on the relative case-fatality patterns reported in a systematic review and meta-analysis of suicide method lethality by Cai et al. [23]. The classification was used to adjust for method severity when estimating the association between rural residence and prehospital death.

Meteorological data. Daily meteorological variables were obtained from the Turkish State Meteorological Service records for Van Province and linked to ASOS dispatch records by calendar date. The variables used in the secondary analysis were daily mean temperature, sunshine duration, precipitation, and wind speed. Because a single provincial meteorological series was used for a geographically large province, this analysis was considered exploratory and was interpreted cautiously.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Van Training and Research Hospital (GOKAEK/2025-08-06, dated October 24, 2025). In the ethics committee application, the study was specified as a retrospective analysis of routinely collected 112 EMS dispatch records covering the years 2023–2025, and the planned start date of the study, including data analysis, was declared as January 2026. Accordingly, the analyses were conducted after completion of the predefined data period and in accordance with the approved protocol. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study, use of anonymised administrative dispatch records, and absence of direct patient contact or intervention. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Primary analysis. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and median with range, and categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The primary outcome was prehospital death among physical-act cases. The association between rural residence and prehospital death was estimated using multivariable logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, and method lethality. These covariates were selected a priori based on substantive plausibility, existing literature, and availability in the ASOS extract. Model discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), and calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Sensitivity analyses included a setting-by-sex interaction, sex-stratified models, and an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) analysis. IPTW was used only as a robustness check for the adjusted association, not to imply causal identification, because several potential confounders such as psychiatric history, socioeconomic status, substance use, EMS response time, and hospital outcomes were unavailable.

Secondary analysis. Daily case counts were analysed using Poisson regression with harmonic seasonality terms (sine and cosine functions with a 365.25-day period), linear calendar trend, and day-of-week adjustment. Negative binomial and zero-inflated Poisson models were used as sensitivity analyses to assess overdispersion and excess zeros. An exploratory distributed lag non-linear model (DLNM; df = 4 for temperature and df = 3 for lag up to 7 days) was also examined. A Bonferroni-corrected alpha level of 0.0125 was used for the four meteorological variables. Lunar phase differences were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

During the study period, 889 eligible suicide/self-harm-coded EMS records were identified. Of these, 848 involved a documented physical act and constituted the primary analytic sample; 41 ideation/threat records were retained as a secondary descriptive group and were not included in the primary mortality model. The primary sample corresponded to a crude annual EMS-attended rate of approximately 26 per 100,000 population. Annual counts were similar in 2023 and 2024 (269 and 272 cases, respectively) and higher in 2025 (307 cases; trend p = 0.046). Given the small number of annual observations and the borderline p value, this pattern was interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of a robust temporal trend.

The demographic and baseline characteristics of the primary sample are summarised in Table 1. Most cases were classified as suicide attempts with the patient alive at EMS handover (64.9%), followed by deliberate self-harm (16.2%), prehospital deaths (13.6%; n = 115), and interrupted attempts (5.3%). Drug or chemical ingestion was the most common method overall (52.6%). Hanging accounted for 62.1% of prehospital deaths, whereas firearms had the highest observed prehospital case fatality rate (70.7%) (Fig. 2). Because hospital outcomes were not available, the alive-at-handover category should not be interpreted as survival to discharge.

Temporal patterns

Cases were more frequent in summer (31.0% of total) and least frequent in autumn (19.8%). Monthly counts were highest in the summer months, particularly July and August, although this seasonal pattern was described descriptively rather than treated as proof of a stable annual trend. Hourly counts increased from the morning through the late afternoon and were highest in the late afternoon/evening period, with the maximum observed around 17:00–18:00 (Fig. 3).

Rural–urban prehospital mortality disparity

The prehospital death rate was 24.7% in rural areas compared with 10.8% in urban areas (crude OR 2.72, 95% CI 1.78–4.15). After adjustment for age, sex, and method lethality, rural residence remained associated with higher odds of prehospital death (aOR 2.18, 95% CI 1.38–3.44, p < 0.001). Model discrimination was acceptable (AUC-ROC 0.79), and the IPTW sensitivity analysis produced a similar estimate (OR 2.09, 95% CI 1.28–3.41). Rural cases also involved high-lethality methods more frequently than urban cases (38.8% vs. 22.3%, p < 0.001), indicating that method differences contributed to, but did not fully explain, the rural-urban prehospital mortality gap (Table 2; Fig. 4).

Meteorological associations

Temperature was associated with a small increase in daily case counts in the uncorrected Poisson model (IRR 1.006 per °C, 95% CI 1.001–1.011, p = 0.022), but this did not reach the Bonferroni-corrected threshold (alpha = 0.0125) and the magnitude was practically small. Sunshine duration, precipitation, and wind speed were not statistically associated with daily case counts. Lunar phase was not associated with case counts (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.692). Discussion

This study provides the first dispatch-based description of EMS-attended suicide and self-harm events from eastern Türkiye and demonstrates that 112 dispatch records can identify important prehospital patterns that are not visible in hospital-based datasets alone. The principal finding was an approximately twofold rural-urban difference in prehospital mortality. Because the dataset did not include measured response intervals, linked hospital outcomes, or detailed socioeconomic and clinical variables, this finding should be interpreted as an association rather than evidence that any single mechanism caused the excess rural mortality. Nevertheless, the observed rural mortality gap, the elevated female share of prehospital deaths, and the predominance of hanging among deaths provide a useful basis for hypothesis generation and for prioritising more detailed linked surveillance.

The rural–urban prehospital mortality disparity

The most important finding was the persistent rural-urban difference in prehospital mortality: 24.7% in rural areas versus 10.8% in urban areas (aOR 2.18). This estimate refers to the adjusted odds of prehospital death among physically enacted suicide/self-harm cases recorded by 112 EMS. The finding was similar in sensitivity analyses, including IPTW, sex stratification, and interaction testing. Its direction is broadly consistent with the broader literature linking rural residence with suicide mortality risk [13, 14]. In our data, rural cases were also more likely to involve high-lethality methods (38.8% vs. 22.3%), and this difference should be acknowledged as one contributor to the rural mortality gap. Importantly, the association between rural residence and prehospital death persisted after adjustment for method lethality, suggesting that method choice alone did not fully account for the disparity.

Several mechanisms may plausibly contribute to the observed rural-urban association, including delayed discovery of the event, longer distances to EMS units or hospitals, seasonal road disruption, limited prehospital resources, and differences in method lethality [15–17]. These mechanisms overlap but are conceptually distinct: delayed discovery refers to the interval before an emergency call is placed, whereas EMS response and transport intervals begin after system activation. The present dispatch-based dataset could not directly measure these intervals; therefore, these explanations should be considered hypotheses for future research rather than conclusions demonstrated by the current analysis. The mountainous geography and severe winter conditions of Van Province make seasonal access barriers particularly relevant, but stratified seasonal analyses would be needed to test whether the rural-urban gap is larger during winter. Future studies should link dispatch, ambulance, hospital, and mortality registry data and should include measured response intervals, transport times, unit capability, and area-level deprivation to clarify the mechanisms underlying the observed association.

The gender paradox and eastern cultural context

The female predominance in non-fatal cases (57.7%) is consistent with established patterns in suicidal behaviour [3]. The female share of prehospital deaths in this study (33.9%) was higher than the national female share reported in Turkish suicide mortality statistics [7] and is compatible with previous reports from Van and eastern/southeastern Türkiye that described a high burden of suicidal behaviour among young women [9–11]. These findings should be interpreted within a structural rather than essentialising cultural framework. Potential contributors include gendered social constraints, family and community pressures, limited access to confidential mental health care, economic marginalisation, and cumulative regional trauma; however, the present dataset did not measure these determinants directly.

The observed pattern therefore calls for culturally and linguistically appropriate prevention strategies, but the dispatch data alone cannot identify which upstream interventions would be effective. Qualitative research, community-based studies, and linked clinical datasets are needed to understand pathways to suicidal behaviour among women and young people in the region and to design prevention strategies that are acceptable, safe, and context-sensitive. Comparative evidence from neighbouring regions, including reports of self-immolation among young women, suggests that some determinants may be shared across broader regional contexts, but direct extrapolation to Van Province should be made cautiously [12, 24].

Hanging as the dominant lethal method

Hanging was responsible for 62.1% of all prehospital deaths, a proportion higher than the national figure reported in long-term Turkish suicide mortality analyses [7] and consistent with the high case fatality of hanging described in method-lethality meta-analysis [23]. Firearms had the highest observed prehospital case fatality rate in this dataset (70.7%). Because the availability of ligature materials is difficult to restrict, the predominance of hanging suggests that upstream prevention may be important; however, specific interventions such as gatekeeper training, crisis hotlines, and school-based mental health literacy should be framed as plausible strategies requiring local evaluation rather than as conclusions directly proven by this dataset [15].

Temporal patterns and operational implications

The temporal distribution showed higher case volumes during the late afternoon and evening. Similar patterns have been reported in previous Turkish prehospital studies [17, 20]. This pattern may be useful for operational planning, such as ensuring evening-shift preparedness and clear crisis-triage protocols, but it should not be overinterpreted because the hourly distribution was descriptive and not modelled as an independent predictor of mortality.

Meteorological associations

The temperature-case association was directionally consistent with environmental studies of suicide [8], but the observed effect was small, did not survive Bonferroni correction, and was based on a single provincial meteorological series. The absence of an association with lunar phase is consistent with contemporary evidence questioning lunar effects on suicidal behaviour [3]. More definitive environmental analyses would require multi-province datasets, spatially resolved exposures, and adequate power for distributed lag models.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, reliance on a single operational dispatch code (‘suicide’) may have introduced misclassification in both directions. Some true suicide/self-harm events may have been dispatched under alternative codes and therefore missed, while some suicide-coded calls may not have represented confirmed suicide attempts after EMS assessment; validation studies in other emergency-care coding systems have shown that coding accuracy for self-harm can be imperfect [25]. Because such misclassification can occur in either direction, the observed EMS-attended rate may either underestimate or overestimate the true burden. Second, outcome ascertainment was limited to the prehospital phase. Patients recorded as alive at EMS handover may have died later in hospital, and no hospital or mortality-registry linkage was available. Third, the rural-urban classification reflected ambulance accessibility rather than census criteria, and residual confounding by unmeasured area-level deprivation, psychiatric history, substance use, socioeconomic status, and household context cannot be excluded. Fourth, the logistic regression and IPTW models were limited to the variables available in the ASOS extract; response times, transport times, unit capability, and advanced life support availability could not be included. Fifth, meteorological analysis used a single provincial exposure series for a geographically large and topographically heterogeneous province, and these findings should therefore be considered exploratory.

Conclusions

This three-year dispatch-based surveillance study provides the first prehospital suicide and self-harm data from Van Province, an eastern Turkish setting that has been underrepresented in EMS research. Rural residence was associated with approximately twofold higher odds of prehospital death, and this association persisted after adjustment for age, sex, and method lethality. Rural cases also involved high-lethality methods more often than urban cases, indicating that the rural-urban gap cannot be interpreted as independent of method choice, although it was not explained by method lethality alone. The findings support the use of 112 dispatch data as a feasible surveillance source and highlight the need for linked dispatch-hospital-mortality registries, measured response-time analyses, and context-sensitive prevention research in eastern Türkiye.
