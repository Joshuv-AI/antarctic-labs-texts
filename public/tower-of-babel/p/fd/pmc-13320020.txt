GRP75 chaperone-driven mitochondria–ER membrane coupling promotes Ca2+-dependent ROS accumulation and ferroptosis during hypoxic stress
Authors: Haoxiao Yan, Penggeng Chen, Yaoyi Zhang, Qishuang He, Hao Liu, Qiao Liu, Kaige Song, Song Yang, Liulan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of regulated cell death triggered by the accumulation of iron-dependent lipid peroxides. Nevertheless, the exact function and mechanism of ferroptosis in oxidative stress injury caused by environmental challenges, especially in hypoxic stress, remains unknown. Here, we demonstrate that hypoxic stress induces an aberrant increase in mitochondria-associated ER membrane (MAM) formation both in vitro and in vivo in largemouth bass. Transcriptome analysis reveals significant upregulation of the ferroptosis pathway. Combining transcriptomics, Western blot, and immunostaining results, we identify GRP75 as a key regulator of MAM formation under hypoxic conditions. Further studies demonstrate that silencing GRP75 protects hepatocytes from hypoxia-induced cellular damage hallmarked by ferroptosis. Mechanistically, the excessive formation of MAM caused by GRP75 accelerating Ca2+ transfer, mediating mitochondrial and ER dysfunction, leading to ROS accumulation. Based on these findings, MAMs are considered an essential component in the execution of ferroptosis.

Molecular oxygen (O2) sustains intracellular bioenergetics and is consumed by numerous biochemical reactions, making it crucial for terrestrial and aquatic organisms (1, 2). Dissolved oxygen (DO), as an important factor in the aquatic environment, decreasing levels can cause hypoxic stress and affect the survival, growth and normal operation of various molecular functions of aquatic organisms (3, 4). In recent years, due to increasing global warming and aquatic eutrophication caused by intensified human activities, more and more hypoxic events have been recorded in aquatic ecosystems, seriously threatening the survival of aquatic organisms (5, 6). Accordingly, in the context of climate change, which is mainly characterized by warming, hypoxic stress in aquatic environments not only poses a great challenge to aquaculture but also threatens the conservation of aquatic biodiversity (7, 8). And exploring the important molecular mechanisms of tissue damage in aquatic organisms caused by hypoxic stress can help deepen the ecotoxicological impacts of global environmental challenges on aquatic organisms. Furthermore, the intensive investigation of key genes governing fish environmental responses lays a solid foundation for the advancement of stress-resistant breeding in fish (9, 10).

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent necrotic cell death caused by the blockade of cellular lipid hydroperoxide detoxification systems (11, 12). It causes oxidative stress damage by damaging the structural and functional integrity of biological membranes (13, 14). Specifically, Fe2+ catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 via the Fenton reaction to generate hydroxyl radicals (·OH), which selectively attack polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) within membrane phospholipids, driving a lipid peroxidation chain reaction and ultimately triggering ferroptosis (15, 16). The accumulation of Fe2+ due to transferrin receptor (TFR) activation under hypoxic conditions, coupled with hypoxia-inducible factor 1A (HIF-1A)-mediated lipid metabolism reprogramming, has led an increasing number of researchers to associate cellular hypoxic responses with ferroptosis (17, 18). Studies in Hypophthalmichthys molitrix and Hexagrammos otakii have confirmed that ferroptosis was the major contributor to tissue damage under hypoxic stress (19, 20). And inhibition of ferroptosis through ACSL4 (Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4) knockout alleviated tissue damage and enhanced hypoxic tolerance in H. molitrix. Evidently, ferroptosis is closely related to oxidative stress caused by environmental pressures (21). However, the molecular regulatory mechanisms of ferroptosis in aquatic organisms remain poorly understood.

In eukaryotic cells, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) establishes a dynamic connection with mitochondria through membrane appositions called mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs) (22, 23). MAMs are stabilized by protein tethers that operate as bridges between these organelles and provide a molecular platform for signal communication (24, 25). GRP75 (glucose-regulated protein 75) is a chaperone primarily localized in mitochondria, which maintains the stability of MAMs by anchoring IP3R1 (inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor 1) and VDAC1 (voltage-dependent anion channel 1) (26, 27, 28). IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 complex mediates Ca2+ flow from the ER to mitochondria, maintaining cellular physiological function and integrity (29, 30). We previously reported that hypoxic stress induced mitochondrial calcium overload in the liver and heart of largemouth bass, leading to ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation (31, 32). In addition, we found that hypoxic stress increased lipid mobilization and caused excessive MDA (malondialdehyde) production (33, 34). However, the potential role of MAMs, as bridges for inter-organelle communication, in mitochondrial calcium overload and mitochondrial dysfunction under hypoxic stress remains to be elucidated. Also, whether the ROS burst and accumulation of lipid peroxide we observed under hypoxic stress were caused by ferroptosis has not been determined.

In this study, we unexpectedly discovered that GRP75-promoted MAMs formation was enriched in hypoxic-treated livers. Overexpression of GRP75 increased the formation of MAMs, enhanced mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, accelerated hypoxia-induced mitochondrial dysfunction, and intensified ferroptosis. Overall, we demonstrated a potential mediatory role of MAMs in hypoxia-induced ferroptosis.

Results

Ferroptosis is present during acute hypoxic stress in the liver

As oxidative stress is a core driver of ferroptosis, we sought to determine the pathways through which hypoxic exposure regulates ferroptosis. We first established a hypoxic model in largemouth bass by controlling nitrogen and oxygen input and detected key indicators of ferroptosis (Fig. 1A). Oxygen radical response and lipid peroxidation are signature events of ferroptosis. In the present study, we observed a significant increase in liver ROS, LPO and MDA levels of largemouth bass after hypoxic exposure (Fig. 1, B–D). Meanwhile, significant downregulation of total glutathione concentration and upregulation in GPx activity were observed in the antioxidant system (Fig. 1, E and F). The above indicators also reflect the initiation of compensatory antioxidant defenses in the liver under sustained oxidative stress. Comparative transcriptomic analysis revealed that the ferroptosis pathway was significantly upregulated following hypoxic exposure, with increased expression of key positive regulators (Acsl4 and Lpcat3) (Fig. 1G). The hallmark feature of ferroptosis is driven by the peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acid phospholipids (PUFA-PLs). We hypothesise that hypoxic stress might change the lipid composition in the liver. The assessment of lipidomic changes deficiency by LC‒MS revealed that the levels of PEs were generally increased after hypoxic exposure, whereas the lysoPE and lysoPC levels were decreased in the liver (Fig. 1J). In particular, we also found that well-established pro-ferroptotic phospholipids such as PE-38:4 (18:0_20:4) and PE-40:4 (18:0_22:4) were enriched (Fig. 1I). We further observed that the expression levels of ACSL4 and POR (Cytochrome P450 oxidoreductase) were significantly increased (Fig. 1, K and L), suggesting enhanced susceptibility to ferroptosis, as ACSL4 facilitates the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids to generate oxidizable lipid substrates, while POR contributes to electron transfer reactions that promote lipid peroxidation and phospholipid hydroperoxide accumulation. Gene expression analysis results revealed differential expression patterns in regulatory factors involved in lipid peroxidation (Alox12, Acsl4, Lpcat3, Ptgs2, Nox1), antioxidant defense (Slc7a11, Gpx4), and iron metabolism (Tfr1, Fth1) (Fig. 1M) (15, 35). Finally, we determined serum AST and ALT levels to assess the extent of liver damage caused by hypoxic exposure. The significant elevation in AST and ALT levels (Fig. 1N) indicates that hypoxic stress impaired liver function in largemouth bass. These results suggest that hypoxic exposure induced changes in ferroptosis-related factors, leading to disruption of cellular redox homeostasis.

Hypoxic exposure augmented hepatic ER-mitochondria interaction

Next, we sought to investigate the molecular mechanisms by which hypoxic exposure mediates ferroptosis, aiming to identify key targets for molecular breeding of stress-resistant fish. We observed a striking phenotype in the liver following hypoxic exposure. The association between the mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum in the liver was significantly enhanced (Fig. 2A). The liver mitochondrial perimeter was significantly increased in the hypoxia group compared to the control group (Fig. 2B). Morphologically, most of the mitochondria in the hypoxia-treated liver were ER wrapped, resulting in increased MAM length compared to controls (Fig. 2C). To avoid overinterpretation based solely on the apparent increase in the number of mitochondria, we further quantified normalized indices of ER-mitochondria interaction. The proportion of mitochondria in close contact with the ER were significantly elevated in the hypoxia group (Fig. 2, D and E). Collectively, our results indicated that hypoxia promotes the formation of MAM in the liver of largemouth bass. MAM components were highly enriched in hypoxia-treated liver, and mitochondrial dysfunction and ERS were induced

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying hypoxia-induced MAM formation, we performed comparative transcriptomics analysis on RNA-Seq datasets from hypoxic-exposed largemouth bass livers. Among 55 MAM-associated genes (Table S4), four genes were significantly upregulated in hypoxic livers (mitochondrial calcium uniporter 4 [MCU], voltage dependent anion channel 1 [VDAC1], glucose-regulated protein 75 [GRP75], and dynamin-related protein1 [DRP1]), while one gene was downregulated (phosphofurin acidic cluster sorting protein 2 [PACS2]) (Fig. 3, A and B). MCU (mediating Ca2+ transport into mitochondria) expression was highest among the four upregulated genes. Therefore, we performed RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis to verify the expression of the IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 complex, which plays a crucial role in Ca2+ transport. Compared with the control group, the mRNA expression levels of the IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 complex were significantly increased in the hypoxic group, as were the protein expression levels of GRP75 and VDAC1 (Fig. 3, C–F and L). Furthermore, co-IP results showed that hypoxia markedly enhanced GRP75-VDAC1 and GRP75-IP3R1 interaction, confirming a direct physical association among the MAM complex proteins (Fig. 3I). Meanwhile, we observed that hypoxic exposure led to a significant increase in hepatic mitochondrial Ca2+ concentration via the upregulation of the MCU channel (Fig. 3, J–L). Considering that mitochondrial calcium overload may affect mitochondrial and ER function via the MAM, we detected the expression of relevant regulatory factors at both the mRNA and protein levels. Western blot results revealed that hypoxic exposure led to elevated levels of ER stress-associated proteins (P-PERK, GRP78, P-EIF2A and CHOP) and mitochondrial fission, autophagy proteins (DRP1 and LC3) (Fig. 3, M and N). Changes in mRNA levels also indicated that hypoxic exposure exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress (Fig. 3O). ATP levels serve as a core biomarker for assessing mitochondrial function. Our experiments revealed a significant decrease in liver ATP levels following hypoxic exposure (Fig. 3H). In summary, we hypothesized that hypoxic exposure mediates mitochondrial calcium overload by regulating the expression of the IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 complex, thereby inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress. This may also contribute to the aggravation of hepatic ferroptosis.

GRP75-KD weakened the degree of MAM coupling

ER-mitochondrial coupling is a key determinant of cell fate in conditions of cellular stress. GRP75 maintains MAM function by mediating calcium signaling and structural anchoring between the ER and mitochondria. We first established a hypoxic model in primary hepatocytes from largemouth bass to investigate whether GRP75 was essential for maintaining MAM integrity and hypoxic tolerance. In transmission electron microscopy, we found that silencing GRP75 expression in primary hepatocytes increased the distance between mitochondria and the ER (Fig. 4, A–C). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that hypoxia significantly increased the ratio of MAM length to mitochondrial perimeter and the proportion of mitochondria in contact with the ER, while these changes were markedly attenuated by GRP75-KD (Fig. 4, D and E). These findings indicated that GRP75 silencing reduced ER-mitochondria contact under hypoxic conditions. Given that GRP75 is known to facilitate the assembly of mitochondria-ER contact sites through the IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 tripartite complex, we next investigated the effects of GRP75 suppression on the expression patterns of MAM elements. At the molecular level, we examined the expression levels of the IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 complex. Results revealed that GRP75-KD significantly reduced the expression of IP3R1, VDAC1, and MCU (Fig. 4, F–K). We then assessed the viability of primary hepatocytes under different treatments. Interestingly, cell viability showed a significant negative correlation with MAM integrity (Fig. 4L). Meanwhile, co-IP results showed that GRP75-KD markedly diminished MAM interaction (Fig. 4M). GRP75-KD inhibited mitochondrial dysfunction and ER stress and attenuated hypoxia-induced ferroptosis

The biological processes associated with mitochondrial bioenergetics were determined in order to assess the impact of GRP75 intervention on mitochondrial function. ROS measurements showed that GRP75-KD markedly reduced Cyto-ROS and mito-ROS levels in primary hepatocytes under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 5, A and B). Analysis of ATP levels and the NAD+/NADH ratio in primary hepatocytes revealed that GRP75 knockdown significantly increased cellular ATP and NAD + levels following hypoxia treatment (Fig. 5, C and F). Using JC-1 and Rhod-2AM probes, we further observed that GRP75-KD restored mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) and reduced mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation in primary hepatocytes (Fig. 5, D and E). Western blot results also demonstrated that silencing GRP75 further alleviated endoplasmic reticulum stress (as evidenced by decreased expression levels of key regulatory proteins P-PERK and CHOP) (Fig. 5G). The mRNA expression results also corroborated the role of GRP75-KD in alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction and ER stress under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 5H). Based on these findings, we further considered whether silencing GRP75, which alleviated cellular oxidative stress levels by inhibiting MAM formation, might also reduce the extent of ferroptosis under hypoxic conditions. We measured the levels of Fe2+, total glutathione, lipid peroxidation and MDA in primary hepatocytes. The results showed that although GRP75-KD had no effect on intracellular Fe2+ concentration (Fig. 5J), it significantly increased total glutathione content while reducing lipid peroxidation and MDA levels in primary hepatocytes under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 5, L–N). Meanwhile, GRP75-KD reduced the expression of Acsl4, which is involved in the generation of peroxidation-prone membrane phospholipids, while increasing expression of the negative regulator Gpx4 (Fig. 5K). Finally, the expression levels of key ferroptosis regulatory proteins further supported our hypothesis (Fig. 5I). In summary, our data indicated that GRP75-KD alleviated ferroptosis under hypoxic conditions by regulating mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum function via inhibition of MAM formation.

GRP75-OE promoted MAM formation and induced mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and ER stress

Next, we aimed to confirm whether overexpression of GRP75 exacerbates hypoxia-induced mitochondrial and ER dysfunction. We established the GRP75 overexpression model in ZFL cells. ER-mitochondria association was assessed by confocal microscopy after staining the mitochondria and ER with Mito-Tracker Green and ER-Tracker Red, respectively (Fig. 6A). The higher levels of colocalization of mitochondria and ER were observed in GRP75-OE-treated ZFL cells compared to the control and hypoxia groups (Fig. 6B). In addition, we found that GRP75-OE increased the expression of MAM components Ip3r1, Vdac1, and Mcu genes to varying degrees (Fig. 6, C–F). Consistent with previous findings in primary hepatocytes, GRP75-OE increased MAM contacts and induced mitochondrial Ca2+ accumulation by promoting the formation of the IP3R-GRP75-VDAC1 complex (Fig. 6G). The results of protein analysis also indicated that GRP75-OE increased the expression of MAM components in ZFL cells under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 6H). Discussion

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death characterized by iron dependency and driven by oxidative damage to cellular membranes (35, 36). Its biochemical execution hinges on two essential components: intracellular labile iron pools and polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA)-rich phospholipids, which serve as substrates for lipid peroxidation—a hallmark of ferroptotic demise (37, 38). Despite growing knowledge of these core metabolic determinants, it remains an open question whether additional biological processes, beyond iron regulation and lipid metabolism, actively participate in or influence the initiation, propagation, or suppression of ferroptosis (39, 40). Elucidating such broader regulatory networks may uncover novel therapeutic targets and deepen our understanding of ferroptosis in physiological and pathological contexts (37, 41), while also contributing to select targets for breeding fish strains with hypoxia tolerance by CRISPR/cas9 technique in aquaculture industry (42).

Here we discovered that hypoxic stress exacerbates hepatic ferroptosis in largemouth bass, accompanied by excessive formation of the mitochondria-associated ER membrane (MAM), through multi-omics integrated analysis and transmission electron microscopy. Although a higher abundance of mitochondria may contribute to more ER-mitochondria apposition, the significant increase in normalized MAM-related parameters indicated that hypoxia enhances ER-mitochondria coupling beyond a simple change in mitochondrial abundance. Together with the elevated expression and interaction of the IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 complex, these data support the notion that hypoxia promotes structural reinforcement of MAMs. Additionally, the formation of MAM mediated by IP3R1-GRP75-VDAC1 complex, provides a communication platform for ferroptosis signal propagation from ER to mitochondria. The transfer of Ca2+ from the ER to mitochondria plays a key role in this process. More importantly, silencing GRP75 disrupted MAM formation, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and protecting cells from hypoxia-induced oxidative damage. Therefore, we propose that MAM serves as a pivotal regulatory platform for ferroptosis by mediating organelle crosstalk between the ER and mitochondria.

Recent advances have refined our understanding of mitochondrial involvement in ferroptosis. While earlier studies established that genetic ablation or pharmacological inhibition of GPX4 triggers ferroptosis in a mitochondria-independent manner (15, 43), compelling evidence now demonstrates that mitochondria play a critical regulatory role in ferroptosis. The electron transport chain (ETC) inhibitors blocked both cystine starvation and cold stress-induced lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis (44, 45). Moreover, mitochondria-targeted reactive oxygen species scavengers inhibited ferroptosis and provided significant cellular protection (46). Mitoferrin 1 and 2 mediated mitochondrial iron overloads, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, altered mitochondrial dynamics, reduced ATP production, and increased ROS generation, ultimately triggering ferroptosis (47). Consequently, we posit that mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs) serve as a pivotal regulatory platform orchestrating ferroptosis execution by mediating inter-organelle crosstalk between the endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria.

The role of calcium in ferroptosis has garnered significant attention, although conclusions from related studies remain controversial. Previous studies have demonstrated that the calcium chelator BAPTA-AM protects cells from both chemically and genetically induced ferroptosis. Furthermore, both inhibiting SERCA to reduce calcium uptake into the ER and blocking calcium transfer from the ER to mitochondria strongly suppress ferroptosis (48). However it has also been shown that intervention with mitochondrial calcium uniporter inhibitor (MCU) protein inhibitors increases cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis (49). Calcium transfer from ER to mitochondria plays a pivotal role in maintaining mitochondria homeostasis and cell death activation (50). Here, we found that disrupting MAM formation by silencing GRP75 reduced mitochondrial Ca2+ levels, alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction and endoplasmic reticulum stress, and mitigated hypoxia-induced ferroptosis. Existing research has also confirmed that D-aspartate (D-Asp) regulates mitochondrial function and ER stress by stimulating the formation of MAM (51). In addition, both genetic intervention (promoting Sirt3 expression) and chemical treatment (Deoxynivalenol stimulation) exacerbated oxidative stress damage by causing mitochondrial dysfunction through mitochondrial calcium overload via the IP3R1/GRP75/VDAC1 complex (52, 53). GRP75 overexpression directly damages mitochondrial membrane potential, enhances mitochondrial Ca2+ levels and ROS production, increases MAM contacts, and induces cell death (54). Mitochondrial calcium overload enhances the tricarboxylic acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, thereby increasing mito-ROS production. This triggers lipid peroxidation via the Fenton reaction, which is crucial for inducing ferroptosis (55). Hence, our study elucidates a key signaling pathway in which, upon hypoxic stimulation, excessive Ca2+ transfer from the ER to mitochondria via MAM to cause mito-ROS production, accelerate lipid peroxidation, and ultimately trigger ferroptosis. Notably, the increase in total GPx activity observed under hypoxia does not necessarily contradict the reduction in Gpx4 expression. The GPx activity assay used in this study reflects the overall activity of the glutathione peroxidase family. Therefore, the elevated total GPx activity may represent a compensatory antioxidant response to hypoxia-induced oxidative stress (56). In contrast, Gpx4 has an important role in reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides within biological membranes and is considered a core suppressor of ferroptosis (57, 58). Thus, even in the presence of increased total GPx activity, reduced Gpx4 expression may still indicate impaired detoxification of membrane lipid peroxides and increased susceptibility to ferroptosis.

While MAM serves as a critical signaling hub for inter-organelle crosstalk that modulates mitochondrial function, their context-dependent role in either mitigating or exacerbating mitochondrial dysregulation-associated pathologies remains contested. Research demonstrates that an aberrant increase in hepatic GRP75-mediated MAM Ca2+-channeling complex formation promotes mitochondrial dysfunction in vitro and in male mouse model of alcohol-associated liver disease (59). In addition, Honrath et al. reported that GRP75 knockdown weakened ER-mitochondrial contacts, reduced mitochondrial ROS production, and improved cell survival under oxidative stress conditions (60). In contrast to our observations, emerging research presents contradictory evidence indicating that suppression of GRP75 expression compromises the structural stability of MAM. This disruption subsequently attenuates calcium flux between endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondrial compartments, exacerbates mitochondrial impairment induced by cytotoxic agents, and ultimately enhances apoptosis activation in ovarian carcinoma models following cisplatin exposure (61). Therefore, the role of MAM in modulating mitochondrial function requires careful investigation under each condition. Similar to our results, enhanced MAM promotes mitochondrial reactive oxygen species and fission while bolstering ferroptosis sensitivity in triple-negative breast cancer subtypes (24). This suggests that the expression of MAM-related genes may serve as a potential biomarker to predict sensitivity to ferroptosis in hypoxia.

There are, however, two major limitations in this study. In this study, we primarily focused on MAM-mediated mitochondrial calcium overload accelerating lipid peroxidation by inducing ROS production, thereby exacerbating hypoxia-induced ferroptosis. However, the role of interorganelle lipid transport events—specifically, the trafficking of phosphatidylserine into mitochondria and phosphatidylethanolamine out of mitochondria—in modulating hypoxia-induced ferroptosis requires further validation. Furthermore, we have primarily verified our current hypothesis in cell models subjected to hypoxia treatment. Although this does not preclude the applicability of the mechanistic conclusions in fish, the role of GRP75-mediated MAM formation in mediating ferroptosis and regulating fish hypoxic adaptation requires further confirmation in vivo experiments. This possibility is now being checked in our lab. Together, our present study concludes that MAM regulates ferroptosis by controlling calcium transfer from the ER to mitochondria, thereby inducing mito-ROS bursts. Thus, MAM could be harnessed to regulate cell vulnerability to ferroptosis under pathological or environmental stress. Our results have also identified key genes governing environmental resistance in fish, providing theoretical guidance for stress-resistant breeding in aquaculture amid escalating environmental challenges.

Experimental procedures

Ethical statement

The experimental procedures performed in largemouth bass followed the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (SICAU) and were approved by the Ethics Committee of SICAU (NO: 2023102022).

Experimental animals

The healthy juvenile largemouth bass were taken from a certain aquatic company in Qionglai City. The experimental protocols for commercial feed formulation, aquatic environment maintenance, and the culture and management of largemouth bass were described in our previous study (6). In brief, 54 uniform largemouth bass (initial body weight: 25.0 ± 1.0g [mean ± SD]) were randomly stocked in 6 recirculating tanks (600 L in water volume), with 9 fish in each tank. During the experiment, the fish were fed with commercially manufactured feed by Tongwei CO. at 8:30 and 17:30 daily.

Experimental protocol and sampling

The fish were divided into two groups: normoxia (Control) and acute hypoxic exposure (Hypoxia). In the Control group, the dissolved oxygen (DO) was maintained above 7.0 mg/L with an air pump. Based on our previous research (5), the DO levels of the Hypoxia group were adjusted to reach the target oxygen level (2.0 ± 0.2 mg/L) within 60 min by pumping N2 and air into a tank closed with plastic film. DO was continuously monitored using a portable water quality meter (Bante900P) during the 8-h experiment. At the end of the experiment, 9 fish were removed from each tank and anaesthetized with MS-222 (40 mg/L) for sampling. Three fish livers were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio) for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and immunofluorescence assay. The residual tissue samples were immediately preserved through snap-freezing in liquid nitrogen, followed by long-term storage at −80 °C to maintain biomolecular integrity for subsequent examinations.

siRNA transfection

Hepatocytes were isolated from the liver of largemouth bass and cultured based on protocols described in our recent publication (6). siRNAs targeting Grp75 (si-GRP75-1: 5′-ACACCACAATTCCCACAAA-3′; si-GRP75-2: 5′-CGAGAACATGGTCAAGAAT-3′; si-GRP75-3: 5′-CATCAGAAGGAGATAAGAA-3′) and negative control (si-NC) were synthesized by Guangzhou Ruibo Biotechnology Co, Ltd. The knockdown efficiency of the three siRNAs was evaluated initially, and si-GRP75-2 was selected for subsequent experiments (Fig. S4). siRNA transfections were performed using the riboFECTTM CP transfection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. MTT assay was used to determine the effect of hypoxia on the cell viability of hepatocytes. The cell culture experiments were repeated at least three times. For transfection, hepatocytes were transfected with 100 nmol/L siRNA for 48 h and treated in hypoxia environment for 12 h. Hypoxic conditions were created by using AnaeroPack system (A7, Mitsubishi Gas Chemistry), as previously described (62, 63).

Plasmid vector constructs

Zebrafish liver (ZFL) cells (CRL-2643, ATCC) were maintained in Ham's F-12 medium containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS, with incubation conditions set at 28 °C under 5% CO2 atmosphere. The LbGRP75 open reading frame (ORF) was cloned into the pcDNA3.1-GFP and pcDNA3.1-FLAG vectors to generate the plasmid that can express the LbGRP75 protein with GFP and FLAG tags, respectively. For transient transfection, the density of ZFL cells seeded in 12-well plates reached 80%, and the recombinant plasmid and its negative control (pcDNA3.1) were mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and added dropwise to the cell medium for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to hypoxic conditions for 12 h by the method described above. The detailed methods are described in Text S3.

RNA sequencing analysis

The utilized cDNA library construction protocols were described in previous publications (5). The quality of the constructed libraries was assessed with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. The evaluated libraries were sequenced by using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform with paired-end 150-bp reads. Subsequently, the reads were mapped to the largemouth bass Ensembl reference genome (GenBank: JAKUMD000000000.1). All the raw reads were deposited on the National Center for Biotechnology Information site (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/, CNP accession: CNP0004509). The methods for differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were based on the previous publications of our laboratory (33).

Metabolite extraction and detection

Protocols for extracting and assaying metabolites from the liver tissue of 12 largemouth bass according to previous publication (31, 64). Briefly, the extracted compounds were analyzed using LC-MS (UPLC, Waters Acquity I-Class PLUS; MS/MS; Waters Xevo G2-XS QTof). Primary and secondary mass spectrometry data were collected in MSe mode under the control of acquisition software (MassLynx V4.2, Waters). Progenesis QI software was used for data processing of peak extraction and peak alignment. Fold change (FC) > 1.50, p-Value < 0.05, and variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1 were used as the standard to identify differential metabolites. The protocols are detailed in Text S1.

Transmission electron microscopy

Upon collection, liver tissues and isolated hepatocytes were immediately subjected to fixation using a solution of 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.15 mol/L phosphate buffer. Following fixation, specimens underwent four thorough washing cycles with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being treated with 1% osmium tetroxide for post-fixation. Dehydration was then carried out through a graded ethanol series. Ultrathin sections were prepared from the embedded blocks and sequentially stained with uranyl acetate and pyridine ferric citrate. Finally, the prepared sections were visualized using transmission electron microscopy, and the ultrastructural features of mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum were analyzed and reconstructed through computational processing with ImageJ software (NIH).

Immunofluorescence staining and confocal scanning analysis

For mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum visualization, ZFL cells underwent sequential staining procedures. Cellular structures were labeled by incubating with Mito-Tracker Green (Beyotime, C1048) and ER-Tracker Red (Beyotime, C1041S) for 30 min at 28 °C. Subsequent nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI for 15 min. Following PBS washing, samples were maintained in phenol red-free RPMI-1640 medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) during microscopic examination. Subcellular organelle colocalization was assessed through confocal imaging performed on a Zeiss LSM800 system (Carl Zeiss). The immunofluorescence staining protocol for GRP75 (HUABIO, R-1510-39) protein was also as described previously (62).

RNA isolation and RT-qPCR

The RNA extraction kit (Foregene) was used to extract total RNA. RNA quality was detected by ND2000 (Thermo). Reverse transcription and RT-qPCR analysis were performed according to protocols described, based on recent publications (5). The detailed methods are shown in Text S2. Data analysis was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt threshold cycle method with β-actin and 18 s as the reference genes. The primer sequences used in the study are listed in Table S2.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) and western blot

Liver tissue and collected cell precipitate were digested in 500 μl RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF and protease/phosphatase inhibitor cocktail for 30 min on ice. The lysates were then centrifuged at 13,000g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected for Western blot analysis. Following electrophoresis separation through SDS-PAGE, protein specimens were electrotransferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were incubated with indicated primary antibodies followed by incubation with secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit; Zenbio, 511203 or anti-mouse; Bioss, bs-0296G-HRP; 1:20000 dilution). Signal acquisition was performed using the Gel Doc XR+ (Bio-Rad) documentation system.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) from MAM fractions was conducted using Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose. Cells/tissues were lysed in lysis buffer, and 500 ug protein/sample was incubated with the indicated primary antibody for 3 h. A/G PLUS-Agarose beads were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. After adding re-suspended beads at 4 °C for 30 min, the collected beads were washed and precipitated protein was examined by Western blotting. Primary antibodies used in the study are listed in the Table S3.

Biochemical analysis

The levels of ferrous iron in serum using the commercial Iron Assay Kit with Colorimetric (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Intracellular Fe2+ levels were quantified via RhoNox-6 staining (S1070S, Beyotime) (65). Briefly, cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with the working solution of RhoNox-6 under light-protected conditions at 28 °C for 30 min. After incubation, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader at Ex/Em = 545/585 nm. The fluorescence intensity was used to reflect relative intracellular Fe2+ levels. Using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), the glutathione peroxidase (GPx), reduced glutathione (GSH), lipid peroxidation (LPO), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), ATP levels, NAD+/NADH ratio, and free malondialdehyde fraction (MDA) were measured in both tissue and cells. The LPO assay kit measures the hydroperoxides directly utilizing the redox reactions with ferrous ions. All treatments were performed in triplicate in three independent experiments according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Lipid peroxidation in live cells was detected by the C11-BODIPY 581/591 fluorescence fluorescent probe (Beyotime Biotechnology Co). Briefly, cells were incubated with 2 μm C11-BODIPY 581/591 under light-protected conditions for 30 min, and fluorescence intensity was subsequently measured with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). The results were expressed as the ratio of oxidized to reduced fluorescence intensity (reduced form: Ex/Em 581/591 nm; oxidized form: Ex/Em 488/510 nm).

ROS, mitochondrial Ca2+ levels and membrane potential (MMP) measurement

Hepatic reactive oxygen species content was quantified through the DCFH oxidation assay (66). Fluorescent measurements were conducted with spectral parameters set at 485 nm for excitation and 538 nm for emission detection. Quantification was achieved by generating a standard curve with 2′,7′-dichloro-fluorescein reference solutions (concentration range: 0.1 nM to 1 μm), with final measurements normalized to total protein concentration and reported as nanomoles of DCF equivalent per milligram of protein.

Total cytosolic ROS (Cyto-ROS) levels were determined by the ROS Assay Kit (S0033S, Beyotime) with DCFH-DA according to the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, cells were washed with PBS, incubated with diluted (1:1000) stock solution at 28 °C for 20 min, and then measured by a fluorescence microplate reader (Ex/Em: 488/525 nm). Mitochondrial ROS (mito-ROS) level was determined by the MitoSOX Red kit (M36008, Invitrogen) with diluted stock solution (Ex/Em: 540/590 nm). The average fluorescence intensity was quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH). Values compared with the control were regarded as changes in mito-ROS levels.

For the determination of tissue Ca2+ levels, mitochondria were first isolated from the liver by mitochondrial isolation kit (C3606, Beyotime). Subsequently, the Ca2+ concentration was measured using the assay kit via colorimetric analysis (C004-2-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). Rhod-2, AM Ca2+ fluorescent dye (Cat. No. 21062, AAT Bioquest) was used to determine Ca2+ changes in the mitochondria within cells. JC-1 (M8650, Solarbio) was used for detecting MMP (Δψm) of fluorescent dyes in cells by fluorometer, based on recent publications (62).

Statistical analysis

SPSS 28.0 software was used to analyze experimental data. All the data were presented as the means ± standard deviation. Prior to parametric analysis, data distribution characteristics were verified through Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality testing and Levene's homoscedasticity evaluation. For multi-group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance was employed with Duncan's post hoc comparisons, while independent two-group analyses utilized unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests. The value of p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Graphical representations were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.2 (GraphPad Software Corporation).
