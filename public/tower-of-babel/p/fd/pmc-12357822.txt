Efficacy and safety of platinum-based, anthracycline-free neoadjuvant chemotherapy for triple-negative breast cancer: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yichao Du, Lu Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study examines the effectiveness of platinum-based anthracycline-free neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) combined with taxanes in treating triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).MethodsStudies published up to January 2025 were systematically retrieved from PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. A pooled analysis was conducted to assess the pathological complete response (pCR) rate, adverse events (AEs), and survival outcomes. Subgroup analyses were performed to examine the influence of different chemotherapy regimens and patient characteristics on treatment efficacy.ResultsA total of 30 studies involving 3,637 TNBC patients were included in the analysis. The pooled overall survival (OS) rate was 69.92% (95% confidence interval (CI): 41.65%-88.33%), with 2-year, 3-year, and 5-year OS rates of 95.38% (95% CI: 86.64%-98.50%), 94.63% (95% CI: 87.91%-97.71%), and 86.97% (95% CI: 80.76%-91.39%), respectively. Among secondary outcomes, the pooled disease-free survival (DFS) rate was 77.53% (95% CI: 69.54%-83.91%), and the pCR rate was 46.45% (95% CI: 42.55%-50.39%). The most frequently reported hematologic AEs included anemia, neutropenia, and leukopenia, while the most common non-hematologic AEs were nausea and vomiting, along with elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST). Subgroup analyses demonstrated that the combination of docetaxel and carboplatin achieved a higher pCR rate (52.00%) compared to paclitaxel plus carboplatin (42.00%). Concerning geographic origin, the highest pCR rate was observed in patients from the United States (50.00%), followed by Germany (46.00%) and China (40.00%). In terms of OS, the highest survival rate was reported with paclitaxel plus lobaplatin (94.00%), followed by carboplatin (80.00%) and cisplatin (45.00%).ConclusionPlatinum-based anthracycline-free NAC regimens combined with taxanes appear to be both effective and well-tolerated in patients with TNBC. Nevertheless, in the absence of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) directly comparing this approach to anthracycline-based regimens, further investigation is warranted to substantiate these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03435-w.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a subtype of breast cancer featuring the absence of estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PgR) expression, as well as overexpression or gene amplification of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (ERBB2/HER2) [1]. TNBC represents around 15–20% of breast cancer diagnoses and is more commonly observed in younger female patients [2]. In comparison to other subtypes of breast cancer, TNBC typically exhibits less favorable long-term outcomes, with overall survival(OS) rates of approximately 83% at three years and 75% at five years [3]. Notably, the mortality rate of advanced TNBC within the first five years post-diagnosis can reach as high as 40% [4]. Due to the absence of relevant receptor expression, TNBC is inherently insensitive to molecularly targeted and endocrine therapies, rendering its treatment options relatively limited, primarily consisting of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. However, significant advancements have been achieved in TNBC management, particularly through the implementation of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), which has increased the proportion of patients eligible for breast-conserving surgery and axillary downstaging procedures, thereby lowering tumor burden and ameliorating long-term outcomes [5].

Despite its aggressive nature, TNBC exhibits a high sensitivity to cytotoxic chemotherapy, and regimens based on taxanes and anthracyclines have become the standard approach in neoadjuvant treatment [6]. Taxane-based agents (e.g., paclitaxel, docetaxel), as microtubule stabilizers, play a critical role in inhibiting cell division and have demonstrated substantial efficacy in the treatment of various malignancies [7]. In particular, within the NAC setting, taxane-based regimens have shown the potential to enhance pathological complete response (pCR) rates, which is closely linked to better survival outcomes [8]. Anthracycline-based chemotherapeutic agents, including doxorubicin, epirubicin, pirarubicin, and daunorubicin, have demonstrated notable effectiveness in treating hematologic malignancies and solid tumors. Studies have reported that TNBC patients receiving NAC regimens incorporating cyclophosphamide and taxanes in addition to standard anthracycline-based therapy achieve a pCR rate of approximately 30–40% [9]. However, despite the effectiveness of anthracycline-based treatments in TNBC, accumulating evidence has highlighted their potential to induce cardiotoxicity and myelosuppression through various mechanisms, which may result in long-term adverse effects such as heart failure or secondary hematologic malignancies [10, 11]. Given the severity of these risks, there has been growing interest among clinical researchers in examining anthracycline-free preoperative treatment protocols for treating TNBC.

In recent years, platinum-based agents, including cisplatin, carboplatin, and lobaplatin, have been increasingly utilized in the neoadjuvant treatment of TNBC. A meta-analysis involving 1,345 patients proved that the incorporation of carboplatin into NAC protocols led to a notable rise in the pCR rate, elevating it from 35.9%-49.1% [12]. More evidence supports the incorporation of platinum-based agents into most TNBC NAC protocols [13]. Studies have also evaluated the efficacy of combined carboplatin and taxane regimens, revealing favorable pCR rates along with reduced toxicity and lower financial burden [14–16]. Furthermore, patients achieving pCR with platinum-taxane regimens have exhibited excellent three-year follow-up outcomes, even in the absence of additional anthracycline-based therapy [17, 18].

Given the growing clinical interest in substituting anthracyclines with platinum-based agents in combination with taxanes for TNBC treatment, as well as the inconsistencies in findings across different studies regarding their efficacy, this study aims to conduct a meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the impact of platinum-taxane NAC regimens, with or without anthracyclines, on TNBC outcomes. Specifically, this analysis will assess their effects on patient survival, pCR rates, and the occurrence of adverse events (AEs). Through a systematic review of these key parameters, our study seeks to provide a robust scientific foundation for TNBC treatment strategies and present valuable evidence-based guidance for clinical practitioners.

Materials and methods

Registration

Our meta-analysis has been registered on PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO) under the CRD420251004117. The study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [19].

Retrieval strategy

PubMed, Cochrane Library, EMBASE, and Web of Science were thoroughly retrieved. The search focused on studies investigating the effects of anthracycline-free taxane-platinum NAC regimens in patients with TNBC. The search period spanned from the inception of each database to January 2025. The search terms included MeSH terms and free-text words related to TNBC, NAC, and preoperative chemotherapy. Detailed search strategies are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Literature screening and data extraction

EndNote 20 was employed as the reference management tool to store and organize the initial search results. Two researchers independently screened the retrieved records as per the predetermined eligibility criteria. Titles and abstracts were reviewed, which was followed by full-text assessments to determine eligibility.

The following data were extracted independently by two researchers: general study characteristics: First author, year of publication, country, study design, sample size, detailed NAC regimen, age, and follow-up duration; outcome measures: OS rate, DFS rate, pCR rate, and incidence of AEs.

Any discrepancies arising during the screening or data extraction process were addressed via consultation with an additional investigator.

Quality assessment

Two researchers independently assessed the study quality. Dissents were settled after discussion with a third investigator.

For randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0) assisted in quality assessment [20]. RoB 2.0 evaluates five domains of bias: bias arising from the randomization process, deviations from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and reported result selection. Each was categorized as “low”, “some,” or “high” risk of bias.

For non-randomized studies, the Methodological Index for Non-Randomized Studies (MINORS) was leveraged for quality evaluation [21]. The maximum score attainable for single-arm studies was 16, while those incorporating a control group could achieve up to 24. According to MINORS, single-arm studies scoring below 8 and controlled studies scoring below 12 were considered low-quality; scores of 9–12 for single-arm studies or 13–18 for controlled studies indicated moderate quality; and scores of 13–16 for single-arm studies or 19–24 for controlled studies indicated high quality.

For cohort studies, study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The assessment criteria encompassed three domains: selection of study participants, comparability between groups, and ascertainment of outcomes. A score of ≥ 7 stars was considered indicative of high quality, 4–6 stars of moderate quality, and < 4 stars of low quality.

For cohort studies, study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [22]. The assessment criteria encompassed three domains: selection of study participants, comparability between groups, and ascertainment of outcomes. A score of ≥ 7 stars was considered indicative of high quality, 4–6 stars of moderate quality, and < 4 stars of low quality.

Statistical analysis

Data synthesis and statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2) and RStudio (version 2024.04.2–764).

The I² statistic [23] and Cochran’s Q test [24]were employed for examining heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was considered significant if the I² value exceeded 50% and the Q test yielded a p below 0.05, leading to the adoption of a random-effects model. If these criteria were not met, a fixed-effects model was employed. The synthesized findings, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were visually represented via forest plots. Pre-specified subgroup analyses were performed based on the specific chemotherapy agents and the countries of origin of the included populations. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger’s test [25]. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed, including sequential exclusion of each study, to examine the stability of combined results. P < 0.05 denoted statistical significance. All numerical results were reported to two decimal places.

Results

Screening results

16,268 articles were identified. After excluding 6,531 duplicates, 9,653 articles were eliminated following an initial screening of titles and abstracts. The remaining 84 articles underwent full-text screening, resulting in the exclusion of 54 articles. Ultimately, 30 studies [15–18, 26–51] were eligible. Quality assessment results

For RCTs, quality assessment was conducted following the guidelines of the RoB 2.0 tool. The included RCTs exhibited a certain level of risk, primarily in the selective reporting domain, as it was not possible to determine whether multiple analytical methods were employed in each study. The overall assessment results are presented in Supplementary Fig. 1, while detailed evaluations of the five domains are provided in Supplementary Fig. 2.

For cohort studies, quality was evaluated through the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). The encompassed cohort studies did not receive scores for non-exposed cohort selection. Furthermore, regarding study comparability, given that all outcomes investigated in this study were rates, certain comparability criteria were deemed inapplicable. Except for the study by Riccardo Lobefaro et al. [47], no included studies received scores for comparability. Detailed assessment results are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

For non-randomized studies, MINORS was utilized for evaluation. Two studies were single-arm studies, while three studies included a control group. Among these, only one study [30]was classified as of moderate quality, whereas all others were considered of relatively high quality. Assessment results are detailed in Supplementary Table 2.

Meta-analysis results

Two-year OS

Two studies reported the two-year OS rate in patients with TNBC who received anthracycline-free NAC regimens. Given the limited number of studies, the preliminary pooled analysis estimated a two-year OS rate of 95.38% (95% CI: 86.64%-98.50%; I² = 0.00%, P_H = 0.65). Further details are displayed in Fig. 2(a).

Three-year OS

Four studies reported the three-year OS rate in the TNBC population who received anthracycline-free NAC regimens. The preliminary pooled analysis estimated a three-year OS rate of 94.63% (95% CI: 87.91%-97.71%; I² = 62.00%, P_H = 0.05). Further details are illustrated in Fig. 2(b).

Five-year OS

Five studies reported the five-year OS rate in patients with TNBC who underwent anthracycline-free NAC regimens. Our preliminary pooled analysis estimated a five-year OS rate of 86.97% (95% CI: 80.76%-91.39%; I² = 81.30%, P_H = 0.00). Further details are provided in Fig. 2(c).

A subgroup analysis stratified by the country of origin of the study population revealed that the pooled five-year OS rate was 91.00% (95% CI: 82.00%-95.00%; I² = 82.30%, P_H = 0.02) for studies conducted in China, 81.00% (95% CI: 74.00%-86.00%; I² = 44.10%, P_H = 0.18) for those conducted in Germany, and 85.00% (95% CI: 81.00%-88.00%) for a single study comprising populations from Spain and the United States. In the subgroup analysis, statistically significant differences in five-year OS rates did not exist among subgroups (P_intergroup = 0.12). Further details are provided in Supplementary Fig. 3.

A subgroup analysis based on specific chemotherapy regimens showed that two studies employing NAC with docetaxel plus carboplatin yielded a pooled five-year OS rate of 85.00% (95% CI: 82.00%-88.00%; I² = 0.00%, P_H = 0.48), while three studies utilizing paclitaxel plus carboplatin had a pooled five-year OS rate of 87.00% (95% CI: 77.00%-93.00%; I² = 90.20%, P_H = 0.00). The subgroup analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in five-year OS rates across chemotherapy regimens (P_intergroup = 0.75). Further details are provided in Supplementary Fig. 4.

OS

Nine studies reported the OS rates in individuals with TNBC who received an anthracycline-free NAC regimen. This study defined “OS rates” as the percentage of individuals were still alive among TNBC patients who received an anthracycline-free NAC regimen after the last update of the original study data.

The pooled analysis demonstrated a OS rate of 69.92% (95% CI: 41.65%-88.33%; I² = 97.20%, P_H < 0.01). Detailed information is presented in Fig. 2(d).

Sensitivity analysis indicated that the study by Ruo-Xi Wang affected the stability of our results. After this study was excluded, the pooled OS rate increased to 75.43% (95% CI: 47.97%-91.09%; I² = 96.00%, P_H < 0.01). Further details are provided in Supplementary Fig. 5. Although nine studies were included, the statistical software did not generate results for the Egger test.

Subgroup analysis based on the country of origin of the study population revealed that when the cohort was derived from China, the pooled OS rate was 73.00% (95% CI: 44.00%-91.00%; I² = 97.50%, P_H < 0.01). Qualitative description of NAC regimens involving taxanes combined with platinum agents with or without anthracyclines

Priyanka Sharma et al. (2020) conducted an RCT in which patients were randomly assigned to one of two groups: Group A received a NAC regimen consisting of carboplatin plus paclitaxel followed by doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide; Group B received carboplatin combined with docetaxel as NAC. The study was designed as an RCT. The results showed that the pCR rate in Group A was 54.00% (95% CI: 40.00%-69.00%), and the OS rate estimated by Kaplan-Meier curves was 79.59%. In Group B, the pCR rate was also 54.00% (95% CI: 40.00%-68.00%), while the Kaplan-Meier estimated OS rate was 90.90%.

When compared with the findings of the present meta-analysis, the study by Priyanka Sharma et al. reported that patients in Group A, who received the neoadjuvant regimen containing carboplatin, paclitaxel, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide, achieved a pCR rate of 54.00% and an OS rate of 79.59%. Both outcomes were higher than those derived from our meta-analysis, which indicated a pCR rate of 46.45% and an OS rate of 69.92% among patients receiving anthracycline-free regimens.

Discussion

Our study represents the first systematic analysis exploring the efficacy and safety of an anthracycline-free taxane-platinum regimen in patients suffering from TNBC. In this meta-analysis, data from 30 studies encompassing 3,637 TNBC patients were synthesized. The results showed an OS rate of 75.43%, with 2-, 3-, and 5-year OS rates of 95.38%, 94.63%, and 86.97%, respectively. The 5-year DFS rate was 81.78%, and the pooled pCR rate was 46.45%.

However, there is currently inconsistent evidence regarding the comparative efficacy of anthracycline-free taxane, platinum regimens versus those containing anthracyclines. In the BrighTNess trial, Loibl et al. reported a pCR rate of 58% among patients who received paclitaxel combined with carboplatin, followed by doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide, a rate that exceeded the pooled pCR identified in our analysis [52]. Similarly, the GeparSixto trial found that incorporating carboplatin into a treatment protocol involving paclitaxel and non-pegylated liposomal doxorubicin resulted in an elevation of the pCR rate from 43% to 57% among TNBC patients [53]. These findings suggest that anthracycline-containing regimens may be associated with a higher pCR rate. However, the aforementioned studies did not involve direct head-to-head comparisons with the “taxane plus platinum” regimen. Critically, the present analysis included only one RCT that directly compared these two regimens. That study reported comparable pCR rates for both regimens, each achieving 54.00%. According to Kaplan-Meier estimates, the anthracycline-containing regimen was associated with higher event-free survival and OS. However, the statistical significance of these differences remains unclear. Given that only a single RCT was identified during the literature search, the current study included only the single-arm data from Group B (carboplatin plus docetaxel) in Sharma’s study as the representative for the anthracycline-free regimen. Therefore, this study does not provide sufficient evidence to determine whether NAC without anthracyclines is superior or inferior to anthracycline-containing regimens in terms of pCR. In terms of safety, the present study identified leukopenia and neutropenia as the most common hematologic toxicities, while nausea and vomiting were the most frequently observed non-hematologic toxicities. Notably, hepatotoxicity, as indicated by elevations in ALT and AST with an incidence as high as 34%, should not be overlooked. Importantly, the included RCT [15] explicitly reported that the carboplatin plus docetaxel regimen was associated with a significantly lower risk of severe long-term toxicity compared to anthracycline-containing regimens. Moreover, the incidence of grade 3–4 AEs, particularly anemia (46%) and neutropenia (60%), was markedly higher in the anthracycline-containing group. In summary, although the available evidence for direct comparisons of efficacy is limited and the anthracycline-free regimen may not offer a clear survival advantage, it is associated with significantly reduced treatment-related toxicity. Accordingly, clinical decision-making may reasonably prioritize the anthracycline-free regimen, which, while maintaining therapeutic efficacy, offers improved treatment tolerability and better quality of life for patients.

This study conducted a subgroup analysis of the pCR rate based on differences in specific therapeutic agents and patient populations across different countries. Furthermore, this study explored the pCR rates linked to two distinct chemotherapy regimens: docetaxel plus carboplatin and paclitaxel plus carboplatin. Our findings demonstrated that the pCR rate observed with the docetaxel-carboplatin combination was higher compared to that achieved with the paclitaxel-carboplatin treatment (52% vs. 42%). Hao Tian et al. [54] reported that the pharmacological mechanisms of docetaxel plus platinum-based agents differ from those of paclitaxel plus platinum-based agents, which may help explain the statistically significant variance in pCR rates noted across two chemotherapy regimens. Two previous meta-analyses suggested that taxane-based and docetaxel-based regimens exhibit comparable objective response rates, or that nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel achieves a higher pCR rate than conventional taxanes (paclitaxel and docetaxel) [55, 56]. However, these prior meta-analyses did not differentiate between specific chemotherapy regimens and focused on breast cancer patients as a whole. Based on the foregoing subgroup analysis, our study also examined five-year survival rates and OS outcomes. In terms of OS, differences were identified among taxane-based regimens combined with different platinum-based agents. The OS rates were ranked as follows: paclitaxel combined with lobaplatin (94%), paclitaxel combined with carboplatin (80%), and paclitaxel combined with cisplatin (45%). Subgroup analysis demonstrated statistically significant differences among these therapeutic regimens. A recent meta-analysis comparing six treatment strategies, including carboplatin at an area under the curve (AUC) of 5, AUC of 6, AUC of 2, and AUC of 1.5; cisplatin at 75 mg/m²; and standard anthracycline- and/or taxane-based chemotherapy, suggested that the incorporation of carboplatin at an AUC of 5 into standard neoadjuvant chemotherapy may confer notable improvements in pCR and DFS, while maintaining a relatively lower risk of toxicity [57]. Furthermore, as a third-generation platinum-based agent, lobaplatin has demonstrated superior efficacy compared with carboplatin in NAC for metastatic breast, ovarian, and esophageal cancers [19, 22, 58–60]. Therefore, our findings possibly provide valuable insights for clinical decision-making.

Additionally, our study identified variations in pCR rates among the countries of origin of the included populations, with the highest pCR rate observed in the American cohort, followed by the German and Chinese cohorts. Concerning OS, subgroup analysis based on population differences also demonstrated that the OS rate was higher in the U.S. group than in the Chinese group. A study comparing breast cancer characteristics between Chinese and U.S. populations suggested that Chinese breast cancer patients are diagnosed at a later stage than their U.S. counterparts, which may contribute to differences in clinical management and lower five-year survival rates. Furthermore, significant differences were observed in tumor dimension, lymph node metastasis, and age at the time of diagnosis. These factors may account for the discrepancies in pCR rates observed across different countries for taxane-platinum NAC regimens [61].

As a literature-based meta-analysis, this study has several noteworthy limitations. First, the number of studies addressing various outcome measures remains relatively small, which may have resulted in limited statistical power for certain subgroup analyses. Consequently, the magnitude of the effects may have been overestimated or underestimated. Second, while the results of the subgroup analyses hold some clinical relevance, they are not derived from head-to-head comparative studies. Third, one of our initial objectives was to elucidate the efficacy of platinum-taxane NAC regimens, with or without anthracyclines, in treating TNBC. However, only one RCT meeting these criteria was retrieved. Although two additional studies evaluating the addition of anthracyclines after platinum-taxane therapy were mentioned in the discussion, their primary research objectives were not aligned with those of this study. As a result, only a qualitative review could be carried out.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that an anthracycline-free NAC regimen combining taxanes with platinum agents achieves a pCR rate of 46.45% and a survival rate of 69.92% in patients with TNBC. Notably, compared to anthracycline-containing regimens, this protocol significantly reduces the incidence of severe AEs, with the highest grade 3/4 hematologic toxicity (neutropenia) occurring in only 19% of patients. Therefore, for TNBC patients who are unable to tolerate the toxicity of anthracyclines or require avoidance of their cumulative long-term toxicities, this regimen represents a feasible and less toxic alternative. However, given the current lack of definitive head-to-head comparative evidence, its equivalence or superiority to the standard anthracycline-containing regimen remains to be confirmed in large-scale RCTs.
