A Novel Atraumatic Polymer-Assisted Peripheral Nerve Repair Device Compared With Microsurgical Neurorrhaphy
Authors: Diep Nguyen, Estelle Collin, Ricardo de Miguel, Edouard Reyes-Gomez, Ajul Shah, Kyle R Eberlin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate the ability of a polymer-assisted device to repair transected peripheral nerve in comparison to microsuture neurorrhaphy.MethodsTwenty rat sciatic nerves were transected and repaired using either the polymer-assisted system or microsutures. Peripheral nerve functional recovery was evaluated through nerve conduction analysis performed at 1, 2, and 3 months. The gastrocnemius muscle mass ratio was measured. Peripheral nerve samples were imaged using microcomputed tomography. Local tissue response and nerve repair (including axonal density) were assessed through histology.ResultsNo significant differences in functional outcomes were observed between the polymer-assisted system and microsutures. Both groups showed signs of nerve conduction as early as 2 month, which increased by the 3 month end point where muscle regrowth reached 60% of the nonoperated contralateral leg. Microcomputed tomography and histological analysis revealed a trend of increased intraneural fibrosis and a significantly lower axonal density when microsutures were used for the repair (P < .05).ConclusionsThe polymer-assisted system may reduce intraneural fibrosis compared to traditional microsutures, whereas functional recovery was found to be equivalent.Clinical relevanceThe polymer-assisted nerve repair device provides an alternative to microsutures without inducing additional trauma to the damaged peripheral nerve ends because of the nonpenetrative nature of the device.Key words: Atraumatic, Peripheral nerve repair, Poly(glycerol-co-sebacate)acrylate, Sutureless

Peripheral nerve injuries affect approximately 200,000 patients each year in the United States.1 These injuries can result in a loss of function and lead to chronic pain, and they can significantly impact patients’ quality of life and impose a substantial socioeconomic burden.2, 3, 4 Despite extensive research for novel treatments and advancements in microsurgical techniques, the functional outcomes of peripheral nerve repair (PNR) are often unsatisfactory with limited recovery achieved.5, 6, 7, 8, 9

Microsuture neurorrhaphy is currently considered the gold standard in clinical practice for PNR.10,11 However, this technique presents challenges, especially because of the need for surgical proficiency.9,12 Moreover, the inherent traumatic nature of the microsuturing process, the variability in microsuture depth of penetration, as well as the presence of a foreign body within the nerve can cause further tissue damage and create tension at the repair site.6,13,14 Tissue damage and tension are associated with increased inflammation, decreased vascularization, and scar tissue formation that can hinder the healing process, disrupt the proper alignment of nerve fibers, and impair functional recovery, ultimately resulting in poor clinical outcomes and the potential for long-term neuropathic pain.8,15, 16, 17, 18, 19

Various surgical techniques and innovative technologies have been developed to overcome those issues. Techniques like connector-assisted repair, which involves placing microsutures into a device away from the nerve coaptation, demonstrated potential to minimize excessive scar formation, reduce tension at the repair site, and improve clinical outcomes.19, 20, 21, 22 Nevertheless, the use of microsuture still pose the risk of trauma to the injured nerve and can be technically challenging.13,23 Another approach involves the use of fibrin glue, which is sometimes used off-label as an atraumatic solution for PNR. However, the results of using fibrin glue have been mixed at both the preclinical and clinical level, primarily because of its lack of strength, which can lead to potential dehiscence at the coaptation.9 Despite their limitations, these techniques highlight the potential benefits of atraumatic and tensionless PNR.8,9,19,23,24

An atraumatic, easy-to-use solution is still needed to support PNR. To address this clinical need, we developed a novel polymer-assisted PNR device using a photoactivated polymer derived from a poly (glycerol-co-gebacate) acrylate [PGSA] backbone.25 This device aims to protect the repair site and support axonal growth. It is composed of a coaptation chamber in which nerve ends are inserted and a light-activated polymer that secures the chamber to the nerve. We have previously demonstrated that this polymer-assisted device provides repairs with a mechanical strength equivalent to that of microsuture repairs.26 Furthermore, PGSA is an elastomeric material designed to provide stability and structural integrity within a mechanically dynamic environment, while complying to surrounding soft tissues. The biodegradation rate of the material can be tuned. Degradation occurs over time primarily through hydrolysis, resulting in a progressive reduction in crosslink density and associated modulus.27

This study aims to compare the safety and performance of the proposed polymer-assisted PNR device with microsuture neurorrhaphy in a rat model of a transected sciatic nerve (Fig. 1).

Materials and Methods

Polymer-assisted PNR device

The polymer-assisted PNR device presented in this study, developed by TISSIUM, is composed of two different components: a three-dimensional-printed coaptation chamber and a light-activated polymer. Both components are based in PGSA.25,27

The three-dimensional-printed coaptation chamber aims to guide the connection of severed nerve ends, relive tension at the coaptation site, and protect the repair to promote nerve repair.8 It is designed as a translucent tubular wrap that facilitates the insertion of nerve ends and allows nerve ends approximation and fascicular alignment. The light-activated polymer, containing FD&C Blue (erioglaucine disodium salt, Merck) is a viscous liquid pre-filled in a single-use syringe, and used to affix the three-dimensional-printed coaptation chamber to both proximal and distal nerve ends. Surgical instruments, which include a syringe tip and a silicone applicator, are used to consistently apply and mold the light-activated polymer around the nerve–coaptation chamber interface. The procedural steps for performing the repair using the polymer-assisted PNR device are summarized in Figure 1. Briefly, the nerve ends are inserted into the coaptation chamber on each side and the two nerve ends were approximated. The light-activated polymer is applied circumferentially at both proximal and distal nerve/coaptation chamber interfaces and polymerized in situ using a blue light pen (405 nm light-emitting diode). One cycle of illumination (30 seconds) within a 2 cm working distance is then applied on all surfaces of the light-activated polymer. Upon activation, the polymer binds to the underlying nerve tissue and coaptation chamber.

Surgical procedures

Twenty 2-month-old Wistar female rats (Janvier Labs) weighing between 205 and 233 g at the time of implantation were used in this study. All animal procedures were approved by the Local Animal Ethics Committee and were carried out in accordance with the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals as well as with Directive 2010/63/EU of the European Parliament. All animals were housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled room with a 12-hour/12-hour day/night cycle with access to food ad libitum.

Rats were subcutaneously administrated buprenorphine (Buprecare, Axience) (0.05 mg/kg) and meloxicam (2 mg/kg) (Metacam, CentraVet) prior to anesthesia under isoflurane gas (Axience). A local anesthesia was performed using lidocaine (Laocaine, MSD) on the surrounding muscles and the sciatic nerve. A dermal incision of approximately 2 cm was made on the lateral side of the right leg to expose the superficial gluteal muscle and the femoral bicep, which were then spread to expose the sciatic nerve for transection. Rats underwent unilateral sciatic nerve transection on the right leg and underwent nerve repair with either the polymer-assisted device (n = 10) or microsutures (n = 10) (Fig. 2A). Polymer-assisted repairs followed the procedure described in paragraph 2.1. Microsuture repairs were performed using two epineural stitches with 9-0 microsutures (ETHILON 2819G BV130-3, Ethicon Inc) (Fig. 2B). Muscle and skin were then closed with a 6-0 polypropylene suture (Ethicon Inc).

Nerve conduction studies

Nerve conduction studies were performed at 1 month, 2 months, and at terminal 3 months post implantation using a neurodiagnostics system (Viking UltraPro S100, NATUS, Mag2Health). Animals were anesthetized under isoflurane and motor nerve conduction studies were performed using subdermal electrodes (Ultra Disposable Subdermal Needle Electrodes, NATUS, Mag2Health). Recordings were captured on the interdigital muscle of the plantar flexor, whereas stimulation was performed at the tibial nerve of the ankle and as well as the sciatic notch at the hip, as previously reported.28

Necropsy

Three months postimplantation, animals were killed. Gross morphology of the operative leg was evaluated by a European Board of Veterinary Specialisation-certified veterinary specialists in pathology. The repaired nerve and surrounding tissues were then explanted and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (histology grade, VWR) for a minimum of 24 hours. The gastrocnemius muscle of both hind legs were harvested and weighed to assess muscle atrophy. The gastrocnemius muscle mass ratio of the operated leg to the contralateral leg was calculated for each animal.

Microcomputed tomography imaging

Sample preparation for microcomputed tomography imaging was adapted from Thompson et al.29 Briefly, following fixation, tissues were transferred into an iodine solution (Lugol solution, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated under agitation for 72 hours at 37 °C prior to scanning. The samples were then scanned using a high-resolution x-ray microcomputed tomography (Quantum FX, Perkin Elmer). After scanning, imaging data were reconstructed and processed using computer software (DataViewer, Bruker).

Histology

Prior to histology, peripheral nerve samples (n = 5) used for microcomputed tomography imaging were washed using phosphate buffered saline (Thermo Fischer Scientific) with 2% sodium thiosulfate (VWR) for iodine removal. Samples were then placed back in formalin 10% until histological processing. Histological processing focused the trimming to visualize a sample at the nerve coaptation site (identified as the midpoint of the chamber or at the suture placement), and a sample of the nerve distal to the coaptation, roughly 3.75 mm away. The distal trimming location was standardized across both groups to ensure consistency. Both sections were embedded in paraffin wax, and slides were cut at a thickness of 2–4 μm and routinely stained with hematoxylin-eosin for routine evaluation, Masson’s trichrome for targeting collagen or immunolabeled with antibodies against NF200 (NEFH/NF200; Invitrogen) for targeting axons. Histopathology evaluation was performed according to ISO 10993-6 by an European Board of Veterinary Specialisation-certified veterinary specialists in pathology.

Histomorphometry analyses were performed on NF200 immunolabeled sections in all the animals to quantify the number of axons per slide. Slides were whole-slide images scanned using an Olympus Slideview VS200 slides scanner using the VS-264C camera and the 40× objective. Histomorphometry evaluation was conducted in QuPath, quantitative pathology and bioimage analysis software, version 0.4.3.30 Nerve surface area was annotated as the region of interest. A machine-learned random forest classifier was employed for the detection of degenerated and healthy axons.

Statistical analysis

Data are represented as mean ± standard deviation. Data normality was assessed using a Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analysis was conducted using either a one-tailed Student t test or an analysis of variance with Tukey’s multiple comparison when data are found to be normal. Non-parametric testing was completed using a Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was considered at P < .05.

Results

Animal follow-up

No abnormal behavior was recorded in either experimental group. Autotomy, characterized by animals gnawing on their distal paw, was observed in several animals of both experimental groups. This is expected following nerve transection and arises from the absence of normal sensory feeback.31,32 As a result, one animal of the polymer-assisted PNR group was killed early because of autotomy observations and was not included in the analysis.

Macroscopic and microscopic evaluation of local tissue response

Coaptations in continuity were reported for all animals for both polymer-assisted and microsuture repair groups (Fig. 2C). At macroscopic level, neither evidence of inflammation nor necrosis was noted around the repairs performed with either the polymer-assisted PNR device or microsutures. The repair device was surrounded by a fascia-like tissue that enabled gliding movement of the coaptation along the tissue planes.

Histology was further conducted to characterize the local tissue response (Fig. 3). A thin fibrotic layer was observed surrounding the nerve tissue in contact with the inner wall of the device. Microscopic analysis revealed no signs of necrosis in the surrounding tissues related to either implant, with comparable degree of fibrosis in both groups. A limited tissue response was observed within the nerve tissue for the polymer-assisted device. A more pronounced host reaction within the nerve was noted for the microsutures group when compared with the polymer-assisted repairs.

Overall, both polymer-assisted device and microsutures demonstrated an acceptable local tolerance with a higher host reaction within the nerve for the microsuture group.

Nerve repair and functional recovery

Motor nerve conduction signals via electrophysiology were conducted to assess nerve repair and functional recovery. Signals were detected as early as month 2 for all animals independent of the tested group (Fig. 4A, B). A gradual improvement overtime of the motor nerve conduction velocity and compound muscle action potential was observed with no significant difference detected between polymer-assisted and microsuture repairs at the 3-month time-period (P > .05).

Division of the sciatic nerve led to denervation of the gastrocnemius muscle, resulting in an apparent muscle atrophy in both groups. By 3 months, a recovery of approximately 60% relative to the contralateral leg was observed, confirming functional nerve recovery and demonstrating muscle reinnervation independent of the tested group (P > .05) (Fig. 4C).

Coaptation imaging and intraneural fibrosis

Microcomputed tomography of the polymer-assisted repairs demonstrated a repaired nerve cable with a relatively uniform diameter along the repair (Fig. 5A). In contrast, imaging from microsuture repairs show a suture line at the coaptation site and was presented with a visibly thicker nerve tissue cable.33 At this level, based on the histopathological findings, nerve fiber degeneration was lower in the polymer-assisted repairs than in the microsuture group, as measured by axonal swelling. Though both groups show myelinated axons, axonal density when using microsutures was significantly lower than for polymer-assisted repair (P >.05) (Fig. 5B). In particular, in areas near the microsutures, intraneural fibrosis and inflammatory responses (Fig. 5C) were noted. These findings were less pronounced for the polymer-assisted repairs. Axonal density distal to the repair site was comparable between the two groups (P >.05) (Fig. S1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org).

Discussion

In 1979, Millesi34 observed a correlation between the advancements in PNR techniques and the improved surgical outcomes facilitated by the advent of modern microsurgery techniques. Despite these advancements, there has been little innovation in the field of PNR.8,12,35 Both preclinical and clinical research continue to point out the shortcomings of microsuture repairs with inconsistent, heterogenous, and often unsatisfactory results linked to patient, type of injury, and techniques used for repair.8,9,12,19 Microsutures, in particular, have been associated with impaired PNR and neuroma formation because of several factors: (1) localized trauma to the nerve and reactive scarring because of foreign material at the coaptation site, (2) concentration of tension around the repair site, and (3) impaired fascicular alignment while approximating nerve ends.8,13,19,35,36 The use of fibrin glue, selected for its atraumatic nature, is often limited because of the reporting of recurrent dehiscence given its lack of fixation strength and fast biodegradation.9,37

In this study, we demonstrate that the polymer-assisted device presents sufficient strength to maintain the coaptation, is biocompatible and supports peripheral nerve regeneration across a nerve repair site (Fig. 2). At a microscopic level, limited inflammation was observed within the nerve, and the surrounding tissues exhibited an acceptable local tissue response to an implanted device (Fig. 3). The mild fibrosis, limited inflammatory cell infiltration, mild degeneration markers, high density of myelinated axons, and organized nerve structure collectively highlight the biocompatibility of the polymer-assisted PNR device.38, 39, 40 This local tolerance is expected given the device’s composition based on PGSA.25 Poly(glycerol sebacate) acrylate is a biocompatible and biodegradable polymer composed of glycerol and sebacic acid, both of which are naturally found in the human body.41 Its biocompatibility is well-documented in the literature and has been clinically tested clinically as a vascular sealant without adverse events.27,42, 43, 44

The performance of the polymer-assisted repairs was compared to that of microsutures using electrophysiology evaluation, gastrocnemius muscle weight, imaging, histopathology and histomorphometry of the implanted system. Nerve conduction studies indicate an early recovery with motor signals detected at the distal plantar flexor starting at 2 months postimplantation (Fig. 4A). An increase in motor nerve conduction velocity and amplitude corresponded to the functioning of the nerve and an improved gastrocnemius muscle weight ratio (Fig. 4B). These functional outcomes, comparable to results reported for microsuture repairs in a rat model, confirm that the polymer-assisted PNR device supports peripheral nerve repair and nerve fiber regeneration as effectively as microsutures by the 3-month timepoint.37

An ideal nerve repair system should provide the possibility to detension the repair site as well as enable fascicular alignment and maintain nerve architecture.19,45 Furthermore, microsuturing can cause compression in the central region of the nerve, resulting in the distortion of nerve fibers, tissue trauma, tension at the repair site, and the introduction of foreign materials.15,36,46

Microcomputed tomography imaging revealed the presence of an enlargement of the nerve at the microsuture repair site (Fig. 5), which was not present in the polymer-assisted repairs. This enlargement is associated with the histological observation of intraneural fibrosis, consequent to the migration or misplacement of the microsuture material. Lower intraneural fibrosis was observed for the polymer-assisted repairs when compared to microsuture. This observation can be attributed to the absence of foreign material within the nerve as well as the detensioning of the repair site (Fig. 5).47, 48, 49 Indeed, the polymer-assisted device was shown based on its geometry, transparency, and mode of action (Fig. 1) to not only provide nerve alignment but also reduce tension at the repair site (Fig. 2).26 Excessive scar tissue formation observed at the repair site has been shown to impede the long-term regenerating axons and negatively impact functional recovery.13,36,50 Indeed, although the precise effects on the nerve recovery are not completely known, inflammation, tension at the repair site and tissue scarring within the nerve have been linked to compromised nerve integrity, reduced myelin levels, and damage to unmyelinated axons because of neurotoxic cytokines and scar tissue compression, leading neuropathic pain and long-term functional deficiencies.13,36,50 Although there were no differences between the polymer-assisted and microsuture repairs in terms of functional outcomes and the number of axons distal to the repair, it is important to note that there may be a long-term impact on the pain and functionality of the nerve. Further studies are required to investigate this potential impact.

This study is not without limitations. First, the comparison of this study is made against direct microsutures without adjunctive support such as wrap and connector-assisted techniques, which may not cover the full range of clinical standard of care for nerve repair. Future studies may look to evaluate the performance of the polymer-assisted PNR device with connector-assisted methods to establish its efficacy and relevance in broader clinical practice. A second consideration is the inherent complexity of microsutures which requires a high level of microsurgical skill and may contribute to procedure variability, especially in the small diameter of the rat sciatic nerve. The polymer-assisted PNR device was designed to reduce the technical complexity of nerve repair and has the potential to support more consistent outcomes. Additional limitations stem from the use of the rat model. Although rat sciatic nerve model is widely used in research of PNR, the regeneration distance is relatively short compared to humans, limiting the translational interpretation.51,52 Moreover, the modest sample size and study duration restrict conclusions about the long-term outcomes of the findings.

This study demonstrates that the polymer-assisted PNR device effectively supports nerve regeneration and provides a sutureless alternative to traditional sutures. The polymer-assisted PNR offers the potential advantage of reduced intraneural inflammation and fibrosis, thus potentially leads to better long-term functional outcomes and less neuropathic pain.
