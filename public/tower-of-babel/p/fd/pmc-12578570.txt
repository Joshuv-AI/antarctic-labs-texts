Atypical Location of a Complex Odontoma in an 11‐Year‐Old Girl: A Case Report With a 2‐Year Follow‐Up
Authors: Melissa Zalane, Cyril Moulart, Laurence Evrard, Jennifer Fallas, Marino Vilbi, Nadia Dahdouh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTComplex odontomas are typically found in the posterior mandible. This case report presents an atypical occurence in the anterior maxilla of an 11‐year‐old girl, describing the role of early detection, precise surgical management, and interdisciplinary collaboration in achieving a successful clinical outcome. A 2‐year follow‐up confirmed stable results with no recurrence or complications.Keywords: case report, delayed tooth eruption, impacted tooth, maxillary diseases, odontoma, piezoelectric surgery

Introduction

According to the 2022 World Health Organization classification of odontogenic tumors, odontomas are benign lesions originating from both epithelial and mesenchymal tissues. They are classified as hamartomas, which are non‐neoplastic developmental malformations composed of mature tissues indigenous to the site of origin and arranged in a disorganized architecture, typically with one predominant component. Odontomas represent the most prevalent benign odontogenic lesion [1, 2].

Odontomas can be classified into compound and complex subtypes [2]. These lesions are frequently asymptomatic and may be incidentally discovered during routine examinations or associated with an unerupted permanent tooth [3]. Their detection typically occurs during the first two decades of life, with a slightly higher prevalence in males than in females [3, 4]. The compound type is more commonly encountered in the anterior region of the maxilla and exhibits a morphology that mimics the tooth‐like structure (known as “denticle”). In contrast, the complex type is typically found in the posterior region of the mandible and comprises dental tissues (cementum, dentin, enamel, and pulp) arranged in a disorganized manner [4, 5]. However, the precise etiology remains unclear. Several hypotheses have been proposed, including trauma during deciduous dentition, infection, inflammatory processes, family history, and the presence of syndromes [6].

This case report describes an atypical presentation of a complex odontoma in the anterior maxilla associated with an unerupted lateral incisor. It includes detailed radiological and histopathological findings and outlines a multidisciplinary management approach over a 2‐year follow‐up period, involving close collaboration between orthodontic and oral surgery teams.

Case History and Clinical Findings

The dental department referred an 11‐year‐old girl to our oral surgery team owing to delayed eruption of the right upper lateral incisor, which was identified during a routine examination. The patient was in good health with no significant medical history, chronic or genetic conditions, or ongoing medication at the time of presentation. Family history was unremarkable and no trauma to the maxillary region had been reported.

During clinical examination, the right upper lateral incisor was un‐erupted, while the deciduous canine (53, FDI notation) remained in its normal position (Figure 1). Palpation of the anterior vestibular maxilla revealed a firm, non‐tender mass without signs of infection or inflammation. The adjacent teeth were intact and did not exhibit any mobility or sensitivity.

Initial clinical photographs showing the absence of eruption of tooth 13 and the presence of the primary canine (53) on the right side. The gingiva appears healthy, with no signs of infection or inflammation.

A panoramic radiograph (Figure 2) revealed a well‐defined, radiopaque, hyperdense lesion in the anterior maxillary area associated with the impacted tooth 12. The lesion is surrounded by a radiolucent margin. The adjacent teeth showed displacement. This radiographic presentation is characteristic of odontomas [2, 3]. Additionally, a dental anomaly was observed—“dens invaginatus”—affecting the upper left lateral incisor (tooth 22).

Initial panoramic radiograph revealing the odontoma enclosing the malformed tooth 12 and obstructing the eruption of tooth 13, as well as a “dens invaginatus” malformation of tooth 22.

This presentation was consistent with an odontoma, prompting further radiographic and histopathological investigations.

Methods: Diagnostic Assessment

A three‐dimensional (3D) evaluation was performed using dental computed tomography to assess the extent of the lesion (Figure 3). The 3D scan revealed a large, irregular, well‐defined radiodense mass in the anterior maxilla. This mass displayed characteristic features of a complex odontoma, including an amorphous radiopaque structure with a thin radiopaque periphery surrounded by a well‐defined radiolucent zone. These findings are consistent with previously described radiographic patterns of complex odontomas in literature [4, 7]. The lesion extended around the apex of the malformed right upper lateral incisor (tooth 12) and obstructed the eruption pathway in the right upper canine (tooth 13).

Three‐dimensional scan showing an irregular, well‐defined radiopaque mass in the anterior maxilla (star), enclosing the root of impacted tooth 12 and obstructing the eruption of the impacted canine, tooth 13.

Methods: Treatment

Surgical removal is the standard treatment for odontomas, with a low recurrence rate following complete excision. In collaboration with an orthodontist, a minimally invasive surgical approach was adopted to carefully remove the odontoma and malformed tooth 12 as part of a multidisciplinary treatment plan. The primary goal was complete excision of the tumor, minimizing damage to the surrounding bone, with special consideration given to the size of the mass and its potential impact on adjacent osseous structures [4, 5, 8].

A piezotome was selected for surgical excision due to its capacity for precise bone cutting and its potential to reduce trauma to surrounding tissues. This technique enables selective bone cutting and preserves an osseous bridge that supports the surrounding tissues. This approach facilitated optimal healing with minimal post‐operative complications and ensured the integrity of the adjacent anatomical structures. In this context, piezosurgery ensured precise excision and reduced potential damage to the neighboring anatomical structures [9].

The surgical procedure was performed under general anesthesia. The key steps include an incision made with a No. 15 blade, followed by mucoperiosteal dissection to expose the tumor. The deciduous tooth 53 was extracted. Upon visualization of tooth‐like structures, the odontoma was carefully excised. The preservation of the osseous bridge was deliberately prioritized to maintain structural support for the surrounding soft tissues. The mass was sectioned into smaller pieces to facilitate resection. The surgical site was closed with resorbable sutures (Vicryl 3.0; Ethicon Inc., Raritan, NJ, USA). The excised mass was sent for histopathological analysis to confirm the diagnosis (Figures 4 and 5).

Surgical steps illustrating the removal of the odontoma while preserving a bony bridge.

The removed odontoma after being cut in parts.

The post‐operative treatment regimen included antibiotics to prevent infection, anti‐inflammatory medications to reduce swelling, and acetaminophen for pain management. Cold packs were recommended to alleviate post‐operative oedema, and the patient was instructed to use chlorhexidine mouthwash to maintain oral hygiene. Additionally, a soft diet was advised to minimize mechanical stress at the surgical site during recovery.

Outcome and Follow‐Ups

Histopathological analysis revealed the presence of a mature tooth structure, and a disorganized mass of mature dentin mixed with enamel matrix. In addition, concentric layers of cementum and scattered pulplike connective tissue were observed. The dentin exhibited architectural disorganization, which is a characteristic of complex odontomas (Figure 6).

(a, b) Histological section of the resected specimen after decalcification, stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), ×100 magnification. All dental components are disorganized: Dentin (yellow arrow), enamel matrix (blue arrow), cementum (black arrow), residual odontogenic epithelium (triangle), and pulp‐like connective tissue (star).

Post‐operative follow‐ups were conducted regularly at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months. No signs of recurrence were observed during this period (Figures 7 and 8).

(a) Panoramic radiograph at 6 months post‐operatively showing the complete removal of the tumor. The eruption pathway of the impacted canine (tooth 13) is now unobstructed. (b) Clinical situation at 1 year, showing excellent gingival healing, the eruption of the left permanent canine (tooth 23), and adequate space for the eruption of the right permanent canine (tooth 13).

(a) Clinical situation at 2 years with the patient undergoing orthodontic treatment. The eruption of the previously impacted right canine (tooth 13), which was obstructed by the tumor, is now observed in the dental arch. The gingiva is healthy, with no signs of infection or inflammation. (b) Panoramic radiograph at 2 years postoperatively, demonstrating no recurrence of the tumor.

Radiographic and clinical examinations at the 2‐year follow‐up demonstrated spontaneous eruption of the impacted maxillary right canine (tooth 13). Occlusal alignment was progressively improving, and the patient is undergoing orthodontic treatment to optimize tooth positioning. The patient reported no functional disturbances or discomfort, and no signs of infection were noted, and satisfaction with the outcome was expressed.

Discussion

Odontomas are the most common odontogenic tumors of the oral cavity, composed of enamel, dentin, cementum, and pulp. These lesions are generally asymptomatic, slow‐growing, and frequently discovered incidentally during routine radiographic examinations. Typically, odontomas are diagnosed in the second decade of life and are frequently associated with unerupted permanent teeth, as highlighted in previous studies [4, 5, 8, 10]. Clinically, odontomas may remain asymptomatic or, in some cases, cause swelling, pain, bone expansion, and tooth displacement [11]. They are more commonly found in permanent than deciduous dentition [12]. Radiographic examination plays a crucial role in diagnosis, allowing the assessment of the developmental stage based on progressive radiopacity over time [11].

Odontomas are classified into two main types: complex and compound. Complex odontomas are characterized by an irregular mass of dental tissue, whereas compound odontomas consist of multiple small tooth‐like structures. Although some studies suggest that complex odontomas are more frequently diagnosed than compound odontomas [4], this observation remains controversial [10]. Typically, complex odontomas are more commonly found in the posterior region of the mandible, making this case atypical due to its occurrence in the anterior maxilla [4, 10, 12].

To contextualize our case, a targeted literature search was conducted in PubMed using the terms “complex odontoma,” “anterior maxilla,” and “case report” to identify relevant publications. Similar cases were reviewed and summarized in Table 1, highlighting shared clinical features, treatment approaches, and outcomes [13, 14, 15]. Cases classified as compound‐complex (mixed) odontomas were excluded to maintain consistency with the WHO‐defined complex subtype [1].

As summarized in Table 1, most reported cases involve adolescents and present with delayed eruption of anterior maxillary teeth. The lesions typically appear as radiopaque masses with well‐defined borders and are often discovered incidentally or in association with failed tooth eruption. Differential diagnoses such as calcifying odontogenic cyst, cemento‐ossifying fibroma, adenomatoid odontogenic tumor, and ameloblastic fibro‐odontoma were considered. However, the lesion's radiopaque, disorganized structure with a narrow radiolucent rim, absence of soft tissue expansion, and association with a malformed impacted tooth were most consistent with a complex odontoma [1, 16]. A biopsy was not performed, as the radiographic features were considered diagnostic and surgical excision was indicated for therapeutic reasons.

Management usually involves surgical excision, under local or general anesthesia, depending on the lesion's size and anatomical location. Outcomes are generally favorable, with minimal recurrence and spontaneous eruption of adjacent teeth in younger patients.

In comparison, this case report exhibits several distinguishing features. The lesion was managed using piezoelectric‐assisted excision, which enabled precise removal while preserving adjacent bone structures. Additionally, the case involved a fused and malformed lateral incisor requiring extraction, as well as a contralateral dens invaginatus—an unusual association. The 2‐year follow‐up confirmed a stable outcome, with no recurrence and spontaneous eruption of the maxillary canine (tooth 13).

Optimal management of odontomas involves early radiographic detection and surgical excision, followed by histopathological confirmation [8, 12, 17]. Panoramic radiography plays a crucial radiological role in early detection, as shown by retrospective studies wherein most cases were diagnosed incidentally [18]. The likelihood of recurrence after complete resection is extremely low [3].

Managing impacted teeth associated with odontomas is still a matter of debate. Some authors advocate the preservation of the impacted tooth whenever possible. In contrast, others recommend extraction if the tooth is malformed or natural eruption cannot be achieved even with orthodontic traction [11, 15]. In our case, the impacted tooth was malformed and fused with the odontoma mass. Additionally, the contralateral lateral incisor exhibited “dens invaginatus” malformation and was extracted in collaboration with an orthodontist to achieve optimal dental symmetry.

Patient age is a significant factor in treatment planning. Studies indicate that older patients are more likely to require extractions. Early removal often facilitates spontaneous eruption, reducing the need for complex orthodontic interventions. In contrast, a delayed diagnosis can result in displacement and requires more complex biomechanical approaches [17, 18]. Further studies are needed to establish a consensus regarding the preservation or extraction of impacted teeth associated with odontomas and to refine treatment strategies for improved functional and aesthetic outcomes.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the clinical relevance of early diagnosis and a multidisciplinary approach in managing complex odontomas. Routine radiographic examinations play an important role in identifying odontomas before the onset of complications such as tooth displacement and malocclusion. The present case also illustrates specific features rarely reported together, including a fused and malformed lateral incisor and a contralateral dens invaginatus. Surgical excision remains the treatment of choice, with early intervention increasing the chances of spontaneous tooth eruption and minimizing the need for orthodontic correction. The use of piezoelectric‐assisted excision allowed for precise removal while preserving surrounding bone structures.

Younger patients tend to respond more favorably, whereas delayed diagnosis may necessitate more complex interventions [17, 18]. This underlines the importance of integrated therapeutic approach when anatomical anomalies are present, especially in young patients. Effective management of odontomas requires close collaboration between dental and medical specialists, including pediatricians, general dentists, orthodontists, and maxillofacial surgeons, to facilitate coordinated treatment planning and favorable patient outcomes. Long‐term follow‐up is recommended to monitor occlusal stability and overall dental development. Further studies are needed to optimize decision‐making regarding the preservation or extraction of impacted teeth associated with odontomas, particularly when structural abnormalities are involved.

Take Home Message

Early radiographic detection and surgical management of complex odontomas in pediatric patients can prevent complications such as tooth displacement and prolonged orthodontic treatment. When associated with structural anomalies like tooth fusion or dens invaginatus, a multidisciplinary approach is essential to ensure functional and aesthetic outcomes.
