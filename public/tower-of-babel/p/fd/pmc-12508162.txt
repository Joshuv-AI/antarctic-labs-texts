Structural peculiarities of the silver-containing nanocomposites based on carboxymethyl cellulose-chitosan polyelectrolyte complexes and their antimicrobial and antiviral applications
Authors: Valeriy Demchenko, Polina Zaremba, Nataliia Rybalchenko, Svitlana Zahorodnia, Liubov Artiukh, Taras Rybalchenko, Olena Demchenko, Illia Sytnyk, Donghu Zeng, Serhii Kobylinskyi, Lyudmila Goncharenko, Maksym Iurzhenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymer nanocomposites based on polyelectrolyte complexes of carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)—chitosan of different molecular weights (low, medium, high) and Ag or Ag/Ag2O nanoparticles were formed by reducing Ag+ ions in the polymer matrix using green tea, ginger or propolis extracts. The structural and morphological features, antimicrobial, antiviral and cytotoxic activity of the synthesized samples were investigated. It was found that when silver ions are reduced in polymer systems of CMC—Ag+—low molecular weight chitosan using green tea and ginger extract, Ag/Ag2O nanoparticles are formed, while the use of propolis extract results in the formation of metallic silver nanoparticles. It was found that when using green tea extract, the smallest particles with an average size of ~ 11 nm are formed, and when using ginger and propolis extracts, particles with an average size of ~ 20 and 44 nm are formed, respectively. It was found that silver-containing nanocomposites based on CMC and low molecular weight chitosan exhibit antibacterial activity against microorganisms S. aureus and E. coli and antiviral activity against influenza A virus type (H1N1) and herpes simplex virus type 1. It was shown that the studied nanocomposites did not exhibit cytotoxic activity against MDCK and Vero cells. All undiluted and diluted samples reduce the proportion of live cells by no more than 28.5%.Keywords: Polyelectrolyte complexes, Na-CMC, Chitosan, Silver-containing nanocomposite, Structure, Morphology, Antibacterial activity, Antiviral activity, CytotoxicitySubject terms: Microbiology, Chemistry, Materials science, Optics and photonics

Introduction

Infectious diseases caused by microorganisms and viruses remain a major global health concern, despite advances in pharmaceutical technologies and drug development. Research and control of viral infections, in particular a large group of respiratory diseases caused by viruses, such as influenza, parainfluenza, respiratory syncytial virus, adenoviruses, rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, etc., occupy an important place in modern medicine1. Also, microorganisms resistant to many antimicrobial agents are developing (Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, yeast-like fungi Candida albicans, etc.)2.

Therefore, there is currently a need in the fields of ecology, medicine and the food industry for new materials with higher bactericidal and antiviral effects and lower toxicity to humans and the environment.

In the last decade, there has been a growing interest in the synthesis and study of the structural features and properties of polymer composites, in particular with metal nanoparticles. This opens up wide opportunities for their use in biomedicine, catalysis, optics, microelectronics, etc.3.

The following methods of obtaining polymer nanocomposites are distinguished: dispersion (grinding of metal particles or their oxides with subsequent introduction into the polymer matrix) and reduction of metal ions in the polymer matrix. As evidenced by literature analysis, the reduction of metal ions within a the polymer matrix is a promising approach for obtaining metal-containing polymer nanocomposites, enabling control over the size and morphology of filler nanoparticles4,5.

For the production of silver-containing nanocomposites, radiation-chemical6, chemical7 or thermochemical reduction8,9 are employed. Additionally, plant extracts are used to reduce Ag + ions in polymer matrices10. During radiation-chemical reduction of silver ions, silver-containing nanocomposites are obtained without the participation of chemical reducing agents, but this requires special equipment for generating high-energy radiation6. When forming nanocomposites by chemical reduction of silver ions, reducing agents such as dimethylformamide11, hydrazine7, sodium borohydride7,12, etc. are used. This is a fast, effective and simple method for producing nanocomposites with controlled morphology, however, chemical impurities that remain in the samples after the reduction reaction are a problem. Thermochemical reduction of silver ions is an environmentally friendly, effective and inexpensive method for the production of silver-containing nanocomposites, but sometimes this approach requires heating film materials with silver ions at fairly high temperatures8,9. Recently, the “green synthesis” of silver-containing nanocomposites has become widespread—the reduction of Ag+ ions using plant extracts10.

Green synthesis of silver nanoparticles using bacteria, plants, yeast, algae and fungi has attracted more attention due to its safety for humans, environmental friendliness and cost-effectiveness, fast and simple synthesis process, non-toxicity13. Plant extracts contain metabolites such as enzymes, amino acids, carbohydrates, proteins, phenols, alcohols, polyphenols, aromatic phenolic compounds, vitamins and others, which are safe for the environment and human health14–19. Silver nanoparticles synthesized using green chemistry have been successfully applied in anti-inflammatory, anti-influenza, bactericidal, antitumor therapy, gene therapy, drug delivery, photocatalytic and antiviral activity, food packaging, cosmetics, water purification, etc.16,20,21.

When reducing silver ions using plant extracts with subsequent formation of nanoparticles, either the extracts themselves or individual polymers serve as stabilizers22,23.

The authors of24 used an aqueous extract of Ageratum conidia to prepare a series of Ag nanoparticles, with the smallest average particle size being 30 ± 1.3 nm with a polydispersity of 0.328 ± 0.009. This study provides evidence that the prepared Ag nanoparticles can be used to treat inflammatory diseases. In25, silver nanoparticles stabilized by polyvinylpyrrolidone with sizes ranging from 70 to 110 nm were obtained. These nanoparticles exhibited good crystallinity and anisotropic structure. It was found that the use of suture material with silver nanoparticles and oxidized silver nanoparticles does not cause allergic reactions in laboratory animals.

Polyelectrolyte complexes based on polymers of natural origin, specifically polysaccharides, are promising materials for the reduction and stabilization of silver nanoparticles with minimal size8. First, from a chemical structure perspective, these are rigid-chain polymers characterized not only by the presence of ionogenic groups but also by a high concentration of reactive hydroxyl OH groups, as well as groups and fragments capable of donor–acceptor interaction and hydrogen bond formation, which opens up the possibility of better stabilization of nanoparticles. Second, they are characterized by high biological activity and are non-toxic, biocompatible and biodegradable substances.

Therefore, the aim of the work is to study the structural features of polyelectrolyte complexes based on carboxymethyl cellulose-chitosan and silver-containing nanocomposites based on them, formed by the reduction of silver ions using green tea, ginger and propolis extracts, as well as to evaluate their antimicrobial and antiviral applications.

Experimental

Materials

To obtain polyelectrolyte complexes (PEC) and polyelectrolyte-metal complexes (PMC) with subsequent formation of nanocomposites, the following reagents (manufactured by “Sigma-Aldrich”) were used: chitosan of low molecular weight (CLMW), degree of deacetylation ~ 75%, Mw ~ 50,000–190,000; chitosan of medium molecular weight (CMMW), degree of deacetylation ≥ (75–85)%, Mw ~ 190,000–310,000; chitosan of high molecular weight (CHMW), degree of deacetylation > 85%, Mw ~ 310,000–375,000; Na-salt of carboxymethyl cellulose (Na-CMC), Mw ~ 90,000; silver nitrate (AgNO3), Mw ~ 169.9 g/mol; extracts from plant raw materials on an ethanol-glycerol basis (Manufacturer: LLC „FITOPHARM”, Ukraine): ginger rhizome extract, propolis extract, green tea leaf extract, the mass fraction of dry matter of the extracts is 8.2%. According to the manufacturer’s data, the acid number is 6.1, 9.8, and 11 mg KOH per 1 g of ginger, green tea, and propolis extract, respectively.

Preparation of polymer systems

Polyelectrolyte complexes were formed by mixing 1% aqueous solutions of cationic and anionic polyelectrolytes of the following composition: Na-CMC—low molecular weight chitosan hydrochloride, Na-CMC—medium molecular weight chitosan hydrochloride, Na-CMC—high molecular weight chitosan hydrochloride, taken in the stoichiometric ratio of functional ionogenic groups at T = (20 ± 2) °C. The film was made by casting into a mold. Dry PEC films were washed in distilled water until neutral pH was achieved, vacuum-dried at T = (20 ± 2) °C to constant weight. The thickness of the PEC films was 100 μm. The chemical structure of the oppositely charged polyelectrolytes, on the basis of which PEC was formed, is given below:

At the next stage, samples of polyelectrolyte-metal complexes (PMCs) were obtained by immersing the PEC films in aqueous solutions of AgNO3 salt with a concentration of 0.1 mol/L for 24 h.

The obtained samples of polyelectrolyte-metal complexes were kept in solutions of plant extracts (green tea, ginger and propolis) for 6 h at a temperature of 80 °C. The mass ratio of dry plant extract : AgNO3 = 10:1.

Zeta potential of solutions

To determine the degree of charge of anionic and cationic polyelectrolytes, the Zeta potential of the initial solutions of Na-CMC and chitosan of low, medium and high molecular weight was studied with the addition of 0.1 M HCl or NaOH solutions on a LiteSizer 500 device (Anton Paar, Switzerland) at T = (20 ± 2) °C.

X-ray diffraction materials

The structural organization of nanocomposite materials was studied by wide-angle X-ray diffraction on an XRD-7000 diffractometer (Shimadzu, Japan), the X-ray optical scheme of which was made according to the Debye–Scherrer method for the passage of the primary beam through the sample under study, using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.54 Å) and a graphite monochromator. The studies were performed by automatic step-by-step scanning in the U = 30 kV, I = 30 mA mode in the scattering angle range (from 3.0 to 50) degrees, the exposure time was 5 s. The temperature of the studies was T = (20 ± 2) °C.

TEM analysis

The morphology of nanocomposites (size, shape and distribution of nanoparticles in the polymer matrix) was studied using a JEM-1230 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan), at T = (20 ± 2) °C. The samples were prepared by grinding them and polymerizing them at room temperature into a cone-shaped epoxy resin sample. Then, using an LKB 8800 ultramicrotome (Germany), thin sections with a thickness of h ~ 50 nm were made, which were placed in an electron microscope using special grids and studied.

Antibacterial activity

To study the antimicrobial activity of the samples, reference bacterial strains were used: Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 and Gram-negative Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, obtained from the Ukrainian Collection of Microorganisms of the Zabolotny Institute of Microbiology and Virology of the National Academy of Sciences of Ukraine, Kyiv, Ukraine.

The antibacterial activity of the nanocomposites was studied using diffusion in agar on the Mueller–Hinton medium26,27. The test cultures were diluted to McFarland 0.5 standard (~ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/ml) and inoculated onto agar (2.5 × 105 CFU/ml). Sample discs (Ø10 mm) were placed on the inoculated medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. The evaluation of antibacterial activity was the determination of the inhibition zone diameters against microbial growth. The experiments were performed in triplicate. A sample without silver nanoparticles was the control.

Cell cultures and viruses

Vero cell culture was obtained from the Cell Bank of the R.E. Kavetsky Institute of Experimental Pathology, Oncology and Radiobiology of the NAS Ukraine (Kyiv, Ukraine).

MDCK cell culture and influenza A virus (IAV), H1N1, strain A/FM/1/47, were obtained from the collection of the L.V. Gromashevsky Institute of Epidemiology and Infectious Diseases of the NAMS Ukraine (Kyiv, Ukraine).

Cells were cultivated in a growth medium consisting of 46% DMEM (Biowest, France), 46% RPMI 1640 (Biowest, France), 8% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Biowest, France), and 100 μg/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Biowest, France). For conducting experiments, a cell suspension with a density of (2–2.5)*105 cells/ml in a volume of 100 μl was added to the wells of a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2.

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1), strain US, was obtained from the Virus museum of the Institute of Antiviral Chemotherapy of the Center for Clinical and Theoretical Medicine (Erfurt, Germany) and was grown on a primary culture of rabbit testicles. The virus was maintained by serial passages in Vero cells. The infectious titer of the virus was 7,4log10TCID50/ml.

Influenza A virus was propagated in MDCK cells. The infectious titer of the virus was 9.6 log10TCID50/ml.

The plates were incubated in a thermostat at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 48 h.

Results and discussion

Zeta potential

Before the formation of polyelectrolyte complexes, the concentration of functional groups of oppositely charged biopolyelectrolytes was determined by the conductometric titration method31. It was found that the concentration of functional groups of CLMW, CMMW, CHMW and Na-CMC is 4.00, 3.52, 3.63 and 2.57 mmol/g, respectively. Then, the charge of cationic and anionic polyelectrolytes in aqueous solutions was studied by adding 0.1 M solutions of HCl or NaOH to them (Fig. 1).

Zeta potential of 1% aqueous solutions of chitosan of different molecular weights (low, medium, high) and Na-CMC at different pH values.

It was found that for chitosan, regardless of its molecular weight, with an increase in the pH of the solution from 2 to 10, the zeta potential of the degree of charge of the polyelectrolyte decreases. For an anionic polyelectrolyte, with an increase in the pH of the solution from 2 to 10, the degree of charge of the sodium salt of carboxymethylcellulose increases. For the formation of polyelectrolyte complexes, the optimal pH value of the solutions was chosen, which is 5 (Fig. 1).

Study of the polyelectrolyte complexes by X-ray diffraction analysis

This is indicated by the presence of two diffraction peaks at 2θm ~ 9.2° and 19.9° on the curves (Fig. 2). It was established that the structure of chitosan of low, medium and high molecular weight is practically identical.

When mixing anionic and cationic polyelectrolytes—Na-CMC and chitosan, polyelectrolyte complexes with a new amorphous structure are formed (Fig. 3, curves 1–3). This is indicated by the amorphous halo at 2θm ~ 24.0°, which characterizes the structure caused by the electrostatic interaction of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes (Fig. 3, curves 2).

It should be noted that the angular position of the amorphous halo of samples of polyelectrolyte complexes based on carboxymethylcellulose and chitosan of different molecular weights is significantly shifted to the region of larger angles compared to the amorphous halo of the anionic polyelectrolyte—Na-CMC (Fig. 3a–c).

This indicates a decrease in the average Bragg distance between the macromolecular chains of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes—carboxymethylcellulose and chitosan of low, medium or high molecular weight in polyelectrolyte complexes.

Analysis of the structure of the studied samples showed that in the diffraction patterns of polyelectrolyte complexes based on Na-CMC and chitosan with different molecular weights, the angular position of the amorphous halo, which characterizes the structure of the PEC, does not change. At the same time, the average Bragg distance between the macromolecular chains of the anionic and cationic polyelectrolytes remains constant when using chitosan of low, medium or high molecular weight in the composition of the PEC (Fig. 3a–c, curves 2).

Structural features of silver-containing nanocomposites

After the reduction of silver ions in polymer systems based on CMC and low molecular weight chitosan using green tea extract, the diffractogram of the resulting sample shows two intense diffraction peaks at 2θm ~ 38.2° and 44.2°, which correspond to the crystallographic planes of the face-centered cubic lattice of silver, characterized by indices (111) and (200), respectively, and confirm the presence of metallic silver in the polymer system (Fig. 4, curve 1)5,9. There are also three low-intensity peaks at 2θm ~ 27.7°, 32.2° and 46.2°, which correspond to the planes (110), (111) and (211), respectively. These correspond to a face-centered cubic structure and are consistent with previous studies on biogenic Ag2O particles34.

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction patterns of nanocomposites: (1) CMC—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW, (2) CMC—Ag/Ag2O—CMMW, (3) CMC—Ag/Ag2O –CHMW, obtained by reduction of Ag+ ions with green tea extract.

When reducing Ag+ ions in polyelectrolyte-metal complexes based on CMC and chitosan of medium and high molecular weight, the intensity of the peaks at 2θm ~ 27.7°, 32.2° and 46.2°, which characterize the Ag2O structure, increases in the diffraction patterns of these samples. This indicates an increase in the amount of silver oxide in the polymer systems (curves 2, 3).

When forming nanocomposites by reducing Ag+ ions in polymer systems based on CMC and chitosan of various molecular weights using ginger extract, the diffractogram of the CMC—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW sample contains low-intensity peaks at 2θm ~ 38.0° and 44.0°, which characterize the structure of metallic silver, and peaks at 2θm ~ 27.7°, 32.2°, and 46.2°, which confirm the presence of Ag2O particles (Fig. 5, curve 1). When using medium and high molecular weight chitosan to form PECs with anionic polyelectrolyte—CMC, the intensity of the peaks responsible for the Ag2O structure significantly increases in the diffractograms of these samples. At the same time, the diffraction peaks that characterize the structure of metallic silver practically do not change compared to the CMC sample—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (curves 2, 3 and 1). This indicates that CMMW and CHMW contribute to better reduction of silver ions with the subsequent formation of a larger number of Ag2O particles.

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction patterns of nanocomposites: (1) CMC—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (2) CMC—Ag/Ag2O—CMMW, (3) CMC—Ag/Ag2O –CHMW, obtained by reduction of Ag+ ions with ginger extract.

When using propolis extract to form silver-containing nanocomposites, the diffractograms of CMC samples with low and medium molecular weight chitosan contain only peaks corresponding to the structure of metallic silver, located at 2θm ~ 38.2° and 44.2° (Fig. 6, curves 1, 2). However, when forming a sample with high molecular weight chitosan, its diffractogram contains peaks that are responsible for both the structure of Ag and Ag2O (curve 3).

Wide-angle X-ray diffraction patterns of nanocomposites: (1) CMC –Ag –CLMW, (2) CMC—Ag—CMMW, (3) CMC—Ag/Ag2O –CHMW, obtained by reduction of Ag+ ions with propolis extract.

According to the manufacturer of the extracts used in this study, the acid number is 6.1, 9.8 and 11 mg KOH per 1 g of ginger, green tea and propolis extracts, respectively. The highest value for propolis extract may indicate a higher content of organic acids in this extract. According to the literature, green tea extracts are characterized by a high content of polyphenols35,36, while propolis extracts contain a large amount of phenolic compounds and flavonoids37. In ginger extracts, the content of these components is usually much lower, which leads to a lower reducing capacity during the synthesis of silver nanoparticles38,39.

Phenolic compounds play a dual role in the green synthesis of metal nanoparticles, acting as both reducing and stabilizing agents. The presence of functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl allows them to effectively reduce Ag⁺ ions while stabilizing the resulting nanoparticles through surface adsorption. In addition, phenolic compounds are well known for their intrinsic bioactivity, including antioxidant, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. When interacting with the surface of nanoparticles, they enhance the biological functionality of nanocomposites through synergistic effects40–42.

The most likely mechanism for the formation of Ag/Ag2O during the interaction of CMC-Chit-Ag⁺ complexes with extract components is the initial formation of AgOH through a reaction between silver ions and the hydroxyl groups of phenolic compounds. Then, AgOH, which is unstable, converts to Ag2O and is reduced by various reducing agents contained in the extracts to Ag43–45. For ginger extract, the reduction reaction practically does not occur due to the extract’s low reducing ability. Complete reduction was observed for low molecular weight chitosan and green tea or propolis extracts. However, incomplete reduction was observed for high molecular weight chitosan. This may be due to the formation of denser complex structures that reduce access to silver atoms for reducing agents. A thorough review of the extant literature reveals that, in contrast to the chemical reduction reactions that used NaBH4, ascorbic acid, sodium citrate, or other reducing agents, a mixture of Ag/Ag2O or pure Ag2O is typically formed when plant extracts are utilized46–51.

Morphology of samples

Figure 7 presents transmission electron microscopy micrographs of silver-containing nanocomposites based on CMC and low molecular weight chitosan. Analysis of the micrographs showed that when using green tea extract as a reducing agent for Ag+ ions in polymer systems, Ag/Ag2O nanoparticles with an average size of 11 nm are formed (Fig. 7a), when using ginger and propolis extracts, larger particles with an average size of 20 and 44 nm are formed, respectively (Fig. 7b, c).

Transmission electron microscopy micrographs of samples (a) CMC—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (reducing agent green tea extract); (b) CMC—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (reducing agent ginger extract); (c) CMC—Ag–CLMW (reducing agent propolis extract).

As can be seen from the micrographs, when using various extracts for the reduction of silver ions, nanoparticles are formed that are statistically distributed in polymer matrices. The smallest particles and the smallest dispersion in the size of nanoparticles are inherent in the CMC sample—Ag/Ag2O–CLMW, where the reducing agent was green tea extract (Fig. 7a). In samples synthesized using ginger extract, nanoparticles are more prone to aggregation, which may indicate a lower activity of this reducing agent (Fig. 7b). When using propolis extract to form samples, particles of the largest size are formed (Fig. 7c).

Literature data indicate that the specific surface area of silver nanoparticles synthesized using extracts can vary significantly depending on the phytochemical composition of the plant extract. For example, a specific surface area of 28.2 m2/g was found for silver nanoparticles synthesized using Moringa oleifera leaf extract52. Similarly, silver nanoparticles were synthesized using Coriandrum sativum (parsley) leaf extract, and the surface area of the silver nanoparticles was 33.72 m2/g53. In another study, silver nanoparticles synthesized using Brassica oleracea var. botrytis (cauliflower) extract had a specific surface area of 19.22 m2/g54. Such data indicate the key role of plant-derived phytochemicals in influencing the morphology of nanoparticles and surface characteristics.

Antibacterial activity of nanocomposites

The polymeric systems CMC–Ag/Ag₂O–CLMW exhibited significant antibacterial activity (Fig. 8). The inhibition zone diameters for S. aureus were as follows: 22.63 ± 1.20 mm (reducing agent: green tea extract, sample 1); 24.00 ± 1.40 mm (reducing agent: ginger extract, sample 2); 22.83 ± 1.10 mm (reducing agent: propolis extract, sample 3). The silver-containing CMC-Ag/Ag₂O-CLMW samples showed slightly lower antibacterial activity against E. coli. The inhibition zone diameters were recorded as follows: 18.36 ± 0.60 mm (reducing agent: green tea extract, sample 1); 15.63 ± 0.30 mm (reducing agent: ginger extract, sample 2); 16.51 ± 0.50 mm (reducing agent: propolis extract, sample 3) (Table 1).

The studied CMC– CLMW samples obtained with the addition of green tea and ginger extracts, which did not contain silver nanoparticles (Fig. 8, Table 1, samples 4 and 5), showed no antibacterial activity against the S. aureus and E. coli. However, the CMC–CLMW (propolis extract) sample exhibited slight activity against S. aureus (Fig. 8). The inhibition zone diameter was 11.58 ± 0.40 mm (Table 1, sample 6). The localized antimicrobial effect against S. aureus may be associated with the natural antimicrobial properties of propolis. The absence of activity against E. coli may indicate the lower effectiveness of the propolis extract against Gram-negative bacteria.

A similar effect was found for biomaterials synthesized by combining sodium alginate and chitosan with three different beekeeping substances—honey, propolis and royal jelly, as well as with a mixture of these three compounds. Antibacterial studies have shown that among the synthesized samples, films enriched with propolis tincture or a mixture of beekeeping products demonstrated high antibacterial activity55. In other studies, it was found that the combination of several antibacterial agents in one composite allows to obtain an effective antibacterial film due to synergy. Composite films were synthesized using compounds such as hydroxyethyl cellulose, ZnO, mesoporous silica and cinnamon essential oil (spice extract)56.

Chitosan-based films with ZnO and Ag nanoparticles were produced by casting. The nanoparticles were loaded with citronella essential oil (CEO) to enhance the antimicrobial activity of the nanocomposite films57. The synergistic effect of the synthesized antimicrobial films was confirmed by comparing the results of the antimicrobial action of the composite with simple chitosan/ZnO/CEO or chitosan/Ag/CEO films.

Antiviral activity of nanocomposites

A moderate virucidal effect against influenza A (H1N1) virus was demonstrated by the CMC–Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (ginger extract), CMC–Ag–CLMW (propolis extract) and CMC–CLMW (propolis extract): they reduce the infectious titer by (1.87 ± 0.09), (1.99 ± 0.10) and (2.01 ± 0.10)log10TCID50/ml, respectively. The samples CMC–CLMW (green tea extract), CMC–CLMW (ginger extract), and CMC–CLMW did not affect IAV (Fig. 9a).

Virulicidal effect of nanocomposites against various viruses, expressed in a decrease in infectious titer relative to the virus control: (a) influenza A virus (H1N1); (b) herpes simplex virus type 1.

The sample CMC–Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (green tea extract) showed the best effect in inhibiting the influenza virus. It demonstrated a decrease in infectious titer by (8.91 ± 0.45)log10TCID50/ml, which is a complete inhibition of virus reproduction in MDCK cells (Fig. 9a).

The studied silver-containing samples CMC–Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (ginger extract) and CMC–Ag– CLMW (propolis extract) reduced the infectious titer of herpes simplex virus by (3.79 ± 0.27) and (3.02 ± 0.23)log10TCID50/ml, respectively. The CMC–Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (green tea extract) reduced the titer of HSV-1 significantly less: by (1.23 ± 0.06)log10TCID50/ml.

Contact of HSV-1 with CMC–CLMW (green tea extract), CMC–CLMW (ginger extract) and CMC–CLMW (propolis extract) did not lead to a change in its infectious titer compared to the control (Fig. 9b).

Cytotoxic effect of the samples

The studied polymer silver-containing nanocomposites based on CMC and CLMW, formed by reducing Ag+ ions with green tea, ginger, and propolis extracts, as well as the CMC-CLMW samples filled with extracts did not exhibit a toxic effect on the MDCK cell culture (Fig. 10a). All undiluted samples reduced the cell viability by no more than 28.5%.

Effect of nanocomposites on the viability of cell cultures: (a) MDCK; (b) Vero.

The studied nanocomposites in the tested dilutions inhibited the functional activity of Vero cells from 1.4 to 14.7%, which indicates the absence of their cytopathic properties (Fig. 10b). Thus, the samples did not exhibit a significant toxic effect on the studied cell cultures.

Conclusions

Using plant extracts for the reduction of silver ions in polymer systems CMC—Ag+—chitosan of low, medium and high molecular weight, silver-containing polymer nanocomposites were synthesized. It was found that the initial samples of polyelectrolyte complexes CMC—chitosan of low, medium and high molecular weight are characterized by a new structure, distinct from that of the individual polyelectrolytes—Na-CMC and chitosan. When reducing Ag+ ions using green tea and ginger extract in a polymer matrix based on CMC and CLMW, Ag/Ag2O nanoparticles with a smaller average size of 11 and 20 nm are formed, and when using propolis extract, larger Ag particles with an average size of 44 nm are formed.

Silver-containing nanocomposites based on CMC and CLMW demonstrated high antibacterial activity. When using different extracts, the diameter of the growth inhibition zones for S. aureus was within 22.63–24.00 mm, for E. coli within 15.63–18.36 mm. A slight antimicrobial effect was found for the CMC–CLMW (propolis extract) sample. The diameter of the growth inhibition zone for S. aureus was 11.58 ± 0.40.

It was established that the best effect in inhibiting the influenza virus was shown by the CMC–Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (green tea extract) sample. A reduction in infectious titer by (8.91 ± 0.45)log10TCID50/ml was observed, indicating complete inhibition of virus reproduction in MDCK cells. Nanocomposites CMC–Ag/Ag2O–CLMW (ginger extract) and CMC–Ag–CLMW (propolis extract) reduced the infectious titer of herpes simplex virus (HSV-1) by (3.79 ± 0.27) and (3.02 ± 0.23) log10TCID50/ml, respectively.

It was found that silver-containing nanocomposites did not exhibit cytotoxic activity against MDCK and Vero cells. All undiluted and diluted samples reduced the proportion of viable cells by no more than 28.5%.

The obtained materials have potential applications for the creation of dressings for the treatment of wounds of various infectious nature, packaging materials for food storage, and membranes for water purification.
