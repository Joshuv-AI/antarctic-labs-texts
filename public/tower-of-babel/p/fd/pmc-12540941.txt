One-pot synthesized g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO magnetic photocatalyst for one-pot synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzochromenes and methylene blue photodegradation
Authors: Araz Taravati, Ahmad Poursattar Marjani, Sepideh Bibak, Hamideh Sarreshtehdar Aslaheh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article presents the synthesis, characterization, and dual-functional applications of a g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst in both environmental remediation and organic synthesis. This study integrated a composite of visible-light-responsive graphitic carbon nitride, narrow-bandgap copper oxide, and magnetically recoverable iron oxide into one synergistic photocatalyst system through a facile solvothermal method. Complete characterizations were made by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM), Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS), Thermogravimetric Analysis/Differential Thermal Analysis (TGA/DTA), Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET), Point of Zero Charge (PZC), Vibrating Sample Magnetometry (VSM), Ultraviolet-Visible Diffuse Reflectance Spectroscopy (UV-Vis DRS), and Cyclic Voltammetry (CV). The photocatalyst exhibited the best degradation efficiency for methylene blue, achieving over 90% removal within 60 min under UV light irradiation. At pH = 9, the catalyst exhibited optimum performance, which was explained by enhanced radical generation and surface interactions. CV confirmed efficient electron transfer within the catalyst, hence its suitability for redox reactions. The composite proved very efficient for the one-pot synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzochromenes, yielding products higher than 89% under neutral and mild conditions. This composite was stable, maintaining over 80% activity after six runs. These results bring g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalysts into the spotlight as efficient, robust, and green material applications in wastewater treatment and green organic synthesis. Introduction

The desire to find environmentally friendly and efficient technologies to overcome pollution issues and improve catalysis processes for the synthesis of chemicals has encouraged extensive exploration of photocatalytic processes[1,2]. Photocatalysis, which utilizes light to accelerate the rate of chemical reactions, offers an eco-friendly and efficient solution to these challenges. Among all the photocatalytic materials under consideration, g-C3N4 has been reported to possess advantages such as an electronic structure, thermal and chemical stability, and a visible light absorption capability. However, some drawbacks of applying g-C3N4 include a poor specific surface area, a high recombination rate of photogenerated electron-hole pairs, and a low solar spectrum light absorption capability3,4.

To improve the photocatalytic activity of g-C3N4, numerous studies have been devoted to the synthesis of g-C3N4-based composites with other semiconductors. CuO significantly contributes to the enhancement of the photocatalytic activity of g-C3N4.CuO is a narrow bandgap p-type semiconductor, about 1.7 eV, and thus effectively absorbs visible light, promoting charge separation when coupled with g-C3N45. The possible formation of a p-n heterojunction upon the interaction of CuO and g-C3N4 may lead to an effective charge carrier separation and increase the lifetime of photogenerated electron-hole pairs. Magnetic iron oxide (Fe3O4), also called magnetite, adds more properties to the g-C3N4/CuO composite. The superparamagnetic properties of Fe3O4 nanoparticles enable the photocatalyst to be easily separated and recovered from the reaction mixture using an external magnetic field. Indeed, that property addresses one of the significant challenges of heterogeneous catalysis: recovering and reusing catalysts6,7. On the other hand, transition metals such as iron and copper in the form of oxides with empty d orbitals cause this catalyst to behave as a Lewis acid and promote the reaction.

Moreover, Fe3O4 also mediates electron transfer, facilitating charge separation and enhancing the photocatalytic activity of the composite. This synergistic effect is expected to significantly enhance the overall photocatalytic performance of the composite material. With the multi-tier framework of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO having interconnected boundaries among its constituent components, the efficiency of the composite photocatalyst material will be improved in its photocatalytic applications.

Organic pollutants, such as methylene blue, a common dye in the textile industry, pose critical environmental challenges due to their degradation. Methylene blue poses an ecological risk to aquatic organisms in marine environments and human health. Several of the significant drawbacks of this pigment are presented in Fig. 1. This necessitates a practical approach to addressing this challenge, which is achieved through photocatalytic degradation.

Methylene blue drawbacks.

It is postulated that the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO nanocomposite will illustrate enhanced photodegradation of methylene blue under visible light exposure. Applications range from environmental remediation to chemical synthesis. Chromene derivatives have been considered essential scaffolds in medicinal chemistry due to their widespread biological activities, including anti-cancer, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial effects. Some chromium derivatives are shown in Fig. 2.

Chromene derivatives with their biological features10.

The typical conventional syntheses of these compounds involve severe reaction conditions, are multistep, and use many environmentally hazardous reagents. A photocatalytic one-pot synthesis approach using g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO under visible light irradiation is expected to provide a more sustainable and efficient route to such value-added compounds. Considering their pharmaceutical importance, 2-amino-4H-chromenes possess a wide range of biological activities11.

Their synthesis using the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO composite may improve photocatalytic efficiency. Effective photocatalysts for such an application may facilitate the synthesis process, reduce energy consumption, and eliminate harmful reagents. One-pot synthesis reactions have attracted considerable interest in recent years due to their efficiency, atom economy, and reduced environmental impact. These are processes in which multiple steps are conducted in a single reaction vessel without separating the intermediates. Hence, simplified processes result in reduced waste generation and improved yields. In the case of heterogeneous photocatalysis, one-pot synthesis reactions are particularly well-suited for the synthesis of complex organic molecules, such as 2-amino-4H-chromenes, under mild conditions. A photocatalyst, such as g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO under visible light, shows how far we have come regarding the green chemistry scene13,14.

This research aims to synthesize and characterize the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst and subsequently investigate its activity in the degradation of methylene blue and the synthesis of chromene derivatives. The current study aims to provide new insights into the design of efficient photocatalysts for environmental remediation and chemical synthesis by examining the composite’s structural, optical, and photocatalytic properties in detail. The results of this investigation can pave the way for a rational design of advanced materials with enhanced photocatalytic properties, thereby contributing to sustainable technological advancements in water purification and the pharmaceutical industry.

Experimental

Materials and instruments

All the chemicals used in this work were obtained from Merck and Sigma and were used as received without further purification. Specifically, these are: melamine, FeCl₂·4 H₂O (iron(II) chloride tetrahydrate), FeCl₃·6 H₂O (Iron(III) chloride hexahydrate), 25% ammonia solution (NH₃), CuSO₄·5 H₂O (copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate), NaOH (sodium hydroxide), acetone, deionized water, ethanol, and methylene blue (used as a model pollutant for photocatalytic degradation studies). All the reagents used in work were analytical-grade and used directly to synthesize and characterize the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst and its applications. The instruments used for characterization and analysis include FTIR (Thermo, Avatar), XRD (Philips, PW1730), FE-SEM (ZEISS Sigma 300), EDS-Mapp (Tescan, Vega 3), TEM (CM120), TGA/DTA (Ta, Q600), BET (Bel, belsorp Mini II, Bel prep Vac II), CV (Sharif solar co, PGE-18), VSM (Vsm Mdkb), UV-Vis DRS (Scinco, S-4100), UV-Visible spectroscopy (Thermo, Biomate), 1H NMR and C NMR (VARIAN, Inova 500 MHz, the results of NMR are included in SI file as Figures S1 and S2). The structural, morphological, thermal, optical, and electrochemical characteristics of the produced materials and photocatalysts were all carefully assessed using these cutting-edge tools.

Methods

Step by step one-pot synthesis of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst

Melamine (in the form of white powder) is placed in a porcelain crucible and kept at a temperature of 550 °C for 24 h, synthesizing bulk g-C3N4 through thermal polymerization. That bulk g-C3N4 (3D) becomes ultra-thin after mechanical exfoliation, and after sonication or liquid exfoliation, it transforms into g-C3N4 nanosheets (2D).

Fe3O4 (in a single process outside of the photocatalyst)

Although Fe3O4 is prepared simultaneously during the photocatalyst synthesis in our method and is not synthesized beforehand, its principles are based on the co-precipitation method. According to this method, either 500 or 750 mg (light orange solution – 30 mL of distilled water) of FeCl·4H2O is separately mixed with 1 g of FeCl3·6H2O (dark orange solution – 30 mL of distilled water) using a stirrer or a heating stirrer. After 15 min, the FeCl2.4H2O solution is added to the FeCl3.6H2O solution (now a darker orange). Then, it is stirred on the heater for 10–15 min. Next, 20 to 25 mL of 25% ammonia solution is added dropwise to the mixture, causing the solution to turn black and precipitate immediately. Finally, we remove the water by placing a magnet under the beaker. It is worth mentioning that ammonia raises the pH, so by adding and draining water in several stages with the help of a magnet, we neutralize the pH.

CuO synthesis method

CuSO4 + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)2 + Na2SO4 was the facile solution used to create copper hydroxide powder for this purpose. Cu(OH)2 was then mechanochemically dehydrated by milling the as-prepared Cu(OH)2 precursor and NaCl, which led to the dispersion of CuO nanoparticles into the salt matrix (Cu(OH)2 + 2NaCl → CuCl2 + 2NaOH and then CuCl2 + 2NaOH → CuO + 2NaCl + H2O)15.

After preparing the precursor solution, it is transferred into the stainless-steel autoclave. The stainless-steel autoclave is sealed and heated to temperatures ranging from 105 to 150 °C. Under such conditions, the reaction can proceed for several hours, often taking approximately 12 h, because nanoparticles require significant time to form. The high temperature and pressure create favorable crystallization conditions, leading to CuO’s growth. Then, the autoclave is allowed to cool. This black precipitate is filtered out; it primarily comprises CuO. The precipitate is washed several times with deionized water and acetone to purify it, eluting unreacted precursors and by-products through filtration. Finally, the nanoparticles are dried at high temperatures, typically around 750 °C, to obtain purified CuO.

Synthesis method of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst

First, we place melamine powder at 550 °C for 24 h, ultimately synthesizing bulk g-C3N4 (3D). We then grind 1.5 g of it in a mortar, resulting in the ultra-thin structure of g-C3N4. Next, we dissolve the obtained material in a mixture of 30 mL of water and 30 mL of ethanol, followed by 30 min of sonication, ultimately producing 2D nanosheets of g-C3N4. It is worth mentioning that the g-C3N4 produced in this method is based on tri-s-triazine (heptazine) g-C3N4. Next, we add 1.37 g of FeCl3.6H2O and 0.5 g of FeCl2.4H2O along with 30 mL of water to the reaction mixture, and sonication is carried out for 30 min.

Next, we add 20 mL of ammonia (25% ammonia solution) drop by drop to the reaction environment, and sonication is performed for 30 min, which leads to the simultaneous formation of Fe3O4 in the process of synthesizing the photocatalyst in the reaction environment.

Then, we add 3.37 g of CuO to the reaction environment and subject it to sonication for 3 h. Then, the sample is placed in an oven at a temperature of 50–80 °C for 24 h (because temperatures above 80 °C cause the oxidation of Fe3O4). Until the sample dries, our black-colored magnetic photocatalyst is in nanoscale dimensions and is a nanocomposite g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO. The successful incorporation of copper and iron in the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO composite was confirmed through ICP-OES analysis. The analysis resulted in the composite containing 45.71% Cu and 7.60% iron Fe. The obtained results indicated the efficient incorporation of CuO and Fe₃O₄ into the matrix of g-C3N4, which is in line with the proposed synthesis method. 2-Amino-4H-benzochromenes synthesis mechanism based on redox reactions using g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst

g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO composite catalyst system is applied to synthesize 2-amino-4H-benzochromenes from 2-naphthol. In Fig. 4, photocatalytic generation of superoxide radicals is initiated; afterward, the reaction proceeds via at least three possible routes: Michael Addition, Knoevenagel Condensation, and Aldol Condensation. The final synthesized nanocomposite exhibits a synergistic and enhanced effect compared to each of its constituent components. Additionally, according to the proposed mechanism in Fig. 4, visible light irradiation generates electron-hole pairs within the catalyst structure. The resulting positively charged holes and electrons, transferred to the conduction band, react with species such as oxygen, leading to the production of superoxide and hydroxyl radicals. This subsequently facilitates redox reactions and the multi-component reaction between the initial chromene compounds. Additionally, regarding the photocatalytic charge transfer mechanism, the irradiation of light on the components of the nanocomposite generates electron-hole pairs within them, facilitating the transfer of electrons between them. This results in the effective separation of electrons and holes, as well as an increased lifetime of charge carriers16,17‚18‚19.

Mechanistic study of the synthesis reaction of chromene compounds using g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst.

In this composite catalyst, g-C3N4 plays a significant role in light absorption and is responsible for efficiently harvesting the light energy needed for the reaction course. Meanwhile, CuO takes responsibility for electron transfer and works efficiently to transfer electrons through the system. On the other hand, Fe3O4 plays a substantial role in this process by providing magnetic separation of the catalyst elements, enabling their effective isolation. Such a well-coordinated mechanism forms chromene compounds under green and mild conditions, involving a highly efficient and sophisticated cascade of electron transfers and various intermediate formations along this catalysis path. On the other hand, according to the band gap reported in DRS analysis for the synthesized photocatalyst, chromene compounds can be synthesized in the presence of visible light (according to redox reactions) because ultraviolet light with a short wavelength, high energy, and high degradation power can cause degradation of precursors (2-naphthol). The presence of g-C3N4, Fe3O4, and CuO in the relevant nanocomposite leads to the formation of a heterojunction and helps reduce electron-hole recombination. It has also been reported in similar studies that composite and hybrid structures facilitate rapid electron transfer, enhance charge carrier separation, and increase photocatalytic activity.

Characterization of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst

Advanced analytical techniques, including FT-IR, XRD, FESEM, EDS mapping, and TEM analysis, were employed to examine the composition, crystal arrangement, and nanoscale structure of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO. Moreover, the prepared catalyst’s surface area and thermal resistance were also investigated using TGA/DTA and BET tests, respectively. Similarly, the material’s surface charge behavior, influenced by pH, was also verified using PZC analysis.

These are further analyzed for redox behavior by CV (provided in SI file as Figure S3) analysis and confirmed by the UV-Vis DRS for their light absorption properties. The photocatalytic activity will be systematically investigated by varying several factors: photocatalyst dosage, pH of the dye solution, contact time, and MB concentration. This investigation will also include recyclability and hot filtration tests to optimize degradation efficiency and confirm the material’s stability. These analyses collectively confirm the potential of the photocatalyst in water purification.

Qualitative comparison of methylene degradation by photocatalyst

This section examined and compared the amount of methylene blue degradation under 3 different conditions. Initially, a methylene blue dye solution with a concentration of 5 ppm with 20 mL volumes at pH = 6.2 (the pH of the dye itself) was exposed to UV light without a photocatalyst. The results showed that UV light caused negligible degradation in methylene blue. Next, 50 mg of the photocatalyst was added separately to two methylene blue dye solutions with a concentration of 5 ppm and a volume of 20 ml at pH = 6.2 (the pH of the dye itself), one of them was placed in a dark environment, and the other under UV light for 10 min.

The effect of photocatalyst dosage on the degradation rate of methylene blue

In the work, an MB solution with a concentration of 5 ppm was prepared by diluting a stock solution of 100 ppm. More precisely, 5 mL of stock solution was pipetted into a 100 mL volumetric flask and diluted to the mark by adding distilled water. The effect of contact time on the degradation rate of methylene blue

The MB solution was prepared from a stock solution of 100 ppm by dilution with distilled water to achieve a final concentration of 5 ppm. In this reaction, 20 mL of the MB solution was mixed with 70 mg of photocatalyst in a beaker. The pH solution was adjusted to 9 using a NaOH solution because this condition optimizes the interaction between the surface of the photocatalyst and the dye molecules. Stirring by a magnetic stirrer ensured the photocatalyst particles were well dispersed in the solution. The reaction mixture was then exposed to UV light, and at different intervals of 5, 10, 30, and 60 min, the solution was withdrawn for analysis.

The effect of Mb concentration on the degradation rate

An MB solution with concentrations of 3, 5, 7, 10, and 15 ppm was prepared by diluting a stock solution of 100 ppm with distilled water. In each experiment, 20 mL of the respective MB solution was mixed in a beaker that contained 70 mg of the photocatalyst. The pH of all solutions was maintained at 9 by adding dilute sodium hydroxide, as it was observed that photocatalytic activities were enhanced in alkaline conditions. All solutions were stirred homogeneously using a magnetic stirrer and then exposed to UV light for 10 min.

Hot filtration

The hot filtration test was performed to investigate the effect of g-C3N4/CuO/Fe3O4 photocatalyst on the reaction process and demonstrate its heterogeneous nature. The reaction was initiated by maintaining controlled conditions, keeping the temperature constant, and stirring constantly to ensure homogeneity. Further, the reaction mixture was checked every 60 s.

After 180 s, the photocatalyst was separated magnetically from the reaction mixture using an external magnet to assess the effect of the photocatalyst beyond this time. After removal, the reaction mixture was left to its natural evolution, and no significant progress was observed in the reaction process. Filtration was performed with great care to prevent contamination or disturbance of the system.

Recyclability

The recycling ability of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst was evaluated in successive cycles of methylene blue decomposition. Initially, the photocatalyst was employed in a typical reaction configuration under identical conditions for each run. After completing one run, the photocatalyst was separated from the reaction mixture using an external magnet, washed with deionized water, and dried at 60 °C to remove residual impurities. To track the photocatalyst’s capacity to sustain its activity over time, the recycled photocatalyst gain was repeated six times in a row thereafter (The related analyses of the catalyst after the reaction time were provided in the SI file as Figures S4, S5, and S6).

Result and discussion

FT-IR analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) is used to identify functional groups and confirm the chemical structure of the photocatalyst. Fig. 5a shows the spectrum of Fe3O4. The stretching vibrational peaks of the Fe–O bond are located at 580 and 430 cm−1. The peak located at 1620 cm−1 is assigned to the bending vibration of the O–H bond. In this case, the peak at 3405 cm−1 indicates the stretching relation of the O–H bond. However, this peak might also correspond to the N–H bond since the presence of the N–H bond in the synthesis of Fe3O4 is not negligible because of the use of ammonia during the synthesis process23. Fig. 5b refers to CuO. The peaks observed at wavelengths 432, 496, and 604 cm−1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of the Cu–O bond. The small peak observed at 1632 cm−1 might also relate to the bending vibration of the O–H bond. The peak at 3430 cm−1 is due to the vibration, probably stretching of the O–H bond, since this value can be attributed to a few hydroxyl groups (or water molecules) that get trapped in the structure of copper oxide during its re-crystallization process during calcination24. From another perspective, this peak could be attributed to the stretching vibration of the N–H bond, which could develop while CuO is produced. Fig. 5c refers to g-C3N4. The 808 and 890 cm−1 peaks refer to the out-of-plane bending modes of the Triazine ring or Heptazine (Tri-s-triazine) structure. Based on the information above, the peaks that have been obtained within the region of wavelength between 1200 and 1650 cm−1 correspond to the following wavelengths: 1242, 1319, 1409, 1458, 1577, and 1637 cm−1, and relate to the stretching modes of C–N and C = N bonds in heterocycles25. The peaks obtained between 2850 and 3450 cm−1, especially peaks at 3276, 3421, and 3180 cm−1, are assigned to the stretching vibrational modes of the N–H bond. The intensity reduction of the peaks corresponding to wavelengths in this range results from transforming amine groups into a state, namely, C–NH2. This low intensity indicates that the residual amine groups of the g-C3N4 composite have been taken away while simultaneously supporting the growth of g-C3N4. Fig. 5d corresponds to the photocatalyst g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO. Since the synthesized catalyst is a three-component nanocomposite, its constituents are iron oxide, copper oxide, and graphitic carbon nitride, overlapping in specific wavelength regions. TGA analysis

Thermogravimetric Analysis/Differential Thermal Analysis (TGA/DTA) was used to assess thermal stability and decomposition behavior. In Fig. 11, it is further observed that the thermal decomposition profile of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst is intriguing, particularly in terms of the molecular changes that vary with temperature. Indeed, at the very beginning, when the temperature ranges from room temperature to 200 °C, the material has shown remarkable stability, displaying only a meager mass loss of 3.56%. This may be mainly attributed to the desorption of physiosorbed water molecules upon breaking of hydrogen bonds and the release of loosely bound surface volatiles. Since this stage involves no significant structural variations of the organic framework, the integrity of the graphitic carbon nitride sheets remains untouched.

TGA/DTA analysis of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst.

This material’s first significant molecular alterations occur at a temperature range of 200–530 °C. Weight loss of 20.22% in this region proceeds smoothly and reflects the onset of structural decomposition, especially at the periphery of the g-C3N4 sheets. It initiates the cleavage of more labile C–N bonds, which link the terminal amino groups (–NH2) to the triazine rings. This step would, therefore, represent a programmed sequence of bond breaking other than the sudden edge-site decomposition previous to the core structure breakdown, as the quite gentle slope of the TGA curve would have indicated.

The other change occurs within the 500–600 °C range. It exhibits a sharp exothermic peak at approximately 500 °C, accompanied by a mass loss of 5.26%. In correlation, this point corresponds to the thermal energy sufficient to overcome the resonance stabilization of the triazine/heptazine ring systems, which are regarded as the backbone of g-C3N4. It proceeds via the regular breaking of the C = N double bonds in the aromatic rings and the breakdown of the planar graphitic structure. Most importantly, this decomposition yields several nitrogenous gases from the complete fragmentation of the organic framework, such as ammonia, molecular nitrogen, and possibly hydrogen cyanide. The exothermic nature suggests that more energy is released during bond breaking than in subsequent molecular rearrangements.

In the last temperature regime, residual organic structures are fully carbonized while the inorganics undergo phase transitions. Here, the residual C–N bonds are cleaved, and the material assumes its final form dominated by metal oxide phases. This progressive mass loss in this region may be attributed to the slow evolution of residual nitrogen-containing species and possible solid-state reactions between the CuO and Fe3O4 components. In other words, this stage represents the complete mineralization of the organic element, resulting in an essentially inorganic residue with modified surface properties and crystal structure.

PZC result

The Point of Zero Charge (PZC) is defined as the pH at which the photocatalyst surface is electrically neutral, providing insight into its adsorption properties. Fig. 13 depicts the PZC curve of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst. This curve shows the variation between the initial pH of the solution and the difference between the final and initial pH values (ΔpH = pHf−pHi). The point of zero charges is a crucial parameter representing the pH at which the net charge at the surface of a material- that is, a photocatalyst in question- is zero. This is important because it determines how the surface interacts with charged species in solution, directly influencing adsorption properties and catalytic performance. The PZC, represented on the graph at pH = 8.48, means that at this pH, the surface of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst is electrically neutral. At this pH, the number of positive and negative charges on the surface is equal, resulting in no net surface charge. This neutrality defines the interaction of the material with other ions or molecules in solution. If the solution pH is below 8.48, the surface of the photocatalyst is predominantly positively charged. This situation arises because, under acidic conditions, protons (H+) are more readily available, leading to the protonation of functional groups on the surface. A positively charged surface thus favors the adsorption of negatively charged species, such as anions. It enhances the interactions between molecules such as hydroxyl ions, which impact the photocatalytic reactivity in applications like Methylene Blue degradation. The surface becomes negatively charged when pH exceeds 8.48 (pH > PZC). In this alkaline region, deprotonation of surface groups occurred, carrying a net negative charge. The negatively charged surface will demonstrate greater affinity towards positively charged species, which in most cases are cations34.

PZC curve of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst.

It demonstrates that the photocatalyst plays a crucial role in enhancing the rate and efficiency of the reaction. Without the photocatalyst, the reaction proceeds at slower kinetics, reaching a maximum of approximately 60%, indicating natural kinetic limitations. After removing the catalyst from the reaction medium, no significant progress was observed in the reaction, as no active site remained for the reaction to proceed, indicating the heterogeneous nature of the catalyst. Also, the TLC test was taken every 60 s. This can be attributed to the higher absorption of light, effective charge separation, and active sites that facilitate the reaction. The plateau of the uncatalyzed reactions underlines the crucial role of the catalyst in overcoming such limitations. Such a result positions the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst as one of the most effective systems for rapid and efficient conversion, making it suitable for environmental remediation or synthetic processes.

It could be a promising candidate for practical environmental applications requiring reusable and efficient photocatalysts, such as water treatment or air purification systems.

The catalytic efficiencies of various catalysts for synthesizing 2-amino-4H-benzochromenes are shown in Table 5. FeTiO3, under solvent-free microwave irradiation, achieves yields of 87–94% in 9–13 min, demonstrating good efficiency and rapid reaction times. The Keggin-type polyoxometalate@dysprosium catalyst, operating in an ethanol/water mixture, yields 88 to 90% in 18 to 25 min, demonstrating a moderate speed with a high yield. SBA-15@methenamine-HPA in water under reflux yields even higher percentages, ranging from 91 to 97%, in 15 min. The Bi-V-O5 catalyst achieves an excellent 97% yield in just 6 min using ultrasonic irradiation in water, demonstrating remarkable catalytic activity. H14[NaP5W30O110] achieves yields ranging from 91 to 93% under reflux in water, with reaction times varying from a very short 3 to 4 min to as long as 25 h, indicating two operational modes. Methanesulfonic acid, used as a catalyst in solvent-free conditions, yields 90–91% of the product, but requires longer reaction times of 3–4 h. ZnO@zeolite Y shows varied yields between 80 and 95% at 110 °C in 10 to 40 min, while Ni-Al2O3 in an ethanol/water mix gives somewhat lower yields of 74 to 87% over longer times of 70 to 120 min. In comparison, the newly developed g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst in this study achieves a competitive 89% yield in just 7 min under reflux in water, combining high efficiency with quick reaction time under mild and environmentally friendly conditions. This catalyst’s magnetic recovery and multifunctional properties further improve its practical value for organic synthesis applications.

Further suggestions

Furthermore, to enhance the findings of this study, several options may be considered. It may support future work by optimising the synthesis parameters, i.e., the ratios of the components in g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO, reaction temperature, and duration, which may result in significant improvements of the photocatalytic performance for specific applications. Again, such a wide range of pollutant testing would be essential in giving a well-rounded view of the catalyst’s efficiency. The efficiency testing of catalysts against different ranges of hazardous dyes and pharmaceutical contaminants in wastewater is indispensable. Understanding the reaction mechanism of methylene blue and other pollutant photocatalytic degradation would go a long way in elucidating pathways and formed intermediates; thus, it would optimize the photocatalytic process. Long-term stability and performance in the case of a catalyst under continuous operation conditions are crucial; this will significantly dictate its practical viability in light of the possible leaching of its components. Integrating the photocatalyst into advanced oxidation processes or membrane filtration systems will increase the general effectiveness of pollutant removal and provide practical solutions for wastewater treatment. Further studies on the efficacy of the catalyst in various light sources, including sunlight and LED systems, will expand its potential applications and usability across a wide range of environmental conditions. Lastly, pilot-scale research is always necessary for scale-up studies of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst, as the efficacy in higher wastewater volumes and under different environmental conditions needs to be verified to facilitate commercialization and subsequent deployment at treatment facilities.

Conclusion

Regarding its applications in environmental remediation and green chemical synthesis, this work demonstrated the excellent capabilities of the g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst. Characterization was performed using various techniques, including XRD, FTIR, SEM, TEM, EDS, BET, UV-Vis DRS, TGA/DTA, VSM, and CV, to confirm the high-crystallinity material, mesoporous structure, outstanding visible light absorption, and magnetic properties. Such property features lead to exceptional photocatalytic performance and applicability. Under UV light irradiation at an optimized pH of 9, the photocatalyst degraded methylene blue with 90% efficiency within 60 min. This superior activity could be ascribed to the synergistic interaction of its components: g-C₃N₄ for excellent light absorption and electron-hole separation, CuO for enhanced charge transfer and catalytic activity, and Fe₃O₄ for magnetic recoverability and electron mediation. The fact that the composite can function optimally in alkaline pH conditions suits the practical requirements of wastewater treatment systems very well. Additionally, the photocatalyst demonstrated high efficiency in the one-pot synthesis of 2-amino-4H-benzochromenes, yielding over 89% under mild and neutral reaction conditions. The efficacy of this process, combined with the ease of catalyst recovery by magnetic separation, suggests its great potential as an economical and sustainable alternative in the organic synthesis of pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals. Recyclability studies further established the strength and stability of the photocatalyst, with more than 80% activity retained even after six cycles. This stability ensures its viability for long-term applications, reducing operational costs and waste generation. As compared to other photocatalysts, such as ZnO thin films, CeO₂-NPs, and mixtures of Cu-Cu₂O-Cu3N, this g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO photocatalyst presents a better combination of efficiency, reusability, and ease of recovery and thus stands out as practical at an industrial scale.

These results demonstrate the great potential of g-C3N4/Fe3O4/CuO composites as an efficient candidate for combating environmental pollution and facilitating green chemical processes. Further research on optimizing synthesis procedures, scale-up methodology, and a wide range of pollutant degradation, coupled with its application in different organic reactions, would be significant. Additionally, it would be helpful to incorporate natural sunlight exposure, testing, and integration into state-of-the-art treatment technologies into the design to enhance the practical applicability related to sustainable technological development.
