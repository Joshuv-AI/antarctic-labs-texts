Pygopagus conjoined twins: A case report and neurosurgical nuances in separation surgery
Authors: Rafael Harter Tomaszeski, Luiza Ribeiro, Lilian Carine Souza de Lima Abdon, Gerson Evandro Perondi, Samir Cezimbra Dos Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Conjoined twins are univitelline, monochorionic, and monoamniotic, with imperfect division. The estimated worldwide prevalence is 1.47/100,000 births, with approximately 50% resulting in live births. They are classified according to the most prominent site of fusion. Pygopagus twins are joined at the sacral region and are the third most common variation, representing 6–19% of conjoined twins. Pygopagus twins may have a fused spinal canal and spinal cord, presenting a neurosurgical challenge during separation surgery.Case Description: We report the case of conjoined twins diagnosed prenatally by ultrasound, who, on radiological investigation after birth, were identified as pygopagus twins with fusion of the lumbosacral vertebral canal and a “U”-shaped spinal union. They underwent successful separation surgery using neuromonitoring.Conclusion: Pygopagus twins with a fused spinal canal and spinal cord are a neurosurgical challenge during separation surgery. It is a rare condition; therefore, meticulous planning is essential for a good prognosis.

INTRODUCTION

Conjoined twins are univitelline, monochorionic, and monoamniotic, with imperfect division. They are classified according to the most prominent site of fusion. Pygopagus twins are joined at the sacral region and represent the third most frequent variation, accounting for 6–19% of conjoined twins.[8,10,11]

Pygopagus twins may have a fused spinal canal and spinal cord, presenting a significant neurosurgical challenge during separation surgery.[8,12] The present article reports a successful case of separation of pygopagus conjoined twins, highlighting important neurosurgical details of neural tissue separation.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 20-year-old mother, with no prior comorbidities, in her second pregnancy, was diagnosed by ultrasound at 9 weeks with a monochorionic monozygotic twin gestation exhibiting incomplete division (conjoined at the sacral region). The patients were delivered through emergency cesarean section at 33 weeks and 2 days due to altered blood flow in fetus 2 as detected by Doppler ultrasound. The APGAR scores were 4/7 for twin 1 and 1/6/8 for twin 2. At birth, the twins were identified as conjoined twins fused at the lumbosacral and perineal regions, presenting with fused male genitalia [Figure 1].

Twins conjoined at the lumbosacral region, consistent with pygopagus conjoined twins, presenting with fused male genitalia.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the neuraxis confirmed fusion at the lumbosacral region, with a shared vertebral canal and a U-shaped fused spinal cord. Twin 2 presented with microcephaly. No radiological abnormalities were observed in the brain of Twin 1 [Figure 2].Computed tomography (CT) of the spine demonstrated posterior vertebral arches at the lumbosacral junction in close proximity but without evidence of osseous fusion [Figure 3]. Twin 1 exhibited partial ossification of 12 thoracic vertebrae, five lumbar vertebrae, and five sacral vertebrae. Twin 2 showed partial ossification of 12 thoracic vertebrae, five lumbar vertebrae, and a single sacral vertebra (S1). At the lumbosacral junction, no bony fusion of the posterior elements was observed; however, continuity of the spinal canal and dural sac was present from the level of L5.

T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging, coronal sections. (a) Shared dural sac. (b) U-shaped fusion of the spinal cord with evidence of tethering.

(a and b) Computed tomography of the spine, coronal sections. Absence of bone fusion between the two twins.

As decided by the multidisciplinary team, surgical separation was scheduled for 6 months of age, involving collaboration between neurosurgery, pediatric surgery, pediatric urology, plastic surgery, and neurophysiology teams. The procedure was thoroughly planned during multidisciplinary meetings and rehearsed using mannequins (common dolls joined together) to optimize the positioning of surgical equipment and determine the sequence of team interventions. The neurosurgical team initiated the separation.

Neurosurgical nuances of the separation procedure

Positioning and incision

The twins were placed in the prone position, with the thorax, abdomen, and head supported, to allow exposure of the spinal region. In collaboration with the plastic surgery team, the initial skin incision line was defined. A midline skin incision was then performed in the craniocaudal direction [Figure 4].

(a) Surgical positioning. (b) Skin incision site marked in red.

Layer-by-layer dissection

Dissection proceeded through the subcutaneous tissue and muscular fasciae until exposure of the dural sacs. The dural sacs of both twins and the site of dural fusion were identified. Adjacent to the fusion site, an extradural lipoma was observed. Partial resection of the extradural lipoma was performed [Figure 5].

Site of dural fusion and extradural lipoma.

Dural opening

Under microscopic magnification, a transverse dural opening was performed. A fused spinal cord and an intradural lipoma adherent to the spinal cord and filum terminale were identified. Following direct neurostimulation, the filum terminale was sectioned, and partial resection of the intradural lipoma was performed. During lipoma resection, a cyst containing sebaceous material was identified, consistent with a dermoid cyst, was identified and excised [Figure 6].

(a) Fused spinal cord and intradural lipom. (b) Dermoid cyst visualized during lipoma resection.

Spinal cord separation

With the aid of neurostimulation, the site for cord section was determined. The midline of the spinal cord elicited positive stimulation for both twins. The decision was made to section the cord at a right paramedian location. Following cord transection, neurulation was performed using 5-0 Prolene sutures [Figure 7].

(a) Neurostimulation to determine the site of cord transection. (b) Appearance following spinal cord separation.

Separation of the posterior dura mater and dural closure

The remaining dura mater was sectioned at the level of the cord transection. A watertight dural closure was achieved. Due to insufficient dura mater to close both dural defects, duraplasty with bovine pericardium was performed in Twin 1. Fibrin sealant was applied to the sutures [Figures 8 and 9].

(a) Final separation of the posterior dura mater after cord division. (b) Appearance after watertight dural closure.

Final macroscopic aspect.

Postoperative outcome

The patients had a satisfactory postoperative course. Histopathological examination of the excised lesions confirmed the diagnoses of dermoid cyst and lipoma. Twin 1 demonstrated good lower limb mobility and full ambulation. Twin 2, due to microcephaly, was non-ambulatory. Neither twin required additional neurosurgical interventions during 1 year of follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Conjoined twins are univitelline, monochorionic, and monoamniotic, with imperfect division. The estimated worldwide prevalence is 1.47/100,000 births, approximately 50% of which are live births.[1,6] They are classified according to the most prominent fusion site: craniopagus (skull), thoracopagus (thorax), omphalopagus (abdomen), pygopagus (sacrum), ischiopagus (pelvis), and rachipagus (spinal canal).[1] The pygopagus subtype is the third most frequent variation, representing 6–19% of conjoined twins.[7]

According to Hirokazu et al., 31 pygopagus twins were described in the literature up to 2013, with a noted female preponderance (81%). Pygopagus conjoined twins can be fused at the perineal region, neural tissues, vascular and gastrointestinal structures, urinary tract, and reproductive organs to varying degrees. They are usually joined at the sacrum and sometimes have a single dural sac and fused spinal cord.[7] Conjoined twins can be diagnosed in the prenatal period by fetal ultrasound and MRI.[6] Prenatal diagnosis is essential for evaluating the prognosis and planning delivery and postnatal surgical correction. Vaginal delivery is contraindicated in these pregnancies due to the possibility of fetal complications, dystocia, uterine rupture, and maternal injuries. Cesarean section is recommended in advanced pregnancies to reduce maternal and fetal risks.[1]

Elective separation is feasible in omphalopagus, pygopagus, thoracopagus, and some craniopagus twins. Emergency separation may be necessary when there is damage to the connection site (e.g., omphalopagus), when one twin threatens the life of the other (e.g., congenital complex heart disease, cardiomyopathy, and sepsis), when dysfunction of both twins occurs due to hemodynamic or respiratory impairment (usually in thoracopagus), or when the condition of one twin is incompatible with life (e.g., anencephaly and stillbirth) and the other has a good chance of survival.[6]

Separation is generally best performed electively after the twins gain weight and achieve clinical stability, particularly in pygopagus cases where vital organs are not shared. In addition, preoperative investigation, imaging, and planning by several specialized teams are necessary for successful separation surgery. Emergency surgery has a significantly higher mortality rate compared to elective surgery. Ethical and legal questions should be addressed before proceeding with surgical separation in cases where one of the twins is unlikely to survive the process.[2,5]

Some issues should be considered, especially for neurosurgeons responsible for separating pygopagus twins: (1) define the existence and extent of neural structure fusion through preoperative imaging, including CT and MRI; (2) in conjunction with the neurophysiology team, use intraoperative nerve monitoring devices in cases of spinal cord involvement; (3) perform watertight dural closure, preferably using autologous tissues, to avoid postoperative cerebrospinal fluid leakage; and (4) finalize the manipulation of neural structures before proceeding to gastrointestinal surgery to avoid postoperative meningitis.[7,10,12]

Postoperative cerebrospinal fluid fistula is reported as a probable complication, mainly due to the complexity of achieving a watertight dural closure in these cases. This condition presents an elevated risk of meningitis, especially since the twins often also undergo surgery on the gastrointestinal tract.[7]

Several cases of surgical separation of pygopagus twins have been reported, but few successful separations involving fused spinal cords.[4] The fused spinal cord can be classified as V-shaped, Y-shaped, or U-shaped.[2] In U-shaped spinal fusion, as in the reported case, it is more difficult to distinguish the cleavage plane due to the difficulty of determining the anatomical midline.[2] Thus, defining the functional midline using neurophysiological stimulation is essential to proceed with the separation. Using three-dimensional models based on CT and MRI of the genesis can also improve the planning process and assist in intraoperative decision-making.[3,8,9,11]

Careful postoperative follow-up is important, as the neurological outcome is uncertain in the long term. Sphincter and perineal changes can be expected, considering the neural union, generally at the sacral level. Sequelae affecting lower limb motor function, especially distally, are possible but less likely. In the reported case, a lipoma adjacent to the junction site increased the likelihood of spinal tethering. The possibility of tethered cord syndrome should be considered and monitored as the patients grow.[8]

CONCLUSION

Successful separation of conjoined twins is a challenge. Pygopagus conjoined twins with fusion of spinal neural tissues present extreme neurosurgical complexity for separation surgery. The extent of organ fusion differs from case to case; therefore, detailed preoperative evaluation, surgical planning, and a multidisciplinary approach are essential to achieve the best outcome. With this case report and literature review, we hope to contribute to the accumulation of experiences and knowledge about pygopagus conjoined twins.
