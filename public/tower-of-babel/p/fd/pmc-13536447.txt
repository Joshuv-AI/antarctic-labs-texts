Strategy of allying general dentistry staffs into pre-anesthetic dental assessment reduces perioperative dental injury risk during general anesthesia
Authors: I-Cheng Lu, Mei-Chun Chen, Po-Yang Chen, Tz-Ping Gau, Edward Chengchuan Ko, Kuang-I Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposePerioperative dental injury remains a common complication during general anesthesia. This study aimed to evaluate whether professional dentistry intervention in pre-anesthetic assessment reduces the risk of perioperative dental injury.Materials and methodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 119,656 patients who underwent general anesthesia between May 2013 and April 2021. Patients were classified into three periods based on the type of medical personnel conducting pre-anesthetic dental assessments: Period I, dental interns and registered anesthesia nurses (May 2013 to September 2016, n = 38,039); Period II, postgraduate year (PGY) dental residents (October 2016 to September 2019, n = 37,750); and Period III, a team of general dentistry staff (dental residents and specialists) (October 2019 to April 2021, n = 43,867). The primary outcome assessed was the incidence of perioperative dental injury.ResultsIntubation-related dental injuries occurred in 41 of 119,656 anesthetic cases (0.034 %). The incidence of tooth loss was significantly lower in Period III assessed by general dentistry staff (0.018 %) compared with Period I, evaluated by dental interns and registered anesthesia nurses (0.055 %) and Period II, mainly assessed by PGY dental residents (0.031 %) (P = 0.016). Male patients had a significantly higher risk of perioperative dental injury (P < 0.05). Moderate to severe tooth mobility was significantly associated with an increased risk of perioperative dental injury (all P < 0.05).ConclusionThe involvement of general dentistry staff in pre-anesthetic dental assessments can reduce the incidence of perioperative dental injuries. Patients scheduled for surgery should receive not only pre-anesthesia consultations from anesthesiologists, but also routine assessments and dental care provided by general dentistry staff.Keywords: Preoperative anesthesia assessment, General dentistry, General anesthesia, Perioperative dental injury

Introduction

Perioperative dental injuries remain one of the most frequently encountered complications of general anesthesia, with reported global incidence rates ranging from 0.02 % to 0.7 %.1–4 These injuries range in severity from minor enamel fractures and crown damage to complete tooth avulsion, commonly affecting the upper incisors.5,6 Despite notable technological advances in anesthetic and airway management practices, the occurrence of dental trauma during anesthesia has not substantially decreased, indicating that further workflow improvements need to be implemented.7 Recent studies further support this observation. A retrospective analysis in Singapore reported a dental injury rate of 0.092 % in general anesthesia cases, which was significantly associated with pre-existing dental pathology, difficult airway conditions, and specific airway instrumentation techniques.8 Similarly, a prospective pediatric study conducted in Syria reported a 9.1 % incidence of dental injuries, primarily associated with challenging intubation scenarios and multiple attempts, and predominantly presenting as soft-tissue trauma.9 Beyond the immediate physical consequences for patients, dental injuries can lead to additional treatment costs, prolonged recovery, and represent a substantial source of patient dissatisfaction and potential litigation.10,11 Studies indicate that dental damage accounts for approximately 20–30 % of all anesthesia- related malpractice claims across various healthcare systems, representing a significant medicolegal and financial burden for both healthcare institutions and practitioners.11,12

Current strategies for preventing perioperative dental injury have evolved but remain fragmented. Traditional pre-anesthesia evaluation typically incorporates a brief oral examination as part of airway assessment protocols, primarily focused on identifying anatomical predictors of difficult intubation rather than conducting a comprehensive evaluation of dental vulnerabilities.4,13,14 Contemporary preventive measures include intraoperative techniques, such as tooth padding with gauze (employed in approximately 74 % of cases), the use of video laryngoscopy or fiberoptic bronchoscopy for intubation to minimize direct laryngeal contact, and preoperative identification of high-risk dental pathology.15 Additionally, preoperative dental consultation and dental interventions may reduce injury risk when implemented systematically.15 However, due to the complexity of surgical procedures and anesthesia management, performing a precise and thorough preoperative dental assessment remains challenging. Furthermore, anesthesiologists and other non-dental healthcare providers often have limited expertise in identifying subtle dental risk factors, such as compromised tooth integrity, existing restorations, periodontal disease, or structural weaknesses in crowns and alveolar bone.14,16 Consequently, assessments conducted solely by anesthesiologists may not adequately mitigate the risk of perioperative dental injuries. While many institutions have implemented routine preoperative oral examinations, these assessments typically rely only on visual inspection and palpation of tooth mobility, which may limit their diagnostic accuracy.17 Moreover, previous research predominantly addresses the incidence and risk factors of anesthesia-related dental injuries, with limited data evaluating the differential impact of provider type conducting pre-anesthesia dental assessments.

This study aimed to evaluate whether integrating general dentistry staff into pre-anesthetic assessments reduces the incidence of perioperative dental injury compared with non-dentist screeners. We hypothesized that structured risk identification and mitigation were expected to lower injury incidence compared with non-dentist screening. These findings provide evidence for dentist-led screening into preoperative evaluation protocols to enhance patient safety during general anesthesia.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study was conducted at a medical center in southern Taiwan, analyzing data collected from May 1, 2013, to April 30, 2021. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kaohsiung Medical University Hospital (KMUHIRB-E(I)-20210227), with a waiver of informed consent granted due to the minimal-risk nature of the study design. All participant data were de-identified prior to analysis. Patients aged 12 years and older who underwent elective surgical procedures under various anesthesia methods were included. A total of 147,386 anesthesia records were screened. Emergency cases and patients younger than 12 years were excluded. Pediatric cases (< 12 years) were excluded because (1) mixed dentition and exfoliating primary teeth produce physiologic mobility that confounds dental-injury risk classification and outcome attribution; (2) pediatric airway devices and peri-operative processes (e.g., cuffless endotracheal tubes, parental consent) differ from adults and entail a distinct injury–risk profile; and (3) our structured pre-anesthetic dental screening checklist was developed for adult dentition. After exclusion, 119,656 records were analyzed (Fig. 1). These cases were stratified into three consecutive assessment periods based on the provider responsible for the pre-anesthetic dental evaluation.

Flowchart of the study.

From 147,386 anesthesia records screened, 27,730 emergency cases and patients younger than 12 years were excluded. The remaining 119,656 elective cases were assigned to three consecutive assessment periods according to the provider performing the pre-anesthetic dental evaluation: Period I (May 2013–September 2016, n = 38,039), Period II (October 2016–September 2019, n = 37,750), and Period III (October 2019–April 2021, n = 43,867).

Data collection

Collected data included demographic information (gender and age group), anesthesia techniques (endotracheal general anesthesia [ETGA] with double-lumen tube, nasal or oral endotracheal tube, tracheotomy tube; intravenous general anesthesia [IVGA]; axillary block; epidural anesthesia [EPI]; local standby anesthesia; mask anesthesia; and laryngeal mask airway [LMA]), and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification.

Dental assessments were conducted using an identical, standardized checklist across all periods, including standardized dental charts (universal numbering system, teeth 1–32), tooth mobility grading (none, mild, moderate, severe), Mallampati score (Class I–IV), number of missing teeth, thyromental distance (TMD, cm), incidence of dental loss, and whether a dental consultation for high-risk dental management was initiated prior to anesthesia. Patients were categorized by the provider performing the assessment: Period I (May 2013–September 2016), dental interns working with anesthesia nurses (n = 38,039); Period II (October 2016–September 2019), PGY dental residents supervised by board-certified general dentists (n = 37,750); and Period III (October 2019–April 2021), general dentistry residents and specialists (n = 43,867). Workflow, forms, and checklist content were held constant across periods. In all three Periods, dental staff were supervised by general dentists and adverse events were reviewed in monthly quality meetings.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was the incidence of unexpected perioperative dental injury, defined as dental trauma or incomplete tooth fractures occurring during anesthesia induction, maintenance, or emergence. Additionally, the relationships between dental injury incidence and anesthesia modality (oral or nasal endotracheal intubation versus laryngeal mask airway) were assessed. Secondary outcomes included the number and incidence of dental consultations for advanced tooth or gingival management following the routine detailed dental evaluations, including Mallampati classification, degree of tooth mobility (none, mild, moderate and severe), missing teeth count, and thyromental distance (TMD).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations) were used to summarize patient data. Chi-square tests evaluated differences in dental conditions and demographics across periods. Logistic regression analysis provided odds ratios (OR) and 95 % confidence intervals (CI) for factors associated with dental injuries including gender, dental mobility and diabetes history. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Demographic characteristics revealed that of 119,656 patients analyzed, 55.8 % were female (n = 66,765). Most patients were aged between 56 and 65 years (23.62 %, n = 28,260), followed by the age group 46–55 years (20.54 %, n = 24,577). ASA physical status was primarily ASA II (62.55 %, n = 74,850), followed by ASA III (32.23 %, n = 38,565) (Table 1).

Dental consultation

The requirements for advanced dental and gingival management following the initial preoperative evaluation significantly declined over time, decreasing from 9.62 % in Period I (n = 3,660) to 8.06 % in Period II (n = 3,044), and further to 7.64 % in Period III (n = 3,353) (P < 0.001) (Table 4). It means that regular dental assessment by experienced dental staff can identify potentially high-risk teeth and avoid unnecessary stabilization of mobile teeth or orthodontic appliances.

Perioperative dental injury

The incidence of perioperative dental injury demonstrated a statistically significant decline across the three Periods: 0.055 % in Period I (21/38,039), 0.031 % in Period II (12/37,750), and 0.018 % in Period III (8/43,867) (P = 0.016) (Fig. 2). Male patients exhibited a significantly increased likelihood of experiencing dental trauma during the perianesthetic period, with an adjusted odds ratio (OR) of 3.18 (95 % CI: 0.158–6.399; P < 0.001). In comparison to patient without dental mobility, moderate mobility demonstrated a significant association with increased injury risk (OR: 3.06; 95 % CI: 1.058–8.853; P = 0.039), and severe mobility was linked to a markedly elevated risk (OR: 13.75; 95 % CI: 6.538–28.92; P < 0.001).

Risk factors for injury

Teeth injuries occurred in 30 of 44,132 patients undergoing endotracheal intubation and in 11 of 38,571 patients undergoing LMA insertion, respectively. There is a clinically significant difference in dental injury rates between the two airway devices (P = 0.011). However, patients intubated with a double-lumen endotracheal tube, a nasotracheal tube, or a standard orotracheal tube demonstrated similar incidences of dental injury, occurring in 1 of 2,695, 3 of 6,464, and 26 of 44,132 patients, respectively. Of the total 41 dental injury cases, most injuries in Periods I and II occurred during oral endotracheal intubation (71.4 % and 58.3 %, respectively), whereas injuries in Period III were predominantly associated with LMA use (50.0 %) (Table 5). Although intubation was associated with significantly more dental injuries (73.2 %, 30/41) compared with LMA use (26.8 %, 11/41), further analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in intubation-related dental injuries in Period III (P = 0.02). However, the incidence of LMA-related dental injury did not decrease from Period I to Period III.

Airway device analyses

To evaluate video-stylet intubating devices (Trachway) in relation to dental injuries, 3 of 15 cases occurred in Period I, 6 of 10 in Period II, and 3 of 4 in Period III. Among these 12 cases, 8 dental injuries occurred during intubation, 1 during endotracheal tube removal, and 3 were identified after tube removal. For conventional laryngoscopy, dental injuries were observed in 12 of 15 cases in Period I, 4 of 10 in Period II, and 1 of 4 in Period III. Among these 17 cases, 9 injuries occurred during intubation, 7 were identified after tube removal, and 1 was associated with naso-esophageal tube insertion. Regarding LMA use, 12 dental injury cases were reported: 2 occurred during LMA placement, 1 during LMA removal, and 9 were identified after device removal.

Discussion

The primary finding of this study was a significant reduction in the incidence of perioperative dental injury decreasing from 0.055 % in Period I to 0.031 % in Period II and 0.018 % in Period III (P = 0.016). Furthermore, integrating general dentistry staff into pre-anesthetic dental assessments resulted in fewer dental consultations for mobile teeth requiring preoperative dental management. In addition, male patient or previous mobile teeth increased the incidence of dental injury. These results demonstrate that increased involvement of dental professionals improves the accurate identification and perioperative management of patients at risk for dental injury. Although the reduction from 21 to 8 cases may appear numerically modest, each perioperative dental injury carries substantial clinical and economic consequences. Individual cases may require urgent prosthodontic intervention, compromise patient satisfaction, and generate significant medicolegal expenses. The general dentistry assessment implemented in this study leverages existing clinical personnel and a standardized checklist, thereby minimizing marginal costs relative to the expenditure associated with a single injury and its medicolegal burden.

Standardized preoperative dental assessment protocols significantly reduce the incidence of perioperative dental injuries, as demonstrated by quality improvement initiatives reporting up to a 90 % reduction following structured dental evaluations.7 Essential components of these protocols include 1) electronic documentation of existing dentition; 2) comprehensive visual inspection and mobility assessment for identifying high-risk conditions (e.g., loose teeth, prosthetic devices); 3) risk stratification based on Mallampati classification and dental pathology;7,18–20 4) protective interventions, such as dental guards or padding, for high-risk patients;2,21 and 5) the use of video laryngoscopes, video stylets, or fiberoptic bronchoscopes for intubation. Institutions employing such structured protocols not only enhance patient safety but also substantially reduce the medicolegal risks associated with anesthesia-related dental trauma.2,7 Based on structured assessments, emergent intubations performed after preoperative screening have been associated with a 25 % reduction in dental injury occurrence compared with unscreened procedures.18 Furthermore, validated guidelines emphasize the value of specialist-led assessments in detecting subtle vulnerabilities and optimizing risk mitigation. 18,20 Although our teams fully adhered to the abovementioned protocols, a 0.031 % incidence of tooth injury still occurred during Period II. This is the reason why general dentistry staff were introduced to assess patients.

This study found a significant increase in the detection rate of preoperative tooth mobility after general dentistry staff were incorporated into the evaluation process, rising progressively from Period I (11.93 %) to Period II (13.17 %) and Period III (14.98 %; P < 0.001). This trend highlights the enhanced diagnostic accuracy and sensitivity of dental specialists compared with traditional anesthesiology assessments. However, Period III demonstrated a dental injury rate of only 0.018 %. The clinical significance of this reduction extends beyond the absolute percentage to possible mechanisms by which general dentistry participation lowered the risk. First, before anesthesia, general dentistry staff might identify vulnerable dentition including pathologic tooth mobility, compromised restorations, ceramic veneers, implants and periodontal disease. Moreover, general dentistry enabled targeted preventive interventions such as customized protective measures (precision-fitted mouthguards or bite-blocks), localized soft tissue padding and dental stabilization. Second, structured communication of risk stratification to anesthesiologists enabled optimal airway management (gentle laryngoscopy, video-assisted or fiberoptic techniques, smaller blades/tubes or limiting attempts when appropriate), thereby reducing tooth–device contact. Prior research consistently demonstrates the superior accuracy and effectiveness of dental assessments conducted by trained dental professionals, underscoring their critical role in identifying subtle dental vulnerabilities that are often overlooked by non-specialists.11,22 Improved identification of dental issues enables targeted preventive measures, ultimately decreasing perioperative complications and improving overall patient safety.23,24 The critical role of preoperative evaluation is underscored by a 2023 study demonstrating that structured dental questionnaires can identify high-risk patients requiring mouthguards, particularly those with ceramic prostheses (OR = 18.2), masticatory pain (OR = 12.8), or dental implants (OR = 9.4).23 Adding dentistry staff to the preoperative assessment protocol reduced preventable injuries by ensuring targeted protection for anatomically vulnerable dentition. Recent evidence further emphasizes the value of structured dental questionnaires and specialist-led evaluations in accurately identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from targeted protective measures.24

From our results, the authors also observed significant procedure-related shifts in dental injury patterns: in Period I, most dental injuries were caused by endotracheal intubation, whereas in Period III, injuries were predominantly associated with LMA placement. A 2024 prospective study of 110 pediatric intubations reported a 9.1 % incidence of traumatic dental injuries (TDIs), with 37.5 % involving tooth damage (concussion or avulsion) and 62.5 % affecting the oral mucosa.25 This aligns with earlier evidence showing that difficult intubation (Mallampati III/IV) increases the risk of TDIs by 4.8-fold due to contact between the laryngoscope blade and teeth.26 Even less invasive techniques, such as the use of laryngeal mask airways (LMAs), carry measurable risks of dental injury, particularly in patients with challenging airway anatomy or when repeated airway manipulations are required.27 Thus, individualized airway management strategies guided by comprehensive preoperative dental evaluations are critical for minimizing the risk of procedural dental trauma.

Severe tooth mobility is a significant patient-related risk factor for perioperative dental injuries in this study, presenting a substantially elevated risk (OR: 13.75). In the literature, patients with compromised dental status, including loose teeth, severe periodontal disease, extensive dental restorations, or unstable prosthetics demonstrate nearly a fivefold higher risk of dental injury during anesthesia compared with those with healthy dentition.15,28 Additionally, anatomical variations that complicate airway management (e.g., limited mouth opening, retrognathia, prominent incisors) further increase the risk of dental injury.3,6 These factors underscore the necessity of meticulous preoperative dental assessments, particularly for patients identified as high-risk based on dental or anatomical features.

Several limitations of this retrospective study must be acknowledged. First, its observational design inherently limits the strength of causal inference compared to prospective randomized trials. Second, potential detection bias exists, as subtle dental injuries may have been underreported in periods without specialized dental evaluations, possibly resulting in an underestimation of the true incidence of dental trauma. Third, variability in evaluation personnel, documentation practices, and institutional policies across the three periods could influence outcomes, complicating direct comparisons. Furthermore, simultaneous changes in airway management strategies, provider training, or quality improvement initiatives may also confound observed associations, thereby affecting the interpretation of the findings. Finally, although our findings are based on a single academic center, several intervention components are generalizable, including standardized dental documentation, explicit risk stratification, and targeted counseling. We therefore present this model as a template that can be adapted to local resources. However, external validity may vary across healthcare systems due to differences in the availability of airway devices, the workflows of preoperative evaluation, and the accessibility of dental professionals.

In conclusion, integrating general dentistry staff into pre-anesthetic assessments significantly reduces perioperative dental injuries by enabling comprehensive identification of vulnerable teeth and periodontal conditions, as well as facilitating targeted preventive interventions. General dentistry staff provide specialized expertise that enhances the evaluation of dental risk beyond standard anesthesiology assessments. Routinely incorporating structured dental evaluations into preoperative care protocols offers an effective strategy to improve patient safety, minimize anesthesia-related dental morbidity, and potentially reduce associated litigation risks. Healthcare institutions are therefore encouraged to support interdisciplinary collaboration between anesthesiology and general dentistry staff to achieve optimal patient outcomes.
