Relationship between modifiable factors and late pregnancy physical activity on infant motor development at 12 months of age: findings from a rural city in the Mid-Southern USA
Authors: Rachel Tinius, Madhawa Perera, Gregory S Hawk, Lauren Powell, Nikitha Rajendran, Maire M Blankenship, Karen Furgal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo assess the relationship of infant growth, feeding practices and tummy time to their motor development at 12 months, with a special focus on how maternal physical activity during late pregnancy relates to infants’ motor skills.DesignLongitudinal study.SettingRural city in the Mid-Southern USA.Participants16 singleton pregnant women in the third trimester and their term infants were recruited, excluding mother–infant pairs with health issues that impact infants’ motor development and restrict mothers’ physical activity.Primary and secondary outcome measuresMaternal physical activity and sedentary time during the third trimester were measured using Actigraph activity monitors. Labour nurses measured neonatal birth weight and length using standard procedures. Infants’ motor percentiles at 4 and 12 months were measured respectively using the Alberta Infant Motor Scale and Peabody Developmental Motor Scales II test by a licensed paediatric physical therapist. Introduction

Motor skill development in the early years of life is an important way to determine the health of a young child, and it is also linked to well-being and health later in life.1 The rate of motor development in early childhood predicts adolescents’ cognitive abilities and educational attainments,2,5 while it also plays a major role in promoting physical activity and physical fitness in adolescents.6,13 The promotion of activity in youth is extremely important as it can help to ameliorate childhood obesity,7 14 15 which often continues into adult years.16,18 Unfortunately, the prevalence of gross motor delays among preschool children has increased steadily and needs immediate attention.19 The delays in motor development are well-aligned with the prevalence of physical inactivity20 and obesity among school-going children in the USA.21 The prevailing epidemics of gross motor development delays, physical inactivity and obesity among children in the country urge clinicians and researchers to assess the factors associated with motor development in early childhood, which play a role and are possibly an ontogenetic cause for the aforementioned issues.16

Motor development in childhood is multifactorial and is thought to be influenced by biological, behavioural, psychological, environmental and socioeconomic factors. For example, increasing children’s age is a constant correlate of motor development in preschool and school-age children, whereas maintaining a healthy weight in childhood, children’s participation in physical activity and higher socioeconomic status of the family often correlate positively with motor development in children aged 3–18 years.22 However, such clear correlates of motor development have not been defined explicitly for infants. Feeding practices in infancy, family environment and infants’ sleep-related factors (eg, sleep position) may impact motor development, but collectively, insufficient evidence was found to make rigid conclusions for this age group.23 Thus, understanding the dynamic relationship of these factors to infant motor development is essential to designing effective interventions to prevent adverse effects of poor motor competence in infancy and tracking its impediments into adulthood.17 22 23 Of those factors, maternal physical activity, infant feeding practices and tummy time are of special interest because such factors are modifiable.22 24 In particular, the effects of maternal physical activity during pregnancy on infant motor development have recently gained considerable attention, as it serves as one of the earliest interventions to boost motor development and physical activity in children while also helping to reduce maternal complications during childbirth.25 26

Evidence implies that maternal physical activity during pregnancy and postpartum impacts newborns’ physical activity in infancy, where these increased physical activity levels in infancy impact motor skill development.2227,29 Thus, conceptually, maternal physical activity during pregnancy directly impacts infants’ motor competence.16 18 Maternal physical activity during pregnancy also favourably impacts neurodevelopment, cerebral maturation and neuro-behavioural aspects of newborns,30,32 suggesting potential associations of physical activity during pregnancy with the motor development of infants (age 0–12 months). However, very few original research studies empirically support the notion that maternal physical activity impacts the motor scores of infants.26 To the best of our knowledge, only one study assessed the impacts of supervised exercise during pregnancy on the motor development of infants,26 and none have assessed how habitual physical activity in pregnancy impacts infants’ motor scores. Additionally, most other studies on infant motor development relied on parental self-reports23 (vs validated motor assessments by a professional), which can lead to significant misinterpretation as well as bias towards wanting to meet social norms. On the other hand, cross-sectional studies are ample on this topic,22 longitudinal studies assessing factors associated with infant motor development in the North American region are lacking.23 24

Considering the clear gap in the literature and the potential public health implications of this topic, this longitudinal study aimed to assess the relationship of infant anthropometrics, feeding practices, tummy time, having siblings and maternal physical activity during late pregnancy to motor development of infants at 12 months of age based on in-person clinical assessments by a licensed paediatric physical therapist.

Methods

Participants

Pregnant participants were recruited between September 2016 and December 2017 between 28 and 32 weeks pregnant as part of a larger ongoing study to assess metabolic health during pregnancy.25 33 All participants were patients delivering infants at the Bowling Green Medical Center in Bowling Green, Kentucky. Participants for the larger study were recruited from local obstetric clinics, university-wide emails and word of mouth.33 Inclusion criteria included ages 18–44, confirmed singleton viable pregnancy with no fetal abnormalities at routine 18–22 ultrasonography, plans to deliver at The Medical Center in Bowling Green, KY, and a signed physicians’ release form to participate in the study procedures.25 Exclusions included multiple gestation pregnancy, currently using drugs, smoking cigarettes, taking daily medications (corticosteroids or anti-psychotics), a history of gestational diabetes or diabetes mellitus or a glucose test result suggestive of gestational diabetes in the current pregnancy, or dietary restrictions that would inhibit ability to consume the high-fat meal as part of the initial study.25 33 For the present study, all women included in the original study were invited to participate in the follow-up study unless the infant was born prematurely (before 37 weeks) or diagnosed with any substantial health issues that could influence their motor development (eg, major gastro-oesophageal or digestive issues).25

Sample size

A total of 16 mother–infant dyads were included in the sample for the study. For the 4-month time point, 33 of the 64 women–infant pairs included in the original study accepted the invitation to participate and returned for the 4-month motor development study visit. Of the 33 who came back at 4 months, 16 mother–infant pairs responded to requests to return a second time at 12 months, and all 16 were able to return at 12 months, which is the sample size for the present study. Many women did not reply to our invitations to return for the motor assessments (31/64 for 4-month visit and 17/33 for the 12-month visit). However, none of the women who accepted our invitation to participate in motor visits and enrolled in this part of the study showed up or completed the motor visit (ie, once enrolled, there was no additional drop out).

Data collection

Patients were not involved in the design of the study procedures. Data collection was performed at four distinct time points, including maternal assessments in late pregnancy, infant anthropometrics 24–48 hours postbirth, infant motor skills at 4 months and infant motor skills at 12 months (figure 1).

Study time points. AIMS, Alberta Infant Motor Scale; PDMS II, Peabody Developmental Motor Scales II.

Late pregnancy

As part of the larger study,25 33 maternal physical activity was collected via Actigraph Link Accelerometer (ActiGraph, Pensacola, Florida, USA) on the nondominant wrist for seven consecutive days. The ActiLife Software determined the percentage of time spent sedentary and participating in a range of activity levels (light, moderate and vigorous). During the initial study visits, maternal anthropometrics and vital signs were also collected by trained study team members. Maternal body fat percentage and body composition were measured using standard skinfold anthropometrics. Seven skinfold sites were measured (Harpenden Skinfold Caliper, Baty International, UK) and each skin fold thickness was entered into a standardised equation that accounted for age.34

Birth

On the infant’s birth, labour and delivery nurses measured neonatal weight, height and head circumference. Neonatal body composition was measured using standard skinfold anthropometry by the study team, testing the same seven skinfold sites that were measured for the mothers, within 48 hours of delivery.

Four months

Infants were scheduled to come for their motor development assessment between 4 months, 0 days and 4 months and 13 days. More details about the 4-month session have been previously published.25 The focus of these analyses is on 12-month data. Infant motor development was assessed using the Alberta Infant Motor Scale (AIMS), a well-established test to determine infant motor development percentiles,35 and performed by a licensed paediatric physical therapist. The AIMS test consists of 58 items and assesses the infant in four positions: prone (21 items), supine (9 items), sitting (12 items) and standing (16 items).35 The AIMS is considered a valid and reliable test of infant motor development.35 Infant motor percentile (not the raw score) was recorded as this accounts for the exact age of the infant, which is important as motor patterns change very quickly in infants (ie, 4 months, 0 days vs 4 months and 13 days). All testing sessions were recorded, and videos were used to allow the test administrator to go back and rescore the test if any clarity was needed.

Twelve months

Each of the mother–infant pairs that completed the AIMS study visit at 4 months old was contacted again to return for the 12-month assessment. If they agreed to participate, they were scheduled within a 2-week window following the infant’s first birthday.

Each visit occurred in the Physical Therapy Exercise Laboratory at the Medical Centre. The Peabody Developmental Motor Skills (PDMS II) test was performed on each infant by the same licensed paediatric physical therapist as in the 4-month session. The PDMS II consists of six different sections: reflexes, stationary, locomotion, object manipulation, grasping and visual-motor integration.36 To determine a gross motor score that could be compared with the AIMS scores, three of the six categories were tested: stationary, locomotion and object manipulation. The stationary subtest measures a child’s ability to sustain control of the body within its centre of gravity and retain equilibrium. The locomotion subtest measures behaviours that children use to transport themselves from one place to another, such as crawling, walking, running, hopping and jumping forward. The object manipulation subtest measures a child’s movements needed to catch and throw objects. Because these skills often do not become apparent until a child reaches 11 months of age, this subtest is only given to children aged 12 months and older. These three categories were compiled to create a total score of the infant’s motor development. This score was converted to a percentile. Similar to the 4-month data collections, all testing sessions were recorded, and videos were used to allow the test administrator to go back and rescore the test if any clarity was needed. For both the 4-month and 12-month infant assessments, the paediatric physical therapist taking the measurements was blinded to all other patient data, removing a major source of potential bias.

Surveys

Surveys were provided to participants prior to the study visit inquiring about a number of factors that could also influence the child’s motor development. They were encouraged to have them completed on arrival, but if they were not able to do this, they were able to complete them while at the study visit. These included information about time spent in different positions (supine, prone, standing), sleeping practices, family dynamics and existing health issues. A survey was developed to assess infant feeding practices at 4 and 12 months. Participants were asked if they were breastfeeding, formula feeding or a combination. If they reported breastfeeding, they were also asked the method of feeding (bottle, nursing or combination). Mothers were only asked about breastfeeding practices at that point in time (4 months and 12 months). For the present study, exclusive breastfeeding was defined as the infant receiving mother’s own milk from the breast or expressed and received through a bottle, and that the infant is not receiving any other foods or formula.37

Statistical analysis

All data were collected and stored using REDCap electronic software. Infant and mother clinical outcomes and demographic characteristics were summarised using means and standard deviations (for quantitative measures) or counts and percentages (for categorical measures). Additionally, each of these variables’ associations with 12-month infant motor scale percentiles were analysed using linear models and two-sample t-tests, as appropriate. Model assumptions, including normality, were assessed using a combination of visual plots and formal testing. Across all analyses, a p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R, V.4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing; Vienna, Austria). The study size was determined for the larger study,25 33 and every attempt was made to retain and bring back as many infants for the present study as possible.

Maternal and infant characteristics

Motor scores

The average AIMS motor score evaluated at the 4-month mark was 18.2±3.6. A more meaningful expression of scores for standardised testing is to report as a percentile rank. The corresponding AIMS percentile (%) at 4-month testing was 54.5±27.3. The average PDMS-II motor score evaluated at the 12-month mark was 30.3±3.8. The corresponding PDMS II percentile (%) was 52.1±17.9. Infant motor skills percentiles at 4 months were positively associated with infant motor skills percentiles at 12 months (r=0.649, p=0.009). For each additional percentile at 4 months, a mean estimated increase of 0.4 percentile points at 12 months was observed, as can be seen in figure 2. Motor score percentiles for the AIMS test at 4 months of age and the Peabody (PDMS II) test at 12 months of age were positively correlated (r=0.649, p=0.009). AIMS, Alberta Infant Motor Scale; PDMS II, Peabody Developmental Motor Scales II.

Physical activity during pregnancy

During the third trimester, maternal objective physical activity was categorised based on the amount of time they spent exercising during a single week. The average amount of time (%) the women spent sedentary was 56.4±13.9. The average amount of time (%) the women spent participating in light activity was 31.7±9.9. The average amount of time (%) the women spent participating in moderate activity was 11.9±4.7.

Infant motor skills percentiles at 12 months were not associated with time spent sedentary (r = –0.134, p=0.634), time spent participating in light activity (r=0.213, p=0.447) or time spent participating in moderate activity (r=–0.050, p=0.858) during the third trimester of the mother’s pregnancy.

Infant anthropometrics

At 12 months of age, average female PDMS II percentile scores (54.5±23.9) and average male PDMS II percentile scores (50.7±14.6) were similar (p=0.735). The average infant weight at the 12-month check-up was 9.4±1.0 (kg) and the average infant height was 77.3±4.0 (cm). There was a significant association between motor score percentiles at 12 months of age and infant birth weight (r=0.553, p=0.026) (figure 3), and between motor score percentiles at 12 months of age and infant birth length (r=0.637, p=0.008) (figure 4).

Infant length at birth was positively correlated to motor skills percentiles at 12 months of age (r=0.637, p=0.008). PDMS II, Peabody Developmental Motor Scales II.

Infant feeding practices

The mean PDMS II percentile for infants at 12 months that still consumed breast milk was 49.5±21.7. The mean PDMSII percentile for infants at 12 months who no longer consumed breast milk was 54.8±14.2. At 12 months, breastfeeding status did not appear to be associated with motor scores (p=0.576).

Infant time spent on the stomach (‘tummy time’)

There was not a significant relationship between infant motor scores and the total time an infant spent in prone (‘tummy time’) at 12 months (r=–0.069, p=0.840).

The impact of siblings

Infant PDMS II scores at 12 months of age appeared higher for infants with siblings (56.4±20.4) compared with those without (45.0±10.9); however, this finding did not achieve statistical significance (p=0.230).

Discussion

This longitudinal cohort study assessed the relationship of infants’ motor scores at 4 months, anthropometrics, feeding practices, tummy time, maternal physical activity during the third trimester and having siblings to the motor development of infants at 12 months. The study found that infants’ motor percentiles at 4 months, birth weight and length were positively associated with their motor percentiles at 12 months. Neither maternal physical activity during the third trimester nor tummy time was associated with infants’ motor percentiles at 12 months. Also, we did not observe a significant impact of siblings or breastfeeding on motor development of infants at 12 months.

Motor scores

Our results revealed a positive dose–response relationship between infant motor development percentiles at 4 months and 12 months, with a mean estimated increase of 0.4 units observed in 12-month percentile score for each additional percentile score at 4 months. This observation supports Stodden et al’s conceptual model of the reciprocal relationship between motor development and physical activity participation.16 According to their hypothesis, higher motor development in infancy inflates infants’ perceived motor competence, even above their actual competence level, encouraging the acquisition of more motor skills by attempting new physical activities that increase their actual neuro-motor development as they grow.16 18 Our observation also aligns well with the notion that infants who start ahead tend to stay ahead.38 Although we found one study that tracked motor trajectories from infancy to later school ages,5 that study did not compare motor development at 4 and 12 months, leaving no studies to compare our findings. However, our observation underscores the importance of assessing and monitoring motor development early in life because it tracks into subsequent developmental stages.

Physical activity during pregnancy

Maternal physical activity in the third trimester, including the percentage of time spent on light and moderate activity, was not associated with the infants’ motor development percentiles at 12 months in our study. Similarly, the sedentary time percentage also did not correlate well with motor percentiles at 12 months. Although this observation is well aligned with our previous study findings at 4 months,25 it does not support previous randomised control trials that advocated the positive effects of exercise during pregnancy for infant motor and neurobehavioural development.26 32 However, both of these randomised control trials had supervised structured exercise programmes for pregnant women,26 32 whereas our study focused on daily habitual physical activity or exercise, which is more practical for them to continue than strictly supervised exercise sessions. Also, one trial recruited pregnant women in their second trimester,26 during which functional circuits of the fetal brain emerge and change more prominently than in the third trimester of pregnancy.38 Our study suggests no harmful effects of maternal physical activity during pregnancy on infants’ motor development; thus, collectively with previous evidence, we recommend continuing to be physically active during pregnancy.

Infant anthropometrics

Infants’ birth weight and length are positively associated with motor percentiles at 12 months in our study. Similar to our findings, a study in Brazil that included infants up to 18 months in their sample reported significant positive correlations for birth weight and length with motor percentiles.39 Another study in Finland reported similar relationships between birth weight and earlier motor development among 1-year-old infants.6 A prior study in the USA also observed this positive dose-response relationship for both birth weight and length with motor development scores among infants up to 47 months.40 A recent systematic review also found a strong association between gross motor development and infants’ birth weight.24

Infant feeding practices

At 12 months, breastfeeding status did not appear to have an impact on infants’ motor development percentiles in our study, which is contradictory to our previous findings at 4 months that revealed infants who were exclusively breastfed had higher motor development percentiles when compared with those whose mothers were supplementing with or using formula exclusively.25 A recent systematic review disclosed that the relationship between breastfeeding and infant motor development is inconclusive,24 while some studies acknowledge the favourable benefits of breastfeeding on motor development and suggest such developmental benefits even track into adulthood.41,43 Some argue that breastfeeding per se does not have motor development benefits, yet nutrients in breast milk have potential motor benefits.44 45 Other studies claim that breastfeeding does not have significant benefits in changing infant motor development.46 However, the observed differentiated impacts of breastfeeding on infant motor development at 4 and 12 months in our study are supported by the notion that nutrient concentrations in human breast milk change over time during the course of human lactation.47

Infant time spent on the stomach (‘tummy time’)

Our study did not observe significant relationships between infant motor percentiles and the tummy time at 12 months, which is consistent with our previous findings at 4 months of age.25 Our results differ from existing literature on the relationships between tummy time and prone sleeping and early motor achievements in infancy.48 49 However, regardless of the infant sleeping position, some studies found that infants reached their motor milestones within the recommended period.50 Other studies have demonstrated the positive impacts of prone sleeping on infant motor development dissipate over time as infants grow older (ie, closer to the 12-month mark).24 51 Regardless, our findings about tummy time should be cautiously interpreted because we assessed tummy time using parents’ self-reports, which are highly prone to bias, and our study’s low sample size was not sufficiently powered to observe significant results.

The impact of siblings

We did not observe a significant impact of having siblings on infants’ motor development percentiles at 12 months of age, which aligns with previous findings of a similar longitudinal study.52 However, although not significant, we observed slightly higher motor percentiles for infants with siblings, which complies with imitation theories proposed in other studies.53 We also need to note that this infant-sibling relationship of motor scores is most likely to change over infants’ developmental trajectories.39 52

Strengths and limitations

Our study’s strengths include its longitudinal design, using accelerometers to capture physical activity during pregnancy, motor assessments conducted by a licensed paediatric physical therapist using standardised tools, and its status as one of the first to quantify the relationship between maternal physical activity and infant motor behaviour.

The small sample size is a noticeable limitation of our study that necessitates caution in interpreting and generalising our findings. We were limited by the number of women we had in the baseline study as a starting point for recruitment. However, despite its small sample size, our study results mirrored many findings of previous larger cross-sectional and longitudinal studies on the topic and supported their common findings. The scarcity of evidence on the topic and similar trends observed in our study compared with larger studies makes our study worthwhile despite the small sample size, which has also been a common issue in lengthy longitudinal studies like ours in medical disciplines.24 54 Not only is it difficult to retain participants over time, but ours were also new mothers adjusting to the many challenges faced during the first year of their baby’s life.55 Similar to many previous studies on the topic,28 52 56 since this longitudinal study followed participants for more than 1 year, we expected fewer women–infant pairs to return for the 12-month study visits. Of note, we did not have mother–infant pairs enrolled at 12 months and then dropping out. We simply had less women responding to our invitations to return at 12 months.

Conclusions

The study identified infants’ motor percentiles at 4 months, birth weight and length as positive correlates of infants’ skills at 12 months. Although tummy time, having siblings, and breastfeeding are not recognised as significant predictors, the study warrants further investigation into their impacts on infants’ motor development. Maternal physical activity during the third trimester of pregnancy did not yield significant associations with infants’ motor skills at 12 months, but we encourage future studies with larger sample sizes to make robust conclusions on the topic.
