Comparison of the effects of bariatric surgery and pelvic floor muscle training on urinary incontinence in elderly women with obesity
Authors: Hui Peng, Yunxian Xian, Jie Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hightlights•Bariatric surgery improves urinary incontinence in obese elderly women.•Weight loss surgery is less effective than pelvic training for incontinence.•Postoperative diarrhea reduces urinary incontinence improvement efficacy.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI), defined as any involuntary leakage of urine, is a prevalent condition among women, especially as they age, significantly affecting their quality of life [1]. The World Health Organization (WHO) recognizes UI as a major health priority due to its substantial impact on older adults' quality of life, affecting multiple life domains and causing psychological, physical, and social issues [2]. UI's psychological impacts include anxiety, frustration, tension, stress, irritability, low self-esteem, and diminished self-confidence. Depression and anxiety prevalence in individuals with UI is reported to be around 20–40 % and 56 %, respectively [3]. UI prevalence among older adults is estimated at 30–70 % [[4], [5], [6]], with higher rates in women. For example, in rural Chinese women aged ≥65 years, UI prevalence reaches 56.3 % [[7], [8], [9], [10]], likely due to anatomical and obstetric risk factors. An Egyptian study (2020) found an 80 % prevalence of incontinence among older women [11]. In Iran, a 2017 study reported that one-third (33 %) of older women had UI [12], and a systematic review and meta-analysis in 2018 estimated a 46 % prevalence among women [13].

Obesity is also associated with various pelvic floor disorders, including UI [14]. Research indicates that increased intra-abdominal pressure in obese individuals may weaken pelvic muscles and increase urinary bladder pressure with urethral hypermobility. Chronic strain on pelvic muscles can further impair pelvic organ function [15]. Women with severe obesity are more likely to have at least one pelvic floor disorder, suggesting a correlation between obesity severity and these conditions [16].

Among women seeking weight loss surgery, UI prevalence is as high as 67 % [17]. Modest weight loss (5–10 % of total body weight) can improve UI and quality of life [18]. While weight loss is considered a first-line treatment for UI, adherence and long-term success of non-surgical weight loss programs are often poor. Bariatric surgery, however, is widely accepted as an effective obesity treatment, providing significant, sustainable weight loss and improving or resolving many obesity-related comorbidities [19]. We hypothesized that bariatric surgery may reduce intra-abdominal pressure through sustained weight loss, thereby alleviating stress on the pelvic floor muscles and improving UI symptoms.

Methods

This study employs a retrospective case-control design. The study cohort includes all pregnancies involving women with obesity diagnosed with urinary incontinence (UI) and planned to undergo bariatric surgery (BS) at a university hospital between January 1, 2023, and June 31, 2024. A Pelvic Floor Muscle Exercise (PFME) group of women with UI and a control group of women with UI were meticulously established through a 1:2 matching process. The matching criteria included preoperative body mass index (BMI), age, type of UI, ICIQ-SF score, and education level. Indications for bariatric surgery followed National Institute of Health guidelines, encompassing a history of unsuccessful non-surgical weight loss attempts and a BMI ≥40 kg/m², or a BMI of 35.0 to 39.9 kg/m² with at least one severe obesity-related comorbidity. All bariatric procedures adhered to a standardized surgical approach throughout the study period (Supplementary Figure 1).

Participants in the PFME group performed unsupervised home exercises three times a day, five days a week, for eight weeks, following the same intensity and duration as supervised sessions. The exercise parameters adhered to the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines for PFME. Weekly phone calls were made to each participant to optimize adherence to the home exercise regimen. The role of PFME as a traditional treatment for improving UI was to compare the therapeutic effects of BS and PFME on UI.

Participants

Women aged 60 or above with stress or mixed UI and a score of at least 6 out of 21 on the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Short Form (ICIQ-SF) were eligible for inclusion. Exclusion criteria included pure urgency incontinence, UI due to neurological conditions, cognitive impairment, stage 3 or 4 pelvic organ prolapse, urethral or vesical fistula, urinary tract infection, hematuria, current treatment for pelvic cancer, bedridden status, or involvement in other UI-related research. Participants were recruited from databases of two public hospitals in China. The actual training completion rate of the PFME group is above 85 %, all patients receive standard care.

Bariatric surgery history and obesity‑related comorbidities were retrieved from our hospital’s electronic database for in‑house BS patients. For external BS cases, operative and disease data were obtained via secure record‑sharing with referring institutions. A standardized form and dual‐review ensured data accuracy.

Pelvic floor muscle exercise

Women in the PFME group performed exercises starting in antigravity positions (lying on their sides, supine, or prone, or kneeling on all fours) and progressing to against-gravity positions (sitting and standing). The exercises included: (1) contracting the urethra; (2) controlling bowel action (or passage of wind); and (3) contracting the urethral orifice, controlling bowel action, and drawing the vagina upwards. Each session consisted of eight contractions per exercise (24 contractions total), with each contraction held for 5–6 s followed by a 10-second relaxation.

After consent, participants were followed at 1, 3, and 6 months. Data on ICIQ‑SF scores, weight, adverse events, and additional treatments were collected via phone, email, or study app and logged centrally. Non‑responders after three attempts were deemed lost to follow‑up and excluded from per‐protocol analysis.

Outcomes assessment

Data for this study were sourced from various medical records, including emergency room encounters, hospital admissions, outpatient clinic follow-up visits, labor, operative reports, and discharge letters from both the urology and gastrointestinal surgery departments.

The primary outcome was assessed using the ICIQ-SF, a self-reported measure of urine leakage frequency, amount, and the overall impact of UI. The ICIQ-SF scores range from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating greater severity. The questionnaire has demonstrated adequate validity (r = 0.93).

Sample characterization included variables such as age, BMI, education level, employment status, lifestyle, type of UI, history of cesarean section, history of abortion, fasting insulin levels, insulin resistance, preterm delivery, and mode of delivery. The study received ethical approval from the local ethics committee, and all participants provided informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) based on data distribution. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic data and comorbidities, while categorical data were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons were carried out using appropriate tests such as the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, independent-samples t-test, covariance analysis, or Chi-squared tests. Variables with a significance level of P < 0.05 were included in multivariate analyses conducted using logistic or linear regression models to identify definitive risk factors. Statistical significance was established at P < 0.05. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Blinding

To minimize measurement bias, all outcome assessments (including ICIQ-SF and PSQI scoring) were performed by independent evaluators who were blinded to the participants' group allocation (BS, PFME, or control). Patients were instructed not to disclose their intervention status during follow-up visits

This group was compared with a matched cohort of 92 women who performed Pelvic Floor Muscle Exercises (PFME) and 92 control women who received no surgery or exercise intervention. Discussion

UI is a prevalent issue among women worldwide, posing significant treatment challenges due to its complex etiology [20]. Obesity is a well-established modifiable risk factor for UI [21]. Our study demonstrated that bariatric surgery (BS) has a significant positive impact on UI symptoms and perceived quality of life in patients with obesity.

Summary of findings

This retrospective case-control study highlights BS significantly improves UI in obese elderly women, though less effectively than PFME. Postoperative diarrhea is a key modifiable risk factor limiting BS efficacy. Although BS improves UI, its effect is less pronounced compared to specialized pelvic floor muscle exercises (PFME). Despite a high postoperative complication rate of 65.2 %, BS still demonstrates a notable positive impact on UI, supporting its consideration for elderly obese patients. Previous research indicates a baseline UI prevalence of 18 % to 66 %, which decreases to 9.8 %–18.3 % one year post-BS [22,23]. Interestingly, BS has a greater impact on improving sleep quality associated with UI and obesity than PFME or routine care.

However, postoperative complications such as diarrhea were identified as potential factors reducing the effectiveness of BS on UI improvement, even leading to recurrence in some cases. At the 12-month International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire Short Form (ICIQ-SF) assessment, patients in the PFME group demonstrated the best outcomes, with the majority scoring within the 1–7 range. In contrast, most BS group patients scored between 7.1 and 14, while the control group showed minimal improvement, with scores predominantly above 14.1 (Fig. 1). These findings suggest that the weight loss achieved through BS positively influences UI and enhances UI-related quality of life.

While postoperative dietary modifications (e.g., avoiding carbonated beverages) are standard in BS care [24], their specific impact on UI remains unclear. Future studies should explore whether such interventions synergize with weight loss to enhance UI outcomes.

Clinical implications

Although a small number of elderly obese patients opt for BS due to its high complication rates, the UI issues in this population cannot be overlooked. The increased incidence of UI with higher BMI underscores the importance of addressing UI in elderly obese patients [25,26]. With the growing popularity of weight loss surgery, more elderly obese patients are considering BS. However, most studies have focused on the negative effects of BS, such as high complication rates, while the positive effects on sleep quality and UI improvement are equally important and should not be ignored [27].

For elderly obese women with UI, PFME should be prioritized as a first-line intervention due to its superior efficacy and non-invasive nature. However, BS offers dual benefits—substantial UI improvement and comorbidity resolution (e.g., sleep apnea)—making it a compelling option for patients with severe obesity or failed conservative therapy. Clinicians should proactively manage postoperative diarrhea, a modifiable risk factor identified in our study, to optimize UI outcomes.

Mechanistic insights

The superior efficacy of PFME likely stems from its direct targeting of pelvic floor musculature, enhancing urethral support and contractile function. In contrast, BS primarily exerts indirect effects through sustained weight loss and reduced intra-abdominal pressure, which may require longer follow-up to maximize UI improvement. This mechanistic divergence underscores the value of combining both approaches in clinical practice.

Relationship between BMI and UI

The risk of UI is closely linked to BMI, particularly in obese elderly women who have experienced pregnancy. Previous studies have reported a baseline prevalence of UI ranging from 18 % to 66 %, with a decrease to 9.8 %–18.3 % at one year post-BS [28,29]. Mihalsky's findings are consistent with ours, showing that UI improvement is directly correlated with both BMI and percentage of total body weight loss (TBWL) at all postoperative time points [30]. Our study emphasizes the potential of BS to improve UI in elderly obese patients.

Postoperative complications and UI improvement

Postoperative diarrhea emerged as a key predictor of reduced UI improvement, highlighting the need for comprehensive preoperative medical assessments and targeted management of postoperative diarrhea in elderly obese patients undergoing BS. Addressing these complications could further enhance the benefits of BS on UI outcomes.

Limitations and future research

While our study makes significant contributions, it has limitations, including a moderate sample size and retrospective design. Additionally, this study specifically investigated the interaction between postoperative complications of BS and UI. To validate and extend our findings, we recommend conducting multicenter, long-term, large-scale, randomized controlled trials to comprehensively explore the relationship between mental and physical conditions and UI improvement post-BS.

Conclusion

In conclusion, bariatric surgery effectively improves urinary incontinence in elderly women with obesity, though pelvic floor muscle training yields superior outcomes. Postoperative diarrhea is a key remediable factor limiting BS efficacy. These findings advocate for personalized treatment selection, integrating BS into multimodal management for patients with obesity-related UI.
