The actively secreted plasma extracellular vesicle troponin (ASPECT) study: circulating troponin in extracellular vesicles across cardiovascular disease cohorts
Authors: Michail Spanos, Priyanka Gokulnath, Aarush Singh, Christopher Azzam, Ronak Wakhlu, Claire Lin, Ana-Chrysa Maravelias, Timothy Oasan, Albree Tower-Rader, Megan Wasfy, Petr Jarolim, James L Januzzi, Saumya Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiac troponin is essential for diagnosing myocardial infarction, yet high-sensitivity assays frequently detect troponin elevations in non-ischemic contexts, complicating clinical decision-making. We investigated extracellular vesicle-associated (EV) versus non-vesicular (NEV) troponin in plasma samples from 266 participants across acute and chronic heart failure, type 1 and type 2 MI, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, end-stage kidney disease, healthy individuals, and exercise states. EV troponin was negligible in necrosis-dominant conditions (MI, kidney disease) but constituted up to 40–60% of total troponin in chronic heart failure or hypertrophic cardiomyopathy; in healthy controls and athletes, troponin concentrations were near or below the detection limit, though detectable troponin was predominantly EV-associated. Unlike plasma troponin, EV troponin weakly correlated with natriuretic peptides or renal indices, suggesting a distinct release mechanism linked to chronic stress or physiological processes. These findings highlight the potential for EV troponin to distinguish active, non-necrotic processes from acute injury. Introduction

Cardiac troponins are important biomarkers for diagnosing myocardial injury, especially in the context of acute coronary syndromes (ACS). Acute myocardial infarction (MI) is defined by the presence of cardiac ischemia in the presence of a rise and/or fall of troponin [1]. These proteins, key components of cardiomyocyte contractile apparatus, are localized mainly in the sarcomere but a small percentage is found in the cardiomyocyte cytoplasm. Sarcomeric and cytosolic troponin are released into the bloodstream when myocardial injury causes compromise of cell membrane integrity. In this context, increased concentrations of troponin may be detected in the circulation within minutes of cardiomyocyte injury particularly when high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) assays are used. These assays have revolutionized the management of ACS, enabling more rapid MI diagnosis and enhanced risk stratification [2].

Although hs-cTn represents the global standard for diagnostic evaluation of acute MI, with use of hs-cTn assays, elevated troponin concentrations have been recognized in a significant number of individuals who do not have acute MI. Such non-MI related troponin excursions are frequently observed in a variety of conditions that involve myocardial stress, damage, or systemic insult without classic ischemic injury [3–6]. Such states associated with abnormal troponin without MI include chronic kidney disease (CKD), heart failure (HF), certain cardiomyopathies, or even extreme exercise. This observation suggests that troponin release from the cardiomyocyte might be attributed to mechanisms other than myocardial necrosis [4, 6]. Besides leading to diagnostic ambiguities [3, 7] the presence of “non MI” related troponin changes imply the potential for a different mode of release of the biomarker into circulation. Understanding how troponin might leave the cardiomyocyte outside of cardiomyocyte necrosis might inform means by which to understand such abnormal circulating concentrations in those without myocardial necrosis.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are membrane-bound vesicles released by cells both under normal physiological conditions and during cellular stress to facilitate intercellular communication. They contain cytosolic proteins, nucleic acids, lipids, and other metabolites, reflecting their origin from intracellular contents. EV release is a ubiquitous cellular process that can be markedly upregulated during stress (e.g., inflammation, hypoxia) [8]. Given that a small percentage of troponin in myocardium is found in the cytosol, we previously examined the possibility that EVs might contain measurable concentrations of troponin. In a pilot study [9] using advanced single-EV nanoscopy, method (qSMLM) troponin molecules were directly visualized within individual EVs for the first time, confirming that vesicular export of troponin is a biologically plausible pathway. This early work established proof-of-concept that troponin can be actively packaged and secreted within vesicles; however, it did not address the broader biological landscape, including the relative burden of EV release of this biomarker in the setting of stress, inflammation, and other pathophysiological stimuli as opposed to free troponin that is released from damaged or dying cardiomyocytes.

To date, no study has systematically examined the compartment-specific release of troponin, EV-encapsulated versus free-form troponin, across diverse cardiovascular conditions. Without understanding this compartment-specific biology, the clinical interpretation of troponin elevations in non-MI states remains limited. Here, we build upon our foundational observations by quantifying EV-associated and non–EV troponin across a spectrum of disease states, including acute MI (Type 1 MI, Type 2 MI), acute and chronic heart failure, obstructive and non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, end-stage kidney disease, physiologic exercise responses, and health. Of note, the present study focused exclusively on cardiac troponin T (cTnT) using the Roche high-sensitivity assay. To our knowledge, no published data currently exist examining the compartmentalization of cardiac troponin (I-cTnI or cTnT) between EV and non-EV fractions. By establishing the degree and pattern of troponin compartmentalization, our study aims to clarify whether different mechanisms of troponin release underlie the diverse clinical scenarios in which elevated troponin is observed.

Materials and methods

Ethics and study population

The Mass General Brigham Institutional Review Board (Protocol Number 2017P000220) approved all study procedures, and all patients provided informed consent prior to blood draws. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Plasma samples from 266 participants across ten cohorts were analyzed: chronic heart failure (CHF; n = 25), acute HF (AHF; n = 25), end-stage kidney disease (ESKD; n = 25), Type 1 MI (T1MI; n = 25), Type 2 MI (T2MI; n = 25), obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (oHCM; n = 30), non-obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (nHCM; n = 30), stress-test cohort with paired pre- and immediate post-exercise draws (n = 31 individuals), endurance athletes (n = 25), and healthy controls (n = 25).

Participants were classified based on established diagnostic criteria. T1MI required angiographic evidence of acute coronary occlusion per the Fourth Universal Definition of MI; T2MI required troponin elevation with supply-demand mismatch without evidence of acute plaque rupture. AHF included patients hospitalized for decompensated HF per ESC 2021 Guidelines; CHF included stable outpatients on guideline-directed therapy without HF hospitalization within 30 days. oHCM and nHCM were defined per 2020 AHA/ACC Guidelines (wall thickness ≥ 15 mm or ≥ 13 mm with positive family history), distinguished by LVOT gradient ≥ 30 mmHg. ESKD required eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m² or dialysis dependence per KDIGO 2012 Guidelines; samples were collected pre-dialysis. Healthy controls had no known cardiovascular disease or chronic medications. Endurance athletes were active competitors training ≥ 5 h/week, sampled during routine training periods (not within 48 h of competition). Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Blood sample collection and processing

Peripheral venous blood samples were collected by trained phlebotomists via standard venipuncture from the antecubital vein using a 21-gauge butterfly needle. Blood was collected into EDTA-anticoagulated tubes (BD Vacutainer K2 EDTA, 10 mL; Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ). For disease cohorts (MI, AHF, ESKD), 30 mL of blood was collected; for healthy controls, 15 mL was collected per the blood donation protocol.

For MI cohorts, samples were obtained at clinically indicated time points within 24–72 h of presentation; timing was not standardized to symptom onset or peak troponin. For heart failure cohorts, samples were collected during index hospitalization (AHF) or at stable outpatient visit (CHF). For HCM cohorts, samples were obtained at routine clinic visits. For ESKD patients on hemodialysis, samples were collected immediately pre-dialysis. Endurance athletes were sampled at rest, at least 48 h after their most recent training session or competition. The stress-test cohort contributed 62 samples (31 pre, 31 post) from 31 unique individuals, drawn immediately before and within 15 min after exercise completion; no delayed timepoint was captured. Some stress-test participants had subclinical/baseline ischemia or chronic coronary disease, but none had acute coronary syndrome at the time of sampling.

Following collection, samples were maintained at room temperature and processed within 2 h. Plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2,000 × g for 15 min at room temperature. The supernatant was carefully aspirated, avoiding the buffy coat layer. Plasma was aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C, and underwent a single freeze–thaw cycle prior to EV isolation and analysis.

EV-enriched troponin isolation and NEV separation

Plasma samples (1 mL each) were processed using size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) to separate extracellular vesicles from free plasma proteins. After systematic comparison of multiple SEC column types (qEV1, qEVOriginal, and qEVSingle with 35 nm and 70 nm pore sizes), we selected qEV1 columns with 70 nm pore size (Izon Science, New Zealand), which demonstrated optimal separation of EV-associated troponin while minimizing free protein contamination. Fractions were collected sequentially: fractions 1–5, which contained the majority of nanoparticles in the EV size range (> 70 nm), were pooled as the EV fraction, while fractions 6–20, containing predominantly free proteins and minimal nanoparticles, were pooled as the non-EV (NEV) fraction. To ensure that troponin measurements reflected the intended compartment, Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filters (100 kDa molecular weight cutoff; MilliporeSigma) were applied differentially based on fraction type. For the EV pool, the retentate was collected: this step removed any co-eluting free troponin (~ 37 kDa) or small aggregates while retaining intact vesicles, ensuring that only genuinely vesicle-encapsulated troponin was measured. For the NEV pool, the filtrate was collected: this step removed any residual vesicles that may have carried over into later SEC fractions, ensuring that only free, non-vesicular troponin was measured. Both fractions were concentrated to standardized volumes (200 µL) for downstream analysis using the Amicon Ultra-4 centrifugal filters (3 kDa molecular weight cutoff; MilliporeSigma).EV identity and purity were confirmed using a four-pronged validation approach: (1) Western blot analysis demonstrating enrichment of canonical EV markers CD81, CD9 (a tetraspanin membrane protein,) and ALIX (an intra-vesicular cargo protein involved in EV biogenesis,) in fractions 1–5 as well as an EV-negative marker that is only present in NEV fractions (GM130); (2) nanoparticle tracking analysis using the Spectradyne nCS1 microfluidic resistive pulse sensing platform, confirming peak particle concentrations (≈ 3.5–4.1 × 10¹¹ particles/mL) in EV fractions with markedly lower counts in NEV fractions; (3) detection of troponin by Western blot using a separate anti-cardiac troponin T antibody in those same fractions; and (4) direct visualization of troponin T molecules within EVs using the quantitative single-molecule localization microscopy (qSMLM [9]). The convergence of these four independent readouts confirmed that troponin measured in the EV fraction represents genuinely vesicle-encapsulated protein rather than free protein contamination. The EV isolation and fractionation workflow is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1. Technical optimization details are provided in the Supplementary Method: Developing and Optimizing the Troponin-Rich EV Isolation Method.

Western blot

The samples were lysed via sonication, and protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), loaded on SDS-PAGE gel, transferred to PVDF membrane, which was blocked with 5% BSA in TBST, and incubated with primary antibodies such as anti- CD81 (Biolegend, 349502, 1:1000), anti- CD9 (BioLegend, 312102, 1:1000), anti-ALIX (Abcam, ab88388 1:1000), anti-GM130 (Thermo Scientific, MA5-35107, 1:1000), anti-cardiac TNNT2 (Abcam, ab45932, 1:1000) in those overnight and HRP conjugated secondary antibodies including HRP conjugated Anti-rabbit (Cell Signaling Technology, 7074 S) and HRP conjugated Anti-mouse antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, 7076 S) for 1 h followed by three 10 min washes with 1X TBST. The blots were developed (Supplementary Fig. 2) using SuperSignal® West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat. No: 34095). Importantly, during Western blot analysis, EV fractions were lysed using SDS-PAGE and high temperatures, disrupting vesicle membranes and releasing intravesicular contents to ensure troponin epitopes were accessible for antibody binding. Similarly, the Roche hs-cTnT immunoassay buffer conditions disrupt vesicle integrity, allowing detection of encapsulated troponin.

Troponin detection

Cardiac troponin T concentrations were measured on the Roche cobas e601 analyzer using the Elecsys® Troponin T hs (high-sensitivity) immunoassay (5th generation) with electrochemiluminescent detection (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, Catalog No: 08469890160). The assay has a limit of blank (LoB) of 3 ng/L, limit of detection (LoD) of 5 ng/L, and limit of quantitation (LoQ) at 10% coefficient of variation (CV) of 6 ng/L. The measuring range extends from 3 to 10,000 ng/L. The 99th percentile upper reference limit (URL) in a healthy reference population is 14 ng/L (sex-specific URLs: 9 ng/L for females, 16 ng/L for males). This FDA-approved platform is the standard assay used in routine inpatient care across most U.S. hospitals, ensuring direct clinical relevance of measured concentrations. For values below the LoQ, concentrations were imputed at 50% of the LoQ (3 ng/L) for statistical analysis, primarily affecting healthy control and athlete samples.

Statistical tests

Both ANOVA and Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to evaluate overall differences in EV/Plasma ratios across disease groups. Following significant omnibus tests, pairwise post-hoc comparisons were performed using Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction. The EV/Plasma and EV/NEV ratios were calculated to assess the relative distribution of troponin in EVs versus non-EVs, with values log-transformed to approximate normality for parametric testing. Separately, Spearman rank correlation coefficients were used to assess monotonic relationships among troponin levels, NT-proBNP, creatinine, and ejection fraction, and are presented as a correlation matrix in Fig. 1A. 95% confidence intervals for Spearman’s ρ were computed using Fisher’s z-transformation. Samples with insufficient quantity (QNS) were excluded from correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, Python, and R. To maintain data integrity and statistical validity, troponin values below the limit of detection (LOD) were modeled as 50% of the LOD (3 ng/L, since the LOD is 6 ng/L). Values near or below the limit of detection were primarily observed in healthy controls, endurance athletes, and pre-/post-exercise samples, but were rare in acute disease groups (MI, AHF, ESKD). Due to the high proportion of values below the limit of detection in the pre-/post-exercise and endurance athlete cohorts, formal paired statistical comparisons of EV troponin between pre- and post-exercise timepoints were not performed. Descriptive statistics and qualitative patterns are reported for these groups. This did not impact the interpretation of high-concentration states. Since the HCM cohort had limited sample availability, only EV and NEV troponin were measured, and the EV/Plasma ratio could not be calculated. Instead, the EV/NEV ratio was determined to provide a relative assessment of vesicular vs. non-vesicular troponin distribution within this group.

Results

Cohorts’ description

To determine the relationship between EV-associated troponin and how it contributes to the total measured troponin levels, we quantified EV, NEV, and total hs-cTnT concentration in the plasma of different cardiovascular and renal diseases, as well as physiologic states, including Type I and Type II MI, AHF, CHF, ESKD, oHCM, nHCM, pre- and post-exercise, endurance athletes, and healthy controls. Pre- and post-exercise cohorts reflect paired samples from patients referred for stress testing, some of whom had baseline/subclinical CAD but no ACS. For the HCM cohorts (oHCM and nHCM), limited sample volumes precluded measurement of total plasma troponin; therefore, only EV and NEV troponin concentrations and their ratio (EV/NEV) are reported for these groups.

The cohort included predominantly male participants. Among the disease cohorts, median age ranged from 60 to 72 years, with ESKD (median 60 [IQR: 54–72] years) and T1MI (median 61 [IQR: 53–66] years) being the youngest, and T2MI (median 72 [IQR: 59–79] years) and AHF (median 70 [IQR: 67–79] years) being the oldest. Notably, endurance athletes were substantially younger (median 31 [IQR: 24–35] years) than all other groups (Table 1). Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was notably reduced in patients with AHF and CHF, with median values of 35% [IQR: 27–50] and 51% [IQR: 35–61], respectively, while the remaining cohorts had LVEF in the normal range. As expected, NT-proBNP levels were markedly elevated in individuals with AHF and ESKD, reflecting significant cardiac stress, while T1MI, T2MI, and HCM groups exhibited lower or moderate elevations (Table 1). Creatinine levels followed the expected pattern, with ESKD patients showing the highest values, while other groups had values within normal or near-normal ranges (Table 1).

Comparison of EV/plasma and EV/NEV ratios across disease groups

We measured troponin levels in total (unprocessed) plasma samples, the EV fraction, and the non-EV fraction. To identify which disease states were associated with higher proportions of EV-associated troponin, we calculated the EV/NEV and EV/Plasma ratios (Fig. 1A and B). A summary of median values and interquartile ranges is provided in Table 2, which shows disease-specific differences in troponin distribution across plasma, EV, and NEV compartments.

In those with T1MI, plasma troponin concentrations were markedly elevated, with an EV/Plasma ratio of only 1.6% and an EV/NEV ratio of 1.9%, indicating that the vast majority of troponin was present in the free (non-EV) fraction. Similarly, those with T2MI demonstrated an EV/Plasma ratio of 6.9% and an EV/NEV ratio of 7.7%, slightly higher than T1MI but still indicating predominantly non-vesicular troponin release.

Individuals with ESKD demonstrated elevated plasma troponin levels with a low proportion of EV-associated troponin (EV/Plasma ratio = 5.2%, EV/NEV = 7.3%), a pattern similar to the MI cohorts.

Among those with AHF (median plasma troponin 76.8 [49.4-117.8] ng/L; Table 2), the EV/Plasma and EV/NEV ratios were 8.1% and 10.6%, respectively, indicating that the majority of troponin released in this population was also in the non-EV fraction, though with a modestly higher vesicular component than MI or ESKD.

In contrast, CHF demonstrated lower overall plasma troponin but substantially higher proportions of EV-associated troponin. CHF displayed an EV/Plasma ratio of 35.2% and EV/NEV ratio of 39.7% (Fig. 1A–B). Similarly, HCM exhibited EV/NEV ratios of 39.1% (nHCM) and 61.9% (oHCM), indicating that a substantial fraction of circulating troponin in these cardiomyopathies is vesicle-associated.

At the lowest end of the spectrum, healthy controls and endurance athletes had minimal circulating hs-cTnT, with median plasma levels near or below the limit of detection (Table 2). In these physiological states, EV/NEV ratios approached ~ 100%. It should be noted that in healthy controls and endurance athletes, the majority of values in both EV and NEV fractions fell below the limit of detection. The low absolute concentrations suggest that in physiological states, circulating troponin is near the assay’s detection threshold regardless of compartment; however, when troponin was indeed detectable, it was found within the EV fraction.

By contrast, the pre-exercise cohort demonstrated slightly higher plasma troponin (median ~ 8.8 ng/L) but a lower EV contribution (EV/NEV ratio ~ 47.5%). Following exercise, plasma and NEV troponin concentrations remained largely unchanged. EV troponin remained below the limit of detection in the majority of paired samples, precluding formal statistical comparison between pre- and post-exercise timepoints. The few samples with detectable EV troponin showed variable responses to exercise, and definitive conclusions regarding exercise-induced shifts in vesicular secretion cannot be drawn from this cohort. Notably, the lower EV/NEV ratio in the pre-exercise group compared with healthy controls is likely explained by population differences: unlike healthy volunteers, these were patients clinically referred for stress testing, some of whom had baseline subclinical ischemia or chronic coronary disease.

Correlation between EV, NEV, and plasma troponin

To assess how well the combined EV and NEV fractions reflected total circulating troponin, we correlated the sum of EV and NEV troponin concentrations (EV + NEV) with plasma troponin levels. A strong positive correlation was observed between EV + NEV and plasma troponin (Spearman ρ = 0.896, 95% CI: 0.852–0.928, p < 0.001, n = 110). This correlation was consistent across all clinical groups, with Spearman coefficients ranging from 0.70 in CHF patients to 0.97 in ESKD patients (Table 3). The NEV fraction comprised the majority of the combined troponin content (median: 86.6%), and individually correlated more strongly with plasma troponin (ρ = 0.96) compared to the EV fraction (ρ = 0.63).

We next conducted a correlation analysis to further explore the relationship between EV and NEV troponin levels (Fig. 2A–D) and other clinical markers, including NT-proBNP and creatinine. Spearman rank correlations (Fig. 2A) revealed a strong correlation between plasma and NEV troponin (ρ = 0.96, p < 0.0001, n = 230), a moderate correlation between EV and plasma troponin (ρ = 0.63, p < 0.0001, n = 229), and a moderate correlation between EV and NEV troponin (ρ = 0.65, p < 0.0001, n = 286). Notably, EV troponin levels were not significantly correlated with NT-proBNP (ρ = −0.04), creatinine (ρ = −0.20), or EF (ρ = −0.08). These findings support the hypothesis that EV-associated troponin reflects a distinct biomarker pathway, separate from traditional mechanisms of troponin release.

Discussion

In this study, we compared EV–associated troponin with NEV troponin across a spectrum of disease states associated with elevated plasma troponin concentrations. We also examined EV and NEV troponin in healthy individuals. By quantifying the proportion of troponin contained within EVs relative to total plasma levels (EV/Plasma ratio) and to the NEV fraction (EV/NEV ratio), we aimed to discern whether different explanations exist for elevated hs-cTnT concentrations in these various states. Our findings confirm that depending on the disease state, a substantial percentage of circulating troponin may be contained within EVs. For example, heart muscle disease such as CHF and HCM may be characterized by a higher proportion of EV troponin, whereas acute myocardial injury (T1MI and T2MI) and ESKD is dominated by NEV troponin. In physiologic states such as healthy controls and endurance athletes, troponin concentrations were frequently below detection limits, limiting conclusions about the mode of release in these populations. These results are the most conclusive to date that EV-based release of troponin may be meaningful and to our knowledge, these are the first results demonstrating heterogeneity in EV and NEV troponin in various disease states. Although troponin is an important diagnostic aid for the evaluation of individuals with suspected ACS, a vexing issue with this biomarker is the frequent observation of elevated concentrations in the absence of obvious acute MI. Abnormal troponin concentrations may create diagnostic uncertainty, leading to mis-diagnosis of MI [10]. In the absence of acute MI, when abnormal troponin concentrations are frequently labelled “injury” but a plausible hypothesis is that other mechanisms for troponin release might be operative. Indeed, change in troponin concentrations are commonly seen in HF without coronary artery disease or in inflammatory disorders [11–14].

Based on the observed EV/NEV ratios and plasma troponin concentrations, the cohorts can be conceptually grouped into four categories reflecting different predominant modes of troponin release (Fig. 3A–B): (1) Necrosis-driven release or impaired clearance, seen in MI and ESKD, characterized by high plasma levels and low EV/NEV ratios (< 10%), consistent with troponin release from cardiomyocyte necrosis or reduced renal elimination; (2) Acute myocardial strain-associated release, observed in AHF, with moderate EV/NEV ratios (~ 11%), suggesting a combination of passive and active release mechanisms; (3) Chronic stress-associated vesicular secretion, seen in CHF and HCM, characterized by high EV/NEV ratios (39–62%) despite lower plasma levels, indicating that vesicular secretion may be a key feature of chronic cardiac stress rather than acute damage; and (4) Physiological vesicular secretion, observed in healthy controls and endurance athletes, where plasma troponin is minimal and predominantly EV-associated when detectable, suggesting a role in normal myocardial physiology rather than a response to pathological stress. We acknowledge that these categories represent a conceptual framework reflecting the predominant patterns observed, rather than definitive mechanistic proof, and that release mechanisms likely coexist to varying degrees in each condition.

Our data highlight distinct EV/Plasma and EV/NEV troponin ratios across the clinical cohorts. For instance, individuals with CHF exhibited an EV/Plasma ratio of approximately 35% and an EV/NEV ratio of 40%, indicating that a substantial proportion of the troponin in these patients was associated with EVs. Similarly, in HCM, a cardiomyopathy with genetic causes, the EV/NEV ratios were approximately 62% in obstructive HCM and almost 40% in non-obstructive HCM, suggesting that troponin release in HCM involves both chronic myocardial strain and active vesicular trafficking. This pattern may represent persistent, active secretion tied to chronic stress, low-grade inflammation, or ongoing remodeling processes. These EV troponin elevations notably did not correlate with NT-proBNP, suggesting that EV troponin captures a facet of disease activity beyond mere wall tension or volume overload. In contrast, individuals with AHF had an EV/Plasma ratio of approximately 8% and an EV/NEV ratio of 11%, values that are higher than in acute MI cohorts but significantly below those observed in CHF, implying that while acute decompensation may involve both active and passive release mechanisms, the latter predominate. Notably, in AHF, the low EV proportion might reflect not only release mechanisms but also hemodynamic factors; for example, circulatory congestion could affect EV distribution or clearance, compounding the predominance of non-vesicular troponin, which may need to be elucidated in future studies.

We observed that in T1MI, as expected the EV/Plasma ratio was only about 2%, and the EV/NEV ratio was approximately 2%, reinforcing the concept that troponin release in T1MI is almost exclusively non-vesicular, driven by extensive cardiomyocyte necrosis. This reflects the acute and catastrophic nature of coronary occlusion, where cell rupture leads to a massive spill of free troponin into circulation. Subtle differences between T1MI and so-called “demand” related T2MI were present: in those with T2MI, a slightly higher EV/Plasma ratio of approximately 7% and EV/NEV ratio of 8% were observed. While the predominant mechanism remains passive release due to myocardial supply/demand mismatch, the higher proportion of EV-associated troponin suggests a modest contribution of active vesicular secretion, possibly triggered by ongoing ischemic stress or inflammatory activation. This aligns with the heterogeneous pathophysiology of T2MI, where myocardial injury is often driven by systemic factors such as hypoxia, anemia, or sepsis rather than direct coronary occlusion [15]. Curiously, individuals with ESKD displayed also a relatively low EV/Plasma ratio (approximately 5%) and an EV/NEV ratio of 7%, suggesting that uremic states might favor predominantly NEV troponin release, potentially attributable to chronic subclinical myocardial injury, combined with inadequate clearance.

In the clinical stress-test cohort, plasma hs-cTnT was slightly higher at baseline (pre-exercise median ~ 8.8 ng/L) than in healthy volunteers, with a lower EV contribution (EV/NEV ~ 47.5%). Immediately after exercise, plasma hs-cTnT remained similar (~ 8.9 ng/L), and descriptive trends suggested a possible shift toward higher EV/NEV ratios (~ 73.6%). This pattern could reflect transient myocardial strain with catecholamine and shear-stress signaling rather than necrosis and may also be influenced by the referral population (some with baseline ischemia/chronic injury). Because samples were drawn immediately post-exercise (not at the known delayed plasma troponin peak), these data emphasize mechanism (mode of release) more than magnitude. Clinically, if confirmed, recognizing an EV-predominant shift after exertion could help avoid misclassification of exercise-related elevations. However, the majority of EV troponin measurements in this cohort fell below the assay’s limit of detection, precluding reliable paired statistical comparison between pre- and post-exercise timepoints. Given the high proportion of censored values, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and definitive conclusions regarding exercise-induced changes in vesicular secretion cannot be drawn.

Lastly, among endurance athletes and healthy controls, absolute troponin concentrations were minimal, with the majority of both EV and NEV values falling below the limit of detection. While calculated EV/NEV ratios approached 100% in these groups, this finding is driven in part by the LOD substitution method applied to censored data and should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, the near-absence of detectable NEV troponin in physiologic states, contrasted with the occasional detection of EV troponin, is consistent with the hypothesis that normal myocardial secretion in healthy individuals may involve active vesicular pathways rather than non-vesicular release. This is clinically relevant, as elevated troponin following endurance exercise is well-documented among individuals without ischemic heart disease, raising considerable anxiety at times [16–18]. The biological basis for EV release of troponin in these settings and its clinical implications warrant further investigation to determine whether EV-associated troponin after exercise might support beneficial adaptive responses or simply reflect normal cardiac “housekeeping.” Moreover, although our data in healthy humans and endurance athletes suggest that physiological troponin release may occur predominantly via EVs, direct experimental evidence from animal models remains limited. Future studies using cardiac-specific EV labeling in transgenic mice and calculating EV/NEV ratios could help confirm whether constitutive vesicular secretion of troponin occurs under basal physiological conditions.

Mechanistically, the meaning of EV-based troponin release from cardiomyocytes requires further understanding. Beyond its canonical role in sarcomeric contraction, troponin subunits possess important non-canonical functions that may directly inform the biological meaning of EV-based versus non-vesicular release. Troponin T3 has been shown to translocate to the nucleus and regulate transcription of calcium channel genes (Zhang et al., 2015 [19]; 2016 [20]). Cardiac troponin I localizes to mitochondria, where it directly binds and modulates F₁F₀-ATP synthase, doubling ATPase activity and increasing mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening under oxidative stress, a mechanism that contributes to ischemia-reperfusion injury (Elezaby et al., 2024 [21]). Additionally, troponin acts as a potent autoantigen after myocardial infarction, with autoantibody formation driving progressive cardiac remodeling and worse prognosis (Furusawa et al., 2023 [22]). Furthermore, Adamcová et al. (2019) demonstrated in human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocytes (hiPSC-CMs) that cardiomyocyte injury triggers profound morphological reorganization of troponin networks, with troponin bundles forming compacted shapes that protrude outside cells, findings that suggest active troponin export may occur during cellular stress [23]. These observations indicate that troponin is not merely a structural protein or passive biomarker, but a molecule with signaling, metabolic, and immunogenic potential. In this context, whether troponin is released freely into the circulation or encapsulated within EVs may have important pathophysiological implications beyond simple biomarker release. In healthy individuals and endurance athletes, EV-mediated troponin release may facilitate routine protein turnover by clearing misfolded or damaged proteins, similar to mitochondrial quality control mechanisms [24]. In chronic disease states like CHF and HCM, persistent myocardial strain, oxidative stress, and inflammation may trigger an adaptive “toxic-waste-valve” mechanism, actively exporting troponin via EVs to prevent intracellular toxicity, a state resembling α-synuclein and tau clearance in neurodegenerative diseases [25]. Additionally, troponin-containing EVs may function in intercellular communication, particularly in fibrotic and remodeling processes, similar to matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) secreted via EVs to regulate tissue remodeling [26]. Alternatively, pro-inflammatory or oxidative triggers may drive EV-mediated export, positioning EV troponin as a potential biomarker of ongoing myocardial remodeling. For example, among those with CHF (in whom a higher percentage of EV packaged troponin was observed in this analysis), change in hs-cTnT was associated with cardiac remodeling parameters [27]. Whether such changes were EV or NEV troponin-based remains to be seen but should be the focus of future analyses. If EV troponin release is an evolutionarily conserved and physiologically relevant mechanism, it may serve as a marker of chronic myocardial adaptation rather than simply myocardial injury. This could explain why EV-associated troponin levels do not correlate with NT-proBNP or plasma hs-cTnT in conditions like CHF, HCM, and ESKD. Instead of signifying acute damage, EV troponin may provide insight into ongoing myocardial remodeling, low-grade injury, or adaptive responses to chronic stress. We acknowledge that the release mechanisms described (necrosis, active vesicular secretion) are not mutually exclusive and likely coexist to varying degrees in each condition. Our clinical phenotyping reflects the predominant pathophysiology based on established diagnostic criteria, but definitive mechanistic attribution would require histological or molecular confirmation at the cellular level, which was beyond the scope of this study.

Clinically, simultaneous quantification of EV and NEV troponin fractions may address a limitation that plasma troponin alone cannot resolve: distinguishing acute from chronic myocardial injury at a single time point. Currently, when a patient presents with an elevated troponin of uncertain acuity, clinicians must obtain serial measurements over several hours to detect a rise-and-fall pattern before confirming or excluding acute injury. Our data suggest that the EV/NEV ratio could provide this information from a single blood draw, potentially reducing the need for serial measurements. Acute necrosis-driven states (T1MI, T2MI) were characterized by low EV/NEV ratios (< 10%), reflecting passive release of troponin from dying cardiomyocytes. In contrast, chronic myocardial stress states (CHF, HCM) exhibited high EV/NEV ratios (40–62%), consistent with ongoing active vesicular secretion rather than acute cell death. If validated prospectively, a single-point EV/NEV ratio could help clinicians distinguish a patient with acutely elevated troponin due to new necrosis or acute myocardial injury (low EV/NEV, warranting urgent evaluation) from one with chronically elevated troponin due to stable cardiomyopathy or chronic myocardial injury (high EV/NEV), potentially avoiding unnecessary serial testing and invasive workup. Furthermore, although this study did not specifically assess outcomes, EV troponin and EV/NEV ratios may have prognostic value, especially because they appear to be independent of NT-proBNP in chronic disease. This lack of correlation may make EV troponin an additional layer of risk stratification, potentially reflecting ongoing, active pathological processes such as inflammation or oxidative stress. It is important to note that conventional cardiac troponin assays do not distinguish between EV-associated and non-vesicular troponin, as sample preparation and buffer conditions can disrupt vesicle membranes. Many commercial immunoassay systems use detergents or chaotropic agents that solubilize lipid bilayers, thereby releasing vesicle-contained troponin into the assay medium. Even in the absence of complete lysis, antibody capture of exposed epitopes on vesicle surfaces may still permit detection. Thus, standard clinical troponin measurements reflect a composite of both compartments. In parallel, recent advances in EV isolation, such as liquid chromatography and microfluidic systems, now enable processing times as short as 3–5 min, a marked improvement over traditional ultracentrifugation or precipitation methods. If implemented clinically, these technologies could support rapid EV quantification and potentially reduce reliance on serial troponin measurements. We emphasize that these findings are proof-of-concept; larger prospective studies are needed to establish diagnostic thresholds and clinical utility in addition to clarifying whether EV troponin trajectories can predict outcomes such as mortality, rehospitalization, or disease progression in heart failure.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted with several limitations in mind. First, the relatively small group sizes (n = 25–31 per cohort) reduce statistical power for detecting subtle intergroup differences, particularly in subgroup analyses. Although the HCM cohort was numerically similar to others, lower available sample volumes further constrained plasma measurements. Second, a substantial proportion of samples from physiologic or lower-disease-burden cohorts, including healthy controls, endurance athletes, and pre-/post-exercise participants, had EV and/or NEV troponin values below the assay limit of detection (LOD; <6 ng/L). The high proportion of censored values in these groups precluded formal paired statistical comparison of EV troponin between pre- and post-exercise timepoints, and EV/NEV ratios calculated using LOD substitution methods may be artificially elevated. Accordingly, conclusions regarding vesicular secretion in physiologic states remain hypothesis-generating and require validation with higher-sensitivity assays. By contrast, the disease cohorts (T1MI, T2MI, AHF, CHF, ESKD) had troponin concentrations well above the LOD, and comparisons among these groups were not affected by this limitation. Third, the single-center design may limit generalizability to broader populations with differing comorbidities, demographics, or treatment practices. Correlation analyses were performed on the pooled dataset; cohort-stratified analyses were underpowered and are deferred to future, larger studies. Fourth, EV fractions were confirmed by Western blot and microfluidic particle sizing, supporting that troponin was encapsulated rather than free protein contamination (Supplementary Fig. 2). Nonetheless, this study utilized total plasma EVs rather than cardiac-specific EV capture, which at this point remains a discovery platform not easily adaptable for large cohort studies (that have limiting amounts of plasma and larger sample sizes). Despite this, the consistent disease- and physiology-specific patterns observed strongly support biologically meaningful compartmentalization. Our use of samples stored at − 80 °C raises the possibility that freeze-thaw cycles could affect EV integrity. However, previous studies have shown that EVs retain their morphology, surface markers, and cargo after storage at − 80 °C with a single freeze-thaw cycle [28, 29]. Furthermore, our consistent detection of EV markers (CD81, ALIX) across all samples suggests that vesicle integrity was preserved. Formal spike-and-recovery validation of the Roche hs-cTnT assay in PBS buffer was not performed; however, the strong correlation between summed EV + NEV troponin (measured in PBS) and plasma troponin (ρ = 0.90, p < 0.001) provides indirect validation that matrix effects did not substantially bias our measurements. Moreover, western blot profiling of different troponin T/I isoforms or fragments, which may vary by disease state was not performed due to limited sample size and variability in availability of appropriate reagents. Future studies should incorporate cardiac EV-specific enrichment and Western-based troponin isoform profiling (Troponin I and T ratios) to refine mechanistic insights. Fifth, the cross-sectional design limits our ability to determine whether EV-associated troponin has prognostic value or reflects dynamic changes in myocardial stress over time. Our study captured troponin profiles at a single time-point per patient; therefore, we could not observe how EV and NEV troponin levels change over time after acute MI. It remains an important future direction to track the EV-associated troponin fraction longitudinally in MI survivors to determine whether vesicular release increases during later recovery phases, and longitudinal studies will be needed to assess whether EV troponin levels track with outcomes such as rehospitalization, mortality, or disease progression. Sixth, we did not investigate the fragmentation state of troponin within EVs. The FDA-approved hs-cTnT ELISA used in this study recognizes a stable central epitope (amino acids 125–147) that remains present in both intact and fragmented troponin molecules. Because proteolytic cleavage does not disrupt this epitope, the assay quantifies total troponin content but cannot differentiate between distinct fragment sizes or post-translationally modified forms. As proteolytic processing of troponin is increasingly understood to vary by mechanism of injury (e.g., necrosis, apoptosis, chronic stress), future work using mass spectrometry or epitope-specific immunoassays will be needed to determine whether EVs preferentially carry intact versus fragmented troponin, and whether fragmentation patterns differ across disease states. Finally, while this study highlights potential mechanistic differences in troponin secretion pathways, it does not directly assess the molecular processes governing vesicular versus free troponin release. Further research should elucidate the regulators of EV troponin trafficking and determine whether targeting EV secretion could provide new therapeutic opportunities.

Summary

Our study reveals that EV-associated troponin is a distinct and potentially informative fraction of circulating troponin, offering new insights into its biological role and clinical significance. The lack of correlation with plasma hs-cTnT or NT-proBNP suggests that EV troponin is released through mechanisms beyond myocardial stretch or necrosis. In MI and ESKD, troponin is primarily NEV-associated, reflecting passive leakage from necrosis or impaired clearance. In AHF, myocardial strain leads to moderate EV/NEV ratios, suggesting mixed release mechanisms. In CHF and HCM, a significant proportion of troponin is packaged in EVs, indicating chronic stress-associated vesicular secretion, while in healthy controls and endurance athletes, troponin concentrations were largely below detection limits in both fractions, though patterns were consistent with a potential role for vesicular secretion in normal myocardial physiology, a hypothesis requiring confirmation with more sensitive assays. These findings introduce a new perspective on troponin biology, underscoring the need for further studies to explore its compartmentalization, mechanistic pathways, prognostic value, and clinical applications in cardiovascular disease.
