TRIMP and session-RPE monitoring in elite women’s handball: a full-season descriptive analysis
Authors: Adrian Struzik, Jarosław Nadobnik, Marta Stępień-Słodkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined internal training-load (TL) profiles in elite female handball players from the Polish national team during the 2024/25 season, comparing athletes competing domestically and abroad. A secondary aim was to describe position-specific load patterns and derive applied observations relevant to workload management in high-performance environments. Over an 11-month competitive period, twenty players were monitored using XPS Sideline (session-RPE), Polar Team Pro and Polar Coach (heart-rate metrics, Edwards TRIMP), and the COSMED K5 system for VO₂ and HR-zone calibration. Key indicators included monotony, a platform-derived workload-ratio index, chronic load, TRIMP distribution, and session duration. The workload-ratio index was treated as a descriptive system-based indicator of short-term relative to longer-term load fluctuation and was not interpreted as a canonical acute: chronic workload ratio (ACWR) or against universal risk thresholds. HR-derived TRIMP and session-RPE were used as complementary indicators of internal training load, as they capture partially distinct physiological and perceptual responses to training. Mean session values were TRIMP 188.8 ± 48.6 AU, RPE 5.17 ± 1.69, duration 89.5 ± 13.9 min, and monotony 3.77 ± 0.94 AU. Middle backs displayed the highest workload-ratio values within the sample. Foreign-based wingers accumulated the greatest loads (274 AU; 33% of sessions ≥ RPE 7), while pivots showed high monotony and lower RPE despite elevated chronic load. A strong correlation (r = 0.78) supported the combined use of TRIMP and RPE for TL assessment. Descriptive differences between domestic and foreign league contexts were not uniform across all indicators. At the overall league-average level, training monotony, mean session RPE, and session duration were higher in the Polish-league group, whereas chronic load, the platform-derived workload-ratio index, and mean session TRIMP were slightly higher in the foreign-league group. Exploratory week-level inspection nevertheless suggested that selected periods associated with international competition may have involved greater short-term internal-load demands. These observations refer to different aggregation levels and should therefore not be interpreted as directly contradictory. These findings support the practical value of combining HR-derived TRIMP and session-RPE in a descriptive monitoring framework and suggest that internal-load patterns may vary according to league context, playing position, and aggregation level. Introduction

Over the past decade, advances in measurement technologies have established load monitoring as a crucial component of training planning. This is particularly important in handball, a sport characterized by high-intensity intermittent exertion and frequent physical contact, where monitoring physiological and biomechanical stress is essential for optimizing physical preparation and reducing injury risk.

In team sports, training load (TL) has become central to managing the training process. The literature distinguishes between external load (e.g., distance covered, PlayerLoad™, number of accelerations and decelerations) and internal load (physiological and perceptual response to the stimulus), which together define the dose–response relationship1,2. The introduction of the sessional rating of perceived exertion (RPE) method enabled the quantification of subjective exertion perceptions while maintaining strong agreement with heart rate–based methods2. More recent frameworks, such as TL 2.0, emphasize the integration of multiple indicators and their interpretation within the context of the sport specificity, competition schedules, and individual athlete characteristics3. Beyond training impulse (TRIMP) and session-RPE, contemporary monitoring frameworks encourage the integration of additional indicators of readiness and fatigue (e.g., wellness questionnaires, sleep and muscle soreness ratings, neuromuscular tests, and contextual external-load metrics) to better explain performance fluctuations and injury risk in team sports environments4. Importantly, the selection of load indicators should align with the applied periodization strategy. Evidence from elite women’s handball shows that different periodization models produce distinct physiological and performance responses, highlighting the need for load metrics that are sensitive to changes in both volume and intensity across phases of the season5.

Handball involves brief but highly intense effort sequences interrupted by incomplete recovery, with physical contact further amplifying biomechanical stress. Analyses of international matches have shown an average of 3.9 ± 1.5 high-intensity actions per minute of play6, and a total PlayerLoad™ of ~ 9 AU·min⁻¹7. In the context of women’s handball, research confirms clear differences in load depending on playing position: wingers cover greater distances and perform more high-speed accelerations, while backs and middle backs generate a higher number of ACC·min⁻¹ actions8. Accounting for these differences is essential for the optimal planning of strength–speed adaptation pathways and prevention of structural overload.

Aim

Coaches of the Polish national team and club staff working with players who compete abroad have observed possible differences in the physical and match-related demands experienced across league contexts. These practical observations motivated the need to implement structured monitoring of internal training load across the season.

The aim of the study was to characterize internal training-load patterns across a full competitive season in elite female handball players affiliated with the Polish national team, using HR-derived Edwards TRIMP and session-RPE. A secondary aim was to compare descriptive load profiles across league contexts and playing positions and to derive cautious applied implications for monitoring practice.

Materials and methods

Modern handball places high demands on physical preparation, particularly in terms of explosiveness, power, and agility, combined with high capacity for power generation in both horizontal and vertical vectors. Meeting these demands requires individualized training approaches informed by performance models, continuous analysis of tactical trends, and ongoing monitoring of athlete responses to training.

Advances in athlete-monitoring technologies have increased the use of internal-load tracking in elite handball settings. In the present study, monitoring focused on heart-rate-derived Edwards TRIMP and session-RPE, which were selected as complementary indicators of internal training load9,10.

The research material comprised monitoring data collected from female players of the Polish national women’s handball team across the 2024/25 competitive season. Data collection began on July 15, 2024, and concluded on May 18, 2025. The sample included 20 players; sample structure, baseline characteristics, and inclusion criteria are presented in Table 1.

Training and monitoring data were obtained using the XPS Sideline platform and Polar H10 chest straps, and Polar Verity Sense optical sensors. Session RPE values were recorded within 30 min post-session. Internal load was quantified using Edwards’ zone-based TRIMP method. Training monotony, chronic load, and a workload-ratio index reflecting short-term relative to longer-term load were derived using predefined procedures within the monitoring system11. The workload-ratio index reported in the present study was therefore retained as a descriptive platform-derived indicator of load fluctuation within the dataset and should not be interpreted as a canonical acute: chronic workload ratio (ACWR). Accordingly, no universal ACWR cut-off thresholds were applied.

Session-level indicators (TRIMP, session-RPE, and duration) were summarized to support microcycle-level interpretation. Monotony was used to describe within-period variability, chronic load reflected longer-term exposure, and the workload-ratio index served as a descriptive platform-based indicator of short-term versus longer-term load fluctuation within micro- and mesocycles rather than as a canonical ACWR.

Two complementary data collection systems were used. Session characteristics (type, duration, attendance) and session-RPE were recorded in XPS Sideline, while HR data for TRIMP calculation were collected via Polar sensors and synchronized with session records for export and analysis. This integration enabled aggregation of coach-reported session content (team/strength/individual), athlete-reported RPE, and Polar-derived HR metrics, supporting week- and phase-level summaries and between-group comparisons.

Subjective internal load was assessed using session-RPE recorded in XPS Sideline and based on the 10-point Foster scale (1998), which allows athletes to rate perceived session intensity, with session load calculated as RPE multiplied by session duration (minutes). The system supports analysis of cumulative load over time, monotony, workload-balance metrics, fatigue trends, and comparisons between players and training groups. In the present study, the workload-ratio metric available from the monitoring platform was retained only as a descriptive system-derived indicator and was not interpreted as a canonical ACWR or used against universal risk thresholds.

TRIMP was calculated as the sum of time spent in each zone multiplied by its respective coefficient:

TRIMP = (time in zone 1 × 1) + (time in zone 2 × 2) + … + (time in zone 5 × 5).

The final TRIMP value, expressed in arbitrary units (AU), reflects the total physiological training load, incorporating both duration (volume) and intensity (effort zone). This zone-based TRIMP method is fully objective, based on HR recordings, and independent of the athlete’s subjective perceptions13,14. When combined with subjective indicators11, TRIMP enables a comprehensive evaluation of the body’s response to physical effort and is particularly useful for monitoring training periodization, adaptation, and strategies to prevent overtraining and injury15.

To minimize measurement error and refine HR zone determination, physiological testing was conducted using direct gas exchange analysis and blood lactate assessment. Maximal oxygen uptake (VO₂ max) and ventilatory parameters were measured using the COSMED K5 portable metabolic system. Before each test, equipment calibration was performed:

Testing was performed on a handball court under sport-specific conditions. The incremental running protocol began at 6 km·h⁻¹, with speed increased by 1 km·h⁻¹ every minute until voluntary exhaustion or attainment of a VO₂ plateau of < 150 ml·min⁻¹ with a respiratory exchange ratio (RER) ≥ 1.10. HR (Polar Team Pro), ventilation V̇E, and VO₂ (COSMED K5) were recorded at a sampling rate of 10 Hz.

HRmax was defined as the highest 5-second HR value recorded at test termination and was used to establish the individualized HR zones for subsequent monitoring (50–59%, 60–69%, 70–79%, 80–89%, ≥ 90% HRmax).

Blood lactate concentration was monitored during exercise, immediately post-test, and after 5 min to characterize energetic cost, attainment of lactate threshold (LT) and anaerobic threshold (AT), as well as recovery kinetics.

Based on COSMED K5 validation data (error < 4%; ICC > 0.99), the procedures ensured high repeatability and allowed laboratory-level measurement accuracy to be achieved in field-based handball monitoring.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Szczecin (approval number: US 003.5.2025). Informed consent was obtained from all individual participants included in the study. All procedures conformed to the Declarations of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistica 13.216, while Microsoft Excel used for preliminary summaries and data visualization. In the first step, descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, median, minimum–maximum range) were calculated to characterize variable distributions17. A data screening procedure followed: outliers were identified using the ± 3 SD criterion; distribution normality was tested with the Shapiro–Wilk test18; and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test19.

When the assumption of equal variances was violated, Welch’s ANOVA was applied. For two-group comparisons, Welch’s t-test was used20. Significant main effects were further explored using the Games–Howell post hoc test, which is robust to unequal variances and group sizes21. When data distribution or variance heterogeneity precluded parametric testing, the Kruskal–Wallis nonparametric test was applied22. To control for Type I error inflation in multiple comparison procedures, p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method at an FDR threshold of 0.0523.

Effect sizes were assessed using Glass’s Δ for two-group contrasts and omega-squared (ω²) for ANOVA models17,24, providing additional insight into the practical significance of the findings. To better quantify estimation uncertainty in the between-league comparisons, bootstrap 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the mean difference (PL–FOREIGN) for each player-level summary variable presented in Table 4. Bootstrap resampling (10,000 iterations) was performed at the player level separately within each league group. The relationship between TRIMP-total and session-RPE was determined using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r)25. All tests were two-tailed, and the level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Results

The Polish national women’s handball team consisted of elite players competing in both the domestic top-tier league and foreign leagues, including the Romanian, Norwegian, French, and German leagues. In addition to league play, athletes participated in national cup competitions and European tournaments, including the Champions League. This competitive diversity resulted in substantial between-player differences in match exposure, creating a natural context for comparing training loads associated with domestic and foreign club environments. Analyses based on TRIMP and session-RPE focused on backs, pivots, goalkeepers, and wingers.

Descriptive analysis showed a group-level monotony of approximately 3.8 AU and a median session-RPE of 5.1. Mean session duration was ~ 90 min (SD = 14 min), with longer sessions in the Polish-league group (91.9 min) compared to the foreign-league group (85.0 min). At the group level, workload-ratio values remained descriptive, platform-derived indicators rather than canonical ACWR values; within this framework, middle backs and several back players showed higher relative values than the rest of the sample. These observations were treated descriptively within the present dataset and were not interpreted against universal ACWR thresholds. TRIMP values showed wide dispersion, with values ≥ 250 AU observed primarily among foreign-based wingers and back players. Basic descriptive statistics are presented in Table 2.

Discussion

The results suggest that combining subjective (session-RPE) and objective (HR-derived TRIMP) indicators may provide a useful descriptive framework for monitoring internal training load across a competitive season in elite women’s handball.

Shorter sessions in the foreign-league group

At the overall league-average level, session duration was lower in the foreign-league group than in the Polish-league group (Table 4). This pattern may suggest greater training density in selected foreign club contexts, although it should be interpreted cautiously given the small subgroup sizes and uneven subgroup structure.

Goalkeepers: high repetition, low maximal stimuli

Goalkeepers showed relatively high monotony and low exposure to high-intensity stimuli. This pattern may be relevant for applied monitoring, but no causal interpretation should be drawn from the present dataset.

Complementary value of RPE and TRIMP

The observed combination of high chronic load, elevated platform-derived workload-ratio values, and high monotony suggests that neither RPE nor HR-based measures alone fully capture the complexity of load response. A combined approach may improve contextual interpretation of training stimuli by capturing partially distinct physiological and perceptual responses to load. Combining TRIMP with session-RPE can improve the interpretation of training stimuli; however, decision-making is strengthened when complemented by external-load data, wellness metrics, and neuromuscular readiness assessments, especially during congested competition phases3,4.

TRIMP appeared sensitive to intensity-related variation, with higher weekly values observed descriptively in backcourt players and wingers than in pivots. However, because TRIMP emphasizes time spent in higher HR zones, it may overestimate load in players with greater movement efficiency and attenuated HR responses. Previous research indicates that combining TRIMP with metabolic power or PlayerLoad™ metrics improves internal-load estimation accuracy26.

Session-RPE remains invaluable when HR monitoring is limited (e.g., during contact-based small-sided games). Foster et al.9 reported a correlation coefficient of r = 0.88 between session-RPE and HR-based load in team sports, a finding broadly consistent with our analysis (r = 0.78). RPE also responded more rapidly to psychosocial stressors (e.g., travel, media exposure surrounding a match), which are not captured by physiological measures.

In our study, the strong correlations between Edwards TRIMP indices and session-RPE (r = 0.78) support prior evidence that integrating subjective and objective metrics reduces the risk of misinterpreting training stimuli2,13. Meta-analytic data further suggest that RPE captures perceived fatigue fluctuations more rapidly than HR- or accelerometry-based indices, particularly in female athletes, where hormonal variation may influence autonomic responses26. Conversely, Edwards TRIMP better reflects cumulative metabolic stress during intermittent, high-intensity efforts typical of handball12.

Exploratory week-level comparisons suggested that periods involving the Champions League or national team competition may have been associated with higher short-term internal-load demands than domestic-league microcycles. PlayerLoad™ values and HR-based load were likewise higher during international matches27. These observations suggest that internal-load patterns may differ between domestic and international competitive contexts; however, the direction of these differences depended on the indicator and comparison level and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

The use of COSMED K5 testing to determine individual metabolic thresholds (LT, AT) and VO₂max enabled precise HR zone definition, thereby improving TRIMP accuracy and reducing misclassification of training intensity.

Finally, heat-map analysis for March–May 2025 (Fig. 1) showed that weekly internal load varied by both league context (domestic vs. foreign) and playing position. In the Polish league, load patterns were more position-specific, with backs showing the most pronounced weekly peaks and the highest sustained loads. Pivots and wingers demonstrated moderate-to-high loads with intermittent peak weeks, while goalkeepers had relatively lower loads. In the foreign-league group, weekly loads appeared more evenly distributed across positions, although missing data in some position-league combinations warrants cautious interpretation. Overall, these descriptive results may help inform load-management strategies that take competitive context and position-specific demands into account, while requiring prospective confirmation in applied practice.

Limitations

Certain limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational design did not include a control group, which is common in elite sport settings where randomization is not feasible. Owing to the specific population studied – elite Polish national team athletes preparing for the World and European Championships – it was not possible to introduce a parallel control group without disrupting the continuity of the training program. Consequently, causal inference is limited.

Second, league-specific calendars, travel demands, and tactical models likely introduced additional variance between domestic and foreign contexts that could not be fully controlled. Although between-league and between-position comparisons partially compensated for the lack of a traditional control group, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

Third, session-RPE may have been influenced by familiarization effects during the initial microcycles and by psychosocial stressors (e.g., travel, media exposure) not captured by HR-based methods. Although the sRPE scale is generally considered reliable after a short familiarization period (2–4 weeks), a residual learning effect cannot be entirely excluded.

Finally, although the sample size (n = 20) is appropriate for research in elite women’s handball, generalization beyond comparable high-performance environments should be made with caution. In addition, the small and uneven subgroup structure across positions and league contexts limited statistical power and constrained the strength of between-group inference. The present findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative load patterns rather than definitive population norms. Moreover, because the analyses were performed on player-level aggregated summaries, the findings describe athlete-level seasonal patterns rather than session-level causal effects.

An additional limitation concerns the workload-ratio metric. Because the value available in the monitoring reports was platform-derived rather than reconstructed from a validated raw day-by-day analytical export, it should not be equated with a canonical acute: chronic workload ratio (ACWR). Therefore, its role in the present study remains descriptive only.

Conclusions

This full-season observational dataset suggests descriptively that weekly internal-load patterns in elite women’s handball may vary according to league context and playing position. Across the competitive period analyzed, backs tended to show the highest weekly loads and the most pronounced peak weeks, while goalkeepers showed lower and less variable internal-load profiles. Pivots and wingers displayed intermediate patterns with episodic peaks. These between-position differences were more evident in the domestic (Polish) league, whereas the foreign-league group appeared to exhibit a more even distribution of weekly loads, although missing observations in some position–week combinations limit direct comparisons.

The combined use of HR-derived TRIMP and session-RPE appears to provide a practical multi-perspective representation of internal load in this elite applied setting. Because the two indicators capture partially distinct aspects of the load response, their joint interpretation may improve ecological validity compared with relying on a single metric. Descriptive differences between domestic and foreign league contexts were not uniform across all indicators. At the overall league-average level, training monotony, mean session RPE, and session duration were higher in the Polish-league group, whereas chronic load, the platform-derived workload-ratio index, and mean session TRIMP were slightly higher in the foreign-league group. Exploratory week-level inspection nevertheless suggested that selected periods associated with international competition may have involved greater short-term internal-load demands. Therefore, league-context differences should be interpreted as dependent on the indicator and level of aggregation rather than as a consistent superiority of one context over the other.

These conclusions should be considered within the constraints of an observational design and a specific competitive environment. League-related scheduling differences, small and uneven subgroup structure, and individual factors, including possible familiarization with session-RPE, represent potential sources of uncontrolled variance. Accordingly, the present results should be viewed as descriptive rather than causal. Future prospective studies should examine whether training adjustments guided by an integrated TRIMP–RPE framework, ideally complemented by external-load and readiness indicators, translate into improved performance and reduced maladaptation during congested match periods.

Applied implications

The applied implications below derive from a descriptive observational dataset and should be interpreted as working hypotheses rather than prescriptive rules. In practical settings, internal-load monitoring in elite women’s handball may benefit from the joint use of HR-derived TRIMP and session-RPE, especially when interpreted alongside playing position, competition context, and recent week-to-week load variation. Particular attention may be warranted for athletes or positional groups showing repeated combinations of high monotony, elevated platform-derived workload-ratio values, and concentrated exposure to high-intensity sessions, although these observations should not be interpreted using canonical ACWR thresholds. However, no universal thresholds are proposed based on the present data, and any training adjustments should be individualized and prospectively validated. The present observations should not be interpreted as injury-risk cut-offs or universal decision rules.

Summary

The aim of the study was to characterize internal training-load patterns in 20 elite female national-team handball players during the 2024/25 season and to compare descriptive patterns across domestic and foreign league contexts. Over an 11-month study period, 3,682 monitoring records were collected using XPS Sideline (session-RPE), Polar Team Pro and Polar Coach systems (heart rate metrics, Edwards TRIMP), and the COSMED K5 ergospirometer for individual HR-zone calibration. Monotony, workload-ratio values, chronic load, and TRIMP distribution were calculated. Results were evaluated using descriptive statistics and appropriate inferential statistical procedures, including Welch-based comparisons, nonparametric tests where required, and standardized effect sizes. Mean session TRIMP was 188.8 ± 48.6 AU, mean RPE was 5.17 ± 1.69, and mean duration was 89.5 ± 13.9 min, with moderate monotony (3.77 ± 0.94 AU). Mean workload-ratio values were 3.89 ± 0.79 and were highest in middle backs. Foreign-based wingers demonstrated the highest loads (TRIMP 274 AU; 33% of sessions ≥ 7 RPE), while pivots had lower TRIMP values (187 AU), higher monotony, and a greater proportion of strength-oriented training content. A strong correlation between TRIMP and session-RPE (r = 0.78) supported the concurrent use of both measures. Descriptive differences between domestic and foreign league contexts were not uniform across all indicators. At the overall league-average level, training monotony, mean session RPE, and session duration were higher in the Polish-league group, whereas chronic load, platform-derived workload-ratio index (A: C), and mean session TRIMP were slightly higher in the foreign-league group. Exploratory week-level inspection nevertheless suggested that selected periods associated with international competition may have involved greater short-term internal-load demands.
