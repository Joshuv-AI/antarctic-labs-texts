Longitudinal monitoring of bone remodelling activity around the acetabular cup after total hip arthroplasty using 18F-fluoride PET/CT scans
Authors: Joeri Kok, Liesbeth Jutten, Jan Geurts, Boudewijn T. Brans, Jacobus J. C. Arts, Marloes J. M. Peters
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsAseptic loosening of the acetabular cup is one of the main causes of implant failure after total hip arthroplasty (THA). Insight in bone remodelling activity around implants could enable early assessment of implant fixation as a predictor for long-term implant stability. In this study, 18F-fluoride positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) is used to quantify longitudinal bone remodelling activity around two different acetabular cup designs.MethodsA prospective cohort of 14 patients undergoing THA was enrolled. These patients received an uncemented hydroxyapatite-coated cup (n = 6) or an uncemented cup with a porous titanium surface (n = 8). PET/CT scanning was performed postoperatively at three, six, and 12 months. Custom-made software was developed to analyze the longitudinal bone remodelling activity around acetabular cup. A set of points surrounding the cup was semiautomatically defined in the CT scan. For each of these points, the standardized uptake value (SUV) was calculated using the corresponding PET data. These points were used for 3D visualization and quantification of bone remodelling activity by averaging SUVs into segments.ResultsFor both acetabular cup designs, the highest SUVs were found in the superolateral region after three months’ follow-up, and a significant decrease of SUVs over time was observed. For the titanium cup, the higher SUV in the superolateral region compared to other regions was still apparent after 12 months’ follow-up.ConclusionA semiautomatic method to evaluate 18F-fluoride PET/CT scans of patients after THA was designed. The technique can be used to analyze and compare longitudinal bone remodelling activity around different acetabular cup designs.Cite this article: Bone Joint Res 2025;14(11):984–993.

Introduction

The annual number of total hip arthroplasties (THAs) averages 172 per 100,000 people for countries in the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD).1 The use of uncemented implants has become more common.2 These implants allow for direct bone ingrowth, but are historically associated with an increased risk for revision compared to cemented designs,3,4 with aseptic loosening of the cup as one of the most common complications,5-8 also after revision.9 Common causes of aseptic loosening are inadequate initial fixation, mechanical loss of fixation over time, and biological loss of fixation due to particle-induced osteolysis.10 The surface design of uncemented implants plays an important role in their fixation. Two commonly used implant surfaces consist of a hydroxyapatite (HA) coating, or a porous titanium coating. Due to its biocompatibility and osteoconductive potential, HA-coated implants accelerate bone remodelling activity, improving the biological fixation on the surface of the implant.11 Open porous titanium designs lead to early stability due to a high friction resistance, and subsequent strong fixation due to trabecular bone growth into the implant structure.12,13

Many techniques to assess whether aseptic loosening has occurred exist, such as routine radiography, CT, dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry, MRI, subtraction arthrography, nuclear arthrography, or bone scintigraphy.14 The use of these methods requires waiting for implant failure to be visible. When evaluating new prosthesis designs, with high success rates and longevity, an imaging modality to study early bone remodelling activity around the implant could serve as an early predictor of long-term outcome. Radiostereometric analysis (RSA) is used to assess early implant migration through implanted markers, which could be a predictor of long-term implant loosening.15-17 Migration of as little as 1 mm has been shown to increase the risk of future revision due to aseptic loosening of the cup.18 However, migration patterns are dependent on prosthesis design, and RSA might not be a universal method to detect all types of failure.15 Furthermore, the technique requires additional implantation of tantalum markers in the bone surrounding the implant. Most importantly, RSA provides no functional biological information about the bone metabolic activity, which could contribute to understanding the biological component of implant fixation failure mechanisms.

Positron emission tomography (PET)/CT can potentially function as a new means for early evaluation of new implant designs. PET is an imaging technique to quantify biological processes and tissue function in vivo, by making use of radiopharmaceuticals labelled with a radioactive molecule that decays via positron emission. 18F-fluoride is a positron-emitting isotope which, if intravenously administrated, rapidly diffuses through the capillaries into the bone extracellular fluid to be preferentially deposited at the surface of bone that has the greatest activity of remodelling and turnover.19 The addition of a co-registered CT scan adds anatomical information to the functional PET information allowing 3D spatial localization of bone remodelling activity.

An analysis method that can be used after PET/CT scanning is polar mapping, commonly used to quantify myocardial infarctions with PET/CT scanning.20 This technique has previously been adapted for use in orthopaedics to analyze the distribution of bone remodelling activity around uncemented femoral stems21,22 and uncemented cups.23

In this study, we aimed to extend the polar mapping method for 3D and 2D visualizations to compare the bone remodelling activity directly on the surface of two uncemented cup designs, one with HA coating and one with an open porous titanium structure. For further interpretation through quantification, five-segment polar maps based on the DeLee Charnley zones,24 as well as 13-segment polar maps, were generated.

Methods

Patients

As part of a prospective randomized RSA study of 53 patients, where five-year postoperative migration of two acetabular cup designs was investigated, a prospective cohort of 14 patients was enrolled for additional PET/CT analysis between March 2013 and April 2014 (in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975, as revised in 2013).25 The protocol was accepted by the medical ethical committee of the Maastricht University Medical Center (NL.33832.068.10), in which patients provided written informed consent. Patients who were scheduled for a unilateral uncemented THA procedure and were willing to participate in the study were included. Exclusion criteria were a history of hormonal therapy, any medication or illness known to affect bone remodelling activity, and a BMI higher than 35 kg/m2. All patients received an uncemented Symax stem (Stryker, USA) and were randomized to receive either an uncemented Trident hydroxyapatite-coated cup with a 1.8 mm peripheral rim, or an uncemented Tritanium cup, with a porous titanium surface (Stryker, USA) (Figure 1). Diameter of the cups ranged from 48 to 56 mm. 18F-fluoride PET/CT follow-ups were performed three, six, and 12 months (ranges: 73 to 119, 168 to 213, and 367 to 402 days, respectively) postoperatively. Surgical technique and postoperative management

All surgeries were performed by experienced orthopaedic surgeons (JG, RtB) using a posterolateral approach without osteotomy of the greater trochanter. Following surgery, patients were treated with 24 hours of intravenous (IV) antibiotic prophylaxis (Augmentin), deep venous thrombosis prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (Fraxiparin) for six weeks, and prophylaxis against heterotopic ossification with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication (Indocid) for 14 days. Full weightbearing was allowed from day 1 after surgery.

18F-fluoride PET/CT scan acquisition

One hour after IV injection of a mean of 101 MBq (SD 5) 18F sodium fluoride (18F-NaF), PET and CT images were acquired with an integrated PET/CT scanner (Gemini TF PET-CT; Philips, The Netherlands). A low-dose attenuation-corrected CT acquisition was performed (120 kV, 30 mAs, voxel-size: 1.17 × 1.17 × 4 mm). Standard filtered back projection CT reconstruction was performed. Subsequently, a static 3D PET scan was obtained by acquiring two bed positions of five minutes, covering the hip joint (voxel-size: 4 × 4 × 4 mm). PET image reconstruction included both non-attenuated and CT-based attenuated data, using time-of-flight technology. The PET and CT images are automatically co-registered.

18F-fluoride PET/CT analysis

To visualize and quantify the bone remodelling activity around the cup, a custom-made routine was set up (Supplementary Video) (Mathematica, version 11.0; Wolfram Research, USA (2016)). To select the region of interest around the cup, the low-dose CT-scan was used to define a shell of points surrounding the c. The implant could be segmented from CT by normalizing the Hounsfield units to range from 0 to 1 and setting a threshold at 0.75 (Figure 2a).

a) A 3D view of the prosthesis segmented from the CT scan. b) Three views of a disk set at the base of the cup and a rod through the centre of the cup, with the coordinate system. c) The hemisphere of points, defined on CT. d) The points defined on CT co-registered on the positron emission tomography (PET) image. e) A 2D projection of the points that can be coloured depending on the measured intensity, and the defined regions where points from the projection are averaged to form polar maps with either five or 13 segments. The letters A (anterior), P (posterior), M (medial), and L (lateral) represent the anatomical orientation of the cup.

In the resulting 3D image of the stem and the cup, a disc and a rod were created and used to identify the location and orientation of the cup (Figure 2b). For each patient, the size of the cup was known and used to set the dimensions of the disc and the rod. The disc was given the same radius as the cup, and the rod had this radius as its length. The rod was placed perpendicular to the centre of the disc. By manually placing the disc at the base of the cup with the rod pointing to the top of the cup, five parameters were obtained. The values X, Y, and Z represent the base coordinates and θ and φ the orientation, where θ represents the angle with the Z-axis and φ the angle in the XY-plane.

Based on the defined location, orientation, and dimensions of the cup, a set of points directly on the surface of the cup was generated (Figure 2c). For the Tritanium cup, this set was a perfect hemisphere. For the Trident cup, the increased 1.8 mm diameter towards its periphery was accounted for by increasing the distance from the centre for all respective points. This resulted in all points being placed on the surface of the implant. The set of points on CT were copied to the co-registered PET image (Figure 2d). For each point, standardized uptake values (SUVs) were calculated from the attenuation corrected activity concentration images as follows:

(1) SUV=A1∗m∗1000A0∗ρ∗0.5Δt/T12,

with A1 the activity in MBq/ml, A0 the injected dose in MBq, m the weight of the patient in kg, ρ the density of the material, which in this case was set to 1 g/ml, ∆t the time between scan acquisition and the 18F-fluoride injection in minutes, and T12 the half-life of 18F (109.771 minutes). The SUVs were normalized by a control region in the bone of the contralateral hip. SUV in the control region was calculated by segmenting the pelvis and femur from CT and converting attenuation corrected activity concentration to SUV according to Equation 1.

Polar maps were created by averaging SUVs of points together into predefined segments. Polar maps containing five and 13 segments were created (Figure 2e). The polar maps were viewed in 3D to evaluate the SUV distribution over the surface of the cup, and were flattened to 2D polar maps for ease of visualization.

Statistical analysis

Corrected uptake values were compared between cup designs using the Mann-Whitney U test. Changes over time were compared for each segment with a Friedman and post hoc test that can be considered as paired Wilcoxon with a correction for multiplicity. For the comparisons between timepoints within cup designs, the uncorrected SUVs were used. Any p-values smaller than or equal to 0.05 were considered to indicate statistically significant differences. To evaluate the robustness of the method, inter- and intraobserver variability and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for placement of the disc and rod for the evaluation of all scans. Intraobserver variability was scored by comparing the resulting calculated SUVs between repeated measurements of one observer (JK), while interobserver variability was calculated by comparing the resulting SUVs measured by two observers (JK, MP). The ICCs were determined for each segment by comparing the SUVs from all patients at all timepoints between observers. All analyses were performed in R version 3.3.1 (R Core Team (2018), R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria).

Results

Patients

A total of 14 patients who were scheduled for a unilateral uncemented THA procedure within the RSA study were included for the additional PET/CT measurements. Indication for surgery was osteoarthritis (n = 13) or avascular necrosis (n = 1). Patients received the uncemented Trident (n = 6) hydroxyapatite-coated or the uncemented Tritanium (n = 8) cup. None of the 14 patients underwent revision surgery within the five-year follow-up period. To the best of our knowledge, no revisions were performed within ten years after surgery (not part of the study protocol).

Taking a closer look at the distribution of SUV over the cups, differences between the designs become apparent (Figure 4). At three months’ follow-up, both cup designs showed higher uptake of 18F-fluoride around the most superior point on the cup compared to other regions on the cup, and up to 3.5 times higher uptake compared to the mean of the contralateral bone. In the Tritanium cups, uptake was still highest in the superolateral region after six and 12 months, although less prominently than after three months. Comparing the Trident and the Tritanium cups, the uptake was noticeably more evenly spread over the Trident cups at the later follow-ups, whereas uptake at the edges of the Tritanium cups reduced to levels below that of the contralateral side. This may indicate that the bone remodelling activity is stimulated more in the loaded (superolateral) regions in the Tritanium cups, whereas the hydroxyapatite coating and increased rim radius on the Trident cup may give rise to a more even and prolonged stimulation of bone remodelling activity.

When looking at individual segments of the five-segment polar map, at six months’ follow-up lower uptake was measured on the medial side (segment 3) of the Tritanium cup compared to the Trident cup (mean 1.1 (SD 0.3) vs 1.9 (SD 0.5); p = 0.012, Mann-Whitney U test) (Figure 5). This indicates a more rapid reduction in bone remodelling activity on the medial side of the Tritanium cup. Similar to the five-segment polar map, significant differences between cup designs were also found on the medial side at six months’ follow-up in the 13-segment polar map, i.e. segments 6 (Tritanium: mean 1.2 (SD 0.4) vs Trident: 2.1 (SD 0.5); p = 0.012, Mann-Whitney U test) and 7 (Tritanium: mean 0.9 (SD 0.4) vs Trident: 1.8 (SD 0.5); p = 0.008, Mann-Whitney U test). Additionally, the 13-segment map elucidated differences at three months’ follow-up, shorter after implantation, i.e. the lateral edge, segment 1 (Tritanium: mean 2.0 (SD 0.5) vs Trident: 2.8 (SD 0.7); p = 0.012, Mann-Whitney U test), and the posterior centre, segment 11 (Tritanium: mean 1.7 (SD 0.4) vs Trident: 2.0 (SD 0.3); p = 0.027, Mann-Whitney U test), showed higher remodelling activity in the Trident cup. Here, the additional segments may reveal the effect of the larger radius rim of the Trident cup.

Inter- and intraobserver variability

Measured SUVs for specific zones in the polar maps showed little variation between and within observers. For the five-segment maps, the mean absolute difference between observers was 0.3 (SD 0.3). For the 13-segment maps, this difference was similar (0.4 (SD 0.3)). Intraobserver differences were smaller (0.2 (SD 0.2) for five- and 13-segment maps). Overall, this resulted in high ICCs for both interobserver (mean 0.97 (SD 0.02)) and intraobserver (0.99 (SD 0.01)) variability for the five- and 13-segment polar maps, indicating good repeatability of the measurements.

Discussion

In this study, we have developed and applied a new method to analyze and compare 18F-fluoride PET/CT-scans of uncemented acetabular cups. The bone remodelling activity profiles at the surface of the Trident and Tritanium acetabular cups were visualized, and differed significantly. Notably, the polar maps revealed a more even distribution of the bone remodelling activity around the HA-coated Trident cup, where, especially at later timepoints, the porous titanium Tritanium cup showed retained high remodelling activity only on the superior side of the cup.

The presented polar mapping method was tested in 14 THA patients. Due to the combination of PET, co-registered CT, and known cup shape, it was possible to evaluate the 18F-fluoride uptake directly at the surface of the cup with high spatial accuracy. Although only 14 patients were examined, significant differences were observed, both between cup designs and between follow-up timepoints with both the five- and 13-segment polar maps. The choice for five and 13 segments differs from the nine-segment maps that Ullmark et al23 used for the cups. First, with the five-segment map, larger areas are covered to show general areas of higher activity, reducing the chance of overinterpretation of differences. Second, with the 13-segment map we could check for altered activity around the edges of the cups. This was of particular interest for this study where the Trident cup has a 1.8 mm increased diameter in this region, which is substantially different from the rim of the Tritanium cup. As a result of the different rim of both cups, a significantly higher uptake at the lateral edge of the Trident cup was observed using the 13-segment polar map, which was not revealed with the five-segment polar map.

Over the surface of the cups, the highest bone metabolic activity was measured in the superolateral region, especially during the 13-week follow-up. This was not unexpected, since this is the area carrying the highest loads. With the introduction of the cup, local stresses change, leading to increased bone remodelling in heavily loaded areas, following Wolff’s law.26 Furthermore, a decline in mean bone activity over time was observed. For healthy patients, this could indicate that the cup was osseointegrating correctly. Comparable results regarding the decrease in 18F-fluoride uptake have been found for the femoral stem in patients with successful osseointegration.22 Kobayashi et al27 stated that 18F-fluoride PET is of limited use within one year after surgery due to non-specific diffuse uptake for the purpose of detecting loosening. However, we suggest using this early reaction of bone to implantation surgery to predict later outcome. We hypothesize that within the first months after surgery, bone remodelling activity values must be elevated to a certain extent, since the bone needs to remodel to adapt to changed local loads after the distribution of woven bone.28 Once a strong interlock between prosthesis and the surrounding bone is achieved, a drop in bone remodelling activity can be expected. In the case that fixation is not successful after several months, bone resorption will occur and lead to high maintained bone remodelling activity. When interpreting scans, the time between surgery and follow-up moment is an important factor to consider.

The patients included in this study were part of a larger prospective RSA study cohort.29 Within the five-year follow-up period of this study, no revisions were reported. RSA showed that all cups stabilized after six months, and no outliers were observed looking at the polar maps.

In the early follow-ups, based on the five-segment polar map comparing the two cup designs, the Tritanium cup showed lower activity in the inferior region. Based on the 13-segment map, higher activity was seen in the lateral edge of the Trident cup. The higher activity in the Trident cup likely comes from the hydroxyapatite coating activating bone cells in areas where no loading takes place, which, without coating, would lead to bone resorption. Additionally, the larger diameter at the rim likely leads to a tighter fit and higher activity in this region. In later follow-ups, the cup designs showed fewer differences, and 18F-fluoride uptake levels returned to similar levels compared to healthy bone for both designs.

A limitation of this study is that the relationship between early bone remodelling activity and long-term implant failure remains unknown. This means that no conclusions can be drawn currently about the ability of the presented method to better predict long-term outcomes compared to RSA analysis. RSA has already shown to be a helpful method in the phased introduction of new implants in primary hip surgery.30 RSA data are available for the patients in this study. However, no cups were revised and thus no comparison can be made between the methods regarding their effectiveness in predicting long-term outcomes. PET/CT with the presented analysis method is unlikely to be cost-effective as a method for monitoring patients for aseptic loosening, even if a clear direct correlation between 18F-uptake patterns and aseptic loosening is shown. However, this method can already be used in a similar manner and as an addition to RSA for the stepwise introduction of new implants. Due to the high success rates of hip implants,31,32 this method will be highly useful in predicting which novel implants will be most stable, rather than predicting complete aseptic loosening. An important strength of this study lies in the improved data visualization, including an indication of uptake patterns around well-integrating HA-coated and porous titanium uncemented acetabular cups. These patterns, with elevated bone remodelling activity in the superolateral region in early follow-ups, have also been shown for other porous and cemented designs, with similar mean SUVs at six weeks follow-up, ranging from 3.14 in an inferomedial region to 7.55 in a superolateral region, and six months follow-up, ranging from 2.78 in an inferomedial region to 5.85 in a superolateral region.23

Another limitation was the method for the calculation of the SUV. The SUV is an easily applicable calculation method that can be used for any type of PET scan, but it has also been criticized for not being a truly quantitative measure.33,34 Renal function accounts for approximately 50% of the uptake of tracer,35 substantially affecting SUV measurements. This can be corrected for through the use of a reference area outside the region of interest. This comes with its own disadvantage, however, in that it complicates comparisons with other studies due to the need for consistent reference area selection. The reference area for this research incorporated a large part of the bone in the contralateral hip, which led to the exclusion of two patients due to prostheses in this reference area. In future studies, dynamic PET scans could be helpful, since they can help to distinguish between biochemical processes more accurately, leading to better estimates of the amount of 18F-fluoride actually taken up during bone formation.36

To conclude, a semiautomatic method was developed to evaluate the bone metabolic activity around uncemented acetabular cups using 18F-fluoride PET/CT scans. The presented method based on polar maps was shown to be robust with low intra- and interobserver variability. Furthermore, visualization as well as quantification was shown to be possible, which could help in interpreting the results clinically. This technique can be used to compare osseointegration over time for different acetabular cup designs. As a proof-of-concept, differences in 18F-fluoride uptake were shown between the porous titanium-coated Tritanium cup and the HA-coated Trident cup. Larger cohort studies comparing different cup designs as well as validation studies are needed to assess the potential of the technique to predict long-term clinical outcomes.
