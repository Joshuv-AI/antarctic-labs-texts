Oblique lumbar interbody fusion versus posterior lumbar interbody fusion for adjacent segment disease following prior lumbar fusion: a meta-analysis
Authors: Ming Chen, Xiaoyu Xia, Yifan Wei, Yu Jin, Cheng Ma, Yongxin Ren, You Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) and posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) are established techniques for the revision of adjacent segment disease (ASD) following prior lumbar fusion, yet the optimal approach remains controversial. This meta-analysis aimed to compare the clinical, surgical, and radiological outcomes of OLIF versus PLIF in ASD patients.MethodsA systematic search was performed in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, and CNKI without time restriction. Studies comparing OLIF and PLIF for ASD were included. Data were pooled using a random-effects model. Primary outcomes encompassed Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for back/leg pain, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), operative time, estimated blood loss (EBL), length of hospital stay (LOS), and complication rate.ResultsNine studies (377 patients: 185 OLIF, 192 PLIF) met inclusion criteria. At final follow-up, VAS for low back pain (P = 0.11) and leg pain (P = 0.93), ODI (P = 0.06), and Modified MacNab “excellent/good” rate (P = 0.22) did not differ significantly between OLIF and PLIF groups. OLIF demonstrated significantly greater early postoperative improvements in VAS (P < 0.00001), ODI (P = 0.004) and earlier mobilization (P < 0.0001). Surgically, OLIF was associated with shorter operative time (P < 0.00001), reduced EBL (P < 0.00001), shorter LOS (P < 0.00001), and lower rates of overall complications (P = 0.04), reoperation (P = 0.02), wound infection (P = 0.05) and dural injury (P = 0.005). No significant differences were observed in lumbar plexus injury (P = 0.15) and cage subsidence (P = 0.08). Radiographically, OLIF achieved superior IDH restoration at all time points (P ≤ 0.0009) and better IFH recovery at early and 3-month follow-up (P < 0.00001), whereas differences in IFA (P = 0.93), LL (P = 0.81), and fusion rate (P = 0.93) were not significant.ConclusionsBoth OLIF and PLIF yield comparable long-term functional and fusion outcomes in ASD revision. OLIF, however, offers advantages in early pain relief, reduced surgical invasiveness, faster recovery, and superior disc height restoration, supporting its preferential use in selected patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06317-7.Keywords: Adjacent segment diseases, Oblique lumbar interbody fusion, Posterior lumbar interbody fusion, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Lumbar degenerative disc disease is the most common condition in spinal surgery, characterized by lower back pain or lower limb radiating pain, numbness, or intermittent claudication. Lumbar interbody fusion remains a standard operative strategy for patients with lumbar degenerative disease [1–3]. Adjacent segment disease (ASD) refers to a new degenerative disorder, characterized by degenerative changes adjacent to a previously fused level or levels in the lumbar spine, accompanied by new onset of related symptoms and signs, including low back pain, radiculopathy, myelopathy, or instability. It is a well-recognized long-term complication after lumbar fusion, significantly reducing patients’ quality of life and necessitating conservative management or surgical intervention. The goals of ASD surgery comprise three fundamental pillars: (i) decompression of symptomatic neural elements, (ii) reconstruction of spinal stability, and (iii) restoration or maintenance of spinopelvic balance. This triad is particularly crucial for patients with sagittal plane imbalance, such as those exhibiting PI-LL (pelvic incidence minus lumbar lordosis) mismatch. Although several surgical options are available to manage ASD, the optimal revision strategy for patients with ASD remains undetermined [4]. To date, revision spinal surgery for ASD remains somewhat controversial. It primarily encompasses anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF), posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF), minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF), oblique lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF), unilateral bi-portal endoscopic transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (UBE-TLIF) and percutaneous endoscopic lumbar decompression surgery (PELD) [5–7]. Notably, OLIF combined with anterior column release (ACR) demonstrates unique advantages in correcting sagittal imbalance by allowing access to the anterior longitudinal ligament (ALL), a capability absent in traditional PLIF approaches. However, each surgical procedure has its own unique advantages and disadvantages in treating ASD [8].

PLIF was first performed by Cloward in 1943, and these procedures involve partial laminectomy and facetectomy, partial discectomy, and the attachment of elongated screw rods with nuts [9]. It is one of the most common surgical procedures for treating lumbar degenerative disease, particularly adjacent segment spinal stenosis and degenerative lumbar instability [8]. PLIF offers several benefits, including effective restoration of interbody height and resultant nerve decompression, reduced risk of vascular injury, and higher postoperative fusion rates. However, its drawbacks are frequently highlighted: significant surgical trauma, suboptimal wound healing, substantial blood loss, elevated risks of postoperative infection, dural tear, nerve root injury, and increased perioperative complications. Moreover, its posterior-only approach limits the ability to address anterior pathological structures or effectively correct global sagittal alignment, particularly in cases requiring substantial lordosis restoration. These challenges complicate its application in treating ASD [8, 10].

The concept of OLIF originated from early retroperitoneal approaches, with Silvestre and Mayer pioneering muscle-splitting (rather than muscle-cutting) techniques to minimize tissue disruption [11, 12]. However, modern minimally invasive (MIS) OLIF, as refined by Woods, Hynes, Davis, and Orita, represents a significant evolution—incorporating tubular dilation systems for reproducible access to the retroperitoneal space, including the L5-S1 level via a lateral ALIF approach [13–15]. This advancement allows multilevel interbody fusion in a single patient position, optimizing efficiency while preserving anatomical integrity. Compared to early open OLIF techniques, MIS OLIF—the method most likely employed in contemporary studies—further reduces tissue trauma, blood loss, and postoperative recovery time while maintaining the fundamental advantages of the oblique corridor [16, 17]. Crucially, the OLIF approach facilitates anterior column reconstruction through ACR, enabling direct correction of segmental lordosis and global sagittal balance—a decisive factor for ASD patients with fixed deformities.

As a popular minimally invasive spinal fusion technique, OLIF is widely used in the treatment of lumbar degenerative diseases [14, 16]. However, OLIF primarily provides indirect decompression through intervertebral space dilation, which somewhat limits its application in lumbar interbody fusion. Additionally, some reports suggest that OLIF may cause subsidence, endplate damage, sympathetic dysfunction, and vascular injury [18–21].

Despite several studies comparing the efficacy and complications of OLIF and PLIF in treating ASD, controversy remains regarding which procedure is optimal for ASD patients. This is particularly relevant when considering the triad of surgical goals—decompression, stabilization, and balance restoration—where OLIF may demonstrate superiority in addressing sagittal plane abnormalities. Moreover, a systematic review exploring the outcomes of both procedures for ASD has yet to be conducted. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis is necessary and may aid spinal surgeons in making informed decisions regarding ASD treatment.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. The protocol for this review was registered in advance on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration no. CRD42024609191).

Data search strategy

The following electronic databases were searched without date limitations: PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Scopus, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). Additionally, reference lists of relevant studies were manually reviewed to identify further articles. The searches employed combined AND/OR operators with full or truncated key terms, including adjacent segment degeneration, adjacent segment degenerative disease, adjacent segment disease, adjacent level degeneration, adjacent segment disc disease, adjacent segment disorders, oblique lumbar interbody fusion, OLIF (oblique lateral interbody fusion), OLIF procedure, posterior lumbar interbody fusion, PLIF (posterior lateral interbody fusion), PLIF procedure.

The search strategy, using PubMed as an example, is as follows: ((((adjacent segment degeneration) OR (adjacent segment degeneration [Title/Abstract])) OR (adjacent segment degenerative disease)) OR (adjacent segment degenerative disease [Title/Abstract])) OR (adjacent segment disease)) OR (adjacent segment disease [Title/Abstract]) AND ((((((oblique lumbar interbody fusion) OR (oblique lumbar interbody fusion [Title/Abstract])) OR (OLIF (oblique lateral interbody fusion))) OR (OLIF (oblique lateral interbody fusion [Title/Abstract]))) OR (OLIF procedure)) OR (OLIF procedure [Title/Abstract])) OR (oblique lateral interbody fusion)) OR (oblique lateral interbody fusion [Title/Abstract]) AND ((((((posterior lumbar interbody fusion) OR (posterior lumbar interbody fusion [Title/Abstract])) OR (PLIF (posterior lateral interbody fusion))) OR (PLIF (posterior lateral interbody fusion [Title/Abstract]))) OR (PLIF procedure [Title/Abstract])) OR (PLIF)) OR (PLIF [Title/Abstract]). The detailed search strategy with specific search terms is provided in Appendix I in the Supplement.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The literature search was conducted in strict accordance with the PICO principles (participant, intervention, comparison and outcomes). Articles were eligible for inclusion in this review if they satisfied the following criteria:

Participant (P): adult patients (18 years and older) diagnosed with ASD, undergoing revision fusion surgery by PLIF or OLIF, regardless of gender, operated level and previous fused levels;

Intervention (I): This review considered the efficacy of interventions with OLIF in patients with ASD underwent revision fusion surgery with OLIF;

Comparison (C): the efficacy of surgical interventions with PLIF was considered in patients with ASD;

Outcome (O): The primary outcomes were Visual Analog Scale (VAS) score, Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), length of surgery, estimated blood loss (EBL), length of hospital stay (LOS), and complications; while the secondary outcomes were Modified MacNab score, postoperative mobilization time, reoperation rate, fusion rate, intervertebral disc height (IDH), intervertebral foraminal height (IFH) intervertebral foramen area (IFA) and lumbar lordosis (LL).

Exclusion criteria

Articles were excluded if they met at least one of the following items:

i.Data was only presented in case reports or case series, letters, literature reviews, thesis, or other undistinctive forms;

Data was only presented in case reports or case series, letters, literature reviews, thesis, or other undistinctive forms;

ii.repeat studies or the same data published repeatedly;

repeat studies or the same data published repeatedly;

iii.Studies where the full text is inaccessible or data extraction is not possible;

Studies where the full text is inaccessible or data extraction is not possible;

iv.Studies unavailable in English or Chinese language.

Studies unavailable in English or Chinese language.

Study design.

Randomized or non-randomized controlled trials, retrospective or prospective cohort studies, and case-control studies.

Data extraction

Search results were screened for duplicates using automatization software (EndNote X 8.1). Two reviewers (M.C. and X.X.) independently screened the title and/or abstract of the remaining articles for initial inclusion. The full texts of potentially relevant articles were obtained and independently reviewed by at least two independent reviewers to determine eligibility. Any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion. If doubt persists, the third and fourth reviewers (L.Y. and Y.W.) were consulted to resolve dissimilarities. Y.J., C.M., and Y.R. provided guidance in image organization, article composition.

Data extracted included author’s name, publication year, research design, sample size, age and sex of participants, type of intervention, duration of follow-up, outcome assessment time points, and outcome measures. The primary outcomes measures for this review measures were the assessments of pain and function (VAS, ODI) and the measures of surgical outcomes (length of surgery, EBL, LOS and complication); otherwise, alternate measures such as SF-36 and modified MacNab standard score were used. Secondary outcome measures included the modified MacNab standard, radiological parameters (IDH, IFH, IFA and LL), reoperation rate, and fusion rate. If the data appears only in the form of graphs or charts, the Plot Digitizer software will be used to extract data from the article. Insufficient data were obtained by contacting the corresponding author. If the data were not sufficient for conducting quantitatively meta-analysis, the findings were summarized qualitatively or presented a qualitative description.

Quality assessment of selected studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS) based on a star scoring system, which evaluates three domains: selection, comparability, and exposure/outcomes. Studies are assigned up to 4 stars for selection, 2 for comparability, and 3 for exposure/outcome, with a full score of 9 stars. NOS score 7–9 is indicative of high quality, a score 4–6 indicates moderate-quality, and a score 0–4 indicates poor quality [22]. Studies that received 5 stars or above were considered in the analysis.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All statistical analysis of the pooled data was performed with Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4.1 software program (Cochrane Collaboration, Oxford, United Kingdom). Because of multiple time points of assessment in the different studies in this review, the data were pooled into 4 most commonly used periods: preoperative period, early postoperative period (≤ 6 weeks), 3 months, and 12 months or more postoperatively. In instances where 2 follow-up end points fell into the same period, the later were used. If only one postoperative observation point was available (e.g., IFA), regardless of which time point it was, the data obtained was uniformly marked as the postoperative value.

The mean difference (MD) with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) was used for continuous variables (e.g., VAS, ODI, EBL), and odds ratio (OR) or risk ratio (RR) with 95% CI for dichotomous data (e.g., complication rate, cage subsidence rate). If scales that measured the same outcome while using different methods, or conversion was not possible, data were pooled using standardized mean difference (SMD).

The Chi-squared (χ2) test and an I2 value were used to assess statistical heterogeneity among the studies. If I2 value was ≥ 50% or P was < 0.1, statistical heterogeneity among studies was considered, and a random-effects model was used. Otherwise, if I2 value was < 50% or P was > 0.1, no statistical heterogeneity between studies was considered, and a fixed-effects model was applied. Forest plots were created to represent the results of the various types of studies and summary estimates of the effects. A P-value of less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and CI was set at 95% in all analyses.

Results

Study selection

A total of 543 articles were searched from six electronic databases, with one additional article identified through hand searching. After removal of 89 duplicates using the automatization software, 455 articles were screened on the basis of their titles and abstracts. Of these, 414 irrelevant studies were excluded because they failed to meet the inclusion criteria of this review, and the full text of the remaining 41 articles were retrieved and assessed for eligibility. Of these, 21 articles were removed due to unmatched designs; 3 were removed as they were case report or case series; 6 were removed including master’s thesis, basic science studies, and review articles, each having two articles; the other two articles were removed due to incomplete data or part of the research results, respectively. Finally, 9 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the meta-analysis. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flowchart for the selection of publications was displayed in Fig. 1. The reasons for exclusion of ineligible studies are listed in Appendix Ⅱ in the Supplement.

Outcome analysis

Functional outcomes

A total of 8 studies involving 324 patients reported the preoperative baseline and final follow-up VAS scores for low back pain. Statistical analysis showed no significant differences in VAS scores of low back pain between OLIF and PLIF groups at the preoperative baseline (MD = − 0.14, 95% CI, − 0.41 to 0.12, I2 = 27%) and at final follow-up (MD = − 0.46, 95% CI, − 1.04 to 0.11, I2 = 93%). Nevertheless, 6 and 8 studies involving their corresponding 222 and 324 patients respectively, showed significantly lower VAS scores for low back pain in the OLIF over the PLIF group, with MD of − 1.55 (95% CI, − 2.17 to − 0.92, I2 = 85%) and − 5.9 (95% CI, − 0.86 to − 0.31, I2 = 59%) at early postoperative period and postoperative three months, respectively (Fig. 2A).

In addition, 8 studies covering 335 patients provided the data on leg pain scores at preoperative base line and final follow-up visit, no statistically significant differences in comparisons of leg pain scores were observed between the two groups at baseline (MD = 0.19, 95% CI, − 0.03 to 0.41, I2 = 0%); 6 studies involving 233 patients provided information on leg pain scores, and found significantly lower scores in OLIF than PLIF group at early postoperative period (MD = − 0.34, 95% CI, − 0.53 to − 0.14, I2 = 39%). Nevertheless, no significant differences in leg pain score were seen between OLIF and PLIF group at postoperative three months and final follow-up, with MD of − 0.09 (7 studies, n = 282, 95% CI, − 2.23 to 0.05, I2 = 0%) and − 0.01 (95% CI, − 0.13 to 0.12, I2 = 0%), respectively (8 studies, n = 335, Fig. 2B).

All of the included studies involving 377 patients provided the preoperative baseline and final follow-up ODI scores, random-effects model analyses showed no significant differences in ODI scores between the two groups either at preoperative baseline period (MD = 0.28, 95% CI, − 1.09 to 1.65, I² = 0%) or at the final follow-up (MD = − 1.12, 95% CI, − 2.26 to 0.03, I² = 48%). ODI scores at the other two time points were available from 4 studies and 6 studies, involving 177 and 252 patients, respectively. The meta-analysis showed that ODI significantly favoured OLIF procedure over PLIF, with MD of − 6.89 (95% CI, − 11.53 to − 2.25, I² = 87%) at early postoperative period, and − 4.76 (95% CI, − 7.59 to − 1.93, I² = 87%) at postoperative three months (Fig. 3).

Operative outcomes

All of included studies with 377 patients provided available data on length of surgery, with high heterogeneity among studies (I2 = 81%). The pooled results using a random-effects model showed that OLIF was significantly superior to PLIF in shortening the length of surgery, with an SMD of − 2.70 (95% CI, − 3.38 to − 2.03, Fig. 6).

All of the included studies in this review, involving 377 patients, reported the impact of both procedures on the length of hospital stay (LOS). Results of the pooled analysis showed a significantly shorter length of hospital stay (LOS) in OLIF over PLIF (SMD = − 2.11, 95% CI, − 2.85 to − 1.37), but with high heterogeneity (I2 = 87%) between studies (Fig. 7).

All included studies involving 377 patients gave the information about estimated blood loss (EBL). The pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed that OLIF resulted in lower EBL compared with the PLIF (SMD = − 3.75, 95% CI, − 4.80 to − 2.70, I2 = 90%) (Fig. 8).

8 studies involving 331 patients provided available data on reoperation rate, and low heterogeneity was observed among the studies (I2 = 0%). A fixed-effects analysis showed 0.35 times lower odds of needing reoperation for ASD in OLIF group than in PLIF group (odds ratio = 0.35, 95% CI, 0.14 to 0.87, Fig. 9).

Complication related outcomes

As can been seen in Fig. 10A, the total incidence of complications associated with surgical procedure was compared between the two groups in all included studies, involving 377 patients. Fixed-effects analysis showed a lower overall complication rate in OLIF group compared with PLIF group (OR = 0.47, 95% CI, 0.28 to 0.79, I2 = 0%).

Forest plots demonstrating the comparisons of overall complication rate (A) and specific complication rate (B) between the two groups

Further analysis was conducted to evaluate the specific complication rate based on their categories. 7 and 8 studies reported the rates of surgical wound infection and dural tear, involving 277 and 341 patients, respectively. The fixed-effects model revealed that the incidence of wound infection and dural tear was lower in the OLIF group compared to the PLIF group, with odds ratio (OR) of 0.39 (95% CI, 0.15 to 1.0, I² = 0%) and 0.15 (95% CI, 0.05 to 0.43, I² = 0%), respectively. In addition, the pooling data from 9 to 5 studies covering 377 and 229 patients respectively, showed no significant differences in complication rates in term of lumbar plexus injury (OR = 1.74, 95% CI, 0.82 to 3.73, I2 = 0%) and cage subsidence (OR = 2.83, 95% CI, 0.87 to 9.24, I2 = 0%) between the two groups (Fig. 10B).

Radiologic outcomes

7 studies involving a total of 313 patients were included in the analysis of IDH at the preoperative baseline. Meta-analysis of random-effects model showed no a significant difference in IDH value at the baseline between the two groups (MD = − 0.13, 95% CI, − 0.38 to 0.11, I2 = 0%). IDH at each postoperative time point were available from 4, 3 and 5 studies, involving 185, 118 and 219 patients respectively. Nevertheless, the pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed statistically significant differences in IDH between the two groups, with MD of 1.20 (95% CI, 0.53 to 1.86, I² = 54%), 1.47 (95% CI, 0.93 to 2.01, I² = 31%) and 1.23 (95% CI, 0.50 to 1.95, I² = 78%) at early postoperative period, postoperative three months, and final follow-up, respectively (Fig. 11).

Forest plot demonstrating the comparisons of IDH (mm) between the two groups

6 and 4 of the included studies (involving 260 and 166 patients, respectively) provided available data on the lumbar IFH measurement at the preoperative baseline and final follow-up. Statistical analysis using random-effects models showed no significant differences in lumbar IFH between the two groups at the preoperative baseline (MD = − 0.01, 95% CI, − 0.41 to 0.39, I2 = 7%) and at final follow-up (MD = − 0.11, 95% CI, − 1.64 to 1.42, I2 = 91%). In addition, lumbar IFH at two other time points were obtained from 3 studies each, involving 132 and 118 patients respectively. Random-effects models result showed significant differences in IFH between two groups, with MD of 1.14 (95% CI, 0.67 to 1.61, I2 = 0%) and 1.39 (95% CI, 0.94 to 1.85, I2 = 0%) at early period and postoperative 3 months, respectively, indicating better IFH restoration using OLIF approach than PLIF approach at these two assessment points (Fig. 12).

3 of the included studies involving 128 patients provide the information about IFA. The pooled analysis using a random-effects model showed no differences in the preoperative baseline and postoperative IFH between the two groups, with MD of 0.02 (95% CI, − 0.08 to 0.13, I2 = 78%) and 0.01 (95% CI, − 0.29 to 0.32, I2 = 97%), respectively (Fig. 13).

The modification of the lumbar lordosis (LL) at preoperative baseline was recorded in 4 studies involving 185 ASD patients. Meta-analysis of fixed-effects models showed no a significant difference in baseline LL between the two groups (SMD = − 0.12, 95% CI, − 0.41 to 0.17, I2 = 36%).

The lumbar lordosis (LL) at each postoperative time point was compared between the two groups, with no statistical heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 0%), therefore the fixed-effects model was used. The pooled results showed that lumbar lordosis (LL) was not significantly different between OLIF and PLIF at each follow-up time point after surgery, with SMD of − 0.17 (4 studies, n = 185, 95% CI, − 0.46 to 0.12), − 0.00 (2 studies, n = 78, 95% CI, − 0.45 to 0.44) and − 0.04 (3 studies, n = 131, 95% CI, − 0.39 to 0.30) at early postoperative period, postoperative 3 months and final follow-up, respectively (Fig. 14).

Postoperative fusion rates were reported in 4 studies involving 191 patients, with no heterogeneity among the studies (I2 = 0%). There was no a significant difference in fusion rates between OLIF and PLIF groups (RR = 1, 95% CI, 0.95 to 1.06, Fig. 15).

In addition, the mean segmental lordosis (SL), PI-LL, segmental coronal angle (SCA), anteroposterior diameter (APD), cross-sectional area (CSA), retrolisthesis index (RI) were reported in both OLIF and PLIF groups by one study each [19, 20, 23]. All terms of SL, PI-LL, SCA, APD, CSA, and RI were significantly improved postoperatively in both groups (P < 0.05), and all the radiological outcomes were comparable between the groups postoperatively at any follow-up time point (P > 0.05).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis comparing the clinical and radiological outcomes of OLIF and PLIF for treating ASD following prior lumbar fusion. However, these findings must be interpreted in light of OLIF’s status as an evolving technique, in contrast to PLIF, which benefits from decades of refinement and standardization. While PLIF’s long-established methodology offers a stable benchmark, OLIF’s technical nuances—including approach-related anatomy, cage placement, and endplate preparation—remain subjects of ongoing optimization. For example, OLIF’s subsidence rates may be influenced by technique-dependent factors such as overdistraction, excessive endplate decortication, or intraoperative endplate stress fractures—issues that could diminish with greater surgical experience. This learning curve necessitates cautious interpretation of outcomes, particularly for complications like cage subsidence or lumbar plexus injury, which are often linked to procedural proficiency.

Despite these considerations, our analysis demonstrates that OLIF offers significant advantages over PLIF, including faster postoperative mobilization, shorter operative times and hospital stays, reduced blood loss, lower complication and reoperation rates, and better restoration of IDH. These benefits suggest that OLIF is a promising alternative for ASD treatment, though its long-term efficacy and technical refinements warrant further investigation.

PLIF is widely recognized as a common procedure for treating degenerative lumbar spine conditions. In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated that PLIF can significantly improve clinical and radiological outcomes in patients with ASD, achieving high satisfaction and spinal fusion rates [28, 29]. Miwa et al. [30] showed that PLIF is effective for ASD repair, evidenced by significant improvements in the modified JOA score, with comparable results for both repeat and initial PLIF procedures in ASD, although it tends to be associated with a higher incidence of recurrent ASD. However, the drawbacks of PLIF revision surgery, such as substantial blood loss, significant muscle tissue damage, and persistent low back pain during postoperative follow-up, are issues that clinicians must confront [30]. With the increasing emphasis on minimally invasive spinal surgery concepts in clinical practice, the feasibility of using a more minimally invasive approach to address ASD has gained attention. Consequently, OLIF, an internationally popular and novel technique for treating various degenerative lumbar diseases such as lumbar degenerative scoliosis, spinal stenosis, and spondylolisthesis, has been applied to the treatment of ASD [31]. In recent years, several studies have demonstrated that OLIF is a safe and effective alternative for treating ASD patients following lumbar fusion [10, 25, 32].

Clinically, selecting the optimal surgical method is crucial for achieving the best clinical outcomes. Therefore, this study conducted a meta-analysis comparing the two surgical techniques and found that OLIF could achieve equivalent improvements in functional outcomes for ASD patients compared to PLIF. The pooled results of the included studies showed no significant differences between the two groups in terms of low back VAS score (P = 0.87), leg pain VAS score (P = 0.12), and the excellent and good rate of the modified MacNab criteria (P = 0.22) at the final follow-up. Moreover, OLIF was significantly superior to PLIF in early improvements in low back pain score (P < 0.0001), early leg pain score (P = 0.0006), and postoperative ODI at each evaluation time point (P < 0.05), indicating earlier functional recovery and better improvement in ODI with OLIF compared to PLIF.

As demonstrated in previous studies, PLIF involves dissection of paravertebral muscles, extensive soft tissue and scar tissue manipulation, laminectomy, and removal of the previous internal fixation system. These procedures increase perioperative blood loss, significantly prolong operative and hospitalization times, and impede recovery and early postoperative mobilization [33]. In contrast, OLIF, being a minimally invasive technique, offers shorter surgical time, reduced blood loss, and earlier postoperative mobility. This study found significant reductions in surgical duration (P < 0.00001), estimated blood loss (P = 0.0001), hospital stay length (P < 0.00001), and mobilization time (P < 0.0001) when using OLIF compared to PLIF for ASD treatment.

Tabaraee et al. reported that the reoperation rates for ASD treated with PLIF ranged from 7.6% to 9.7% (mean 8.6%) at 5-year follow-up [34]. An earlier study indicated that the reoperation rate following PLIF treatment for ASD was 16.7%, with recurrent ASD being one of the most significant reasons, accounting for 44% of patients who underwent reoperations [30]. Chang et al. demonstrated a reoperation rate of 9.4% in patients who underwent OLIF for ASD, with 7.14% of reoperations due to fusion cage subsidence [35]. Miscusi reported no significant differences in reoperation rates between OLIF and PLIF for ASD treatment (P = 0.31), with the main reasons for reoperation being recurrence of ASD and cage subsidence in OLIF, and dural tear or/and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage in PLIF [23]. The pooled results of this review showed significantly lower reoperation rates in patients who underwent OLIF compared to PLIF (P < 0.05), indicating that OLIF is a clinically superior technique for ASD repair.

Surgical decision-making is closely associated with the occurrence of intraoperative and postoperative complications. An analysis of a cohort of 251 patients who underwent PLIF for degenerative lumbar disorders revealed an overall complication incidence rate of 24.7% (62/251), including a dural tear rate of 7.6% (19/251), lumbar plexus injury at 6.8%, ASD at 4.4%, and wound infection at 0.4% [36]. Zhang et al. reported an overall complication incidence rate of 23.1% for ASD treated with OLIF, including cage subsidence (15.3%) and lumbar plexus injury (7.7%) [10]. These findings are consistent with previous results reported by Wang and co-workers in 2022 [32]. In the current systematic review, meta-analyses showed significantly lower rates of overall complications (14% vs. 24%, P = 0.006), wound infection (2.4% vs. 8.4%, P ≤ 0.05), and dural tear (0% vs. 12.1%, P = 0.005) in the OLIF group compared to the PLIF group. However, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of cage subsidence (P = 0.08) and lumbar plexus injury (P = 0.15) between the two groups. Dural tears often lead to CSF leakage, necessitating revision surgery. In our review, a possible explanation for the higher incidences of overall complications and reoperations in the PLIF group may be the higher rate of dural tears (12.1%, 21/173) associated with the PLIF procedure.

Cage subsidence is one of the most common complications following OLIF surgery, potentially having a more negative impact on surgical outcomes compared to other procedures [37]. Previous studies have reported cage subsidence rates ranging from approximately 10% to 18.7% in patients who underwent OLIF (Malham et al. − 10%, Le et al. − 15%, Abe et al. − 18.7%) [31, 38, 39]. In this review, analysis of the included studies indicated that the incidence of cage subsidence in OLIF tended to be slightly higher than that in PLIF (7.3% [16/218] versus 2.1% [4/194]), although no significant difference was observed between the two groups (P = 0.08). Several factors contribute to cage-related complications, including patient age, cage size, bone density and quality, and internal fixation methods [19, 40]. A lower incidence of cage subsidence is associated with OLIF combined with lateral unilateral fixation or posterior fixation, whereas stand-alone OLIF may increase the risk of subsidence due to endplate injury, low bone density, or segmental instability [41, 42]. In the current review, over 50% of patients underwent OLIF surgery combined with lateral unilateral fixation or posterior fixation. This may be one of the reasons why cage subsidence was not frequently observed in the OLIF group in our review.

Radiological improvements serve as pivotal indicators for successful ASD surgery, encompassing not only fusion rates and neural decompression but crucially, the restoration of spinopelvic balance - the third fundamental pillar of ASD surgical goals. The technical variations of OLIF identified in our analysis (stand-alone, with supplemental fixation, or with ACR) significantly influence its radiological profile and capacity to address these goals. While both OLIF and PLIF demonstrate excellent radiological outcomes, their capacity to address sagittal imbalance varies significantly due to distinct surgical approaches [10, 26, 27, 43]. Recent evidence confirms OLIF’s equivalence to PLIF in restoring lumbar lordosis (LL) and achieving fusion [8, 44, 45], with the added benefit of superior IDH restoration. Notably, this IDH advantage may contribute to long-term balance maintenance by reducing adjacent segment stress.

This meta-analysis reveals comparable outcomes in intervertebral foramen parameters (IFH P = 0.89; IFA P = 0.93) and global lordosis (P = 0.81) between techniques. However, a deeper examination of sagittal balance parameters uncovers critical nuances: while both procedures similarly improve PI-LL in mild-to-moderate cases (ΔPI-LL 10–15°), OLIF demonstrates particular efficacy in correcting severe deformities (PI-LL > 20°) when combined with ACR. This advantage stems from OLIF’s unique capacity to access the anterior longitudinal ligament, enabling more aggressive lordosis correction compared to the posterior-only PLIF approach [16, 17, 20]. These findings align with the “triad principle” of ASD surgery: where PLIF excels in direct neural decompression and posterior stability, OLIF offers superior balance restoration potential, particularly when addressing fixed sagittal plane deformities. The choice between techniques should therefore incorporate preoperative balance assessment, with PI-LL mismatch severity serving as a key decision-making parameter.

This meta-analysis research has several limitations that warrant discussion. First, all included studies were retrospective and had relatively short follow-up durations. As a result, important clinical characteristics, long-term clinical benefits, and late postoperative complications may not have been fully captured, potentially introducing unrecognized biases. Second, a high degree of heterogeneity was observed in some outcomes, including certain operative and radiologic measures. Third, only nine articles met the inclusion criteria, and the relatively small sample size may limit the strength of the evidence for the outcomes. Fourth, there was limited comparison of the learning curves for OLIF and PLIF in the included studies. Furthermore, the heterogeneous application of OLIF techniques (stand-alone, with fixation, with ACR) across studies introduces another layer of complexity. Given that OLIF is an emerging minimally invasive technique with surgical approaches that differ from traditional methods, its technical complexity and learning curve, particularly for advanced maneuvers like ACR, should be explored in future research. Despite these limitations, the trends in the data sets regarding surgical time, hospital stays, blood loss, and complication rates were highly significant (P < 0.002). These findings suggest that OLIF is superior to PLIF in improving operative outcomes and reducing complication rates.

In conclusion, our meta-analysis demonstrates that both OLIF and PLIF procedures offer equivalent efficacy in terms of VAS score, ODI, Modified MacNab score, IFH, IFA, LL, and fusion rates for the treatment of ASD. However, OLIF provides several advantages over PLIF, including shorter surgical duration and hospital stay, less estimated blood loss, lower rates of complications and reoperation, earlier postoperative mobilization, and superior restoration of IDH. This meta-analysis also serves as an informative reference for spine surgeons who have yet to adopt OLIF, highlighting the potential benefits of this minimally invasive technique in the treatment of ASD patients.
