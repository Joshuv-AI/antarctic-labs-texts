The correlation of cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging findings with clinical diagnoses and its acute impact on management changes in a regional Australian hospital
Authors: Clement Tan, Vaikunthan Thanabalasingam, Matthew I. Hiskens, Mark D. Higgins, Pavan Thaneeru, Zhihua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is a noninvasive, detailed imaging modality regarded as the gold standard for assessing cardiac structure, function, and tissue characteristics. Data on its impact in acute settings, particularly in regional centers, remain limited. We evaluated the clinical impact of CMR on diagnostic accuracy and management decisions in acute cardiovascular presentations at a single regional Australian hospital. This retrospective, single-center study included 111 adult acute patients who underwent CMR in 2024. Pre- and post-CMR diagnoses and management plans were compared using Cohen’s kappa to assess agreement and quantify overall impact. CMR led to significant diagnostic reclassification, as supported by fair agreement between pre- and post-CMR diagnoses (κ = 0.305, P < .001). Management plans changed in 66.6% of cases, with fewer unnecessary treatments and altered procedural decisions; agreement was slight (κ = 0.079, P = .046). CMR significantly improved diagnostic precision and influenced downstream clinical management in acute cardiac settings.

1. Introduction

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is an advanced and highly effective imaging technique that has become an essential tool in the assessment and diagnosis of a wide range of cardiovascular conditions. Over the last 50 years, it has grown in accessibility and application, allowing clinicians to gather highly comprehensive information about the heart’s structure and function.[1] CMR is widely regarded as the gold standard when it comes to measuring cardiac chamber volumes and function. Unlike other imaging methods, it offers the ability to capture images from virtually any plane, without being restricted by factors such as body habitus, making it particularly advantageous compared with transthoracic echocardiography, which can be limited by the patient’s body type.[2,3]

In addition to its utility in assessing cardiac function, CMR excels in evaluating myocardial tissue. Delayed enhancement imaging following administration of gadolinium-based contrast agents allows detailed tissue characterization and facilitates the detection of scar tissue, fibrosis, and myocardial injury, all of which are critical for diagnosing significant cardiovascular conditions.[4] More recently, innovations such as myocardial parametric tissue mapping have further enhanced the precision of myocardial tissue characterization, enabling finer distinctions between normal and damaged myocardium.[1,4] Native longitudinal relaxation time (T1) and transverse relaxation time (T2) mapping, key components of non-contrast CMR protocols, allow quantitative assessment without gadolinium, although some mapping approaches, such as extracellular volume, remain contrast dependent.[5]

CMR also plays a significant role in evaluating myocardial perfusion. By assessing blood flow to the myocardium, CMR provides valuable insights into ischemia and other perfusion abnormalities. Studies have shown that CMR is superior to other noninvasive techniques, such as single-photon emission computed tomography, in detecting myocardial perfusion defects, and it achieves this without exposing patients to ionizing radiation – an important advantage for individuals requiring repeated imaging.[1,6]

The interest and adoption of CMR without gadolinium contrast have also been gaining ground, supported by studies demonstrating that combining native myocardial tissue characteristics with advanced computer analytics can achieve diagnostic performance comparable to that of contrast-enhanced CMR.[1,4] The ability to perform high-quality CMR without contrast is particularly important because it may improve accessibility, shorten study time, and avoid contrast-related limitations – especially in patients with renal impairment or in settings where contrast administration is not feasible. It also offers additional advantages, including improved safety in patients with contrast allergies, avoidance of concerns about gadolinium retention, and greater feasibility in resource-limited environments, while emerging artificial intelligence-driven and radiomics-based techniques continue to expand the diagnostic capability of contrast-free CMR.[5,7,8]

With its numerous capabilities, CMR has become an indispensable and versatile imaging tool for investigating a wide range of cardiovascular diseases. Its broad diagnostic capability allows CMR to differentiate between cardiomyopathies – including infiltrative disorders – identify myocarditis, assess pericardial pathology, evaluate heart failure and valvular disease, and distinguish myocardial infarction from stress-induced cardiomyopathy, while also assessing myocardial viability and precisely localizing fibrosis associated with arrhythmias.[2,9] By integrating detailed anatomical, functional, and tissue-level information within a single examination, CMR supports accurate diagnosis, guides treatment decisions, and informs prognosis and therapeutic response.[4,6,10]

To date, CMR has been predominantly used in the assessment of stable outpatients, but its increasing availability, efficiency, and clinical utility have resulted in increased inpatient referrals. Despite this, there have not been many concrete objective assessments of the incremental influence CMR has on clinical decision-making over other well-established modalities or clear recommendations on its use in acute settings.[9,11] Therefore, with the continuous rise in the importance of safeguarding healthcare resources worldwide, demonstrated evidence for the direct additive impact CMR has on clinical assessment and management of acute cardiology patients has never been more paramount. Thus, in this single Australian regional center study, we sought to evaluate how CMR findings correlated with initial clinical diagnoses in acute clinical settings and how these findings influenced management plans for patients.

2. Methodology

2.1. Setting

Located in Australia, Mackay is Queensland’s fourth-largest regional city. A single regional hospital serves a large area within central-north Queensland encompassing 90,364 km2. It is a public hospital that provides emergency, medical, and surgical care in a facility of 257 beds. The hospital services smaller peripheral hospitals within the catchment, including the Whitsundays, Collinsville, Dysart, Bowen, and Moranbah. The closest tertiary referral hospital is approximately 387 km away. Mackay has approximately 122,000 people, a current Modified Monash Model rating of 2, and an Australian Statistical Geographical Classification of 2.[12] The cardiology department has a cardiac care unit with 7 beds and a single cardiac catheterization laboratory. It is staffed with 4 interventional cardiologists (two part-time), 1 full-time general cardiologist, 1 part-time cardiac electrophysiologist, and a cardiac clinical measurements team. The radiology department is staffed by 4 consultant radiologists during business hours, with after-hours imaging reports provided through an external teleradiology service. The region is supported by a single Siemens Healthineers Magnetom Vida wide-bore 3-tesla (3T) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system.

2.2. Study design and data source

This was a retrospective single-center correlational study conducted from January 2024 to December 2024. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Townsville Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee (EX/2025/QTHS/116343), and patient data were released in accordance with the state’s public health guidelines. We followed the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data extension of the STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology guidelines for reporting observational studies using routinely collected health data.[13]

We identified all acute CMR scans performed over this period. The inclusion criteria involved adult patients who underwent CMR as part of their acute admission in 2024, those whose CMR scans were satisfactorily completed with an accompanying complete and verified report by a radiologist, and those with clearly stated pre-CMR clinical diagnoses such as acute myocardial infarction or acute coronary syndromes, cardiomyopathies, acute heart failure, ventricular tachycardia, and myocardial and/or pericardial diseases. Patients were excluded from the study if the CMR scan was not completed satisfactorily, if they had contraindications to receiving a CMR scan, and/or if CMR scans were not reported in completion by a radiologist.

Data were collected and analyzed retrospectively from the integrated electronic medical record system and imaging reports. We included general patient demographics and a range of relevant cardiac comorbidities. These included active smoking, alcohol use, and active intravenous drug use, as well as a history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus. Cardiovascular conditions such as atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter, heart failure, ischemic heart disease, valvular heart disease, and any form of cardiomyopathy were also recorded. Patients without any of the above conditions were categorized as having no documented comorbidities. For the pre-CMR period, we documented the suspected diagnosis, which also translates to the clinical indication for CMR in this study, and the initial treatment plan. Post-CMR, we recorded the diagnosis made and whether the diagnosis changed following the scan. We then examined how CMR influenced management plans by recording changes made to the initial management. Following analysis of the dataset, the final categories used for diagnoses and management pre- and post-CMR are listed in Table 1. Categories were made and agreed upon by investigators with the aim of being specific while being able to encompass a range of diagnoses and management plans undertaken. Notably, for this study, the category of “Cardiomyopathy” includes all forms excluding ischemic cardiomyopathy, that is, primary causes such as genetic, mixed, and acquired causes, and secondary causes such as cardiomyopathies from other systemic disease entities. Ischemic cardiomyopathy and coronary artery disease (CAD) were categorized as one, and the assessment of myocardial viability was treated as a distinct diagnostic indication.[14] All CMR scans were reported by senior consultant radiologists, with any discordant cases reviewed and resolved through departmental discussion among multiple senior consultant radiologists.

2.3. Cardiac magnetic resonance protocol

All CMR studies were performed on the same MRI system, which is equipped with high-performance gradients and multi-transmit radiofrequency technology. The system also incorporated BioMatrix Technology with physiological monitoring, enabling electrocardiogram- and respiration-gated cardiac imaging. CMR scans were performed using a standardized protocol including cine balanced steady-state free precession imaging for ventricular volumes and function; black-blood T2-weighted turbo spin-echo (TSE) imaging with fat saturation for myocardial edema assessment; black-blood T1-weighted TSE imaging for anatomical characterization; half-fourier acquisition single-shot TSE imaging; phase-contrast velocity mapping for assessment of aortic and valvular flow; and native T1 mapping for quantitative tissue characterization. Gadobutrol (Gadovist/Gadavist) from Bayer, was used as the contrast agent for all contrast-enhanced studies. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) imaging was performed using phase-sensitive inversion recovery sequences acquired in multiple planes 10 to 20 minutes after intravenous gadolinium-based contrast administration, with inversion time guided by an inversion time scout to null normal myocardium. Stress perfusion imaging was not performed. The median scan-to-report time was 227 minutes (interquartile range: 198–226).

2.4. Statistical analysis

Demographic data are presented as frequencies and/or proportions. Agreement between the clinician-determined indication pre-CMR and the post-CMR diagnostic category, and agreement between planned patient management pre-CMR and the management implemented post-CMR, was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. Variables were categorical with no assumed ordinal relationship. The unweighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated using the kappa2() function from the irr package in R (Version 4.2.2) to quantify inter-rater reliability while accounting for agreement expected by chance. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived for all kappa estimates. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

In total, 111 patients were included in this study, and 1 patient was excluded because CMR was not completed satisfactorily due to artifacts from irremovable chest wall foreign objects. The baseline demographics and relevant cardiac comorbidities are listed in Table 2. The majority of the patients were male (63.9%). The median age within the included patients was 51 years (interquartile range: 37–63). The most common cardiac comorbidities were hypertension in 34.2%, dyslipidemia in 23.4%, followed by diabetes mellitus at 18.9%. Of note, all patients received a recent transthoracic echocardiogram prior to their CMR study.

Following CMR, diagnoses were revised in a significant number of cases. The number of patients diagnosed with cardiomyopathy decreased to 35.1%. The number of patients diagnosed with CAD or ischemic cardiomyopathy increased to 15.3%. In the post-cardiac arrest group, 4.5% were found to have viable or nonviable myocardium. Myocarditis was adjudicated according to the 2018 updated Lake Louise Criteria, requiring at least one T2-based marker of myocardial edema (T2-weighted black-blood imaging or T2 mapping) and one T1-based marker of myocardial injury (phase-sensitive inversion recovery LGE, native T1 mapping, or extracellular volume fraction) to classify a scan as consistent with acute myocarditis.[5] With the use of this, the number of patients with confirmed myocarditis on CMR decreased to 11.7%. The number of findings pertaining to the “other” category was similar post-CMR at 7.2%. Notably, 26.1% of patients had normal CMR findings, despite prior clinical suspicion of underlying pathology; this meant an absence of CMR features suggestive of the pre-CMR clinical diagnoses and/or an otherwise normal CMR.

Overall, the agreement between the clinicians’ initial indication for CMR and the final diagnosis determined by CMR findings was assessed using Cohen’s kappa. There was a fair level of agreement between the 2 (κ = 0.305, 95% CI: 0.18–0.43, z = 4.84, P < .001), based on data from 111 subjects. The overall percent agreement between pre- and post-CMR diagnoses was 47.8%. These findings suggest that CMR use led to reclassification or exclusion of initial diagnoses in a substantial proportion of patients.

3.2. Management before and after cardiac MRI

The respective management pre- and post-CMR are listed in Table 4. Pre-CMR, pharmacological treatments were planned in 50.5% of cases. In the post-CMR period, pharmacological management that was adjusted or newly commenced decreased to 30.6%; however, it still reflected the most frequent form of management. Plans for early percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or cardiac electrophysiological procedures were made in 18.9% of cases pre-CMR. In this instance, most of these were for early PCI. Further interventions were performed in 14.4% of patients after CMR results were reviewed. Similarly, the proportion of patients referred for early surgical interventions with cardiothoracic surgery teams for consideration of procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafts, pericardiectomy, and intracardiac mass removal increased from 3.6% to 8.1% following CMR.

Multidisciplinary team discussions with a combination of tertiary center colleagues from fields of cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, vascular surgery, and medicine were held for 2.7% of patients before CMR and increased slightly to 4.5% post-CMR. While 24.3% of patients had no clear management plan before CMR, 9.0% were directed toward further diagnostic investigations post-CMR. This was mainly due to CMR features suspicious of infiltrative cardiomyopathies and recurrent pericardial effusions. Notably, 33.3% of patients had no change in their pre-CMR management plan following imaging, including cases where no further investigations were deemed necessary.

To assess the influence of CMR findings on clinical management, agreement between management plans pre- and post-CMR was analyzed using Cohen’s kappa. Agreement was slight (κ = 0.079, 95% CI: 0.001–0.156, z = 1.99, P = .046), with an overall percent agreement of 27.0%, suggesting that MRI findings frequently altered the intended clinical management pathway through the provision of diagnostic clarity.

4. Discussion

This study highlights the pivotal role CMR has with 111 patients in an acute clinical setting, conducted at a regional Australian hospital, on how it refines cardiovascular diagnoses and impacts downstream management decisions.

From a diagnosis perspective, the most common indications for CMR were cardiomyopathy and myocarditis, followed by CAD. To improve clinical clarity, assessment of myocardial viability was analyzed as a distinct diagnostic category, given its direct implications for revascularization and surgical decision-making. Cardiomyopathy made up 53% of indications; however, the diagnosis of cardiomyopathy reduced to just 35.1% of cases post-CMR. These findings were akin to those of a similarly sized study by Hampal et al in the United Kingdom, which had cardiomyopathy as the most common indication, followed by myocardial viability and CAD at 53%, 17%, and 12%, respectively, where the diagnosis of cardiomyopathy reduced from 53% to 17% post-CMR.[15] For larger-scale studies, like the European Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance registry and the study by Rajwani et al, cardiomyopathies also made up a large proportion of the initial indication, and the use of CMR benefited in making definitive diagnoses of cardiomyopathies in 16% and 85% of cases, respectively.[11,16] These findings support the potential that CMR has over conventional imaging modalities, such as echocardiograms in inconclusive cases, reducing overdiagnosis and/or unnecessary treatments in patients with suspected cardiomyopathies.

Historically, endomyocardial biopsy was the gold standard in the diagnosis of myocarditis. However, it has now been replaced with CMR as a key noninvasive diagnostic tool, using the updated 2018 Lake Louise Criteria, which combines T1- and T2-based imaging sequence markers of myocardial injury. CMR is also crucial in prognosticating myocarditis, with LGE being a stronger predictor of mortality as compared with elevated extracellular volume.[1,5] All confirmed myocarditis cases in our study received gadolinium-based contrast, which enabled comprehensive assessment according to the 2018 Lake Louise Criteria and supported definitive diagnosis in each instance. CAD as an indication for CMR was lower in our study at 12.6%, much like that of the study by Hampal et al, as compared with larger-scale studies, including European Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance and von Knobelsdorff-Brenkenhoff et al. In both these studies, CAD as an indication was at 26.2% and 31%, respectively, which were similar to that of their commonest indication for CMR.[15–17] Notably, both these larger-scale studies included stress CMR studies that have been historically recommended as an outpatient modality for evaluation of CAD, which could have accounted for a rise in CAD as an indication for CMR and hence, the similarity in indication frequencies to the commonest indication.[16–18] Another possibility for this could have been because of increased resources in tertiary centers, such as the presence of more MRI machines or round-the-clock access to MRI scans, allowing for CMRs to be done more efficiently. The observed changes in confirmed cases of myocarditis and CAD and/or ischemic cardiomyopathy in our study demonstrate an added sensitivity for CMR in identifying pathologies.

Overall, the diagnostic shift observed is supported by the statistical assessment of fair agreement between pre- and post-CMR diagnoses. This highlights that while clinicians often had a reasonable initial suspicion, CMR findings contributed substantially to either confirming, refining, or completely overturning initial diagnoses. This level of diagnostic reclassification reinforces the strength of CMR as a tool for enhancing diagnostic accuracy in heterogeneous cardiac presentations, especially in scenarios where echocardiography or biomarkers may offer limited specificity.

From a management perspective, the use of CMR significantly influenced management pathways in 66.6% of patients in our cohort, prompting escalation and de-escalation of clinical interventions. These findings are congruent with findings from other studies, both of similar and larger scales.[15–17,19] The observed reduction in pharmacological interventions, which were largely cardiac medications, post-CMR from 50.5% to 30.6% was most significant, suggesting a narrowing of treatment to those with CMR-confirmed pathology, thereby avoiding unnecessary medication exposure. Similarly, the slight reductions for requirements of cardiac catheterization laboratory or cardiac electrophysiology procedures, accompanied by an increase in referrals for surgical interventions, were also observed post-CMR. This could likely be attributable to improved and more in-depth anatomical and functional information supplied with the use of CMR.

There is growing evidence supporting the use of CMR prior to coronary angiograms in inpatient settings.[20] This is largely attributable to CMR’s unique ability to guide interventions, assess myocardial viability, and reduce the risks associated with invasive procedures, including exposure to ionizing radiation and iodinated contrast agents. Additionally, CMR offers a comprehensive, noninvasive evaluation of myocardial perfusion, tissue characterization, and coronary anatomy, all within a single study.[3,21] The continued development of coronary CMR angiography – through advancements in motion correction, image acceleration, and reconstruction techniques – is expected to further enhance image quality and diagnostic performance, thereby optimizing the management of CAD.[22]

In our center, access to coronary angiograms for PCIs and other advanced diagnostics for CAD – such as fractional flow reserve, optical coherence tomography, and intravascular ultrasound – is more readily available compared with the single MRI machine at our regional center, which already serves a large population and operates CMR studies only during standard working hours. Importantly, patients within our study with observed pre-CMR management of coronary angiograms and/or PCIs were all done in accordance with the national guidelines.[23]

Regardless, initial management with either CMR or PCIs has been shown to have no difference in adverse cardiac outcomes; however, CMR more accurately identified patients needing revascularization, improved PCI yields, reduced unnecessary procedures, and supported safe hospital discharges.[24] In our study, the influence of CMR on downstream clinical management was even more pronounced compared with the diagnostic shift, as reflected by only a slight level of agreement between pre- and post-CMR management plans. Which in this context, is indicative of the role CMR has in guiding clinical decisions and improving management through enhanced diagnostic insights.

From a broader perspective, CMR is well positioned for continued growth due to its capability of providing detailed yet comprehensive information on an array of cardiovascular diseases, all within a single study. The observed discrepancies between pre- and post-CMR-confirmed diagnoses, alongside the low agreement in management decisions pre- and post-CMR, suggest that earlier integration of CMR into cardiovascular diagnostic and treatment strategies may shorten diagnostic journeys, reduce misdiagnosis, enhance safety, optimize treatment, and improve vital resource usage. Furthermore, a growing body of literature advocates for more CMR-related trials to help further develop it into a tool for the future of personalized medicine, novel therapies, imaging biomarkers, artificial intelligence, and machine learning.[2,22]

Our findings support a meaningful role for CMR in contemporary chest pain pathways. In patients with intermediate-risk presentations or inconclusive initial testing, CMR provides precise differentiation between ischemic and nonischemic etiologies such as myocarditis, myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries, or cardiomyopathies. By enabling diagnostic reclassification and guiding management, CMR can reduce unnecessary invasive procedures, optimize therapy, and inform follow-up intensity. These benefits align with the national, European Society of Cardiology, and American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guideline priorities for evidence-based, patient-centered care.[3,23] Incorporating CMR as a protocolized second-line imaging modality could enhance diagnostic accuracy, align therapy with underlying pathology, and optimize resource use, particularly in resource-constrained health systems.[2,5] Further studies are needed to determine whether CMR-integrated chest pain pathways improve clinical outcomes and healthcare efficiency.

4.1. Limitations

There are limitations in this study. This study provided results based on a small sample obtained from a single hospital. Furthermore, there is a relatively significant gender disparity, with most of the cohort being male. The smaller number of indications in the category of “other” for conditions such as myxomas, thrombi, and pericardial disease evaluation would further limit the extrapolation of the diagnostic and management data for these conditions. All of these could have affected the generalizability of the findings to other settings or populations. Interpretation of kappa values should be considered in the context of category prevalence and marginal imbalance, as high prevalence of certain diagnostic and management categories may attenuate kappa despite clinically meaningful disagreement. Finally, this is a retrospective study; therefore, data collection was reliant on the completeness of medical records, which may introduce potential biases or inaccuracies.

5. Conclusion

In this study done within a single regional Australian center, CMR demonstrated a positive incremental impact on a range of cardiovascular diagnoses and management in acute settings, helping clinicians align treatment decisions more appropriately with accurate diagnoses. Prospective studies could build upon these findings to explore whether CMR-based management strategies translate into quantifiable improvements in patient outcomes and key resource usage. Therefore, CMR undoubtedly holds strong potential to address key critical needs in diagnosing and treating cardiovascular conditions and would likely continue its trajectory as a forefront of cardiac imaging, enhancing clinical effectiveness in cardiac care delivery.
