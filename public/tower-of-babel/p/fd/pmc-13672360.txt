A multimodal instruction dataset and benchmark for ultrasound understanding
Authors: Shijie Wang, Yingnan Wu, Yilun Zhang, Zeyu Lai, Wei Lou, Dexing Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While promising in general medical imaging like CT and MRI, large vision-language models (LVLMs) struggle in ultrasonography due to a profound visual-semantic gap and a scarcity of instruction-following data. Translating the inherently noisy and operator-dependent ultrasound images into precise clinical descriptions remains a complex reasoning challenge for general-purpose vision-language models. To bridge this gap, we develop SonoInstruct, a large-scale multi-source dataset curated from over 30 ultrasound sources, providing 110k+ images and 260k+ instruction-following instances. For comprehensive evaluation, we establish SonoBench, a multi-dimensional benchmark suite that assesses eight core capabilities, including performance in out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios. To verify the effectiveness of our dataset, we fine-tuned Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct on SonoInstruct, yielding Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono, which achieves a 30.3% relative improvement over the base model on the SonoBench benchmark. These results demonstrate that SonoInstruct effectively bridges the gap between noisy sonographic images and clinical reasoning, establishing a robust foundation for AI-driven ultrasound applications.

Introduction

Large Vision-Language Models1,2 (LVLMs) have revolutionized clinical informatics by significantly enhancing automated medical interpretation3. However, current research remains heavily skewed toward standardized modalities such as CT and MRI4. In these domains, high spatial resolution and signal stability simplify cross-modal alignment. In contrast, ultrasonography presents a unique challenge characterized by a substantial visual-semantic gap. Ultrasound images result from complex and highly variable acoustic interactions, leading to substantial appearance heterogeneity. Interpreting these images is not merely an object recognition task; rather, it is a complex reasoning process in which clinicians decode sonographic artifacts (e.g., posterior shadowing, enhancement) into distinct semantic concepts (e.g., “calcification”, “fluid-filled”). General-domain LVLMs, lacking exposure to this specialized visual-linguistic correspondence, often fail to ground their reasoning in these subtle sonographic features, leading to hallucinations or clinically irrelevant descriptions5,6.

Data scarcity poses a severe bottleneck for ultrasound LVLMs7. Most public datasets are confined to discriminative tasks such as classification and segmentation8–10, lacking the semantic richness required for instruction tuning. Although LLaVA-ultra11 and Dolphin-AI12 are pioneers in ultrasound vision-language modeling, neither is open-source, hindering community-wide development of models that can articulate clinically grounded reasoning. A comparable limitation also plagues model evaluation and validation. While early benchmarks like U2-Bench13 and LLAUS14 have explored ultrasound-specific evaluation, their lack of publicly released data, code, or model weights severely limits reproducibility. Consequently, the field still lacks a transparent, clinically-grounded framework to support reproducible and collaborative advancement in ultrasound LVLM research.

To address these challenges, we present SonoInstruct and SonoBench: a large-scale instruction-tuning dataset and a comprehensive evaluation benchmark, for ultrasound vision-language modeling. Results

Dataset description

Publicly available ultrasound resources are predominantly limited to simple image-level labels or pixel-wise segmentation masks, resulting in a severe scarcity of instruction-following data for ultrasound LVLMs. To bridge this gap, we present SonoInstruct (Table 1), a large-scale dataset constructed by systematically repurposing and integrating heterogeneous open-source ultrasound resources. By harnessing more than 30 public datasets (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2) and incorporating expert knowledge extracted from ultrasound textbooks and clinical guidelines, we curated a diverse corpus of over 260,000 instruction-following image-text pairs. As shown in Fig. 1a, to ensure comprehensive task coverage, SonoInstruct is organized into four complementary subsets: SonoUnderstanding (105k pairs) establishes foundational diagnostic perception through anatomy recognition, feature detection, and lesion diagnosis; SonoGrounding (11k pairs) enables fine-grained spatial reasoning by transforming segmentation masks into bounding box coordinates; SonoReport (8k pairs) facilitates automated clinical report synthesis by distilling raw reports into structured formats; and SonoKnowledge (140k pairs) provides expert-level knowledge curated from ultrasound literature and clinical guidelines. Together, these subsets constitute the largest and most diverse instruction-tuning resource for ultrasound to date, enabling large-scale development and evaluation of ultrasound-specialized LVLMs. As detailed in Fig. 1c, SonoInstruct maintains exceptional anatomical diversity across 16 major categories, with a primary concentration in obstetrics and gynecology, featuring 28,113 cases for Fetus, 17,331 for Ovary, and 12,800 for Cervix. Our framework provides comprehensive ultrasound-specific expertise together with a standardized evaluation protocol.

To rigorously assess model capabilities, we introduce SonoBench, a comprehensive evaluation benchmark encompassing eight distinct clinical competencies. The benchmark covers perception tasks (AR: Anatomy Recognition, FD: Feature Detection, LD: Lesion Diagnosis, VG: Visual Grounding), generation tasks (RG: Report Generation), advanced knowledge reasoning tasks (GQA: Guideline QA, RQA: Research QA), and out-of-distribution (OOD) diagnostic generalization. The expert-annotated OOD subset enables robust assessment of model performance beyond the primary training distribution. All evaluation samples are strictly separated from the training data to prevent data leakage. In Table 1, we compare SonoInstruct and SonoBench against representative benchmarks in terms of modality, scale, data source, evaluation split, out-of-distribution (OOD) support, and open-source availability. Our framework combines large-scale ultrasound instruction tuning data with a clinically validated, OOD-aware evaluation protocol, enabling robust and reproducible assessment of ultrasound-specific vision-language models.

Experimental setup and baselines

To rigorously evaluate the efficacy of SonoInstruct, we fine-tune the language models of Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct15 and InternVL3.5-2B-Instruct16, while freezing their vision encoders to preserve pre-trained visual feature integrity. The resulting models are denoted as Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono and InternVL3.5-2B-Sono, respectively. We evaluate these two models against an extensive set of state-of-the-art vision-language models: (1) Closed-source foundation models, including GPT-517 (versions 5.2/5.1), Gemini-318 (Pro/Flash), and Grok-4.119; (2) Open-source generalists across varying scales (e.g., the Qwen3-VL family15, GLM-4.6v20, DeepSeek-VL-7B21, InternVL3.5-8B16, Gemma-3-4b-it22, Aihub-Phi-4-mm23, SmolVLM24, Jina-VLM25 and LLaVA-1.526); and (3) Medical-specific LVLMs such as HuatuoGPT-Vision-7B27, MedGemma-4B-it28 and MedVLM-R129. This comprehensive benchmark allows for a robust assessment of our ultrasound-specialized instruction-tuning approach against both general-purpose reasoning engines and medical-domain vision-language models.

As shown in Figs. 1b and 2, the SonoInstruct-tuned model Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono achieves an average score of 72.6% on SonoBench, establishing a new state-of-the-art among open-source large vision–language models and surpassing the best-performing closed-source baseline, Gemini-3-flash-preview (68.3%). Diagnostic performance

Diagnostic tasks in SonoBench evaluate both foundational perceptual recognition and higher-level pathological discrimination, primarily including Anatomy Recognition (AR), Feature Detection (FD), and Lesion Diagnosis (LD). Figure 2a shows that baseline LVLMs frequently struggle with anatomy recognition despite its intuitive nature for trained clinicians. This underperformance suggests that generic multimodal pretraining does not sufficiently encode the morphological priors required for sonographic interpretation.

After fine-tuning with SonoInstruct, Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono achieves 95.7% accuracy in AR. The improvement in Lesion Diagnosis is particularly pronounced, with an absolute gain of 41.5% over its general instruction-tuned baseline. These gains suggest improved robustness compared to generalist models, with fewer misclassifications across challenging cases. The diagnostic case studies presented in Fig. 3 further illustrate these trends. In several examples, baseline models generate diagnostically plausible responses that are not always fully consistent with the visual evidence in the ultrasound images, suggesting a potential reliance on general textual priors. In comparison, SonoInstruct-tuned models demonstrate improved consistency between the predicted diagnoses and the observed sonographic patterns. The qualitative visualizations also indicate more accurate localization of relevant pathological regions and clearer recognition of lesion boundaries in certain cases, suggesting that ultrasound-specific instruction tuning may help enhance visually grounded diagnostic reasoning.Fig. 3Case studies of SonoBench tasks.This panel illustrates the performance across different models on SonoBench clinical tasks. Knowledge reasoning performance

Knowledge reasoning tasks, such as Guideline QA, assess the model’s ability to retrieve and apply structured clinical knowledge in conjunction with visual input. Figure 2a reveals that model scaling primarily benefits knowledge-intensive domains. For example, the performance gap between Qwen3-VL-235B-Instruct and Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct in guideline-based QA is substantial, whereas the difference in pure visual feature detection tasks is negligible. This divergence suggests that increasing parameter count enhances knowledge retention and abstraction capacity more effectively than low-level visual discrimination.

Interestingly, reasoning-enhanced variants incorporating explicit “thinking” mechanisms provide only marginal and inconsistent improvements. Although such models may slightly improve performance in knowledge-heavy QA, they do not consistently benefit ultrasound-specific tasks and may even degrade performance in visually grounded settings. This trade-off implies that general reasoning frameworks are not a comprehensive solution for the specialized challenges of medical imaging, where perceptual precision often outweighs abstract reasoning chains.

Visual grounding performance

Visual grounding represents one of the most challenging capabilities for generalist LVLMs in medical imaging. Ultrasound interpretation requires precise localization of anatomical structures and pathological regions, often at fine-grained spatial resolution. Figure 2a shows that baseline models exhibit extremely low Intersection-over-Union (IoU) scores, with the base model achieving only 10.6%. Even strong closed-source systems such as Gemini-3-flash achieve an IoU of 59.4%, reflecting partial but still limited spatial alignment.

After fine-tuning with SonoInstruct, Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono achieves a robust 70.4% IoU, while InternVL3.5-2B-Sono reaches 65.0%. These results represent a substantial improvement in pixel-level grounding. The magnitude of this gain suggests that the primary bottleneck in medical visual grounding is not model scale alone but the absence of specialized supervision that explicitly aligns textual instructions with sonographic anatomy at a perceptual level.

The case studies in Fig. 3 highlight typical failure patterns in generalist models. In contrast, the SonoInstruct-tuned models produce tightly aligned predictions that correspond closely to ground-truth annotations. This improvement suggests that domain-aligned instruction improves visual–semantic alignment, partially mitigating limitations of current LVLM architectures.

Report generation performance

Report Generation (RG) evaluates the model’s ability to produce clinically relevant sonographic descriptions by integrating perceptual findings with common medical reporting conventions. As shown in Fig. 2a, applying SonoInstruct fine-tuning leads to improved RG scores, suggesting that domain-specific data helps the model better capture typical sonographic terminology and reporting patterns. However, a closer analysis of the qualitative results in Supplementary Fig. 8 reveals a more nuanced reality. While Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono shows substantial improvement in generating clinically relevant responses, it remains susceptible to clinical hallucinations, which is a persistent challenge for all vision-language models in this domain. As illustrated in the comparative case study (Supplementary Figures 8, 9, 10), models may generate structured and clinically plausible reports that occasionally include anatomical descriptors or measurements not supported by the underlying image evidence. This tendency toward hallucination is a primary factor behind the relatively low scores observed across all models under LLM-as-a-Judge metrics. Because the judge model penalizes factual inconsistencies and unsupported clinical details more severely than stylistic errors, these hallucinations highlight a critical gap between structural fluency and absolute diagnostic grounding. Addressing these inconsistencies remains essential for ensuring the ultimate value of automated ultrasound interpretation in real-world clinical workflows.

Across all evaluated dimensions, the results suggest that high-fidelity, domain-aligned instruction data can substantially improve the performance of compact 2B-parameter models on medical ultrasound tasks. Improvements are consistently observed across multiple metrics, indicating enhanced visual grounding, diagnostic reasoning, knowledge integration, and report generation. These findings support a data-centric perspective for clinical AI development, in which domain alignment helps compensate for limited model scale and improves performance on specialized medical tasks. In addition, the human expert evaluation results in Supplementary Table 3, based on assessment by a physician expert, show that the proposed model achieves performance comparable to human expert evaluation on several tasks, further suggesting the potential clinical utility of the proposed benchmark and training strategy. Furthermore, in Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 11, we provide a detailed analysis across different anatomical regions. Despite the long-tailed distribution of training data across anatomical sites, the proposed model does not exhibit severely degraded performance on underrepresented regions. However, due to annotation inconsistencies and task-specific data availability, some anatomical categories are only included in certain tasks and are therefore not fully suitable for direct averaging across all benchmarks. As a result, the reported averaged scores for anatomical regions should be interpreted as reference results rather than strict holistic performance indicators.

The integration of SonoUnderstanding leads to the most substantial improvement in diagnostic performance. Compared with the base model, AR (Anatomy Recognition) accuracy increases from 59.4 to 95.7%, while LD (Lesion Diagnosis) improves by 38.2 percentage points. These results demonstrate that SonoUnderstanding substantially enhances the model’s ultrasound-specific visual perception and diagnostic understanding capabilities.

While the base model struggles with visual grounding (VG score of 10.6%), incorporating SonoGrounding substantially improves localization performance, increasing the IoU score to 70.4%. This large gain indicates that domain-specific grounding supervision provides stronger alignment between textual queries and corresponding anatomical regions in ultrasound images. Qualitative examples further suggest that the model more accurately identifies relevant spatial regions and distinguishes target structures from surrounding tissues. These results highlight the importance of incorporating explicit spatial supervision in improving grounding performance in medical ultrasound settings.

Incorporating SonoReport is designed to enhance the generative capabilities of the model. We observe a substantial improvement in the RG (Report Generation) score, increasing from 27.4 to 44.8%. By training on report-style instructions, the model learns to generate ultrasound reports that better follow standard medical reporting conventions rather than generic image descriptions.

The inclusion of SonoKnowledge enhances the model’s ultrasound-specific medical knowledge. The GQA score increases to 72.1%, while the RQA score reaches 75.7%, suggesting that literature-derived triplets and curated clinical guidelines provide valuable medical context for complex reasoning tasks.

We further conduct additional ablation studies to better understand the contribution of different data components and architectural choices. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, our model achieves statistically significant improvements over the baseline models. Moreover, comparing single-subset training with the full mixed dataset (Supplementary Fig. 5) reveals that, despite a few exceptions (e.g., VG and RG tasks), multi-component training consistently yields stronger overall performance and generalization. We also investigate the effect of the vision encoder by unfreezing the ViT backbone (Supplementary Fig. 6), which improves visual perception tasks such as feature detection and lesion diagnosis, but degrades performance on knowledge-intensive tasks, likely due to misalignment between updated visual representations and the language model. Finally, we extend the Anatomy Recognition task to an open-ended setting, observing a substantial drop in accuracy across models; however, incorporating a small amount of open-ended training data effectively restores performance to near-original levels (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this study, we addressed the critical scarcity of specialized foundation models for ultrasonography by introducing a comprehensive framework comprising the SonoInstruct dataset and the SonoBench evaluation framework. A key observation from our benchmarks is the persistent visual-semantic gap inherent in current Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs). We found that while generalist models can leverage large-scale pre-training to answer knowledge-based clinical queries, they often underperform on fundamental perceptual tasks, such as Anatomy Recognition. This underscores a critical deficiency in their understanding of sonographic morphology. However, our results demonstrate that high-quality, domain-specific instruction tuning effectively bridges this gap. The integration of SonoInstruct served as a catalyst, enabling lightweight models (e.g., Qwen3-VL-2B-Sono) to achieve substantial gains not only in basic perception but also in complex diagnostic tasks (e.g., a + 41.5% improvement in Lesion Diagnosis), evolving them into proficient clinical experts.

Our analysis further examines the distinct roles of model scaling and reasoning mechanisms in medical imaging. We observed that while increasing model parameters significantly benefits knowledge-retention tasks like Guideline QA, it yields diminishing returns for purely visual perception tasks. Similarly, models equipped with general “reasoning” or “thinking” mechanisms exhibited only marginal and inconsistent improvements, revealing a trade-off where enhanced logic does not necessarily translate to better visual grounding. This is particularly evident in pixel-level tasks; general-purpose LVLMs struggle severely with visual grounding, whereas our fine-tuned models achieved a robust IoU of over 70%. These findings suggest that for specialized domains like ultrasound, blind scaling or generic reasoning modules are less effective than targeted, high-quality visual instruction alignment.

Despite these advancements, our study has several limitations. First, our framework is built upon static image–text data and does not incorporate ultrasound video, thus lacking the ability to capture temporal dynamics that are often important in clinical examinations. In addition, the ultrasound images were collected from heterogeneous public sources and clinical export systems, which may introduce variability in image quality, resolution, compression artifacts, and acquisition protocols across different devices and institutions. Such heterogeneity may affect the robustness and generalizability of the model in real-world clinical settings. Second, similar to other LVLM-based approaches, the model may produce hallucinated outputs, occasionally generating plausible but inaccurate descriptions that are not fully grounded in the visual input. Third, the training data may exhibit distributional imbalance across anatomical regions, with certain categories such as breast and thyroid being overrepresented due to the inherent bias in publicly available datasets. To mitigate this issue, we ensure that the test set is balanced across different anatomical regions to enable fair evaluation. These limitations highlight the need for future work on incorporating temporal information, improving output reliability, and addressing data imbalance.

Methods

The SonoInstruct dataset construction

We present SonoInstruct, a large-scale multimodal instruction-tuning dataset specifically curated to bridge the gap in ultrasound-specific perception and reasoning. As illustrated in Fig. 5, SonoInstruct aggregates more than 30 public datasets (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), resulting in a diverse corpus of 260k+ instruction-following pairs. This multi-source integration is further enriched by filtered biomedical instructions and literature-derived knowledge. To ensure comprehensive task coverage, the dataset is organized into four primary subsets: SonoUnderstanding (105k pairs), SonoGrounding (11k), SonoReport (8k), and SonoKnowledge (140k).Fig. 5Pipeline for SonoInstruct dataset construction.(1) Data curation: Multimodal ultrasound data, including images, segmentation masks, descriptions, and labels, are collected from diverse open-source repositories and medical literature (e.g., PubMed, PMC) covering 16 anatomical categories. Pipeline for SonoInstruct dataset construction.

(1) Data curation: Multimodal ultrasound data, including images, segmentation masks, descriptions, and labels, are collected from diverse open-source repositories and medical literature (e.g., PubMed, PMC) covering 16 anatomical categories. (2) Data processing: Raw data are standardized through OCR-based text extraction, mask-to-bounding-box transformation, and LLM-driven rule-based standardization across over 30 datasets. To establish SonoUnderstanding, we employ a systematic integration of template-based synthesis and LLM-assisted generation (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2) to produce high-fidelity vision-language instruction pairs. This process transforms heterogeneous clinical annotations into structured, multi-turn conversational and multiple-choice instruction-following pairs. By normalizing diverse source data into a unified metadata format, we bridge the gap between expert clinical perception and model reasoning across three specialized tasks: Anatomy Recognition (AR), Feature Detection (FD) and Lesion Diagnosis (LD). Anatomy Recognition task evaluates the model’s ability to distinguish and orient anatomical regions within various sonographic planes (e.g., “Which organ is visualized in this scan?”). Based on our curated datasets covering diverse anatomical regions, we construct Q&A pairs using fixed question templates supplemented with randomized options to ensure robust evaluation. Interestingly, while such identification is trivial for human clinicians, our observations indicate that general-purpose LVLMs frequently struggle with basic anatomical orientation more than they do with complex diagnostic reasoning. Feature Detection task focuses on characterizing fundamental ultrasound-specific attributes, such as echogenicity, texture homogeneity, and posterior acoustic enhancement. To build these instructions, we utilize the fine-grained features annotated in our datasets as grounding context; we then prompt an LLM (GPT-5) to synthesize diverse and clinically relevant Q&A pairs that describe these specific morphological hallmarks (Supplementary Fig. 2). The QA pairs are grounded in real ultrasound images, structured clinical annotations, diagnostic reports. This task enables the model to achieve precise recognition of the sonographic properties that define sonographic morphology. Lesion Diagnosis task targets high-level pathological characterization through manually curated benign and malignant labels alongside standardized clinical scoring systems such as TI-RADS for thyroid or O-RADS for ovarian masses. We utilize both fixed templates and LLM (GPT-5) prompting to generate diverse multiple-choice and conversational Q&A pairs. For multiple-choice questions, we employ standardized inquiry templates and dynamically generate plausible but incorrect distractors alongside the ground-truth answer to challenge the model’s diagnostic reasoning. This methodology ensures that the model can synthesize complex visual cues into clinically relevant classifications while maintaining alignment with professional diagnostic standards.

While pixel-level segmentation masks are prevalent in medical imaging, standard LVLMs often lack the inherent architecture to directly utilize or generate dense masks. To bridge this gap, SonoGrounding transforms binary masks into Region-of-Interest (ROI) bounding boxes, enabling precise spatial reasoning within a text-based instruction framework. Our construction process involves the systematic curation of diverse ultrasound datasets across multiple anatomical regions, specifically selecting data samples that contain high-quality manual masks or bounding box annotations. For datasets originally containing only segmentation masks, we employ the OpenCV library to automatically extract the precise coordinate information required to generate corresponding bounding boxes. Following the spatial tokenization paradigm established by the Qwen-VL series, we normalize all extracted coordinates to a [0, 1000] scale to ensure consistency across varying image resolutions. We then utilize template-based questioning to generate grounding-specific instruction pairs, where the model is prompted to detect specific lesions or anatomical structures and respond with the normalized bounding box coordinates in a structured JSON-like format. This transformation enables more accurate visual grounding by aligning textual instructions with localized sonographic regions.

We construct SonoReport to facilitate the automated generation of comprehensive clinical reports. Leveraging the KMVE dataset30, we transform raw ultrasound reports into a structured instruction-tuning format by randomly adding one of the template questions which asking model to generate an ultrasound report to the dataset. Besides, to further strengthen the model’s proficiency in generating accurate and clinically consistent medical image descriptions, we introduce a large language model-based data refinement strategy. Specifically, we leverage advanced LLMs (GPT-5) to reorganize and distill unstructured raw medical text data into structured multi-granularity question-answer (QA) pairs. These refined QA samples cover diverse hierarchical medical information, including fine-grained lesion attributes such as size, shape, boundary and echo characteristics, as well as comprehensive clinical observations and diagnostic summaries. This effective medical-oriented data augmentation paradigm enables our model to fully learn detailed localized diagnostic features as well as logical, coherent clinical reasoning, thereby greatly improving the rationality and professionalism of its generated medical descriptive content.

To equip the model with expert-level knowledge, we construct SonoKnowledge, a comprehensive ultrasound-specific dataset with over 100,000 entries curated from literature, clinical guidelines, medical VQA datasets and medical exams. We construct a high-quality VQA collection by integrating diverse data sources and a multi-stage filtering pipeline. First, we retrieved 10,467 ultrasound-related articles (2023–2025) from the PMC-OA subset and 112 ultrasound clinical guidelines (2017–2025) for organs such as the breast, thyroid, and ovaries31–33. Specifically, the literature was collected through PubMed using the MeSH term “Ultrasonography”, with filtering restricted to “Free full text” articles and guideline-related subcategories, followed by intersection with the PMC-OA collection to ensure open-access availability. Following the LLaVA-Med34 pipeline, we extracted image-caption-text triplets via OCR and employed GPT-5 to generate multi-turn dialogues that emphasize visual features over general facts (Supplementary Fig. 1). Finally, we generated 61,532 VQA pairs, including 1025 guideline-based QA samples derived from authoritative clinical guidelines and consensus documents.

To further enhance robustness, we incorporated relevant samples from large-scale medical datasets, including PubMedVision27 and Huatuo-26M35. Keyword matching and LVLM-based verification were applied across all sources to ensure strict relevance to the ultrasound domain. Specifically, we first applied a keyword-based filtering step using ultrasound-related terms such as ultrasound, B-mode, Doppler, echocardiography, sonography, and fetal ultrasound to select candidate questions that are likely relevant to ultrasound imaging and its clinical applications. Building upon this, we employed a large vision-language model (Gemini-3-Flash) to further examine the associated images, verifying whether they correspond to ultrasound modalities by identifying characteristic visual patterns, such as grayscale texture, fan-shaped fields of view, or spectral Doppler signals. Non-ultrasound images were discarded at this stage. We curated 82,961 ultrasound QA pairs from open access medical QA datasets.

Quality assurance protocol

High-quality data is paramount for medical domain adaptation. We implemented a unified “Generation-then-Verification” pipeline to minimize noise and hallucinations.

First, we applied visual modality gating to remove irrelevant figures. A vision-language model (Gemini-3-Flash) acts as a gatekeeper to automatically discard non-ultrasound images extracted from PDFs. Specifically, among the 10,467 documents we processed, we identified 13,224 ultrasound images and 33,085 non-ultrasound images, as a substantial portion of figures in the source documents consist of charts and diagrams rather than clinical imaging.

We then conducted visual-semantic verification to mitigate hallucinations. To mitigate hallucinations, we employed an LLM-based discriminator (Gemini-3-Flash) to evaluate the consistency between generated question-answer pairs and their associated visual context. This stage removes samples that rely on non-inferable external knowledge, thereby ensuring that the generated interactions remain grounded in observable ultrasound features. In particular, we excluded questions involving patient-specific metadata (e.g., age, sex, or clinical history) or requiring clinical information that could not be directly inferred from the ultrasound images. To assess the reliability of this verification process, we randomly sampled 250 question-answer pairs for independent evaluation by two annotators with medical backgrounds. The decisions of the LLM-based verifier demonstrated a 95.8% agreement rate with human annotations, indicating strong consistency between automated filtering and expert assessment.

Finally, we performed semantic deduplication. Redundant samples were removed using SentenceTransformer36 embeddings and cosine similarity. This step is designed to eliminate semantically duplicate or highly similar content, thereby reducing redundancy and improving the diversity and overall quality of the dataset.

In addition, we further conducted manual spot-checking of randomly sampled QA pairs from different subsets. Human reviewers examined the visual relevance, question validity, and answer correctness of the generated samples. The inspection results showed that the majority of sampled QA pairs were well-grounded in the ultrasound content without obvious systematic errors, indicating that the proposed automatic verification pipeline provides reliable quality control for large-scale QA generation.

These five subsets were constructed using label-based templates (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2) and were strictly drawn from the predefined test split of the original dataset, ensuring a clear separation from the training data. In addition, we enforce a balanced distribution of organ types across all subsets, avoiding over-representation of any specific anatomical region.

To probe expert-level knowledge alignment, we included Guideline QA (GQA) and Research QA (RQA), which were synthesized via LLMs to cover complex diagnostic standards and cutting-edge literature. Specifically, GQA is derived from newest open-access ultrasound guideline documents sourced from professional organizations such as the American College of Radiology (ACR) and the International Society of Ultrasound in Obstetrics and Gynecology (ISUOG), covering breast, thyroid, ovary, liver, echocardiography, and gynecologic. RQA is constructed from 125 ultrasound case report (2024–2026) publications indexed in PubMed. We further ensure that these source documents are strictly separated from the model training corpus and do not overlap with any training data, thereby preventing potential data leakage and enabling a more reliable assessment of knowledge generalization.

To ensure a rigorous assessment of the model’s generalization capabilities beyond the training distribution, we curated a specialized Out-of-Domain (OOD) QA set. This subset exclusively comprises 76 clinical cases sourced from the Medical Imaging Standard Database of the China National Health Commission, focusing on critical diagnostic areas including breast, thyroid and ovarian lesions. These cases were meticulously selected to represent diverse pathological categories, ranging from benign cysts and fibroadenomas to malignant invasive carcinomas. Expert annotators systematically labeled each case with fine-grained sonographic features (e.g., margins, echogenicity, calcifications) and definitive benign or malignant classifications. The task format for this OOD set is strictly designed as multiple-choice questions, requiring the model to select the correct diagnostic or morphological descriptor from a set of plausible clinical options. The entire data acquisition and annotation process was conducted in strict adherence to ethical requirements, ensuring that all patient data was fully de-identified.

Implementation details

The development of our ultrasound-specialized models follows a supervised fine-tuning (SFT) paradigm, utilizing Qwen3-VL-2B-Instruct and InternVL3.5-2B-Instruct as the foundational architectures. To preserve the foundational visual feature extraction capabilities inherited from the pre-trained vision encoders, we adopt a partial freezing strategy; specifically, the vision encoder and the Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) projector remain frozen throughout the training process, while only the Large Language Model (LLM) backbone undergoes gradient updates.

Training is conducted using the LLaMA-Factory (v0.9.4) framework, built upon PyTorch (v2.7.1) and CUDA 12.8. We utilize the AdamW optimizer with a weight decay of 0.01 and a decoupling of weight decay from the learning rate. The training objective is optimized over 2 epochs using a cosine learning rate decay schedule, initiated with a 3% linear warmup period to reach a peak learning rate of 2 × 10-5. To ensure stable convergence and efficient memory management, we set the global batch size to 32 (with a per-device micro-batch size of 1 and gradient accumulation steps of 32).

For data processing, all sonographic images are resized to a maximum resolution of 448 × 448 pixels to maintain a balance between fine-grained anatomical detail and computational efficiency. The maximum sequence length for the text backbone is capped at 2048 tokens to accommodate structured clinical reports and complex reasoning chains. All experiments and benchmark evaluations were executed on a high-performance compute cluster equipped with NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPUs (24GB VRAM).
