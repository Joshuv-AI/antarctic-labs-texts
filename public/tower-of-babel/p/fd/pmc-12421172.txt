Well-being among people with long and short COVID: a serial cross-sectional study in Victoria, Australia
Authors: Aimée Altermatt, Anna L Wilkinson, Katherine Heath, Defeng Jin, Thi Nguyen, Alexander J Thomas, Tianhui Ke, Yanqin Zhang, Rebecca E Ryan, Lisa Gibbs, Alisa Pedrana, Dean Lusher, Stephanie Fletcher-Lartey, Mark Stoové, Katherine B Gibney, Margaret Hellard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is critical to disseminate all evidence on long COVID’s impact on people’s lives to inform policy and practice. We aimed to assess five measures of well-being, before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection between people with long COVID (defined as symptoms lasting more than 1 month) and people with short COVID (defined as symptoms resolving within 1 month).MethodsParticipants from the Optimise Study, a longitudinal cohort study in Victoria, Australia (September 2020–August 2022), had self-reported history of SARS-CoV-2 infection and self-reported long COVID status. Serial cross-sectional analysis compared participants with long and short COVID on Personal Well-being Index, number of COVID-19-like symptoms experienced, number of days exercised and frequency of experiencing positive and negative emotions.Results217 participants were included, aged 20–86 years (median age 43, IQR: 31–57), 75% women. Compared with those with short COVID, participants with long COVID had lower well-being before (mean difference (MD)=−8.3, 95% CI (-14.7, –2.0), p-adjusted=0.07), during (MD=−10.3, 95% CI (–16.5, –4.0), p-adjusted=0.03) and after (MD=−9.91, 95% CI (–16.71, –3.11), p-adjusted=0.05) infection and experienced more COVID-19-like symptoms during infection (MD=1.72 (0.72, 2.72), p-adjusted=0.03). In December 2022, 71% (40/56) reported difficulty performing tasks in the past 4 weeks.ConclusionOn average, we observed lower well-being among participants with long COVID, including before SARS-CoV-2 infection, suggesting an underlying difference in well-being between groups. Long COVID continued to impact physical functioning, but ongoing changes were not detected by personal well-being scales.

Background

Defined broadly as the persistence of symptoms after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, long COVID is an international concern, and evidence to inform guidelines and clinical care for people experiencing long COVID is still emerging. The WHO defines post-COVID-19 condition (long COVID) as the continuation or development of new symptoms 3 months after the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, with these symptoms lasting for at least 2 months with no other explanation.1 The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) defines long COVID as symptoms that continue or develop from 4 weeks after a COVID-19 infection.2 Alongside varying definitions, there remains a lack of clarity on symptoms,3 treatments and diagnostics4; public health epidemiology including risk factors, prevention, incidence and prevalence5; and social epidemiology, including people’s experiences of long COVID and its burden on their lives.6 7

It is critical to understand the population-level impact long COVID has on individuals to inform public health response. Self-reported data, measured by subjective well-being,8 can help assess the broader impact of long COVID on affected populations and guide clinical practice.9 While there are mixed reports of whether personal well-being levels changed during the COVID-19 pandemic in Australia,1012 studies have consistently found evidence of lower well-being among people with long COVID. An Australian study reported lower levels of life satisfaction compared with people with acute or no COVID-1913; a Japanese study found that long COVID lowered health-related quality of life (HRQoL)14 and a systematic review found an association between long COVID and poor quality of life.15 However, most research14 16 17 was conducted in people after long COVID emerged, leaving a gap in our knowledge on the health and well-being of people experiencing long COVID before their long COVID occurred.

The Optimise Study (Optimise) was a longitudinal cohort study of adults residing in Victoria, Australia, conducted 2020–2022.18 Over the study period, Victoria experienced five lockdowns, with the 7 day average number of COVID-19 cases peaking at 38 370 in January 2022.19 Optimise provided an opportunity to examine long COVID because the study commenced early in the pandemic, participants were vulnerable to SARS-CoV-2 infection, high-frequency data collection occurred with minimal loss-to-follow-up, and incident long COVID cases were observed.20 Optimise collected self-reported data before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection and include participants with acute (short) or sustained (long) COVID-19 symptoms. We aimed to assess five measures of well-being, comparing people with long and short COVID before and after SARS-CoV-2 infection. We also describe the experiences of people living with long COVID using domains of the 36-Item Short Form Survey instrument (SF-36).21

Methods

Study design and setting

Optimise was a longitudinal cohort study conducted in Victoria, Australia. Participants completed baseline surveys on recruitment between September 2020 and December 2021 and monthly surveys until August 2022. Less regular follow-up of participants has continued beyond August 2022. Detailed descriptions of Optimise are reported elsewhere.202224

Participants

Participants were aged 18 years or older, residing in Victoria, Australia. Optimise purposively oversampled people at increased risk of (i) contracting COVID-19, (ii) developing severe COVID-19 or (iii) adverse consequences of COVID-19 restrictions. There was purposive recruitment of participants from culturally and linguistically diverse communities.22 Optimise aimed to recruit 1000 participants, based on targets for key population groups.24

Data sources/measurement

Surveys and diaries

Participants completed a baseline survey detailing their socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, postcode) and personal well-being, among other topics. Every 4 weeks, participants were sent survey links by email or SMS or received calls from a researcher to complete one follow-up survey and four diaries. Follow-up surveys covered domains including COVID-19 testing, personal well-being, presence of COVID-19-like symptoms and physical exercise in the past 4 weeks. Diaries collected data on recent COVID-19 testing and frequency of positive and negative emotions the previous day.

COVID status snapshot surveys

In August 2022, all retained study participants were invited to complete an additional cross-sectional survey. This COVID status snapshot survey contained additional questions about history of SARS-CoV-2 infection (‘how many times have you had COVID-19?’) and long COVID status (‘have you ever experienced long COVID?’), which were not included in regular follow-up surveys. The snapshot was designed to assess long COVID history within the Optimise Study at that point in time. Participants who reported a long COVID history in the August 2022 snapshot were invited in December 2022 to complete another survey (the long COVID snapshot survey) on their experiences with long COVID. Participants were reimbursed $35 for completing the long COVID snapshot and research staff followed up participants via telephone to enhance completion rates.

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Included participants in this study (i) had completed the COVID status snapshot survey in August 2022 and (ii) had self-reported a date of a positive test result for a SARS-CoV-2 infection during the study period (September 2020–August 2022).

Main outcomes

Using our previous research,25 we selected five outcomes collected across prospective Optimise follow-up surveys and diaries since September 2020. The first outcome was a validated scale, the mean Personal Well-being Index (PWI), a subjective measure of life satisfaction (0–100). The remaining four outcomes were study-specific well-being measures: mean number of COVID-19-like symptoms experienced (0–12), mean days exercised for 30 minutes or more in the past week (0–7), frequency of positive emotions (always/often or sometimes/rarely/never) and frequency of negative emotions (always/often/sometimes or rarely/never). Refer to online supplemental A for details on how outcome measures were collected.

Exposure: long and short COVID

A COVID status (long, short) was assigned to each participant using responses from the COVID status snapshot. Following the NICE definition,2 long COVID was defined to participants as ‘experiencing new health problems that persisted for more than 1 month after being infected with COVID-19’. Participants who answered ‘yes, I think I have/have had long COVID, but I have not been diagnosed by a health professional’ or ‘yes and I have been diagnosed with long COVID by a health professional’ were classified as having long COVID. Participants who responded ‘no’ were classified as having short COVID. Participants who replied ‘prefer not to say’ were excluded from analyses.

Infection timeline

To construct a timeline of surveys and diaries (the source of outcome data) completed before, during and after a SARS-CoV-2 infection, we established each participant’s infection date. Participants classified as having long COVID were asked in December 2022 to recall the date of the SARS-CoV-2 infection that led to long COVID. This self-reported date was used as the infection date associated with their long COVID infection. For participants classified as having short COVID, the infection date was the self-reported date of a positive COVID-19 test collected through surveys and diaries during Optimise. Some participants with short COVID had multiple infections during the study period. We selected the infection date that had at least three surveys completed before and after it. This maximised the data available and minimised bias from missing data (online supplemental B).

The SARS-CoV-2 infection date described above was used as the midpoint of each participant’s timeline. To compare outcomes between the long and short COVID groups before, during and after infection, we created seven survey periods each of 28 days in length either side of infection. There were three pre-infection periods, one infection period (containing the infection date/midpoint) and three post-infection periods. Participants could have completed a survey or diary on any day in each 28-day period but could only contribute one survey and one diary per period. Participants did not have to contribute to all periods to be included (online supplemental C).

Secondary outcomes

Participants reporting long COVID in August 2022 (the COVID status snapshot survey) were asked in December 2022 (the long COVID snapshot survey) to rate their general health and completed questions 1–2 and 13–16 from the SF-36,21 modified to ask specifically about long COVID’s impact. These questions covered general health in the past week, general health relative to before developing long COVID and problems with work or other daily activities related to long COVID in the past 4 weeks. They were also asked, ‘Do you still have long COVID?’. Those whose symptoms had resolved were asked the duration of their symptoms (in months).

Statistical methods

We used summary statistics to describe baseline characteristics and COVID-19 infection history of the study sample, stratified by long and short COVID groups.

Mean PWI, number of symptoms experienced and number of days exercised were calculated at each survey period, for both groups. Differences between groups were assessed via an independent samples Welch’s t-test with 95% CIs and p values calculated. To explore individual-level changes over time, we conducted a supplementary analysis of paired outcome data.

X2 analysis tested for differences in proportions between the long and short COVID groups in frequency of experiencing positive and negative emotions. To ensure suitable cell size for comparison in contingency tables, we collapsed categories to combine ‘always’ and ‘often’ responses to positive emotions and collapsed ‘rarely’ and ‘never’ responses to negative emotions.

To correct for multiple testing, p values from X2 and t-tests were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.26 We reported adjusted p values, where an adjusted p value <0.05 indicates a statistically significant result.

To assess whether differences between long and short COVID groups persisted when the sample was stratified by PWI reported at baseline (surveys completed on recruitment into Optimise), we conducted a supplementary analysis, wherein we repeated the serial cross-sectional analysis of PWI scores separately for participants with PWI ≥65 at baseline, and PWI <65 at baseline. The threshold of 65 was chosen as it split the sample into groups of sufficiently large sizes for comparison. A threshold of 70 represents a ‘normal’ PWI score in Australia.27

Median symptom duration was calculated separately for those with resolved and those with ongoing long COVID symptoms. For those with ongoing symptoms, symptom duration was calculated as the number of months (a month was defined as 30 days long) between the infection date and the survey completion date.

PWI responses were classified as missing if any one of the seven questions was left unanswered or if mean PWI scores were equal to 0 or 100.8 Missing data (such as incomplete PWI responses, ‘prefer not to say’ responses to days exercised or missed surveys) did not contribute to the mean calculation for that survey period. The number of participants contributing to the mean calculations at each timepoint is summarised in online supplemental D.

Analysis was performed in R version 3.6.328 using tidyverse,29 gtsummary30 and rstatix31 packages.

Ethics approval for Optimise was provided by the Alfred Human Research Ethics Committee (approval number 333/20).

Results

Participants

Optimise recruited 779 participants in total; 653 (84%) were retained as of August 2022 and 499 (76% of 653) responded to the COVID status snapshot in August 2022. Of the 499 respondents, 243 (49%) reported ever testing positive to COVID-19, of whom 61 (25%) reported long COVID history, 181 (74%) had never had long COVID and one preferred not to say. Of the 181 who reported having COVID-19 but not long COVID, 161 (89%) had previously self-reported a date of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Of the 61 reporting long COVID, 56 (92%) responded to the long COVID snapshot in December 2022, confirming the date of their SARS-CoV-2 infection that led to long COVID. The final analysis dataset therefore consisted of 217 participants, 56 (26%) classified as having long COVID and 161 (74%) classified as having short COVID (figure 1).

Flowchart of participants included in this study. The final dataset consisted of 217 out of the 499 participants who completed the COVID status snapshot in August 2022. The remaining 282 respondents to the COVID status snapshot were excluded as follows: no reported history of COVID-19 (n=256); history of COVID-19 but preferred not to say if experienced long COVID (1); reported long COVID in August 2022 but did not complete the December 2022 long COVID snapshot (5); and classified as short COVID but no self-reported date of SARS-CoV-2 infection (20).

Descriptive data

Participants (n=217) were aged 20–86 years (median age 43, IQR: 31–57). Most were women (75%), born in Australia (68%) and spoke English as a main language at home (87%). 29% of the long COVID group and 41% of the short COVID group had a chronic health condition at baseline. There was a higher proportion of participants speaking a language other than English at home in the long COVID group (21%) than the short COVID group (11%). A greater proportion of people in the long COVID group had multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections; 22% (44/196) of people with one infection, 59% (10/17) of people with two infections and 50% (2/4) of people with three or more infections had long COVID (table 1). However, 82% (46/56) of people with long COVID reported developing long COVID on their first SARS-CoV-2 infection (table 3).

Main results

Mean PWI, number of symptoms, days exercised and experience of positive and negative emotions for the long and short COVID groups at seven timepoints are summarised in table 2 (figure 2). Before, during and after infection, participants in the long COVID group had lower average PWI scores than participants in the short COVID group (p-adjusted=0.03 during infection). Participants with long COVID experienced more COVID-19-like symptoms (mean 6.3, 95% CI (5.45, 7.19)) than those with short COVID (mean 4.6, 95% CI (4.14, 5.07)) (p-adjusted=0.03). Days exercised were lower in the long COVID group at all timepoints, but not statistically significantly different (figure 2). Paired t-tests found no meaningful individual-level change in mean PWI, number of symptoms or days exercised over time (online supplemental E).

In December 2022, of the 56 participants with resolved or ongoing long COVID, 23 (41%) rated their general health as ‘somewhat worse’ than before developing long COVID and seven (12%) rated their general health as ‘much worse’. Discussion

Among 217 Victorians with SARS-CoV-2 infection between September 2020 and August 2022, 26% had experienced long COVID by August 2022. Long COVID was more common among people with multiple SARS-CoV-2 infections; however, most people developed long COVID on their first infection. The long COVID group was characterised by lower average personal well-being (measured by PWI), more symptoms during the acute phase of infection and fewer days exercised, including before a SARS-CoV-2 infection. There was no lasting change in PWI observed in either group. These findings provide insight into the differences in infection and well-being profiles of people who develop long COVID, and how it continues to impact their lives.

Life satisfaction and quality of life measured after infection are lower among people with long COVID.1315 Our study found that on average, people with long COVID reported lower PWI, even before SARS-CoV-2 infection. While, to our knowledge, there are no other studies that have measured PWI before long COVID, our results are consistent with a prospective cohort study in the USA reporting that pre-infection psychological distress may be a risk factor for long COVID.32 Global umbrella7 and scoping33 reviews from 2022 also identified underlying health and severity of disease as risk factors for long COVID, alongside age, sex and ethnic background. We did not detect baseline demographic differences between the long and short COVID groups. It is unclear why in our study, the groups were similar in demographics but differed consistently in PWI. Other studies may offer insights, such as differences in health behaviours34 impacting life satisfaction.

Our outcomes were informed by our previous work25 where participants with long COVID reported reduced quality of life, less frequent exercise and mood changes since developing long COVID. However, we did not detect lasting changes in PWI, symptoms, days exercised or frequency of positive or negative emotions. Our available measures, including PWI, may not be sensitive or specific to the impacts of long COVID. Alternatively, participants with long COVID experienced such low well-being during the study period that they simply remained at a low base or they have some underlying health issue that both contributed to lower PWI and potentially put them at risk of developing long COVID. Otherwise, long COVID may not significantly impact PWI. Importantly though, using questions from the SF-36, we detected negative effects of long COVID, namely, that participants were struggling to achieve their daily goals and rated their health now as ‘worse than before developing long COVID’. To quantify the impact of long COVID, it is critical to effectively capture changes to people’s lives; our study suggests that the patient-report metric of PWI is either not affected or ill-equipped to detect changes. We must therefore find a measure that does detect meaningful changes and find a way to measure this longitudinally. Other studies used the HRQoL measure,15 and several tools35 36 have been developed by researchers to capture long COVID’s impact on quality of life, but it is unclear how these would perform longitudinally.

Strengths and limitations

One study strength was oversampling participants at increased risk of contracting COVID-19, developing severe COVID-19 or experiencing adverse consequences of COVID-19 restrictions. This allowed us to measure the impact of long COVID on vulnerable members of the community. Second, we made use of all available data collected before, during and after SARS-CoV-2 infection. These data were collected regularly and were unlikely to be subject to differential recall bias. However, some caution in the interpretation of our results is warranted. We did not control for pre-SARS-CoV-2 conditions or confounders, nor did we conduct a longitudinal analysis because we did not have a sufficiently large sample size for an adjusted model. Instead, given the relatively new emergence of long COVID, we maximised the strengths of Optimise and conducted exploratory analyses using a summary measures approach, assessing differences between individuals at comparable points in their infection timelines, thus controlling for time leading into and elapsed since infection as a confounder. Our non-parametric summary measures approach imposed fewer distributional assumptions on our outcomes than a mixed effects model would, and we conducted a comparison of means which would facilitate interpretation by our public health audience. We defined COVID as ‘long’ 1 month post infection, following the NICE definition, rather than WHO’s definition of 3 months. This is unlikely to have a major impact on our results, however, as most participants reporting long COVID had symptoms lasting more than 3 months. Long COVID status was self-reported; however, self-reported long COVID should be considered credible.37 Our study may be underpowered due to its small sample size. Therefore, true effects may not have been detected. There was insufficient data for longer follow-up duration of the sample; we followed participants up to just 3 months either side of infection. This means there may have been divergent outcomes between the groups over longer durations that we were unable to observe. We note that in our sample, the baseline PWI score was lower than the normal threshold in the general population. While we do not believe this impacted our study findings, which focused on a comparison between groups, further research could explore the clinical significance of lower than normal PWI reported during the COVID-19 pandemic. Our study was limited to participants with SARS-CoV-2 infection so that we could compare outcomes before and after infection. It was beyond the scope of the study to include persons who had not experienced SARS-CoV-2 infections. Future studies with larger sample sizes or longer follow-up duration could inspect a dose–response gradient for no infection, short COVID and long COVID. We were also unable to disentangle our outcomes on the general effects of the pandemic, which was beyond the scope of this study. Finally, Optimise was a non-representative sample of Victorians, and long COVID prevalence in our study should not be extrapolated to the general population.

Conclusion

On average, people with long COVID reported lower well-being levels in the 3 months before and after infection, suggesting people with long COVID differ in important ways from those with short COVID. While our measures detected important differences in health and well-being outcomes that may influence pre-disposition for acquiring long COVID, they detected no lasting differences over time, despite long COVID participants reporting ongoing issues. This highlights a need for measures that are sensitive to detecting the range of long COVID effects over time. Further, qualitative research could provide useful insights into the effects of long COVID on people’s lives.
