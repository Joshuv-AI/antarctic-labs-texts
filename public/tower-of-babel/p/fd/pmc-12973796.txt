The microbiome and PTSD: a scoping review across preclinical and clinical studies
Authors: Ronja Berendse, Marieke Verkleij, Joost Daams, Sian Hemmings, Ramon Lindauer, Aniko Korosi, Jasper B Zantvoord, Anja Lok
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is a psychiatric condition that substantially impairs quality of life and global health. Emerging evidence implicates that the human microbiome contributes to PTSD pathophysiology via gut–brain–immune interactions, although the underlying mechanisms and therapeutic implications remain unclear.Objective:This review aimed to systematically map the evidence linking microbiome alterations to PTSD, with a focus on mechanistic pathways, therapeutic potential, and research gaps.Methods:This scoping review was conducted in Medline, Embase, and PsychINFO from inception to 18-03-2025. Eligible studies included human participants with PTSD and preclinical rodent models employing validated PTSD paradigms. Outcomes of interest included microbiome diversity and composition, gut–brain axis biomarkers, and effects of microbiome-targeted interventions.Results:Fifty studies were included, comprising 20 human, 29 preclinical and one cross-species study. Human observational studies frequently observed reduced overall microbial diversity, along with a loss of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria, such as Ruminococcaceae and Lachnospiraceae, and an increased abundance of Veillonella, Odoribacter, and Catenibacterium linked to gut permeability and inflammation. Human intervention studies testing probiotics, prebiotics, fermented soy, and dietary fibre showed preliminary evidence for symptom and related metabolic and inflammatory marker improvements; however, microbiome effects were inconsistent. Preclinical models revealed stress-induced reductions in Bifidobacteria, Verrucomicrobia, and Parabacteroides, and increases in Coprobacillus and Anaeroplasma. Functional consequences included impaired barrier integrity, altered SCFA levels, and heightened immune activation. Preclinical interventions, particularly Mycobacterium vaccae, as well as probiotics, synbiotics, acetate, and MDMA, mitigated microbial alterations, reduced anxiety-like behaviours, and modulated neuroimmune pathways.Conclusion:Current evidence supports an association between PTSD and microbiome alterations, with convergent human and preclinical findings. However, human research remains limited by small, cross-sectional designs, which preclude causal inferences. Rigorous longitudinal and interventional studies are required to establish causality and assess microbiome-targeted therapies as adjuncts in PTSD treatment.KEYWORDS: Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD), PTSD pathophysiology, gut–brain axis, therapeutic potential, microbiome alterations, gut–brain–immune interactions

Introduction

Posttraumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) is a common and debilitating psychiatric condition that may develop after exposure to traumatic events such as combat, natural disasters, or sexual assault (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Core symptoms include intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in mood and cognition, and hyperarousal, all of which significantly impair quality of life, social functioning, and general health (Kessler et al., 2017; Koenen et al., 2017; World Health Organization, 2024). Globally, lifetime prevalence is estimated at 3.9%, rising to 5.6% among trauma-exposed individuals, underscoring its substantial public health impact (World Health Organization, 2024).

Recommended first-line treatments for individuals with PTSD are trauma-focused psychotherapies, including cognitive processing therapy (CPT), prolonged exposure (PE), and trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (TF-CBT). Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR), CBT, and narrative exposure therapy (NET) are considered conditional treatment options (American Psychiatric Association, 2025; World Health Organization, 2023). Pharmacological treatments such as Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) and Serotonin and Norepinephrine Reuptake Inhibitor (SNRIs) demonstrate only modest efficacy and are recommended as second-line approaches (American Psychiatric Association, 2025). Despite these treatments, a considerable number of patients remain symptomatic, discontinue treatment or experience relapse; a substantial proportion exhibit limited response and up to 25% relapse after initial recovery (Kline et al., 2025; Semmlinger et al., 2024). Failure to recover from PTSD can lead to significant impairments in psychosocial functioning, affecting family relationships, work performance, and overall quality of life (Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, 2014). Together, the existing evidence highlights the need for novel mechanistic frameworks and innovative therapeutic approaches.

One emerging avenue is the role of the microbiome in PTSD pathophysiology. Conceptualized as an integrated ecological system comprising microorganisms, their genetic material, and metabolites, the microbiome regulates host immunity, metabolism, and neurophysiology (Berg et al., 2020; Integrative HMPRNC, 2019). The microbiome comprises bacteria, viruses, fungi, and archaea, among others. The human body contains many distinct microbial niches, with the gut being the most extensively studied. While much of the evidence stems from research on depression (Foster & McVey Neufeld, 2013; Radjabzadeh et al., 2022), recent studies suggest links between gut microbiome alterations and PTSD (Al Jowf et al., 2023; Pan et al., 2025), mediated by immune, endocrine, and vagal pathways (Doenyas et al., 2025). For instance, a recent review reported no consistent differences in α-diversity (within-sample richness and evenness) or β-diversity (differences in community composition between samples) of the microbiome between PTSD and trauma-exposed controls, but identified altered abundances of specific taxa, including Mitsuokella, Odoribacter, and Catenibacterium, which correlated with PTSD severity (Winder et al., 2025). Yet, evidence remains fragmented and heterogeneous, highlighting the importance of synthesizing current knowledge to guide future research. Most research has focused on the gut microbiome, but emerging evidence suggests that microbial communities outside the intestine may also play a role in mental health. Other microbial niches, including oral, skin, urogenital, and respiratory microbiomes, are increasingly recognized as modulators of mental health. For instance, oral microbial variation has been linked to depression (Wingfield et al., 2021), and skin microbial composition has been associated with psychological wellbeing (Tyson-Carr et al., 2025). This broader perspective highlights the need to extend PTSD research beyond gut-focused investigations.

This scoping review synthesizes human and preclinical studies on the microbiome-PTSD relationship, exploring potential mechanisms, therapeutic implications, and research gaps. By extending beyond gut-focused research and including observational and intervention studies across microbial niches and species, it aims to provide a translational perspective on this emerging field.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This scoping review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines. The protocol for this scoping review was preregistered on DataverseNL on 13 March 2024 (Berendse et al., 2024). The authors report there are no competing interests to declare.

Eligibility criteria

This review included both human and preclinical research that focused on the relationship between the microbiome and PTSD, considering all potential microbial niches (e.g. gut, oral, respiratory, urogenital skin), with the aim of identifying those niches for which research is currently available. For human studies, inclusion required the use of standardized diagnostic criteria based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-III, DSM-IV, or DSM-5) (American Psychiatric Association, 1987, 2000, 2022), either through clinician-administered interviews or validated questionnaires. Rodent models were included if they used validated paradigms known to replicate core PTSD-related phenotypes (e.g. hyperarousal, avoidance, intrusive memory), based on the framework established by Verbitsky et al. (2020). Eligible models included the single prolonged stress paradigm (Lisieski et al., 2018; Souza et al., 2017), social defeat stress (Golden et al., 2015; Hammels et al., 2015), predator stress (Bakshi et al., 2012; Zoladz et al., 2012), electric shock stress (Bali & Jaggi, 2015; Siegmund & Wotjak, 2007), double-hit models (Daskalakis & Yehuda, 2014), and paradigms incorporating fear-potentiated startle (Noble et al., 2017), auditory-cued conditioning (Curzon et al., 2009; Phillips & Ledoux, 1992), juvenile social exploration (Cohen et al., 2007), and restraint stress (Buynitsky & Mostofsky, 2009). These models were selected for their demonstrated face, construct, and predictive validity against PTSD DSM-5 criteria and translational benchmarks (Deslauriers et al., 2018; Verbitsky et al., 2020).

All original research articles, regardless of study design, were included to capture a broad spectrum of evidence on the microbiome-PTSD relationship. Such studies comprised randomized controlled trials (RCT), quasi-experimental trials, cross-sectional studies, case-control studies and cohort studies. Reviews and meta-analyses were excluded. No restrictions were imposed based on the context in which participants were recruited, including inpatient or outpatient settings.

Controlled studies

Studies comparing the microbiome of participants with PTSD to those without PTSD (either trauma exposed or non-exposed controls) were included. The presence of comorbidities was accepted if the studies clearly distinguished the PTSD group from the non-PTSD group.

Outcomes

The following measures were included as outcomes: microbiome composition and diversity measures (e.g. α-and β-diversity, relative abundance of bacterial taxa, functional analysis) across microbial sites (e.g. gut, oral, respiratory, urogenital, skin); gut–brain axis measures (e.g. cytokine levels, neurotransmitter levels, or other markers of gut–brain axis function) and intervention-related measures, e.g. the impact of interventions on the microbiome or the effects of microbiome modifications on PTSD outcomes using validated PTSD scales (e.g. Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale (CAPS-5), PTSD Checklist (PCL-5) (Weathers et al., 2013a, 2013b)), or behavioural assessments in rodents.

Literature search and study selection

In collaboration with a medical librarian (J.D.), systematic searches were conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, and PsycINFO. An initial search was conducted in 2023 and updated in March 2024 and March 2025 using the same core strategy. Each update replaced the previous search, and the final version is reported in full in the Supplementary Materials. The final search covered all databases from inception to 18 March 2025. Search terms were related to PTSD and the microbiome for each of the databases. A full account of our search strategy is included in the supplementary materials (Supplementary File S1, S2 and Figure 1). Results were exported to Rayyan for screening (Ouzzani et al., 2016). In addition to the search results, recontextualize reference lists of relevant studies were examined to identify additional eligible studies for inclusion; this process did not yield any additional studies. Two reviewers (R.B. and J.Z.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved studies. Full-text articles were retrieved for potentially eligible studies and the same reviewers independently assessed them for inclusion, using pre-defined eligibility criteria. Any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or after consultation with a third reviewer. The review protocol was preregistered on DataverseNL (Berendse et al., 2024) and is also available as Supplementary File S3.

Data extraction, charting process and reporting results

Data extraction was performed using standardized data extraction forms to collect the following information from each included study: study characteristics (e.g. author, year, country), study methodology (e.g. study design, study aims), participant characteristics (e.g. sample size, human/rodent, age, gender), microbiome-related measures (e.g. microbial niche (for example faecal or saliva)), sequencing methodologies (16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing, metagenomic sequencing, culturing), PTSD-related measures (e.g. diagnostic criteria, PTSD interview/questionnaires, rodent model), statistical analyses used and main findings related to the relationship between the microbiome and PTSD. One reviewer (R.B.) performed initial data extraction, and the second reviewer (J.Z.) subsequently extracted data from a randomly selected sample (20%) of the articles to ensure accuracy and consistency. A narrative synthesis approach was used to summarize the findings of the included studies and to highlight inconsistencies or gaps in the literature. In line with scoping review methodology, no risk of bias assessment was performed.

The generative AI tool ChatGPT, version 4, was used to assist with writing and revising text. All content generated by the tool was reviewed and modified by the authors for accuracy and scientific integrity of the manuscript.

Results

Search outcomes

A total of 50 studies met the eligibility criteria, including 20 human studies, 29 preclinical studies and one cross-species study (Figure 1). To facilitate clarity, we grouped these studies into four categories: (American Psychiatric Association, 2022) Observational studies in humans with PTSD (Koenen et al., 2017) Microbiome-based intervention studies in humans with PTSD (Kessler et al., 2017) Observational studies in preclinical PTSD models, and (World Health Organization, 2024) Intervention studies in preclinical PTSD models. Human studies

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the eligible studies (n = 20) published between 2012 and 2025, with the inclusion of one cross-species study. Because of its dual design, the cross-species study is listed in both the human and pre-clinical tables. Individual study sample sizes ranged from n = 10 to n = 470, with a total of 1640 participants. This total excludes participants from the two Mendelian randomization (MR) studies, which were based on statistics from large-scale genome-wide association datasets (PGC-PTSD and FinnGen) rather than direct participant recruitment (He et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023). Due to the overlap in source cohorts and the lack of individual-level data, these MR-studies are presented separately and were not included in the aggregated sample size count.

Most studies involved adult participants, except for one longitudinal cross-species study, which assessed children exposed to trauma, with microbiome sampling extending into late adolescence (Yirmiya et al., 2024). Study designs varied, ranging from cross-sectional (Alex et al., 2024; Bajaj et al., 2019; Geier et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017; Li et al., 2025; Malan-Müller et al., 2022, 2024; O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Yoo et al., 2023, 2025), longitudinal cohorts (Gocan et al., 2012; Herbert et al., 2023; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; Voigt et al., 2022; Zeamer et al., 2023) to RCTs (Brenner et al., 2020; Voigt et al., 2025), Mendelian randomization studies (He et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023), and one single-case experimental design (Yirmiya et al., 2024). Control groups differed across studies: seven studies used trauma-exposed controls of which two of which also used non-trauma-exposed controls, two used non-trauma-exposed controls only, two used population-based controls, one relied on within-group comparisons, and seven did not include a control group. Participant composition also varied: several studies included only men (often military) (Bajaj et al., 2019; Brenner et al., 2020; Geier et al., 2024; Gocan et al., 2012; Herbert et al., 2023; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; Li et al., 2025; Yoo et al., 2023, 2025), one only included (pregnant) women (Alex et al., 2024), and others mixed-sex samples with female representation ranging from 45% to 80% (Hemmings et al., 2017; Malan-Müller et al., 2022, 2024; O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Voigt et al., 2022, 2025; Yirmiya et al., 2024; Zeamer et al., 2023). Ethnic backgrounds were reported inconsistently; if available, most participants identified as White or ‘Caucasian’, followed by Black or ‘African American’, ‘Asian’, ‘Hispanic/Latino’, ‘Alaskan Native’ and ‘Multiracial identities’. Trauma types spanned combat exposure, assault, intimate partner violence, childhood adversity, occupational trauma, and injury-related trauma. Reported comorbidities included depression, anxiety, mTBI, chronic pain, sleep disturbances, substance use disorders, and liver disease (Table 1).

Microbiome and posttraumatic stress symptoms assessment

PTSD was assessed using validated clinical instruments, most frequently the CAPS-5 (Brenner et al., 2020; Hemmings et al., 2017; Malan-Müller et al., 2022; O'Hare et al., 2025a) and the PCL-5 (Alex et al., 2024; Geier et al., 2024; Herbert et al., 2023; Li et al., 2025; Malan-Müller et al., 2024; O'Hare et al., 2025b; Voigt et al., 2022, 2025; Zeamer et al., 2023). Most studies assessed the microbiome through 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing of stool samples, with two using saliva samples (Alex et al., 2024; Malan-Müller et al., 2024) or alternative approaches such as serum metabolite profiling (Voigt et al., 2022). Mendelian randomization studies relied on GWAS summary statistics rather than direct sequencing (He et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023). Reported outcomes commonly included microbial community structure, diversity measures, and relative abundances of microbial taxa, with some studies also addressing functional markers related to gut health (e.g. intestinal permeability, systemic inflammation), see Table 2.

Associations between microbiome and PTSD (observational studies)

Overall, findings from the observational studies of the microbiome in humans with PTSD are mixed (Table 2). Several studies reported significant reductions in α-diversity in individuals with PTSD compared to controls (Bajaj et al., 2019; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; Yirmiya et al., 2024), while others did not find significant differences (Alex et al., 2024; Geier et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017; Li et al., 2025; Malan-Müller et al., 2022, 2024; O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Yoo et al., 2023, 2025; Zeamer et al., 2023). β-diversity differences were also inconsistent; some studies reported significant alterations (Alex et al., 2024; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; Malan-Müller et al., 2024; Yirmiya et al., 2024), whilst others did not (Bajaj et al., 2019; Geier et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017; Li et al., 2025; Malan-Müller et al., 2022; O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Zeamer et al., 2023).

At the taxonomic level, several studies reported reductions in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing genera such as Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Faecalibacterium (Bajaj et al., 2019; Malan-Müller et al., 2022; Yirmiya et al., 2024; Zeamer et al., 2023). Other reported microbial shifts associated with PTSD included increased Veillonella and reduced Dialister (Yirmiya et al., 2024), higher levels of Catenibacterium, Collinsella, and Holdemanella (O'Hare et al., 2025b), and higher summed relative abundance of four genera (Mitsuokella, Odoribacter, Catenibacterium and Olsenella) (O'Hare et al., 2025a). Broader phylum-level changes included associations between PTSD and decreased relative abundance of Actinobacteria, Lentisphaerae, and Verrucomicrobia (Geier et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017), along with reduced Haemophilus sputorum and increased Prevotella histicola (Malan-Müller et al., 2024). Other findings included increased relative abundance of Eikenella and Firmicutes in participants with increased PTSD symptom severity (Alex et al., 2024), reduced relative abundances of Noxia, Haemophilus sp., Prevotella sp., and Campylobacter sp. (Levert-Levitt et al., 2022), and reduced Actinobacteriota (Geier et al., 2024). In occupational cohorts, firefighters showed higher relative abundance of Negativibacillus and Parabacteroides distasonis and lower relative abundance of Bifidobacterium bifidum and Anaerostipes hadrus compared to matched non-firefighter controls (Yoo et al., 2023). In a larger firefighter sample, PTSD symptom status was associated with higher Veillonella torques and lower Alistipes putredinis abundance (Yoo et al., 2025). Finally, Voigt et al. reported decreased levels of gut-derived SCFA metabolites, including acetate, lactate, and succinate, in individuals who met PTSD criteria on the PCL-5 compared with trauma-exposed controls (Voigt et al., 2022). These findings suggest PTSD is associated with shifts in SCFA-producing and other gut microbial taxa, despite inconsistent diversity results.

Associations between microbial taxa and PTSD symptom severity or subdomains were reported across multiple studies (see Table 2). PTSD was associated with a decreased relative abundance of Actinobacteria, Lentisphaerae, and Verrucomicrobia, which were linked to increased PTSD symptom severity (Hemmings et al., 2017). Saliva-based microbiota were associated with hyperarousal and re-experiencing symptoms (Levert-Levitt et al., 2022), several genera showed inverse associations with distinct PTSD symptom clusters (Geier et al., 2024), and specific taxa, including Flavonifractor plautii, Ruminococcus gnavus and Bifidobacterium species, were predictors of worse PTSD symptom severity (Zeamer et al., 2023). The summed relative abundance of Mitsuokella, Odoribacter, Catenibacterium and Olsenella was higher in individuals diagnosed with PTSD and correlated positively with CAPS-5 scores (O'Hare et al., 2025a), and microbial profiles similarly differed in pregnant women, with distinct patterns observed in those reporting higher stress-related symptom levels compared to women with lower symptom levels (Alex et al., 2024). Beyond symptom associations, Zeamer et al. showed that PTSD-related microbial features were linked to altered functional connectivity in frontal and parietal brain regions and to functional metagenomic pathways, particularly those related to L-arginine metabolism (Zeamer et al., 2023). Community composition was also linked to immune-related functional categories (Hemmings et al., 2017), and PTSD participants exhibited lower gut-derived SCFA metabolites together with elevated markers of intestinal permeability and inflammation (Voigt et al., 2022). Cross-species evidence suggested reduced mother-child microbial synchrony and found that faecal microbiota transplantation from PTSD-affected adolescents induced anxiety-like behaviour in germ-free mice (rodents raised in sterile conditions without any resident microbiome) (Yirmiya et al., 2024). Overall, the diversity of observed associations highlights the growing evidence base connecting microbiome alterations to PTSD symptoms while also underscoring the need for further studies to establish consistency and further clarification of the potentially multifaceted relationship between the microbiome and PTSD across symptom domains and biological pathways.

Mendelian randomization studies

Two Mendelian randomization studies (He et al., 2024; Xiao et al., 2023) investigated the relationship between gut microbial taxa and PTSD using summary-level GWAS data from the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium for PTSD (PGC-PTSD, Freeze 2; n = 23,212 PTSD cases and 151,447 controls) and the FinnGen consortium (n = 2,100 PTSD cases and 307,558 controls). PTSD diagnosis in each study was based on structured clinical interviews or medical records consistent with DSM-III, DSM-IV, or DSM-5 (American Psychiatric Association, 1987, 2000, 2022). Microbiome metrics, i.e. relative abundances of bacterial taxa from phylum to genus level, derived mainly from the MiBioGen consortium (n ≈ 18,340), included relative abundance of bacterial taxa from phylum to genus level. Across studies, Dorea and Sellimonas were identified as potentially protective, while Phascolarctobacterium and members of the Ruminococcaceae family were associated with increased PTSD risk.

Interventions

Table 3 summarizes four human studies testing microbiome-targeted interventions.

Three of these studies examined single nutritional approaches: Lactobacillus reuteri DSM 17938 supplementation (Brenner et al., 2020), a fermented soy product (FSWW08) (Gocan et al., 2012), and a prebiotic fibre supplement administered alongside psychological treatment (Voigt et al., 2025). One study investigated a high-fibre plant-based diet (Herbert et al., 2023).

All studies assessed microbiome- or health-related outcomes before and after the intervention.

In a randomized controlled trial, no significant differences were observed between the probiotic (L. reuteri supplementation) and placebo groups in inflammatory markers, intestinal permeability, or gut microbiome diversity and composition (Brenner et al., 2020). Although the probiotic group showed a trend towards reduced plasma CRP-levels, this effect did not reach statistical significance. PTSD severity was not associated with alterations in specific microbial taxa (Brenner et al., 2020). Daily FSWW08 intake was associated with marked reduction in PTSD symptoms alongside reduced sleep disturbance, fatigue, loss of appetite, and gastrointestinal complaints, and all participants met criteria for clinical response (defined as >30% reduction in CAPS total score) (Gocan et al., 2012). Voigt et al. reported no significant changes in microbiome α- or β-diversity, and PTSD severity was associated with higher serum levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and lipopolysaccharide-binding protein (LBP) and lower levels of circulating SCFA metabolites (Voigt et al., 2025). Herbert et al. found that the high-fibre plant-based diet was feasible and well tolerated, with participants reporting apparent reductions in PTSD symptoms (PCL-5), depressive symptoms (PHQ-9), and pain interference, although these changes were not statistically tested (Herbert et al., 2023).

Pre-clinical studies

Study characteristics

Tables 4 and 5 summarize study characteristics of 29 included pre-clinical studies and one cross-species study; comprising 15 observational studies and 15 intervention studies. The cross-species study with a dual design is also included in the human study table. Most studies used C57BL/6J mice (n = 6) (Baskin et al., 2023; Gautam et al., 2018; Hoke et al., 2022; Laudani et al., 2023; Otaru et al., 2024; Yadav et al., 2023) or Sprague-Dawley rats (n = 9) (Fox et al., 2017; Khan et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2020; Pearson-Leary et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2024; Takajo et al., 2019; Tanelian et al., 2022, 2023a, 2024); some included female or mixed-sex cohorts (Baskin et al., 2023; Mazzari et al., 2023; Otaru et al., 2024; Tanelian et al., 2023a, 2023b, 2024). Control rodents were typically home-cage housed, and some studies stratified trauma-exposed animals as resilient vs. susceptible phenotypes (Laudani et al., 2023; Pascual Cuadrado et al., 2022; Pearson-Leary et al., 2020).

Microbiome assessment and PTSD models

Common paradigms included the single prolonged stress (SPS) paradigm, social defeat stress (SDS), maternal separation and unpredictable stress (MSUS), blast exposure, a double-hit model combining sleep disruption and social defeat stress, and fear conditioning/extinction. Behavioural assessments targeted multiple aspects of PTSD-like behaviour, including anxiety-like behaviour, anhedonia, social interaction, fear learning, and cognitive function, and many studies employed multiple tests such as the Elevated Plus Maze (EPM) (Pellow et al., 1985), Open Field Test (OFT) (Seibenhener & Wooten, 2015) and Social Interaction Test (SIT) (File & Hyde, 1978).

Associations between microbiome and PTSD (observational studies)

In most studies, no changes in α-diversity were observed after stress exposure (Baskin et al., 2023; Gautam et al., 2018; Laudani et al., 2023; Otaru et al., 2024; Pascual Cuadrado et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024; Takajo et al., 2019; Tanelian et al., 2023a; Yirmiya et al., 2024). In some studies, rodents exposed to social defeat stress or single prolonged stress either showed reduced α-diversity (Hoke et al., 2022; Tanelian et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023) compared to unstressed rodents, or increased α-diversity was reported (Pearson-Leary et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020). One study reported reduced α-diversity in males compared to females, both at baseline and following stress exposure (Tanelian et al., 2024). Significant β-diversity shifts were observed in all studies except Tanelian et al. (2023a), which showed convergence of microbial communities after stress exposure, whereas the other studies reported divergent patterns with increased separation between stress and control groups. Reductions in the relative abundance of Bifidobacteria (Baskin et al., 2023; Tanelian et al., 2023a), Verrucomicrobia (Gautam et al., 2018; Hoke et al., 2022; Laudani et al., 2023), and Parabacteroides (Shen et al., 2024) were reported in rodents exposed to social defeat stress, single prolonged stress (SPS), or repetitive blast injury, typically in comparison to unstressed controls. In studies that stratified rodents based on behavioural outcomes, these reductions were most pronounced in stress-susceptible subgroups relative to stress-resilient or control rodents (Laudani et al., 2023; Shen et al., 2024; Tanelian et al., 2023a). Lactobacillus was increased in resilient groups (Gautam et al., 2018; Tanelian et al., 2022, 2024), i.e. in SPS-exposed animals that did not develop PTSD-like behaviours following trauma exposure. Susceptible animals, i.e. in SPS-exposed rodents displaying PTSD-like behaviours, showed increases in Coprobacillus, Anaeroplasma, and Anaerovorax (Tanelian et al., 2022, 2023a) as well as Clostridium ramosum (Yirmiya et al., 2024) and Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio alterations (Gautam et al., 2018; Hoke et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2020). Functional alterations in stressed rodents included changes in gut metabolic modules (Laudani et al., 2023), reduced acetate (Tanelian et al., 2022), increased branched-chain fatty acids (Tanelian et al., 2023a, 2024), and markers of intestinal/gut barrier dysfunction and inflammation (Baskin et al., 2023; Hoke et al., 2022; Tanelian et al., 2022, 2023a, 2024; Yadav et al., 2023).

Intervention studies

The majority of intervention studies (n = 10) investigated subcutaneous or intranasal Mycobacterium vaccae immunization of rodents before or during stress exposure (Amoroso et al., 2019, 2020; Bowers et al., 2021; Fox et al., 2017; Hassell et al., 2019, 2023; Loupy et al., 2019, 2020; Mazzari et al., 2023; Reber et al., 2016a). Other interventions included oral probiotics or synbiotics (Akinluyi et al., 2023; Khan et al., 2025), Lactobacillus rhamnosus JB-1 compared with sertraline (Liu et al., 2020), pharmacological treatment with MDMA (Qu et al., 2023), and oral supplementation with sodium acetate (Tanelian et al., 2023b). Across all studies, intervention effects were assessed relative to unstressed home-cage or single-housed controls as well as stressed vehicle- or saline-treated controls. See Table 5 for details on the intervention studies in pre-clinical PTSD models.

Microbiome physiological outcomes

In a model of SPS combined with chronic alcohol exposure, probiotic treatment consisting of an 11-strain Lactobacillus-based mixture, and a synbiotic combining this probiotic formulation with inulin, restored SCFA levels and improved intestinal barrier integrity. Synbiotic treatment produced a stronger upregulation of occludin expression than probiotics alone or untreated rats (Akinluyi et al., 2023), occludin being a key tight-junction protein essential for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. In adult male C57BL/6NCrlmice exposed to chronic subordinate colony housing, intranasal Mycobacterium vaccae mitigated stress-induced colonic inflammation (Amoroso et al., 2020). In SPS-treated rodents, probiotic treatment, consisting of a four-strain formulation containing Streptococcus faecalis, Clostridium butyricum, Bacillus mesentericus, and Lactobacillus sporogenes, increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidota, reduced Proteobacteria, and improved gut integrity by decreasing intestinal permeability and restoring villus height and crypt depth (Khan et al., 2025). In chronic social defeat (CSD) stress, MDMA did not alter α-diversity but restored β-diversity and prevented the stress-induced increase of Oscillibacter (Qu et al., 2023). In a study employing chronic subordinate colony housing (CSC), M. vaccae immunization resulted in increased α-diversity and decreased β-diversity, stabilized gut microbial composition without preventing CSC-induced increases in Helicobacter, and was associated with increased relative abundances of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, reduced colonic inflammation, and enhanced regulatory T-cell activity (Reber et al., 2016b).

Anxiety and related behavioural outcomes

Across studies, microbiota-targeted interventions modulated anxiety- and stress-related behaviours compared with both stressed vehicle-treated and unstressed controls. Across CSC and related paradigms, subcutaneous or intranasal M. vaccae resulted in reductions in certain anxiety-related or defensive behaviours and, in the CSC model, increased proactive coping, while effects on social anxiety and social preference were inconsistent (Amoroso et al., 2019, 2020; Reber et al., 2016b). In a double-hit model, proactive coping was also increased, while no consistent effects on social anxiety or social preference were reported (Bowers et al., 2021). In studies employing SPS, probiotics and acetate supplementation decreased anxiety-like behaviour and attenuated social impairments (Khan et al., 2025; Tanelian et al., 2023b). Early-life adversity models confirmed that M. vaccae reduced anxiety and improved social interactions (Mazzari et al., 2023). In CSD models, MDMA promoted stress resilience across all stressed rodents, whereas Lactobacillus rhamnosus JB-1 and sertraline administration increased aggressor avoidance and social deficits in stress-susceptible mice, while having no measurable effects on classical anxiety-like behaviour, as assessed in tests such as the EPM and OFT (Liu et al., 2020; Qu et al., 2023). In both the fear-potentiated startle and inescapable shock paradigms, M. vaccae administration reduced anxiety-related behaviours in stressed rats. In the FPS paradigm specifically, these effects were linked to changes in CRH regulation (Loupy et al., 2019, 2020).

Fear learning and extinction

In FPS paradigms, subcutaneous M. vaccae enhanced between- and within-session fear extinction, while having no effect on baseline startle responses (Fox et al., 2017; Loupy et al., 2019). In a similar FPS study employing the same fear-potentiated startle paradigm, M. vaccae enhanced extinction without affecting fear acquisition (Hassell et al., 2019). In an auditory-cued paradigm, M. vaccae prevented stress-induced resistance to extinction and reduced generalized fear responses, with no effect on fear acquisition (Hassell et al., 2023).

Molecular and neurobiological outcomes of microbiota-targeted interventions

M. vaccae modulated stress-related pathways by altering serotonergic signalling, including reducing tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (tph2) expression in the median raphe, increasing 5-HT1A receptor (htr1a) expression in the dorsal raphe, and decreasing organic cation transporter 3 (slc22a3) expression in stress-related raphe subregions (Fox et al., 2017), although no long-term serotonergic gene expression changes were observed (Hassell et al., 2019). M. vaccae reduced CRH mRNA expression in the central amygdala and bed nucleus of the stria terminalis, while CRH levels were correlated with slc22a3 expression in the dorsal raphe ventrolateral region (Loupy et al., 2019). Intranasal administration of M. vaccae has been reported to increase regulatory T-cell activity, suppress stress-induced immune activation, and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α (Amoroso et al., 2020). M. vaccae completely prevented CSC-induced increases in interferon-gamma, reduced body weight loss, mitigated colonic damage (Amoroso et al., 2019), normalized hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis functioning, reduced microglial priming, increased hippocampal interleukin-10 and enhanced serotonergic metabolism (Reber et al., 2016a). Subcutaneous administration of M. vaccae also prevented stress-induced increases in interleukin-6 (IL-6) expression, preserved microglial regulatory signalling, and modulated hepatic immune-metabolic pathways (Loupy et al., 2020). Following early-life adversity, M. vaccae administration reduced glucocorticoid resistance in splenic immune cells and increased splenic myeloid cells in males. In females, it reduced neutrophil infiltration and lowered levels of interleukin-4 (IL-4), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF; Mazzari et al., 2023). Transplantation of microbiota from stress-susceptible rats to naïve, non-stressed recipients was associated with increased corticosterone, elevated neuroinflammatory markers, and increased blood–brain barrier permeability, whereas these effects were absent in rats colonized with microbiota from stress-resilient donors (Pearson-Leary et al., 2020).

Probiotic treatment restored brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels in the cortex of SPS-exposed mice (Khan et al., 2025). Lactobacillus rhamnosus JB-1 and sertraline reduced hippocampal corticotropin-releasing hormone receptor 1 (CRHR-1), mineralocorticoid receptor (MR), and glucocorticoid receptor (GR) expression, and lowered levels of Ly6Chi monocytes and the chemokine CCL-2 (Liu et al., 2020). Acetate supplementation reduced urinary epinephrine responses, decreased interleukin-1β (IL-1β) mRNA and microglial activation in the ventral hippocampus, increased histone acetyltransferase 1 (HAT1), decreased histone deacetylase 2 (HDAC2), and elevated serum β-hydroxybutyrate (Tanelian et al., 2023b). M. vaccae administration improved sleep regulation in double-hit, rodent model by normalizing rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, preventing fragmentation, reducing cortical hyperarousal, and restoring non-REM δ-rebound (Bowers et al., 2021). In CSD stress, MDMA prevented splenomegaly and reduced IL-6 levels (Qu et al., 2023).

Together, these findings demonstrate broad molecular and neurobiological effects of microbiome interventions, but heterogeneity in models, outcomes, and interventions underscores the need for systematic mapping and further mechanistic studies.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesized evidence examining the relationship between the microbiome and PTSD across 50 studies, including 20 human studies, 29 preclinical animal studies, and one cross-species study. To our knowledge, the current review represents the most comprehensive mapping of the field to date, extending prior narrative reviews by systematically describing observational and interventional studies, covering multiple microbial niches, and integrating cross-species evidence. Overall, the findings suggest a possible bidirectional interaction between PTSD and microbiome dynamics, yet they also reveal substantial heterogeneity and key research gaps that must be addressed before translation into clinical applications can be considered.

Across both human and animal studies, microbial diversity emerged as a frequently investigated, but inconsistently replicated, outcome. Several human studies demonstrated reduced α-diversity in PTSD, consistent with literature linking lower microbial richness to psychiatric vulnerability; however, findings were mixed: some studies reported reduced α-diversity (Bajaj et al., 2019; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; Yirmiya et al., 2024), whereas others found no significant differences relative to trauma-exposed or healthy controls (Alex et al., 2024; Geier et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017; Li et al., 2025; Malan-Müller et al., 2022, 2024; O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Yoo et al., 2023, 2025; Zeamer et al., 2023). Preclinical models revealed similar inconsistencies, with reports of reduced α-diversity (Hoke et al., 2022; Tanelian et al., 2022, 2024; Yadav et al., 2023), no change in α-diversity (Baskin et al., 2023; Gautam et al., 2018; Laudani et al., 2023; Otaru et al., 2024; Pascual Cuadrado et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024; Takajo et al., 2019; Tanelian et al., 2023a; Yirmiya et al., 2024) and even increased α-diversity (Pearson-Leary et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020), following stress paradigms. Regarding β-diversity, human studies also showed inconsistent findings, with some reporting significant alterations (Alex et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; Malan-Müller et al., 2024; Yirmiya et al., 2024), whilst others did not (Bajaj et al., 2019; Geier et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025; Malan-Müller et al., 2022; O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Zeamer et al., 2023). In contrast, significant β-diversity shifts were observed in all animal models except one (Takajo et al., 2019), suggesting that trauma may more robustly alter community structure in controlled preclinical settings than in human cohorts.

In human studies, converging findings at the taxonomic level have indicated reductions in SCFA-producing taxa such as Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and Faecalibacterium (Bajaj et al., 2019; Malan-Müller et al., 2022; Yirmiya et al., 2024; Zeamer et al., 2023), along with increases in genera including Veillonella, Odoribacter, and Catenibacterium (O'Hare et al., 2025a, 2025b; Yirmiya et al., 2024). Several studies have identified specific microbial taxa that are linked to PTSD symptom severity and distinct symptom clusters. For instance, saliva-based microbiota were found to be associated with hyperarousal and re-experiencing symptoms (Levert-Levitt et al., 2022). Specific taxa, such as Flavonifractor plautii, Ruminococcus gnavus and Bifidobacterium species, were identified as predictors of worse PTSD symptom severity (Zeamer et al., 2023), with Flavonifractor plautii and Ruminococcus gnavus linked to negative mood and cognition, while Bifidobacterium species were associated with hyperarousal (Zeamer et al., 2023).

Moreover, cluster-specific microbial profiles were described across several studies, with certain taxa linked to symptom severity in individuals with PTSD (Alex et al., 2024; Geier et al., 2024; Hemmings et al., 2017; Levert-Levitt et al., 2022; O'Hare et al., 2025a; Zeamer et al., 2023). Collectively, these compositional patterns suggest the presence of a pro-inflammatory microbial profile, which may contribute to immune dysregulation and the pathophysiology of PTSD symptoms.

Several studies have provided mechanistic evidence connecting microbiome alterations to PTSD-relevant biological pathways. In humans, lower gut-derived SCFA metabolites together with markers of increased intestinal permeability and inflammation were reported in PTSD (Voigt et al., 2022), and PTSD severity was associated with elevated systemic inflammatory markers (e.g. LPS, LBP) and lower SCFA metabolites in an intervention cohort (Voigt et al., 2025), supporting the concept of microbiome-driven neuroinflammation as a contributor to PTSD pathophysiology. Preclinical studies extended these findings by demonstrating causality: faecal microbiota transplantation from stress-susceptible donors induced anxiety- and depression-like behaviours in experimentally naïve, non-stressed recipient rodents, accompanied by neuroinflammatory changes and increased blood–brain barrier permeability (Pearson-Leary et al., 2020), and FMT from human adolescents with PTSD also induced anxiety-like behaviour in germ-free mice (Yirmiya et al., 2024). Furthermore, interventions, such as pro- and synbiotics, that restored SCFA levels or improved intestinal barrier integrity attenuated anxiety-like behaviours and normalized immune responses. For instance, the synbiotic used by Akinluyi et al. (2023), a Lactobacillus-dominant probiotic mixture combined with inulin, restored SCFAs and upregulated occludin expression more strongly than probiotics alone. Similarly, the probiotic formulation used by Khan et al. (2025), containing Streptococcus faecalis, Clostridium butyricum, Bacillus mesentericus, and Lactobacillus sporongenes, increased Bacteroidota abundance, improved intestinal permeability, and reduced PTSD-related symptoms. In addition, acetate supplementation reduced neuroinflammation and modulated epigenetic markers (Tanelian et al., 2023b).

Mechanistic contributions also derive from immune and serotonergic modulation induced by M. vaccae (Amoroso et al., 2019, 2020; Bowers et al., 2021; Fox et al., 2017; Hassell et al., 2019; Loupy et al., 2019, 2020; Mazzari et al., 2023; Reber et al., 2016a). Together, these results strengthen the hypothesis that microbial metabolites and immune signalling mediate gut–brain communication in PTSD.

Interventional studies suggest some therapeutic potential of microbiome modulation, though findings remain very preliminary. In humans, trials of probiotics, prebiotics, fermented foods, and dietary fibre have suggested symptomatic improvements (Gocan et al., 2012; Herbert et al., 2023). However, in the studies that included microbiome sequencing, the interventions did not reliably alter gut microbiome composition, with changes being minimal or inconsistent (Brenner et al., 2020; Voigt et al., 2025). Findings from preclinical intervention studies were more consistent, with M. vaccae immunization, administered intranasally or subcutaneously, consistently enhancing stress resilience, reducing anxiety-like behaviours, improving extinction, and modulating immune-serotonergic pathways across multiple paradigms (Amoroso et al., 2019, 2020; Bowers et al., 2021; Fox et al., 2017; Hassell et al., 2019; Loupy et al., 2019, 2020; Mazzari et al., 2023; Reber et al., 2016a). Other interventions such as oral probiotics and synbiotics (Akinluyi et al., 2023; Khan et al., 2025) and acetate supplementation (Tanelian et al., 2023b), showed partially overlapping effects by improving gut integrity, reducing stress-related behavioural impairments, and attenuating neuroinflammatory or sympathetic responses. MDMA has been shown to restore β-diversity and promote resilience to the behavioural and inflammatory consequences of CSD stress (Qu et al., 2023). Lactobacillus rhamnosus JB-1, compared to sertraline, exhibits immune and HPA axis effects (Liu et al., 2020). At this stage, it is important to emphasize that the translational gaps remain substantial, with human evidence limited to small, heterogeneous pilot studies, and current evidence does not yet support the clinical application of microbiome-targeted strategies as standalone treatments or as a complement to existing evidence-based psychotherapies and pharmacological treatments.

Several limitations of the existing literature warrant careful consideration. First, human studies included in this review were generally small-scale (n = 10-470), cross-sectional, and demographically narrow (often white, male, veteran populations), restricting generalizability. Cross-sectional designs preclude causal inference, leaving open whether microbiome alterations precede PTSD onset, result from (chronic) PTSD symptoms, or reflect bidirectional interactions. Second, confounding factors such as diet, medication, comorbidities, and lifestyle were inconsistently controlled, despite their known influence on the microbiome. Third, heterogeneity in diagnostic instruments (e.g. CAPS vs. PCL-5) used across studies, microbiome sequencing approaches (16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing vs. shotgun metagenomics), and bioinformatics pipelines further complicates comparability. Preclinical studies, while providing mechanistic insights, varied widely in animal strains, sex, stress paradigms, and behavioural assays, limiting reproducibility and translation to human PTSD. Finally, research beyond the gut microbiome remains sparse. Early evidence implicating the oral and potentially skin microbiota in psychiatric vulnerability highlights the need for a broader, multisite perspective (Tyson-Carr et al., 2025; Wingfield et al., 2021).

To advance the field, research should move towards longitudinal, large-scale human studies that can map temporal and potentially causal relationships between microbiome alterations and PTSD trajectories. Standardized methods in microbiome sequencing, clinical phenotyping, covariate handling, and reporting are needed to enable cross-study comparability and data synthesis. Cohorts should be diverse across sex, age, ancestry, and culture, and should account for dietary and environmental variability. Mapping gut–brain–immune interactions in vivo will possibly benefit from integrating diverse analytical approaches, such as microbiome profiling, metabolomics, transcriptomics, and neuroimaging. Clinical translation requires rigorous RCTs of microbiome-targeted interventions, designed with adequate sample sizes, placebo controls, and clinically meaningful endpoints. Finally, work should expand beyond the gut, incorporating oral, skin, urogenital, and respiratory microbiomes as potential modulators of systemic physiology and psychiatric risk.

Conclusion

This review highlights preliminary evidence linking PTSD and the microbiome, with convergent findings on altered microbial composition, reduced SCFA production, and disrupted immune function as potential mechanistic pathways. Preclinical interventions indicate that microbiome modulation may hold therapeutic promise, yet translation into clinical practice requires larger, rigorous human clinical trials. The complexity of PTSD as a multisystem disorder underscores the importance of ecological, integrative approaches that account for microbiome-immune-brain interactions across diverse microbial niches. Addressing current methodological limitations and expanding translational research efforts will be critical to determine whether microbiome research can yield novel diagnostic biomarkers and (adjunctive) therapies for PTSD.
