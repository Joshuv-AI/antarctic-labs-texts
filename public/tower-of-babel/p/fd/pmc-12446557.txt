Anti-amyloid therapies in the Brazilian public health system: challenges and barriers
Authors: Raphael Machado Castilhos, Elisa Resende, Adalberto Studart Neto
Published: 2025

The recent approval of donanemab by Brazil's National Health Surveillance Agency (Agência Nacional de Vigilância Sanitária - Anvisa) in 20251 was met with enthusiasm by much of the medical community and by families of individuals living with Alzheimer's disease (AD). It was based on the results of a clinical trial that showed statistically significant differences in the rate of cognitive decline among individuals with early-stage AD.2 However, the results did not reach the minimum clinically important difference (MCID).2,3 While this regulatory decision brings hope for disease-modifying treatments in AD, it also raises complex challenges regarding the potential integration of this therapy into the Brazilian public healthcare system (Sistema Único de Saúde - SUS). We discuss the main barriers and implications related to the incorporation of donanemab into Brazilian SUS, aiming to contribute to a necessary debate guided by the principles of equity, sustainability, and person-centered care.

Structural limitations of the Brazilian public healthcare system

The Brazilian SUS, which provides care for most of Brazil's population, faces significant structural limitations, including restricted access to specialized diagnostic tests, a shortage of infusion centers, and a lack of trained professionals with expertise in dementia care. Anti-amyloid therapies require regular intravenous infusions in a supervised clinical setting, and patients receiving these treatments must be monitored with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to detect amyloid-related imaging abnormalities, a potentially serious neurological adverse event.4 However, SUS lacks widespread infrastructure for routine infusions. Access to MRI is insufficient and unevenly distributed across the country. Implementing these therapies would require the development of a network of infusion units integrated with specialized neurological care, along with expanded neuroimaging capacity, conditions still far from being met within the current system.

Diagnostic barriers and lack of access to biomarkers

Anti-amyloid therapies are indicated only for patients with a confirmed diagnosis of early-stage AD (mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia) and evidence of cerebral beta-amyloid accumulation.5 This requires access to specific biomarkers, such as amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) imaging or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis to quantify Aβ42 and p-tau, both of which are largely unavailable in the Brazilian public health network. In SUS, access to PET scans is extremely limited and concentrated in a few urban centers. Although CSF analysis is technically feasible, it faces logistical difficulties, a lack of standardization, the public healthcare system does not cover it, and faces resistance from both patients and healthcare professionals. Without accessible biomarkers, there is a high risk of treating individuals who do not have AD, compromising efficacy and increasing the likelihood of adverse events.

Workforce limitations and regional disparities

Another critical issue is the lack of trained professionals capable of diagnosing and managing AD in its early stages. Early clinical recognition of dementia syndromes requires training in cognitive neurology, geriatrics, or geriatric psychiatry, areas still underrepresented in most regions of Brazil. Regional disparities in access to specialist physicians, neuropsychologists, multidisciplinary teams, and dementia reference centers significantly limit equitable access to care. In many parts of the country, especially in the North and Northeast, there is a pronounced shortage of professionals and specialized dementia care services. These limitations would significantly hinder the implementation of the diagnostic and treatment protocols required for these new therapies.

Cost and budgetary sustainability

One of the most immediate obstacles to adopting these therapies within SUS is their high cost. International estimates suggest that the annual cost per patient may exceed US$32,000,6 which surpasses the financial capacity of most public health systems, particularly in middle-income countries such as Brazil. Depending on the number of eligible patients, indiscriminate adoption of these therapies could result in a budgetary impact incompatible with the sustainability of SUS. There are substantial indirect costs associated with diagnosis and monitoring, including serial MRI scans, laboratory tests, and neurological follow-up. These factors further increase per-patient expenditures. Economic models in high-income countries have already indicated that the cost-effectiveness of these therapies is marginal, even under ideal conditions.7 In Brazil, where healthcare resources are limited, any incorporation must be preceded by rigorous evaluation through cost-effectiveness analyses tailored to the national context.8

Legal actions and the risk of judicialization

The approval of anti-amyloid therapies such as donanemab opens the door to increased judicialization of healthcare.9 Given the expectations raised by regulatory approval, patients and families may seek legal action to access the medication through court orders, even in the absence of formal evaluation by the National Commission for Health Technology Incorporation in the SUS. Such legal actions tend to benefit individuals with higher socioeconomic status, better access to information, and legal resources, thereby exacerbating inequalities in healthcare access.10 Furthermore, unregulated judicial access may lead to off-label use, without adequate diagnostic confirmation or monitoring infrastructure, increasing the risk of serious adverse events and the misuse of public funds.

Conclusions

Despite their theoretical potential, these therapies have not achieved MCID, carry significant risks and entail prohibitive costs. In this context, premature or uncoordinated adoption of such interventions by the Brazilian SUS may exacerbate health inequalities and divert resources away from more impactful strategies such as prevention, early diagnosis, and comprehensive care. This scenario reinforces the need for transparent, evidence-based deliberation before any official incorporation of these therapies. It is crucial to prevent fragmented and potentially harmful decisions that could undermine the collective interest and the principles of equity and rational resource allocation that underpin Brazil's public healthcare system.
