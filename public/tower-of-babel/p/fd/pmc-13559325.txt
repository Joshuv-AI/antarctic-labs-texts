Safe transition from video-assisted thoracic surgery to robotic-assisted thoracic surgery: early experience and stepwise learning curve of robotic-assisted thoracic surgery
Authors: Yong Seong Lee, Hyun Oh Park, Chung-Eun Lee, In-Seok Jang, Jong-duk Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTransitioning from video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) to robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) presents a significant learning curve, which may compromise patient safety during early adoption. This study reports the early experience of a single, highly experienced VATS surgeon utilizing a novel “stepwise hybrid approach” to safely implement a RATS program.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on the first 41 consecutive patients who underwent robotic lung resection between February 2025 and February 2026. To mitigate risks, the initial three cases were performed using a stepwise hybrid technique, gradually increasing the proportion of robotic utilization while relying on conventional VATS for critical dissections. Clinical, surgical, and oncologic outcomes were evaluated, and the learning curve was analyzed.ResultsThe cohort consisted predominantly of lobectomies (95.1%). The mean total operation time was 225.1±52.3 minutes, and the mean console time was 114.9±45.4 minutes. The conversion rate to open surgery was low (4.9%). Postoperative recovery was stable with a mean hospital stay of 9.2±2.6 days. There was a 0% 30-day mortality rate, and major complications (Clavien-Dindo Grade IIIa or higher) occurred in only 7.3% of patients. Oncologically, a mean of 14.6±7.9 lymph nodes were harvested, achieving an R0 resection rate of 97.6%.ConclusionsThis stepwise hybrid transition approach is feasible and demonstrated an acceptable perioperative profile within the context of an expert VATS surgeon introducing a structured robotic program. Rather than universally ensuring procedural safety across all surgical settings, this model provides a pragmatic educational framework to decouple initial robotic interface adaptation from acute clinical risks, though broader multi-center validation remains warranted.

Introduction

Minimally invasive surgery, particularly video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS), has been established as the standard of care for the surgical treatment of lung diseases, including non-small cell lung cancer (1,2). In recent years, robotic-assisted thoracic surgery (RATS) has gained widespread popularity due to its technical advantages, such as a three-dimensional high-definition (3D-HD) view, tremor filtration, and wristed instrumentation with increased degrees of freedom (3,4). These features facilitate precise dissection, especially in complex procedures like lymph node dissection and anatomic lung resections (5,6).

Despite these inherent advantages, transitioning from VATS to RATS poses a significant challenge for surgeons due to the steep learning curve associated with adopting a new surgical platform (7,8). The lack of tactile feedback, the need to adapt to console manipulation, and the complexities of robotic docking can potentially compromise patient safety during the initial phase of adoption (9,10). Therefore, establishing a safe and structured transition strategy is paramount, even for surgeons highly experienced in conventional VATS (11,12).

To mitigate the risks associated with this early learning curve, we applied a “stepwise hybrid approach”—gradually increasing the proportion of robotic procedures over the initial cases while relying on established VATS techniques for critical steps like fissure dissection and vessel division (13).

This study aims to report the early experience of RATS lung resections performed by a single experienced VATS surgeon. By evaluating the surgical and clinical outcomes of the initial 41 consecutive cases, we seek to demonstrate the safety, feasibility, and oncological adequacy of adopting a stepwise transition model, providing a practical blueprint for thoracic surgeons initiating a robotic program. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1413/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient selection

We retrospectively reviewed a prospectively maintained database of patients who underwent minimally invasive surgical resection for lung lesions at a single institution between February 2025 and February 2026. This study included the initial 41 consecutive cases of RATS performed by a single surgeon who had approximately 10 years of extensive experience in VATS. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gyeongsang National University Hospital (No. 2026-03-013-001), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

Surgeon’s baseline experience

Prior to the initiation of this RATS program, the single attending surgeon possessed an extensive clinical volume exceeding 10 years of dedicated minimally invasive thoracic surgery, highlighted by the successful completion of over 800 cumulative VATS cases. Crucially, all 800 of these baseline cases consisted strictly of major anatomical lung resections (specifically lobectomies and segmentectomies), completely excluding any sub-lobar limited resections, such as wedge resections. This high-volume, anatomy-specific baseline expertise was established as a mandatory institutional prerequisite before implementing the rapid, three-case stepwise hybrid protocol described below.

Surgical technique (the robotic approach)

All completely robotic procedures (phase 3) were conducted using the da Vinci Surgical System. Under general anesthesia and double-lumen endotracheal intubation, the patient was placed in the lateral decubitus position. A standardized four-arm robotic portal approach was utilized. The definitive surgical technique incorporated a fissureless, posterior-to-anterior anatomical dissection plan, which is highly preferred at our institution. For lobectomies, the pulmonary vein was isolated and divided first, followed by the specific pulmonary artery branches and the lobar bronchus, using robotic staplers. Systematic mediastinal lymph node dissection, including stations 2R, 4R, 7, 8, and 9 for right-sided tumors, and stations 5, 6, 7, 8, and 9 for left-sided tumors, was routinely performed.

Stepwise hybrid transition protocol and intraoperative “down-shift” criteria

To ensure patient safety and mitigate the risks associated with the early learning curve, a “stepwise hybrid approach” was deliberately applied to the initial cases. Rather than abruptly shifting to complete RATS, the transition was systematically phased by progressively increasing the proportion of robotic utilization while relying on established VATS techniques for critical anatomical steps.

❖ Phase 1 (initial integration—case 1): the operation commenced with a conventional VATS approach. The most technically demanding steps, such as hilar dissection and the management of major pulmonary vessels, were performed using VATS. The robotic system was exclusively docked and utilized for the final division of the bronchus and remaining vascular structures.

❖ Phase 1 (initial integration—case 1): the operation commenced with a conventional VATS approach. The most technically demanding steps, such as hilar dissection and the management of major pulmonary vessels, were performed using VATS. The robotic system was exclusively docked and utilized for the final division of the bronchus and remaining vascular structures.

❖ Phase 2 (partial robotic dissection—case 2): in this intermediate phase, the initial fissure dissection and exposure of the pulmonary artery branches were performed using VATS. Once the critical vascular structures were clearly identified and exposed, the robotic system was docked to perform meticulous dissection and division of these vessels.

❖ Phase 2 (partial robotic dissection—case 2): in this intermediate phase, the initial fissure dissection and exposure of the pulmonary artery branches were performed using VATS. Once the critical vascular structures were clearly identified and exposed, the robotic system was docked to perform meticulous dissection and division of these vessels.

❖ Phase 3 (complete RATS—case 3 and beyond): following the successful completion of the hybrid phases, the procedure transitioned to a complete robotic approach. From this case onward, the entire anatomical lung resection, including initial fissure division, complete hilar dissection, vessel division, and comprehensive mediastinal lymph node dissection, was performed exclusively using the robotic platform in accordance with established oncologic guidelines (14).

❖ Phase 3 (complete RATS—case 3 and beyond): following the successful completion of the hybrid phases, the procedure transitioned to a complete robotic approach. From this case onward, the entire anatomical lung resection, including initial fissure division, complete hilar dissection, vessel division, and comprehensive mediastinal lymph node dissection, was performed exclusively using the robotic platform in accordance with established oncologic guidelines (14).

Crucially, progression through these consecutive phases was not blindly dictated by case numbers alone; rather, it was strictly governed by intraoperative safety milestones. Furthermore, a rigorous decision-making framework was established prior to the study, defining explicit intraoperative “down-shift” criteria that would trigger an immediate reversion from RATS to a conventional VATS-assisted or open thoracotomy approach. These safety triggers included: (I) severe, calcified lymphadenopathy completely obliterating the surgical planes around the pulmonary artery branches; (II) an extreme incomplete fissure precluding safe posterior-approach dissection via the robot; or (III) uncontrollable microvascular or macrovascular bleeding that could not be instantly managed with robotic instrumentation. If any of these parameters were met, a down-shift to VATS was mandated to prioritize patient safety.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Clinical data, including patient demographics, preoperative pulmonary function, and comorbidities, were extracted. Perioperative outcomes—encompassing total operation time, console time, estimated blood loss (EBL), conversion to open surgery, length of hospital stay, and morbidity—were evaluated. Postoperative complications were graded according to the Clavien-Dindo classification system (15). For patients with malignancies, oncologic outcomes such as the total number of harvested lymph nodes and nodal upstaging were recorded. Descriptive statistics were utilized; continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. To rigorously characterize the surgical learning curve and evaluate proficiency acquisition, trendlines for Total Operation Time and Console Time across consecutive cases were generated and fitted using a third-order polynomial regression model. This regression framework was chosen to accurately capture the initial adjustment phase, the subsequent steep acceleration of proficiency, and the eventual plateauing of operating times. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The cohort comprised 41 patients, including 26 males (63.4%) and 15 females (36.6%), with a mean age of 66.8±7.8 years. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (48.8%) and diabetes mellitus (26.8%). The majority of patients had an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score of II (80.5%). Preoperative clinical staging primarily indicated early-stage lung lesions, with the largest portion categorized as clinical stage IA2 (39.0%). Perioperative and clinical outcomes

The surgical approaches predominantly involved anatomic lung resections, specifically lobectomy (95.1%, n=39). The mean total operation time was 225.1±52.3 minutes, while the mean robotic console time was 114.9±45.4 minutes. Conversion to an open thoracotomy was required in only 2 cases (4.9%). To ensure full transparency regarding our early safety profile, both conversions occurred during phase 3 at Case 5 and Case 8. Notably, these involved a conversion to conventional VATS rather than an open thoracotomy, primarily driven by technical limitations and the tactical prudence exercised during the surgeon’s early adoption period. There were no conversions to open surgery or unexpected down-shifts during the initial structured hybrid phases (Cases 1–3).

The postoperative recovery trajectory was highly stable. The mean chest tube duration was 6.0±2.1 days, and the average length of hospital stay was 9.2±2.6 days. Crucially, the 30-day mortality rate was 0%. Most postoperative morbidities were minor (Clavien-Dindo Grade I, 90.2%), with only 3 patients (7.3%) experiencing major complications requiring intervention (Grade IIIa). A detailed clinical breakdown of these three Grade IIIa events demonstrates that one patient (Case 17) experienced postoperative pneumonia accompanied by vocal cord paresis, one patient (Case 19) developed a pneumothorax with subcutaneous emphysema, and one patient (Case 22) experienced alcoholic delirium combined with a pneumothorax. All three cases were successfully resolved using appropriate localized thoracic drainage or standard medical management, without any permanent sequelae or long-term functional deficits. From an oncological perspective, the mean number of total harvested lymph nodes was 14.6±7.9. Nodal upstaging was identified in 9 cases (22.0%), and complete macroscopic and microscopic clearance (R0 resection) was achieved in 40 cases (97.6%), verifying the oncological adequacy of the early RATS phase.

Analysis of the operative times across the chronological case sequence revealed a distinct learning curve pattern dictated by the stepwise strategy. During the initial hybrid phase (Cases 1 to 3), console times were intentionally kept minimal (ranging from 54 to 66 minutes) as the majority of the dissection was executed via VATS. Upon transitioning to complete RATS (Case 4 onwards), an expected initial increase in total and console times was observed, which subsequently demonstrated a steady, progressive decline as the surgeon’s proficiency with the robotic console improved.

Discussion

The transition from VATS to RATS represents a significant paradigm shift for thoracic surgeons. In many institutions, particularly where VATS techniques have reached a high level of mastery, there is often a paradoxical reluctance among experienced surgeons to adopt robotic systems. Because these surgeons already consistently achieve excellent clinical and oncological outcomes with conventional VATS (1,2), the steep learning curve, absence of haptic feedback, and the potential for intraoperative catastrophes associated with early robotic cases are frequently perceived as unjustifiable risks to patient safety (9,16). Therefore, while RATS offers superior 3D-HD visualization and wristed dexterity (3,4), establishing a safe and structured transition strategy is not merely a technical requirement, but a crucial psychological bridge for established VATS surgeons.

This study highlights that an experienced VATS surgeon can successfully and safely launch a RATS program by implementing a “stepwise hybrid approach”, achieving a 0% 30-day mortality and minimal major complications in the first 41 cases. Standard literature suggests that the learning curve for robotic lobectomy typically requires between 20 to 40 cases to achieve proficiency in terms of operative time and complication rates (7,8,17). However, traversing these initial 20 cases is often cited as a period of high clinical stress. By dividing the learning process into manageable technical phases—beginning with VATS for critical vessel exposure and using the robot only for division, then progressing to full robotic dissection—we effectively decoupled the technical learning curve from clinical risk (12,18). This allowed the surgeon to acclimatize to the 3D-HD depth perception and foot-pedal coordination of the robotic system without jeopardizing patient safety during high-stakes maneuvers like pulmonary artery dissection.

In this context, one might question whether a brief, two-case hybrid phase is truly sufficient to overcome the steep initial learning curve of robotic lobectomy, which literature suggests requires 20 to 40 cases to achieve mastery. We wish to clarify that the primary objective of our hybrid phase was never to bypass or truncate the learning curve itself, as full procedural proficiency undeniably requires continuous longitudinal experience. Instead, the framework was specifically designed to “decouple” the initial acute stress of mechanical interface adaptation—such as system docking, multi-arm instrument positioning, and adjustment to 3D binocular depth perception—from the distinct clinical risks associated with primary hilar tissue dissection. By deploying familiar VATS techniques for critical dissection steps during the first two cases, the surgeon safely acclimated to the robotic interface. The safety of the subsequent fully robotic cases was heavily anchored by the surgeon’s prior decade of extensive VATS anatomical mastery, rather than an abrupt shortcut to robotic expertise. Therefore, this model should be viewed as a tool for “safe entry” rather than an immediate shortcut to surgical mastery.

Indeed, our perioperative outcomes strongly validate this strategy. A conversion rate of 4.9% and a major complication rate of 7.3% in a foundational robotic cohort compare highly favorably with established large-scale, multicenter VATS and RATS databases (19,20). Furthermore, the oncologic principles of lung cancer surgery were not compromised during this transition. The harvest of a mean 14.6 lymph nodes and a nodal upstaging rate of 22.0% are consistent with, or even superior to, early VATS experiences, indicating that the robotic platform’s enhanced dexterity allowed for meticulous, comprehensive mediastinal lymph node dissection right from the program’s inception (6,21).

The learning curve plotted from our cohort beautifully illustrates the reality of the stepwise approach. The artificially low console times in the first three cases reflect the deliberate reliance on VATS. As complete RATS was adopted, operative times naturally peaked but quickly stabilized and trended downwards, demonstrating that the foundational skills acquired during the hybrid phase smoothly translated into complete robotic proficiency (16).

Study limitation

Despite the encouraging clinical outcomes, several methodological limitations must be acknowledged. First, this study is inherently limited by its retrospective, single-center design encompassing a relatively small sample size of 41 cases. Second, all procedures were performed by a single surgeon possessing extensive baseline VATS expertise, which significantly limits the immediate generalizability of findings to trainee surgeons or low-volume centers. Most importantly, this study lacks a concurrent or historical propensity-score matched VATS control group, as well as an abrupt-transition RATS cohort. Without a direct comparative arm, any definitive claims regarding the “superiority” of this stepwise hybrid approach over an abrupt transition remain speculative, though highly plausible based on our 0% mortality and low conversion rates. Consequently, our findings demonstrate the clinical feasibility and institutional safety of this transition model rather than proven clinical superiority, and large-scale, multi-center comparative trials are required to fully validate this educational pathway.

Conclusions

Initiating a RATS program does not have to be synonymous with an initial spike in patient morbidity. For thoracic surgeons already proficient in VATS, adopting a stepwise hybrid approach—gradually shifting the burden of dissection from traditional thoracoscopy to the robotic platform over the first few cases—offers a remarkably safe and effective blueprint. This strategy secures low complication rates and excellent oncologic clearance, flattening the clinical risk curve while successfully navigating the technical learning curve of robotic surgery.
