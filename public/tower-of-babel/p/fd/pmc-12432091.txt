Analysis of fecal microbiome in Chinese patients with non-severe aplastic anemia
Authors: Xiaolan Zhang, Jingjing Xie, Heng Wang, Jun Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
By analyzing the fecal microbiome data of patients with non-severe aplastic anemia (NSAA), this study aims to explore the potential role of dysbiosis in the immune-mediated pathogenesis of NSAA. This study included 21 newly diagnosed NSAA patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine between July 2018 and June 2021, along with 24 healthy controls who underwent routine health checkups. Fecal samples were collected for DNA extraction. At the species level of phylum, class, order, family, genus and species, the intestinal microbial community of NSAA patients was significantly different from that of healthy controls. Alpha diversity analysis showed that the Chao and Observed species indices were significantly lower in the NSAA group compared to the control group ( P < 0.05 ), indicating that the gut microbiota diversity in NSAA patients was lower than that in normal individuals. Further multivariate statistical analysis revealed that the relative abundance of the following gut microbiota was higher in the NSAA group: Actinobacteriota, Coriobacteriia, Bifidobacteriales, Coriobacteriales, Pasteurellales, Prevotellaceae, Muribaculaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, Coriobacteriaceae, Pasteurellaceae, Prevotella spp., Muribaculum spp., Barnesiella spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Mitsuokella spp., Collinsella spp., Alloprevotella spp. Meanwhile, the abundance of Lachnospirales spp. in the gut microbiota of NSAA patients was found to be lower than that of healthy controls. Correlation and model prediction analyses of the dominant microbial species in both the NSAA and control groups revealed a strong competitive relationship between Bacteroides spp. and Prevotella spp. NSAA patients exhibit a decrease in the abundance of Lachnospirales spp. and an increase in the abundance of Prevotella spp., which may be closely related to immune dysfunction mediated by NSAA Treg/Th17 imbalance, which, in turn, may contribute to the development of hematopoietic failure.

Introduction

Aplastic anemia (AA) is a severe hematological disorder characterized by pancytopenia and bone marrow failure. Its pathophysiological features include the expansion and activation of effector T cells, along with the destruction of bone marrow hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells. The combination of immunosuppressive and hematopoietic stimulation therapies provides an effective treatment strategy; however, approximately 20% of patients remain refractory to various treatment options. Microecology is the study of the structure, function, and interdependent relationships between normal microbial communities and their host. Bacteria and other microorganisms (fungi, protozoa, viruses, etc.) form a microbiotic ecosystem within the human and animal bodies. The gut microbiome is primarily composed of four major phyla: Actinobacteria, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidetes [1, 2], which influence numerous physiological functions, including hematopoiesis and immune function. Dysbiosis or reduced microbial diversity has been shown to trigger or exacerbate various diseases [3–5], such as diabetes, multiple sclerosis, and systemic infections. According to statistics, 13% of cancer cases caused by infections worldwide in 2018 were attributed to microorganisms [6]. A loss of gut microbial diversity and the overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens are associated with increased treatment-related mortality and graft-versus-host disease following hematopoietic stem cell transplantation [7]. Recent studies suggest that a healthy microbiota is crucial for the differentiation, development, and function of myeloid progenitor cells [8]. Short-chain fatty acids produced by gut microbiota metabolism can regulate T cell differentiation, enhance B cell metabolism, differentiation, and antibody production [9], and maintain steady-state hematopoietic function [10]. Antibiotic-induced depletion of gut microbiota in mice leads to pancytopenia and a reduction in bone marrow hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells [11]. Epidemiological surveys on AA indicate that its onset may be related to environmental factors, yet the relationship between AA and specific infections remains unclear, and the antigens triggering the initial immune response in AA have yet to be identified.

The development of high-throughput sequencing, metabolomics, and bioinformatics technologies has unveiled a more diverse microbiota and its metabolic products, with increasingly clear functional roles. We hypothesize that dysbiosis plays a critical role in the immune-mediated bone marrow damage in AA. This study employs high-throughput sequencing to investigate the changes in the fecal microbiome of NSAA patients, aiming to enhance the understanding of the pathogenesis of NSAA and provide a basis for the development of new therapeutic targets and preventive measures.

Materials and methods

Study population and grouping

A total of 21 newly diagnosed NSAA patients (5 males, 16 females, aged 18–82 years, median age 55) were included in this study. These patients were admitted to the Hematology Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine between July 2018 and June 2021. The diagnosis and disease severity classification were abided by Camitta [12] criteria. Exclusion criteria included active infections, other autoimmune diseases, psychiatric disorders, primary cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, hepatic, renal, or hematopoietic diseases, and pregnant or lactating women. A control group of 24 healthy individuals (8 males, 16 females, aged 20–78 years, median age 48) was selected from routine health check-up participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the hospital, and all participants provided informed consent. Bioinformatics Analysis.

The library sequencing and data processing were conducted by OE biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).The raw data were in FASTQ format. The original paired-end sequences were processed using Trimmomatic software to remove contaminants. The filtering parameters included removing ambiguous bases N and applying a sliding window approach to trim sequences when the average base quality fell below a threshold of 20. The filtered paired-end sequences were then merged using FLASH software, with the following parameters: minimum overlap of 10 bp, maximum overlap of 200 bp, and a maximum mismatch rate of 20%.

To ensure the accuracy of the results, the sequences containing ambiguous bases or homologous single-base repeats, as well as those shorter than the required length, were excluded. The filtering parameters used were: removal of sequences containing N bases and retention of sequences with a base quality score of Q20 or higher for at least 75% of the length. Additionally, UCHIME was used to detect and remove chimeric sequences.

After pre-processing the sequencing data to generate high-quality sequences, Vsearch software was used to cluster sequences into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) based on sequence similarity (≥ 97% identity). The representative sequences for each OTU were selected using the QIIME software package, and these sequences were aligned with a database for annotation. The 16 S sequences were compared using the Silva database (version 138), and species annotation was performed using the RDP classifier with a confidence threshold of > 0.7. The ITS sequences were compared using the Unite database, with species annotation done using BLAST.

Statistical methods

The basic data from the 16 S analysis were processed using QIIME 1.8.0 software, and statistical analyses were performed using R Studio. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test and t-tests were used to evaluate the significance of differences in species abundance, with a P-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Alpha diversity analysis

The average good coverage of the NSAA group and the normal group was above 99%, indicating that the sequencing depth had basically covered all species in the samples. The software R (v3.2.1) was used to perform Alpha diversity violinplot between the NSAA group and the normal group to visually display the differences in Alpha diversity between the groups. As shown in the following figure (Fig. 2ABCD), from the community diversity index (simpson, shannon) and richness index (chao, observed species), the chao index and observed species index of NSAA group were lower than those of normal group (P < 0.05). simpson index and shannon index were slightly higher than normal group (P > 0.05). The rank sum Test Wilcoxon Test was further applied to compare and analyze the differences between groups of diversity, and it was found that the chao index and species index of the NSAA group were lower than those of the normal group (P < 0.05), indicating that the bacterial diversity of NSAA patients was lower than that of normal people (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, by analyzing the rarefaction curve, it can be observed from the Shannon index image (Fig. 3A) that as the sequencing depth increases, the Shannon index of all groups shows a gradually stabilizing trend. This outcome suggests that the current sequencing depth is adequate for fully characterizing the diversity of the microbial community, thus offering a solid data foundation for analyzing community diversity. Discussion

The correlation between adverse hematological effects and gut microbiome imbalance or dysbiosis is well-established in several human diseases, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), obesity, and in environments of severe antibiotic exposure. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiome has been associated with hematological abnormalities in both humans and mice. Mouse studies have shown that antibiotic-induced reduction of the microbiota and subsequent bone marrow suppression are linked to the absence of heat-stable microbial products. These microbial products can circulate in the bloodstream and promote hematopoiesis via basal inflammatory signaling. Key molecules such as MyD88/TICAM1, NOD1, and STAT1 have been identified as critical regulators in maintaining hematopoietic homeostasis through gut microbiome modulation [13].

Our previous research [14] demonstrated that a successful bone marrow failure mouse model could be established by transferring major or minor histocompatibility antigen-mismatched donor mouse lymph node cells into untreated or sub-lethal irradiated recipient mice. This animal model, which is internationally recognized for studying human disease-related aplastic anemia, has proven useful for investigating the pathogenesis of AA and screening therapeutic drugs. In conventional and specific-pathogen-free (SPF) environments, mice exhibit significant differences in hematopoietic differentiation, immune cell activation, and immune-mediated bone marrow injury. A diverse gut microbiota plays a protective role in regulating and maintaining hematopoietic function, potentially stimulating the recipient’s immune system, thereby inhibiting allogeneic donor T-cell activation and expansion, and reducing damage to the host’s hematopoietic bone marrow.

This study is the first to incorporate NSAA patients and perform gut microbiome analysis using 16 S rRNA sequencing. Our results indicate that, from the perspectives of phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species, NSAA patients exhibit significant differences in gut microbiota composition compared to healthy controls. Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the Chao index and observed species index were both lower in the NSAA group compared to the normal group (P < 0.05), suggesting that the gut microbiota diversity in NSAA patients is lower than in healthy individuals. This reduction in microbiome diversity may be linked to the hematopoietic failure and immune dysregulation observed in NSAA patients, aligning with both domestic and international studies in this field.

Further multivariate statistical analysis of the microbiota revealed that the NSAA group had a higher relative abundance of certain gut microbes, including Actinobacteriota, Coriobacteriia, Bifidobacteriales, Coriobacteriales, Pasteurellales, Prevotellaceae, Muribaculaceae, Bifidobacteriaceae, Coriobacteriaceae, Pasteurellaceae, Prevotella spp., Muribaculum spp., Barnesiella spp., Bifidobacterium spp., Mitsuokella spp., Collinsella spp., Alloprevotella spp. According to the relative expression level of Lachnospiraceae spp. in NSAA patients and normal controls the abundance of Lachnospiraceae spp. decreased by 77% compared with normal controls. Based on the relative expression level of Prevotella spp. in NSAA patients and normal controls, the abundance of Prevotella spp. increased by 109% compared with normal controls.

Furthermore, studies have shown that compared with normal individuals, the abundance of intestinal flora in patients with immune thrombocytopenia is reduced at the phylum level [15], Bacteroides spp. were enriched at the genus level, while, Lachnospiraceae NC2004 were depleted [16]. This is consistent with the results we obtained in our study on the gut microbiota of NSAA patients. In patients with myelodysplastic syndrome, the abundance of Prevotella spp. bacteria is decreased [17]. However, some studies have also found that the Prevotella spp. is significantly increased in high-risk patients compared to low-risk and intermediate-risk patients [18].

In contrast, a study by Shao [19] demonstrated that the gut microbiota of Severe Aplastic Anemia (SAA) patients differed from that of healthy controls. Specifically, the relative abundance of the gut microbiota in SAA patients was lower than in healthy individuals, with increased levels of Clostridium citroniae and decreased levels of Fecalibacterium prausnitzii and Prevotella. Notably, a strong negative correlation was found between Prevotella and Clostridium citroniae, suggesting that microbiome dysregulation plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of SAA. This discovery is consistent with our previous research results, indicating that the diversity of the intestinal microbiota in both SAA and NSAA is decreased. However, there are differences in the composition of the intestinal microbiota between the two, suggesting that SAA and NSAA may have different mechanisms in the pathogenesis of immune disorders. Additionally, compared to healthy controls, SAA patients exhibited higher levels of Enterobacteriaceae and Lactobacillaceae, but lower levels of Bacteroidetes, Clostridia, and Actinobacteria. At the species level, Escherichia coli and Clostridium citroniae were more abundant, while Prevotella spp., Fecalibacterium prausnitzii, and Ruminococcus albus were less abundant. Following treatment with mannitol and gentamicin for gut inflammation, SAA patients showed significant improvements in hematological parameters [14, 20], further supporting the hypothesis that gut microbiome imbalance influences the progression of bone marrow failure in these patients.

This study also found that NSAA patients had a lower abundance of Lachnospirales spp., which is consistent with previous reports. Correlation and model prediction analyses of the NSAA and control groups revealed that Bacteroides spp. and Prevotella spp. exhibited a strong competitive relationship in the microbiota of NSAA patients.

Microbial classification is based on similarities in morphology and physiological function. Lachnospirales belongs to the phylum Firmicutes, class Clostridia, and order Clostridiales, and consists of strict anaerobes commonly present in the human gut. These bacteria participate in the metabolism of various carbohydrates into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including butyrate, acetate, and propionate [21]. SCFAs are the major metabolic products of the gut anaerobic microbiota and play a crucial role in modulating host-pathogen interactions. They also serve as important mediators in the interaction between the gut microbiome and the immune system, regulating T-cell differentiation, enhancing B-cell metabolism, differentiation, and antibody production, and balancing pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory mechanisms. Butyrate has been shown to increase the production of Treg cells in the thymus of mice [22], while propionate stimulates the generation of peripheral Treg cells [23]. Acetate and propionate can induce the differentiation of naive T cells into Th17 cells [24].

Related research also indicated that the reduced relative abundance of Lachnospirales may be linked to the pathogenesis and progression of aplastic anemia. Antibiotics and immunosuppressants may have less impact on Lachnospirales, and post-allo-HSCT (hematopoietic stem cell transplantation) patients with aplastic anemia showed an increase in Lachnospirales levels after immune dysregulation and bone marrow failure were corrected. Studies have shown that Lachnospirales species help maintain the immune surveillance function of CD8 + T cells [25]. Lachnospirales strains isolated from human fecal samples influence Treg cell numbers and function. Treg cells are abundant under stable gut conditions and play a key role in maintaining intestinal and immune homeostasis. AA patients exhibit immune dysfunction, with T-cell abnormalities playing a major role in disease progression. Treg cells, a subgroup of T lymphocytes with immunoregulatory functions, play a pivotal role in the pathophysiology of AA. Loss of Treg cells enhances the production of autoreactive T cells, thus promoting AA development. Microbial species such as Lachnospirales contribute to the production of SCFAs, which, in turn, inhibit histone deacetylase (HDAC) and induce differentiation of T lymphocytes into effector T cells and Tregs. This process is mediated by acetylation of histone H3, which triggers the upregulation of FOXP3 and promotes Treg proliferation via activation of the GPR43 receptor [26]. – [27].

Furthermore, Han et al.‘s study [28] found that the relative abundance of Lachnospirales was positively correlated with the Treg/Th17 cell ratio, potentially mediated through acetylation of histone H4 in CD3 + T cells. In AA patients, protein and mRNA levels of FOXP3 and protein levels of NFAT1 are reduced or absent. The transcription factor NFAT1 induces FOXP3 expression by binding to its promoter, and low NFAT1 levels lead to reduced FOXP3 expression [29]. AA patients also exhibit decreased expression of CXCR4 (but not CXCR7), impairing the migration ability of peripheral Treg cells, resulting in a reduced frequency of Treg cells in both peripheral blood and bone marrow. Additionally, AA patients have a diminished ability of Treg cells to suppress INF-γ production by effector T cells [30].

BM-MSCs (bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells) from AA patients are defective, unable to promote CD4 + CD25 + FOXP3 + Treg cell expansion through transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), leading to a reduction in Treg cells and immune dysregulation, which further exacerbates bone marrow failure [31]. Research by Tong Laigen et al. found that the hemoglobin count in AA patients was positively correlated with the expression of peripheral blood Treg cells, suggesting that Treg cell levels are closely linked to the severity of anemia in AA patients [32]. In conclusion, the reduction in Lachnospirales levels in AA patients may be associated with Treg cell function and quantity, and could play a role in the pathophysiology of AA.

The bidirectional regulation between the gut microbiome and the immune system occurs through various mechanisms, including the involvement of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and pathogen-specific receptors expressed on many cell types. Studies have shown that an increase in the abundance of Prevotella species in mucosal sites is associated with both localized and systemic diseases, such as periodontal disease, bacterial vaginosis, rheumatoid arthritis, metabolic disorders, and low-grade systemic inflammation [33]. The elevated abundance of Prevotella is linked to enhanced mucosal inflammation mediated by T helper 17 (Th17) cells, consistent with its potent ability to drive Th17 immune responses in vitro. Prevotella primarily activates Toll-like receptor 2 (TLR2), which induces antigen-presenting cells to produce Th17-polarizing cytokines, including interleukin-23 (IL-23) and IL-1. Furthermore, Prevotella stimulates epithelial cells to release IL-8, IL-6, and CCL20, promoting Th17-mediated mucosal immune responses and neutrophil recruitment. It also triggers dendritic cells (DCs) to release IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-23, further driving the Th17 immune response and the production of IL-17 by Th17 cells. The mucosal inflammation induced by Prevotella facilitates the systemic dissemination of inflammatory mediators, bacteria, and bacterial products, potentially impacting the overall progression of systemic diseases. In comparison to strictly commensal bacteria, Prevotella exhibits more pronounced pro-inflammatory characteristics, with enhanced release of inflammatory mediators by immune cells and various stromal cells. These findings suggest that certain Prevotella strains may play a pathogenic role in clinical settings by promoting chronic inflammation and contributing to the pathogenesis of human diseases.

Our findings suggest that gut microbiome modulation could represent a promising therapeutic strategy for treating aplastic anemia. Understanding the potential protective mechanisms of the gut microbiome may provide new targets for therapeutic interventions. However, this research constitutes a small-sample exploratory analysis. All participants in the study were drawn from the East Asian Chinese population. The findings reveal features specific to the gut microbiota of the Chinese NSAA population and should not be generalized to other ethnic groups. It is anticipated that future studies will involve larger sample sizes and prospective multicenter cohort investigations to achieve a more precise understanding of the gut microbiota characteristics in NSAA patients.
