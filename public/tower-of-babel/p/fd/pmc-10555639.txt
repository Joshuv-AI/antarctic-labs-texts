Cervical intradural traumatic neuroma without history of trauma: illustrative case
Authors: Elias Elias, Kimmo J Hatanpaa, Matthew MacAllister, Ali Daoud, Charbel Elias, Zeina Nasser
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDTraumatic neuroma typically refers to a reactive process in the injured peripheral nerve, characterized by an excessive growth of axons, Schwann cells, and fibroblasts at the proximal end of the nerve after its interruption. The authors report a case of a traumatic neuroma in the cervical nerve root in a patient with no history of trauma.OBSERVATIONSThe patient presented with sensation loss in the right-hand ulnar distribution, right flank around the T4–11 region, and right small toe along with motor power weakness over the right upper and lower extremity. Magnetic resonance imaging revealed an intradural extramedullary mass lesion with extension along the C7 nerve root. Histological examination showed traumatic neuroma. A total resection of the lesion along with the resolution of sensory and motor deficits was achieved directly after surgery.LESSONSTraumatic neuroma should always be kept in the armamentarium for diagnosis of an intradural nerve sheath tumor.

Traumatic neuroma typically refers to a reactive process in the injured peripheral nerve, characterized by an excessive growth of axons, Schwann cells, and fibroblasts at the proximal end of the nerve after its interruption.1 This condition is often accompanied by neuropathic pain. While atypical locations of traumatic neuroma, such as the facial nerve and supraorbital nerve, have been reported, there have been only 6 documented cases of traumatic neuroma in the cervical nerve root. It is worth noting that most traumatic neuromas have a clear history of trauma, as they do not occur spontaneously. We present a case of traumatic neuroma in the anterior cervical nerve root in a patient without any reported history of trauma.

Illustrative Case

History

A 54-year-old man presented with a past medical history of hypertension and hyperlipidemia and a more recent history of progressive right hemibody paresthesias/weakness. His symptoms had been slowly progressive for approximately 6 weeks. There was no trauma or inciting event. The patient had a foot drop on the right, and his outpatient provider thought he may have amyotrophic lateral sclerosis. He had sensation loss in the right-hand ulnar distribution, right flank around the T4–11 region, and right small toe.

Physical Examination

On examination, his right-hand grip was 4/5, right dorsiflexion was 4/5, and the extensor hallucis longus muscle was 4/5. His sensation was diminished to light touch in the ulnar right hand, right trunk, and right small toe. He also had a positive right-sided Hoffmann sign.

Cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed an enhancing mass filling the right C6–7 neural foramen and extending into the spinal canal, with a large intradural extramedullary component. This mass was isointense on T1- and T2-weighted imaging without contrast but had subtle abnormal T2 hyperintensity in the cord at the C6–7 level measuring approximately 40 mm × 11 mm in the transaxial dimensions by 16 mm craniocaudal. It displaced and compressed the cord to the left. The space for the cord was significantly limited to 8–9 mm transverse by 5 mm anteroposterior (Fig. 1).

Sagittal (left) and axial (right) magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrating T2 hyperintensity in the cord at the C6–7 level, intradural in nature, and extending to the foramen along the nerve root.

Operation

We performed a cervical decompressive laminectomy over the C6–7 level. The dura was opened in a midline fashion. The tumor was visualized heading out the foramen at the C6–7 level. There were a series of dorsal nerve rootlets entering the tumor. These were stimulated, and no motor response was observed. Beneath the tumor, there was a larger rootlet exiting that stimulated with a motor response in the trapezius/triceps.

This root was believed to be the motor root, and the roots entering the tumor were all believed to be sensory. The sensory nerve rootlets involved in the tumor were all cauterized with bipolar cautery and divided. Care was taken to preserve the motor root. The tumor was then coagulated, and the capsule was defined exiting toward the foramen. The dural incision was then extended in a T shape out of the C6–7 foramen to the right over the tumor capsule. The tumor was then removed with forceps and microscissors. The ultrasonic aspirator was then used to debulk the tumor. The dural sleeve of the sensory root and tumor was essentially eroded by tumor growth, but the motor sleeve below was well preserved. Care was taken to not disturb the motor root sleeve. The tumor was carefully dissected free and debulked. The tumor was grasped with pituitary rongeurs and delivered into the field, where further debulking was performed. Ultimately, the end of the tumor was visualized, and it was amputated at the most distal visualized portion. The dura was closed. We removed the facet and had to go all the way laterally to chase the tumor and free the nerve root; hence, we destabilized the cervical spine at a junctional level, so a lateral mass C5–6 and pedicle C7 and T1 fusion was performed (Figs. 2–4). Total resection of the lesion was achieved.

Pathology Findings

The resected mass was entirely submitted for histological evaluation. Microscopic slides from the specimen demonstrated frequent disorganized nerve twigs embedded in a dense fibrotic stroma (Fig. 5). The nerve twigs were immunopositive for S-100 and neurofilament, as expected. Immunostains for meningothelial cells and meningioma were repeatedly negative on multiple blocks within the lesion (SSTR2, PR, EMA). An immunostain for STAT6, a marker for solitary fibrous tumor, was negative. An immunostain for CD31 highlighted blood vessels. A special stain for amyloid was negative (Congo red). An immunostain for vimentin highlighted fibroblasts within the stroma. The MIB-1 proliferation index was 0%. An area containing entrapped ganglion cells, consistent with involvement of a dorsal root ganglion by the lesion, was identified. The slides were reviewed by 3 neuropathologists, who agreed that there was no evidence of a neoplasm and that the findings were most consistent with traumatic neuroma.

A: The mass consists of disorganized nerve twigs (arrows) embedded in a dense collagenous stroma (asterisk) resembling scar tissue. B: An immunohistochemical stain for neurofilament (NF) highlights haphazardly oriented axons within the nerve fascicles. H&E = hematoxylin and eosin.

Postoperative Outcome

The patient was satisfied with the result of the operation and had significant improvement directly postoperatively. He was observed clinically 3 months after surgery, and he was symptom free, had regained full motor power, and had intact sensation. MRI 6 weeks postoperatively showed full resection of the tumor.

Discussion

Observations

When a peripheral nerve is damaged, the axons near the injury site try to grow and branch out to bridge the gap. However, if these regenerating axons are unable to reach the other end of the nerve because of an obstruction, they form a cluster called a “traumatic neuroma.” As the traumatic neuroma develops, Schwann cells with thin myelin sheaths surround the multiplying and dividing axons.2

The histological findings in our case were consistent with the typical pathological features of traumatic neuroma and were in line with 3 case reports of cervical traumatic neuroma in the literature.3–5 Traumatic neuromas of the spine can present similarly to schwannomas and neurofibromas, as they are mass-occupying lesions. Symptoms can include sensory or motor disturbances, radicular pain, dysesthesias, and bladder dysfunction disturbances depending on the level or location of the affected nerve root.

Initially, based on the patient’s MRI and clinical presentation, we expected a schwannoma or neurofibroma. It is common to confuse a traumatic neuroma with a palisading encapsulated neuroma or neurofibroma. Neurofibromas histologically consist of abundant fibrous tissue and nerve fibers within the tumor stroma. The diffuse enlargement of the involved nerve makes it difficult to distinguish between nerve tissue and actual tumor. In contrast, traumatic neuromas can be differentiated from neurofibromas by the involvement of all elements of the nerve fascicles and identification of a damaged nerve. Schwannomas histologically consist of both Antoni types A and B tissues, with type A being compact and cellular and type B being loosely arranged. These histological features are not observed in traumatic neuromas or neurofibromas.

Although schwannomas and neurofibromas are classified as benign neural sheath tumors, there is a potential for malignant transformation, albeit less frequently in schwannomas. However, traumatic neuromas have no evidence of malignant transformation and are not considered primary neoplastic tumors. Indications for surgery are debatable, and resection is recommended when the lesion becomes symptomatic or if a recurrent tumor is suspected in a patient with a history of previous cancer-related surgery.

Our patient had no self-reported history of preceding trauma that could explain the development of a traumatic neuroma in the cervical nerve root. A literature review provided some explanation for the formation of a traumatic neuroma. This includes minor brachial plexus injury, a forceful pull on a baby’s head during birth, or a forgotten traction injury to the upper extremity during childhood.6,7 Identifying the history of physical traction in every case is often challenging, which suggests the possibility of unnoticed minor trauma occurring later in life, predisposing individuals to the development of a traumatic neuroma. Preoperative imaging of our patient’s lesion demonstrated isointensity on T1-weighted imaging and slight hyperintensity on contrast T2-weighted imaging. Our finding is similar to the cases reported by Salas et al.4 and Torun et al.3; however, it was unlike the 2 cases reported by Nomura et al.,5 in which the mass appeared to be an isointense signal on both T1- and T2-weighted sequences, with no contrast enhancement after gadolinium injection.

Lessons

In conclusion, resection of a traumatic neuroma with the aid of intraoperative electrophysiological monitoring can result in the full recovery of motor function and improvement in neuropathic pain. Traumatic neuroma should always be kept in the armamentarium for diagnosis of an intradural nerve sheath tumor.
