Transcriptomics-based analysis to explore the effects of culture media and cell models on the function of B. pseudocatenulatum strains of intestinal origin
Authors: Peng Du, Rong Guo, Ya dong Yao, Jun Min Zhu, Jian gang Feng, Hui Sun, Jie Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in maintaining host health by modulating metabolic pathways and immune functions. Although its biological significance is well recognized, systematic research into the functional characterization of intestinal bacteria remains scarce, particularly concerning the impact of culture environments and host cell model selection on functional assessment. This study focused on a strain of Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum isolated from the feces of healthy individuals. The objective was to investigate the regulatory effects of its metabolites, produced under different medium conditions, on gene expression across multiple host cell models.ResultsMetabolomic analysis revealed that the choice of culture medium (BHI vs. GAM) significantly reshaped the metabolic profiles of B. pseudocatenulatum. Transcriptomic data demonstrated that metabolites induced by different media led to markedly distinct gene expression patterns within the same host cell line. Furthermore, even under identical metabolite exposure conditions, the four cell models (MODE-K, NCM460, Henle-407, and HEK-293T) exhibited highly divergent and cell-line-specific regulatory responses.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the decisive influence of both medium composition and cell model selection on the outcomes of functional studies concerning gut commensal bacteria. This research not only provides a scientific basis for optimizing experimental designs in microbial functional studies but also underscores the necessity of rigorously evaluating culture conditions and model suitability in microbiota research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13067-1.

Background

The gut microbiota is essential for maintaining host health and preventing disease. It supports intestinal homeostasis by modulating metabolic, immune, and inflammatory responses while protecting the host from exogenous pathogens [1]. Recent advancements in biomedical research have positioned gut microbiota studies at the forefront of the field. With the advent of advanced technologies, research methodologies have evolved from traditional culture techniques to high-throughput sequencing and multi-omics analyses [2–4]. These technological shifts have significantly deepened our understanding of the composition and functions of the gut microbiota, as well as their interactions with the host. Growing evidence highlights how microbial metabolites and secretory proteins contribute to intestinal diseases [5–7]. However, the influence of culture conditions and the selection of cell models on gut microbial research remains underexplored.

Culture medium conditions can directly influence microbial metabolic pathway selection by modulating microbial metabolic states, thereby determining the composition and biological functions of microbial metabolites [8]. Brain heart infusion broth (BHI) and Gifu anaerobic medium (GAM) are two commonly used media for the in vitro cultivation of gut anaerobes. Among them, BHI is considered a high nutrient medium, with carbon and nitrogen source contents that are substantially higher than those of many conventional media [9]. Previous studies have demonstrated that excessive nutrient availability may trigger regulatory mechanisms such as catabolite repression, consequently altering microbial metabolic output profiles. For example, under BHI culture conditions, Staphylococcus aureus produces higher concentrations of acidic metabolites [10]. In contrast, GAM contains components including yeast extract, bovine liver extract, peptone, inorganic salts, and bile salts, which more closely resemble the nutritional composition and ecological environment of the intestinal tract and are therefore considered more suitable for mimicking the gut microecosystem [11]. Gotoh et al. demonstrated that gut microorganisms cultured in GAM can efficiently secrete key metabolites such as short chain fatty acids [12]. With respect to host response models, intestinal epithelial cell lines such as NCM460, MODE-K, and Henle-407 exhibit distinct advantages in terms of species origin, differentiation status, and barrier and immune response characteristics. These models enable the simulation of intestinal epithelial responses to microorganisms and their metabolites at multiple levels [13–15]. In addition, HEK-293T cells are frequently used for the construction of signaling pathway reporter systems due to their well defined genetic background, high transfection efficiency, low basal signaling activity, and stable signaling pathways. As such, they represent an ideal tool for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying host microbe interactions [16].

Bifidobacteria are a group of Gram-positive, anaerobic bacteria and represent important commensal microorganisms in the human gut, where they are closely associated with host immune regulation and the maintenance of intestinal homeostasis [17]. These bacteria enhance intestinal health, strengthen the intestinal barrier, mitigate certain intestinal diseases, and inhibit pathogen proliferation through diverse mechanisms [18]. Additionally, Bifidobacterium produces metabolites such as vitamins, polyphenols, conjugated linoleic acid, and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which benefit both the intestinal microbiota and the host’s overall health [19]. Notably, the metabolic outputs of bifidobacteria vary substantially under different culture conditions. In carbohydrate-rich media, bifidobacteria predominantly produce lactic acid and acetic acid, which can modulate intestinal pH and suppress the growth of pathogenic microorganisms. In contrast, when cultured in cellulose based media, bifidobacteria generate secondary metabolites including short chain fatty acids, which in turn influence host immune responses [20, 21].

In this study, a strain of Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum 86 (B. pseudocatenulatum-86) isolated from the fecal samples of healthy individuals was cultured under different medium conditions. The effects of its secretome on transcriptomic profiles were systematically evaluated across multiple host cell models, including MODE-K, NCM460, Henle-407, and HEK-293T. The findings are expected to provide a systematic reference for optimizing in vitro culture conditions for gut bacteria and for rationally selecting host cell models in future research.

Methods

Reagents

BHI medium (CM1135B, OXOID), GAM medium (05422, Nissui), high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (C11995500BT, Gibco), fetal bovine serum (16000-044, Gibco), 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA (1×) (25300-054, Gibco), and Anti-Anti antibiotic solution (100×) (15240-062, Gibco). TRIzol reagent (AG21101) was sourced from Acres Bio.

Bacterial strains and growth conditions

A strain of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 isolated from fecal samples of healthy individuals was used. B. pseudocatenulatum-86 exhibited stable growth under strictly anaerobic conditions in both BHI and GAM media, making it suitable for subsequent experimental investigations.

The strain was revived from glycerol stock cultures and inoculated at a ratio of 1:10 into pre reduced BHI or GAM medium, followed by incubation for 48 h at 37℃ under anaerobic conditions consisting of 85% N2, 10% CO2, and 5% H2. Genomic DNA was then extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® DNA extraction kit, and raw sequencing data were generated using a next generation sequencing platform. De novo genome assembly was performed sequentially using SPAdes for multi K mer assembly, GapFiller for gap closure, and PrInSeS G for sequence correction. A high quality draft genome was ultimately obtained, enabling strain identification and long term preservation.

Cell lines and culture conditions

The study utilized Henle-407 (human intestinal epithelial cells; Purchased from Shanghai Yaji Biotechnology Co., Ltd, CC-Y1582), NCM460 (human colonic epithelial cells; purchased from sunncell, SNL-519), HEK-293T cells, and MODE-K (mouse colonic epithelial cells; a gift from Haokai Lab). All cell lines were cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Bacterial culture supernatant collection

B. pseudocatenulatum-86 was initially cultured for 24 h and subsequently diluted at a 1:100 ratio into BHI or GAM media for further incubation over 4 days. The negative control (NC), consisting of culture medium without bacterial inoculation, was used as a strictly matched control in this study. The NC was prepared following the same procedure as the B. pseudocatenulatum-86 treatment groups, with the sole difference being the absence of bacterial inoculation. The culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C) and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. To achieve quantitative comparability in subsequent cell-based experiments, the volume of supernatants added was normalized based on the OD600 value at the time of collection, thereby compensating for variations in bacterial biomass between different groups. Furthermore, to eliminate potential interference from culture media background on transcriptomic analysis, corresponding sterile media were employed as vehicle controls. All significance analyses were performed relative to their respective media controls to ensure that the observed effects were indeed derived from metabolites secreted by the B. pseudocatenulatum-86.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (GK10001; GLPBIO). MODE-K (1 × 104 cells/well) and NCM460 (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for 18 h to allow for adherence. Cells were then treated for 24 h with various concentrations (5%, 10%, 20%, and 30%, v/v) of BHI and GAM media (BHI-derived sup and GAM-derived sup) or their respective sterile media controls (BHI or GAM). Subsequently, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of CCK-8 working solution (1:10 dilution in DMEM), and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader after a specific incubation period at 37 °C. Relative cell viability was calculated by normalizing the absorbance of treatment groups to that of the control group after background subtraction.

Cell processing

The bacterial culture supernatants (derived from BHI or GAM media) were applied to Henle-407, NCM460, HEK-293T, and MODE-K cells. Following 5 h of incubation, the supernatants were discarded, and the cells were collected for further analysis. Each experimental condition included three independent biological replicates, with each replicate derived from an independently cultured bacterial sample.

Metabolome sequencing and analysis

Liquid samples (stored at -80 °C) were thawed on ice and vortexed for 10 s. A 50 µL aliquot of each sample was mixed with 150 µL of extraction solution (acetonitrile: methanol = 1:4, V/V) containing an internal standard. The mixture was vortexed for 3 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min (4 °C). A 150 µL aliquot of the supernatant was collected, incubated at -20 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 3 min (4 °C). Finally, 120 µL of the supernatant was transferred for LC-MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples, prepared by pooling equal aliquots from all study samples, were processed using the same protocol and injected at regular intervals to monitor instrument stability. Metabolomic analysis was performed by Shanghai Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY Premier HSS T3 column (1.8 μm, 2.1 × 100 mm) at 40 °C. The mobile phases consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B). The gradient program was: 5% B at 0 min, increased to 20% B within 1 min, ramped to 99% B over 2 min, held for 1.5 min, and returned to 5% B within 0.1 min, followed by 1.4 min re-equilibration. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min with an injection volume of 3 µL. Mass spectrometric detection was conducted on a Q Exactive (QE) instrument equipped with an ESI source in both positive and negative modes. Full-scan MS (m/z 70–1000) was performed at a resolution of 60,000, followed by data-dependent MS/MS (ddMS²) for the top 10 precursor ions. Spray voltages were 3.8 kV (positive) and 3.4 kV (negative); sheath and auxiliary gas flow rates were 60 and 20, respectively. Raw data were converted to mzML format using ProteoWizard and processed via XCMS for peak detection and alignment. Missing values were handled using a combined strategy: features with > 50% missingness were imputed with one-fifth of the minimum value, while those with < 50% were imputed via the K-nearest neighbor (KNN) method. Signal drift was corrected using support vector regression (SVR). Metabolite annotation was performed using in-house and public databases, retaining features with an identification score > 0.5 and a QC CV < 0.3. Differential metabolites were finally identified based on VIP values from the OPLS-DA model.

RNA extraction and library construction

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent per the manufacturer’s instructions. Purity and concentration were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific), while integrity was assessed with an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Transcriptome libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V5 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, and sequencing was performed by Shanghai Ouyi Biotechnology Co.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Libraries were sequenced on the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform. For each experimental condition, three independent biological replicates (n = 3) were performed to ensure reproducibility. Raw reads in FASTQ format were processed using fastp to eliminate low-quality sequences, yielding clean reads. HISAT2 software was employed for reference genome mapping and FPKM-based gene expression quantification, while HTSeq-count determined gene read counts. Principal component analysis (PCA) in R (v3.2.0) assessed biological replicate consistency. Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2, which applies a statistical framework based on the negative binomial distribution. To control the false positive rate caused by multiple testing, raw P values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to estimate the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and |log2fold change|>1 were defined as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified significantly enriched functional categories.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells (n = 3) using total RNA extraction reagents (Tissue: Biosharp, BL1620A; Cells: Yeasen, 19221ES50), followed by DNase l treatment to eliminate genomic DNA contamination. After assessing RNA concentration and purity with a NanoDrop 2000, 1 ug of total RNA was reverse transcribed into first-strand cDNA using the iScript” cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad, 1708891). qRT-PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems 7500 system. The reaction mixture contained SYBR” GreenMaster Mix (ABl, A57156), specific primers, and cDNA templates. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95℃ for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95℃ for 15 s and 60℃ for 60 s. Melt curve analysis was performed post-amplification to verify product specificity. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene, and relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2^ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphs were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. the Student’s t-test was used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Data are shown as mean ± SD, with p < 0.05 considered significant.

Results

Comparative metabolomic profiling of BHI- and GAM-derived B. pseudocatenulatum-86 supernatants

To explore the impact of different nutritional conditions on the metabolic profile of B. pseudocatenulatum-86, non-targeted metabolomics analysis was performed on the supernatants of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 grown in BHI-derived sup and GAM-derived sup. Initially, Pearson correlation analysis of QC samples in both positive and negative ion modes showed that correlations between QC samples were near 1 (r = 0.9999) in positive ion mode, with similarly high correlations observed in negative ion mode (Figure S1A-B). These results indicate excellent stability and reproducibility of the analytical system, ensuring the reliability of subsequent differential metabolite data.

Volcano plot analysis revealed significant metabolic alterations under both culture conditions (Fig. 1A, B). Compared to their respective blank media controls, BHI-derived sup contained 693 upregulated and 771 downregulated metabolites, while GAM-derived sup contained 674 upregulated and 755 downregulated metabolites. Venn diagram analysis further identified 1,085 shared differential metabolites between the two groups (Fig. 1C). Notably, BHI-derived sup contained 406 unique differential metabolites (208 upregulated, 198 downregulated), whereas GAM-derived sup contained 371 unique differential metabolites (184 upregulated, 187 downregulated), suggesting that different nutritional environments induced distinct metabolic responses. Heatmap analysis of the top 20 significantly altered metabolites revealed clear enrichment patterns (Fig. 1D). Fatty acids and conjugates, as well as carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates, were primarily enriched in BHI-derived sup. In contrast, amino acids, peptides, and analogues, along with benzimidazoles, exhibited higher abundance in GAM-derived sup. These classification differences are consistent with the overall metabolic profile characteristics induced by the two media.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further elucidated the specificity of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 metabolic patterns under different conditions (Fig. 1E-F). In BHI-derived sup, differential metabolites were primarily enriched in pathways such as propanoate metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis/degradation, and β-alanine metabolism (Fig. 1E). In GAM-derived sup, metabolic activity was mainly concentrated in the biosynthesis of various antibiotics, nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, and galactose metabolism (Fig. 1F). In summary, the metabolic characteristics of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 are highly dependent on the components of the culture media used, exhibiting significant nutrient-driven differences.

To ensure that the observed transcriptomic alterations resulted from specific regulatory mechanisms rather than generalized cytotoxicity, we evaluated the impact of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 metabolites on host cell viability. CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that treatment with 5%–20% (v/v) of BHI- and GAM-derived supernatants for 24 h did not significantly affect the viability of MODE-K and NCM460 cells (survival rate > 95%). A slight reduction in viability (~ 80%–85%) was only observed at the 30% concentration (Figure S3A-B). Consequently, 20% (v/v) was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent experiments. At this dose, all tested cell lines (MODE-K, Henle-407, NCM460, and HEK-293T) maintained high viability (> 95%), confirming that the transcriptomic profiles represent specific signaling pathway modulations rather than non-specific secondary stress responses (Figure S4).

Effect of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 supernatant cultured in BHI and GAM media on gene expression in MODE-K Cells

B. pseudocatenulatum-86 was cultured in BHI and GAM media. The resulting supernatants were collected and applied to treat MODE-K cells, referred to as MB-86 (BHI) and MG-86 (GAM). Cell samples were then collected for transcriptome sequencing analysis.

DEGs in MODE-K cells were analyzed and visualized using volcano plots (Fig. 2A, B), Venn diagrams, and heat maps (Fig. 2C). DEGs were identified based on a |log2fold change|>1 and a P-value < 0.05. The analysis identified 299 significantly upregulated and 834 significantly downregulated genes in MB-86 group, whereas MG-86 group exhibited 724 significantly upregulated and 345 significantly downregulated genes. Venn diagram analysis identified 2,669 genes unique to MB-86 group and 3,089 genes unique to MG-86 group. Heat map analysis further examined the expression levels of these unique genes, revealing that genes significantly upregulated in MB-86 group exhibited opposite trends in MG-86 group, a pattern reciprocally observed for MG-86 group.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated significant differences between MB-86 and MG-86. In MODE-K cells treated with MB-86, DEGs were predominantly enriched in the cGMP-PKG signaling pathway (Fig. 2D). Conversely, the MG-86 group showed enrichment in lipid and atherosclerosis and fatty acid elongation (Fig. 2E). Venn diagram analysis identified 42 pathways uniquely enriched in the MB-86 group, including aldosterone synthesis, proximal tubule bicarbonate reclamation, calcium reabsorption, bile secretion, and DNA replication (Fig. 2F). The MG-86 group uniquely enriched 26 pathways, including fatty acid elongation, unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis, metabolism in cancer, sulfur relay system, and fructose/mannose metabolism (Fig. 2F).

Notably, pathways enriched in the MB-86 group predominantly fell under the “Organismal Systems” category, whereas those enriched in the MG-86 group were primarily associated with “Metabolism” (Fig. 2G). These findings underscore the distinct signaling pathway responses of MODE-K cells to the two supernatants. To validate the accuracy of the RNA-seq results, representative key genes specifically enriched in different experimental groups were selected for qRT-PCR validation. The results showed that in the DNA replication pathway specifically enriched in the MB-86 group, the key gene POLD4 was significantly upregulated in the MB-86 vs. MB-NC comparison. Similarly, in the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids pathway specifically enriched in the MG-86 group, the core gene HACD4 also exhibited a significant up-regulation trend in the MG-86 vs. MG-NC comparison (Figure S2A-B). These qRT-PCR validation results are highly consistent with the expression trends observed in the transcriptomic analysis, further confirming the reliability of the sequencing data.

PCA confirmed notable distinctions between the MB-86 and MG-86 experimental groups. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) accounted for 94.47% and 2.56% of the variance, respectively, and distinctly separated the two groups in the PCA plot (Fig. 2H). This analysis reinforces the distinct gene expression patterns and biological characteristics observed in MODE-K cells treated with MB-86 and MG-86 supernatants.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis demonstrated distinct patterns between the HB-86 and HG-86 groups. Discussions

The growth and accumulation of metabolites in bacteria are highly dependent on environmental conditions and media components, such as pH, carbon and nitrogen sources, growth factors, and minerals [22, 23]. In this study, we found that B. pseudocatenulatum-86 exhibited distinct metabolic profiles in BHI and GAM media. BHI is a highly nutritious medium, and its extremely high carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) ratio significantly influences microbial substrate utilization patterns [9]. Metabolomic results showed that fatty acids and conjugates, as well as carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates, were significantly enriched in the BHI-derived supernatant. This may be attributed to the high glucose concentration in BHI triggering the “Carbon Catabolite Repression” mechanism, leading the strain to prioritize efficient energy metabolism and carbon chain extension, which subsequently results in the substantial accumulation of fatty acid precursors and carbohydrate intermediates [24]. In contrast, GAM medium contains complex components such as yeast extract, liver extract, and bile salts, which more closely resemble the intestinal physiological microenvironment [25]. In our study, the GAM-derived supernatant was enriched with a large number of amino acids, peptides, and their analogues. This metabolic signature is closely linked to the abundant organic nitrogen sources available in the medium; the bacteria degrade complex environmental proteins and convert them into bioactive peptides and amino acid derivatives [26]. These findings suggest that when evaluating the metabolic functions of intestinal symbionts, the influence of media composition on metabolic pathway selection must be fully considered to ensure that experimental results possess accurate biological interpretability and physiological relevance.

Bifidobacterium metabolites play a crucial role in host immunomodulation, metabolic homeostasis, and the maintenance of physiological functions. Their specific effects vary depending on the composition of the culture medium [27]. Bifidobacteria typically utilize glucose and fructose through the ‘bifid’ pathway to produce ATP, acetate, and lactate. Other sugars are degraded by additional glycosyl hydrolases, and their metabolic adaptations depend on the availability of carbon sources [28]. Acetate produced by Bifidobacterium has been shown to enhance epithelial cell-mediated intestinal defense, protecting the host from Escherichia coli infection [27]. Acetate and lactate lower the gut pH, which helps prevent microbiota imbalance and pathogen growth. Lactate also crosses the blood-brain barrier, acting as a neuromodulator in the brain [29, 30]. Bifidobacterium produces high levels of conjugated linoleic acid, which has a variety of health benefits, including potent anti-inflammatory, immunomodulatory, anti-obesity, and anti-cancer effects [31, 32]. This study revealed that the culture environment (BHI and GAM media) significantly influenced the metabolite composition of B. pseudocatenulatum-86, which subsequently led to distinct regulatory effects of its secretome on host cell gene expression.

KEGG enrichment analysis showed that in the same host cells, the DEGs induced by the BHI group were primarily enriched in pathways related to “Organismal Systems.” Previous studies have indicated that while BHI medium promotes rapid biomass growth through high nutrient loading, this explosive proliferation often reshapes microbial metabolic patterns, leading to the substantial accumulation of organic acids such as lactate and acetate [33–35]. In contrast, the signaling pathways enriched in the GAM group were significantly concentrated in the “Metabolism” and “Environmental Information Processing” categories. This suggests that in the more physiologically relevant GAM environment, the Bifidobacterium secretome exerts a stronger regulatory effect on the fundamental metabolic processes of the host. In alignment with the study by Wu et al., strains isolated under similar nutritional conditions exhibited robust metabolic activity, with over half of their functional genes involved in carbohydrate, amino acid, and energy metabolism [36]. These findings are highly consistent with our results, further confirming the superiority of GAM medium in inducing the metabolic regulatory functions of commensal bacteria.

Furthermore, this study reveals that under identical culture conditions, Bifidobacterium metabolites induced significantly differentiated gene expression profiles across various cell lines, while signaling pathways exhibited high levels of heterogeneity. This finding suggests that the biological effects of Bifidobacterium are largely contingent upon the host cell type, demonstrating pronounced environment-dependent characteristics. However, despite these transcriptomic variations, a striking commonality was observed across all host cells: the enriched KEGG pathways were predominantly concentrated in the “Metabolism” category. This phenomenon highlights the functional versatility of the bacterial secretome, as its components can exert specific regulatory effects tailored to host cells of different origins. Moreover, the universal up-regulation of metabolism-related pathways suggests that certain metabolic constituents—such as core nutrients, metabolic intermediates, or other bioactive factors—may directly stimulate fundamental cellular metabolic activities. The coexistence of specificity and commonality observed in MODE-K, Henle-407, NCM460, and HEK-293T cells indicates that the biological effects of Bifidobacterium possess both distinct cell-type dependency and robust consistency in their macro-regulatory orientation.

This study provides significant insights into the experimental design of future gut microbiota research. First, it challenges the arbitrary selection of culture media in traditional studies and emphasizes that nutritional composition is the core of experimental design. Second, it advocates for multi-dimensional cell model systems to overcome the inherent biological limitations and evaluation biases of single-cell lines. Crucially, our findings serve as a universal caution for the field: microbial functions are not static traits but dynamic outcomes of the ‘microbe-medium-host’ triangle. Relying on a single medium or cell model may lead to biased or incomplete conclusions, potentially masking the true biological potential of commensal species. Consequently, the focus of future research will increasingly shift toward exploring how specific media components precisely reshape the functional landscape of gut bacteria, using multi-model validation to ensure the reproducibility and translational value of probiotic evaluations.

Despite the systematic investigation of the regulatory effects of Bifidobacterium metabolites on host cells under varying culture conditions, several limitations remain. First, this study primarily relies on in vitro cellular models. Although validation across multiple cell lines has enhanced the robustness of our conclusions, the in vitro environment cannot fully replicate the complex physiological dynamics of the intestinal tract, such as peristalsis, interspecies microbial competition, and systemic feedback from the host circulatory system. Consequently, we plan to employ animal models in future studies to further validate our findings within more complex biological systems. Second, this research focused on characterizing the differential genes and pathways expressed under specific cell types or culture media, leaving the consistently co-regulated genes and pathways without in-depth discussion. We intend to perform extensive data mining and functional characterization of these common regulatory elements in future work. Lastly, although our metabolic profiling identified key differential metabolites of B. pseudocatenulatum-86 under various nutritional conditions, the extreme complexity of its secretome currently precludes the establishment of precise causal links between individual metabolic components and specific host differentially expressed genes. Consequently, we have designated the direct identification of specific bioactive metabolites as a priority for our next stage of research, which will utilize multi-omics integration of metabolomics and transcriptomics to pinpoint functional molecules. Future studies involving cell intervention assays with purified candidate metabolites will be essential to validate the exact molecules driving these transcriptomic alterations.
