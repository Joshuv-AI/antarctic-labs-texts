Consensus-based research priorities for post-collision care in the Western Cape province of South Africa
Authors: Naseef Abdullah, Tim Nutbeam, Colleen J. Saunders, Craig Wylie, Nigel Lang, Willem Stassen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionRoad traffic injuries constitute a significant global health burden, causing 1.3 million deaths and 50 million injuries annually, with 92 % of fatalities occurring in low-and middle-income countries (LMICs). Despite this disproportionate impact, research priorities in post-collision care often reflect high-income country contexts, creating a critical misalignment between evidence generation and contextual realities in LMICs.MethodsThis mixed-methods study employed a three-phase approach to identify research priorities for post-collision care in the Western Cape, South Africa. Phase 1 consisted of a comprehensive literature review to identify preliminary thematic areas and research questions. Phase 2 involved a stakeholder engagement workshop using modified nominal group techniques (NGT) with purposively sampled participants representing emergency medical services, fire services, law enforcement, community members and academia. Phase 3 entailed systematic prioritisation, where participants independently scored each theme and associated research questions on a Likert scale.ResultsEight thematic domains were identified and ranked in order of priority. EMS safety (highest priority), communication and coordination, public awareness and prevention, transportation and access to care, first-responder capabilities, training implementation, resource optimisation, disaster and mass casualty management, and specialised care accessibility. The highest-ranked individual research question concerned the minimum set of practical skills and resources required by first responders to effectively provide immediate post-collision care. Technological integration emerged as a cross-cutting priority across multiple themes.ConclusionThe study represents the first published systematic approach to identifying post-collision care research priorities in South Africa. Diverging from previous exercises that emphasise advanced interventions or system integration, this study highlights foundational challenges of EMS safety and communication as top priorities, reflecting the contextual realities of emergency service delivery in South Africa. The findings provide a strategic roadmap for researchers, funders, and policymakers to direct resources toward questions with maximal potential to improve post-collision care and strengthen health systems in similar LMIC contexts.

Introduction

Road traffic injuries (RTI) present a large health burden globally, accounting for >1.3 million deaths and up to 50 million injuries annually [1]. This burden disproportionately affects younger populations and is the leading cause of death for individuals aged 5 to 29 years, with profound implications for economic productivity and societal development [1]. The burden of road injuries is unevenly distributed, with >92 % of fatalities occurring in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) despite these nations having fewer vehicles per capita than high-income countries (HICs) [[1], [2], [3]]. The risk of road fatalities in LMICs is three times higher than in HICs, with Africa having the highest fatality rate of 19 deaths per 100,000 population; this is exacerbated by infrastructural challenges and delayed access to definitive care [1,4].

Despite the magnitude of the road injury burden a critical misalignment exists between research priorities and contextual realities. Historical patterns indicate that research priorities in post-collision care have predominantly been influenced by the context of HICs, often overlooking the unique needs and constraints of LMICs [[5], [6], [7]]. This limits the relevance and applicability of evidence emanating from HICs to LMICs. The prioritisation of research topics is often fragmented and influenced by competing agendas rather than being guided by the perspectives of those directly affected by road injury, including patients, emergency medical service (EMS) providers and policymakers. This misalignment creates a critical gap in understanding and addressing the most pressing issues in post-collision care within LMICs. Structured and inclusive approaches to priority setting are essential to ensure that research aligns with local needs and maximises its impact [8].

Priority Setting Partnerships (PSP) have emerged as a valuable method for identifying and ranking research priorities through collaborative engagement between diverse stakeholders [9,10]. PSP is the process of involving stakeholders at the beginning of the research process when deciding what to research [10]. This approach ensures that research focuses on questions that matter the most to patients and the healthcare professionals who directly experience the challenges associated with injury in LMICs. They bring together diverse perspectives, foster consensus, and create a research agenda that is both contextually relevant and actionable. By integrating expert knowledge with lived experiences, PSPs enhance the legitimacy, transparency, and practical relevance of research recommendations [11]. PSPs have been widely used in other healthcare domains, but their application to road injury and emergency care in LMICs remains limited [9,10,12,13].

This study reports on the findings of a PSP conducted in the Western Cape, South Africa, focusing specifically on research priorities in post-collision care. Leveraging the Western Cape’s established community-based first responder program, we incorporated diverse perspectives on post-collision care that extend beyond traditional prehospital frameworks [14,15]. Through the engagement of a diverse group of stakeholders, including EMS providers, fire services, law enforcement agencies, researchers, and community representatives, this PSP identified key domains where research could potentially yield substantial improvements in prehospital and early hospital care for road injury victims. This work represents the initial phase of a long-term collaborative initiative aimed at addressing local significant priorities. The priorities emerging from this process will provide strategic direction for researchers, funders, and policymakers to direct resources toward questions with the maximal potential to improve patient outcomes and strengthen health systems in LMICs.

Methods

This three-phase mixed-methods study was conducted between December 2024 and February 2025. It employed the nominal group technique (NGT) to identify and prioritise research questions related to post-collision care in LMICs, with a particular focus on the Western Cape province of South Africa. Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Cape Town (HREC 083/2025).

Phase 1

We conducted a comprehensive literature review using PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases. The search encompassed all aspects of out-of-hospital emergency care for RTI in LMICs, with a particular emphasis on South Africa. Following this review, we identified key thematic areas and formulated preliminary research questions for each theme based on the evidence gaps.

Phase 2

We facilitated a stakeholder engagement workshop, using a modified NGT to validate, expand and refine the themes and research questions that were identified during the literature review. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling via professional networks and established relationships with stakeholder organisations across the Western Cape. The sampling strategy aimed to balance clinical expertise with operational and community experience, ensuring representation across the post-collision care continuum. Inclusion criteria ensured representation from (i) Emergency medical dispatch, (ii) public and (iii) private EMS providers, (iv) rescue technicians, (v) fire services, (vi) law enforcement officers, (vii) community members, (viii) EMS management, and (ix) academia. Participants were invited via email to attend the in-person workshop and were asked to provide informed consent prior to the commencement of the workshop. We targeted approximately equal representation from key stakeholder groups, with slight oversampling from public EMS (52 % of final sample) given their predominant role in emergency response within the Western Cape context. The final sample composition reflected both service availability and willingness to participate, with urban settings comprising 48 % of participants, mixed urban and rural settings comprising 38 %, and exclusively rural settings 14 %. This distribution was considered appropriate as it adequately captured the diversity of operational context within the Western Cape while ensuring sufficient representation from each stakeholder category to contribute meaningfully to consensus development.

The workshop followed a structured NGT protocol modified from De Ven and Delbelcq’s original approach to exploratory healthcare studies [16]. NGT is a consensus-based method directed at problem-solving, idea generation or determining priorities [17]. During this phase, NGT was employed to facilitate the convergence of opinions on the post-collision care priorities among the diverse participants. As depicted in Appendix 1, this process included presenting the seven themes and corresponding research questions derived from the literature review to those in attendance, after which participants were given 20 min to silently reflect on the questions and consolidate their views related to each of the themes and research questions. Participants were then asked, in a round-robin fashion, to present a single idea related to their views, during which they would formulate additional ideas or modifications to the proposed questions. This process continued until saturation was reached (defined as data information redundancy) and allowed for the introduction of new themes or research questions [18]. Ideas were discussed and clarified, and similar concepts were consolidated based on consensus among participants.

Throughout the engagements, three of the researchers (WS, CW and NA) made notes on the discussion points raised by participants. These notes were consolidated and categorised using simple descriptive content analysis.

Phase 3

During the final phase of the priority-setting workshop, a systematic procedure was implemented to establish research priorities. Using a Microsoft Forms survey (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, United States), participants were asked to independently score each theme and associated research question on a scale of 1 to 7, where 7 indicated the highest level of importance. This was designed as an importance rating exercise rather than a ranking system (i.e. ordering of preferences), allowing multiple research questions to receive equally high ratings if participants deemed them similarly important.

Priority scores for each theme and research question were derived by summing the numerical scores assigned to each by all participants. The theme with the highest cumulative score was designated the highest priority, with the remaining themes ranked in descending order. Within each theme, individual research questions were similarly ranked based on their cumulative scores. Additionally, median scores and interquartile ranges were calculated for each question to provide further insight into stakeholder preferences. Median scores represent the central tendency of importance ratings, while IQRs demonstrate consensus levels – with narrower ranges indicating stronger agreement among participants. Medians were used to rank research questions with tied cumulative scores.

Results

Phase 1: Literature review

Following a review of the literature, we identified seven themes related to post-collision care in LMICs. These themes, definitions and specific research questions within each theme, as derived from the literature, are described as a Supplementary table in Appendix 1.

Phase 2: Nominal group technique

A total of 21 stakeholders participated in the PSP workshop, while 20 (95 %) completed Phase 3 (ranking). The majority of participants (n = 11, 52 %) were from public sector EMS, followed by private sector EMS (n = 3, 14 %) and community representatives (n = 3, 10 %). Academia, fire services, law enforcement, rescue services, and the South African Police Services each comprised 5 % (n = 1) of the total. Nearly half of the participants (n = 10, 48 %) were from urban settings, while 38 % (n = 8) operated in both urban and rural areas. The remainder of the participants (n = 3, 14 %) were exclusively based in rural settings of the Western Cape province.

Three categories were derived from the qualitative data: 1) Communication is fragmented, 2) Community engagement builds trust, and 3) Providers have a need for training and post-incident support.

Communication is fragmented

The stakeholder engagement revealed that the emergency response to RTIs is characterised by profound communication challenges and systems fragmentation. Critical gaps in inter-service communication and coordination were identified, highlighting a disconnect between various emergency services, including EMS, law enforcement, and the community. The lack of a unified communication mechanism emerged as a significant barrier to inter-service communication and collaboration, with stakeholders emphasising the need for centralised coordination, standardised protocols, and collaborative approaches that transcend traditional organisational boundaries.

Community engagement builds trust

Community engagement and trust-building were identified as fundamental to improving emergency response effectiveness. The workshop participants noted that there was an erosion of trust between emergency services and local communities, advocating for the co-creation of awareness programmes, shared responsibility models, and more transparent communication strategies. This approach goes beyond traditional public awareness campaigns, proposing a transformative model of community participation that includes advisory boards, collaborative knowledge development, and systematic approaches to prevention that recognise the complex social dynamics underlying road traffic incidents and emergency responses.

Training and post-incident support

Training and capacity building emerged as a critical discussion point, with stakeholders highlighting the importance of comprehensive, multi-service preparedness strategies. The workshop revealed significant disparities in first responder capabilities, pointing to the need for formalised training programs, credentialing mechanisms, and holistic support systems that extend beyond immediate technical skills. Participants stressed the importance of post-incident support, wellness programs, and creating a more integrated approach to emergency service preparation that recognises the psychological and professional development needs of all parties involved in post-collision care.

Discussion

This is the first published systematic approach to identify priorities for post-collision care research in South Africa. The PSP followed a process that has previously been successfully applied in both HIC and LMICs settings [13,40]. This process identified and ranked eight thematic domains and associated research questions for post-collision care in the Western Cape, South Africa. By integrating perspectives from diverse stakeholders across EMS and community representatives, our study demonstrated the value of the NGT approach in generating contextually specific and stakeholder-informed research priorities. Priorities diverged from those derived through literature review alone, thus providing a robust foundation for developing a regionally appropriate research agenda.

The highest priority areas identified were EMS safety, communication and coordination, and public awareness and prevention. The most highly ranked individual question concerned the minimum practical skills and resources required by first responders to provide effective immediate post-collision care, highlighting the contextual salience of tier 1 systems in LMICs. Another notable finding was the consistent emphasis on technological integration across multiple themes. From digital technologies in dispatch systems to technology-enhanced learning systems, stakeholders recognised the transformative potential of technological innovations in addressing resource constraints and improving emergency care delivery. However, our results also illuminate the paradoxical nature of technology adoption in the LMIC context, where implementation barriers, including inadequate infrastructure, prohibitive costs, sustainability challenges, and reliability concerns in resource-limited settings, can limit effectiveness. This duality positions technology simultaneously as an enabler of system improvement and a potential implementation barrier, highlighting the critical need for a contextually appropriate solution. These findings suggest that future research priorities should emphasise the development and rigorous evaluation of low-cost, robust technological interventions specifically designed for LMIC operating conditions. Such an approach aligns with established health system strengthening frameworks and warrants specific attention in future research initiatives targeting post-collision care improvement in similar LMIC settings.

A significant strength of this study is the structured and inclusive approach taken to engage a wide spectrum of stakeholders, including emergency services, rescue practitioners, police officers and community representatives. The use of a modified NGT ensured that research priorities were not merely academic constructs but reflected the lived experiences of those directly involved in post-collision care. The study also benefits from the triangulation of evidence sources, drawing both from literature and lived experience.

Previous priority-setting exercises in trauma and emergency care that have been conducted in HICs and even locally in South Africa have prioritised issues such as advanced prehospital interventions, quality determinations or system integration strategies [6,12,13]. In contrast, this PSP emphasises the foundational challenges of EMS safety and communication in the Western Cape, South Africa. A recent PSP for musculoskeletal trauma in sub-Saharan Africa [7] identified resource constraints and workforce development as central themes, which aligns with this study’s prioritisation of training implementation and first-responder capabilities. However, this study differs in that it elevates EMS safety as the top research priority, an area that is rarely prioritised elsewhere. This divergence likely reflects the contextual realities of service delivery in South Africa, where violence against EMS personnel and unsafe working conditions are well-documented [[41], [42], [43], [44]]. That “scene safety” emerged as a dominant theme also highlights differences in the lived experience of emergency responders in LMICs compared to HICs, where this issue may be less pronounced or differently conceptualised. These findings reinforce the need for a contextually grounded research agenda for post-collision care in LMICs. The prioritisation of EMS safety underscores the acute challenges faced by frontline responders—not just in terms of clinical decision-making but also personal security and psychological resilience. For policymakers, this highlights the importance of investing in systems that safeguard EMS personnel, standardise scene safety practices, and address violence and trauma exposure.

Similarly, the high prioritisation of communication and public awareness suggests that interventions to improve interoperability between services and to empower bystanders could yield substantial system-level benefits. These findings also underline the importance of integrating low-cost, scalable solutions—such as digital platforms for communication or community-based training—into national and provincial EMS strategies.

Emergency Care providers and researchers should note the wide variability in priority ratings within some domains (e.g. communication), suggesting areas of consensus as well as contention that merit further exploration. Importantly, the emergence of disaster and mass casualty management as a de novo theme points to a recognised but under-researched area that may require dedicated inquiry, particularly in light of the prevalence of high-casualty events in South Africa [45].

The identified priorities provide a strategic roadmap for researchers, funders, and policymakers in the Western Cape and potentially other similar LMIC contexts. The pronounced emphasis on EMS safety, communication, and public awareness indicates that future research should be contextually sensitive.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, despite the intentional diversity in sampling, the modest sample size (n = 21) may have potentially underrepresented key demographics, particularly rural community members. Second, the prioritisation process required simplifying complex research domains, which could obscure nuanced priorities. Thirdly, while participants provided independent ratings to identify important areas of focus, it is important to note that this did not constitute a formal ranking exercise - rather, it allowed for the identification of areas with perceived importance when many items received similar consideration. Finally, the study focused on a single province; although findings are likely relevant to similar LMIC contexts, generalisability to all settings should be approached with caution. Nonetheless, these research priorities are essential for the design of future research studies. This PSP should inform subsequent funding applications and foster collaborative initiatives addressing the substantial burden of RTIs in South Africa. Future research should aim to replicate, validate and/or adapt this process across other provinces and LMIC settings while investigating implementation strategies for high-priority research questions in real-world contexts.

Conclusion

This Priority Setting Partnership has identified key research priorities for post-collision care in the Western Cape, reflecting the realities and challenges faced by frontline providers and communities in this setting. The emergence of themes such as scene safety and disaster response highlights the contextual distinctiveness of post-collision care within the South African context. These findings offer a Sub-Saharan African relevant and stakeholder-driven roadmap for researchers, funders, and policymakers and serve as a foundation for future collaborative studies aimed at understanding and enhancing the healthcare provision of RTIs in LMICs. Subsequent efforts should focus on translating these priorities into funded research projects, capacity-building initiatives, and policy developments that reflect the needs and lived experiences of those delivering and receiving post-collision care.
