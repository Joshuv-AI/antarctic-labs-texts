Single-Stage Revision Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction With Concomitant Lateral Extra-articular Tenodesis, High Tibial Osteotomy, and Medial Femoral Condyle Osteochondral Allograft Transplantation
Authors: Tomás F. Vega, Thomas E. Moran, Napatpong Thamrongskulsiri, Logan D. Moews, Gabriel Octavio Pérez Lloveras, Felipe Casanova, Jacob T. Morgan, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Combined ligamentous insufficiency, malalignment, and focal cartilage lesions in young, active patients represent a complex reconstructive challenge. Recent advances in patient-specific instrumentation, osteotomy planning, and biologic grafting techniques have enabled single-stage surgical solutions that address both instability and joint preservation.Indications:The patient presented with grade 2B Lachman and rotational instability after a previous anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (ACLR). Indications for this combined approach included (1) ACL graft insufficiency with persistent rotational instability, treated with revision ACLR and lateral extra-articular tenodesis; (2) a 4 cm2 focal chondral defect of the trochlea, treated with osteochondral allograft transplantation (OCA); and (3) 6° varus malalignment in the setting of revision ACLR and cartilage restoration, treated with medial opening wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO). A concomitant lateral meniscal root tear contributing to rotational instability was managed with root repair.Technique Description:Preoperative 3-dimensional computed tomography imaging with patient-specific instrumentation guided the osteotomy and tunnel trajectories to avoid interference between fixation constructs. After graft harvest and diagnostic arthroscopy, the femoral ACL tunnel was reamed, and the tibial tunnel was created through the patient-specific guide. The OCA was sized, harvested, and implanted flush to the trochlea. The medial opening wedge HTO (1° valgus overcorrection) was stabilized with a precontoured plate and tricortical bone wedges. The lateral extra-articular tenodesis graft, harvested as a 10 × 80 mm iliotibial band strip, was passed deep to the lateral collateral ligament and fixed at the proximal Kaplan's fiber region with a 4.75-mm polyetheretherketone suture anchor in 90° of flexion and neutral rotation.Results:Early follow-up demonstrated restored coronal alignment, well-positioned tunnels, and early osteotomy healing. The patient achieved full range of motion by 3 months and progressed with strengthening. The literature supports that combined ACLR, alignment correction, and cartilage restoration procedures restore stability and alignment while preserving native knee function, with 10-year studies reporting durable improvements in patient-reported outcomes.Discussion/Conclusion:Single-stage revision ACL reconstruction with concomitant LET, medial opening wedge HTO, and osteochondral allograft transplantation is a feasible and effective strategy for young, active patients with combined ligamentous insufficiency, malalignment, and focal cartilage lesions, offering joint preservation with early functional recovery.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. If the individual may be identifiable, the author(s) has included a statement of release or other written form of approval from the patient(s) with this submission for publication.

Video Transcript

In this video, we present a single-stage revision of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, lateral extra-articular tenodesis, medial opening wedge high tibial osteotomy, trochlear osteochondral allograft transplantation, and lateral meniscal root repair. The procedure was performed by Dr Jorge Chahla and his team at Midwest Orthopaedics at Rush.

Background

Innovations in the approach and surgical techniques for biologic knee preservation have provided solutions for young, active patients with combined knee ligamentous insufficiency, meniscal injury, focal chondral defects, and malalignment. These cases remain challenging for surgeons, given the complexity of clinical indications and the dynamic interplay of surgical variables.

Indications

The patient is an active, healthy 33-year-old male who presented with medial-sided knee pain and subjective instability after a twisting injury while jumping out of the back of a truck. His past surgical history was relevant for a previous anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (ACLR) with soft tissue allograft approximately 10 years ago at an outside hospital.

On examination, notable findings included a positive patellar grind, medial joint line tenderness to palpation, grade 2B Lachman, and a 2+ pivot shift. Radiographs demonstrated well-preserved joint space, previous tibial and femoral ACL tunnels without significant widening, 6° varus hip-knee-ankle angle, 92° lateral distal femoral angle, 86° medial proximal tibial angle, and 0.4° joint line convergence angle. A magnetic resonance imaging study was notable for an intact, but vertically oriented, ACL graft, a trochlear cartilage defect, and postmeniscectomy changes in the medial meniscus.

A diagnostic arthroscopy was performed to guide definitive surgical management. This identified an intact, but deficient and vertically oriented ACL graft, a grade 4 2 × 2 cm2 chondral defect of the trochlea, and a lateral meniscal root tear. Focal grade 2 changes to the medial femoral condyle were present, and the medial meniscus was found to have 75% of its periphery intact.

Given this patient's clinical, radiographic, and arthroscopic findings, he was indicated for a revision ACLR with bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft, lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET), lateral meniscal root repair, trochlear osteochondral allograft transplantation (OCA), and medial opening wedge high tibial osteotomy (HTO).

Importantly, given the interrelated nature of the patient's complex pathology, the senior author (J.C.) opted to perform these surgeries in a single surgical session rather than in stages. A milieu of ACL deficiency, rotational instability secondary to likely injury to the anterolateral complex, meniscal root tearing, and malalignment creates a specific set of conditions in which all factors must be addressed to protect the others. 4 A clinically unstable, varus-aligned knee, such as this patient's, with a 2B Lachman and 2+ pivot shift and already extensive chondral and meniscal damage, exposes the patient to ongoing intra-articular insults to the menisci and articular cartilage. The senior author considers that performing a diagnostic arthroscopy is key to establishing an accurate surgical plan to comprehensively address both the injury that brought the patient to the clinic and the structural characteristics that predisposed them to developing these conditions in the first place. Because of the risk of interference with a combined medial opening wedge HTO and revision ACLR, 3-dimensional reconstructed computed tomography scans of the right knee were obtained for surgical planning guided by patient-specific instrumentation (BodyCad). An osteotomy with 7° of medial opening for 1° valgus overcorrection was planned, with the osteotomy guide providing the trajectory for the creation of the ACL tibial tunnel to avoid interference with screws utilized for plate fixation.

Technique Description

BPTB Autograft Harvest

A longitudinal incision was made, extending from the proximal patella to the distal aspect of the tibial tubercle. A BPTB graft was harvested from the central third of the patellar tendon, with 10 × 20 mm and 10 × 25 mm patellar and tibial bone blocks, respectively.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

Next, standard anterolateral and anteromedial portals were created within the incision, and a diagnostic arthroscopy confirmed a complete tear of the previous ACL graft, a posterior root tear of the lateral meniscus, and a grade 4 trochlear cartilage lesion.

Femoral Notch Preparation

After debriding the previous ACL graft with the use of a 5.5-mm bone cutting shaver (Arthrex), a microfracture awl was utilized to mark the anatomic footprint of the anteromedial bundle of the ACL in 90° of flexion. The knee was subsequently hyperflexed to 120°. A 2.4-mm beath pin was advanced through a 7 mm-offset over-the-top guide (Arthrex), which ensures 2 mm of back wall preservation. The guide pin was subsequently overreamed with a 10-mm acorn reamer (Arthrex) to a depth of 20 mm. A No. 2 Ethibond (Ethicon) passing suture was then passed through the tunnel with the use of the beath pin and left in place for later graft passage. 1

Lateral Meniscal Root Repair

Next, with the leg in “figure-4” position, the anatomic footprint of the lateral meniscus root was debrided with the use of a ring curette (Smith & Nephew) and an arthroscopic shaver (Arthrex). A laterality- and side-specific, curved meniscal root aiming guide (Smith & Nephew) was then placed within the joint through the anteromedial portal onto the anatomic posterior root insertion. The bullet of the guide was placed so that the external aperture of the tunnel would emerge proximally on the anterolateral tibia to avoid interference with the planned HTO and tibial ACL tunnels. The 2.4-mm guide pin and sheath were then advanced into the joint in transtibial fashion. A self-capturing suture passer (FIRSTPASS MINI; Smith & Nephew) was then utilized to pass 2 braided sutures (ULTRABRAID; Smith & Nephew) through the lateral meniscal posterior root in a simple fashion, approximately 4 to 5 mm from their medial edge. A nitinol wire is then advanced through the transtibial tunnel, retrieved from the anterolateral portal using a looped retriever (Smith & Nephew), and used to shuttle the braided repair sutures through the transtibial tunnel. Fixation of the root repair was deferred until later in the case, and the sheath was left in place to aid in visualization of the transtibial tunnel throughout the remainder of the case.

Arthrotomy

Next, the anteromedial tibial surface was exposed subperiosteally and prepared in anticipation of the HTO. A standard medial parapatellar arthrotomy was then made to perform the trochlear OCA.

Trochlear Socket Preparation

The requisite plug size is measured using a cannulated sizer (Arthrex), ensuring that the entire chondral defect is contained. A 2.4-mm guide pin is then advanced through the cannulated sizer into subchondral bone in the center of the defect before being scored with the recipient harvester. After scoring, a coring reamer is used to drill a recipient site socket of a maximum depth of 7 to 8 mm under a stream of continuous cold saline irrigation to avoid heat necrosis of the surrounding subchondral bone and cartilage. 2 The recipient socket is then marked and measured for depth at the 12-, 3-, 6-, and 9 o'clock positions.

Next, the corresponding area on the donor allograft is outlined with the same cannulated sizer used to mark the recipient site before harvesting, and the donor allograft is similarly cored under a continuous stream of saline irrigation. The subchondral bone of the donor plug is then trimmed to match the corresponding depth at the 12-, 3-, 6-, and 9-o’clock positions of the recipient site before making a crosscut. Marrow elements are removed using a triple-antibiotic solution pulse lavage (Arthrex) and compressed carbon dioxide. Lastly, the donor allograft plug is manually placed in the recipient socket, ensuring correct orientation, using a tamp to achieve a flush fit while avoiding mechanical injury to the graft.

High Tibial Osteotomy

After placing a radiolucent retractor (Arthrex) posteriorly along the tibia to protect the neurovascular bundle, the patient-specific guide (BodyCAD) was placed into its templated position on the anteromedial tibia and pinned in place. Fluoroscopy confirmed appropriate placement of the guide in accordance with the preoperative plan. A 2.5-mm drill was then advanced through each templated aiming guide in sequence, with the drill depth corresponding to the depth-stop bit in the aiming guide. Next, a broad flat osteotome was advanced through the anterior, middle, and posterior aspects of the guide to the preplanned depths under fluoroscopic visualization. After confirmation of the osteotomy mobility, the osteotomy was gradually distracted with a calibrated spreader to the templated correction. After confirming the coronal and sagittal correction on fluoroscopy, a precontoured plate (BodyCAD) was placed centrally along the anteromedial tibia in the templated position. A patient-specific drill guide with templated depth stops based upon the desired screw length was then placed along the plate. After drilling, three 6.5-mm fully threaded cancellous and three 4.5-mm cortical titanium alloy screws (BodyCAD) were placed through the plate for fixation proximal and distal to the osteotomy, respectively. The osteotomy gap was filled with tricortical bone wedges before taking a final radiograph.

Tibial Tunnel Creation and Graft Passage

Next, attention turned to the tibial ACL tunnel. A 2.4-mm guidewire was advanced through the patient-specific drill guide (BodyCAD) into the anatomic insertion of the ACL on the tibia, and subsequently overreamed to 10 mm. The BPTB graft was then passed into the femoral tunnel in standard fashion and fixed using a 7 × 20 mm titanium interference screw (Arthrex).

After cycling the knee to remove creep from the construct, tibial fixation was completed with a 9 × 20 mm titanium interference screw (Arthrex) in maximal extension. 1

The lateral meniscal root repair was then secured in tension with a suture button (Endobutton; Smith & Nephew) over the anterolateral tibial cortex before turning our attention to LET.

Lateral Extra-articular Tenodesis

A 5-cm longitudinal incision was made along the distal aspect of the iliotibial (IT) band. A 10 mm-wide incision was made within the central aspect of the IT band. 3 The limb of tissue was then transversely cut proximally to harvest an 8 cm central strip for the LET graft. The graft was then passed deep to the lateral collateral ligament in retrograde fashion. The proximal origin of the Kaplan fibers was marked with electrocautery, and a pilot hole was drilled angled 20° proximal and 20° anterior to avoid convergence with the femoral ACL tunnel before fixating the LET graft with a polyetheretherketone suture anchor (SwiveLock 4.75 mm; Arthrex) with the knee in 90° of flexion and neutral rotation in that location. 5

Potential Complications

Potential complications include bleeding, infection, arthrofibrosis, surgical site pain, tunnel convergence, compartment syndrome, graft failure, peroneal nerve injury, and lateral cortex fracture.

Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation initially prioritizes protection of meniscal and cartilage procedures before transitioning to standard ACL protocols. Patients are nonweightbearing for the first 4 weeks but begin range of motion (ROM) and quadriceps activation on postoperative day 1. From weeks 4 to 6, they achieve full ROM and begin toe-touch weightbearing with the brace locked in extension.

Between weeks 6 and 12, patients progress to full weightbearing, wean off the brace, and start controlled strengthening and stationary bike work. By weeks 12 to 16, they incorporate low-impact plyometrics and sport-specific exercises. Functional testing between 6 and 9 months helps guide return to sport.

Results

At the 3-month follow-up, the patient had regained a full ROM and was progressing in physical therapy. Postoperative mechanical alignment radiographs demonstrated successful 1° valgus overcorrection of the previous varus deformity and well-positioned tunnel and graft, with an HTO plate that was nondisplaced and showed evidence of early healing. No fractures or dislocations were noted.

Combined ligamentous insufficiency, meniscal and cartilage lesions, and malalignment represent a complex surgical problem in young and active patients seeking joint preservation. Furthermore, heterogeneity in treatment indications and patient-specific factors present challenges in understanding the short- and long-term outcomes of biologic knee reconstruction. There is a relative paucity of studies examining clinical outcomes of these combined procedures. However, mid- to long-term case series examining combined ACLR and medial opening wedge HTO have demonstrated significant functional improvement for patients and high rates of native knee survivorship.

Schuster et al 7 reported outcomes of combined ACLR, HTO, and abrasion arthroplasty or microfracture in a series of 21 patients (mean age, 47.3 years) with combined ACL insufficiency, varus alignment, and full-thickness chondral defects. At a mean of 12 years postoperatively, the mean International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) score improved from 47 preoperatively to 70 at final follow-up. Furthermore, no patients underwent conversion to arthroplasty.

Schneider et al 6 also reported outcomes of a series of 35 patients (mean age, 39 years) and 36 knees who underwent combined ACLR with BPTB autograft and medial opening wedge HTO for anterior instability in the setting of early medial compartment arthritis. At the mean final follow-up of 10 years, the authors reported mean IKDC and Lysholm scores of 71.8 and 82, respectively. Mechanical axis correction was from 4.2° of varus to 0.8° of valgus at final follow-up. Also, 22 of 36 (61%) knees demonstrated no progression of tibiofemoral arthritis, and 11 of 35 (31%) patients returned to sports at a similar competitive level.

Discussion/Conclusion

Single-stage revision ACLR with concomitant LET, medial opening wedge HTO, and osteochondral allograft transplantation is a feasible and effective strategy for young, active patients with combined ligamentous insufficiency, malalignment, and focal cartilage lesions, offering joint preservation with early functional recovery.
