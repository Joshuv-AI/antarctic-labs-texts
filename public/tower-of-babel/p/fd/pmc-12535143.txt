The co-regulation of HPV infection by vaginal microbiota and the immune system
Authors: Li Zhuoyao, Wang Xiaohan, Miao Xinyu, Zhang Songling
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection is the most critical risk factor for cervical cancer. The vaginal microbiota and the host immune status are both closely related to HPV infection. The vaginal microbiota regulates multiple immune signaling pathways. Conversely, the immune status also affects the stability of the microbiota. The two interact with each other and jointly regulate the outcome of HPV infection. Establishing a tripartite coordinated regulatory mechanism model involving the microbiota, immune system, and HPV will facilitate the development of individualized predictive models in the future, identification of precise intervention targets, and maximization of HPV clearance.Keywords: Vaginal microbiota, Cytokines, Immunomodulation, Inflammation, Human papillomavirus

Clinical epidemiology reveals that HR-HPV infection underlies 95% of cervical cancer cases worldwide, highlighting its central role in disease pathogenesis. HR-HPV genotypes, such as HPV16 and HPV18, are classified as high-risk due to their oncogenic potential, whereas HPV6 and HPV11 are considered low-risk. Epidemiological studies indicate that up to 70% of women aged below 50 years have contracted HR-HPV, with annual incidence ranging from 5 to 15%. However, only 10% of infected individuals fail to clear the virus, resulting in persistent infection [1–3]. Identifying the determinants of persistent HPV infection is essential for the primary prevention of cervical cancer.

The vaginal microecosystem comprises the vaginal microbiota (VMB), endocrine regulation, anatomical structure, and local immune system. Studies indicate significant dysbiosis in the vaginal microecosystem of HPV-infected individuals, characterized by elevated VMB diversity, metabolic dysregulation, and immune dysfunction [4, 5]. Castanheira et al. proposed that VMB alterations modulate HPV infection via local immune response and microenvironmental changes [6]. Conversely, other studies suggest that VMB dysbiosis may arise secondary to immune-driven shifts in the vaginal environment [7].

In summary, the interplay between vaginal microbiota and host immunity has a significant influence on the clinical progression of HPV infection. This review synthesizes current evidence on the roles of vaginal microbiota and immune responses in HPV infection, elucidates their bidirectional interactions, and explores their mechanistic contributions to HPV persistence or clearance. Understanding the role of vaginal microbiota and immune status in HPV infection is helpful for developing new biomarkers to identify susceptible individuals at an early stage, or for finding new intervention targets to promote HPV clearance.

I. Changes in vaginal microbiota and the immune system during HPV infection

A hallmark of healthy vaginal microbiota is the predominance and abundant colonization of Lactobacillus species, which maintain vaginal homeostasis through lactic acid production and pH regulation [8]. Pioneering work by Lee et al. (2013) first employed next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology to systematically evaluate HPV-induced alterations in vaginal microbiota composition, demonstrating significantly increased microbial diversity and reduced Lactobacillus abundance in HPV-positive women compared to their HPV-negative counterparts [9]. Multiple lines of evidence now confirm that HPV infection induces characteristic vaginal dysbiosis, manifesting as decreased Lactobacillus predominance, expanded anaerobic microbiota such as Gardnerella, and significantly increased microbial diversity [10–13].

To systematically characterize vaginal microbiota (VMB) composition, researchers have established a classification system comprising five distinct community state types (CSTs): L. crispatus (CST I), L. gasseri (CST II), L. iners (CST III), low-Lactobacillus and bacterial vaginosis-associated bacteria (CST IV), and L. jensenii (CST V) [14]. CST IV is characterized by the augment in VMB diversity, mainly marked by a decrease in Lactobacillus abundance and an increase in anaerobic bacterial species [6]. In healthy women, CST I and CST V are predominant [13]. However, the CST IV rate significantly increased in the population with persistent HPV infection [15].

In addition to alterations in vaginal microbiota (VMB), the immune system within the vaginal microenvironment of HPV-infected individuals also undergoes significant changes. A longitudinal prospective clinical study conducted by Moscicki et al. revealed that compared to HPV-naïve individuals (WNHPV), HPV-positive women exhibit downregulation of key cytokines including IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, IL-12, IL-13, IFN-γ, IFN-α2, MIP-1α, and TNF-α [16]. In women diagnosed with invasive cervical cancer, there is a notable elevation in the levels of pro-inflammatory and chemokine cytokines (IL-36γ, TNFα, RANTES, MIP-1α, MIP-1β, IP-10), hematopoietic cytokines (Flt-3L, GM-CSF), adaptive immune response cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, sCD40L), as well as anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-10) [17].

The coordinated changes in vaginal microbiota (VMB) and immune responses manifest throughout both HPV infection and clearance phases. A longitudinal clinical study by Li et al. demonstrated that successful HPV16 clearance was associated with increased proportions of CST I and CST III, accompanied by restoration of eubiotic VMB composition [18]. The re-establishment of eubiosis triggered a characteristic immunological sequence: initial hypercytokinemia during the early post-clearance phase, succeeded by progressive attenuation to physiological cytokine levels [16] (Fig 1).

These findings collectively demonstrate that vaginal microbiota and host immune responses critically regulate HPV infection outcomes, including viral persistence, disease progression, and eventual clearance. Do they have any interaction with each other? In HPV infections, which party's change is the initiating factor and how does it affect the other party? The following section examines the potential mechanistic pathways underlying these observed interactions.

II. The vaginal microbiota influences HPV infection by modulating the immune response

The vaginal microbial community dominated by Lactobacilli can provide dual protection against HPV-related lesions, including biochemical defense through lactic acid-mediated acidification and immune protection through controlling inflammation and enhancing mucosal surveillance, jointly blocking the entire process from persistent infection to canceration [19–22].

A recent in vitro study demonstrated that Lactobacillus strains isolated from cervical-vaginal samples of HPV-cleared women, particularly L. gasseri LGV03, modulate the IRF3 pathway, enhancing interferon (IFN) production and bolstering innate antiviral immunity. Mechanistically, Lactobacillus activates the NF-κB pathway, upregulating IFN-α and IFN-β mRNA expression while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β) and chemokines (IL-8), thereby mitigating inflammatory damage during persistent HPV infection [23]. In vitro studies further confirm that Lactobacillus facilitates HPV clearance by restoring immune homeostasis and counteracting viral immune evasion mechanisms [24]. However, not all Lactobacillus species exert protective effects. L. iners, a dominant component of CST III, has been associated with elevated HPV susceptibility. Its metabolites potentiate the IL-17 signaling pathway, promoting viral persistence and cervical lesion progression [24, 25].

When anaerobic bacteria characteristic of CST-IV displace Lactobacillus, the physiological inflammatory response shifts to a chronic pathological state marked by elevated pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α, IL-8) [25]. Gardnerella, the predominant CST-IV species, forms dense biofilms on the vaginal epithelium and secretes vaginolysin, impairing secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) function in the mucosal barrier. Additionally, Gardnerella and co-occurring anaerobes such as Prevotella produce sialidase (SNA) that degrades protective mucins, triggering epithelial cell lysis and exfoliation [26]. Consequently, these anaerobic bacteria promote persistent HPV infection through dual mechanisms: disruption of the mucosal barrier and induction of pathological inflammatory responses.

Additional bacterial species contribute to HPV-associated pathogenesis through distinct mechanisms. Porphyromonas species have been shown to upregulate pro-inflammatory factors, particularly IL-1β, in cervical epithelial cells in vitro, thereby promoting cervical carcinogenesis [27]. A cross-sectional study revealed a significant enrichment of Staphylococcus and Streptococcus species in HPV-positive individuals compared to HPV-negative controls. These bacteria exacerbate chronic inflammation through toxin-mediated pathways and immunomodulatory effects, thereby impeding viral clearance and promoting HPV persistence [28, 29]. Furthermore, specific bacterial genera have been implicated in modulating innate immune responses during HPV infection, primarily through alterations in RIG-I-like receptor (RLR)-mediated signaling pathways [30].

Moscicki et al. conducted longitudinal follow-up studies revealing that, compared to HPV-naïve women (WNHPV), HPV-positive individuals displayed heightened susceptibility to vaginal microbiota dysbiosis. Notably, these microbial shifts were not observed during acute infection but emerged progressively during persistent HPV infection, resolving only after prolonged viral clearance [16]. This temporal pattern suggests that microbiota dysbiosis likely represents a consequence rather than a causative driver of HPV infection. The temporal relationship between microbiota succession and HPV clearance kinetics remains poorly understood, necessitating carefully designed longitudinal investigations.

Moreover, the question remains whether HPV regulation necessarily depends on alterations in microbiota. An observational study conducted by Łaniewski et al. characterized immune mediator-Lactobacillus interactions, demonstrating that specific cytokines (IL-36γ, MIP-1β, RANTES, IL-10, IL-2, IL-4, Flt-3L, sCD40L) exhibit cervical cancer associations contingent on vaginal microbiota composition, while others (TNFα, TNFβ, MIP-1α, GM-CSF, IL-10) maintain their functional relationships irrespective of Lactobacillus dominance status. Notably, IL-36γ consistently correlated with carcinogenesis regardless of microbial composition [17]. These findings indicate that HPV infection and progression may occur through both microbiota-dependent and independent pathways.

These suggest that the vaginal microbiota emerges as a contextual modifier of HPV infection, influencing but not strictly governing viral pathogenesis.

III. The host immune status inversely affects the stability of the microbiota

There is a bidirectional interaction between the vaginal microbiota and the host immune system. The microbiota actively influences the immune response, while at the same time, it is also regulated by the host immune status. This interaction jointly participates in regulating the outcome of HPV infection.

When the host is in an immunosuppressed condition, the vaginal microbiota becomes more susceptible to dysbiosis, consequently elevating the risk of HPV infection. An observational study revealed that HIV-positive women exhibit significantly higher vaginal microbiota diversity (p < 0.001) and greater prevalence of CST IV compared to their HIV-negative counterparts. The dysbiosis vaginal microenvironment conferred a 3.25-fold elevated HR-HPV risk (35.1% vs. 10.8% prevalence, P < 0.001), suggesting microbiota stability critically influences viral susceptibility in immunocompromised hosts [31]. Notably, the microbiota-HPV association demonstrates HIV-status dependency, exhibiting a robust correlation in HIV-negative women but no significant association in HIV-positive individuals, this seems to indicate that the immune status has a much greater influence in HPV susceptibility.

However, emerging evidence presents a contrasting perspective. An observational study conducted by Chávez-Torres et al. found equivalent HR-HPV prevalence and microbiome profiles between ART-treated WLWH and SNW [32]. Notably, the highest systemic immune activation levels were detected in dually HIV/HR-HPV-infected individuals. This might be because all the subjects in this study received antiretroviral therapy, which normalized their immune status to that of the general population. Alternatively, there might be other unexplained factors contributing to this unexpected finding. Other immune status alterations, such as a decreased CD4 + T cell count, also show a microbiota state that is more conducive to HPV infection [31].

Inflammation, as a fundamental immune response to injury or infection, when chronically elevated either locally or systemically, induces microbial dysbiosis that promotes HPV colonization. Bacterial vaginosis (BV), a clinically significant inflammatory condition, exemplifies this relationship through its characteristic elevation of proinflammatory cytokines (including IL-1α/β, IL-18, MIP-1α/β, IL-8, TNF-α/β, and MIF) and expansion of CCR5 + CD4 + T cells, concurrent with a microbial shift from Lactobacillus-dominant to anaerobic-predominant flora featuring Gardnerella, Prevotella, and Trichomonas species. This dysbiosis state frequently co-occurs with other sexually transmitted infections (Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae), collectively enhancing HPV susceptibility [33].

Elevated systemic inflammatory markers, including high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP) and eosinophil percentage (EOS%), are consistently observed in HPV-positive individuals. These elevated levels demonstrate significant associations with specific microbial compositional changes, particularly showing a positive correlation between Gardnerella abundance and CRP levels (β = 0.42, p = 0.040) [34]. This relationship provides mechanistic evidence that systemic immune dysregulation contributes to vaginal microbial dysbiosis, creating a self-perpetuating cycle that favors HPV persistence and progression.

Furthermore, an in vitro study found that HPV infection systematically subverts host defense mechanisms by concurrently disrupting NF-κB and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways, resulting in suppressed expression of antimicrobial peptides. This metabolic reprogramming impairs the biosynthesis of amino acids essential for Lactobacillus survival, thereby inducing vaginal microbiota dysbiosis. The resultant microbial imbalance reciprocally facilitates HPV persistence and disease progression by compromising epithelial barrier integrity and antiviral immune responses [35].

Overall, impaired host immune function directly inhibits the clearance of the virus by cellular means or indirectly disrupts the microbial ecological balance, thereby maintaining the virus's colonization and promoting its progression.

IV. Comprehensive Model of Microbiota-Immune Interaction Mechanism and Intervention Prospects

There is a dynamic interaction between the vaginal microbiota and the host immune system, which regulates the outcome of human papillomavirus (HPV) infection. The microbial community regulates HPV infection by regulating the cytokine network, modulating the innate immune signaling pathways of epithelial cells, and initiating the adaptive immune response. Conversely, the immune status affects HPV infection by influencing the composition of the microbial community (Fig. 2). Probiotics, defined as live microorganisms conferring health benefits to the host, demonstrate significant clinical efficacy in HPV infection management. Longitudinal probiotic administration is associated with markedly enhanced HPV clearance rates, with treated women exhibiting twice the probability of resolving HPV-associated cytological abnormalities compared to control populations [36, 37]. Mechanistic studies reveal that probiotic interventions exert multifaceted therapeutic effects through microbial ecosystem restoration, anti-inflammatory response induction, and systemic immune potentiation [42–44]. A prospective controlled experiment found that vaginal transplantation of the natural probiotic strain Lactobacillus crispatus chen-01 isolated from the vagina could significantly reduce HPV viral load, improve HPV clearance rate, and improve vaginal inflammation, with no adverse reactions [38]. Mechanistically, probiotic intervention modulates both local microbiota composition and systemic inflammatory profiles, thereby creating an immunological milieu less permissive to HPV persistence.

Emerging translational research highlights the therapeutic potential of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) in HPV infection management. Clinical trials demonstrate that the TCM formulation Paiteling shows significantly greater efficacy than interferon-α in treating high-risk HPV infections (p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.2). At the molecular level, Paiteling induces caspase-dependent apoptosis in cervical cancer cells via selective inhibition of the HPV E6/E7-PI3K/AKT signaling axis [39].While these findings establish its direct antitumor effects, further investigation is needed to determine whether Paiteling's therapeutic benefits involve microbiota modulation or host immune potentiation.

A prospective clinical controlled study found that the 6-month clearance rate of patients with persistent high-risk human papillomavirus (HR-HPV) infection and confirmed low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (LSIL) by histology using the traditional Chinese medicine reagent Jiawei Ermiao Granules (JWEMGs) reached 73.85%. The elimination of the infection is accompanied by the restoration of the vaginal microbiota to a normal state dominated by Lactobacilli. Meanwhile, in vitro experiments have shown that JWEMGs activates the ALPK1/NF-κB pathway, thereby enhancing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and also maintaining the integrity of the epithelial cell barrier [40]. The distinctive advantages of TCM preparations, including their favorable safety profile, minimal adverse effects, and holistic therapeutic approach, position them as promising candidates for HPV infection management, particularly for patients seeking alternatives to conventional therapies.

Immunotherapeutic approaches are emerging as promising adjuvants in HPV management by enhancing host immune surveillance. The standardized shiitake mushroom mycelium extract AHCC (Active Hexose Correlated Compound) has demonstrated particular immunomodulatory efficacy. AHCC exerts its effect by activating natural killer cells and promoting the maturation of dendritic cells. In cases of persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus over a period of 2 years, a 58.8% overall clearance rate was achieved. This immunopotentiation strategy offers particular promise, though the durability of response requires further evaluation [41].

A comprehensive understanding of the dynamic interactions between microbial communities and host immunity provides significant clinical advantages, facilitating both the development of targeted HPV therapies and the identification of high-risk individuals through biomarker discovery. Yang et al. developed prediction models using genomic biomarkers (12 genes, 17 genera and 7 species), and these models demonstrated excellent performance in the detection of HPV16 infection (with AUC values of 0.861, 0.819 and 0.918 respectively) [45]. Future refinements to these models should incorporate multi-omics data, spanning viremic, transcriptomic, and proteomic profiles, to enhance predictive resolution. Such integrated approaches will enable more precise risk stratification and earlier intervention strategies, ultimately improving HPV prevention efforts.

Eliminating HPV is a major issue we are currently facing. The challenge lies in early identification of susceptible individuals and early intervention for infected populations. Long ago, researchers discovered that host immunity is closely related to HPV infection. With the development of technology, studies have found that vaginal microbiota is also closely related to HPV infection. However, few studies have linked the three together. As far as I know, this article is the first to explore the impact of the interaction between vaginal microbiota and immune status on HPV infection from this perspective. Nevertheless, this article also has certain flaws. Most existing studies are small sample, single-center studies, lacking broad representativeness. Moreover, many studies are cross-sectional studies. During the process of HPV infection and clearance, vaginal microbiota and immune status are dynamically changing. Data collected at a single time point are insufficient to explain the issue and require more longitudinal studies to dynamically monitor changes and obtain more convincing results.
