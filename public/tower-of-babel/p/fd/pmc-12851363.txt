Introduction

The global land-surface air temperature has increased by 1.53 °C since the preindustrial era of 1850–1900, showcasing significant regional warming disparities [1]. Recent shifts in Earth's climate and extreme weather events (EWEs) can be attributed to anthropogenic influences. This is evidenced by a marked increase in the frequency of extreme climatic events such as cyclones, torrential rainfall (including floods and flash floods), landslides, dust storms, thunderstorms, monsoon disruptions, heat and cold waves, and heavy snowfall [2].

Escalating global warming and climate change directly pose a threat to agricultural yields, undermining food security by disrupting crop production [3]. Malnutrition, particularly in the form of hidden hunger (i.e. deficiency of micronutrients) is another globally prevalent issue that has been observed with a notable escalation in developing countries. According to the World Health Organization, in 2022 approximately 390 million adults aged 18 and above worldwide were underweight while an estimated 2.5 billion were overweight [4]. One of the strategies that have been adopted to address malnutrition is the promotion of nutritional interventions that involve healthy diets from sustainable food systems.

Half a century ago, millets dominated India's agricultural landscape, constituting 40 % of cultivated grains, surpassing even wheat and rice pre-Green Revolution. However, from the 1960s, rapid increases in paddy and wheat production left millet behind due to a lack of support and minimum prices, resulting in a decline in millet cultivation area. Government policies favoring commercial crops further exacerbated this decline and intensified farmers' reliance on high-yield seeds demanding costly chemical inputs. The transition from diversified traditional farming like intercropping or crop rotation to mono-cropping degraded food security, and the volatile climate heightened farmers' vulnerability leading to reduced yields.

Historically, millets were primary dietary staples for many Indian tribal populations. However, with increased access to rice and other foods through government distribution systems and markets, Indigenous communities shifted their perception of millet, associating them more with subsistence agriculture than commercial production. This shift contributed to a decline in millet cultivation within these groups.

In India, millet production spans 15.48 million hectares, yielding around 17.2 million tons of millet, with a crop yield of 1,111 kg/ha [5]. However, its contribution to overall food grain production decreased from 22.17 % to 6.94 % between 1950–1951 and 2011–2012 [6,7] and studies indicated the decline in millet cultivation area was due to government policies favoring major cereals and other crops. Despite this, finger millet production increased by 47.41 % and pearl millet by 247.48 %, showing a notable rise in crop yields despite reduced cultivation area. In comparison to rice and wheat, millets offer higher levels of protein, fiber, vitamins, and minerals [7].

Modern agricultural systems primarily depend on annual monocultures of major cereal crops such as rice (Oryza sativa), wheat (Triticum spp), and maize (Zea mays) that are vulnerable to temperature fluctuations and water scarcity. To maintain yields, intensive use of agrochemical inputs like fertilizers and pesticides is common. Climate change projections indicate potential yield losses for wheat of 3.5–12.9 % [8], maize 34.6–35.4 % [9], and rice 10–15 % [10], exacerbating the inadequacy of protein supplies for both humans and animals. However, an alternative exists in the form of millets, which despite their resilience to climate stresses [11], and considerable nutritional value, remain underrepresented in agricultural research, policy, and markets. Millets, among the earliest domesticated crops, are cultivated widely in arid and tropical regions of Eurasia and Africa and hold significant cultural and economic importance [[12], [13], [14]].

India's vulnerability to climate change is evident, ranking fifth in the Climate Risk Index 2018 [15]. Escalating storms and droughts have affected over half of the country, and unprecedented floods pose significant challenges to agriculture. Increased precipitation negatively impacts paddy, while long-term pesticide use contributes to soil infertility. Hybrid seeds' vulnerability to extreme weather events exacerbates yield losses, creating a critical situation for Indian farmers. Thus, cultivating more resilient crops like millet becomes imperative. Millet exhibits tolerance to drought, heat stress, diseases, and pests. They have smaller panicles and seeds which mature quickly and withstand both drought and waterlogging conditions. Adaptability to dry and semi-arid climates [16], minimal water requirements compared to rice, and superior nutritional value make millets a sustainable alternative for ensuring food security. Their shorter growing season and resistance to pests reduces the need for pesticides, fostering sustainable agriculture amidst climate change [17] and their ability to withstand high temperatures positions them as a prime option for tropical countries seeking drought-resilient food solutions [18,19]. For the reasons above, support and production of millets was undertaken by the Odisha Millet Project which we will highlight as a case report.

Case presentation

The Indian State of Odisha, like many other regions around the world, has been experiencing the impacts of climate change, including changes in temperature, precipitation patterns, and extreme weather events. These changes have had severe consequences for agriculture, including declines in cereal crop yields. Odisha's vulnerability to natural disasters prompted renewed interest in millets, offering insights into mitigating climate change's impact on food production and achieving sustainable development goals (SDGs). According to reports from the Ministry of Agriculture and Farmers' Welfare, the millet cultivation area saw a significant 60 % decline during 2016–2017 [20]. This drop could be attributed to reduced demand, and the redirection of irrigated land toward wheat and rice production, thereby resulting in the depletion of vital nutrients such as vitamin A, protein, iron, and iodine among women and children [20].

Recognizing the significance of highly nutritious and climate-resilient millets, the Government of Odisha launched the Millet Mission in 2017 which aimed to promote millets amidst climate change by making millet a staple food, and how they have enhanced community nutrition and food security, particularly in regions facing droughts and extreme heat.

Extreme weather events in Odisha, India

The state of Odisha, lying in sub-tropical India is vulnerable to various natural calamities like droughts, floods, and cyclones. Such frequent occurrences of extreme climatic events have been detrimental to the livelihood resilience of the poor and marginalized people. An analysis by the Council on Energy, Environment, and Water reveals a significant rise in droughts over the past decade, alongside cyclones and floods. Moreover, IMD data highlights a marked increase in heat wave occurrences in Odisha from 1970 to 2019. Notably, over the past 15 years, 52 % of the state's heat wave days occurred, signaling a troubling trend (Fig. 2).

This uptick in extreme weather has impacted crop production, particularly Kharif crops, which are sown from May to June and harvested from September to October. Rice production, a staple of Odisha's diet, has been notably affected, with decreased grain yields posing a significant concern [22]. To counter the decline in rice production exacerbated by climatic events, millets, once traditionally grown but overshadowed by modern agricultural practices, have reemerged as a viable solution in the state of Odisha, India.

However, the public distribution system (PDS) in India (introduced to alleviate hunger by providing staple foods like rice and wheat at a nominal cost to economically weaker households) continues to prioritize the distribution of rice and wheat over millet. Despite its significance for both regional and national food security, millets have not been given a high priority status within Indian agriculture.

Recognizing the significance of highly nutritious and climate-resilient millets, in 2017 the Government of Odisha launched the Odisha Millets Mission (OMM) as the flagship program of the Department of Agriculture and Farmers Empowerment. It was crafted to promote millets specifically in tribal areas of Odisha, aiming to revive their cultivation both on farms and in people's diets.

The mission was established with a clear focus on revitalizing traditional millet farming practices, aiming to encourage household consumption, conservation, and the promotion of millet across the state, setting up decentralized processing units and, improving the productivity of millet crops. This has led to the inclusion of Millets into key Government of India Schemes such as Integrated Child Development Scheme (ICDS), Mid-Day-Meal (MDM), and Public Distribution System (PDS), a significant step in enhancing their accessibility and consumption among diverse populations.

The Mission provided institutional support such as guaranteed purchases and higher prices, sparking increased awareness about millet cultivation. This included the adoption of improved farming techniques to tackle nutritional and climatic challenges effectively.

Traditional millet farming practices were utilized which involved the selection and cultivation of local millet varieties that are well-adapted to the specific climate, soil conditions, and cultural preferences of the region [23]. These practices are typically sustainable and environment friendly as they often involve minimal tillage, which helps in soil conservation and reduces greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, farmers employ agroecological principles such as crop rotation, intercropping, and the use of organic manures, enhancing soil fertility and long-term sustainability [23].

OMM implemented initiatives to incentivize and support farmers, particularly the tribal communities, throughout the entire value chain, from cultivating the crops to marketing the products. Through Direct benefit transfer (DBT) the OMM offers direct subsidy transfer to farmers for three years to incentivize millet production and adoption of improved agronomic practices. This approach underscored the commitment to not only reviving millet cultivation but also ensuring its sustainability and economic viability for farmers in Odisha.

As a direct result of OMM, the state of Odisha witnessed a 215 % surge in the gross value of millet produced per farmer household, soaring from Rs3,957 in 2016–17 to Rs12,486 in 2018–19. Concurrently, the area under millet cultivation expanded from 2,949 hectares to 5,182 hectares during the same period, with a notable 120 % increase in yield rates.

Before the year 2022, nearly 0.2 million farmers across 19 districts were actively engaged in millet cultivation, marking a significant expansion of this sustainable practice. Notably, since the inception of the OMM, approximately 32,300,000 kg of millets have been procured, underscoring the program's tangible impact on production and distribution. To further promote millet cultivation and consumption, the Odisha government has instituted Mandia Dibas (Millet Day) on November 10 annually, dedicated to raising awareness and popularizing the crop among communities. The concerted efforts of OMM have positively influenced the millet value chain, leading to a notable increase in both demand and supply, thereby fostering economic growth and nutritional well-being across the region.

Discussion

Nutritional and emissions profile of millets

Millets offer numerous health benefits that warrant their inclusion in diets across the nation. Epidemiological studies have highlighted the protective health benefits of minor millet-rich diets against non-communicable diseases [24]. Given that millets are C4 plants, they also contribute to climate change mitigation by converting carbon dioxide into oxygen, unlike wheat and rice, which are susceptible to heat and emit methane, exacerbating climate change [18]. Firstly, they are rich in dietary fiber, essential for digestive health, blood sugar regulation, and weight management [24]. Additionally, millets abound in vital vitamins and minerals like magnesium, phosphorus, and iron, contributing to bone health, energy production, and red blood cell formation [25]. With a low glycemic index, millets aid in blood sugar control, making them advantageous for individuals with diabetes [26]. Furthermore, their antioxidant properties shield against chronic diseases, while being gluten-free renders them suitable for those with intolerances or celiac disease [26].

Incorporating millet into diets can significantly enhance essential nutrition, particularly for children and women, potentially addressing issues like iron deficiency and cardiovascular diseases [27].

In India, the prevailing nutritional landscape presents a multifaceted challenge, often referred to as the "triple burden,'' which encompasses malnutrition, obesity, and deficiencies in essential micronutrients [28]. This complex issue underscores the urgent need to adopt a diet rich in nutrients to address diverse nutritional needs and health concerns across different segments of the population. Against this backdrop, the public distribution system (PDS) assumes a pivotal role in ensuring access to a diverse range of traditional and nutritious grains. This approach not only addresses food security concerns but also lays the foundation for long-term health promotion and disease prevention efforts nationwide.

Impact of OMM on Indigenous Communities

In the state of Odisha, millets have historically played a significant role in the lives of Indigenous communities, contributing to their cultural heritage, food security, and economic well-being. Millets such as finger millet (ragi), pearl millet (bajra), and sorghum (jowar) have been staple foods for many Indigenous groups in Orissa for centuries, deeply embedded in their traditional cuisines and dietary practices.

The adoption of the OMM has deeply influenced the livelihoods of Indigenous communities. Through the cultivation of millets using traditional agricultural methods, these communities have now been able to create sources of income, open up employment avenues, and fortify their economic resilience. Moreover, the cultivation of millets goes hand in hand with traditional farming practices that prioritize sustainability and biodiversity conservation. By cultivating diverse millet varieties suited to local ecosystems, Indigenous farmers contribute to the preservation of agrobiodiversity, maintaining a rich genetic heritage for future generations. The promotion of millet has not only strengthened Indigenous food systems but also empowers communities to sustain their cultural heritage and livelihoods in a rapidly changing world. OMM has encouraged households to cultivate millets by exposing them to a variety of recipes through training sessions, food festivals, and awareness campaigns.

Scalability of Odisha Millets Mission

Several other Indian states, including Karnataka, and Telangana, have integrated millet into their food security programs. OMM has introduced millets into social safety net programs, recognizing their potential to combat malnutrition and climate change.

Cambridge University partnered with OMM to explore the possibility of the design of OMM as an alternative to the Green Revolution framework. UN-IFAD and UN-FAO have supported the framework of OMM as suitable for taking up agro-ecological initiatives.

However, consumer preferences still favor rice over millet under PDS, necessitating behavioral change campaigns, supportive policies, and investments to promote millet consumption. A comprehensive approach, as demonstrated by OMM, endorsed by the World Food Programme, and recommended by the Government of India, is crucial for reforming PDS and elevating India's Global Hunger Index ranking. By embracing millets in public diets, India and the world can foster nutritional resilience and sustainable food systems nationwide.

Realizing the benefits of millets, the Indian government proclaimed 2023 as the “International Year of Millets” to promote the cultivation and consumption of millets throughout the nation. Moreover, the Food and Agricultural Organization of the United Nations (FAO) declared 2023 to be the “International Year of Millets” to draw greater attention to revitalizing millet consumption [20].

Conclusion

There is a growing emphasis that both developed and developing nations must adjust and adapt to ever-changing and ever-evolving environmental conditions. Millets possess the potential to address global food and nutritional security concerns, particularly in regions facing environmental challenges. The cultivation of millets as replacements for water-intensive crops offers a solution to mitigate strain on water reserves and reduce depletion.

The Odisha Millets Mission has revitalized traditional farming practices in tribal areas, leading to increased millet cultivation, higher yields, and improved household incomes. Its success highlights the potential for similar initiatives nationwide, benefiting livelihoods, nutrition, and climate resilience among vulnerable populations. Future research can prioritize enhancing millet productivity and profitability through improved varieties, enhanced agronomic practices, and modern technology adoption.
