Echocardiography in Stroke Management: Necessary or Overused?
Authors: Nicole Ann Tesoro, Jasvin K. Manha, Jerold Fleishman, Sumanth Bandaru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stroke remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, leading to the development of efficient and evidence-based management strategies. Echocardiography is a crucial diagnostic tool for identifying cardiac sources of embolism, particularly in cardioembolic and cryptogenic strokes, yet its routine use in all stroke patients remains controversial. While evidence-based guidelines recommend a patient-specific approach, rigid hospital quality metrics have incentivized widespread echocardiography, even in low-yield cases such as large artery atherosclerotic strokes. This disconnect raises concerns regarding cost-effectiveness, resource allocation, and potential over-testing, delaying discharges and prolonging hospital length of stay. A more tailored approach, focusing on guideline-driven indications, is necessary to optimize stroke care. Future research should refine diagnostic criteria and align quality metrics with evidence-based practices to ensure high-value, patient-centered stroke management.

Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of death and long-term disability. In the United States, more than 600,000 first ischemic strokes and nearly 200,000 recurrent ischemic strokes occur annually. 1,2 Once reperfusion and early stabilization decisions are addressed, the next major task is mechanism identification because secondary prevention is etiology-specific and depends on identifying patients in whom additional testing will change management. A patient with atrial fibrillation, left ventricular thrombus, infective endocarditis, or patent foramen ovale may require a fundamentally different prevention strategy than a patient with symptomatic carotid disease or classic small-vessel infarction3–5.

Echocardiography therefore occupies an important place in stroke etiologic evaluation. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is noninvasive and readily available, whereas transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) better visualizes the left atrial appendage, interatrial septum, valves, prosthetic material, and aortic arch.4,6,7 The central clinical question is not whether echocardiography can identify meaningful pathology, but which patients are most likely to benefit from it and whether the result will change management.

This article is a narrative review that aims to summarize what contemporary stroke guidelines and echocardiography guidance statements say about cardiac imaging after stroke, what major U.S. quality programs say about stroke care processes, and what the published literature shows about the diagnostic and management-changing yield of echocardiography in different stroke populations. Methods

This narrative review synthesizes contemporary evidence relevant to echocardiography after ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). PubMed-indexed guidelines, systematic reviews, cohort studies, and narrative reviews were examined. Official documents from major U.S. organizations, including the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA), the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE), The Joint Commission, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) Paul Coverdell National Acute Stroke Program, and the National Quality Forum (NQF) were also reviewed. Emphasis was placed on sources addressing 1) where echocardiography sits in stroke etiologic evaluation, 2) how often echocardiographic findings alter mechanism classification or management, and 3) whether major U.S. quality programs explicitly require routine echocardiography. Sources were reviewed through April 2026.

The typical stroke care algorithm

Patients who arrive to the emergency department presenting with suspected acute stroke symptoms require immediate general assessment and stabilization. All will need emergency brain imaging with a non-contrast Computed Tomography (CT) scan, CT Or Magnetic resonance (MR) angiography of head and neck, or CT perfusion imaging to guide acute management and exclude intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) before initiating therapy. For patients with ischemic strokes confirmed on imaging, thrombolysis with thrombolytic therapy should be promptly initiated if eligibility criteria are met. If the patient is not a candidate for thrombolysis, aspirin alone or in combination with clopidogrel should be administered to reduce the risk of further thrombotic events. In cases of large vessel occlusion, mechanical thrombectomy is considered within established time windows, based on clinical and imaging findings.2

If brain imaging reveals ICH, the focus shifts to stabilizing the patient, controlling blood pressure, reversing any coagulopathy, and considering surgical interventions for life-threatening hematomas or significant mass effect. In such cases, systolic blood pressure should be reduced to a target of less than 140 mmHg as quickly and as safely as possible to minimize hematoma expansion.2,8

Once the patient is stabilized, further diagnostic evaluation is necessary to determine the underlying cause of the ischemic stroke. This includes differentiating among stroke subtypes.

The TOAST (Trial of ORG 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment) Classification categorizes ischemic strokes into five subtypes: 1) Large artery atherosclerosis, 2) Cardioembolism, 3) Small artery occlusion, 4) Stroke of other determined etiology, and 5) Stroke of undetermined etiology. Large artery atherosclerosis occurs when stenosis or occlusion of major brain arteries or branch cortical arteries disrupts blood flow. Cardioembolic strokes result from emboli originating in the heart and are commonly associated with atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, or recent myocardial infarction. Small artery occlusion strokes, including lacunar strokes, stem from blockage of small penetrating arteries and are typically linked to chronic hypertension or diabetes. Strokes of other determined etiology arise from rare causes like nonatherosclerotic vasculopathies, hypercoagulable states, or hematologic disorders. Strokes of undetermined etiology occur when no clear cause is found or when multiple potential causes prevent a definitive diagnosis.3

Echocardiography plays a key role in cases where a cardiac source of embolism is suspected and in cryptogenic strokes, where it may identify covert cardiac sources such as a patent foramen ovale (PFO), atrial septal aneurysms, or other structural abnormalities.6,7

While cardioembolic strokes account for approximately 15–30% of ischemic strokes, the diagnostic yield of routine echocardiography is substantially lower in patients with ischemic stroke subtypes where a non-cardiac mechanism has already been reasonably established, such as large-artery atherosclerosis or classic small-vessel occlusion.9 In these populations, echocardiography rarely alters acute management or secondary prevention strategies unless additional clinical or imaging features suggest a concurrent cardioembolic source. The small vessel nature of lacunar strokes typically negates the need for echocardiography unless there are additional findings that warrant cardiac evaluation.10

Guideline recommendations on echocardiography after stroke

Contemporary guideline language is more selective than the phrase routine stroke echocardiography implies. The 2026 AHA/ASA acute ischemic stroke guideline prioritizes rapid recognition, vascular imaging, reperfusion decisions, thrombectomy selection, physiologic management, and systems of care; echocardiography is not presented as a universal hyperacute step required before acute treatment decisions.2

The 2021 AHA/ASA guideline for prevention of stroke after stroke or TIA is more directly relevant because it frames secondary prevention around diagnostic evaluation and management by etiology. 4 Contemporary summaries of that guidance describe echocardiography as reasonable in patients with cryptogenic stroke, with TEE particularly useful in selected patients with embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) when the result could influence management.4

The ASE guidance is even more explicit about selectivity. It recommends echocardiography when a cardiac source of embolism is suspected and when results are likely to affect therapeutic decisions; conversely, it does not support echocardiography when another probable stroke mechanism is already established and echocardiographic findings are unlikely to change treatment.6 Taken together, these positions are directionally consistent: echocardiography is valuable when cardioembolism is plausible or unresolved and less compelling as reflex testing in patients with a clear noncardiac mechanism.

Diagnostic yield and impact on management

The diagnostic-yield literature helps explain why selective use is emphasized. A 2021 systematic review and meta-analysis of cardiac imaging in ischemic stroke or TIA of undetermined cause found that major cardioembolic sources were identified infrequently: approximately 1.3% by TTE and 5.9% by TEE. These pooled estimates suggest that TEE can uncover more high-risk pathology than TTE, but absolute yield depends heavily on patient selection.14

In a prospective multicenter cohort of patients with stroke or TIA of undetermined cause, Thomalla et al. found treatment-relevant abnormalities in 18.9% with TEE versus 14.1% with TTE; TEE added 6.4% additional treatment-relevant findings overall and 14.1% in patients 60 years or younger. Importantly, TEE changed stroke-cause classification in 11.5% of patients.15 These data support TEE in carefully selected cryptogenic or ESUS populations rather than as a universal screening tool.

Likewise, the prospective CEIS study reported that echocardiography findings changed therapy in 22.5% of selected stroke patients, with patent foramen ovale accounting for much of the actionable signal, particularly in younger patients.16 However, when echocardiography is applied more broadly to unselected cohorts, the management-changing yield is lower. Brunser et al. reported a change in management in 10.7% of patients overall, with direct therapeutic changes in only 6.0%.17 More recently, a multicenter prospective study of routine TTE in ischemic stroke or TIA of undetermined cause found a major cardioembolic source in only 1.0% of patients; nearly all of those patients already had major electrocardiographic abnormalities, and only 0.1% of patients without such abnormalities had a major source detected.18

Stroke subtype also matters. In lacunar stroke patients, Wang et al. found a low management-changing yield from extensive embolic workup; anticoagulation-relevant findings occurred only in the non-lacunar group.10 The key issue, then, is not whether echocardiography ever matters, but in whom it matters enough to alter treatment.

Quality metrics and accreditation programs

A direct review of current U.S. quality documents shows that no major national U.S. program reviewed here explicitly requires routine echocardiography for every stroke admission.14–18

The Joint Commission’s current stroke measures focus on venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, antithrombotic treatment, anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation or flutter, thrombolysis, statins, stroke education, rehabilitation assessment, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale documentation, and endovascular reperfusion metrics―not echocardiography. 2,5 The AHA Get With The Guidelines- Stroke recognition criteria similarly emphasize seven achievement measures centered on reperfusion timeliness and evidence-based medical therapy.11,12 CMS ties hospital reporting and value-based programs to stroke measures such as antithrombotic therapy and anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation or flutter, without specifying routine echocardiography. 11 The CDC Coverdell program tracks similar in-hospital stroke metrics, without an echocardiography requirement.2,11 Historically endorsed stroke measures from NQF follow the same pattern.5,11

Accordingly, current national quality programs are better understood as emphasizing measurable acute-care and discharge processes while leaving greater discretion in diagnostic workup. If broad echocardiography use occurs in practice, the reviewed documents do not support the claim that it is directly mandated by a national stroke performance measure.

Is there a disconnect between guidelines and practice?

There may still be a practical disconnect, but it is better described as practice variation rather than as an explicit conflict between guidelines and national metrics. A global survey found that TTE was used routinely in 70% of cryptogenic stroke patients, whereas TEE was used routinely in only 17%, underscoring heterogeneity in how centers operationalize cardiac workup.15 In a nationwide analysis of certified stroke units in Germany, median TTE and TEE use rates were 63.3% and 21.3%, respectively, with substantial between-center variation.15

Several factors may explain broader use in routine care than the guidelines alone would predict: a desire to complete the etiologic workup during the index admission, concern about loss to outpatient follow-up, local order sets, variable access to TEE or prolonged cardiac monitoring, and the understandable reluctance to miss a treatable cardiac source. These forces may broaden testing in practice, but they are not the same as a national performance measure mandating universal echocardiography.

This distinction matters. If the objective is high-value care, the solution is not to argue that echocardiography is unhelpful in stroke. Rather, the solution is to clarify which patients are most likely to benefit and which modality is most likely to change management.

The tailored approach

To address the challenges, a more nuanced approach is necessary. Evidence-based guidelines emphasize tailoring diagnostic procedures to the specific needs of the patient, rather than adhering to rigid protocols. Hospitals must navigate the balance between meeting quality metrics and ensuring that echocardiography is used when indicated. By adopting a patient-specific approach and prioritizing evidence-based guidelines over rigid adherence to quality metrics, healthcare providers can minimize unnecessary tests, reduce risks, and improve overall clinical outcomes. Furthermore, shared decision-making and the informed consent process can also supplement evidence- based patient choices that are economically and medically appropriate.10,13,19

Future research should evaluate which patients truly benefit from echocardiography and develop clearer evidence-based criteria. For instance, whether to routinely screen patients on anticoagulants, with arrhythmias, or with widespread ischemia on CT scan.5 Likewise, organizations should also specify indications of diagnostic tests in performance measures in order to mitigate overtesting and address inefficiencies in patient care. Through this, healthcare systems can provide high-value, cost-effective, and evidence-based care as well as enhance the efficiency of diagnostic testing for stroke.

Conclusion

Echocardiography remains an important component of stroke etiologic evaluation, but the contemporary literature does not support the blanket statement that every hospitalized stroke patient should routinely undergo echocardiography. AHA/ASA and ASE guidance instead support a mechanism-based approach in which cardiac imaging is most valuable when cardioembolism is suspected, when the stroke is cryptogenic or ESUS, or when findings are likely to alter secondary prevention.2,4,6

The literature on diagnostic yield is consistent with that position: clinically actionable findings are most common in selected patients and with TEE, whereas routine TTE in broad or low-pretest-probability populations has a low major-source yield.14,18 Equally important, current U.S. quality programs do not explicitly require routine echocardiography for all stroke admissions.

The most defensible conclusion, therefore, is not that echocardiography is unnecessary in stroke care, but that its use should be selective, indication-based, and tied to the likelihood that the result will change management. That framing better reflects the guidelines, the quality-measure landscape, and the available data on diagnostic yield.
