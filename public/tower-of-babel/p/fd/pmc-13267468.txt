Checkpoint inhibitors, obinutuzumab plus PET‐adapted ultra‐low dose nodal radiotherapy yield high efficacy in treatment‐naïve follicular lymphoma: Results from the phase II ‘FLUORO’ study
Authors: Arina Martynchyk, Geoffrey Chong, Richard Khor, Denise Lee, Sze Ting Lee, Andrew M. Scott, Anneke C. Grobler, Belinda A. Campbell, Thanuja Thachil, Kate Manos, Genevieve Douglas, Alexandra Romano, Jodie B. Palmer, Melinda Burgess, Colm Keane, Eliza A. Hawkes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFollicular lymphoma (FL) first‐line chemoimmunotherapy yields high efficacy but serious toxicity in 65% of patients. In high tumour burden treatment‐naïve FL, we aimed to exploit known immunostimulatory and cytotoxic properties of combination checkpoint inhibition (atezolizumab) and obinutuzumab (6 cycles) with positron emission tomography (PET)‐adapted ultra‐low dose radiotherapy (RT; 4Gy) to all PET‐avid sites after cycle 2, followed by 2 years of obinutuzumab maintenance. Sixteen eligible patients were enrolled (15 evaluable for response) to our Phase II trial (NCT04962126). Median age was 53 years with 94% intermediate/high Follicular Lymphoma International Prognostic Index (FLIPI) score. The primary end‐point of complete response (CR) was achieved in 93% (95% confidence interval [CI]: 70%–99%) with objective response rate (ORR) of 100% (95% CI: 75%–100%). With 41‐month median follow‐up, 3‐year progression‐free survival (PFS) was 80% (95% CI: 55–93) and OS was 100% (95% CI: 79.6–100). Grade 3–4 adverse events occurred in 31% of patients (2 immune‐related). Baseline PET radiomics and immune transcriptomics demonstrated profiles suggesting promise as predictive markers of immunotherapy response, consistent with our prior findings. The novel multimodality combination of obinutuzumab, atezolizumab and ultra‐low dose nodal RT in treatment‐naïve FL has promising efficacy and safety, offering a potential novel chemo‐free option. Further studies using low‐cost RT to augment immunotherapy are warranted.This trial was registered in clinicaltrials.gov, registration number: NCT04962126.

INTRODUCTION

Follicular lymphoma (FL) is the most common indolent B‐cell lymphoma. 1 , 2 First‐line chemotherapy combined with anti‐CD20‐directed antibodies rituximab or obinutuzumab yields long‐term remissions in >65% of patients. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 In the landmark GALLIUM study, achieving a positron emission tomography (PET) complete response (CR) was highly prognostic, emphasising the importance of achieving CR from treatment in FL. 7 However, up to 75% of patients treated with chemoimmunotherapy regimens in the GALLIUM study experienced grade 3+ adverse events (AEs). 5 , 8 Notably, higher toxicity rates in the bendamustine cohort were seen with respect to infections and haematological toxicity. 8 Rituximab monotherapy is a lower toxicity alternative favoured in many jurisdictions for low tumour burden FL; however, it offers inferior disease control in treatment‐naïve patients with high tumour burden. 5 , 8 , 9 , 10 There is a desperate need for newer chemotherapy‐free combinations with similar efficacy but lower toxicity which can be delivered in community clinical settings.

FL outcomes are strongly influenced by the presence and function of tumour‐infiltrating lymphocytes within the tumour microenvironment (TME). The most common TME targeting therapeutic class to date has been immunotherapy targeting programmed cell death 1 and its ligand (PD1/PDL1 inhibitors) using for approximately 40% of cancers. 11 Ours and others' studies have demonstrated promising outcomes from combining PD1/PDL1i with CD20‐targeting agents rituximab or obinutuzumab (O). 10 , 12 , 13 More specifically, the combination of obinutuzumab and atezolizumab (a fully humanised IgG4 PDL1 inhibitor) in relapsed/refractory FL was associated with an overall response rate (ORR) of 54% (complete response; CR 23%) with 12% experiencing (3 episodes in 26 patients) grade 3+ toxicity. However, these doublet regimens have yet to achieve the durable efficacy in either relapse or first‐line of that seen using combined chemoimmunotherapy for treatment‐naïve high tumour burden FL. 12

Adding radiotherapy (RT), a potent immunostimulatory and cytotoxic treatment modality in FL to such immunotherapy‐based combinations has strong rationale. FL is exquisitely radiosensitive, with ultra‐low doses (4 Gy in 2 fractions) achieving high response rates and durable local disease control with negligible risks as evidenced by the landmark FORT trial where durable in‐field remission was achieved in 70.4% of patients with 4 Gy. 14 Furthermore, RT is synergistic with PD1/PDL1 inhibitors in solid tumours and causes rapid and early neoantigen release, enhancing immune targeting. RT has been shown to increase intratumoural and peripheral CD8 T‐cell populations. Elevated CD8 T‐cell levels within the tumour or circulation are associated with improved responses to PD1/PDL1‐based therapies and may act synergistically with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). As demonstrated in an earlier phase I study, PDL1i with RT is effective and safe with doses of up to 30 Gy in 10 fractions in relapsed B‐cell non‐Hodgkin lymphoma. 15 , 16 , 17 Combining PDL1i, obinutuzumab (with its additional antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity activity) and RT offers the theoretical benefits of enhanced disease control by exploiting the immune‐stimulating and cytotoxic synergy between these therapies, yet favourable toxicity.

Here, we describe the results from our investigator‐led phase II study testing the efficacy of first‐line obinutuzumab and atezolizumab with 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) PET‐adapted ultra‐low dose RT in advanced‐stage, treatment‐naïve high tumour burden FL patients.

METHODS

Study design and patient population

The ‘FLUORO’ study (NCT04962126) is a multicentre, single‐arm, phase II trial in adult patients with treatment‐naïve histologically proven grade 1–3A FL according to the World Health Organisation classification. 18 Patients had Ann Arbor stage II–IV disease, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance 0–2, adequate organ function, measurable disease and high tumour burden disease as determined by the presence of one Groupe d'Etude des Lymphomes Folliculaires (GELF) or British National Lymphoma Investigation (BNLI) criteria for treatment. 19 , 20 Key exclusions were active autoimmune disease, immunosuppression, interstitial lung disease, life‐threatening disease complications necessitating urgent cytoreduction, central nervous system involvement, active hepatitis B or C and positive human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) serology. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by an independent ethics committee (HREC/73339/Austin‐2021). All patients provided written informed consent.

Treatment

Patients received six cycles of atezolizumab 1200 mg and obinutuzumab 1000 mg (O + A) intravenously every 21 days (3 weeks) as induction treatment. Obinutuzumab cycle 1 included doses on days 8 and 15. Interim combined 18F‐FDG PET/computed tomography (CT) was performed after cycle two (PET/CT2). Patients with FDG‐avid disease at that time point underwent ultra‐low dose involved‐site RT (4 Gy in 2 fractions, delivered over 2 consecutive days) to the residual sites. At completion of induction O + A, participants with stable disease, partial response (SD/PR) or complete response (CR) received maintenance obinutuzumab 1000 mg 8 weekly for up to 12 cycles. Patients with progressive disease (PD) after six cycles ceased study treatment but continued survival follow‐up (Figure 1).

RT planning

Only patients who had residual FDG‐avid lymphadenopathy >1 cm at PET2 assessment were treated with RT to minimise toxicity in O + A early responders. The International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group guideline was used as a basis for RT planning. 21 Involved site RT aims to treat all evident PET‐avid tumour with an adequate margin to encompass potential adjacent subclinical disease. Extranodal site volumes are variable and are based upon tumour visibility, potential for infiltrative growth and expected side effects. A maximum of six involved‐site RT fields could be treated during the study (Appendix S1). This was a pragmatic decision to limit the volume of irradiated bone marrow and unaffected lymphoid tissue and to accommodate the practicalities of treatment scheduling, particularly it allowed for alternating day treatments if large numbers of sites could not be treated in a single appointment. The RT volumes covered lymph node(s) that were >1 cm on axial imaging and FDG‐avid on the PET/CT2. There was no attempt to cover areas of previous lymphoma that had responded to induction therapy. The clinical tumour volume (CTV) included a margin beyond the gross disease of 1 cm radially and up to 3 cm longitudinally along lines of lymphatic drainage, with consideration of anatomical boundaries (Appendix S1). The planning target volume (PTV) incorporated the CTV plus a margin for set‐up uncertainty, with additional margin applied for respiratory motion where reasonable. The standard margin was 0.5–1.0 cm, with a maximum allowed PTV expansion of 3 cm. Each selected disease site was allowed to be treated with megavoltage photon therapy (3D‐conformal RT or intensity‐modulated RT) or electron beam therapy at the investigator's discretion. The prescribed dose was 4 Gy in two fractions. RT was prescribed to the covering isodose, ensuring that 90% of the PTV is covered by 98% of the dose (D90 = 98% or more) with a maximum dose of 110% (V0.035cc < 110%). Appropriate image verification was performed per institutional practice and dependent on body site (Figure S1). The RT time point after cycle 3 was selected in order to allow initial tumour debulking to reduce field size and to target relatively immunotherapy‐resistant areas of disease.

Study assessments

PET/CT was performed at baseline, after cycle 2 (PET/CT2), optionally after RT delivery (PET/CT3), then after cycle 6 (PET/CT4), as well as every 6 months during maintenance for any patients who achieved less than CR at end of induction. All PET scans were performed on Australasian Radiopharmaceutical Trial Network accredited PET scanners according to standard protocols, with a low‐dose, non‐contrast CT also performed simultaneously. 22 Central analysis of PET radiomics at each time point included the following parameters: maximum standardised uptake value (SUVmax), total metabolic tumour volume (TMTV) and total lesion glycolysis (TLG). These were performed using MIMEncore (Version 7.3.4, MIM Software Inc. Cleveland, Ohio, USA). Lesions were delineated using a ‘lesion ID’ workflow, which is a semi‐automated preselection of FDG‐avid structures defined by a SUV ≥4. Delta TMTV and delta TLG were calculated as follows: percentage change (Delta%) = (PET after cycle 6 – baseline)/baseline × 100. Bone marrow biopsy was performed at baseline and for CR confirmation if any disease was present at baseline.

Safety assessments were performed prior to each cycle and 90 days after completion of treatment. Follow‐up was performed every 3 months until 5 years post treatment.

End‐points and statistical analysis

The primary end‐point was CR rate according to Lugano criteria. 23 Secondary end‐points included ORR, time to next treatment (TTNT), progression‐free and overall survival (PFS and OS) and AEs according to CTCAE v5.0. 24 , 25 TTNT was calculated as a duration from day 1 of first treatment to day 1 of the next treatment. Kaplan–Meier methodology was used to calculate survival proportions (OS/PFS). Exploratory end‐points included correlation of tissue, blood and PET/CT biomarkers such as gene expression profiles (GEP), T‐cell repertoires and radiomics with treatment response. Differences in GEP were determined using the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney test. 26 Fisher's exact test was used to analyse differences in radiomic parameters.

All discrete results were summarised as proportions, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For sample size calculation, a Simon two‐stage design was employed, with 80% power and one‐sided alpha of 0.05 to reject the null hypothesis of an unacceptable CR rate at end of induction of 30% (p0 = 30%). 27 In the first stage, in a sample size of 15 patients, if six or more CR were achieved, the study would continue to recruit an additional 31 patients for a total of 46. The null hypothesis would be rejected if 19 or more CR were observed in 46 patients.

Formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded tissue biopsy samples were obtained from nine patients before commencing treatment. Ribonucleic acid (RNA) was extracted using Qiagen AllPrep deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)/RNA formalin‐fixed paraffin‐embedded kit according to the manufacturer's guidelines and quantified by Qubit RNA high sensitivity assay kit. RNA expression was digitally quantified using the NanoString PanCancer Immune panel (730 immune‐related genes plus 40 housekeeping genes), as previously published. 28 Data were normalised as per the standard Nanostring Advanced Analysis protocols and differential gene expression analysed using Rosalind® platform (https://rosalind.bio/).

Treatment delivery

Figure 1 demonstrates patient composition and treatment details. Fifteen of the 16 patients (94%) received the prescribed six cycles of O + A induction. One patient discontinued treatment due to Grade 3 infusion‐related reaction during Cycle 1 of obinutuzumab. This patient was included in the safety analysis but excluded from the efficacy analysis.

Three patients achieved CR after two cycles of O + A and proceeded directly to the additional four cycles of O + A. Twelve patients (75%) with PR on PET/CT2 were administered ultra‐low dose RT post Cycle 3 as per protocol. RT was delivered to a median of three RT fields (range 1–5). Of these 12 patients, 7 (58%) received RT to three to five sites and 5 (42%) to one to two sites (Table S1). After completion of RT, patients received additional cycles of A + O induction to complete a total of six cycles. No patients included in the efficacy analysis were lost to follow up.

Treatment efficacy

CR, the primary end‐point, was achieved in 93% of evaluable patients at any time point (14/15 95% CI: 70–98) and ORR was 100% during induction (Figure 2A, Figure S2). In the one patient achieving a PR as best response, the Deauville Score was 5. After only two cycles of O + A, ORR was 100%, with CR in 20% (3/15; 95% CI: 43–48). Twelve patients achieved PR at this time point who proceeded with radiotherapy based on local results, by central review, six had DS 4 and five had DS, one had DS2. At the end of study, 73% (11/15; 95% CI: 48–89) of patients remained in a CR. With a median of 47‐month follow‐up (range: 31.25 to not reached), no deaths and four PD events had occurred. Two patients had PD at PET/CT4, one of which was confirmed as histological transformation to diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma. A total of 11 serious AEs occurred in eight patients (Table 2). Seven of these occurred during induction treatment and 73% (8/11) were infections, including four COVID‐19, three other respiratory infections and one urinary tract infection. Median hospitalisation duration for SAEs was 4 days (range 2–26). Only 1/16 (6%) patient discontinued trial treatment due to toxicity (obinutuzumab grade 3 infusion‐related reaction). Drug interruptions due to toxicity occurred during induction due to infection in 6/16 (19%) patients (4 COVID‐19, 2 viral) and during maintenance in eight patients (7 COVID‐19, 1 cystitis). No significant acute toxicity attributable to the addition of RT was noted.

Exploratory analyses and PET radiomics

Baseline median SUVmax (maximum standardised uptake value), SUVmean (mean standardised uptake value), total metabolic tumour volume (TMTV) and total lesion glycolysis (TLG) are presented in Table S2. Baseline mean TLG and TMTV levels were higher (p = 0.02) in patients who achieved PR after two cycles of induction and with PD at any time point compared (4568.8 and 1125.9, respectively) to patients who achieved CR at PET/CT2 and remain in CR (2160.9 and 535.4 respectively). Patients with PD at any time point had significantly lower delta TMTV at the end of induction PET compared to those who remained progression‐free.

Biomarker analysis

Gene expression profiling was performed on nine available pretreatment tumour biopsies with eight samples passing quality control parameters. Differential gene expression analysis comparing patients who progressed versus those who remained progression‐free revealed distinct separation in transcriptional patterns despite the modest cohort size. Several genes were significantly downregulated in patients who progressed, including interleukin‐7 receptor (IL7R), L‐selectin (SELL), AKT (v‐akt murine thymoma virus oncogene homologue) serine/threonine kinase 3 (AKT3), human leucocyte antigen‐G (HLA‐G) and interferon lambda 1 (IFNL1), although these findings did not meet false discovery rate thresholds (Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to describe outcomes from the combination of anti‐CD20 therapy, PDL1‐inhibition and RT in lymphoma. In this phase II study of O + A with PET‐adapted ultra‐low dose RT in treatment‐naïve low‐grade FL, exceptionally high response rates (CR 93%, ORR 100%) were demonstrated with 73% of patients remaining in CR at 4 years of follow‐up. The safety profile was manageable, with only 31% of patients experiencing grade 3+ toxicity during induction. No additional immune‐related AEs occurred from the inclusion of RT with PDL1i compared to historical controls. 10 These results compare to those of front‐line chemoimmunotherapy regimens, where CR rates are up to 80% and reported grade 3–4 toxicity is 70%–75%. 5 , 8 We note our patient population, albeit small, had comparable FLIPI risk profiles to large contemporary first‐line phase III trials. 3 , 5 , 6 The results also are similar to patients with stage III FL treated with wide field RT.

The rich TME composition in FL is suited to immune checkpoint targeting. Promising results have emerged from combination with CD20‐directed therapy. In patients with relapsed rituximab‐exposed disease, O + A yielded an ORR of 54% in an earlier Phase 1b trial, while in treatment‐naïve FL, a previous phase II study of rituximab–nivolumab demonstrated an ORR of 92% with CR in 59%. 10 , 12 Our results demonstrate a clear benefit of adding RT to O + A in the front‐line setting with the ORR of 100% and CR in 93%. This high efficacy supports the growing preclinical mechanistic evidence that low‐dose RT increases immune cell infiltration into the TME and increases antigen release and cross‐priming of T cells and provides rationale for incorporating RT into future immunotherapy‐based regimens. 29

The observed safety profile from the combination of anti‐CD20 therapy, PDL1‐inhibition and ultra‐low dose ISRT to all residual disease confirms earlier reports of the safety of including RT in combined modality regimens for patients with lymphoma. 15 , 30 ultra‐low dose involved‐site radiation therapy (ISRT) is confirmed as an extremely well‐tolerated therapy, with no appreciable toxicities attributable to RT seen in this study. ultra‐low dose RT also potentially avoids the known dose‐dependent immune suppression from RT, which could be counterproductive in the context of immunotherapy. 31

These study findings from using a chemotherapy‐free regimen align with prior chemotherapy trials that show PET radiomics can risk stratify FL patients. Even in this small patient cohort, quantitative PET metrics were associated with treatment outcomes; for example, patients with less than a CR or alternatively PD at any time point had higher baseline TLG and TMTV. Patients who relapsed also had a lower end‐of‐induction delta TMTV, compared with responders. These results are consistent with studies demonstrating high baseline TMTV and TLG and lower delta TMTV confer inferior PFS and early PD within 24 months, independent of FLIPI. 32 , 33

In this study, immune gene expression profiles were associated with outcomes, for example, reduced expression of SELL and IL7R genes conferred PD. Previous studies have demonstrated that downregulation of these genes has been linked to the loss of the central memory T‐cell phenotype and a transition towards an exhausted T‐cell state which can impact the response to anti‐tumour therapy. 34 , 35 , 36 Interestingly, a recent study showed that upregulated expression of SELL and IL7R in CD4+ and CD8+ CAR T cells was associated with improved CAR T cells’ persistence and potentially efficacy. 37 Recurrent genetic alterations in the PI3K–AKT signalling pathway are well documented in cancers, and overexpression of AKT has been reported to be associated with lymphoma resistance. 38 In contrast, association of downregulation of AKT3 and PD in our cohort may reflect distinct biological functions of different AKT isoforms. These translational features are important markers of response and warrant further investigation.

The main limitation of this prospective phase II study is its small sample size, representing only stage I of Simon's two‐stage design due to early closure. As a result, in‐depth subgroup analyses and evaluation of the long‐term durability of the observed high response rates and reporting of late toxicities from RT were not possible. In this design, we also cannot specifically assess the contribution of the RT to overall efficacy in individual patients as responses to obinutuzumab and atezolizumab can be delayed; however, this design deliberately uses early RT to induce enhanced immunostimulatory activity but implements RT after cycle 2 assessments to avoid toxicity in those patients with early sensitivity to O + A. To address this, we did compare our responses to those of published data of O + A and rituximab+nivolumab.

In summary, this Phase II study in patients with treatment‐naïve FL demonstrates promising efficacy from a novel combination incorporating PET‐adapted ultra‐low dose RT into an immunotherapy‐based regimen, using obinutuzumab plus atezolizumab, with an encouragingly low toxicity profile. Early indications from our translational substudies are that PET radiomics and immune gene analyses provide promise as biomarkers for response in immunotherapy combinations. Further exploration of the synergy between RT and immunotherapy is under development in FL with newer agents including bispecific antibodies and in larger, randomised studies using modern and carefully designed RT protocols.
