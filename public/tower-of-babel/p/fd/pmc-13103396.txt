North–South asymmetry of Parkinson’s disease mortality in Brazil between 2009 and 2023: a spatial analysis
Authors: Ariana Moura Cabral, Slawomir J. Nasuto, Julián Benito-León, Clarissa Cardoso dos Santos-Couto-Paz, Eduardo Rocon, Adriano de Oliveira Andrade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated potential regional disparities in the mortality of individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD) in Brazil. A nationwide ecological study was conducted investigating the spatial distribution of sex- and age-adjusted mortality rates of individuals with PD, whose deaths were caused or associated with the disease, in the Brazilian intermediate regions between 2009 and 2023. Global and local spatial analyses were conducted using the global and local Moran’s indices at a 95% confidence level to assess the presence of spatial dependence in mortality rates and identify spatial clusters in the country. The findings demonstrated that the PD mortality adjusted rates are uneven across the Brazilian territory, with higher rates in the south and lower in the north. It is hypothesized that the well-established gradient between the north and south can be caused by various factors, including population ageing, longer survival among people with PD, as well as environmental factors but can also be influenced by socio-economic vulnerabilities and disparities in access to specialized health services. In Brazil, PD has become a serious public health problem with marked regional differences over the years. It is therefore essential to develop public policies in Brazil that protect the health of residents with PD, mitigate risk factors, and ensure better living conditions throughout the disease’s progression.

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is one of the most common neurodegenerative disorders, affecting mainly the elderly population1. The number of people with PD worldwide has more than doubled compared to the 1990s, surpassing 6 million in 2016. Projections indicate that this number will continue to increase due to accelerated population growth and increased life expectancy2. In various regions of the world, PD has not only become more prevalent, but has also been responsible for millions of deaths annually with a consistent increase in recent years, suggesting that PD has been a global public health challenge3.

In Brazil, one of the most populous nations undergoing rapid population ageing as a result of demographic transition4, current estimates appear to align with global trends, although there are not any precise national records on the number of cases of the disease due to the fact that the notification of PD cases in the records of the public health system is not required5. The recent cross-sectional study by Schlickmann et al.6 suggests that 535,999 individuals were living with the disease in Brazil in 2024, a number that, according to the authors, is expected to increase to around 1.25 million in the coming decades. In addition, studies based on national mortality data, such as the studies by Viana and Fantin5, Vasconcellos, Rizzotto and Taglietti7, and Figueiró et al.8, have demonstrated an annual increase in PD-related deaths.

While the cross-sectional study by Schlickmann et al.6 reveals the Midwest and Northeast as the Brazilian macroregions with the highest prevalence of people living with PD among their residents, population-based studies have suggested that the south of the country, particularly the South macroregion, has the highest mortality rates from PD5,8, as well as the highest hospitalization rates among individuals with the disease7. However, none of these studies conducted an objective assessment of the geographical distribution of PD across the national territory.

As there are no national records of PD cases and the representativeness of the sample in continental countries like Brazil is still one of the main limitations of cross-sectional studies, such as the one by Schlickmann et al.6, aimed at estimating the prevalence of the disease, mapping the geographic patterns of mortality among individuals with PD in the Brazilian territorial context can contribute to a better understanding of the spatial dynamics of the disease and its burden in Brazil.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate potential long-term regional disparities in the mortality of people with Parkinson’s disease in Brazil, understanding not only spatial patterns of mortality due to PD, but also characterizing spatial patterns of mortality from other underlying causes in residents with PD. In particular, this study introduces the combined use of global and local Moran’s indices for the spatial analysis of mortality in PD in order to identify local agglomerations in addition to global spatial dependence.

Although there are few studies employing these indices9 and, more generally, spatial analysis10 to understand the distribution of deaths in PD, this type of analysis has been widely used in various fields, including environmental science, epidemiology, urban planning, and transportation11–18, to understand geographic patterns. In particular, several studies have demonstrated that Moran’s indices are valuable tools in mortality analysis, as they help identify spatial patterns and clusters in mortality rates19–22.

In Brazil, studies like this are important not only for identifying potential environmental factors associated with the onset and progression of the disease in the population but also for supporting the planning, management, and evaluation of surveillance and healthcare policies for individuals with PD at the regional and national levels. Additionally, a more comprehensive assessment of the distribution and burden of the disease-related mortality in Brazil is important to better reflect the reality of low- and middle-income countries.

Socio-demographic and clinical profile of deaths

Between 2009 and 2023, a total of 19,979,585 deaths were registered among Brazilian residents. Of these, 57,656 were attributed to PD as the primary cause, while 58,832 listed PD as an associated cause on the death certificate. Therefore, a total of 116,488 individuals with PD died over this 15-year period, which constitute approximately 0.58% of all deaths registered in Brazil between 2009 and 2023.

The annual sex- and age-adjusted mortality rates due to PD in the Brazilian intermediate regions ranged from zero to 6.42 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants. Temporal aggregation of these rates into triennial periods, resulting in rates between zero and 15.56 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants, highlighted the spatial trends observed in annual rates every three years, minimizing regional short-term fluctuations in rates. Over the years, high mortality annual and triennial rates have generally been concentrated in the intermediate regions located in the states of the South and Southeast macroregions. In contrast, the intermediate regions in the states of the North and Northeast macroregions have had lower mortality rates (Fig. 3).

Spatial analysis of the adjusted mortality rates due to PD corroborates the spatial distribution observed visually, indicating significant spatial dependence in PD mortality over the period. Among Brazilian intermediate regions, Moran’s global index ranged from 0.16 to 0.51 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document}) for annual rates and from 0.43 to 0.65 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document}) for triennial rates, suggesting that regions with high PD mortality rates were geographically near high-mortality areas, while regions with low mortality rates were situated close to areas with similarly low mortality rates. In addition, localized clusters were observed, with high-high clusters predominantly located in the southern part of the country and low-low clusters more frequently observed in the northern regions (Fig. 4).

The spatial extent of these two predominant agglomerations varied over time, reflecting fluctuations in the number of intermediate regions included each year. The triennial spatial patterns indicated that, among regions with high mortality rates surrounded by similarly high-rate regions, Rio Grande do Sul and Santa Catarina consistently represented the South macroregion, whereas São Paulo and Minas Gerais were the primary representatives of the Southeast macroregion. Conversely, low-low clusters were mainly located in Acre, Amazonas, Pará and Tocantins in the North macroregion and in Bahia, Ceará, Maranhão, Pernambuco and Piauí in the Northeast.

The rates observed in other regions, particularly in states with high mortality rates that border regions with similarly high rates, do not appear to exhibit exclusive spatial dependence, as they were not categorized into any of the spatial patterns (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p > 0.05$$\end{document}). For instance, some intermediate regions located in Espírito Santo (Southeast) were areas with high mortality rates, neighbouring regions with similarly high rates, but they did not exhibit a statistically significant spatial dependency.

For mortality rates adjusted considering PD as an associated cause, annual rates ranged from zero to 8.89 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants, while triennial rates ranged from zero to 17.68 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants (Fig. 5). These rates associated with the disease also showed significant spatial dependence throughout the study period (Moran’s global indices between 0.18 and 0.47 for annual rates and between 0.38 and 0.54 for triennial rates, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document}). Across the territory, two predominant spatial patterns were identified: clusters of low adjusted mortality rates mainly concentrated in the north of the country and clusters of high adjusted mortality rates predominantly located in the south (Fig. 6).

Although the north-south gradient of disease-associated mortality resembles that of mortality due to the disease, clusters of low disease-associated mortality became more concentrated, especially during certain periods. During years characterized by public health emergencies in Brazil, such as the Zika virus epidemic (2015–2016) and the COVID-19 pandemic (2020), low-rate clusters were largely confined to intermediate regions of states in the Northeast. To assess the potential influence of regional differences in diagnostic completeness on the study findings, annual and triennial mortality rates due to PD were also estimated after redistributing both ill-defined causes of death (i.e., the first correction scenario) and garbage codes (i.e., the second correction scenario). Overall, both redistribution strategies led to increased adjusted rates due to the disease in all intermediate regions with recorded ill-defined deaths or with garbage codes, but the magnitude of this increase was directly related to the proportion of unspecified causes, with larger corrections observed in areas with lower diagnostic completeness.

After redistributing ill-defined causes of death, the corrected annual rates ranged from zero to 6.80 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants, whereas triennial rates varied between zero and 15.89 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants (Fig. 7). The distribution of these rates exhibited significant global spatial autocorrelation, with global Moran’s index ranging from 0.16 to 0.50 (p < 0.05) for the annual rates and from 0.42 to 0.65 (p < 0.05) for the triennial rates, and local spatial clusters of the corrected rates were identified in regions that largely overlapped with those observed for the uncorrected rates (Fig. 8).

On average, approximately 97.74% of the intermediate regions maintained their original spatial pattern each year after correction, with changes affecting about three regions annually. Similarly, triennial patterns also suggested that the redistribution of ill-defined causes of death did not substantially modify the overall spatial configuration observed for uncorrected triennial rates. Discussion

This study is the first attempt to objectively investigate differences in the spatial distribution of mortality of individuals with Parkinson’s disease in Brazil. In this context, this study introduces the combined use of global and local Moran’s indices for the spatial analysis of PD mortality. Unlike the previous study by Dinneweth and Gadeyne9, which was limited to the global index, our approach enhances spatial pattern detection by integrating both indices. Our approach facilitates a comprehensive assessment of spatial clustering by capturing both the overall spatial pattern and the specific locations where clusters emerge.

Furthermore, although previous studies have examined the mortality of PD in Brazil5,7,8, this is the first population-based study to analyse deaths in which PD was recorded as either the underlying cause of death (i.e., PD as a primary cause of death) or as an associated cause of death (i.e., PD as a secondary cause of death). By characterizing the mortality profile of people with PD, whether as the underlying or an associated cause of death, this study highlights the burden of the disease in Brazil and the challenges faced in its management in the country.

In this study, the results also suggest significant regional differences in sex- and age-adjusted mortality rates among individuals with PD, with well-defined spatial clusters located in the north and south of Brazil. For both annual and triennial adjusted rates, the intermediate regions located in the North and Northeast macroregions generally concentrated the lowest mortality rates, while the high rates were predominant in intermediate regions in the South and Southeast macroregions. The difference in mortality of individuals with PD in these two regions may be due to several factors, including differences in the prevalence of risk factors, differences in access to health services, or socio-demographic and economic disparities in these regions.

The concentration of high mortality rates in the South and Southeast regions over the years may appear contradictory, given that these macroregions are among the most economically developed in Brazil (i.e., human development indices between 0.76 and 0.81 according to the last census). However, this profile may be attributed to the increase in life expectancy and rapid growth of the population in these regions, which contribute to a higher prevalence of age-related diseases, such as PD, also leading to higher mortality rates. This may also help explain the consistently higher number of deaths observed among individuals aged 60 years and older with the disease, both when the disease is recorded as the underlying cause of death (Fig. 1) and when it is listed as an associated cause of death (Fig. 2).

This phenomenon appears to be similar to the trends observed in densely populated countries experiencing rapid population ageing, such as China24. However, the recent study by Figueró et al.8 examining PD mortality temporal trends among older adults in Brazil (2002–2021) reports increasing age-specific mortality rates, which may suggest a potential increase in mortality risk among the elderly population beyond changes in population age structure and survival with PD. The authors also indicates that this temporal increase is not homogeneous across age groups, with the most pronounced growth observed among individuals aged 70–79 years.

In particular, this heterogeneous growth among older age groups, characterized by faster increases in mortality among individuals aged 70–79 years and slower growth at ages 80 years and older, points to complex demographic dynamics rather than a monotonic rise in mortality risk. Such patterns are compatible with mortality selection and age-period-cohort dynamics in elderly populations, without implying changes in the intrinsic risk associated with the disease. This may also help explain, for example, the inversion in sex ratio observed after age 80, with a higher number of deaths among women.

While this finding contradicts the expectation of higher prevalence and mortality among men51, differences in survival between the sexes are plausible. This is because, in Brazil, women are more likely to seek medical care both more frequently and at earlier stages of disease progression52,53, which may facilitate earlier diagnosis of PD, co-morbidities and associated diseases, as well as improved clinical management, and increased survival. As a result, a greater proportion of women may reach very advanced ages living with the disease, thereby influencing the observed mortality patterns in older age groups.

However, the absence of data on diagnosis and treatment onset restricts a better understanding of post-diagnosis survival and life expectancy among individuals with the disease. Additionally, although age-specific mortality trends reported in the literature support the spatial patterns identified in this study and contribute to understanding mortality risk across the life course, the absence of prevalence data in the country constrains the distinction between changes in exposure, survival, and actual mortality risk.

Thus, another factor that contributes with high mortality concentration in south is the improved diagnosis of the disease. As these regions have a better healthcare infrastructure25 and more neurologists26, there may be earlier detection and more accurate diagnoses of PD compared to other areas. This could lead to a higher reported prevalence of the disease, which, in turn, might contribute to the observed increase in mortality rates. Consequently, this may create a misleading perception of higher mortality from PD when, in fact, it reflects primarily earlier diagnosis.

Additionally, the concentration of high mortality rates in the south of the country may reflect the greater exposure of residents to risk factors historically introduced by the main economic activities of these areas. In particular, the spatial dependence of adjusted mortality rates, regardless of cause of death (i.e., PD as a primary or secondary cause of death), in various intermediate regions located in states of the South (e.g., Rio Grande do Sul and Santa Catarina) and Southeast (e.g., São Paulo and Minas Gerais) over several years corroborates this idea.

This is because, although the states in these macroregions have geographical and economic differences, both are regions where residents have historically been, and continue to be, exposed to pesticides and heavy metals, either directly or indirectly. In the history of both, agricultural production and mining have played important roles in the economic consolidation of these areas.

The states in the South were once major agricultural centres in Brazil, with the highest proportions of agricultural establishments using pesticides compared to other federative units. This extensive use has exposed the population, particularly agricultural workers, to different pesticides over the years, which could play a role in increasing risk of mortality due to PD. Studies on occupational exposure to pesticides in the south of the country support this hypothesis, revealing that most residents with PD (over 74%) have lived in rural areas, worked in agriculture, and had direct or indirect pesticide exposure27, whose exposure has been associated with a twofold increase in the risk of death compared to unexposed residents (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$HR = 2.32 [1.15, 4.66]$$\end{document}, p = 0.018)28.

These regions are not only a well-established agricultural region, but also an important coal mining area in Brazil since the late nineteenth-century. Over the years, this activity in the South has contributed to the release of heavy metals into the environment, a concern shared by all states in the South29. Thus, although Brazilian research directly associating PD with occupational or environmental exposure to heavy metals is limited, the potential risk for populations in this region is plausible.

The states in the Southeast have also been significant agricultural centres with pesticide consumption in several Brazilian agricultural establishments in the past, second only to the South region30. However, despite its agricultural importance, historically some regions, such as those in Minas Gerais, has been characterized by extensive mineral exploitation since the colonial era31, with a territory particularly rich in mineral reserves, including iron (Fe), manganese (Mn), copper (Cu), antimony (Sb), arsenic (As), gold (Au), aluminium (Al), and uranium (U)32.

Intensive mining activities in Minas Gerais have resulted in the release of large quantities of waste into the environment over time, contaminating soil and fluvial networks32. In addition to the accumulation of extracted mineral fractions, mining activities also introduced heavy metals and toxic elements used in the extraction process, as well as by-products generated during extraction, such as mercury (Hg), zinc (Zn) and Cu33,34.

Exposure to many of these elements (e.g., Fe, Cu, Mn, Hg and Cu) is thought to be linked to the development of PD and may also contribute to its progression35. However, further research in this direction is still needed in Minas Gerais, as well as its neighbouring areas, given the occurrence of several environmental disasters caused by mining tailings dam failures in recent years36,37, the long-term effects of which may influence future PD incidence and mortality indicators in the population.

The well-established agricultural activity of South and Southeast in the country may also help explain the concentration of high rates in the south of the Midwest macroregion (i.e., intermediate regions in Mato Grosso do Sul) over some years. This is because frontier territories like this facilitate the northward expansion of agricultural activities, as observed with the expansion of sugar cane production38. More than geographically connecting the South and Southeast regions to the Midwest, Mato Grosso do Sul is an important geopolitical region that also facilitates Brazil’s connection with its neighbouring countries in South America, particularly in the development of the Bi-Oceanic Corridor39.

This connection with neighbouring units in other countries may also help to explain the concentration, for some years, of high mortality rates only in the border region of Mato Grosso do Sul, reflecting some interference in the dynamics of mortality rates due to the sharing of economic activities, as well as potential environmental risks. In this sense, analyses at the South America level could provide a better understanding of how mortality in neighbouring countries in South America reflect in Brazil.

Although the redistribution of ill-defined causes and garbage codes did not significantly change the overall spatial distribution of PD mortality with approximately 97.74% and 96.24% of intermediate regions preserving their original spatial patterns, respectively, the disappearance of statistical significance or the transition to a low-high pattern in some regions previously classified as low-mortality clusters within the north of the country, even though these specific regions affected were few and varied over time, points to local spatial instability. This finding suggests that some regions in north classified as low mortality may not have experienced genuinely lower mortality levels but rather exhibited higher proportions of poorly defined or non-specific causes of death. Once these were reassigned, the apparent spatial stability weakened, highlighting the sensitivity of cluster detection in relation to data quality adjustments.

Thus, the sensitivity analyses reinforce the internal validity of the study findings by showing that the north-south spatial gradient persists beyond adjustments for differential cause-of-death coding. Nevertheless, though the redistribution of both ill-defined causes and garbage codes mitigates classification bias, it cannot fully account for deeper structural disparities in healthcare access, diagnostic capacity, and death certification practices, especially if specific conditions, such as Parkinson’s disease, are less likely to be diagnosed and documented in resource-limited settings.

Therefore, although the concentration of low adjusted mortality rates in intermediate regions of the North and Northeast may suggest a lower mortality burden, this spatial pattern may also reflect barriers to accessing specialized PD treatment and related health services, rather than a truly lower underlying risk. This is because the availability of neurologists nationwide has increased in recent years, but their distribution remains unequal, with a lower concentration in the north and a higher concentration in the south of the country26.

On the one hand, a lack of specialists can lead to delays in diagnosis or under-diagnosis. Thus, PD may go unnoticed or be misattributed to other causes, as is similarly observed in other low- and middle-income countries40. This, however, may lead to an inaccurate representation of the PD prevalence and mortality in these Brazilian regions, which can affect the implementation of public health policies and the allocation of resources to these regions to better recognise and treat the disease.

On the other hand, the disparities in treatment accessibility, among other factors, may lead residents with PD and their families to migrate in search of better healthcare and improved quality of life. In this sense, studies that investigate the migration and flow of patients with PD who seek specialized treatment throughout their lives could provide a deeper understanding of the potential influence of migration on this spatial pattern. In addition, such studies could facilitate the mapping of Brazilian regions with a high demand for specialized health services for treating PD.

A spatial asymmetry, creating two opposing poles, as observed for Brazil, is also evident in the spatial patterns of PD mortality reported in other countries, such as Belgium9, Spain10 and the United States41. Although the spatial distribution of PD mortality rates in these countries also appears to be shaped by country-specific characteristics, probably reflecting a complex interaction of genetic, environmental, socio-economic, and healthcare-related factors, the emergence of a bipolar pattern in these different regions of the world, marked by a gradient that contrasts with that observed in Brazil, is particularly interesting.

The fact that there is a north-south gradient in Brazil and a south-north gradient in the countries located north of the Tropic of Cancer raises an interesting question that has received limited attention in previous studies evaluating geographical variation in PD mortality: Could increasing geographical latitude be related to higher Parkinson’s disease mortality? This may be because regions situated within the tropical zone receive more sunlight, whereas those closer to the poles receive less. Thus, the bipolar spatial pattern observed may also be influenced by vitamin D insufficiency or deficiency related to sunlight exposure, i.e. UVB (ultraviolet B) radiation exposure. However, those hypotheses should be further verified.

Some case-controls studies in countries located north of the Tropic of Cancer, such as Finland42, the United States43–45, and England46, have suggested a link between the disease and vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency. However, the association between increasing latitude towards the poles and higher Parkinson’s disease mortality remains difficult to explain due to the lack of studies in other regions, particularly countries in the Southern Hemisphere.

This hypothesis highlights the need to carry out research into the spatial distribution of PD deaths in other locations to better assess the influence of geographic latitude on the distribution of deaths due to and associated with the disease around the world. This is because the majority of the few studies available fail to reflect the current distribution of PD deaths. Moreover, these studies exhibit methodological variations, including differences in mortality rate standardization approaches, some adjusting rates solely by age, as well as relying only on visual assessments of rate distribution or primarily emphasizing global spatial dependence.

Understanding global disparities in distribution of mortality due to PD is important for identifying the underlying factors involved in the development and progression of the disease in different populations, as well as for global and regional planning of public health policies for the most vulnerable groups. In this context, the current study is valuable for demonstrating the spatial patterns of a developing country with global dimensions while highlighting the local disparities faced by Brazilian residents with Parkinson’s disease. However, like other nationwide ecological studies, this study has inherent limitations, predominantly because it is based on secondary data.

Even with the higher quality of data from the Brazilian Mortality Information System and greater coverage of death counts in the different Brazilian macroregions over the last two decades47,48, one of the main concerns is the under-diagnosis of the disease, which can lead to deaths to be recorded under other causes and, as a consequence, there is an under-reporting of deaths of individuals with the disease in certain regions. Another limitation concerns the lack of some data in the database that would be useful for controlling potential confounding variables, such as data on diagnosis and treatment onset, individual level of access to health services, occurrence of exposure to environmental risk factors, and socio-economic status.

This study was also limited by the lack of incorporation of other relevant socio-demographic variables into the rate adjustments, such as race. Although race may help explain differences in mortality rates, it is self-reported in Brazil and may not fully capture the country’s racial heterogeneity, which limits its use as an adjustment variable. Although the findings of this study, which were based on rates adjusted jointly by sex and age, contribute to a better understanding of the spatial distribution of PD-related mortality, they do not permit the assessment of age- or sex-specific mortality risks, nor do they facilitate the identification of spatial clusters exhibiting elevated mortality in particular age groups or for a specific sex. Furthermore, given the ecological nature of this study, the analysis relies on aggregated data, thereby limiting the establishment of relationships between variables at the individual level.

Conclusion

In Brazil, Parkinson’s disease has emerged as a public health problem with marked disparities between Brazilian intermediate regions and, more generally, between macroregions. To address the impact of the disease in the country, it is necessary to provide a multifaceted public health response. This includes advancements in the early diagnosis of the disease and surveillance of PD cases at regional and national levels, which would allow for a better understanding of the impact of deaths among individuals living with the disease in the country.

Specifically in underserved areas of Brazil, with geographical and financial barriers to health care, it is important to address the shortage of professionals specialized in movement disorders, either by encouraging the work and permanence of these professionals in these regions, or by training non-specialized health-care workers to better recognize the disease in primary care and, as a consequence, indicate the patient early for specialized care. Greater awareness of the disease among the population, through national public education campaigns similar to those conducted in the country for other diseases, can also help individuals with PD and their family members recognizing the symptoms earlier and seek medical assistance.

The concentration of high mortality rates due to and associated with Parkinson’s disease in the south of the country also highlights the need to implement and strengthen public health strategies that ensure the health of residents and improve the quality of life of those with the disease. These strategies should prioritize the elimination of potential environmental risk factors (e.g., reducing pesticide consumption, encouraging agroecological practices, raising awareness of occupational safety in agricultural activities, and enforcing standards for agrochemical and heavy metal residue levels in soil, water and food); and ensure continuous access to appropriate treatment, rehabilitation and palliative care throughout the disease course.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a nationwide ecological study on the pattern of spatial distribution of mortality of adults with Parkinson’s disease in Brazil between 2009 and 2023.

Study area

Brazil, situated in South America, is one of the largest countries in the Americas, spanning a land area of 8.5 million square kilometres. Its territory is geographically delineated into five macroregions, each with distinct socio-economic characteristics: the North, Northeast, Midwest, Southeast, and South. Development levels vary widely across the Brazilian territory. The Midwest has grown rapidly due to the agribusiness and the influence of the District Federal, which has spurred improvements in infrastructure and services. In contrast, the North and Northeast still face challenges like inequality and remain among the least developed regions in the country.

Studied population

The studied population comprised adults with PD who died between 2009 and 2023 in Brazil. All cases with death certificates listing Parkinson’s disease, classified as G20 in the 10th revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10), were included. This encompassed deaths in which the disease was mentioned either as the underlying cause of death (i.e., the disease or injury that initiated the chain of morbid events leading directly to death), or as an associated cause of death (i.e, immediate, intermediate, or contributing condition that intervened in or significantly contributed to death), thus considering multiple causes of death.

Mortality estimates

Official data on deaths, disaggregated by sex, age, place of residence, year of death and causes of death, were obtained from the Mortality Information System (in Brazil, known as the Sistema de Informação sobre Mortalidade), provided by the Brazilian Ministry of Health. The last data acquisition was conducted in January 2025 to incorporate the final version of the data for the latest available year (i.e., 2023) in the nationwide database.

To assess the spatial distribution of the PD-related mortality in the population over time, age- and sex-adjusted mortality rates were estimated for deaths due to PD and for deaths in which the disease was listed as an associated cause. These estimates were calculated annually and triennially (i.e., five 3-year periods: 2009–2011, 2012–2014, 2015–2017, 2018–2020, 2021–2023) for each Brazilian intermediate region and expressed per 100,000 inhabitants.

To take into account differences in age distributions and the sex ratio across Brazilian intermediate regions, adjusted rates were estimated by the direct method, using the 2022 Brazilian Census as a standard. First, age- and sex-specific mortality rates were calculated, as the ratio of the number of deaths to the corresponding population estimates provided by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics for each intermediate region and period, and subsequently weighted by the corresponding proportions of the standard population to derive the age- and sex-adjusted mortality rates. For these estimates, ages were categorized into 10-year intervals (i.e., 20–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, and 80+ years), starting at age 20 because no deaths were recorded for younger adults with the disease.

In this study, records were excluded when the reported municipality of residence was invalid. In addition, records without data on the individual’s sex or age at death, as well as those where the reported age in the Mortality Information System did not match the age calculated from the birth and death dates, were excluded.

Statistical analysis

In this study, to characterize the socio-demographic and clinical profile of deaths, a descriptive analysis was conducted for the explanatory variables such as cause of death (i.e., PD as a primary or secondary cause of death), sex, age (treated as both a continuous and a categorized variable), race, and geographical area. Numerical variables (e.g., age) were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies to describe the distribution of deaths across population subgroups. In addition, differences in the distribution of categorical variables between groups were assessed using Pearson’s \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2$$\end{document} test, aiming to identify whether the observed distributions of deaths differed across categories. When multiple pairwise comparisons were required, differences in proportions were evaluated using the z-test for two proportions, with Bonferroni correction. For all statistical analyses, a significance level of 5% was adopted.

In addition, a spatial analysis was carried out to investigate the spatial distribution of the mortality rate in the Brazil during the investigated periods. For this purpose, the first-order spatial autocorrelation of the adjusted rates by intermediate regions were analysed for each period, using the global Moran’s index (I) at a significance level of 5%. In the analysis, a spatial weight matrix was constructed using the first-order queen contiguity, in which regions that share a border or a vertex were considered neighbours (Fig. 12).Fig. 12Steps involved in estimating global and local Moran’s indices and performing spatial clustering.

Steps involved in estimating global and local Moran’s indices and performing spatial clustering.

The global Moran’s index measures spatial dependence. This index ranges from − 1 (perfect dispersion) to 1 (perfect clustering), where a positive I (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I > 0$$\end{document}) indicates a positive spatial autocorrelation, meaning that neighbouring regions tend to exhibit similar rates (i.e., similar rates are spatially close). In contrast, a negative I (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I < 0$$\end{document}) represents a negative spatial autocorrelation, where neighbouring regions are more likely to have dissimilar rates (i.e., dissimilar rates are spatially close). The absence of spatial autocorrelation, or spatial randomness, is indicated by values close to zero (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I \rightarrow 0$$\end{document})23.

In addition, the local Moran’s index, a common local indicator of spatial association (LISA), was estimated to identify patterns of spatial association within the Brazilian territory, considering a significance level of 5%. Based on the local Moran’s index, local clusters were obtained and the intermediate regions were classified as: high-high (i.e., regions with high mortality rates surrounded by others with similarly high rates), low-low (i.e., regions of low rates surrounded by similar low-rate regions), high-low (i.e., regions of high rates surrounded by low-rate regions) and low-high (i.e., regions with low rates close to locations with high rates)23.

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1), which is a programming language and open-source software for data visualisation and statistical analysis. The analyses were performed in RStudio, an integrated development environment.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the potential impact of regional differences in the quality of cause-of-death certification on the distribution of Parkinson’s disease mortality in Brazil, a sensitivity analysis was conducted using two alternative strategies for correcting adjusted mortality rates due to PD prior to spatial analyses. The first strategy was based on the redistribution of deaths classified as ill-defined causes prior to the adjusted rate estimates, while the second extended the redistribution to garbage codes, encompassing the main intermediate mechanisms of death and non-specific diagnoses considered unsuitable as valid underlying causes.

In the first strategy, ill-defined causes were defined as ICD-10 Chapter XVIII codes (R00–R99), representing symptoms, signs, and abnormal findings not elsewhere classified. In the second, garbage codes were defined as the following ICD-10 categories: ill-defined causes (R00–R99), septicemia (A40–A41), pulmonary embolism (I26), heart failure and unspecified heart diseases (I50–I51), aspiration pneumonitis (J69), respiratory failure (J96), acute and unspecified renal failure (N17, N19), sudden cardiac death (I46.1), hepatic failure (K72), essential hypertension (I10), pulmonary edema (J81), and arterial embolism and thrombosis (I74).

These considered conditions represent intermediate mechanisms of death or non-specific diagnoses with a high potential to bias cause-specific mortality estimates. They were classified as garbage codes based on the level 1 and level 2 categories proposed by the Global Burden of Disease Study54,55, which identify diagnoses most likely to compromise the validity of underlying cause-of-death statistics, as well as the Brazilian evidence from mortality surveillance systems56, which have documented these diagnoses as frequent intermediate or non-specific causes of death.

In both approaches, half of deaths originally classified as ill-defined causes (i.e., in the first strategy) or garbage codes (i.e., in the second strategy) were proportionally redistributed across well-defined natural causes, excluding external causes of death (ICD-10 Chapter XX, codes V01-Y98), within the same region, period (year or triennial), age group, and sex. Following redistribution, mortality counts were recalculated, sex- and age-adjusted rates due to PD were recomputed using the same standard population adopted in the main analysis, and all spatial analyses were repeated.

The results of these scenarios were compared with those of the main analysis to assess the robustness of regional patterns. For both investigated scenarios, annual and triennial differences in local spatial classifications before and after mortality rate correction were assessed using Bhapkar’s test for marginal homogeneity. In addition, the level of agreement between the spatial classification before and after rate correction in each year and triennial was assessed using Cohen’s kappa (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}). Both were used to distinguish between systematic changes in spatial pattern distributions and stability at the level of individual units. All statistical tests were conducted at a significance level of 5%.
