Prophylactic oxytocin infusion for reducing blood loss during elective caesarean delivery: A randomised controlled trial
Authors: Praveena Govindan, Jyotsna Sharma, Kubera N.S., Pankaj Kundra, Dilip Kumar Maurya, Anish Keepanasseril
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesOptimum dose, route, and timing of oxytocin administration to prevent postpartum haemorrhage during caesarean section are uncertain. We compared the effect of oxytocin infusion initiated either at skin incision or after head delivery on blood loss and ease of foetal head delivery during elective caesarean section.MethodsIn this open-label, randomised trial with parallel arms, 68 women with singleton pregnancies in cephalic presentation, undergoing elective caesarean delivery at term in a tertiary centre in Puducherry, were enrolled. Randomisation was performed using a simple random approach. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes, which were opened after informed consent to allocate participants to receive an oxytocin infusion (6 mIU/min), started either at the time of skin incision (intervention group) or after foetal head delivery (control group). All received oxytocin 5IU bolus after head delivery, followed by infusion for four hours postpartum. The primary outcome was the amount of blood loss.ResultsMean blood loss was lower among those started on oxytocin skin incision than the control group (485.8±159.2 mL vs. 589.1±216.6 mL, P=0.029). Median haemoglobin decline was significantly lower in the intervention group (0.6 vs. 1.0 g/dL, P=0.011). Ease of foetal head delivery, need for additional uterotonics, and neonatal outcomes were comparable between the groups.Interpretation and conclusionsThe initiation of oxytocin at the skin incision may lead to significantly lower blood loss and a lower fall in haemoglobin level compared to its initiation after foetal head delivery among women undergoing elective pre-labour caesarean section at term.

Obstetric haemorrhage, especially postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), accounts for up to 60% of maternal mortality in developing nations.1 The amount of blood loss is a significant concern to obstetricians during caesarean delivery, with PPH complicating 3-15% of the cases.2 Uterine atony is the major contributor to PPH in the majority of cases, with oxytocin established as a uterotonic agent for the prevention of PPH, even during caesarean section. The response to intravenous oxytocin is immediate and has a short half-life of 3 to 12 min; therefore, it is necessary to administer it as an infusion for prolonged action.3 Giving oxytocin at the time of elective caesarean delivery is likely to mimic normal physiology and induce uterine contraction if started early at the time of skin incision, thereby reducing blood loss.

Recommendations and guidelines on the prevention of PPH give varying recommendations on the dose and route, the majority without guidance on the timing of administration of oxytocin in caesarean section.4,5 The timing of administration vary from the time of skin incision or visceral peritoneal opening to the time of placental separation.6,7 A recent meta-analysis on the timing of administration found only four studies, including 700 women, that observed a reduction in blood loss and the need for additional uterotonics when oxytocin administration was initiated before the foetal delivery.6 However, the timing of administration among the four studies also differed, with one administered at the time of skin incision, which resulted in a significant reduction in blood loss, the need for uterotonics, and a decrease in haemoglobin levels compared to the preoperative period. This study aimed to compare the effect of timing of initiation of oxytocin infusion, at the time of skin incision, with that started after foetal head delivery, on blood loss and ease of foetal head delivery among women undergoing elective caesarean section.

Methods

Study design, setting and population

This randomised controlled trial, open label with parallel arms, was conducted at the department of Obstetrics & Gynaecology, Jawaharlal Institute of Post Graduate Medical Education and Research (JIPMER), Puducherry, a tertiary care centre in the southeastern region of India, from June 2016 to May 2017. The Institute Ethics Committee (Human Studies) of the Institute approved the study. The CTR number is CTRI/2016/12/007531.

Women aged more than 18 years, undergoing elective caesarean delivery at term with a singleton pregnancy in cephalic presentation, were offered to participate in the study. Pregnancies with placenta previa, suspected adherent placenta, abruptio placentae, polyhydramnios, previously more than one caesarean section, severe anaemia (haemoglobin less than 8 g/dL), heart disease, and coagulopathy were excluded.

Randomisation and concealment of allocation

We used a computer-generated, predetermined schedule of randomisation that used a simple random approach for intervention allocation. The allocation assignments were secured in sequentially opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), and the envelope number was marked on the outside. The study prepared the randomisation list, and allocation concealment was prepared by an independent individual not involved in the study. The allocation sequence was stored in a computer database to prevent any interference. The envelope was opened once informed consent was obtained, revealing the allocation to one of the intervention arms.

Intervention and comparator groups

Intervention group

Oxytocin (6 μIU/min) was initiated during skin incision. In a 500 mL normal saline (NS) bottle, three units of oxytocin were added, and it was administered using a dial flow meter at a rate of 60 mL/h.

Control group

Started Oxytocin after foetal head delivery. Dose and administration details were similar to the intervention group.

After foetal head delivery, Oxytocin 5 IU bolus was given intravenously in both groups. If the uterine tone was inadequate after three minutes, further choice of uterotonics was made at the discretion of the obstetrician and anaesthesiologist. After the skin closure, oxytocin infusion (10 IU to 500 mL of NS), was continued at 100 mL per hour for 4 h in both groups.

Monitoring

All patients included in the study were monitored during the intraoperative period and subsequently in the recovery room and postoperative ward. They were monitored for evidence of uterine atony, blood loss, blood pressure, heart rate, and urinary output.

Assessment of blood loss and need for additional uterotonic

Total blood loss was estimated volumetrically (based on the volume of blood and amniotic fluid measured in separate suction chambers) and gravimetrically (based on the weight difference of pads weighed before and after surgery). Total blood loss was calculated as the sum of blood volume in suction jars and the weight difference of the pads used. The decision related to excessive haemorrhage requiring additional uterotonics during a caesarean was made by the senior obstetrician (co-investigators) who performed the surgery. On postoperative day 1, haemoglobin estimation was done.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the amount of blood loss during caesarean section. The secondary outcomes included the use of additional uterotonics or surgical management of postpartum haemorrhage, ease of foetal head delivery, adverse effects of an oxytocin bolus dose, duration of surgery, neonatal APGAR score, neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission, and postoperative haemoglobin level. Ease of delivery was assessed as (i) ‘difficult’, defined by the surgeon’s inability to deliver the head with standard techniques, often requiring manoeuvres/instruments, and (ii) as ‘easy’ when the head can be delivered with minimal effort, without the need for special techniques.

Sample size calculation

Assuming that the mean difference of blood loss following initiation of the prophylactic oxytocin infusion at the skin incision is lower by 120 mL, as observed in a previous report,8 using the standard deviation of 186.7 and 149.3, with a power of 80% and a confidence interval of 95%, we recruited 68 women in the study. The calculation was performed using openEpi version 3, an open-source sample size calculator.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows, version 20 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), adhering to the intention-to-treat principle. The statistician conducting the study was masked to allocate groups. Continuous variables were summarised as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median with Inter-quartile Range (IQR), depending on the distribution. They were compared using Student’s t-test and the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and proportions and assessed using the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P value of <0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Hypertension was the most common co-morbidity, and previous caesarean section (not eligible for trial on assessment with RCOG criteria) was the most common indication. The time intervals between the skin incision and baby delivery, as well as between baby and placental delivery, were similar in both groups. Neonatal outcomes, including birth weight and APGAR scores at 5 min, were similar in both groups, and none of the babies required admission to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Discussion

The reduction in mean blood loss and the fall in haemoglobin was significantly lower among the group that received the oxytocin at the start of the skin incision compared to its initiation after head delivery. No side effects were noted in either group, with only one requiring another uterotonic for reducing blood loss among the group that had initiation after foetal head delivery.

Despite widespread use, there is limited data to guide optimal oxytocin dosing in patients undergoing elective caesarean delivery.6,7 The recommended dosage of oxytocin varies widely among professional bodies, ranging from 5 IU as a slow IV bolus to 40 IU/L as an infusion.5 Even during vaginal birth, a nonlinear (J-shaped) relationship between prophylactic oxytocin dosing and mean blood loss, as well as blood loss exceeding 1000 mL and the need for additional uterotonics, was reported in a systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of 13 randomised trials (n=8961 participants).9 Dosage and timing of oxytocin initiation for preventing PPH during caesarean section vary from region to region, and most are empirical.

A non-labouring uterus at term remains more sensitive to oxytocin, where doses between 2.5-6.0 mIU/min initiate contraction when oxytocin is administered for labour induction. So, starting oxytocin at a dose of 6 mIU/min may lead to contraction of the uterus without increasing the risk of deleterious effects of high doses, given as bolus or infusion. Adequate uterine tone can be achieved during labour and delivery with small doses, accelerating contractions after delivery without the risk of hemodynamic changes such as hypotension, which can increase significantly when administering a bolus of five units or more.3,9

Recent network meta-analysis suggested that the use of an intermediate bolus of 3-5 units with an infusion of < 60 units /hour achieves an optimal reduction of blood loss, whereas the risk of side effects was increased with >5 units (as bolus) or >60 IU/h (as infusion) during caesarean births.10 Due to the safety concerns, Heesen et al11, in 2019, released an international consensus statement on the use of uterotonic agents during caesarean birth, citing the lack of evidence for women having an elective caesarean birth on the oxytocin dosing and the variation in the timing and speed of administration recommends low-dose prophylactic oxytocin regimens for cesarean births: a 1 IU bolus with 2.5 to 7.5 IU/h infusion for elective cesarean births and a 3 IU bolus over ≥30 seconds followed by a 7.5 to 15 IU/h infusion for intrapartum cesarean births.

The reduction in mean blood loss (130 mL) and a lower fall in haemoglobin, as indicated in the study, were consistent with a recent meta-analysis of four studies, which showed a decrease in blood loss when oxytocin was started before foetal delivery. However, the present study failed to demonstrate any difference in the need for additional uterotonics when oxytocin was given before versus after foetal head delivery, as was shown in the meta-analysis.6 The lower reduction in haemoglobin may be explained by the lower blood loss resulting from the earlier initiation of contraction, achieved by administering oxytocin at the skin incision, which contributes to the lower fall in haemoglobin. The ease of foetal head delivery was comparable; however, three in the control arm, compared to one in the intervention group, had difficulty with head delivery.

Since the study was limited to low-risk women undergoing elective caesarean section, devoid of major PPH risk factors, the generalisability of the results is limited. Even as the operators followed the study protocol, the effect of the duration of their experience and the variation in their techniques/practices on the study outcomes should be considered another limitation. The study was underpowered (post hoc, it was found to be 61%) to conclusively suggest the better regime in reducing the blood loss. So, future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to address the important question of the timing of initiation of oxytocin to prevent PPH among women undergoing caesarean section.
