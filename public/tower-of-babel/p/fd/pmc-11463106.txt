Cases of endophthalmitis caused by Candida albicans and Candida dubliniensis identified via internal transcribed spacer deep sequencing
Authors: Kazunobu Asao, Noriyasu Hashida, Kazuichi Maruyama, Daisuke Motooka, Shota Nakamura, Kohji Nishida
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe report two cases of fungal endophthalmitis induced by Candida species identified based on internal transcribed spacer 1 (ITS1) sequencing.Case presentationIn two cases, endophthalmitis was suspected, and the patients underwent pars plana vitrectomy. Case 1 was a 64-year-old woman with a history of cataract surgery 10 days prior. She had a history of anal primary melanoma, which metastasized throughout the body and subsequently relapsed. Vitreous culture and ITS-1 deep sequencing revealed the presence of the rare fungus, Candida dubliniensis. Case 2 was a 54-year-old man with a history of liver cancer and kidney failure. Culture methods and ITS1 deep sequencing both revealed the presence of Candida albicans. Both patients exhibited good visual prognoses after treatment with topical and systemic antibiotics.ConclusionsWe present two cases of fungal endophthalmitis caused by two Candida species identified by both the culture method and ITS1 deep sequencing. The fungal pathogen was identified by ITS deep sequencing three days after sample submission; the culture method yielded results after 1 week. These findings support the applicability of ITS1 sequencing for timely pathogen identification for cases of fungal endophthalmitis and provide detailed taxonomic information at the species level.Keywords: Candida albicans, Candida dubliniensis, Endophthalmitis, Deep sequencing, Internal transcribed spacer, Metagenomic analysis

Introduction

The development of endophthalmitis can severely affect a patient’s visual prognosis, potentially resulting in severe visual impairment [1, 2]. In particular, postoperative infections, although infrequent, can result in vision loss if not promptly and appropriately treated [1, 2]. Clinically, exogenous endophthalmitis, such as acute bacterial endophthalmitis, results from microbial infection occurring as a complication of ocular surgery, intravitreal injections, or ocular trauma, with coagulase-negative staphylococci being a common causative agent [1–3]. Although fungal postoperative endophthalmitis is rare compared to acute bacterial endophthalmitis, it is commonly reported in tropical countries [4]. Recently, contamination of viscoelastic material has been reported as the cause of fungal postoperative endophthalmitis, with Fusarium and Candida species identified as the pathogens [5].

Chronic postoperative endophthalmitis, which is rare, is often attributed to fungi or commensal bacteria [1–3]. In such cases, the onset of inflammation occurs 2 weeks after surgery, and persistent inflammation worsens gradually over days or weeks [1–3]. Fungal endogenous endophthalmitis accounts for 5–10% of all endophthalmitis cases and typically occurs in immunocompromised hosts, such as patients receiving anticancer drugs or steroids, those with diabetes, and those undergoing hemodialysis [6, 7]. The causes of fungal endogenous endophthalmitis are diverse, occurring in patients with a history of central venous catheter use, abdominopelvic surgeries, onychomycosis, or trauma [6, 7]. Fungal infection results in systemic susceptibility, focusing on infection from distant organs [8]. Fungal pathogens could enter the bloodstream and cause an infection within the eye [8]. Although fungal endophthalmitis is a rare complication [6], it can result in severe visual impairment [5–8].

Candida is the most common fungal pathogen; it is associated with catheter infections, bursa abscesses, and trauma [9]. Ocular candidiasis occurs in 10% of patients with candidemia [9–10]. Therefore performing a fundus examination in all patients with candidemia is recommended [11]. Candida species are the most common pathogens responsible for ocular infections in compromised hosts, accounting for 97% of fungal pathogens [9, 12, 13]. Notably, Candida albicans is detected in 60–65% of cases of fungal endophthalmitis [9, 12, 13]. However, the prevalence of non-albicans Candida species, such as Candida glabrata, Candida parapsilosis, and Candida dubliniensis, is gradually increasing [14]. In particular, Candida dubliniensis is closely related to Candida albicans and represents a major issue for public health owing to its drug resistance potential [15].

It is difficult to identify pathogens comprehensively, particularly fungi, using established detection methods, such as PCR and culture, because culture conditions and primer sets must be optimized [6–8]. As a result, culture-based identification of fungi usually requires at least 7 days to 2 weeks [6–8]. Furthermore, existing primer sets can only be used to identify specific fungal species via the PCR-mediated amplification of 18–28 S rRNA [16]. However, recently developed next-generation sequencing platforms have enabled metagenomic analyses based on fungal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) rRNA sequences for the comprehensive identification of fungi [17]. The ITS region has a high degree of sequence variation, providing a basis for the identification of fungi at the species level [17, 18]. Recently, ITS deep sequencing has gradually increased to identify fungal pathogens in ocular infections or clinical settings from very small volumes of clinical samples of approximately 100 µL [17, 18]. Accordingly, this approach may be beneficial in cases of endophthalmitis, as the accurate and prompt identification of causative pathogens is essential for early and effective antibiotic selection to preserve visual acuity [19].

The aim of this report was to investigate whether ITS deep sequencing can successfully identify pathogens in cases of fungal endophthalmitis and its utility for species-level identification and selection of antibiotics.

Findings

Case 1

The patient was an immunocompromised 64-year-old woman who had undergone cataract surgery on the left eye 10 days prior at our hospital. She had a history of myasthenia gravis, oral steroid use, primary anal malignant melanoma, and systemic metastasis of melanoma. The malignant melanoma infiltrated into the uterovaginal region, cervix, urethra, and rectum, and the patient had previously undergone chemotherapy. She had a history of fungemia originating from anal ulcers caused by primary anal melanoma and was treated with systemic antifungal drugs. Subsequently, the culture results of the blood sample confirmed the presence of Candida dubliniensis.

One month before the cataract operation, the best-corrected visual acuity was 35/50 and 20/50 for the right and left eye, respectively. Intraocular pressure was within normal limits in both eyes, and no obvious abnormal findings were observed at the anterior or posterior segments of either eye.

In the early postoperative period, five days after surgery, the best-corrected visual acuity of the left eye improved to 45/50, while the visual acuity in the right eye remained stable. However, 10 days after surgery, during follow-up, the patient suddenly noticed a decrease in vision, with her visual acuity in the left eye worsening to 20/50 and intraocular pressure at 12 mmHg. In the left eye, severe anterior chamber inflammation was noted, characterized by findings such as a cell grade of + 2, keratic precipitates, injection of conjunctiva, and hypopyon (Fig. 1A). The fundus was not visible (Fig. 1B). Despite the absence of traction or retinal detachment, slight vitreous opacity was observed on the B-scan image (Fig. 1C). Electroretinography (ERG) was performed to confirm retinal function, revealing abnormalities in rod function in the left eye, whereas that of the right eye was within normal range (Fig. 1D). Blood test results revealed elevated levels of β-d-glucan (16.4 pg/mL), white blood cells (12,700 × 103/µL), and C-reactive protein (CRP) (1.43 mg/dL). Based on the ERG findings, retinal malfunction was suspected. This disorder was potentially caused by the advancement of the infection to the posterior segment. Therefore, the patient underwent emergency pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) to prevent any further loss of visual acuity.

At the time of PPV, intraocular vancomycin (10 mg/500 mL), ceftazidime (20 mg/500 mL), and voriconazole (10 mg/500 mL) were administered in Balanced Salt Solution Plus (BSS Plus) (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, US) through the infusion line, with continuous irrigation during surgery. The vitreous specimen was cultured and subjected to metagenomic analysis after the operation. ITS1 deep sequencing was performed as reported in a previous study [14], and the presence of Candida dubliniensis was detected on day 3 (relative abundance, 99.6%; Fig. 1E). The culture results revealed the presence of the rare species Candida dubliniensis on day 7. Under the supervision of an infectious disease specialist, intravenous liposomal amphotericin B (3–5 mg/kg) was administered for 10 days, along with hourly topical 0.2% fluconazole. One month after surgery, the fundus remained stable (Fig. 1F), and the patient’s visual acuity improved to 20/25. However, during follow-up, blood test results revealed a worsening of renal function, prompting the cessation of intravenous antibiotics. Subsequently, administration of oral fluconazole (200 mg/day) was initiated for 3 weeks. At the last visit, 6 months after surgery, her visual acuity was 20/25 and the fundus was stable. During the follow-up period, there were no complications associated with the surgical procedure, such as postoperative wound infection, vitreous hemorrhage, and retinal detachment.

In this report, bacterial and fungal cultures were prepared at the Osaka University Hospital, under the following culture conditions: carbon dioxide gas culture at a temperature of 35 °C. The culture medium included blood agar, chocolate agar, Sabouraud agar, and desoxycholate, as needed. The culture period ranged from two days to two weeks, and drug susceptibility testing was concurrently performed. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values for antifungal susceptibility were as follows: >0.06 µg/ml for micafungin, > 0.125 µg/ml for flucytosine, > 0.25 µg/ml for fluconazole, > 0.125 µg/ml for miconazole, > 0.125 µg/ml for itraconazole, 0.015 µg/ml for voriconazole, and 0.25 µg/ml for amphotericin B. Metagenomic analyses were performed using ITS1 deep sequencing at the Research Institute for Microbial Diseases at Osaka University, and all procedures were conducted by specialized staff. DNA was extracted from vitreous samples, and each library was prepared following the Illumina standard protocol with a primer set (ITS1-F: CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA and ITS2: GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC) targeting the ITS1 region of the fungal ribosomal RNA for 24 h. Subsequently, 301-bp paired-end sequencing of the amplicon was performed using a MiSeq v3 600 cycle kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) for 3 days. Paired-end reads were merged, filtered, and denoised using DADA2. Taxonomic assignment was performed using QIIME2 (ver2020.2) against the ntF-ITS1 database. Notably, the cost of investigating the comprehensive pathogens per sample was relatively low.

Case 2

An immunocompromised 54-year-old man was referred to our hospital. In terms of systemic diseases, the patient had a history of decompensated liver cirrhosis (Child-Pugh score class C), liver cancer, kidney failure, and acute cholecystitis. He underwent percutaneous transhepatic cholangio-drainage, and a chest tube was inserted to alleviate pleural effusion to treat acute cholecystitis.

For routine follow-up, the patient previously visited our hospital three months prior, during which his visual acuity was 60/50 in his right eye and 50/50 in his left eye. The intraocular pressure in both eyes was 15 mmHg. Several days before the patient presented to our hospital, he experienced a sudden onset of floaters in his left eye and a decrease in vision. Concurrently, his general condition deteriorated, and he was transferred to our department from another hospital for systemic treatment.

On examination, the best-corrected visual acuity of his left eye was hand motion and that of the right eye was 60/50. The intraocular pressure was 11 mmHg in his left eye and 14 mmHg in his right eye. In the right eye, no obvious abnormal findings were observed at the anterior or posterior part. In the left eye, severe anterior chamber inflammation was noted, characterized by findings such as a cell grade of + 2, keratic precipitates, hyperemia of the conjunctiva, and hypopyon (Fig. 2A). We could not confirm the presence of fundus due to severe inflammation of the anterior segment of the eye (Fig. 2B), and B-scan images revealed slight vitreous turbidity with no retinal detachment (Fig. 2C). An ERG was performed to confirm retinal function; this revealed a reduction in the a-wave and b-wave amplitudes in the left eye compared with those in the right eye (Fig. 2D). The blood test results revealed elevated levels of β-d-glucan (204.5 pg/mL) and CRP (2.86 mg/dL). To confirm the presence of fungal pathogens, we performed a blood culture, which showed the presence of Candida albicans. The MIC values for antifungal susceptibility were as follows: >0.03 µg/ml for micafungin, > 0.125 µg/ml for flucytosine, > 0.25 µg/ml for fluconazole, > 0.25 µg/ml for miconazole, > 0.06 µg/ml for itraconazole, 0.015 µg/ml for voriconazole, and 0.5 µg/ml for amphotericin B. Based on the ophthalmic and systemic examinations, endophthalmitis-induced retinal dysfunction was suspected, and the patient underwent emergency PPV. Intraoperatively, intraocular vancomycin (10 mg/500 mL), ceftazidime (20 mg/500 mL), and voriconazole (10 mg/500 mL) dissolved in BSS Plus were administered through the infusion line and continuously irrigated. The vitreous specimen was cultured and subjected to metagenomic analysis. ITS1 deep sequencing revealed the presence of Candida albicans on day 3, with a relative abundance of 98.5% (Fig. 2D). The fungal culture results also revealed the presence of Candida albicans on day 7. Intravenous fluconazole (200 mg/day) was administered for 9 days, resulting in notable improvement in fundus findings during the follow-up period. Based on the patient’s systemic condition, the antifungal drug was switched from intravenous drip to oral administration. With oral fluconazole (400 mg/day) for 14 days, the fundus showed remarkable improvement (Fig. 2E). Two weeks post-surgery, visual acuity improved to 20/40. Oral fluconazole (400 mg/day) was administered for a total of three weeks. One month later, visual acuity was maintained and no complications, such as vitreous hemorrhage and retinal detachment, were observed after the surgical procedure during the follow-up period.

Discussion and conclusions

Endophthalmitis is a medical emergency that can lead to poor visual prognosis; thus, it is crucial to determine whether the causative pathogen is bacteria or fungi for appropriate treatment. According to the metagenomic analysis, 16SrRNA analyses can be used to identify bacteria, while ITS sequences can be used to identify fungi. In addition to culture, combining the two types of metagenomic analyses simultaneously for the comprehensive identification of causative pathogens is useful. Our results suggest that ITS1 deep sequencing is useful for the timely detection of fungal pathogens in patients with endophthalmitis and distinguishing fungi at the species level. Based on ITS deep sequencing, a single fungal taxon can be identified as the causal agent of fungal endophthalmitis; this information may be useful for selecting appropriate antifungal drugs.

Culture results for patients with postoperative acute endophthalmitis revealed that approximately 60% of pathogens were Gram-positive cocci, 30% were Gram-negative bacteria, and fungi were rare [1, 6, 20]. Among fungal infections, Aspergillus, Fusarium, and Candida species were identified to cause fungal endophthalmitis associated with cataract surgery and vitrectomy [1, 6, 20]. Notably, C. dubliniensis is isolated in only approximately 1–2% of cases of candidemia and is a rare infectious agent [21, 22]. Fungal endophthalmitis due to C. dubliniensis infection has been reported in immunosuppressed patients and those with systemic diseases, such as liver abscess, chronic kidney failure, and HIV [15]. In cases of fungal endophthalmitis due to C. dubliniensis, the pathogen initially invades other organs, enters the bloodstream, and causes an endogenous infection in the eye [15, 21, 22]. In our cases, the hosts were susceptible to infection (i.e., the individuals had a history of melanoma, liver cancer, or kidney failure), explaining the development of fungal endophthalmitis. Once severe infection occurs in compromised hosts, prompt and precise treatment is essential to preserve visual acuity; thus, the detection time is the most important factor for prompt treatment [19]. In both cases, 7 days were required to culture the fungal pathogen after sample submission, while identification through ITS deep sequencing was completed within 3 days. Cultivating pathogenic agents via culture methods is difficult because different agents require different culture conditions [1, 2, 23]. In particular, fungal culture presents difficulties, such as lower sensitivity and longer duration [1, 2, 23]. Furthermore, although PCR has enabled rapid identification with high specificity, only specific species can be identified owing to primer design [1, 2, 23]. In contrast, metagenomic analysis is useful in comprehensively identifying pathogens in samples at the species level from minute specimen amounts. For ITS sequencing, only 100 µL of the specimen is required for identification, and despite this limited volume of specimen, this technique allows for repeated identification of suspicious pathogens.

Based on culture alone, it is difficult to distinguish C. albicans from C. dubliniensis because these two species have similar biological characteristics and phenotypes [24, 25]. Clinically, it is necessary to differentiate between C. albicans and C. dubliniensis because the latter exhibits high expression of the transporter Mdr1p and has decreased susceptibility to antifungal drugs, such as fluconazole [26, 27]. Previously, Candida-induced endophthalmitis has been treated with azole drugs, including fluconazole and voriconazole [6, 26, 27]. Physiochemically, fluconazole has good penetration into the vitreous body; however, Candida species have shown the potential for resistance to fluconazole [6, 26, 27]. In Case 1, initial culture results failed to identify the pathogens; however, the patient had a history of C. dubliniensis fungemia. In this case, aggressive immunosuppressive therapy may have contributed to the C. dubliniensis fungemia, and an antifungal drip was administered intravenously. Fluconazole resistance can be readily induced following exposure to the antifungal drug [6, 26, 27], and C. dubliniensis may have acquired fluconazole resistance in this case. Under the supervision of an infectious disease specialist, treatment with liposomal amphotericin B, a broad-spectrum antifungal agent, was initiated. On day 3, ITS1 deep sequencing revealed the presence of C. dubliniensis as the causative pathogen. Given that the patient had no history of renal failure or allergic reactions to antifungal agents, liposomal amphotericin B was deemed appropriate for treatment. In patients with candidemia, systemic administration of amphotericin B or combined systemic administration of amphotericin B and 5-fluorocytosine are effective [6, 26–28]. Therefore, amphotericin B is recommended for severe cases of candidemia and infections involving species other than C. albicans [28, 29]. In Case 2, intravenous fluconazole was initiated, resulting in significant improvement in fundus findings during the follow-up period. As previously reported, Candida albicans species are the most common cause of candidemia and are relatively sensitive to fluconazole [6, 26–28]. In cases of mild to moderate ocular inflammation, the administration of fluconazole may be sufficient [29]. However, as reported in previous studies, it is necessary to carefully adjust antifungal drugs while considering the patient’s medication history and monitor any signs associated with the deterioration of general health, such as renal failure and electrolyte abnormalities 30.31.

ITS1 deep sequencing could accurately identify two types of Candida species in these cases. In both cases, as determined by ITS1 deep sequencing, the occupancy rate of the detected fungal taxon in the samples was close to 100%, whereas a variety of fungi were identified in the control and Candida was not detected in the negative group. A previous report has indicated that a species can be considered the causative bacterium in bacterial endophthalmitis if its occupancy rate is > 30% [19]. However, in fungal infections, the occupancy rate is nearly 100%, and the growth of a single fungal species may be characteristic of fungal endophthalmitis. Distinguishing between bacteria and fungi would be useful for selecting appropriate antimicrobial or antifungal agents [19]. If the culture test is negative and the ITS sequencing reveals the causative pathogens, then antibiotics should likely be continued [19]. In both the cases reported in the present study, species-level pathogen information was useful for selecting appropriate antibiotics after considering drug resistance. By performing shotgun metagenomic sequencing, which targets whole DNA, it is possible to detect fungi and search for the presence or absence of genes related to drug resistance [32, 33]. However, shotgun metagenomic sequencing is costly, and data analysis and detection take time [32, 33]. For targeting bacteria, various databases, and analytical methods for searching for drug-resistance genes have been established, but these are still in the development stage for fungi [17, 32–34]. In cases of endophthalmitis, the primary goal was to detect the causative pathogens in the short term. Therefore, we performed a metagenomic analysis of the fungal ITS region. It is difficult to evaluate drug susceptibility but the fungal species level can be promptly identified [34, 35].

In this case series, a major limitation is the difficulty in selecting appropriate cases. However, ITS sequencing holds the potential for aiding diagnosis in cases where distinguishing between infection or inflammation in the ophthalmic field is challenging. Microbial culture may produce false negatives, depending on sample collection technique and time since symptom onset. Marked challenges associated with performing metagenomic analysis, such as the need for specialized laboratory settings, access to next-generating sequencing devices, and the requirement for skilled data scientists and technicians, persist. Moreover, when there are only a few samples for pathogen detection, the costs for identification tend to be relatively higher. Therefore, further studies with larger samples are required to reduce the running costs accordingly. The are also some limitations associated with sample preparation, including the small sample size due to the low incidence of fungal endophthalmitis. Moreover, the limited number of vitreous bodies may have resulted in missed fungi during identification. Thus, further studies are required to determine the validity of ITS sequencing for detecting pathogens in patients with fungal endophthalmitis; these results should be compared with those obtained from culture methods.

In summary, we report two cases of fungal endophthalmitis in which the pathogen was identified using ITS1 deep sequencing. ITS deep-sequencing data could identify the rare C. dubliniensis, which has a low abundance of approximately 0.1% of all isolates. Fungal endophthalmitis is characterized by the growth of a single fungus as the causative agent. In diagnosing and treating fungal endophthalmitis, ITS deep sequencing can provide rapid pathogenic information at the species levels, potentially guiding the selection of appropriate antibiotics.
