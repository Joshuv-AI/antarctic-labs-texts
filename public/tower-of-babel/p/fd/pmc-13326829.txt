“Intrapericardial Approach” for Venous Outflow Reconstruction in Living‐Donor Liver Transplantation for Budd‐Chiari Syndrome: Surgical Techniques and LongTerm Outcomes
Authors: Koichiro Hata, Tetsuya Tajima, Satoshi Ogiso, Shinji Uemoto, Etsuro Hatano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnlike deceased‐donor liver transplantation, living‐donor liver transplantation (LDLT) for Budd‐Chiari Syndrome (BCS) presents distinctive challenges in hepatic venous (HV)‐outflow reconstruction because diseased HV–inferior vena cava (IVC) cannot be entirely replaced with healthy donor vessels. In the Asia‐Pacific region, where LDLT predominates, BCS frequently involves the hepatic IVC, thus requiring innovative surgical strategies for HV–IVC reconstruction. Here we introduce a novel series of surgical technique—“intrapericardial approach (IPA)”—which reconstructs HV‐outflow tract in the pericardium using only healthy vascular tissues, thereby minimizing post‐transplant BCS recurrence. Detailed procedures are illustrated for four typical LDLT scenarios, classified by graft‐type (left‐ or right‐lobe) and by the need for IVC reconstruction (acute or chronic BCS). A representative surgical video is also provided. Of our 2089 consecutive liver transplants, 20 patients underwent LDLT for BCS: 10 with conventional IVC‐preserving techniques (cavoplasty and/or patch‐plasty) and 10 using IPA. Across all four clinical scenarios aforementioned, the key concept of IPA—creating new HV‐outflow with only healthy vessels—was consistently applicable. IPA was associated with significantly lower post‐transplant BCS recurrence (p = 0.04) and improved both relapse‐free and overall recipient survival (p = 0.02 and 0.03, respectively) compared to conventional techniques, yielding 100% graft and patient survival through 10 years. This report also highlights key considerations and pitfalls for pre‐, intra‐, and post‐transplant management in BCS‐LDLT. In conclusion, IPA appears to be associated with reduced post‐transplant BCS recurrence and favorable short‐ and long‐term outcomes, offering a novel practical solution for challenging HV–IVC reconstruction in BCS‐LDLT.

Introduction

Budd‐Chiari Syndrome (BCS) is an intractable veno‐occlusive disease characterized by thromboembolic obstruction of hepatic venous outflow tracts including hepatic veins (HV) and/or inferior vena cava (IVC) [1]. A notable geographic variation exists in disease presentation: hepatic IVC involvement is far more prevalent in the Asia‐Pacific region than in Western countries [2]. In Japan, IVC lesions are observed in up to 93% [3], where living‐donor partial liver transplantation (LDLT) comprises 90% of all liver transplantation (LT) procedures. While deceased‐donor liver transplantation (DDLT) allows complete replacement of diseased HV–IVC structures with healthy donor vessels, LDLT cannot offer this advantage. Consequently, HV–IVC reconstruction in BCS‐LDLT remains challenging in these regions [2, 4].

Conventionally, HV–IVC reconstruction in BCS‐LDLT has involved patch‐plasty [5] and/or cavoplasty [6] of the native IVC, or replacement of diseased HV–IVC with prosthetic/artificial conduits [7] or cryo‐preserved vein grafts, etc. [8, 9, 10] However, once BCS lesions recur, patient mortality remains high due to limited deceased‐donor availability in such countries [11, 12] and technical difficulties in re‐LDLT for recurrent BCS [13]. Therefore, the initial surgical strategy for IVC lesions and HV–IVC reconstruction significantly influences both short‐ and long‐term outcomes in BCS‐LDLT.

To address this need, we developed a novel series of surgical techniques—“intrapericardial approach (IPA)”—which reconstructs the HV‐outflow tract in the pericardium using only healthy blood vessels to minimize post‐transplant BCS recurrence. This study aims to: (1) illustrate detailed procedures for each of the four representative LDLT scenarios, defined by graft‐type (right‐ or left‐lobe) and the necessity of IVC reconstruction (with or without sufficient collateral circulation), accompanied by a representative surgical video; (2) highlight key considerations, technical nuances, and pitfalls across pre‐, intra‐, and post‐transplant management in BCS‐LDLT; and (3) evaluate the clinical effectiveness of IPA by comparing post‐transplant BCS recurrence and patient survival rates with conventional techniques in our large cohort exceeding 2000 LTs.

Preoperative Preparations

First, in BCS‐LT, the advantage of DDLT—allowing complete replacement of diseased HV–IVC with healthy donor vessels—is more pronounced than in other indications. Therefore, in patients unresponsive to conservative treatments or exhibiting acute liver failure, early listing for DDLT should be prioritized, even in regions with limited deceased‐donors, rather than defaulting to LDLT as the sole option.

Generally, IPA requires no special preparation; however, for acute BCS with insufficient collateral circulation, preparing veno‐venous (VV) bypass is advisable. Leveraging the widespread availability of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) devices following the COVID‐19 pandemic, we routinely initiate VV‐bypass using an ECMO device immediately after induction of general anesthesia (before surgery) for acute BCS. The VV‐bypass—placed from the infra‐renal IVC via the femoral vein to the superior vena cava (SVC) via the right internal jugular vein—not only relieves lower‐body congestion but also allows sufficient time to evaluate, procure, and reuse healthy autologous IVC segments for reconstruction [14, 15]. Preoperative coordination with anesthesiologists is therefore essential. The benefit of preemptive decompression of subdiaphragmatic congestion mostly outweighs the potential disadvantage of mild bleeding tendency associated with low‐dose anticoagulation to maintain extracorporeal circulation. When preoperative assessment is inconclusive, we usually test‐clamp the IVC intraoperatively; if hemodynamic instability occurs, a cannula is then inserted directly into the infra‐renal IVC to initiate VV‐bypass.

Surgical Procedures

α. Left‐Lobe LDLT for Chronic BCS

First, we present the left‐lobe LDLT technique for chronic BCS with well‐developed collaterals, where neither intraoperative VV‐bypass nor IVC reconstruction was required (Figure 1, Video S1). After opening the pericardium, the IVC root is isolated and secured therein. The IVC is transected both cranially (intrapericardial) and caudally (ventral) to its diaphragmatic penetration, and both ends are suture‐closed. This technique is useful in left‐lobe LDLTs, as the HV passes more ventrally than in right‐lobe grafts. Complete circumferential detachment of the IVC from the diaphragm is usually unnecessary. Linear staplers are also useful to facilitate subdiaphragmatic transection and closure.

Left‐lobe LDLT for chronic BCS with well‐developed collateral circulation. After taping and securing the IVC root within the pericardium, the IVC is transected cranially (within the pericardium) and caudally (ventral to the diaphragm), then suture‐closed on both ends (see Video S1). Following total hepatectomy including hepatic IVC removal, the common trunk of MHV and LHV of the left‐lobe graft is anastomosed either to the original IVC root (if healthy) or directly to a new orifice of the RA (if affected/involved). Since the liver position is shifted several centimeters cranially, leaving the recipient's PV long enough or an interposition vein graft to extend the PV is usually needed. Neither VV‐bypass nor IVC reconstruction is necessary if well‐developed collaterals adequately compensate for the caval flow. After total hepatectomy including the hepatic cava, the common trunk of the middle and left hepatic vein (MHV + LHV) of a left‐lobe graft (Segment 2 + 3 + 4) is anastomosed either to the original IVC root—if healthy—or directly to a newly created orifice in the right atrium (RA)—if intrapericardial IVC is affected. This technique shifts the graft position several centimeters cranially; therefore, leaving the recipient portal vein (PV) long enough or an interposition vein graft for extension is sometimes needed. For PV lengthening, an external iliac vein graft is often used, as it provides both adequate dimensions and healthy vessel structure, even in patients with BCS or other cirrhosis. Neither VV‐bypass nor IVC reconstruction is necessary if well‐developed collaterals adequately compensate the caval flow.

β. Left‐Lobe LDLT for Acute BCS

In acute BCS, subdiaphragmatic lower body congestion is generally severe due to underdeveloped collateral circulation. Early initiation of VV‐bypass following anesthesia induction is therefore essential. Before or immediately after PV transection, a second drainage cannula is inserted into a PV branch (e.g., dilated inferior mesenteric vein) or directly into the PV stump to relieve the splanchnic congestion. Early establishment of this “two‐way VV‐bypass”, which diverts congested blood from both the lower body and abdominal organs to SVC–RA, is critical for reducing intraoperative blood loss and stabilizing hemodynamics during LDLT for acute BCS [14].

Likewise, the IVC root is encircled/secured in the pericardium. Following en‐bloc hepatectomy with the hepatic cava, the autologous hepatic IVC (auto‐IVC) is isolated on a backtable and thoroughly inspected both intra‐ and extraluminally. The healthy segment of the auto‐IVC is identified and harvested. To minimize the potential risk of HV‐outflow stenosis/obstruction following possible BCS recurrence post‐transplant, the auto‐IVC is reversed cranio‐caudally and the originally caudal (suprarenal) end—farthest from the BCS lesion and thus presumed healthiest—is anastomosed to the IVC root in the pericardium [14]. The opposite (originally cranial) end is anastomosed to a prosthetic conduit to bridge the caval defect (Figure 2A). The LHV + MHV trunk of a left‐lobe graft is then anastomosed end‐to‐side to the optimal site on the healthy auto‐IVC (Figure 2A). When the BCS lesion does not extend to the IVC or is minimal, the original HV confluence is resected and patch‐repaired, allowing direct anastomosis of the LHV + MHV trunk to a new opening on the healthy IVC wall cranial to the original confluence (Figure 2B).

γ. Right‐Lobe LDLT for Chronic BCS

The same procedure as described above is performed until the total hepatectomy including hepatic IVC. If sufficient collaterals have developed, neither VV‐bypass nor IVC reconstruction is required. The right hepatic vein (RHV) should be anastomosed to the recipient's IVC root in the pericardium or directly to a new opening on the RA, which usually requires an intervening vein graft for extension (Figure 3A). When a healthy segment of the auto‐IVC is available, it should be retrieved and utilized. If unavailable, the external iliac vein serves as an alternative: approximately 10 cm segment is harvested, cut‐opened longitudinally, and reconfigured circumferentially to form a graft approx. 3 cm in diameter and length. When neither option is available, we have utilized an enlarged splenic vein—harvested from the splenic hilum following giant splenectomy—as an interposition graft using the branch‐patch technique. To prevent BCS recurrence post‐transplant, HV‐outflow should be reconstructed using healthy vascular tissue whenever feasible.

Unlike left‐lobe LDLTs (Figure 1), the IVC must be detached circumferentially from the diaphragm and resected en bloc with the recipient liver to ensure the shortest route for RHV reconstruction. Even when the shortest route is obtained, it usually requires an intervening vein graft for extension. Typically, V5, V8, and RHV are formed into a single hole, and a large intervening vein graft is anastomosed thereto on a back‐table to easily reach the IVC root in the pericardium. (B) Acute BCS without adequate collaterals. Surgical procedures until IVC reconstruction are described in the legend for Figure 2A. The right‐lobe graft is then transiently put in the appropriate/natural position to mark the optimal site for RHV anastomosis. After opening a large orifice on the right side of the autologous IVC, to which a single‐holed venous outflow is anastomosed in an end‐to‐side fashion. δ. Right‐Lobe LDLT for Acute BCS

Unlike chronic BCS, collateral vessels are insufficiently developed in acute BCS; therefore, VV‐bypass is better initiated early in surgery or even before. Similarly, the IVC root should be taped/secured within the pericardium. Unlike left‐lobe cases, the diaphragmatic portion of the IVC must be circumferentially and thoroughly mobilized from the diaphragm to allow the RHV or its intervening vein graft to pass through the shortest anatomical route. Particular care is required, as dilated, fragile phrenic veins—often serving as collaterals—are embedded in the fibrous diaphragmatic base, a hallmark of IVC‐involved BCS. Following double‐clamping the RA, the IVC is transected intrapericardially and removed en bloc with the liver.

The procedures hereafter have been detailed elsewhere [14]. Briefly, the autologous IVC is easily isolated extracorporeally and thoroughly examined on a backtable [15]. After retrieving the healthy segment, the auto‐IVC is reversed cranio‐caudally to more reliably prevent post‐transplant BCS recurrence: The originally caudal end—the farthest from the BCS lesion and thus considered the healthiest—is anastomosed either to the IVC root or directly to the RA in the pericardium [14]. Provided that only the IVC segment farthest from the primary BCS lesion is selected, its integrity is verified by meticulous inspection and palpation of both the adventitial and luminal surfaces, and the graft is used after cranio‐caudal inversion, the likelihood of increasing the risk of post‐transplant BCS recurrence is very low.

Unlike chronic BCS, the remaining IVC defect is necessarily reconstructed using an artificial graft such as expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE) due to inadequate collaterals development. Then RHV is anastomosed end‐to‐side to the healthy auto‐IVC (Figure 3B). These procedures enable to create HV‐outflow using only healthy vessels even in LDLT, thereby minimizing the potential risk of BCS recurrence.

Long‐Term Outcomes

Patients and Methods

Overall, a total of 2089 LTs were performed in 1974 patients at our center (June 1990–March 2023). Of these, 22 patients were diagnosed with BCS. A patient with secondary BCS from polycystic liver disease was excluded from analysis. Thus, BCS accounts for 1.0% of all LTs (21/2089). After excluding a patient undergoing DDLT, a total of 20 LDLT recipients—17 adults (age at LT ≥ 18 years) and 3 children (< 18 years)—were enrolled. The median age was 32.5 years (range: 3–66). Results

Patient Characteristics & HV–IVC Reconstruction

Excluding the two earliest cases with missing IVC data, 17 of the remaining 18 patients had IVC lesions (94.4%; Table 1). Only one patient in Group‐Control had no IVC lesion. All three children (one 3‐year‐old and two 11‐year‐olds) were included in Group‐Control, while all patients in Group‐IPA were adults. In Group‐Control, autologous retro‐hepatic IVC was preserved in all 10 cases; four (40.0%) underwent cavo‐plasty or patch‐plasty—alone or combined—to relieve IVC stenosis/occlusion, to or near which the liver‐graft HV was anastomosed [10]. In contrast, in Group‐IPA, IVC lesions were not preserved nor formed (0%, p = 0.09 vs. Group‐Control) but rather resected in eight patients (resection rate 80%, p < 0.001, Table 1), with four requiring IVC reconstruction, while the remaining four did not due to well‐developed collaterals. In two patients, IVC lesions were minimal that were resected and patch‐closed. Of these 10 patients, techniques α–δ (Figures 1, 2, 3) were applied in three, two, one, and four cases, respectively. In Group‐IPA, IVC resection, reconstruction, and VV‐bypass procedures significantly prolonged operative time compared to Group‐Control (15.1 [14.0–16.9] vs. 11.5 [9.9–16.0] hours, p = 0.047).

Sites of BCS Recurrence

BCS recurrence was defined as post‐transplant congestive liver dysfunction caused by HV‐outflow stenosis requiring intervention (e.g., transcatheter balloon dilation). Cases without a pressure gradient between intrahepatic HVs and its downstream IVC–RA were not considered recurrence. In our 20 patients who underwent LDLT, seven (35%) developed BCS recurrence. Of these, three (43%) had stenosis involving a zonal segment from the HV anastomosis to the suprahepatic IVC, one (14%) had isolated suprahepatic IVC stenosis, and two (29%) had stenosis confined to the HV anastomosis. The remaining one (14%) developed extensive thrombotic occlusion extending from the intrahepatic HVs through the HV anastomosis to both the supra‐ and infra‐hepatic IVC after discontinuation of anticoagulants and hydroxyurea for essential thrombocythemia at 15 years post‐transplant. No patient exhibited a recurrent lesion confined solely to the infra‐hepatic IVC.

Interventions for Recurrent BCS

In Group‐Control, 6 of 10 patients developed BCS recurrence (post‐LDLT HV‐outflow stenosis), and two of these underwent percutaneous transluminal angioplasty (PTA) with stent placement. Notably, one of the two required 12 PTA + stenting procedures pretransplant and, after post‐transplant recurrence, has undergone more than 10 additional PTA (+ several stenting) over 25 years, reflecting the extremely refractory nature of the condition. The patient with widespread thromboembolism extending from the intrahepatic HVs to the IVC, as aforementioned, was not eligible for PTA but treated with systemic anticoagulation + thrombolytic therapies; two patients died early, on postoperative day 22 and at 3 months; and one died at 18 months post‐transplant from acute pulmonary embolism secondary to recurrent BCS. No patient could undergo re‐transplantation. In contrast, in Group‐IPA, only one of 10 patients developed HV anastomotic stenosis, which was resolved with a single balloon dilatation without stenting.

Recurrence Rates & Patient Survivals

Although limited by a small sample size and non‐contemporaneous design, the cumulative incidence of BCS recurrence was significantly lower in Group‐IPA than in Group‐Control (p = 0.04, log‐rank test, Figure 4A). Consequently, both relapse‐free (Figure 4B) and overall recipient survival (Figure 4C) were significantly better in Group‐IPA than in Group‐Control (p = 0.02 and 0.03, respectively), yielding 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year overall graft and patient survival rates of all 100%.

Postoperative Considerations

Reconstructed blood flows, including hepatic arteries, PV, HVs, and/or IVC, should be routinely monitored by Doppler ultrasonography early after LDLT. Long‐term vigilance is also warranted in BCS patients, as underlying prothrombotic predisposition/etiologies may persist or only partially resolve even after transplantation. While protein‐C, protein‐S, or antithrombin deficiencies may improve post‐LT, risks associated with antiphospholipid syndrome or myeloproliferative disorders can sustain or even worsen, contingent on disease control. Lifelong anticoagulation should be considered for BCS patients post‐LT, tailored to individual risk profiles and disease etiologies [2, 16, 17]. If discontinuation is unavoidable for trauma, surgeries, or life events, etc., adequate restoration of anticoagulation and fibrinolytic function must be confirmed, along with careful monitoring of thrombogenic markers such as D‐dimer.

Since BCS often affects young adults, we have experienced three female patients who developed BCS during gynecological hormone therapy, including infertility treatments, and eventually underwent LDLT. Two resumed fertility treatments post‐LDLT and successfully gave birth, with one experiencing four pregnancies and three deliveries starting 3.5 years post‐transplant; anticoagulation was maintained throughout. She switched from oral warfarin to twice‐daily subcutaneous heparin injections from prepregnancy through post‐partum for over five years. No BCS recurrence, thrombosis, or bleeding events occurred, even during three cesarean sections. She is currently living happily with her husband and three children, maintained on direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs). This favorable outcome, unattainable with artificial conduits, underscores the long‐term superiority of IPA, which exclusively utilizes healthy vessels for revascularization.

Artificial conduits for HV/IVC reconstruction basically require anticoagulants even after adequate recovery of anticoagulant and fibrinolytic profiles [7, 18]. For instance, in a patient with cryptogenic BCS who underwent technique‐δ, subarachnoid hemorrhage developed eight years post‐LDLT, necessitating discontinuation of anticoagulants. Shortly, localized IVC thrombosis/occlusion occurred at the prosthetic conduit (Figure 3B), despite full restoration of anticoagulant/fibrinolytic capacity. Nevertheless, the thrombus did not extend to the HV anastomosis and the HV–IVC outflow remained unaffected and intact throughout, highlighting sustained robustness of IPA utilizing healthy vessels.

Given relatively younger age and long life expectancy of most BCS patients, surgical strategies must accommodate a variety of future scenarios. Anticoagulation regimens should be individualized, considering pharmacokinetics, underlying disorders [19], use of artificial grafts, liver functional recovery, vascular configuration and flow dynamics, occupation, patient preferences, and life events, etc. A multidisciplinary approach involving hepatologists, cardiologists, hematologists, and/or obstetrician‐gynecologists is necessary to optimize long‐term management.

Tips and Pitfalls

Technical Tips

The RA wall is thinner and more fragile than many LT surgeons expect, necessitating meticulous care when clamping or performing HV–RA anastomosis. Deep RA clamping requires vigilance to avoid compromising myocardial perfusion. The author encountered an intraoperative sudden arrhythmia, likely attributable to diminished perfusion in the right coronary artery branches caused by deep RA clamping. Since then, we routinely include a cardiovascular surgeon in the surgical team, not only for direct HV–RA anastomosis but also when firm/deep clamping of the RA is required. BCS‐LT should therefore be regarded as not only LT but also complex vascular/cardiac surgery.

Technical Pitfalls

Post‐transplant liver regeneration is fundamental to LDLT in the first place; however, good liver regeneration can be a “double‐edged sword” when using artificial grafts for HV reconstruction. The smaller the liver, the greater the post‐transplant liver regeneration, yet artificial conduits never accommodate such liver growth or deformity. The risk is further accentuated in smaller (graft‐to‐recipient weight ratio ≤ 0.8%) or younger (especially from 20s donor) livers, which can more than double in size within 1–2 weeks post‐transplant [20]. Accordingly, HV anastomoses to prosthetic conduits should be designed to anticipate forthcoming kinking, overstretching, and torsion of the HVs caused by parenchymal re‐expansion upon reperfusion and subsequent liver growth/regeneration [21]. From this standpoint, our technique—reconstructing HV‐outflow exclusively with healthy autologous or donor‐derived vessels with sufficient dimensions—offers significant advantages.

Anticoagulation Management

Anticoagulation plays a central role in the management of BCS throughout the initial, perioperative, and chronic phases. An important pitfall in initial treatments is the routine use of unfractionated heparin, which should be avoided [1, 22, 23]. In our cohort, two of 20 patients (10%) developed heparin‐induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) due to prior exposure to unfractionated heparin at referring hospitals. One patient unavoidably required argatroban for perioperative anticoagulation including intraoperative VV‐bypass [14], resulting in a significant increase in blood loss. Generally, HIT‐antibodies wane time‐dependently, which is likely accelerated by post‐transplant immunosuppression. In fact, negative conversion was confirmed postoperatively in both cases. Nevertheless, persistent HIT‐antibody positivity during critical phases can cause serious complications. Overall, the safest perioperative anticoagulation strategy for BCS is to initiate low‐molecular‐weight heparin, switching to a vitamin‐K antagonist once the clinical course stabilizes [1, 23, 24]. Although DOACs also appear safe and effective, long‐term evidence remains limited [24, 25]. In BCS associated with antiphospholipid syndrome, lifelong anticoagulation—preferably with warfarin—is recommended, as DOACs have yet to demonstrate non‐inferiority [26]. Randomized controlled trials are desirable but challenging given the rarity of BCS‐LT. Furthermore, long‐term calcineurin inhibitor exposure causes progressive renal dysfunction, with 27% of LT recipients developing chronic kidney disease within 10 years post‐LT [27]. Since DOACs are primarily renally excreted, they are contraindicated in advanced renal impairment. Comprehensively, anticoagulation should be individualized, considering underlying etiologies, renal function, the use of artificial conduits, and overall clinical context [28].

Discussion

This report presents a novel series of techniques—“intrapericardial approach (IPA)”—for HV‐outflow reconstruction in BCS‐LDLT. The key concept is to avoid diseased segments of the HV–IVC and use only healthy vessels for creating HV‐outflow, thereby minimizing the risk of BCS recurrence. Regardless of the clinical presentation (acute or chronic) or the graft type (right‐ or left‐lobe), this key concept is consistently applicable across all four typical scenarios of BCS‐LDLT. An accompanying surgical video is also provided as an educational reference. Given its association with significantly lower rates of disease recurrence, as well as better relapse‐free and overall recipient survival, IPA may represent a promising solution for challenging HV–IVC reconstruction in BCS‐LDLT. Comparisons of post‐transplant BCS recurrence across studies are still unreliable because the definition of “recurrence” remains inconsistent; in contrast, patient survival after BCS‐LT is reported more consistently. Given that approximately 93%–94% of BCS in Japan involve IVC lesions, and that the annual rate of deceased liver donation remains low at only 0.96 per million population as of 2024, the 1‐, 5‐, and 10‐year graft and patient survival rates of all 100% achieved with IPA represent highly favorable outcomes under these constraints.

In principle, the most downstream anastomosis should be performed in the pericardium, but one might argue that if a healthy IVC wall is obtained, pericardiotomy may not always be mandatory. In one patient, the IVC was fully mobilized from the diaphragm, and a healthy segment of IVC wall was seemingly obtained; therefore, the HV was anastomosed end‐to‐side immediately caudal to the pericardium without pericardiotomy. However, this patient developed HV anastomotic stenosis 16 months post‐LDLT—the sole recurrence in Group‐IPA. The patient received one balloon dilation and has remained symptom‐free for over ten years thereafter. This case highlights the importance of intrapericardial, rather than supradiaphragmatic, anastomosis to ensure long‐term anastomotic patency.

BCS is a syndrome arising from diverse underlying disorders, and the extent of thrombotic occlusion varies substantially among patients; therefore, it could be argued that the IPA does not universally need to be applied in all cases. However, in our BCS‐LDLT cohort, all recurrent lesions developed at or downstream (toward the RA) from the graft HV anastomosis to supra‐hepatic IVC, and no patient exhibited recurrence confined to the infrahepatic IVC. After excluding the patient who developed extensive thromboembolism from the intrahepatic HVs to the IVC following discontinuation of therapy for essential thrombocythemia, the remaining 86% of BCS recurrences (six of seven patients) would likely have been preventable if the IPA had been employed. Collectively, these findings suggest that adopting IPA can substantially reduce BCS recurrence, regardless of the sites and the severity of BCS lesions. Therefore, we also adopt IPA as our standard approach in DDLT. Total replacement of the diseased HV–IVC segment with healthy vasculature is technically far more straightforward in DDLT than in LDLT, because the whole‐liver graft includes the donor's healthy IVC.

A notable procedure‐specific complication was diaphragmatic hernia in 2 of 10 patients—one with a right‐lobe and the other with a right posterior‐segment graft. This is likely attributed to intentionally loose closure of the pericardial/diaphragmatic window to prevent HV–IVC outflow stenosis. In contrast, no such complications were observed following left‐lobe LDLTs, likely because liver positioning effectively closed the window. Based on these experiences, we are considering preventive measures—such as mesh closure of the diaphragmatic/pericardial window—when using right‐sided grafts.

As a limitation, this is a single‐center retrospective cohort study with a relatively small sample size of 20 BCS‐LDLT cases, even though the total number of institutional LTs reached 2089. However, the primary aim of this work was not a definitive comparative effectiveness evaluation but rather a detailed technical description, with the outcome data provided to support the technical feasibility and potential utility of the approach. However, given the rarity of BCS‐LDLT—only 1.0% of all LT procedures—conducting a large‐scale prospective trial is not realistically feasible [1, 2]. Additionally, the 30‐year observation period may confound the findings due to evolving perioperative managements, including pharmacological advances. Nevertheless, the significant reduction in BCS recurrence is likely attributable to surgical strategies rather than immunosuppressive regimens.

In conclusion, the intrapericardial approach appears to be associated with reduced post‐transplant BCS recurrence and favorable short‐ and long‐term LDLT outcomes. This novel technique provides a feasible and practical solution for complex HV–IVC reconstruction in BCS, especially in regions where LDLT predominates or IVC lesions are prevalent.
