Discharge Remote Monitoring Systems for Glycemic Management After Hospital Discharge
Authors: Nirali Shah, Camilla Levister, Riana Jumamil, Carol Levy, Margaret Crawford, Christian Hicks, Selassie Ogyaadu, Aslihan Ipek, Abigail Tamru, Madeleine Rouviere, Leah Kaplan, Ying Xu, Harsimran Singh, Daniel Cherñavvsky, Grenye O'Malley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary In this feasibility study of the use of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) after hospital discharge, CGM use was well accepted with high satisfaction scores.Transplant and high‐severity cohorts were not meeting glycemic targets immediately after discharge.This study adds to the limited literature on postdischarge glycemic care by demonstrating that CGM integration into telehealth diabetes care is feasible and by identifying areas for future research.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) affects approximately 25% of hospitalized patients in the United States, with 30‐day readmission rates of 14%–20%, nearly twice that of patients without DM [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Risk is heightened in patients who have undergone surgery, received transplants, use steroids, or experienced hypoglycemia near the end of hospitalization [5]. Structured discharge plans incorporating remote patient monitoring may support postdischarge glycemic management and reduce readmissions.

Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) has been shown in randomized controlled trials to improve glycemic control with increased time in range (TIR), decreased glucose variability, reduced hospital readmissions, and higher patient satisfaction [6, 7, 8, 9]. However, research on implementing real‐time CGM at hospital discharge remains limited [8]. Identified barriers include technological illiteracy, patients feeling overwhelmed during recovery, and limited healthcare professional support [10]. A prior study using flash glucose monitoring at discharge was terminated early due to poor recruitment [11], and a recent randomized controlled trial using intermittent CGM postdischarge found only nonsignificant trends in TIR improvement [12].

We present here a prospective, single‐arm feasibility study of patients with in‐hospital dysglycemia who were successfully recruited to implement Dexcom G6 (San Diego, CA, USA) at the time of hospital discharge with postdischarge telehealth interventions. Our analysis focuses on glycemic management across 30 days post‐discharge.

This single‐site, open‐label, prospective single‐arm study evaluated the impact of real‐time remote monitoring of blood glucose and vital signs after hospital discharge. This study was approved by the site's Institutional Review Board and conducted following its Program for the Protection of Human Subjects. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Recruitment was conducted from February 2023 to October 2023 at a single urban medical center. Given the small sample size and exploratory nature of this feasibility study, analyses were descriptive. No formal hypothesis testing was performed, and the study was not powered to detect statistically significant differences.

Eligible participants were 21 years of age or older who were treated for dysglycemia during hospitalization. Major exclusion criteria included acute delirium, active bleeding disorder, lack of appropriate sites for devices, and barriers to accurate monitoring of vital signs, including persistent atrial fibrillation. Recruitment efforts focused on high healthcare utilization populations, including admissions for solid organ transplant and newly diagnosed DM. Participants were recruited directly from the hospital floors.

After informed consent, participants were trained in the setup and use of CGM (Dexcom G6, San Diego, CA, USA). Training was targeted at 0–2 days before anticipated discharge. CGM alerts went to a participant's personal or study phone and were customized to the participant; low alert was set to be no lower than 70 mg/dL, and high alert was set to no higher than 300 mg/dL. Study team members (3 physicians and 1 nurse practitioner on a rotating basis) received real‐time alerts on Dexcom Follow for readings below 55 mg/dL lasting more than 30 min and above 350 mg/dL lasting more than 60 min. Study investigators would determine the need for contact, and if needed, the participant was contacted using secure text or phone call. Study investigators reviewed CGM data via the Dexcom clinician‐facing portal (Clarity) every 48–72 h and evaluated if the participant needed to be contacted for medication changes or additional education was indicated, including dose adjustments and DM management coaching. Recommendations were based on investigator's judgment, and no titration algorithms were included in this study.

Monitoring continued until readmission or significant emergency room visit or up to 30 days post‐discharge. Participants answered questions regarding satisfaction and usability of the CGM at the end of their participation.

The participants were divided into four cohorts based on the type of DM and index admission reason: type 1 DM admitted for any reason, transplant admission, high‐severity admission, and low‐severity admission. If the 5‐year survival rate for the reason for admission was < 85%, they were assigned to the high severity group. High‐severity diagnoses included ischemic stroke, type 2 diabetic ketoacidosis, multiple myeloma, septic shock, and acute kidney injury [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Low‐severity diagnoses were related to chronic disease, hyperglycemia without ketoacidosis, infections, and physical injury. Participants in the transplant group could have a history of type 2 DM before admission or have new steroid‐induced or posttransplant DM.

Demographic and survey data were summarized. All insulin and telehealth data were included in the analysis for all participants who completed the study. Median insulin doses at discharge and by day 10 postdischarge are reported. CGM data was included if there was a minimum of 70% available CGM data during the first and last 10 days of the outpatient period of wear. The middle period was of variable length depending on the length of time in the study. CGM data is reported according to standard ranges with TIR 70–180 mg/dL. Median and interquartile range (IQR) are reported unless otherwise specified.

NVivo (Lumivero, Version 14), a qualitative data analysis software, was used to manually categorize content topics and identify themes from telehealth notes [19]. A codebook was developed, and coding disagreements between team members instigated discussion and refinement of the codebook. The number of telehealth contacts per theme was counted and presented by participant subgroups.

Thirty‐six participants (mean age 50.9 ± 11.3 years, 52.8% female, 77.8% non‐White) were included in this analysis (Table 1). Four participants withdrew from the study due to poor data capture or changing their minds about participation and are not included in this analysis. Eight participants (22.2%) had type 1 DM, and 28 participants (77.8%) had type 2 or steroid‐induced DM. The median duration of DM was 15.2 years. Length of stay for the index admission when the participants were enrolled was 8 days (IQR 4.0–11.8).

Six participants were readmitted to the hospital (16.6% readmission rate), and three participants had a significant emergency room visit during the study, so they ended participation at a median of 14 days (IQR 11–18, range 1–28). Reasons for readmission or emergency room visit included postoperative complications (2), posttransplant complications (3), infection (2), neurological symptoms (1), and heart failure exacerbation (1). The remaining 27 participants completed 30 days of monitoring.

The transplant cohort was discharged with steroids, and many had titration of steroids during the study period. The high‐severity cohort had the shortest hospital stay (Table 1). The low‐severity cohort had the longest duration of DM compared with the other type 2 DM groups.

Thirty‐four participants (94%) had > 70% data availability during each 10‐day period to be included. CGM‐based TIR in the transplant group was 44.4% (IQR 27.1%–52.2%) in the first 10 days and 64% (IQR 51.5%–68.2%) in the last 10 days (Table 2 and Figure 1). TIR for the high‐severity group was 39.5% (30.9%–41.2%) and 64.5% (31.8%–84.6%), respectively. The TIR in the low‐severity and type 1 DM groups were higher with TIR in low‐severity cohort of 63.9% (IQR 55.6%–76.5%) and 57.7% (IQR 44.8%–78.5%) and in type 1 DM cohort 70.2% (IQR 54.2%–83.9%) and 78.4% (IQR 69.1%–84.4%), for the first and last 10 days, respectively. Mean time above 250 mg/dL was numerically lower during the last ten days for the transplant, high severity, and type 1 DM groups (Table 2). The majority (94.4%) of participants were discharged with insulin. After 10 days of remote monitoring and telehealth adjustment, there was a median percent titration of basal insulin of 33.3% (IQR 27.3%–33.3%) in the transplant group, 12.2% (–9.3% to 47.2%) in the high‐severity group, −8.35% (–35.6%–0%) in the low‐severity group, and 10.7% (0%–233%) in the type 1 DM group. There was a median percent change in bolus insulin dosing of 27.3% (25%–50%) in the transplant group, 18.2% (IQR 14.3%–180%) in the high severity group, and −5.3% (−40% to 0%) in the low‐severity group (Table S1). In the type 1 DM group, some participants were using carbohydrate ratios to calculate their mealtime boluses. As they did not record food logs, the total mealtime doses are unavailable.

The primary themes of telehealth interactions included: (1) study investigator contact for CGM remote monitoring alerts; (2) insulin dose adjustments; and (3) glucose management coaching (Figure 2). Glucose management coaching encompassed guidance on insulin administration (as some patients were new to taking insulin) and nutrition counseling. Among these, insulin dose titration was the most frequently discussed topic across all groups. Transplant participants had the highest median telehealth interactions (20 over the 30‐day period), primarily driven by insulin dose adjustments and study investigator interventions following remote monitoring alerts.

Heat map of median number of telehealth interactions by subgroup and theme. Major themes identified included intervention after remote monitoring alerts (e.g., “Team reached out to participant and instructed participant to treat hypo.”), insulin dose adjustments (e.g., “Participant reported that prednisone dose is increasing … Called to discuss insulin titration.”), and glucose management coaching (e.g., “Advised participant to take large snack dose instead of dinner dose if eating after midnight.”).

The high‐severity and low‐severity groups had the same median number of telehealth interactions (12 over the 30‐day period) while the type 1 DM group had a median of 11 interactions in the same period. For these groups, study investigator contacts for remote monitoring alerts and glucose management coaching accounted for nearly equal proportions of telehealth encounters, following insulin dose adjustments in frequency.

Patient‐reported satisfaction with ambulatory use of CGM was high, with scores exceeding 4.7 (out of a maximum possible score of 5) across all measures, including training, ease of use, comfort of use, and overall experience. In addition, patients reported a high likelihood (4.9 out of 5) of using real‐time CGM for their future glucose monitoring needs (Table S2). These results were consistent across all groups. No device‐related adverse events, including skin reactions, were reported.

This feasibility study suggests that the postdischarge period represents a clinically relevant window for CGM implementation. Continuous glucose data may facilitate early identification of glycemic abnormalities and inform clinical decision‐making during a period when outpatient follow‐up is often limited.

Compared to a recent randomized trial using intermittent CGM postdischarge [12], our study implemented continuous real‐time monitoring with active telehealth support. Although both studies observed trends toward improved TIR, differences in patient populations and intervention intensity limit direct comparison. These findings support further evaluation of CGM‐based postdischarge care in higher‐risk populations with active mediation titration.

The high‐severity cohort had the shortest hospital stay, possibly due to conditions requiring acute stabilization with early discharge. The 30‐day readmission rate for patients with DM was 16.6%, which falls within the reported national average of 15%–25%, a range attributed to the high complexity of this patient population. Importantly, none of the readmissions in our cohort was attributed to hyperglycemia or hypoglycemia. Observed changes in TIR were numerically greater in the transplant and high‐severity cohorts, while the low‐severity cohort showed a modest decrease, likely reflecting higher baseline TIR. Hypoglycemia exposure remained low and consistent with American Diabetes Association recommendations.

Telehealth interactions were frequent, particularly in the transplant group, driven by CGM alerts and insulin titration needs related to steroid adjustments and clinical recovery. Insulin titration was based on the investigator's judgment rather than a standardized protocol, which may limit reproducibility and generalizability; however, this approach reflects real‐world clinical practice, where insulin adjustments are typically individualized based on patient‐specific factors. Telehealth support may facilitate responsive management during this period, though its independent effect cannot be determined in this study.

Given the small sample sizes, these findings should be considered descriptive; however, greater TIR changes and higher telehealth utilization in select groups suggest that future interventions could prioritize higher‐risk populations while incorporating individualized glycemic targets.

Patient‐reported satisfaction with CGM was high across measures of training, usability, and overall experience, supporting its acceptability in the postdischarge setting despite the challenges of recent hospitalization.

Strengths of this study include a diverse, clinically complex population, successful enrollment in a feasibility setting, and practical insights for implementing technology‐supported DM management during the hospital‐to‐home transition. Several limitations should be acknowledged: the lack of randomization introduces selection bias and limits causal inference. Given the small sample size and exploratory nature of the study, formal statistical testing was not performed; therefore, it cannot be determined whether the observed changes exceed expected random variation. In the transplant cohort, glycemic changes may have been influenced by postoperative recovery and steroid adjustments independent of the intervention. The withdrawal of four participants may reflect adherence challenges and introduce bias to participant‐reported outcomes. As a single‐site study, generalizability and scalability remain uncertain.

This study provides preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility and acceptability of CGM with telehealth in the postdischarge setting. These findings are hypothesis‐generating and support the need for multicenter randomized studies to evaluate efficacy, safety, cost‐effectiveness, and scalability.
