The theory of hypertensive heart disease and heart failure: revisiting the evidence and pathophysiology
Authors: Maria Giulia Bellicini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Arterial hypertension is a major epidemiological risk factor for left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and heart failure (HF). Within this framework, the theory of hypertensive heart disease (HHD) has historically been proposed as the conceptual substrate for HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). The aim of this state-of-the-art review is to re-examine whether secondary hypertrophic remodelling due to chronic pressure overload represents an intrinsically dysfunctional myocardial state prone to HF, beyond the well recognized role of severe excess afterload as a haemodynamic precipitant of decompensation.This state-of-the-art review critically examines historical, experimental, imaging, histopathological, and clinical trial evidence addressing the relationship between arterial hypertension, afterload, myocardial remodelling, and HF.Across these domains, available evidence does not demonstrate arterial hypertension as a sufficient condition to cause HF, nor does it establish hypertensive myocardial remodelling as an intrinsic cardiomyopathy predisposed to decompensation. Clarifying this distinction has important implications for phenotyping, diagnosis, and interpretation of HF in hypertensive populations.

INTRODUCTION

Arterial hypertension is among the most prevalent cardiovascular conditions worldwide, affecting approximately 1.4 billion people and representing a major contributor to the global burden of heart failure (HF) [1]. Chronic exposure to elevated arterial pressure induces adaptive structural changes in the heart, most notably concentric left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) and remodelling. These alterations have historically been conceptualised under the framework of hypertensive heart disease (HHD) and interpreted as intermediary stages linking hypertension to clinical heart failure (HF) decompensation. This conceptual framework has been reinforced by epidemiological studies demonstrating strong associations between hypertension and incident HF, as well as by experimental models in which extreme and sustained increases in afterload lead to ventricular failure. More recently, HHD has frequently been cited as the paradigmatic substrate for HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), further consolidating the theory that hypertensive remodelling representsan intrinsically stiff and diastolically dysfunctional myocardial state. However, important conceptual and evidence gaps remain. In clinical settings, severe excess afterload is well recognized as a haemodynamic stressor capable of precipitating acute HF decompensation (particularly in the presence of preexisting haemodinamically relevant myocardial or valvular disease, but not exclusively) analogous to what is observed in conditions such as severe aortic stenosis. However : no data demonstrate that secondary hypertrophic remodelling represents an intrinsically failing cardiomyopathy sufficient to induce acute HF decompensation. At the same time, although arterial hypertension is highly prevalent among participants enrolled in HF trials, these populations rarely consist of patients with isolated hypertension alone. Rather, they typically represent heterogeneous phenotypes characterized by coexisting significant cardiac abnormalities. In this state-of-the-art review, we critically reassess the evidence linking arterial hypertension to HF, in particular HFpEF, focusing on the distinction between epidemiological association, haemodynamic triggering, and causal myocardial disease. By integrating historical observations, experimental data, imaging findings, histopathology, and contemporary clinical evidence, we aim to clarify whether HHD should be regarded as a maladpatitve secondary cardiomyopathy intrinsically prone to failure or primarily as an adaptive response to a specific haemodynamic context.

THE HISTORICAL EVOLUTION OF THE HYPERTENSIVE HEART DISEASE CONCEPT

The earliest pathological observations of cardiac changes attributed to hypertension were mainly morphological. Autopsy studies from the ninetienth century consistently reported increased cardiac weight and concentric left ventricular wall thickening in individuals with chronic arterial hypertension compared with normotensive controls. Hypertensive hearts were typically characterized by increased mass, thickened ventricular walls, and a normal or maybe end-diastolic cavity size, a geometric configuration that would later be described as concentric hypertrophy [2–4].

As these structural features became increasingly recognized, they were progressively incorporated into clinicopathological interpretations of disease progression. Early interpretations frequently attributed death in patients with essential hypertension to HF, often assuming that the progressive consequences of chronic pressure overload led to myocardial insufficiency. Several observers estimated that 50–80% of hypertensive patients ultimately developed fatal cardiac failure. [5–11]. However, subsequent large autopsy-based analyses challenged this interpretation, demonstrating that terminal HF in hypertensive patients was frequently associated with significant coronary artery disease (CAD). In Averbuck autopsy serie, for example, 85% of hyperensive dying with myocardial insufficiency had severe coronary sclerosis with or without thrombosis; in Clawson serie 36% of deaths due to hypertension were attributed to severe coronary sclerosis or thrombosis, whereas 21% were due to hypertension-related cerebrovascular or renal complications [12,13]. [12,13].

Even in influential pathophysiological frameworks, the causal relationship between hypertension and HF remained unresolved, as cardiac involvement frequently coexisted with other conditions, particularly CAD [14,15]. Clearly, LVH confers an additional independent risk [16–18]; however, whether its presence represents an entirely adverse feature of the disease remains unclear [14].

The Framingham Heart Study subsequently provided a major contribution by generating long-term, population-based data on the association between hypertension and incident HF. In multivariable proportional hazards models adjusting for age, antecedent myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, diabetes, clinically audible valvular heart disease, and electrocardiographic LVH, hypertension remained independently associated with HF in both men [hazard ratio 1.84, 95% confidence interval (95% CI) 1.35–2.51] and women (hazard ratio 2.60, 95% CI 1.77–3.81) in over 20 years of follow-up [19].

Other contemporaneous observational studies confirmed associations and temporal precedence between hypertension and HF but were unable to establish hypertension as a causally sufficient condition for acute decompensation [20,21]. Notably, etiological classification of HF reflected the limited imaging tools available at the time and relied largely on the presence or absence of previous overt myocardial infarction or the presence of a clear ascultatory murmur. In Framingham Heart Study, for example, HF was ascertained using combinations of physical signs of congestion and chest radiography findings, such as cardiomegaly which is not specific and often reflect only atrial enlargement. LVH was identified exclusively by electrocardiographic criteria, while valvular heart disease was defined solely on auscultatory findings, making the detection of severe valvular pathology neither sensitive nor specific. Coronary heart disease was incorporated into multivariable models as a history of myocardial infarction or angina pectoris, without characterization of ischemic burden or severity [19]. Within this framework, HF occurring in patients without documented infarction or clinically apparent valvular pathology was frequently attributed to hypertension by exclusion, reflecting its high prevalence in this population.

Subsequent decades have produced increasingly large cohort studies and pooled analyses examining the association between blood pressure levels and incident HF. A recent meta-analysis including 47 large cohort studies and more than 20 million participants confirmed a strong, graded, and independent association between SBP and DBP and the risk of HF [21]. However, none of these studies enrolled patients with a defined HHD phenotype. Rather than representing isolated arterial hypertension, the populations enrolled in these trials were heterogeneous, with hypertension frequently coexisting alongside CAD, prior myocardial infarction, or valvular heart disease. In such settings, HF events could reflect the decompensation of an already vulnerable heart (mainly in the presence of reduced left ventricular ejection fraction or severe valvular dysfunction, because elevations in afterload are generally well tolerated in normofunctioning heart) when exposed to a marked increase in afterload, as also conceptualized in the CHAMPIT framework. [22], This differs fundamentally from the notion that hypertensive myocardial remodelling per se constitutes an intrinsically dysfunctional substrate prone to acute decompensation.

In summary, no randomized clinical trials have been specifically designed to test HHD as a sufficient cause of HF. As a result, despite their scale and statistical robustness, available epidemiological data do not establish whether elevated blood pressure alone is sufficient to cause HF in structurally and functionally normal hearts, including those exhibiting isolated secondary hypertrophic remodelling.

LEFT VENTRICULAR HYPERTROPHY AND HEART FAILURE

The association between arterial hypertension, LVH, and HF has traditionally been conceptualized as a progressive sequence. However, the extent to which this sequence is supported by longitudinal evidence remains uncertain.

In a comprehensive review published in 2011, Drazner [23] examined experimental and clinical data addressing the relationship between arterial hypertension, concentric LVH, and HF. Much of the experimental evidence was derived from animal models of severe pressure overload (most commonly based on aortic constriction or stenosis) in which an abrupt and fixed increase in afterload is mechanically imposed, resulting in a transient ventricular dilatation and dysfunction with pulmonary oedema [24]. This early instability is typically followed by the development of concentric hypertrophy with a relatively stable compensatory hyperfunction, until the myocardium undergoes progressive gradual decline in contractile efficiency, leading to chronic cardiac insufficiency. In these models, concentric hypertrophy typically precedes ventricular dysfunction, however, decompensation is primarily driven by sustained excessive afterload acting on the ventricle as a whole, rather than by intrinsic failure of the hypertrophied myocardial wall. Similarly, progression from LVH to left ventricular dysfunction has been demonstrated in humans with aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, conditions characterized by extreme, fix and nonphysiological loading states [25,26].

Moreover, in observational human studies, concentric LVH did not consistently progress to systolic dysfunction in the absence of intervening myocardial injury, and the development of overt HF most often followed superimposed events such as myocardial infarction [27,28].

In summary, experimental observation supporting concentric LVH as a necessary or sufficient precursor of HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) was lacking. In a similar manner, the evolution of LVH into HFpEF remains insufficiently substantiated by direct evidence.

HFpEF current knowledge lacks experimental models and is largely derived from clinical trials and observational cohorts defined by enrolment criteria and diagnostic conventions. In the Dahl's salt-sensitive rats, severe hypertension initially induces concentric hypertrophy with preserved ejection fraction and enhanced pump performance, without evidence of congestion or clinical HF. Although early alterations in pressure–volume relations may resemble diastolic abnormalities, filling pressures remain only minimally elevated. With continued pressure overload, ventricular dilation develops, systolic function declines, and overt HF emerges in association with rising filling pressures and pulmonary congestion. Notably, HF occurs only after loss of systolic function, whereas hypertrophy with preserved ejection fraction represents a compensated adaptive state [29,30].

Subsequent literature has largely refined pathophysiological models and diagnostic frameworks, without providing new longitudinal data demonstrating concentric LVH as a common or sufficient precursor of HF in essential hypertension [17,31]. Taken together, these findings support the concept that excessive afterload can directly induce left ventricular dysfunction and acute HF. However, they do not substantiate the notion that LVH per se inherently progresses toward diastolic or systolic HF. Rather, ventricular decompensation appears to reflect the failure of global adaptation to sustained mechanical stress, not an intrinsic tendency of LVH to evolve into myocardial insufficiency.

HYPERTENSIVE HEART DISEASE AND HEART FAILURE WITH PRESERVED EJECTION FRACTION

HFpEF is currently described as a heterogeneous clinical syndrome predominantly affecting older, multimorbid patients, in whom cardiometabolic comorbidities are highly prevalent [22]. Contemporary mechanistic reviews increasingly conceptualize these comorbidities as upstream contributors to myocardial remodelling, including LVH and myocardial fibrosis, ultimately associated with diastolic dysfunction and elevated filling pressures [32–38]. In particular, HHD has traditionally been regarded as the paradigmatic substrate underlying HFpEF. Importantly, however, this proposed causal sequence is largely inferred rather than demonstrated, reflecting the absence of longitudinal human data showing a consistent progression from HHD to HFpEF as a distinct clinical entity [17,32].

The interpretation of hypertension as the principal aetiological substrate of HFpEF has been strongly shaped by the design and enrolment criteria of HFpEF clinical trials, where arterial hypertension consistently emerged as the most prevalent baseline characteristic among enrolled patients. However, hypertension entered these trials as a comorbidity rather than a mechanistic inclusion criterion, and its high prevalence largely reflects the demographic and clinical profile of the recruited populations rather than evidence of a causal pathway [39–44].

As emphasized in contemporary reviews, HFpEF trial populations predominantly comprise older patients with multiple coexisting conditions, including obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and atrial fibrillation, each independently associated with elevated natriuretic peptide levelsand symptom burden [17,32].

Furthermore, a key methodological limitation highlighted in recent reviews is that most HFpEF trials relied heavily on natriuretic peptide thresholds and prior HF hospitalizations for enrolment, while systematic exclusion of HFpEF mimics was frequently incomplete or anamnestic rather than based on comprehensive echocardiographic or haemodynamic reassessment. As a result, trial cohorts likely included a heterogeneous mix of pathophysiological conditions, ranging from severe valvular heart disease and cardiomyopathies to patients whose sole qualifying feature was an elevated NT-proBNP level, often within the context of multiple coexisting conditions, including avanced age, obesity, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and atrial fibrillation, each independently associated with elevated natriuretic peptide levels and symptom burden, even in the absence of HF [17,32]. [45]. Notably, guideline-recommended natriuretic peptide thresholds were developed primarily as rule-out tools in specific clinical settings and support diagnosis only when interpreted within an appropriate clinical and imaging context, rather than a specific indicators of HF [22,46].

Consistent with these observations, Peikert et al.[32] reported a nonnegligible prevalence of cardiac amyloidosis and significant valvular heart disease within HFpEF cohorts, with a substantial proportion remaining unrecognised. Peikert et al. [32] further noted that although hypertension is highly prevalent in HFpEF cohorts, LVH and concentric remodelling are neither universal nor specific features of the syndrome, and a substantial proportion of patients labelled as HFpEF do not exhibit a hypertrophic phenotype. Moreover, the review explicitly acknowledges that mechanistic links between hypertension, myocardial remodelling, fibrosis, and HFpEF remain presumed rather than demonstrated, being largely derived from associative data and biologically hypothetical pathways rather than validated longitudinal trajectories [32].

The neutral or modestly positive outcomes observed in HFpEF trials further complicate causal interpretation. Despite near-universal exposure to antihypertensive therapies and frequent targeting of neurohormonal pathways implicated in HHD, interventions such as renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibition have consistently failed to demonstrate robust mortality benefits in HFpEF populations.

Taken together, these observations suggest that HFpEF trials have primarily reinforced the epidemiological association between hypertension and HFpEF while simultaneously exposing diagnostic fragility of the syndrome. Rather than confirming HHD as a cardiomyopathy that predictably progresses to HFpEF, trial data support the interpretation of HFpEF as a broad, design-dependent construct lacking rigorous exclusion of major HF causes (like severe valve disease) and objective confirmation of congestion, in which hypertension represents a common background condition rather than a proven causal substrate.

In a recent cohort from a large Italian tertiary centre, systematic reassessment using comprehensive echocardiography performed before diuretic therapy and repeated with a second core-laboratory–style review demonstrated that most patients admitted with acute HF and preserved or mildly reduced ejection fraction with objective evidence venous congestion had an identifiable HFpEF mimic-condition, including severe valvular disease, cardiomyopathies, isolate right ventricular disfunction, precapillary pulmonary hypertension, severe pericardial disease, advanced atrioventricular block, or terminal renal failure. Under these stringent diagnostic conditions, fewer than 10% of patients fulfilled the guideline-defined concept of HFpEF as acute HF occurring in the absence of major structural heart disease, a residual group that included individuals with isolated HHD without other identifiable causes [46].

In summary, without clear validation of HFpEF as a pathophysiologically unified disease capable of independently precipitating acute HF, HHD cannot be regarded as its deterministic substrate.

IMAGING CHARACTERISTICS OF HYPERTENSIVE HEART DISEASE

Echocardiography has been the primary imaging modality used to characterize heart involvement in arterial hypertension and so has largely shaped current concepts of HHD. In hypertensive populations, conventional echocardiography consistently demonstrates concentric LVH with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction [47–52]. Abnormalities of diastolic function are frequently reported, and progressive deterioration of diastolic indices has been proposed as a key pathophysiological link between hypertension and HF [48].

Despite this central role attributed to diastolic dysfunction, dedicated observational studies systematically characterizing its prevalence and natural history in isolated hypertension remain remarkably scarce. As a result, many echocardiographic diastolic patterns cited in contemporary frameworks are inferred from expert consensus rather than derived from longitudinal investigation [48]. Among the limited prospective data available, the PIUMA study evaluated Doppler indices of left ventricular filling in untreated patients with essential hypertension and no prior other cardiovascular disease, identifying reduced early filling and a lower age and heart rate adjusted E/A ratio as common findings associated with cardiovascular risk [53]. However, according to current ASE/EACVI recommendations, impaired left ventricular relaxation, is widely prevalent in the general population, and is typically associated with normal left ventricular filling pressures [54,55]. Notably in major HFpEF trials, diastolic dysfunction was not systematically assessed, as echocardiography was inconsistently performed and protocol requirements varied widely [39–44].

In some contexts, echocardiographic estimates of pulmonary pressures have been used as indirect markers of haemodynamic significance and congestion, complementing diastolic indices in the assessment of suspected HF Current ESC/ERS pulmonary hypertension guidelines recommend interpreting echocardiographic pulmonary pressure estimates primarily through peak tricuspid regurgitation velocity (TRV) in conjunction with additional echocardiographic signs. A TRV of 2.8 m/s or less indicates low probability, 2.9–3.4 m/s intermediate probability, and more than 3.4 m/s high probability of pulmonary hypertension when supported by other signs [22]. However, population-based data further show that resting sPAP increases with age, and values around 40 mmHg may be observed in older individuals without overt congestion, particularly when right atrial pressure surrogates (inferior vena cava size and collapsibility) remain within normal limits.

Consistent with these observations, in a recent acute HF cohort, where venous and pulmonary congestion were adjudicated using objective imaging criteria and major confounders such as severe tricuspid regurgitation were excluded, ROC analysis identified an estimated sPAP threshold close to 50 mmHg (≈48.8 mmHg) as the most accurate discriminator of presence of peripheral and pulmonary congestion [56]. Importantly, elevated sPAP values clustered with the presence of HFpEF mimics rather than with guideline-defined hypertension-related HFpEF in patients with preserved left ventriular ejection farction [46].

More recently, myocardial deformation imaging has been proposed as a tool to detect subclinical dysfunction in HHD. Several studies have shown that impaired global longitudinal strain correlates with the severity of diastolic dysfunction, with lower GLS values independently associated with more advanced diastolic grades even in the presence of preserved ejection fraction [48,56]. However, these findings lack specificity for HF and nor do they establish a causal pathway from hypertension to HFpEF.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) has increasingly been applied to investigate myocardial remodelling in arterial hypertension, with the aim of identifying a structural substrate linking pressure overload to HF. Across studies, CMR consistently demonstrates increased left ventricular mass and concentric remodelling as the predominant morphological features of HHD. Nevertheless, despite advanced tissue characterization techniques, no specific or pathognomonic CMR phenotype has been identified [57].

Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) has been reported in subsets of hypertensive patients, particularly in the presence of established LVH. In the seminal study by Rudolph et al. [57], focal LGE was observed in approximately half of hypertensive patients with LVH; however, the extent of enhancement was limited (≈5% of left ventricular mass), lacked a reproducible spatial pattern, and showed no consistent relationship with ventricular mass, volume or systolic function [58]. LGE distribution was typically patchy and nonischaemic, often involving mid-wall regions or right ventricular insertion points. Importantly, these early studies predated contemporary parametric mapping techniques and genetic testing, and therefore could not reliably exclude phenotypic overlap with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or other myocardial diseases. In more recent cohorts applying stricter exclusion criteria, LGE has been primarily used to identify and exclude alternative diagnoses rather than to define hypertensive myocardial pathology [59].

Indeed, in patients with isolated hypertension, Native T1 values and global extracellular volume are comparable to those of normotensive controls. When present, increases in mean ECV are modest, typically on the order of 1–2 absolute percentage points, and are observed mainly in association with LVH, largely paralleling myocardial mass expansion [58–60].

Taken together, neither echocardiographic functional assessment nor advanced CMR tissue characterization provides clues or evidence that arterial hypertension alone constitutes a cardiomyopathy-like myocardial condition and a sufficient substrate for HF.

HISTOPATHOLOGICAL FINDINGS AND HYPOTHETIC PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL MECHANISMS IN HYPERTENSIVE HEART DISEASE

Histopathological evidence supporting myocardial fibrosis as a defining feature of HHD remains scarce and largely historical. Most available autopsy and biopsy studies date back to the pre-2000 era, were conducted in small patient cohorts, and frequently predated the routine use of modern cardiac imaging techniques [61–63]. Only a small number of studies with comparatively larger sample sizes have been published thereafter, and these generally involved highly selected populations rather than patients with uncomplicated arterial hypertension.

In particular, a biopsy-based study conducted in the early 2000 s assessed myocardial collagen content in hypertensive patients referred for invasive evaluation because of clinically overt HF or diagnostic uncertainty [64,65]. Although this study reported higher collagen volume fraction in patients with HF compared with individuals without, the absolute degree of fibrosis was modest. In a separate autopsy-based investigation, HHD was identified as the cause of sudden death in only a small minority of cases, after alternative structural causes of death had been ruled out, but the aetiological diagnosis was inferred by exclusion in predominantly among relatively young individuals without prior clinical HF [65].

Contemporary HFpEF pathophysiological paradigms, widely adopted in the hypertension literature, place microvascular dysfunction, arterial stiffness, altered ventricular–arterial coupling, and impaired cardiovascular reserve at the centre of the mechanistic link between hypertension and HF.

For eexmple reviews by Diez et al. and Nemtsova et al., outline biologically plausible frameworks in which chronic pressure overload activates cardiomyocyte and fibroblast signalling pathways through mechanotransduction, neurohormonal activation, profibrotic cytokines, oxidative stress, and low-grade inflammation, leading to cardiomyocyte hypertrophy, extracellular matrix remodelling, and interstitial collagen deposition[66][67]. However, cellular and molecular mechanisms proposed to underlie myocardial remodelling in HHD are largely derived from experimental and translational studies rather than validated longitudinal human data and remain largely unproven to cause HF at the clinical level. Notably, some authors noted that progression from concentric to eccentric remodellingappeared to be uncommon in the absence of intervening myocardial infarction [34,35].

Moreover contemporary paradigm-based models frequently describe coronary microvascular dysfunction, arterial stiffening, altered ventricular–arterial coupling, and impaired cardiovascular reserve as central components of HHD; however, these constructs are explicitly presented as presumed sequences rather than as demonstrated causal pathways.

Evidence of dysfunction and rarefaction of coronary microcirculation in hypertension is derived predominantly from experimental models and cross-sectional human observations rather than from longitudinal clinical studies [68,69]. Available human data do not demonstrate temporal precedence or longitudinal progression from microvascular alterations in hypertensive individuals to the development of a HFpEF syndrome [68,69]. Arterial stiffening and elevated blood pressure represent closely coupled phenomena within the same haemodynamic continuum. Although increased arterial stiffness may precede and promote systolic hypertension, it is sustained pressure overload that primarily drives hypertensive cardiac remodelling. Nevertheless, neither arterial stiffening nor blood pressure elevation alone appears sufficient to explain the development of clinical HF [71].

Despite extensive mechanistic and observational evidence linking arterial stiffness and altered ventricular–arterial coupling to adverse cardiovascular outcomes, the available literature, largely based on cross-sectional and observational studies, does not provide longitudinal human data demonstrating that these alterations are sufficient, in isolation, to cause clinical HF in hypertensive patients [72].

Similarly, reduced chronotropic and vasodilatory reserve, frequently described in older hypertensive individuals, reflects functional limitations attributable to ageing, comorbidity burden rather than a hypertension-specific myocardial mechanism. Although differences in chronotropic response and vasodilator reserve have been reported in HFpEF populations, these findings suggest a predominant role of autonomic and systemic factors. Consistent with this interpretation, despite the clear association between chronotropic incompetence and impaired exercise capacity in patients with HF, causality with congestion remains debated [73].

INnsummary while microvascular dysfunction, arterial stiffness, and impaired cardiovascular reserve are integral components of contemporary mechanistic models, the absence of longitudinal, hypertension-specific human evidence precludes attribution of a causal progression from hypertensive myocardial remodelling to heart failure. These pathways therefore remain biologically possible but insufficiently substantiated to define HHD as a cardiomyopathy intrinsically prone to HF.

CONCLUSION

Arterial hypertension is unequivocally associated with an increased risk of HF and remains a major determinant of cardiovascular morbidity. Chronic pressure overload leads to adaptive myocardial remodelling, including concentric hypertrophy, which forms the basis of the traditional concept of HHD. Severe excess afterload may nevertheless precipitate acute HF as a haemodynamic stressor in specific clinical contexts, particularly when imposed on hearts with preexisting myocardial dysfunction or severe valvular disease, in a manner analogous to advanced aortic stenosis.s. Instead progression of hypertension induced LVH to HFpEF or HFrEF has not been demonstrated as a consistent pathway. Recognizing this distinction is essential to prevent over-attribution of HF to hypertension and to determine which structural cardiac phenotypes, and under which haemodynamic circumstances, patients are genuinely prone to decompensation. Future research should therefore prioritize prospective longitudinal human studies incorporating systematic exclusion of alternative causes, objective documentation of congestion, and standardized imaging-based phenotyping, in order to clarify whether HHD represents a distinct causal pathway to acute HFor merely an adaptative compensatory heart remodelling.
