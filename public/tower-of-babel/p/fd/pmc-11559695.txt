Pneumothorax revealing late recurrence of infantile myofibromatosis
Authors: Clemence Yguel, Jean Michel Vignaud, Angelica Tiotiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Adult recurrence of infantile myofibromatosis is exceptional. Here, we report the case of a 23-year-old woman with a late recurrence of infantile myofibromatosis revealed by spontaneous pneumothorax. The chest computed tomography scan found both cavitary and nodular bilateral pulmonary lesions. In infancy, she had multicentric myofibromatosis with digestive, cutaneous, and bone involvement, spontaneously regressive before the age of 15 months. Histological analysis of lung samples showed identical findings as from the cutaneous biopsy performed in infancy, confirming the recurrence of infantile myofibromatosis as pulmonary metastasis. New biopsy and long-term follow-up are highly recommended in the management of such cases.

Infantile myofibromatosis (IM), a rare disorder of fibroblastic/myofibroblastic proliferation, is one of the most common benign fibrous tumors of infancy occurring in children <24 months of age.[1] There are solitary (myofibroma) and multicentric (myofibromatosis) forms affecting generally skin, subcutaneous tissue, muscle, bone, and occasionally viscera.[2] If solitary forms tend to spontaneously regress, visceral involvement detected in 11%–19% of multicentric cases is associated with a poor prognosis and high mortality (70%) related to possible life-threatening complications.[23] Sporadic adult-onset myofibromas or late recurrence can occur, most frequently on the head, neck, and trunk.[12]

We report a late recurrence of IM in the lungs revealed by spontaneous pneumothorax.

Case Report

A 23-year-old woman was hospitalized for a spontaneous complete right pneumothorax that occurred during a fitness session. She was a never-smoker student in psychology. The medical history was marked by the presence of IM diagnosed at birth with digestive (gastrointestinal polyps), cutaneous (right axillary lesion), and bone involvement (osteolytic lesions in T8, T11, L3, pelvis, femur, and tibia). The histological diagnosis was established by biopsies of the skin lesion and gastrointestinal polyps. The patient had parenteral nutrition for several months for symptomatic exudative enteropathy and a corset to avoid skeletal complications. The evolution was favorable with spontaneous regression before the age of 15 months. In the absence of recurrence, the follow-up was interrupted after 8 years.

Pleural right drainage was realized in an emergency before the performing of chest computed tomography scan showing bilateral pulmonary cystic lesions and solid nodules. Following the imaging, several diagnoses were suggested: cystic fibrohistiocytic tumors, lymphangioleiomyomatosis, pulmonary Langerhans cell histiocytosis, lymphoproliferative lung disorders, lymphoid interstitial pneumonia/follicular bronchiolitis, Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome, traumatic, or infectious causes. However, there were no infectious signs or trauma for this patient, and no abnormalities on the blood analyses. In this context, she was addressed for thoracoscopy with lung biopsy and pleurodesis. The histological analysis of lung samples identified two cystic lesions with irregular thickness walls, lined by cuboidal epithelium with an underlying layer of mildly atypical myofibroblastic-like cells, with slightly wavy morphology and no specific architecture, admixed with inflammatory cells (predominantly plasma cells and lymphocytes) in a myxoid stroma; on the inner wall, spindle cells were arranged parallel to the cyst lumen and have fibroblastic-like morphology with elongated nuclei and small nucleoli without mitosis. By immunohistochemistry, spindle cells expressed smooth muscle actin (SMA) but neither desmin nor Human Melanoma Black 45. In the myxoid areas, the spindle cells were negative for SMA, desmin, caldesmon, estrogen receptors, progesterone receptors, erythroblast transformation-specific-related genes, and lymphoid markers. The stroma inflammatory infiltrate was predominantly lymphocytic sometimes expressing CD1a+ with macrophages CD68+. The comparison with the samples from the skin biopsy obtained in infancy showed a similar aspect with myofibroblastic-like cell proliferation organized in entangled bundles within connective tissue punctuated by inflammatory elements [Figure 1]. The final diagnosis was a late recurrence of IM as pulmonary metastasis.

The evolution was favorable with the ablation of the drain. The strategy adopted after discussion in a multidisciplinary meeting involving pulmonologists, pediatric and adult oncologists, hematologists, radiologists, thoracic surgeons, and radiotherapists was “watch-and-wait” (w&w). At 5-year follow-up, there was no progression of the lung lesions on the imaging without any treatment.

Discussion

Several particularities make this case original. There is a late recurrence of a rare benign tumor disease-specific for infancy. IM is a tumor with perivascular myoid differentiation, with an incidence in newborns of 1/400,000.[1] Three-quarters of IM are diagnosed at birth, 89% within the first 2 years of life. Rare cases were reported in older children and adults.[12] Females (like in our case) are less frequently affected because IM is a disease with male predominance (sex ratio: 2.7:1).[1] Familial IM cases with early multicentric forms were described as related to the inheritance of mutations in platelet-derived growth factor receptor β gene (PDGFRB) that promotes the growth of mesenchymal cells and recruits vascular smooth muscle cells and pericytes to developing blood vessel walls.[3] Somatic PDGFRB mutations were found in 68%–75% of sporadic cases.[4] The role of other genes involved in angiogenesis (e.g., NOTCH3 and NDRG4) was reported suggesting genetic heterogeneity.[12] Recommendations for genetic testing in patients with IM were recently published.[5] The genetic testing was not yet realized for our patient viewing the favorable evolution and the absence of other cases in her family, but it will be done if relapse of the disease. The mechanisms underlying tumor growth/recurrence and regression are unknown.[6] Usually, the relapse occurred during the first months/years after diagnosis and late recurrence is exceptional.[4] Only two late recurrences were previously reported, none in the lungs. One woman with subcutaneous, bone, and pancreatic myofibromatosis in infancy developed intramuscular and bone lesions at the age of 24 years in the third trimester of her second pregnancy, and another one with a superficial scalp lesion at age 2, presented three bone recurrences at ages 9, 12, and 23, respectively.[26] Several authors suggested that hormonal influences may have led to these recurrences, but this hypothesis is not yet confirmed.[2] The progression of the disease is typically slow. w&w strategy is preferred for the management in practice and was adopted for our patient. Surgery is necessary when symptomatic or esthetic complaints and was realized for both previous cases with a late recurrence reported in the literature. Systemic therapy is reserved for life-threatening progressive diseases. Vincristine/dactinomycin and vinblastine/methotrexate were used with good responses. Treatment by imatinib and sunitinib showed benefits in PDGFRB-mutated disease. IM has a good prognosis with a 5-year event-free survival rate of 51% and an overall survival rate of 95% in patients with multicentric disease.[3]

This is the first report of a pneumothorax due to lung involvement of IM. Usually, the imaging showed nodules, mass, or atelectasis in patients with lung IM.[789] However, necrosis could be found in the central area of nodules on histological samples and associated with cavitary lesions on imaging (our case) explaining why this entity is classified as cystic fibrohistiocytic tumor of the lung.[110] Pneumothorax was already reported in patients with lung histiocytofibroma, dermatofibroma, and dermatofibrosarcoma.[10] The histological diagnosis is mandatory in such cases because the clinical presentation can be misleading. The findings on the lung biopsy confirmed the recurrence of IM in our case. Fortunately for the patient, the evolution was favorable after drainage without other therapeutic interventions.

This case report highlights the importance of a long-term follow-up including clinical examination and imaging even for the benign tumors of infancy because late recurrence is possible, sometimes with atypical presentation or life-threatening complications. A new biopsy is mandatory to confirm the diagnosis in such cases. With this description, IM could be added as a possible etiology of pneumothorax.
