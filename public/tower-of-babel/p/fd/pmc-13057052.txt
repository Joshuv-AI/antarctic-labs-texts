Defect mediated pulse terahertz emission from thiocyanate-treated hybrid perovskite nanoparticles: role of the orientation of built-in surface electric field
Authors: Carlito S. Ponseca, Mohammed O. Musa, Feng Wang, Ignas Nevinskas, Andrius Arlauskas, Chiara Musumeci, Ali Shuaib, Fatemah Aldousari, Jens Eriksson, Kaibo Zheng, Feng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hybrid perovskites have demonstrated exceptional versatility as optoelectronic materials since their introduction as light absorbers and charge transport media in photovoltaic devices. These materials are now utilized in photodetectors, photocatalysis, and light-emitting devices, offering emission capabilities spanning from the ultraviolet to the mid-infrared spectrum. Recent studies in the far-infrared regime have shown that pulsed terahertz (THz) radiation can be effectively generated, enhanced, and detected. Nevertheless, the fundamental emission mechanisms is still an ongoing debate, which have been ascribed to nonlinear optical effects, surface electric fields, photo-Dember effect, bulk photovoltaic phenomena, or a combination of these factors. Here, we demonstrate that increasing the thiocyanate concentration in formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI₃) thin films—a widely used strategy for defect passivation—leads to a commensurate increase in the peak-to-peak amplitude of the generated THz pulse. Spectroscopic and microscopic analyses of both surface and bulk properties reveal that THz emission is predominantly influenced by the orientation of the built-in surface electric field, the degree of crystallinity, and the dynamic interplay between these factors, rather than solely by defect concentration. Our results offer valuable insight into optimizing hybrid perovskite-based THz emitters and detectors through surface defect engineering while also clarifying the limitations of such approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42017-w.

Introduction

Hybrid organic–inorganic perovskites (HOIPs), first introduced roughly a decade ago as highly efficient active material for photovoltaic applications, have since been extensively studied across a broad spectrum of optoelectronic technologies, including photodetectors1–3, scintillators4–6, and photocatalysts7–9. As light-emitting materials, HOIPs demonstrate photoelectronic performance comparable to their purely inorganic counterparts with emission capabilities spanning the ultraviolet10, visible11, and near-infrared12 spectral regions. HOIPs have also been reported to exhibit substantial mid-infrared Kerr nonlinearity, potentially offering new pathways for the design of mid-IR nonlinear optical devices.13 In the far-infrared regime, recent literature has demonstrated that hybrid perovskites can generate pulsed terahertz (THz) radiation. One of the earliest reports by Guzelturk et al., showed that thin films of methylammonium lead iodide (MAPbI₃), when irradiated with pulsed laser light of photon energy above the bandgap, emits THz pulses centered around 0.65 THz and extending up to 3 THz.14 Subsequent studies have revealed further advances: THz emission from HOIPs can be enhanced,15 modulated at high frequencies (up to 50 GHz),16 integrated into large-aperture photoconductive antennas,17 and even employed in detection schemes.18,19 As their utility continues to expand, there is a growing need to understand the photophysical mechanisms that govern HOIPs’ behavior in the far-infrared domain, in order to enable further optimization. However, the mechanism of THz emission remains a subject of active debate. Guzelturk et al. attributed the observed ultrafast transient photocurrent to the photo-Dember (PD) effect driven by differences in the diffusion coefficients of electrons and holes.14 In contrast, Obraztsov et al. suggested that the emission is consistent with the bulk photovoltaic effect arising from a combination of injection and shift currents. Yet, due to limitations in time resolution during their photocurrent measurements, they acknowledged the difficulty in distinguishing whether the photocurrent was induced directly by optical excitation or indirectly via secondary processes such as thermal effects.20 Other mechanisms have also been proposed. These include surface electric field-driven emission,21 high-order shift currents such as fourth-order nonlinear optical effects in CsPbBr₃,22 ultrafast spin currents in two-dimensional hybrid metal halides,23 and ultrafast photogalvanic currents in two-dimensional phenethylammonium lead iodide.24 Furthermore, Cheng et al.25 observed a helicity-dependent THz photocurrent in their MAPbI₃ thin films attributing its origin to the circular photogalvanic effect (CPGE)—where right and left circularly polarized light produce distinct photocurrents. However, it is important to note that CPGE is a second-order nonlinear optical effect, where the photocurrent density is proportional to the square of the electric field amplitude of the incident light.25 We note further that in several other THz spectroscopic studies of hybrid perovskites, it has been identified that the CPGE is primary mechanism responsible for THz emission.26–28 It is essential to consider the specific characteristics of the starting materials, preparation methods and parameters, as well as the pre- and post-processing treatments applied to these films since these factors significantly influence the photophysical properties of the resulting samples—such as whether they are two-dimensional or three-dimensional, single or polycrystalline, and the presence or absence of phonon bands. Each film possesses unique features, and therefore, may not necessarily exhibit identical mechanisms.

In this study, we investigate the effect of thiocyanate (SCN) treatment in formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI₃) thin films. By incrementally increasing the SCN content, we observed a corresponding enhancement in the peak-to-peak amplitude of the emitted THz pulse as measured by THz emission spectroscopy. Given that SCN is known for its defect passivation properties in HOIPs, and as confirmed through a range of surface and bulk spectroscopic and microscopic techniques, we surmise that the enhancement arises from surface-defect-mediated processes. Specifically, photogenerated charge carriers are accelerated by built-in surface electric fields. But more importantly, it is not the concentration of these surface fields, but rather their orientation—enabled by improved crystallinity—that proves critical for efficient charge acceleration and enhanced THz emission. This work contributes to the fundamental understanding of HOIPs in THz regime and offers deeper insight not only into how surface defect engineering can be strategically employed to optimize far-infrared emission but also in unfolding the limits of its efficacy, thereby extending the utility of these materials for future THz optoelectronic applications.

Results and discussion

Figure 1a shows the time-domain THz radiation emitted from MAPbI₃ thin films with stoichiometric composition: equal amounts of MAI and PbI₂, 10% excess MAI, and 10% excess PbI₂. All three traces exhibit a single-cycle pulse with a duration of approximately 2.5 ps. The corresponding frequency-domain spectra (Fig. 1b) revealed an emission peak around 0.4 THz and extending up to 1.5 THz. THz radiation can be emitted from the surface of a semiconductor when it is irradiated by an ultrafast laser pulse (typically a few hundred femtoseconds) with photon energy exceeding the material’s bandgap. Two primary mechanisms have been proposed to explain the origin of such ultrashort electromagnetic response. The first involves nonlinear optical process where the intense optical excitation induces a transient current through second (χ2)- or third-order (χ3) changes in the material’s polarization. If THz emission arises from such nonlinear effect, the amplitude of the emitted electric field should scale nonlinearly (e.g., quadratically or cubically) with the intensity of the incident laser. However, our prior study on identical HOIP thin films have shown a linear relationship between pump power and THz field amplitude (slope of ~ 2 in a log-log plot), indicating that nonlinear optical processes are not the dominant mechanism.21 Recent work by Li et al. has demonstrated that two-dimensional Ruddlesden-Popper (RP) halide perovskites are capable of emitting THz pulses, which they attributed to a second-order nonlinear polarization involving both electric dipole and electric quadrupole contributions.29 However, it should be noted that this mechanism is likely observable only under sub-band-gap excitation at 800 nm, necessitating the use of highly intense pump light. As demonstrated in our previous work, such a second-order process is not present in our films.21 The second mechanism is based on surge currents, which may occur through two scenarios. In the first, known as the photo-Dember (PD) effect, asymmetry in carrier mobilities results in net current generation: high-mobility electrons are slowed by low-mobility holes, and vice versa, due to mutual electrostatic attraction. For the PD effect to dominate, a large disparity between electron and hole mobilities is required—as seen in materials like InAs and InSb, where the electron-to-hole mobility ratios exceed 90. In contrast, experimental30–34 and theoretical35,36 studies on HOIPs report mobility ratios not larger than 5, suggesting that the PD effect is unlikely to be the primary contributor to THz emission. Alternatively, THz radiation can also be emitted when photogenerated carriers are accelerated by an electric field—either externally applied or internally built-in. In our case, external bias is excluded as the THz emission experiments were performed on unbiased, bare thin films. This points toward the role of built-in surface electric fields, which can arise from surface defects introduced during film fabrication—e.g., via compositional tuning, doping, or additive incorporation. To investigate this mechanism, we systematically varied the film growth conditions and compared the resulting peak-to-peak amplitudes of the emitted THz fields. The correlation between emission strength and preparation conditions supports the interpretation that surface defect-induced electric fields are the dominant factor governing THz generation in our MAPbI₃ thin films.

A subtle yet measurable difference in the electric field amplitude of the emitted THz radiation was observed for MAPbI₃ thin films with stoichiometric composition and those containing a 10% excess of either MAI or PbI₂ (Fig. 1a). Previous studies have reported that varying the MAI: PbI₂ ratio directly influences the type and concentration of defects in HOIP thin films. Gujar et al. demonstrated that excess PbI₂ tends to induce p-type behavior,37 whereas excess MAI results in n-type conductivity.38 In addition to compositional variation, we also examined the effect of annealing time under otherwise identical growth conditions for films with a 1:1 MAI: PbI₂ ratio. Specifically, we varied the annealing time from 1 min to 10 and 30 min. Prior work by Kazemi et al. has shown that longer annealing times can significantly alter the surface defect landscape in HOIPs.39 Figure 1c summarizes the peak-to-peak amplitudes of the emitted THz pulses under these different growth conditions. Both compositional adjustments (samples 1–3) and changes in annealing time (samples 3–5) were found to affect the THz emission strength. However, no consistent trend was observed across the samples. While these observations suggest a link between THz emission amplitude and the nature or density of surface defects, the precise origin of the variations remains inconclusive. To further investigate this effect, a series of formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI₃) thin films were fabricated using identical precursor ratios and annealing conditions but with varying amounts of SCN additive. The corresponding peak-to-peak amplitudes of the emitted THz radiation are presented in Fig. 1c for films containing 0–10% SCN (samples 6–11). A general increase in THz emission amplitude was observed with increasing amount of SCN, except for the sample with 10% SCN, which deviated from this trend. The origin of this anomaly will be addressed in a later section.

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) images of the nanoparticle thin films (Fig. 2a–f) reveal a clear dependence of grain size on the amount of SCN. The root-mean-square (RMS) roughness (Fig. 2g) initially decreases from 19 nm for film without SCN to 17 nm with 2% SCN, and to 15 nm with 4% SCN. However, with further increases in SCN, the roughness progressively rises to 24 nm, 40 nm, and 51 nm for 6%, 8%, and 10% SCN, respectively. This trend is consistent with prior reports indicating that increasing amounts of SCN40,41 or ammonium thiocyanates42,43 led to larger grain sizes. Given that all other growth parameters were identical and only the amount of SCN was varied, the type of surface defects is expected to remain largely similar across samples. Consequently, variations in the total surface area (TSA)—defined as the actual three-dimensional surface area considering nanoscale topography—could likely account for differences in surface defect concentration. A larger TSA implies a lower density of surface defects per unit area, resulting in a weaker built-in surface electric field, hence, a lower peak-to-peak amplitude of the emitted THz radiation. Based on this reasoning, films with 0–4% SCN, which exhibit higher surface-to-volume ratios would be expected to produce stronger THz emission while films with larger grains (6% and 8% SCN) should yield weaker emission. However, the experimental data reveal the opposite trend suggesting that additional mechanisms beyond surface area effects are influencing the THz emission behavior.

The contact potential differences (CPD) between the AFM tip and the surface of the films, as determined by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM), are presented in Fig. 2h (see supplementary material, Fig. S1, for Vcpd maps of the films). The results indicate that samples with smaller grain sizes (0%, 2%, and 4% SCN) exhibit somewhat similar CPD values, whereas an increase in SCN (6%, 8%, and 10%) leads to a noticeable buildup. Complementary macroscopic Kelvin probe (KP) measurements were conducted to assess the work function (WF) of the thin films. As shown in Fig. S2, films with higher SCN amounts—corresponding to larger particle diameters—display increased WF values. While some discrepancies exist between the two measurement methods, e.g., KPFM reveals nearly constant CPD signals for smaller nanoparticles with a subsequent continuous rise at larger diameters, whereas KP shows a consistent WF increase that decreases at 10% SCN, these can be attributed to the differing spatial scales involved: KPFM probes localized nanoscopic regions while KP measures macroscopic (millimeter-scale) areas. Nonetheless, both techniques demonstrate a general trend of increasing values with higher SCN content. Collectively, the observed enlargement of nanoparticles via AFM, along with the elevated surface potential and WF measured through KPFM and KP, respectively, correspond directly to the increased peak-to-peak amplitude of THz emission. These findings suggest that the primary mechanism of THz radiation emission involves the acceleration of photogenerated charges by a strengthened surface electric field, which intensifies with SCN amount, despite the anticipated reduction in defect density for larger nanoparticles.

Structural, optical, and non-contact electrical characterization techniques were employed to better understand not only surface defect concentrations but also the bulk quality of the FAPbI₃ thin films and their impact on THz emission. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns shown in Fig. 3a reveal broader and less intense peaks for film without SCN indicative of poor crystallinity. As the SCN concentration increases, these peaks become sharper and more defined, with the 10% SCN film exhibiting two prominent peaks at 14.9° and 26°, signifying enhanced crystallinity. Since X-rays probe deep into the material, these results primarily reflect a reduction in bulk defects. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra in Fig. 3b further support this trend. Higher SCN concentrations result in increased PL intensity and narrower emission bandwidths. Since the PL emission is centered around ~ 800 nm—close to the optical absorption edge (see Fig. S3)—it likely originates from direct band-to-band transitions rather than from defect states, which typically emit at lower energies. This confirms that the observed PL arises from the bulk of the material. The enhancement in PL intensity and narrowing of emission features with increasing SCN content are consistent with improved bulk crystallinity, corroborating the XRD findings. For nanoparticles with smaller diameters, the high surface-to-volume ratio implies a higher density of surface defects and, consequently, stronger built-in surface electric fields. Nonetheless, the XRD data reveal multiple peaks, denoting a variety of crystallographic orientations within the film. The surface electric fields arising from defects on these differently oriented planes can cancel each other due to their varying directions, thus reducing the net effective electric field despite its large population. In contrast, larger nanoparticles (with higher SCN concentrations) exhibit fewer and sharper XRD peaks, reflecting fewer crystallographic orientations, i.e., improved crystalline order. Although the surface-to-volume ratio is lower—implying fewer surface defects—the more uniform crystal structure facilitates better alignment of the built-in surface electric fields. This preferred orientation enhances the net surface potential, as reflected in the increased CPD and WF measurements at higher SCN concentrations. This explanation is consistent with findings by Deicher et al., who reported that the principal axis of the electric field gradient aligns with the symmetry axis of the defect–probe atom configuration, linking electric field orientation to the crystallographic arrangement of defects.44 Similarly, Zheng et al. demonstrated, through both experiments and simulations, a linear relationship between dielectric properties (e.g., capacitance) and the degree of orientational order in nanoparticles.45 Therefore, even though large-diameter nanoparticles exhibit fewer surface defects, the enhanced orientational coherence of the built-in surface field due to improved crystal alignment results in a stronger effective electric field—leading to higher THz peak-to-peak amplitudes. It is important to note that this effect has not been observed in traditional inorganic semiconductor THz emitters, which are typically single-crystalline and possess built-in fields with fixed orientations. We note however that at the highest SCN concentration of 10%, where the nanoparticles reach their largest diameters, the THz peak-to-peak emission amplitude decreases. This suggests that while the crystallinity and field alignment remain high, the density of surface defects—and thus the strength of the built-in surface electric field—becomes too low to effectively drive charge carrier acceleration. As a result, the THz emission is weakened. This interpretation is further supported by the lower WF observed in the 10% SCN film, as obtained by KP measurements (Fig. S2).

Ultrafast, non-contact photoconductive measurements were also carried out using the optical-pump THz-probe (OPTP) technique. This time-resolved spectroscopic method enables the measurement of photoconductivity (Δσ), which reflects the product of the quantum yield (ϕ)—the efficiency of photon-to-electron conversion—and the charge carrier mobility (µ). Several studies have employed OPTP to investigate charge carrier dynamics in hybrid perovskite materials.46–49 Figures 4a and 4b show the photoconductivity kinetics of FAPbI₃ thin films with varying SCN amounts as a function of pump-probe delay time over a 400 ps window. These plots reveal clear variations in both the peak photoconductivity at early time scales (Fig. 4a) and the decay dynamics at later times (Fig. 4b). At zero pump-probe delay (t = 0), where the quantum yield is typically assumed to be unity, the mobility of the film without SCN is measured at 48.5 cm²/V·s. The mobility increases steadily with SCN concentration, reaching a maximum of ~ 57 cm²/V·s at 8% SCN, before declining to 44.2 cm²/V·s at 10% SCN. The increasing mobility from 0 to 8% SCN aligns with the improved crystallinity observed in the XRD and PL results (Fig. 3a and 3b), indicating reduced structural disorder and fewer scattering centers that impede carrier motion. Additionally, larger grain sizes allow charge carriers to travel longer distances without encountering grain boundaries, further enhancing mobility. Furthermore, as demonstrated by the contact potential difference (Fig. 2h) and Kelvin probe (Fig. S2) analyses, these films exhibit better aligned surface electric fields due to enhanced crystal orientation, thereby facilitating more efficient charge carrier transport. This improvement is reflected in the increased mobility of the charge carriers observed in OPTP measurements (Fig. 4a), which directly correlates with the measured rise in peak-to-peak THz emission amplitudes (Fig. 1b), as higher mobility enables the generation of stronger transient currents. A similar mechanism underlies the operation of high electron mobility transistors (HEMTs), where high-mobility electrons are responsible for generating intense THz pulses.50.

An unexpected trend was observed for the film containing 10% SCN. Based on the discussion above on the impact of improved crystallinity and a more aligned built-in surface electric field on charge carrier mobility, one would anticipate this film to exhibit the highest mobility. However, it displays the lowest measured mobility of 44.2 cm²/V·s (Fig. 4a). We attribute this counterintuitive result to the significantly reduced concentration of surface defects in this film, which arises from its notably low surface-to-volume ratio. Although the enhanced crystallinity results in a well-aligned surface electric field, the insufficient density of surface defects limits the overall strength of the built-in field. As a result, the film is less effective in accelerating photogenerated carriers, leading to reduced mobility and a correspondingly lower THz peak-to-peak emission amplitude (Fig. 1c). In other words, this paradoxical outcome can be traced to the fundamental interplay between crystallinity, defect density, and the resulting surface electric field. In this film (d ~ 50 nm, Fig. 2g), the exceptionally low surface-to-volume ratio drastically suppresses the formation of surface defect states, which is responsible for promoting charge transport and strengthening the built-in field. Although the enhanced crystallinity produces a highly ordered lattice and a well-aligned surface electric field, the absence of a sufficient density of defect sites critically undermines the overall field intensity. This weakened field is incapable of imparting the necessary acceleration to photogenerated carriers, thereby restricting their mobility as plotted in the OPTP kinetics in Fig. 4a (pink trace). The direct consequence of this retardation is a measurable decline in the THz peak-to-peak emission amplitude, as shown in Fig. 1c. Essentially, the very structural modification that improves lattice alignment simultaneously deprives the system of the defect-driven mechanisms required to sustain a strong built-in field, leading to diminished THz emission efficiency.

Finally, the behavior of charge carriers over a 400 ps timescale was analyzed using the normalized photoconductivity kinetics plotted in Fig. 4b. Previous studies have shown that in HOIPs, carrier mobility remains constant for at least 1 ns as measured by OPTP, and even up to several milliseconds according to microwave conductivity measurements.49 Since the photoconductivity is the product of carrier concentration and mobility—and the mobility remaining constant over the duration of the present measurements as shown in previous works—the observed decay in photoconductivity can be attributed solely to a reduction in carrier population, i.e., charge trapping either at the surface or within the bulk. For the film without SCN, photoconductivity drops to 50% of its initial value after 400 ps, while in the film with 10% SCN, it retains 95% at the same pump-probe delay time. This stark contrast can be explained by a combination of factors. First, the higher surface-to-volume ratio in films with smaller nanoparticle sizes leads to a greater density of surface defects, which act as efficient trapping centers. Second, the presence of multiple crystallographic orientations in these films results in a poorly aligned and weaker built-in surface electric field, as discussed earlier. This weaker field is less effective at separating photogenerated electrons and holes, increasing the likelihood of recombination shortly after excitation. Third, the smaller nanoparticles further confine charge carriers, increasing the probability of recombination due to charges’ spatial proximity with each other. Finally, in films with smaller diameters and higher defect densities, surface recombination can occur almost immediately after photoexcitation, which in turn suppresses the built-in surface electric field. This explains both the lower work function values observed in KP measurements and the diminished THz peak-to-peak emission. In contrast, as SCN content increases, the nanoparticle diameter grows, reducing surface defect density and promoting better alignment of internal electric fields. This leads to more effective acceleration and separation of charge carriers, thereby reducing the probability of recombination and resulting in extended carrier lifetimes. These findings are consistent with previous reports by Liu et al.42, Duim et al.43, and Lin et al.51 where extended charge carrier lifetime consistent were obtained when SCN were added. For the largest nanoparticles, and within the signal-to-noise limitations of our OPTP setup, only a marginal decrease in photoconductivity kinetics is observed up to 400 ps (Fig. 4b, pink trace). Although charge carriers persist over extended periods, they do not enhance the THz peak-to-peak amplitude. In fact, this phenomenon adversely affects the efficient generation of THz radiation, as optimal emission requires a rapid increase and subsequent reduction of mobile carriers. This outcome further justifies the reduced THz peak-to-peak amplitude observed in films containing 10% SCN (Fig. 1c).

Summary

The peak-to-peak amplitude of the emitted THz radiation from FAPbI₃ thin films was found to correlate with increasing amounts of SCN. The primary emission mechanism is attributed to the acceleration of photogenerated charge carriers, facilitated not by a high concentration of surface defects, but by a well-aligned built-in surface electric field in larger nanoparticles. The addition of SCN enhances both the surface electric field and the crystallinity of the films—factors that contribute to stronger THz emission. However, this enhancement has limitation. As nanoparticle diameters continue to increase, the concentration of surface defects becomes so low that, despite the presence of a well-aligned electric field, charge carrier acceleration is no longer sufficiently effective, leading to a reduction in THz emission. This nuanced interplay highlights the importance of balancing crystallinity and surface defect density when designing synthesis strategies. While improved crystallinity is beneficial, maintaining an optimal level of surface defects is essential for maximizing far-infrared pulse emission.

Materials and methods section

Preparation of Cs₀.₁₇FA₀.₈₃PbI₃ with different grain sizes

The grain size of Cs₀.₁₇FA₀.₈₃PbI₃ films was controlled by varying the amount of Pb(SCN)₂ additive. Stock Solution A (1.0 M) was prepared by dissolving CsI (1.0 M, Sigma) and PbI₂ (1.0 M, Sigma) in anhydrous DMF. Stock Solution B (1.0 M) consisted of FAI (1.0 M, Dyesol) and PbI₂ (1.0 M, Sigma) dissolved in anhydrous DMF. Stock Solution C was prepared by dissolving FAI (3.0 M, Dyesol) and Pb(SCN)₂ (1.0 M, Sigma) in anhydrous DMF at a 3:1 molar ratio.

The perovskite precursor solution was then formulated by mixing Solutions A, B, and C in a molar ratio of 0.17 : (0.83 – x) : x, where x denotes the proportion of Pb(SCN)₂ used to tune the grain size. This solution was spin-coated directly onto Al₂O₃ substrates at 4000 rpm for 60 s using the antisolvent method, in which 100 µL of chlorobenzene was dispensed approximately 8 s after the start of spinning. Film formation was completed by annealing at 100 °C for 10 min, followed by a second annealing step at 140 °C for approximately 20 min.

THz pulse emission and optical pump—THz probe experiments

The THz pulse emission experiments were carried out in reflection geometry with samples illuminated at a 45° angle to their surface normal. The setup was based on an amplified Yb: KGW laser system (PHAROS, Light Conversion Ltd.) operating at 1030 nm with the pulse duration of 160 fs and the repetition rate of 200 kHz. The average power of 6 W from the laser was directed into an optical parametric amplifier (OPA, ORPHEUS, Light Conversion Ltd.), which can tune the central wavelength from 640 nm to 2600 nm. The samples investigated were irradiated with an OPA output beam delivering an average optical power of 100 mW (d = 2 mm) and a pump fluence of 15 µJ/cm² at a wavelength of 650 nm, which is s polarized. All films underwent this saturated excitation conditions to ensure that discrepancies in the observed THz peak-to-peak amplitudes were attributable solely to sample differences, rather than alterations in pump parameters. The sample-emitted THz pulses were detected with a GaAsBi photoconducting switch (TeraVil Ltd.) activated by a small fraction (average power of ~ 5 mW) of the Yb: KGW laser beam. The optical pump – THz probe experiment layout was similar, except the THz radiation was generated by a p-InAs crystal, which is known as an efficient surface THz emitter, while the perovskite samples were illuminated through a 3 mm aperture with wavelength of 800 nm. All experiments were performed at room temperature.
