Comparative analysis of ABC, LTP, and WAK families in non-specific immunity of wheat and sunflower
Authors: Mykola Patyka, Renjun Wang, Serhii Khablak, Lesia Bondareva, Tetyana Patyka, Mykola Dolia, Valentyn Spychak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plants activate complex defense mechanisms against biotic and abiotic stressors, where non-specific resistance provides broad-spectrum protection. This study presents a comparative bioinformatics analysis of three membrane protein families—ABC transporters, lipid transfer proteins (LTPs), and wall-associated kinase receptors (WAKs)—in winter wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) and sunflower (Helianthus annuus L.). Using public databases, we identified 150 ABC transporters, 80 LTPs, and 55 WAKs in wheat, and 120 ABC, 65 LTPs, and 40 WAKs in sunflower. Structural analysis confirmed conserved domains, including ABC_NBD, TMD, PR-14, and WAK kinase. Transcriptomic data revealed distinct immune strategies: winter wheat effectively implements systemic acquired resistance (SAR), particularly through ABCG transporters (Lr34/Sr57 type) that modulate hormone balance and defense metabolites. In contrast, sunflower predominantly activates a rapid localized immune response (LAR), characterized by ROS signaling triggered by WAK receptors functioning as damage sensors. Functional divergence was observed: wheat WAKs and LTPs are primarily involved in cell wall remodeling and physical barrier induction, while in sunflower, they focus on signaling and membrane stabilization under oxidative stress. Our findings suggest that the evolutionary adaptation of monocots and dicots led to the functional specialization of conserved immune components according to their distinct pathogen spectra and physiological features. These results provide a molecular basis for understanding the diversity of non-specific immunity mechanisms in strategically important crops.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51029-5.

Introduction

Plants, as sessile organisms, are constantly exposed to a diverse array of biotic and abiotic stressors. Microorganisms, including fungi, bacteria, and viruses, are significant biotic factors that cause substantial crop losses worldwide. According to FAO estimates, up to 40% of potential yield is lost annually due to pests and diseases1. Simultaneously, the increasing frequency and intensity of abiotic stresses such as drought, salinity, extreme temperatures, and heavy metals, driven by global climate change, further exacerbate plant vulnerability and can modulate their susceptibility to pathogens2. According to recent studies3, abiotic factors have a significant impact on the immune response and yield of sunflowers. In a regional context, entomological pressure should also be taken into account. In particular, research by Dolya et al.4 indicates that click beetles (Elateridae) have a significant impact on sunflower crops in the forest-steppe zone of Ukraine, thereby increasing the overall biotic vulnerability of the crop. Thus, understanding and enhancing the natural defense mechanisms of plants is critically important for ensuring food security and the development of sustainable agricultural systems with minimal environmental impact. The stability of agroecosystems directly depends on the ability of crop plants to withstand complex environmental pressures.

The plant immune system is a multilayered and complex network of defense responses. Traditionally, two main layers are distinguished: basal immunity, also known as PAMP-Triggered Immunity (PTI), and effector-triggered immunity, or ETI5,6. PTI is activated upon recognition of conserved microbial molecular patterns (PAMPs) or endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) by plasma membrane-localized pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as receptor-like kinases (RLKs) and receptor-like proteins (RLPs)7. This layer provides a first line of defense characterized by broad-spectrum activity but relatively low intensity. This interaction between plants and their environment is a cornerstone of agroecology, and understanding the molecular basis of plant resilience is key to engineering more robust and adaptive agricultural landscapes. Pathogens that successfully overcome PTI often secrete effector proteins that suppress basal immunity8.

In response, plants have evolved intracellular receptors, predominantly from the NLR family, which recognize these effectors, triggering ETI – a robust response often accompanied by the hypersensitive reaction (HR) and localized cell death at the site of infection9,10.

In addition to the classical PTI and ETI, so-called quantitative or non-specific resistance plays a significant role in shaping plant resistance. This type of resistance is often associated with durable protection against a broad spectrum of pathogens and is controlled by genes that do not belong to classical R-genes (NLRs)11–13. Non-specific resistance is particularly valuable for agriculture because it is more stable against the emergence of new pathogen races compared to race-specific ETI.

Key players in non-specific immunity are proteins functioning at the interface between the cell and the environment, particularly membrane receptors and transporters. Among these, three families are of particular interest: ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, lipid transfer proteins (LTPs), and wall-associated kinase receptors (WAKs)14. ABC transporters are a large family of proteins that mediate ATP-dependent transport of a wide variety of substrates across membranes, including phytohormones, secondary metabolites, lipids, and toxins15–18. Some ABC transporters, such as Lr34/Yr18/Sr57/Pm38 in wheat, are directly linked to durable, broad-spectrum resistance19,20. LTPs are small secretory proteins localized in the apoplast that participate in the formation of physical barriers, lipid transport, and signaling processes21–23. WAKs are receptor-like kinases that interact with cell wall components and function as sensors of cell wall integrity, activating defense signaling cascades in response to damage or pathogen attack24,25.

Winter wheat (Triticum aestivum), as a representative of monocotyledonous grasses, and sunflower (Helianthus annuus), as a representative of dicotyledonous asterids, are strategically important crops. They have distinct evolutionary trajectories and are adapted to different pathogen spectra and growing conditions2627, [Wheat is known for its ability to develop effective]. Wheat is known for its ability to develop effective systemic resistance, mediated by genes like Lr3420, which provides protection against biotrophic pathogens that slowly colonize tissues. Sunflower, often affected by necrotrophic pathogens that rapidly kill cells (e.g., Sclerotinia sclerotiorum), develops robust localized responses and mechanisms of tolerance to abiotic stresses28.

Based on these observations, this study hypothesizes that the evolutionary divergence of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants, as well as adaptation to different ecological niches and pathogen spectra, has led to the functional specialization and differential roles of conserved components of non-specific immunity, particularly ABC transporters, LTPs, and WAK receptors, reflecting different molecular-ecological defense strategies.

This work aims to conduct a comparative bioinformatics analysis of the role of ABC transporters, lipid transfer proteins (LTPs), and WAK receptors in shaping non-specific resistance in winter wheat and sunflower, to identify common and distinct features of their functioning, and to evaluate their contribution to the different immune response strategies of these crops. Understanding these evolutionarily shaped mechanisms is crucial for developing new, more effective approaches to creating resistant cultivars and optimizing agricultural technologies under increasing stress pressure on agroecosystems.

While individual roles of these protein families have been studied, their integrated comparative analysis in a monocot–dicot framework remains limited. This study fills this gap by examining how these conserved components are differentially repurposed to support divergent immune strategies. We hypothesize that the evolutionary divergence between monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants has led to distinct functional specializations of conserved ABC, LTP, and WAK protein families. Specifically, we expect these families to be integrated into a systemic acquired resistance (SAR) strategy in wheat and a rapid localized response (LAR) strategy in sunflower, reflecting their adaptation to different ecological niches and pathogen spectra.

Materials and methods

Triticum aestivum L. (winter wheat) and Helianthus annuus L. (sunflower) were selected for comparative analysis. These species are important crops with available complete genome data29,30. Their selection is also based on contrasting immune response characteristics: wheat is known for its SAR mediated by genes like Lr3420, while sunflower effectively counteracts necrotrophs through localized ROS signaling28,31. The reference genome sequences and functional annotations used were IWGSC RefSeq v2.1 for Triticum aestivum and HanXRQv2 for Helianthus annuus, both retrieved from the Ensembl Plants database.

Candidate genes were identified using local BLASTp (v2.13.0+; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) searches. To ensure high stringency, we applied an E-value threshold of ≤ 10⁻¹⁰ and a minimum sequence identity of 40% over 80% of the query length.

Searches were conducted using keywords related to protein family names (e.g., “ABC transporter”, “lipid transfer protein”, “wall-associated kinase”), as well as conserved domain identifiers (PFAM and InterPro). Selection criteria included the presence of at least one characteristic domain and/or functional annotation indicating a potential role in the cell wall, membrane transport, or immune response.

Identified sequences were checked for the presence of signal peptides (for LTPs) and transmembrane domains (for ABC and WAK) using tools like SignalP (v6.0; https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?SignalP-6.0) and TMHMM (v2.0; https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/service.php?TMHMM-2.0).

Analysis of domain structure and protein functions

Functional domain annotation was performed using InterProScan (v5.61-93.0; https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro/search/sequence/) and Pfam37 web servers.

Homologs (orthologs and paralogs) in other plant species, including Arabidopsis thaliana, were searched using BLASTp (v2.13.0+; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) with an E-value threshold of ≤ 10⁻⁵ and sequence identity ≥ 30%.

Three-dimensional protein structures and functional sites were modeled using Phyre2 (v2.0; http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/phyre2) and I-TASSER (v5.1; https://zhanggroup.org/I-TASSER/). Model quality assessment was performed using standard metrics (Confidence Score in Phyre2 and C-score in I-TASSER).

Identification of Genes (BLAST parameters)

Candidate genes were identified using local BLASTp (v2.13.0+; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) searches with high stringency parameters: an E-value threshold ≤ 10⁻¹⁰ and a minimum sequence identity of 40% over 80% of the query length.

Analysis of gene expression

Transcriptomic data for Triticum aestivum were obtained from the Wheat Expression Browser (http://www.wheat-expression.com)38. Datasets related to biotic stress responses (e.g., infection with Puccinia, Blumeria, Zymoseptoria, Fusarium) and abiotic stress responses (e.g., drought, salinity) were analyzed. For Helianthus annuus, data were obtained from the Sunflower Genome Database30. Datasets related to responses to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, Botrytis cinerea, and abiotic stresses were analyzed. Gene expression levels were analyzed using TPM, RPKM, or FPKM values, depending on the available data format. Statistical analysis of differential expression (comparing expression levels between control and experimental conditions) was performed using appropriate tools available on the web resources or using R packages including DESeq2 using R packages including DESeq2 (v1.38.3; https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/DESeq2.html) and limma (Bioconductor) after downloading raw data, if possible. A p-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant (with correction for multiple testing, e.g., using the Benjamini-Hochberg method, if applied). R (e.g., ggplot2, pheatmap) and Python (pandas, seaborn, matplotlib) packages were used for visualizing expression profiles. Transcriptomic datasets included responses to biotrophic (Puccinia striiformis) and necrotrophic (Sclerotinia sclerotiorum) pathogens. For differential expression analysis, raw counts were processed using the DESeq2 package in R. Genes were considered differentially expressed (DEGs) if they met the criteria of |log2Fold Change|≥1.0 and an adjusted p-value (Benjamini-Hochberg) < 0.05.

Raw counts were processed using the DESeq2 package in the R environment. Genes were considered differentially expressed if they exhibited an absolute log₂ fold change (|log₂FC|) ≥ 1.0 and an adjusted p-value (P_adj) < 0.05.

Functional grouping and comparative analysis

Identified genes and the proteins they encode were grouped based on: Protein family type (ABC, LTP, WAK/RLK); Presence and type of conserved domains; Predicted cellular localization; Expression profiles in response to different stressors (biotic vs. abiotic, different pathogen types); Functional annotation obtained from databases and literature.

The results of grouping and analysis were presented in the form of comparative tables and schemes to identify common and distinct features of the functioning of these proteins in wheat and sunflower and to build a model of non-specific immunity.

Software and databases

Web servers and software packages listed above were used for bioinformatics analysis. All analyses were performed on standard computing equipment. Gene structure visualizations (exon-intron patterns) were generated using the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS 2.0) (v2.0; http://gsds.gao-lab.org/). Chromosomal localization maps were constructed using the TBtools software suite (v1.108; https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools).

Chromosomal distribution and gene structure

The identified immunity-related genes were unevenly distributed across chromosomes in both species. In Triticum aestivum, members of the ABC and WAK gene families were localized on all homologous groups; however, an increased density of specific subfamilies was observed on chromosomes of groups 3, 5, and 7, which have previously been associated with clusters of genes conferring resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses. A similar pattern of uneven distribution with the formation of local gene clusters was also detected in Helianthus annuus, consistent with reported evidence of segmental duplications and tandem amplifications in both genomes.

Exon–intron organization analysis performed using GSDS 2.0 revealed relative structural conservation within individual subfamilies. In particular, members of the ABCG subfamily in wheat and sunflower exhibited conservation of the characteristic exon number and a similar NBD–TMD domain architecture, indicating evolutionary preservation of their functional specialization in membrane transport before the divergence of monocot and dicot lineages.

Annotation data from the reference assemblies (T. aestivum IWGSC RefSeq v2.0 and H. annuus HanXRQr2.0), available in Ensembl Plants and NCBI, confirm variability in gene length and exon number within the analyzed families. ABC transporters are characterized by a relatively complex exon–intron organization with multiple exons, whereas LTP genes generally exhibit a compact structure (often comprising 1–2 exons). WAK genes occupy an intermediate position in terms of structural complexity and display a conserved organization of the kinase domain.

A detailed analysis of structural parameters of representative ABC, LTP, and WAK genes confirmed substantial variability in exon–intron organization within these families. In wheat, members of the ABCG subfamily (e.g., Lr34/Sr57; TraesCS7D02G317700) are characterized by a complex structure comprising more than 20 exons and a large gene size (> 10 kb), whereas LTP genes display a compact organization (1–2 exons). WAK genes occupy an intermediate position, typically containing 4–6 exons and exhibiting a conserved kinase domain architecture.

A similar trend is observed in sunflower (HanXRQv2), where ABCG genes contain 19–23 exons, LTP genes predominantly 1–2 exons, and WAK genes 4–5 exons. Such structural conservation of subfamilies across monocots and dicots indicates evolutionary preservation of their functional specialization (A complete dataset of structural characteristics is available in the Supplementary Materials).

Chromosomal Distribution and Gene Structure. The identified immunity-related genes were non-randomly distributed across the chromosomes of both species. In T. aestivum, ABC and WAK genes showed significant clustering on chromosomes of homologous groups 3, 5, and 7, which are known to harbor major biotic stress resistance loci. Analysis via the GSDS 2.0 tool revealed a high degree of conservation in exon-intron structures within each subfamily. Specifically, for orthologous ABCG transporters, the position and number of introns (ranging from 18 to 25 in wheat and 19 to 23 in sunflower) were highly consistent, suggesting that their functional roles in membrane transport were established before the divergence of these lineages. Comparative analysis of functional specialization of ABC, LTP, and WAK in wheat and sunflower

Analysis of the functional annotation and expression profiles of the identified genes revealed distinct differences in the roles of ABC transporters, LTP proteins, and WAK receptors in the non-specific resistance of wheat and sunflower.

ABC transporters

in wheat, a significant portion of the identified ABCG transporters (particularly orthologs of the Lr34/Sr57 and Lr46/Sr58 genes) exhibited high expression levels in response to infection by biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens, consistent with their known role in SAR19,20. These transporters are likely involved in the transport of salicylic acid (SA), abscisic acid (ABA), or other signaling molecules that induce systemic resistance, as well as in the export of phytoalexins or other defense metabolites. In sunflowers, ABC transporters are also represented by a numerous family, but their functional specialization, based on expression profiles, is more focused on local detoxification, lipid transport, and membrane stabilization, which is important for responding to necrotrophic pathogens and oxidative stress. For example, identified ABCG transporters in sunflower (orthologs of HaABCG31, HaABCG25, HaABCG29) show induction of expression upon exposure to necrotrophs and abiotic stressors.

LTP proteins

In wheat, LTP genes (e.g., TaLTP1-5) are primarily expressed in epidermal and vascular tissues and, based on annotation, are involved in cuticle formation, wax deposition, and lipid transport, contributing to the strengthening of physical barriers against pathogen penetration22,39. Some wheat LTPs may also participate in the transport of signaling lipids important for SAR. In sunflowers, LTPs are also numerous, but their expression profiles and annotation indicate a more pronounced role in signaling pathways related to stress response and stabilization of cell membranes under the action of necrotrophic toxins or abiotic factors.

WAK receptors

WAK receptors in both species contain characteristic extracellular WAK domains interacting with cell wall components and intracellular kinase domains. However, their functional specialization differs. In wheat, WAK receptors (e.g., TaWAK2, TaWAK6) are primarily associated with recognizing DAMPs generated upon cell wall damage and inducing cell wall strengthening through callose and lignin deposition, which is an important barrier against pathogen penetration25. In sunflower, identified WAK receptors (e.g., HaWAK-RLK1, WAKL orthologs) act as key sensors of cell wall damage and activators of rapid local ROS signaling and MAPK cascades, which is a central mechanism of response to necrotrophic pathogens28. These patterns of functional specialization across key protein families – ABC transporters, LTP proteins, and WAK receptors – highlight the evolutionary tuning of non-specific immunity to distinct pathogenic challenges in wheat and sunflower (Table 2; Fig. 2).

Analysis of gene expression profiles in response to biotic and abiotic stresses

Analysis of transcriptomic data confirmed the differential expression of ABC, LTP, and WAK genes in response to various types of stressors in wheat and sunflower.

In wheat, genes associated with SAR (e.g., Lr34/Sr57) showed induced expression not only at the site of primary infection but also in systemic tissues, supporting their role in systemic resistance. Genes for WAK and LTP in wheat were often induced in response to mechanical damage or attack by pathogens that degrade the cell wall, consistent with their role in strengthening physical barriers.

Evolutionary differentiation and interaction with different pathogen types

The observed differences in functional specialization and immune response strategies between wheat and sunflower, mediated by ABC, LTP, and WAK, can be explained by evolutionary adaptation to different ecological niches and dominant pathogen types.

Wheat, as a grass with a rigid, lignified cell wall, has evolved effective mechanisms to counteract biotrophic pathogens (e.g., rust, powdery mildew) that require living host cells. SAR, mediated by ABCG transporters, provides systemic preparedness and limits the spread of biotrophs without massive cell death. WAKs and LTPs in wheat support this strategy by reinforcing physical barriers, making it difficult for pathogens to penetrate.

Sunflower, with its more flexible cell wall, which is more susceptible to the action of necrotrophic enzymes, has developed a robust localized response aimed at rapidly limiting the spread of infection at the site of attack. The key role of WAK receptors as damage sensors and triggers of the ROS burst allows for a rapid response to necrotroph attack, which actively degrades the cell wall. LTPs and ABC transporters in sunflowers also function primarily locally, contributing to detoxification and membrane stabilization, which is important for cell survival under the action of necrotrophic toxins or abiotic factors. The lack of pronounced SAR in sunflowers may be related to the fact that an energetically costly systemic response is less advantageous under conditions of frequent exposure to local stressors, where the speed of reaction at the site of damage is more critical (Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings support the original hypothesis that conserved membrane proteins are differentially repurposed to support either systemic (wheat) or localized (sunflower) defense responses.

The conducted bioinformatics analysis suggests significant cellular and molecular differences in the mechanisms of non-specific resistance between winter wheat and sunflower, supporting our hypothesis about the functional divergence of conserved immune components. ABC transporters, LTP proteins, and WAK receptors, although present in both species as evolutionarily conserved elements, demonstrate distinct specialization reflecting the different adaptive strategies of these crops.

Wheat’s strategy, focused on developing systemic acquired resistance (SAR), is highly effective against biotrophic and hemibiotrophic pathogens characterized by relatively slow tissue colonization. The key role of Lr34/Sr57 type ABCG transporters is consistent with a role in providing durable, race-non-specific resistance in wheat is well-documented40. Our bioinformatics analysis of expression profiles supports this role, showing induced expression of corresponding genes in systemic tissues upon infection. The mechanism of action of Lr34/Sr57 likely involves the transport of signaling molecules such as SA or ABA, which induce a “primed” state in uninfected plant parts, increasing their readiness for defense response. Additionally, these transporters may be involved in the export of antimicrobial compounds or cell wall components, contributing to barrier strengthening. The role of WAK receptors and LTPs in wheat, as shown by our analysis, is also primarily integrated into this systemic response, contributing to the reinforcement of physical barriers of the cell wall and cuticle throughout the plant, which is an effective complement to systemic signaling.

In contrast, sunflowers have evolved a strategy of rapid localized response (LAR), optimized for counteracting necrotrophic pathogens and abiotic stresses. Necrotrophs rapidly kill host cells, and a systemic HR-like response would be counterproductive. In this context, rapid recognition of cell wall damage via WAK receptors and activation of potent local ROS signaling is critically important28. Our data suggest that WAK receptors in sunflowers function as primary sensors of DAMPs, triggering cascades leading to rapid ROS accumulation and activation of defense genes at the site of attack. Specifically, the activation of ABCG transporters in wheat during biotrophic infection likely facilitates the long-distance transport of signaling lipids or antioxidants, which is a hallmark of SAR. Conversely, the rapid induction of WAK receptors in sunflower identifies them as primary triggers of the oxidative burst (ROS) necessary to arrest necrotrophic spread. ABC transporters and LTPs in sunflower, as indicated by expression and annotation analysis, are also primarily involved in local processes – detoxification of oxidative stress products, transport of defense compounds directly to the affected zone, and stabilization of cell membranes, which is important for cell survival under the action of necrotrophic toxins. The absence of pronounced SAR in sunflower may be related to the fact that an energetically costly systemic response is less advantageous under conditions of frequent exposure to local stressors, where the speed of reaction at the site of damage is more critical.

The physiological and anatomical features of monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants, particularly the structure of the cell wall and epidermis, strongly correlate with the observed differences in immune strategies. The more rigid, lignified cell wall of wheat is a more effective physical barrier, which possibly favored the evolution of systemic mechanisms, while the more flexible cell wall of sunflower requires a faster and more intense local response to its damage.

From an ecological engineering perspective, these results provide a molecular blueprint for enhancing the sustainability of cropping systems. For instance, manipulating the expression of specific ABC transporters in wheat could reduce the need for synthetic fungicides, thereby lowering the chemical load on the environment and preserving soil microbial biodiversity. Similarly, enhancing localized defense mechanisms in sunflowers through targeted breeding for specific WAK alleles could improve crop performance on marginal lands prone to abiotic stresses, promoting agricultural diversification and reducing pressure on more fertile ecosystems. This approach aligns with the core principles of ecological engineering, which seek to design and manage sustainable ecosystems that integrate human society with its natural environment for the benefit of both.

The evolutionary divergence of the functions of conserved ABC, LTP, and WAK proteins between wheat and sunflower is a clear example of adaptive evolution41. Although the basic molecular functions are conserved, their roles within the overall immune network and the specificity of their interaction with signaling pathways have significantly changed under the pressure of different pathogens and environmental conditions. For example, WAK receptors, known in dicots (e.g., Arabidopsis) as receptors for oligogalacturonides (pectin DAMPs), may have evolved in grasses with their different cell wall structure (more hemicellulose and lignin) to recognize other DAMPs or mechanical signals.

The practical significance of the obtained results is substantial, especially in the context of ecological engineering and the development of climate-resilient agricultural systems. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of non-specific resistance mediated by ABC, LTP, and WAK opens new breeding opportunities. For wheat, promising approaches include searching for and introgressing alleles of ABCG genes that confer SAR, as well as WAK and LTP genes involved in cell wall remodeling. For sunflowers, it is important to identify and utilize WAK-RLK genes, ABC transporters, and LTPs that ensure an effective local ROS response and detoxification. Integrating markers for non-specific resistance with classical R-genes can lead to the creation of cultivars with broader and more durable immunity. Genome editing techniques (CRISPR/Cas) can be used for targeted modification of the expression or function of these genes to enhance resistance.

Limitations of the Study: This work is primarily based on bioinformatics analysis of public data. Experimental validation is necessary to fully validate the proposed hypotheses. This includes analyzing gene expression using RT-qPCR in response to specific stressors, functional studies of identified proteins (e.g., using knockouts or overexpression), analysis of protein-protein interactions, and measurement of physiological parameters (e.g., ROS levels, phytohormone content) in transgenic plants.

Future Directions: Future research should focus on the experimental validation of the key genes and mechanisms identified in this study. Investigating the interaction between ABC, LTP, and WAK at the molecular level, as well as their integration with other plant immunity signaling pathways, is also promising. Studying the allelic diversity of these genes in wheat and sunflower germplasm collections may reveal new sources of resistance for breeding.

The observed conservation of exon-intron architecture in ABCG genes across monocots and dicots further reinforces our hypothesis that these transporters are fundamental, ancient components of the systemic acquired resistance (SAR) framework.

Study limitations and novelty

This study provides the first integrated comparative framework for ABC, LTP, and WAK families in the context of divergent monocot–dicot immune strategies. However, its primary limitation is the reliance on in silico predictive data. While transcriptomic patterns are highly suggestive of functional specialization, experimental validation – such as RT-qPCR, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene knockout, or biochemical assays for ROS and phytohormone levels – is necessary to confirm these roles.

Novelty and Limitations of the Study. This study provides the first integrated comparative framework analyzing ABC, LTP, and WAK families specifically within the context of divergent monocot–dicot immune strategies (SAR vs. LAR). While these families are well-known, their functional divergence in winter wheat and sunflower through an evolutionary lens offers new insights into crop-specific resilience. However, a primary limitation is the predictive, in silico nature of our analysis. While transcriptomic patterns are highly suggestive of functional specialization, these results require further in vivo validation. Future research involving RT-qPCR for expression confirmation and CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing is necessary to definitively establish the roles of the identified candidates in plant defense.

Conclusions

The comparative analysis of ABC, LTP, and WAK protein families in wheat and sunflower supports the hypothesis of functional specialization of these conserved components in shaping divergent immune strategies. While winter wheat prioritizes a systemic acquired resistance (SAR) framework, primarily through the long-distance signaling functions of ABCG and LTP proteins, sunflower relies on a rapid localized response (LAR) mediated by WAK receptors acting as primary damage sensors. These findings demonstrate that despite the conservation of these protein families, their regulatory integration into immune cascades has been evolutionarily adapted to the specific physiological needs and pathogen profiles of monocots and dicots. This study provides a molecular foundation for the identification of potential candidates for targeted crop improvement, although further experimental validation via gene knockout or overexpression is required to confirm the precise functional contribution of the identified genes to non-specific immunity.
