Implementation of a stepwise process for somatic testing in patients with a new diagnosis of germline negative epithelial ovarian cancer
Authors: Casey L. Lawler, Sara M. Klennert, Kristin C. Cole, Karen S. Schaepe, Laura J. Ongie, Megan E. Grudem, Carolyn M. Klampe, Myra J. Wick, Vonda M. Wall, Sanjna Rajput, Clarissa L. Polen-De, Amanika Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•High rates of somatic testing for patients with negative germline testing can be accomplished using a stepwise approach.•Referral for germline testing followed by somatic testing in EOC patients helps ensure completion of germline testing.•Patients with advanced stage EOC lack knowledge and understanding of their completed germline and somatic tests.

Introduction

Maintenance chemotherapy with a poly adenosine diphosphate-ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitor in the upfront setting for patients with advanced epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) has provided optimism in gynecologic oncology practices worldwide. Originally studied for treatment of recurrent ovarian cancer, PARP inhibitors were first approved in 2014 (Kim et al., 2015).

SOLO1, a randomized multicenter trial, demonstrated that patients with stage III or IV high grade serous or endometrioid ovarian cancer with a germline or somatic BRCA mutation had a 70 % lower risk of disease progression or death with use of olaparib following first-line chemotherapy (HR 0.30, 95 % CI, P < 0.001) (Moore et al., 2018). In December 2018, olaparib became the first FDA approved PARP inhibitor for first-line maintenance therapy in advanced stage ovarian cancer for patients with a germline or somatic BRCA mutation (Arora et al., 2021). Subsequent trials (VELIA, PRIMA, PAOLA-1) have corroborated the benefit of upfront maintenance treatment but also confirm that the greatest benefit is in those with BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutations, or homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), with less benefit in patients that have homologous recombination proficient (HRP) tumors (Coleman et al., 2019, Ray-Coquard et al., 2019, Gonzalez-Martin et al., 2019). Together, these trials reinforce that knowledge of HRD status is valuable prognostic information for patients and can be used in decision making for maintenance therapy.

We previously reported on a quality improvement initiative in which we increased germline genetic testing rates for this patient population from 48.1 % to 74.2 % (Swanson et al., 2018). However, new systems are required to develop processes for universal somatic testing, particularly in light of systemic treatment advances and the variable benefit depending on tumor mutational status.

Here we describe a quality improvement project aimed at increasing the number of patients with newly diagnosed germline BRCA mutation negative, advanced stage EOC who receive recommendations for somatic testing before completion of first-line chemotherapy. In parallel to the quality improvement project, we conducted a qualitative research study designed to understand patients’ experience and comprehension regarding germline and somatic testing obtained for their EOC cancer using patient interviews.

Methods

Study setting

Mayo Clinic Rochester is a tertiary referral center where patients with newly diagnosed ovarian cancer may be seen in the Division of Gynecologic Oncology Surgery and/or the Department of Medical Oncology. Patients come from a large geographical region and the majority have a local community provider co-managing oncology care. All patients in this study underwent surgery with a surgeon in the Division of Gynecologic Oncology Surgery. Patients may have sought chemotherapy and genetic counseling elsewhere in the Mayo Clinic system or at an outside institution.

Project design

A collaborative interdisciplinary group sought to increase somatic testing recommendations in patients with newly diagnosed germline BRCA negative EOC undergoing surgery in our institution using the DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyze, Improve, Control) quality improvement framework. Team members included individuals from the Department of Clinical Genomics, Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Department of Medical Oncology, Department of Laboratory Genetics and Genomics, and the Department of Oncology Research. Given that patients may have had portion of their genetic testing elsewhere, we did not use completion of somatic testing as the primary measure of improvement, but rather the documented recommendation for testing. A goal was set to increase the proportion of patients with advanced stage (III or IV) disease receiving somatic testing recommendations from a baseline of 53.5 % to 75 % in our practice.

We identified patients who participated in semi-structured phone interviews exploring their experience with both germline and somatic testing for ovarian cancer and their understanding of the difference in testing, why it was performed, and the relevance to their care (Supplement 1).

Project implementation

The project team utilized brainstorming, a fishbone exercise and current-state process mapping to identify potential causes contributing to the gap in somatic testing recommendations (Supplement 2). Contributing factors included inconsistent processes, ineffective workflows with multiple hand-offs, and reliance on manual steps. Stakeholders then systematically evaluated options for somatic testing including test reliability, assay specifications and reporting, ordering logistics, and potential cost to the patients.

A streamlined process for all patients with newly diagnosed EOC was previously implemented to ensure they would receive recommendations for genetic counseling for germline genetic testing. A genetics consultation was ordered to coordinate with a patient’s post-operative examination at approximately 6 weeks after surgery. With this process, we previously reported that 74.2 % of patients were referred to genetic counseling (Swanson et al., 2018). It was felt that maintenance of this genetic testing pathway was imperative, and that decline in use of this successful process would potentially harm patients. Therefore, this previously implemented pathway served as the counter-balance measure in this quality improvement project.

Changes were made to include somatic testing recommendations in written format in the discharge paperwork for patients that completed surgery (Supplement 3). At their post-operative examination, patients were provided with newly developed educational material highlighting the differences between germline and somatic testing (Supplement 4). The provider would advise the patient that if their germline genetic testing did not reveal a BRCA1 or BRCA2 pathogenic mutation then somatic testing on their surgical specimen would be recommended. Somatic testing was subsequently ordered by the surgical team if the patient’s germline results returned negative for BRCA1/2. If a patient elected not to pursue germline testing, then somatic testing of the surgical specimen was recommended at the post-operative examination and ordered at that visit.

Study cohorts and data collection

Two cohorts of patients with newly diagnosed ovarian cancer who had undergone surgery with a gynecologic oncologist at Mayo Clinic Rochester were assembled: a historic cohort with surgery dates between January 1, 2019 through June 30, 2019 and an implementation cohort with surgery dates between October 1, 2020 through March 31, 2021. Pathology reports were reviewed to exclude patients with borderline ovarian tumors and non-epithelial ovarian cancer. The following data were manually abstracted from each patient’s medical record: patient age at ovarian cancer diagnosis, stage, receipt of neoadjuvant chemotherapy, referral status to genetics for germline testing, germline testing results, discussion of somatic testing and somatic testing results.

All stages were included in our overall analysis for rates of germline testing referral and mutational status. We confined somatic testing to those with stage III-IV EOC, comparing the percentage of patients without a germline BRCA mutation who received recommendations for somatic testing prior to completing chemotherapy, with the primary goal to achieve >=75 % somatic testing recommendations in this group. Stage I-II EOC patients were excluded for somatic testing recommendations based on treatment guidelines with PARP inhibitor maintenance for advanced stage EOC only at the time of project conception. Patients with a pathogenic germline BRCA1 or BRCA2 mutation were excluded from the analysis in both cohorts as somatic testing was not thought to be necessary for treatment decisions for these individuals. Patients with pathogenic germline mutations in other genes such as RAD51C, BRIP1, MSH6, etc, as well as those with a germline variant of unknown significance in any gene, were included in the analysis for both cohorts as guidelines at the time of the study were for PARP inhibitor maintenance for germline or somatic BRCA1/2 patients only.

Differences in medical chart documentation existed between the historic and intervention cohorts for somatic testing “recommendations” as the project implemented standardized content for recommendations in the intervention cohort which did not exist in the historic cohort. For this reason, we included all patients that had scanned in somatic testing results pertaining to their EOC as having been “recommended” to have somatic testing even if there was no dialogue found in a clinical note with this recommendation.

We conducted semi-structured telephone interviews with twenty-three patients from the historic cohort to explore their experiences with germline and somatic genetic testing for ovarian cancer. The interviews focused on participants’ understanding of the distinctions between the two types of testing, their perceived purpose, and the relevance of testing results to their care. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed by an independent qualitative researcher using a two-stage approach. The initial phase employed deductive content analysis to capture the breadth of participant responses in relation to the interview guide. Building on this foundation, a second stage of thematic analysis was conducted to identify emergent patterns and provide a more nuanced account of how patients made sense of genetic testing and its implications for their treatment and family context.

Statistical analyses

Comparisons between the two cohorts in the quality improvement project were evaluated using the two-sample t-test for age, the Wilcoxon rank sum test for stage and the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for each of the dichotomous variables and process measures. All calculated p-values were two-sided and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using the SAS version 9.4 software package.

Somatic

Of the 43 patients in the historic cohort with stage III-IV ovarian cancer without a germline BRCA mutation, 53.5 % (23/43) received recommendations for somatic testing and 100 % (23/23) of these patients completed somatic testing. Fourteen of these patients (14/23, 60.9 %) were found to have a somatic mutation or HRD. Of the 26 patients in the intervention cohort with stage III-IV EOC without a germline BRCA mutation, 84.6 % (22/26) received recommendations for somatic testing. Somatic testing was completed in 18 of the 22 patients who received recommendations for testing at the time of this analysis with 27.8 % (5/18) having been found to have a somatic mutation or HRD. Qualitative patient interviews

All twenty-three patients interviewed from the historic cohort had undergone genetic counseling, germline testing, and somatic testing. While nearly all participants (22/23) recalled some discussion of genetic testing in relation to their ovarian cancer, twenty reported meeting with a genetics provider. Most patients (22/23) expressed a general understanding that genetic testing had implications for both their treatment and their family members. However, two-thirds (15/23) demonstrated limited understanding of the types of genetic testing available for ovarian cancer, and only four participants were aware of both germline and somatic testing as distinct components of care. When asked specifically, only two patients clearly stated that they had undergone genetic testing; six believed they had not, and the majority (15) were uncertain about whether testing had been performed.

Discussion

Our multi-disciplinary quality improvement team sought to increase somatic testing recommendations for germline BRCA negative patients with a new diagnosis of stage III-IV EOC by exploring ways to standardize recommendations and to improve the testing process at our institution. We were able to increase somatic testing recommendations in these patients from 53.5 % at baseline to 84.6 % after executing changes in our workflow using quality improvement processes.

Stepwise testing

A stepwise testing process for germline and somatic testing was felt to be valuable in our practice. First, it allows all patients with EOC to meet one-on-one with a certified genetic counselor (CGC) where family history is reviewed, and the most appropriate germline testing is ordered based on personal and family history. The Society of Gynecologic Oncology, National Comprehensive Cancer Network, American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) recommend germline testing for all EOC patients and comprehensive germline testing continues to be vital for these patients and their family members to fully understand their possible cancer risks (Lancaster et al., 2015, Daly et al., 2020, Konstantinopoulos et al., 2020, Hereditary Cancer Syndromes and Risk Assessment, 2019). Secondly, we wanted to make sure that patients understood that negative somatic testing does not eliminate the need for germline genetic testing. Germline testing analyzes genes related to hereditary disease which may not be included or may be analyzed differently on somatic testing. For this reason, we utilized referral rates to CGC in the historic and intervention cohorts to make sure there was no decrease in referral rates following the implementation of somatic testing recommendations as our counterbalance measure. Our referral rate to genetics remained unchanged between the cohorts (90 % historic vs 96.3 % implementation, P = 0.30).

While parallel testing of germline and somatic could be performed, we felt that it would not be cost-effective for those patients found to have a germline BRCA mutation and would increase unnecessary work for the ordering provider. Approximately one-fifth of EOC patients have a pathogenic germline BRCA mutation and are recommended for PARP inhibitor maintenance treatment; thus, somatic testing is unlikely to alter their clinical plan or counseling in the upfront setting.

The downside to this stepwise approach is the additional time it takes to get somatic results for the patient and their oncology provider. By waiting for the germline results to be available and the results to be communicated to the surgical team who orders the somatic test, 3–4 additional weeks may have passed before somatic testing is ordered. In most situations this did not alter treatment plans as patients are still completing their primary chemotherapy treatment and are not ready for maintenance treatment. However, patients may be eager to know their treatment course early on and whether maintenance PARP inhibitors will be part of their treatment plan. The delayed timeframe of receiving somatic testing results may be of more importance in those treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy followed by surgery. In these situations, the window from surgery to maintenance PARP inhibitor is shorter than those with upfront surgery, so obtaining this information in a timely fashion is important.

ASCO reviewed the evidence of germline and somatic testing in EOC and provided a consensus guideline for testing recommendations in 2020. This guideline stated that all women with EOC should have germline genetic testing and only those without a pathogenic germline BRCA mutation, should then undergo somatic testing for BRCA1/2 (Konstantinopoulos et al., 2020). This approach aligns with what our process implemented.

If the stepwise approach were reversed and somatic testing was finalized before requesting genetic counseling referral, a subset of patients may not receive the recommendations for germline testing. A multicenter, observational cohort study in the Netherlands performed somatic testing in all eligible patients with EOC. In this study, 88.2 % of patients that were identified as having a pathogenic somatic mutation went on to germline testing. Hence, 12 % of patients at risk of having a germline mutation had a missed opportunity to receive counseling and guidance on further testing. Only 13 % of those without a somatic BRCA mutation were referred to genetic counseling and testing (Vos et al., 2020). An additional concern with the somatic testing first approach is that up to 5 % of germline mutations will be missed, likely due to the inability to detect large genomic rearrangements (Ledermann et al., 2016, Ledermann et al., 2014, Ellison et al., 2018). This is a missed opportunity for patients and families to be made aware of their cancer risks and patients may not be recommended to receive PARP inhibitor maintenance.

Homologous recombination testing

Myriad myChoice CDx® was ordered for the majority of EOC patients receiving somatic testing in our implementation cohort. At the onset of our project, we surveyed internal Mayo oncology providers about what information they wanted to be provided with in the somatic testing results. They requested homologous recombination (HR) information as they thought this would help guide treatment decisions. If an oncology provider preferred somatic testing using a different company such as Foundation One or Tempus this would be completed upon request. At the time of this project, Myriad myChoice CDx ® and Foundation One® provided information on somatic testing and some format of HR. Myriad provided a genomic instability score which is a combination of loss of heterozygosity (LOH), telomeric allelic imbalance, and large-scale state transitions. Foundation One® results provide information on LOH, tumor mutational burden and microsatellite instability. Tempus® tumor testing did not provide HR information at that time but did provide tumor mutational burden and microsatellite instability.

Our practice of ordering HR with somatic testing was implemented before ASCO published guidelines pertaining to HR testing. In 2020, the consensus guideline stated that no recommendations could be made for performing currently available HRD testing assays in patients with EOC (Konstantinopoulos et al., 2020). Our practice has not changed to suspend obtaining HR information for EOC patients as this information is already provided with somatic information without increased costs. We continue to feel that this information is useful in treatment decisions as oncology providers make patients aware of the difference in response to PARP inhibitor maintenance treatment with HRD and HRP tumors.

Limitations

Several limitations of our project should be noted. First, as a tertiary cancer center, we occasionally lose patients to follow-up if they do not return for oncology care after surgery for EOC. As a result, we may lack complete data on whether these patients pursued germline or somatic testing. Second, our institution has a dedicated medical genetics practice, with geneticists and certified genetic counselors embedded in a high-volume breast and ovarian cancer clinic. This allows many patients to be seen on the same day as their postoperative visit, likely contributing to our high rate of completed germline testing for EOC patients (79.3–100 %) (Swanson et al., 2018). However, this model may not be generalizable, as access to timely genetic counseling appointments remains limited at both academic and non-academic institutions. Third, we do not have complete data on the use of PARP inhibitors in the primary treatment setting for EOC patients in our cohort. Lastly, we lack information on out-of-pocket costs for somatic testing. Although we provided letters of medical necessity to insurers that initially denied coverage, we do not have definitive data on whether testing was ultimately reimbursed. We assume most testing was covered, as patients did not report outstanding medical bills, but this remains an area of uncertainty.

Limited funding for the qualitative research component of this project only allowed interviews to be conducted for patients in the historic cohort. Thus, any measurable improvement in patients’ understanding of their genetic testing following standardized documentation and patient education material remains unknown.

Conclusion

We successfully implemented somatic testing recommendations for germline BRCA negative patients with advanced EOC prior to the completion of upfront treatment, enabling timely and personalized discussions about maintenance PARP inhibitor use. There were notable limitations to increasing somatic testing recommendation rates, specifically in those patients who did not return for post-operative care, but we nonetheless achieved our goal of increasing testing recommendations for newly diagnosed patients.

Our qualitative findings reveal a substantial gap in patient understanding of genetic testing, highlighting the need for improved communication strategies to support truly informed, patient-centered care. Utilization of genetic testing educational resources such as those developed during this project for patients to review after their visit and to be available to share with their family members is a simple strategy for other oncology centers to consider enhancing patients’ genetic testing knowledge.
