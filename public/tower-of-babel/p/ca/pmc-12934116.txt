Triggers, prodrome, aura, and headache interactions in migraine with typical aura: a prospective deep-phenotyping study
Authors: Srdjan Mavija, Aleksandra Radojičić, Igor Petrušić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsThis study aimed to prospectively and comprehensively phenotype migraine with typical aura (MwA) attacks, with a particular focus on the interaction between trigger factors and clinical features of the prodrome, aura, and headache phases. A secondary aim was to examine how characteristics of individual phases influence the subsequent course of an attack and to provide data-driven hypotheses relevant for future MwA classification.MethodologyThis was a prospective, multicenter observational study conducted at two tertiary headache clinics. Participants recorded up to three MwA attacks over 12 months using an electronic questionnaire completed within one day after each attack. Data on potential triggers, prodromal symptoms, detailed aura features (visual, somatosensory, and higher cortical dysfunctions), and headache characteristics were collected. Associations between attack phases, including trigger factors, were analyzed.ResultsA total of 90 participants (74% female) reported 144 MwA attacks. Triggers were reported in 82% of attacks, with lack of sleep (36%) and the menstrual cycle (24%) being the most frequent. Prodromal symptoms occurred in 75% of attacks, most commonly neck stiffness (29%) and photophobia (28%). The number of triggers positively correlated with the number of prodromal symptoms (r=0.372, p<0.001). Visual aura occurred in 98% of attacks, with 43% showing sudden onset and 31% presenting as monocular visual disturbances. Somatosensory symptoms were reported in 50% of attacks, and dysphasic symptoms in 40%. The aura preceded headache in 81% of attacks. Severe headache intensity was reported in 62% of cases, while exhaustion occurred in 81%. The presence of tingling, numbness, and dyspraxia during the aura phase was significantly associated with the development of cutaneous allodynia.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that MwA attacks represent a continuous and complex neurobiological process rather than a sequence of isolated phases, in which triggers and prodromal symptoms shape aura complexity, headache severity, and central sensitization. The frequent occurrence of atypical aura durations, sudden onset, and monocular-like visual disturbances in a prospective setting indicates that current MwA diagnostic frameworks may be overly restrictive. A more inclusive classification approach may be required to better capture the clinical heterogeneity of MwA and reduce underdiagnosis or misclassification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02309-y.

Introduction

Migraine with typical aura (MwA) is a highly heterogeneous, multisensory, and multifactorial brain disorder [1, 2], characterized by substantial variability in prodromal symptoms, aura manifestations, and headache features [1]. Individuals with MwA experience reversible neurological disturbances, including visual, somatosensory, speech, and other higher cortical dysfunctions, typically lasting 5–60 min and either preceding or accompanying headache pain [3]. In addition, most individuals with MwA also experience migraine without aura during their lifetime [3]. This clinical heterogeneity reflects an increased susceptibility of the migraine brain to both internal and external triggers, such as emotional stress, fasting, alcohol intake, sleep deprivation, physical exertion, hormonal fluctuations, weather changes, bright light, and strong odors [4, 5]. Although numerous studies have examined migraine triggers [4–7], there remains limited prospective evidence on how these triggers influence the prodromal phase and subsequently shape the characteristics of the aura and headache phases in MwA attacks.

The migraine prodrome phase is defined by the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3), as a symptomatic, non-painful period occurring up to 48 h before headache onset [3] and is reported in up to 80% of patients [8]. Commonly described prodromal symptoms include nausea, fatigue, hypersensitivity to light, neck pain or stiffness, and dizziness or light-headedness [9, 10]. However, data on the frequency and clinical relevance of prodromal symptoms specifically in MwA attacks remain scarce.

Aura manifestations in MwA are particularly diverse. Visual symptoms may include both positive and negative phenomena, ranging from simple flashes of light to complex perceptual disturbances, such as distortions in size, shape, or color perception [11]. Similarly, somatosensory symptoms may be positive (e.g., tingling) and/or negative (e.g., numbness), often producing unilateral sensations involving the hand, face, or other body regions [3]. Dysphasia and other higher cortical dysfunctions, including memory disturbances and dyspraxia, although less frequently reported, are not uncommon during the aura phase [12] and may be associated with cognitive dysfunction [13] and structural brain alterations [14, 15]. Together, these features underscore the marked clinical heterogeneity of MwA and highlight the need for a comprehensive approach to aura characterization to improve disease understanding and diagnostic accuracy [16, 17]. Furthermore, the dynamic interplay between different phases of MwA attacks, including prodrome, aura, headache, and postdrome phases, remains insufficiently explored. In particular, the relationship between aura complexity and headache-associated symptoms, such as photophobia, phonophobia, cutaneous allodynia, and nausea, is poorly understood.

Herein, we report a prospective, two-center study aimed at deeply characterizing MwA attacks and their associated triggers, and at exploring how features of individual attack phases influence the subsequent clinical course of an MwA attack.

Methodology

This was a prospective, two-center observational study conducted at two tertiary headache outpatient clinics affiliated with university hospitals in Belgrade (Serbia) and Banja Luka (Bosnia and Herzegovina). The study population consisted of adult patients diagnosed with migraine with typical aura (MwA) according to the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [3].

Participants

Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) episodic migraine with low attack frequency [18]; (2) absence of migraine preventive therapy for at least one year prior to study enrollment; (3) at least two MwA attacks in the year preceding inclusion; (4) age between 18 and 55 years; and (5) absence of structural brain pathology on magnetic resonance imaging. Exclusion criteria included: (1) presence of other primary headache disorders (except occasional migraine without aura or tension-type headache); (2) any neurological, cardiovascular, or metabolic disorder identified through medical history or clinical examination; and (3) inability to independently complete an electronic questionnaire after structured instruction by a neurologist experienced in migraine research. Participants were additionally excluded if they failed to complete the questionnaire within one day of an MwA attack or if no MwA attacks occurred during the 12-month observational period.

The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committees of both participating centers and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants received a detailed explanation of the study procedures and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Data collection

Participants were instructed to complete a structured electronic questionnaire, accessible via smartphone or computer, after each MwA attack during a 12-month follow-up period. Data were collected for up to three MwA attacks per participant. This limit was chosen to enhance compliance, increase the number of included participants, and reduce potential bias related to overrepresentation of individuals with more frequent attacks.

Trigger factors

For each MwA attack, participants reported the presence or absence of predefined trigger factors, including: emotional stress, specific foods (e.g., chocolate, cheese), increased physical exertion, alcohol intake, fasting, dehydration, lack of sleep, exposure to bright light, menstrual or perimenstrual period, weather changes, and exposure to strong odors. The total number of triggers per attack was also recorded.

Prodrome phase

Prodromal symptoms were recorded as present or absent. They included: neck stiffness, decreased concentration, sleepiness, blurry vision, fatigue, nausea, yawning, photophobia, phonophobia, osmophobia, dizziness/vertigo/light-headedness, increased appetite, decreased appetite, thirst, insomnia, depressive mood, anxiety, and euphoric mood. The total number of prodromal symptoms per attack was calculated. Participants also reported whether they subjectively perceived a feeling of an impending attack.

Aura phase

Variables collected during the aura phase included duration of visual and somatosensory aura (in minutes), onset of visual symptoms (sudden or gradual), extent of visual field involvement (quarter, half, or more than half), starting location within the visual field, and laterality of visual symptoms (both eyes or one eye). To assess ocular involvement, participants were instructed to alternately close each eye and report symptom persistence. Additional variables included the type and color of visual symptoms [11, 12, 17], body regions and sides affected by somatosensory symptoms, presence of higher cortical dysfunctions (e.g., dyspraxia), and dysphasia or memory impairment [12]. Hypersensitivity to light, sound, and odors, as well as nausea and mood changes during the aura phase, were also recorded.

Headache phase

Headache-related variables included the temporal relationship between aura and headache, headache intensity (mild, moderate, severe), laterality, pain localization (forehead, temporal, parietal, occipital), pain quality (dull, stabbing, throbbing), and headache duration with or without acute medication (< 4 h, 4–72 h, > 72 h). Associated symptoms, including nausea, exhaustion, and cutaneous allodynia, were recorded. Use of acute medication (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or triptans) during the attack was also documented.

Participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire as soon as possible after each MwA attack and no later than 24 h after onset to minimize recall bias. Information on headache duration and postdrome exhaustion was collected after resolution of the headache phase. Participants were contacted every three months to ensure compliance and monitor attack occurrence.

Statistical analysis

Demographic data, trigger factors, and attack characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Associations between trigger factors, prodromal symptoms, aura features, and headache characteristics were assessed using Spearman’s rho coefficient for continuous variables and χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, as appropriate. For categorical comparisons, effect sizes were quantified using Cramér’s V coefficient. To account for multiple comparisons, results were corrected using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedure. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 21.0).

Results

Study population

Nine participants did not experience an MwA attack during the one-year follow-up and were therefore excluded from the analysis. In total, 90 participants (52 from Belgrade and 38 from Banja Luka) were included, collectively reporting 144 MwA attacks. Of these participants, 15 reported three MwA attacks, 22 reported two attacks, and 53 reported one attack. The cohort consisted of 67 (74%) women and 23 (26%) men, with a mean age of 37.2 ± 7.7 years (range 20–52 years). Coexisting migraine without aura was reported by 56 (62%) participants. Among all recorded MwA attacks, 59 (41%) occurred in the morning, 52 (36%) during the day, and 33 (23%) in the evening.

Characterization of MwA triggers

In 26 (18%) MwA attacks, no trigger was reported. A single trigger was reported in 48 (33%) attacks, two triggers in 32 (22%), three triggers in 22 (15%), four triggers in 10 (7%), and more than four triggers in 6 (4%) attacks. The most frequently reported trigger was lack of sleep (n = 52, 36%), followed by menstrual or perimenstrual period (n = 26, 24%; adjusted for sex), emotional stress (n = 32, 22%), and weather changes (n = 30, 21%) (Fig. 1).

Characterization of the prodrome phase

Prodromal symptoms were absent in 36 (25%) MwA attacks. One prodromal symptom was reported in 21 (15%) attacks, two in 19 (13%), three in 22 (15%), four in 19 (13%), and more than four in 27 (19%) attacks. The most frequently reported prodromal symptoms were neck stiffness (n = 42, 29%), hypersensitivity to light (n = 40, 28%), decreased concentration (n = 32, 22%), sleepiness (n = 31, 21%), and blurry vision (n = 29, 20%) (Fig. 2). A subjective feeling of an impending attack was reported in 32 (22%) MwA attacks.

Deep profiling of aura symptoms

Visual aura was reported in 98% of MwA attacks (Table 1). Visual aura duration was less than 5 min in 5% of attacks and longer than 60 min in 10%. Visual symptoms had a sudden onset in 43% of attacks and most commonly involved half of the visual field (51%). The most frequent starting location was the peripheral right visual field (40%). Visual symptoms were reported as affecting one eye in 44 (31%) attacks and both eyes in 97 (69%). The most commonly reported visual phenomena were bright zigzag lines (47%), phosphenes (45%), blurred scotoma with a glowing rim (43%), and sensations described as “looking through heat waves or flickering air” (43%). Visual symptoms were polychromatic in 75% of attacks, with blurred/grey (77%) and white/bright (65%) being the most frequently reported colors.

Somatosensory aura occurred in 50% of MwA attacks (Table 2). Somatosensory symptoms lasted less than 5 min in 28% and longer than 60 min in 14% of attacks. Tingling was present in all somatosensory auras and most commonly involved the hand (83%), face/head (58%), tongue (37%), and leg (19%). Numbness accompanied tingling in 74% of cases. In addition, dyspraxia was reported in 44% of MwA attacks accompanied by somatosensory aura.

Dysphasic symptoms were reported in 58 (40%) MwA attacks and were characterized by paraphasic errors in all cases. Gibberish speech was reported in 28%, dysnomia in 27%, memory recall difficulties in 16%, problems understanding others in 10%, and remembering difficulties in 7% of attacks.

Mood changes were reported during the aura phase in 66 (46%) MwA attacks. Hypersensitivity to light (78%), sound (56%), and odors (34%), as well as nausea (46%), were also frequently reported during the aura phase.

Characterization of the headache phase

The aura phase preceded headache onset in 116 (81%) MwA attacks (Table 3). In 12 (8%) attacks, aura was not followed by headache. Headache intensity was reported as severe in 62% of attacks, and pain was unilateral in 71%. Pain was most commonly localized to the temporal (58%) and frontal (53%) regions. The most frequently reported pain quality was dull (51%), followed by stabbing (40%) and throbbing (38%). Headache duration was less than 4 h in 74 (56%) attacks, 4–72 h in 50 (38%), and longer than 72 h in 8 (6%) attacks. Photophobia during the headache phase was present in 115 (85%), while phonophobia was reported in 89 (67%) attacks. Nausea was present during the headache phase in 82 (62%) attacks, while exhaustion during the headache and postdrome phases was reported in 107 (81%) attacks. Cutaneous allodynia occurred in 20 (14%) MwA attacks.

Acute medication was not used in 31 (21%) attacks. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs were used in 80 (56%) attacks, and triptans in 33 (23%) (Table 3).

Associations between demographics, triggers, prodrome symptoms, aura and headache characteristics

No significant associations were found between demographic variables and triggers, prodromal symptoms, aura features, or headache characteristics (Supplementary Table 1). The number of reported triggers positively correlated with the number of prodromal symptoms (Table 4). Increased physical activity was associated with fatigue during the prodrome phase, while lack of sleep correlated with a higher number of prodromal symptoms. Exposure to strong odors was associated with hypersensitivity to odors and dizziness/vertigo/light-headedness during the prodrome phase, as well as with micropsia/macropsia and tingling sensations in the face during the aura phase. No other associations between triggers and MwA characteristics were significant (Supplementary Table 2).

The number of prodromal symptoms positively correlated with memory problems and mood changes during the aura phase (Table 4). Dizziness/vertigo/light-headedness was positively associated with micropsia/macropsia. Decreased concentration was positively associated with memory problems and mood changes during the aura phase. Hypersensitivity to odors and nausea during the prodrome phase were positively associated with corresponding symptoms during the aura phase, and nausea during the prodrome and aura phases were positively associated with nausea during the headache phase. Fatigue during the prodrome phase was positively associated with the presence of cutaneous allodynia during the headache phase. No other associations between prodromal symptoms and MwA characteristics were significant (Supplementary Table 3).

Cutaneous allodynia during the headache phase was positively associated with tingling in the hand, numbness, dyspraxia, and difficulties understanding others during the aura phase (Table 4). Tingling on the face/head was positively associated with stabbing headache quality, while nausea during the aura phase was positively associated with nausea during the headache phase. Hypersensitivity to light during the aura was positively associated with photophobia during the headache phase. Hypersensitivity to sound during the aura was positively associated with both photophobia and phonophobia during the headache phase. No other associations between aura features and headache characteristics were significant (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

This study provides a detailed prospective deep profiling of MwA attacks, characterizing the complex interplay between triggers, prodromal symptoms, aura manifestations, and the subsequent headache and postdrome phases. By employing a prospective design, this study captures near–real-time data and overcomes key limitations of retrospective studies, thereby providing evidence that may inform future refinements of the ICHD-3 classification.

Research on migraine triggers is inherently challenging due to substantial interindividual variability and the complex interaction between multiple internal and external factors [19]. This complexity is further amplified by migraine heterogeneity, including the coexistence of migraine with and without aura, episodic versus chronic forms, medication use, and comorbidities. Multicenter prospective studies focusing on a well-defined subgroup, such as individuals with MwA, offer an opportunity to characterize the trigger spectrum within this population more accurately. Moreover, investigating the relationship between trigger burden and attack complexity may yield insights into MwA pathophysiology.

In the present study, the most frequently reported MwA triggers were lack of sleep, menstrual cycle, emotional stress, and weather changes, consistent with prior clinic-based retrospective studies identifying sleep disturbance and hormonal fluctuations as dominant precipitants in both migraine without aura and MwA [4, 5, 19–21]. These findings also corroborate earlier work aimed at identifying consistent triggers in migraine with aura [22]. However, whether MwA and migraine without aura differ in their trigger profiles, prodromal manifestations, and headache characteristics remains uncertain. In the absence of standardized, large-scale prospective datasets directly comparing MwA and migraine without aura, it is difficult to determine whether observed differences reflect true biological distinctions or methodological variability. Accordingly, our findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using harmonized phenotyping and prospective designs are needed to clarify potential subtype-specific patterns. Importantly, we observed a positive correlation between the number of reported triggers and the number of prodromal symptoms, suggesting that exposure to multiple stressors may reflect increased brain susceptibility and result in a more complex prodromal presentation. This observation is reinforced by the fact that most MwA attacks were associated with more than one trigger. These findings align with the concept of migraine attack susceptibility, whereby internal threshold-modulating factors interact with external, potentially modifiable triggers to provoke an attack once a critical threshold is exceeded [21]. From a clinical perspective, identifying and minimizing highly frequent triggers may influence lifestyle adaptations and improve treatment outcomes [4]. Notably, metabolic and behavioral risk factors represent promising targets for large-scale public health interventions [23].

Prodromal symptoms were present in 75% of MwA attacks, most commonly neck stiffness and hypersensitivity to light. Although previous studies have reported prodrome frequencies ranging from 30% to 80% [8], the prospective nature of our data strengthens the evidence that prodromal symptoms are common and clinically relevant. Notably, only 22% of attacks were accompanied by subjective awareness of an impending attack, indicating that prodromal symptoms are often present but not consciously recognized as warning signs. This highlights the potential role of personalized digital tools, such as wearable biosensors, to detect physiological changes during the prodrome phase before subjective symptom recognition [24]. Early detection could enable timely administration of acute treatment prior to aura or headache onset, potentially preventing or attenuating the attack [25]. Emerging evidence suggests that treatment during the prodrome phase may be effective in selected patients [26], and prodromal symptoms themselves may constitute an independent therapeutic target [10]. Our findings further suggest that a higher number of prodromal symptoms is associated with greater aura complexity and increased headache severity, supporting the rationale for early intervention aimed at modulating multiple pathophysiological mechanisms across MwA phases.

Visual aura remained the hallmark of MwA, occurring in 98% of attacks, consistent with previous studies [11, 16, 27]. Bright zigzag lines, phosphenes, and scintillating scotoma were the most frequently reported visual phenomena, in line with earlier detailed characterizations of visual aura [11, 28]. Although most visual auras lasted 15–30 min, 10% persisted longer than 60 min, supporting growing evidence that prolonged aura is more common than previously assumed and may warrant consideration in future ICHD-3 revisions [11]. Notably, 31% of visual auras were reported as affecting only one eye. While this may suggest retinal involvement, similar rates of co-occurring somatosensory or dysphasic symptoms regardless of monocular or binocular visual symptoms point to overlapping mechanisms between MwA subtypes. Previous studies have reported monocular symptoms in up to 25% of MwA attacks [29], highlighting diagnostic challenges given that ICHD-3 criteria emphasize bilateral homonymous visual field involvement [3]. Additionally, 43% of visual auras were described as having a sudden onset, deviating from the criterion of gradual symptom spread required for a “typical aura” [3]. However, a gradual onset of visual symptoms was also reported in approximately two-thirds of MwA attacks in previous studies [27]. These observations underscore the need for further multimodal investigations to refine MwA classification.

Somatosensory aura was present in 50% of MwA attacks, most commonly involving tingling and numbness of the hand and face, consistent with prior reports [27, 30]. The high prevalence of dyspraxia during somatosensory aura highlights that higher cortical dysfunctions are likely underestimated in standard clinical assessments [12]. Dysphasic symptoms occurred in 40% of attacks and were characterized primarily by paraphasic errors, gibberish speech, and dysnomia, corroborating earlier findings [14, 17]. Mood changes, sensory hypersensitivities, and nausea were also common during the aura phase, supporting a continuum of symptoms across attack phases. The observed associations between nausea in the prodrome, aura, and headache phases suggest shared pathophysiological mechanisms, potentially involving hypothalamic dysfunction [31]. Structural and functional alterations in subcortical brain regions in MwA further support the complexity of prodromal and aura phenotypes and their shared neural substrates [32–34]. Moreover, the association between complex aura symptoms and headache severity or cutaneous allodynia suggests that the extent or intensity of cortical spreading depression may influence subsequent central sensitization [2, 35].

The temporal relationship between aura and headache observed in this study aligns with findings from large registries [16, 27], with aura preceding headache in most attacks. Headaches following aura were frequently severe and accompanied by pronounced exhaustion during the headache and postdrome phases, consistent with registry data indicating increased pain severity when headaches are preceded by aura [16]. Although most MwA attacks lasted less than four hours, this was often achieved through acute treatment. The moderate reliance on acute medication and the high prevalence of postdromal exhaustion suggest that current management strategies may insufficiently address the full burden of MwA attacks beyond pain relief [36].

Key strengths of this study include its prospective design, well-defined MwA cohort, and recruitment by headache specialists from two independent tertiary centers. Additionally, participants did not have comorbidities and were not receiving preventive migraine therapy, reducing potential confounding effects [37]. Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The self-reported nature of data collection may be subject to recall bias or underreporting of brief or atypical symptoms, although training for understanding the questionnaire and the short time allowed to complete the questionnaire after the MwA attack likely mitigated this risk. Moreover, although participants were followed prospectively and they received clear instructions and were reminded after every clinical visit, some MwA attacks may not have been reported. Consequently, underreporting of MwA attacks during the observation period cannot be excluded and should be considered when interpreting attack frequency and symptom patterns. In addition, a potential limitation is that visual symptoms occurring very laterally within the homonymous visual fields may be perceived and reported as monocular, despite instructions to alternately close each eye, introducing the possibility of misclassification of symptom laterality. Furthermore, only commonly reported triggers were assessed, and the overlap between triggers and premonitory symptoms remains a recognized challenge in migraine research [38]. Therefore, some exposures reported as triggers may in fact represent the first symptoms of the prodrome phase. For example, the observed association between the reported trigger “exposure to strong odors” and the prodromal symptom “hypersensitivity to odors” suggests that, in certain cases, the perceived trigger may reflect an already evolving attack rather than an external precipitating factor. Our findings should therefore be interpreted with this overlap in mind. Similarly, vertigo, dizziness, and light-headedness were grouped together during analysis; though these symptoms overlap in patient descriptions, clinicians view them as mechanistically and symptomatically distinct [39]. Either way, further patient education and recognition of the MwA heterogeneity and the systematic recording of triggers and prodromal symptoms are likely to improve our understanding of the MwA [40]. Moreover, the design of this study was not focused on collecting everyday triggers and in future studies, MwA patients who have more than one MwA attack per month could be included in studies that are more oriented towards an everyday diary and exposure to triggers on a daily basis. This type of study could reveal the most potent triggers of MwA attacks. Finally, the study was limited by the recruitment of patients only from tertiary referral centers, which could lead to selection bias.

Despite these limitations, this study provides novel insights into the relationships between common MwA triggers, prodromal symptoms, and overall attack complexity. Future studies incorporating multimodal approaches [41] and real-time digital phenotyping [24] may further expand our understanding of aura phenomenology and its interaction with other migraine phases. Such a comprehensive approach could inform future research, refine diagnostic criteria, and create new personalized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

This bicenter prospective study provides a comprehensive, data-driven characterization of migraine with typical aura, demonstrating that MwA attacks represent a continuous and highly interconnected neurobiological process rather than a sequence of discrete and independent phases. Internal and external triggers appear to lower the attack threshold and are closely linked to the burden of prodromal symptoms, which in turn modulate the complexity of aura manifestations and the severity of the subsequent headache phase. Moreover, the presence of a greater number of symptoms across phases may reflect higher overall attack burden or severity, although our study was not specifically designed to quantify severity in this manner.

The strong association between the number of reported triggers and prodromal symptoms supports the concept of cumulative susceptibility and highlights the prodrome as a critical window in the evolution of MwA attacks. Importantly, prodromal symptoms were frequent but often not consciously recognized as warning signs, underscoring the need for improved patient education and the potential value of digital phenotyping and early-intervention strategies.

Deep profiling of aura revealed substantial clinical heterogeneity, including frequent somatosensory and dysphasic symptoms, atypical aura durations, sudden onset of visual phenomena, and a notable proportion of monocular-like visual disturbances. These findings challenge the strict boundaries of current ICHD-3 criteria and suggest that typical aura encompasses a broader spectrum of phenotypes than currently acknowledged. Moreover, the observed associations between complex aura features and cutaneous allodynia support a link between aura-related cortical and subcortical dysfunctions and central sensitization during the headache phase.

Overall, the results indicate that MwA should be conceptualized as a dynamic continuum in which triggers, prodrome, aura, headache, and postdrome are tightly interrelated. A more inclusive and multidimensional approach to MwA classification, incorporating detailed aura phenotyping and prodromal characteristics, may improve diagnostic accuracy, reduce misclassification, and inform personalized therapeutic strategies. Future studies integrating longitudinal, multimodal, and real-time data acquisition are warranted to refine MwA subtyping and advance precision medicine in migraine care.
