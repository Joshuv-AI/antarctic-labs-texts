Development and Implementation of an Interprofessional Education Course in Geriatric Care for Dental and Nursing Students
Authors: Marianna Mantzorou, Theodoula Adamakidou, Georgia Gerogianni, Alexandra Koreli, Kalliopi Konstantopoulou, Anastassia Kossioni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThis study aimed to describe the development and implementation of an interprofessional education (IPE) course in caring for older people for dental and nursing students and to investigate its impact on their perceptions of IPE and interprofessional collaborative practice (IPCP).MethodsThe IPE course was delivered to a convenience sample of 87 eighth semester dental and nursing students in a nursing home. A pilot course was first conducted and evaluated at the same location. The final course lasted 5 weeks and included preparatory students' training, followed by team site visits of half day duration. The site visits included a large group introductory session; small group visits to preselected residents' rooms providing interviews, clinical examination and general and oral health promotion counselling; development of comprehensive care plans; and a large group debriefing session. The students' perceptions of IPE and IPCP were assessed using SPICE‐R2. Gibbs' reflective diaries and an anonymous evaluation form were completed in the debriefing session.ResultsAfter training, students' attitudes towards IPE and IPCP were statistically significantly improved in both disciplines (p < 0.005). However, both pre‐test and post‐test scores were significantly higher in nursing students (p < 0.001). The students gave the educational activity a high evaluation, scoring 52.1 out of 60.ConclusionsBoth professions positively evaluated the IPE activity and demonstrated improvement in their IPE and IPCP perceptions. Institutional support and flexible curricula are necessary prerequisites for implementing IPE courses.Keywords: gerodontology, gerontological nursing, integrated care for older people, interprofessional collaborative care, interprofessional education, transversal skills

Introduction

The global population is ageing at an unprecedented rate, presenting complex challenges to healthcare systems worldwide. In Europe, 21.6% of the population was over 65 years in 2024, with the highest rates recorded in Italy (24.3%), Portugal (23.8%), and Bulgaria (23.8%), while the share of individuals over 80 years will reach 15.3% by 2100 [1]. This demographic shift underscores the urgent need for effective, integrated care models tailored to the unique needs of older adults. The WHO Healthy Ageing concept necessitates building an environment supporting the intrinsic capacity and functional ability of older people through a continuum of care across the lifespan. This is achieved with the collaboration of adequately trained healthcare professionals and community workers [2].

Among the most promising educational tools supporting interprofessional collaborative practice (IPCP) is interprofessional education (IPE) in which students from various health professions “learn about, from and with each other” aiming to improve patient outcomes [3]. IPE in older people care is essential because healthcare challenges are often complex, requiring coordinated efforts from diverse disciplines to deliver effective and holistic care [3, 4]. Τhe need for IPCP implementation in geriatric care is further stressed by the limited number of specialists in the field across all healthcare professions [5, 6, 7].

The benefits of IPE are substantial. IPE may enhance healthcare team dynamics, reduce medical errors, and improve patient safety and satisfaction [8]. Additionally, IPE prepares future healthcare professionals to navigate interdisciplinary challenges, ultimately fostering a more integrated and efficient healthcare system.

Implementing IPE involves interactive learning experiences such as case studies, simulation exercises, and collaborative clinical placements, enabling participants to develop skills in communication, teamwork, and role understanding [9, 10].

Many healthcare professional and scientific organisations, including those for Dentistry [11, 12] and Nursing [13, 14] endorse study programmes that incorporate IPCP competences. A significant opportunity for developing IPE courses in dental education has been presented by the Commission Delegated Directive (Eu) 2024/782 [15], which mandates the inclusion of “gerodontology” and “interprofessional collaborative care” as compulsory topics within the minimum training requirements for dentists in the EU dental study programmes.

Both gerodontology and gerontological nursing are ideal for developing joint IPE courses because of their multidisciplinary nature and the diverse clinical environments in which they are taught. The interdisciplinary nature of geriatric care, combined with the shared geriatric competences across professions such as dentistry, medicine, nursing, pharmacy, and social work, provides significant advantages for advancing IPE focused on caring for older adults [16]. Additionally, selecting nursing homes as training environments further enriches these educational opportunities [17, 18].

However, the development of IPE courses faces many challenges, and there is limited relevant information for educators. Therefore, the aim of this study was to describe the development, implementation, and evaluation of an interprofessional education (IPE) course in older people care for dental and nursing students, and to investigate its impact on their perceptions of IPE and interprofessional collaborative practice (IPCP).

Materials and Methods

Participants

The IPE course was developed by the disciplines of gerodontology of the Dental School of the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens, Greece and gerontological nursing of the Nursing Department of the University of West Attica, Greece. Both disciplines offer theoretical and outreach training in geriatric care in various settings such as nursing homes, hospitals, day centers for older people, etc. The course was delivered to a convenience sample of 8th semester dental and nursing students who received simultaneous training at the same nursing home, on dates specified by their study programmes. Participants were previously informed on the aims and content of the study during the courses' lectures. They were advised that their participation was voluntary, and their responses would be anonymous. Participants who chose not to attend the IPE course were offered alternative training sessions on the same dates at different locations. The study was approved by the Ethics committee of the Dental School of the National and Kapodistrian University of Athens (585/2023).

Venue

The venue was a nursing home in Athens that served as a multidisciplinary training center for caring for older adults.

Development of the Course

Before the development of the IPE course, separate training sessions were conducted by the dental and nursing schools at the same nursing home during the spring semester. Educators from both professions had the opportunity to observe similarities in the objectives, content, and required competences between the two courses. Moreover, both course directors were specialised in geriatrics/gerontology and shared a common teaching philosophy including IPCP. These coincidences created opportunities for discussions, leading to the development of a joint IPE pilot course focused on the care of frail older people.

To develop the course, the educators had several face‐to‐face and online meetings. An extensive study of relevant literature was conducted to gain insights into the development and structure of similar IPE courses, particularly as institutional support and formal training were not provided. Additionally, personal communication was carried out with colleagues from various schools worldwide with experience in the implementation of IPE courses.

Two major learning competences were identified: (a) to promote IPCP competences, and (b) to enhance comprehensive geriatric assessment skills. Based on the collected information and the previous teaching experience, the educators developed the course's content, educational methodology and techniques, students' assessment strategies and course evaluation methods. Finally, preparatory webinars and electronic teaching material were developed and delivered to prepare students for the IPE training conducted at the nursing home.

The implementation of the course required prior agreement and close collaboration with the nursing home administration, chief nurses and social workers. Specific residents were pre‐selected with the help of chief nurses based on their medical history and functional ability to match the course objectives. Residents were initially informed about the aims and content of the study by the chief nurses and afterwards by the educators. They were invited to participate by offering their time and allowing students to learn from them. Informed consent to participate in the interviews and examinations conducted by the students was obtained from the residents or their legal representatives.

Pilot Testing of the Course

Eight pairs of nursing and dental students volunteered to participate in the pilot study, conducted in the spring semester 2018/19. The pilot course included pre‐visit training through webinars and study of electronic teaching material, team nursing home visits, and post‐course evaluations by both students and educators. Students completed reflective diaries based on Gibbs Reflective Cycle, as part of their self‐assessment and formative assessment, while educators completed an evaluation questionnaire with open‐ended questions. The results of the pilot study were very positive. Themes that emerged from content analysis of students' diaries included teaching and learning from each other, benefits for older people care, in‐depth understanding of older person's needs, and integrated care. The students identified a need for further developing interdisciplinary education programmes to ensure integrated care. The educators' opinion was also very positive; they highlighted the need for institutional support and faculty development in IPE, as well as involvement of more disciplines to broaden the perspectives of trainees in gerontological interprofessional care. The positive outcomes of the pilot IPE session encouraged educators to make necessary adjustments to their study programmes and develop a comprehensive course with longer duration and increased students' enrollment.

The Final Course Implementation

Before the site visits, the educators collaborated closely with the care home administration and chief nurses to preselect residents who would offer students exposure to specific sociomedical and oral conditions.

Participating students were selected on a first come, first served basis after being provided with detailed information about all gerodontology or gerontological nursing outreach activities offered by the two schools. Pre‐visit face‐to‐face and online training was provided on the objectives and structure of the course, the basic concepts of IPE and integrated care for older people, and discipline‐specific topics. Moreover, core electronic material was provided, including information on the WHO integrated care for older people (ICOPE) and IPCP, descriptions of the professions of dentistry and nursing, as well as discipline‐specific interviews, clinical examination techniques and health promotion strategies for frail older adults.

The IPE team site visits involved 87 undergraduate dental and nursing students, organised into groups of 14 to 20, and had a half day duration. The course was conducted once a week for 5 weeks. Sessions started with a large group introductory and icebreaking segment lasting 45 to 50 min, which included participant introductions, description of the process, formation of mixed small groups of 2–3 dental and nursing students and distribution of preselected residents' files for study.

The students then visited the preselected residents in their rooms for 60–90 min, providing interviews and clinical examinations using structured questionnaires, followed by individualised general and oral health promotion counselling. Caregivers and family members, when present, also participated in the counselling sessions. Both professions recorded sociomedical and dental/nursing related information and assessed declines in intrinsic capacity using the WHO ICOPE screening tool [19]. Since a large portion of the sociomedical and functional history was relevant to both professions, only one student was responsible for asking the related questions. The dental examination assessed the status of teeth, soft tissues, lips, dentures, and oral hygiene. The nursing assessment included blood pressure measurement, pain assessment, functional assessment (Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living/ADL), cognitive assessment (Mini Mental State Examination Scale), and safe patient transferring. Those examinations necessitated close collaboration between students from different professions and exchange of knowledge and skills. For example, nursing students educated dental students on how to safely transfer patients, while dental students instructed nursing students on performing oral screenings and providing efficient oral hygiene.

The next step was the development of common care plans (1 h) by the small mixed groups of students. The care plan chart required documentation of the resident's sociomedical and functional condition, discipline‐specific findings, goals of the comprehensive care plan, and the required interventions.

Training was concluded with a large group debriefing session (1.5–2 h) where the residents' cases and individual care plans were presented by the small groups and discussed among students and educators. The necessary preventive/therapeutic interventions were communicated to the resident and/or to the chief nurse.

Students' Assessments

To assess changes in students' perceptions of IPE and IPCP, an anonymous questionnaire was administered to all 87 students during both the introductory and debriefing sessions. This questionnaire collected demographic and educational data, and included the Student Perceptions of Interprofessional Clinical Education‐Revised2 instrument (SPICE‐R2) [20, 21, 22, 23]. To match the individual pre‐ and post‐session assessment sheets, students were asked to cite the three last digits of their mobile phones. The students recorded their sex, age, profession, working experience, employment in the health sector, previous collaboration with other health professionals during their study programmes or employment, and any previous participation in IPE programmes. SPICE‐R2 consists of ten 5‐point Likert‐scale items with three subscales measuring students' perceptions of interprofessional teamwork and team‐based practice, roles/responsibilities for collaborative practice, and patient outcomes from collaborative practice. The scale score could range from 10 to 50, with higher scores indicating more positive perceptions towards IPE and IPCP. The scale was previously translated into Greek and showed acceptable internal consistency.

Moreover, at the debriefing session, students were asked to fill in the reflective practice diaries describing the event, considering their feelings and thoughts, evaluating the event and developing an action plan. Diaries were used for both self‐assessment and formative assessment.

Course Evaluation

All 87 students completed anonymous course evaluation forms, indicating their discipline. The forms included six close‐ended questions scored from 1 (very poor) to 10 (excellent) and an open‐ended question inviting comments on any programme weaknesses and suggestions for improvement. The total score could range between 6 and 60.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analysed using descriptive statistics (mean, median, standard deviation, ranges, frequencies). The normality of the distribution was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test and non‐parametric tests were applied when necessary. To analyse variation between professions in various dependent variables, as well as in the students' attitudes towards IPE before and after the activity, univariate analyses were performed using chi‐square tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, Student's t‐tests, paired samples t‐tests, and Wilcoxon signed ranks test. The level of statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05.

Moreover, seven educators from both disciplines supervised the IPE training.

Assessment of Students' Attitudes Towards IPE and IPCP After Training

After the training, students' perceptions towards IPE and IPCP statistically significantly improved in both disciplines, as reflected in the total SPICE‐R2 scores and its individual components (p < 0.005). The exception was the “Interprofessional Teamwork and Team‐based Practice” component which did not significantly improve in dental students (Table 2). Looking at the individual items of SPICE‐R2, significant improvements in the total sample were recorded in six items: #1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 9. Three items showed statistically significant improvement in dental students and six in nursing (Table 2). Both pre‐test and post‐test scores were significantly higher in nursing students (p < 0.001). Before training, the rating for Item #1 “working with students from different disciplines enhances my education” was significantly lower among dental students (3.9) compared to nursing students and improved following the activity (4.4). Notably, the score for this item was already high among nursing students (4.7) prior to the activity.

Based on both the reflective diaries and the open‐ended question in the course evaluation form, most students highlighted the positive interaction they experienced with professionals from another discipline and valued the productive time spent engaging with the older residents. This experience enabled them to appreciate the vital importance of comprehensive care for older adults delivered by a multiprofessional team. They also highly valued the comprehensive geriatric assessment competences gained through the IPE training. They felt well respected for their knowledge and opinion by the interprofessional team of students. They also felt well prepared by their educators to face the challenges involved in assessing older adults. Although the duration of the session was considered lengthy (half day), they appreciated the additional time, which allowed for a comprehensive assessment of the residents and an in‐depth discussion of treatment plans.

Students, however, identified some weaknesses in their communication techniques and acknowledged the need for further personal development. Additionally, they expressed a desire to participate in more IPE sessions, which would provide the opportunity to assess a greater number of residents with diverse sociomedical and dental conditions. They also wished to be involved in implementing and evaluating comprehensive care plans through follow‐up visits. Finally, students suggested including more professions in the training to enhance interdisciplinary collaboration.

Residents' Satisfaction

Another important finding from the implementation of the IPE course was the increased satisfaction expressed by many participating residents. Several of them happily agreed to participate in multiple sessions and provided valuable feedback for enhancing the experience. They expressed great appreciation for being recognised as members of the “educational team” and for the opportunity to engage with younger adults. On the other hand, some residents who initially agreed to participate experienced fatigue on the day of the session and subsequently declined to attend.

Satisfaction Among Nursing Home Administration and Staff

The nursing home administration and staff expressed high satisfaction with the implementation of the programme, recognising its role in promoting both the general and oral health of residents, as well as improving their social interaction with younger adults from the local community.

Discussion

This study described the development and implementation of an IPE course on the care of older people, involving undergraduate dental and nursing students, enrolled respectively in the mandatory gerodontology and gerontological nursing courses. The findings demonstrated that the students viewed the learning experience very positively, with their perceptions of IPE and IPCP showing significant improvement following completion of the course. The detailed description of the procedure for developing an IPE course may serve as a valuable resource for educators wishing to create IPE programmes, particularly since prior experience and formal faculty training are usually limited or lacking.

Although both disciplines improved perceptions of IPE and IPCP, dental students exhibited lower total and single‐component scores, both before and after the session. Moreover, dental students' perceptions showed a significant change in three out of 10 items after training, compared to six among nursing students. It should also be noted that dental students' evaluation score of the IPE session was lower compared to nursing students.

The smaller improvement of dental students' perceptions of IPE and IPCP after training was also recorded in previous studies [24, 25, 26]. This finding may be explained by differences in the nature and culture of the two professions as well as the associated curricula. Dental students typically receive their clinical training in relative isolation from other professions, with the perception of an independent future dental practice [7, 24, 27]. Although more dentists were employed in the health sector compared to nurses, their previous interprofessional collaboration experience was lower, mainly with dental aids and dental technicians.

On the other hand, teamwork experience is a common finding during nursing studies [28, 29] and nursing education culture promotes a holistic approach to care [30]. According to the nursing curriculum, students are exposed to interprofessional teamwork throughout their clinical training starting from the early years of their studies and continuing through their internship. In this study, nursing students were in the last semester of their studies and had completed half of their internship. During clinical training and internships, students realize that holistic and integrated care provision requires teamwork [29].

Although the duration of the IPE activity was short and the effect of teamwork on residents' health outcomes could not be seen, students' perceptions of “Patient Outcomes from Collaborative Practice” were significantly improved. An increase in the number of IPE sessions was among the most frequent suggestions made by students, along with the desire to assess a larger number of residents with diverse health conditions, beyond those preselected by educators. It should be noted that preselection of residents' cases is essential because some of them may refuse to participate or feel fatigued on the day of the programme. By maintaining a long preselected list, educators can ensure the successful implementation of the session.

The residents' contribution to the course was profound. Most of them happily agreed to participate and help students learn from them. A few participated in multiple sessions and considered this task very rewarding. They were enthusiastic about being assigned as members of the educational team and offered very useful feedback on the course implementation. Previous IPE studies also stressed the importance of older individuals' participation and offered them certificates of appreciation or awarded them community adjunct faculty of the school [31].

The students in the present study expressed their wish to participate in more IPE sessions. The duration of IPE activities ranges from a few hours to many months and from one session to several ones spread throughout the study programmes [7, 32]. Providing IPE for longer periods of time may have a positive impact on students' perceptions of teamwork as well as on patients' outcomes, since they progressively acquire more knowledge of their professional role [21].

The students also wished to include more professions in the activity. The integrated care team for older people includes all health professionals caring for older individuals such as physicians, dentists, nurses, speech and language therapists, pharmacists, occupational therapists, physiotherapists, psychologists, and others, as well as non‐health professionals such as social workers, sociologists, anthropologists, lawyers, etc. However, it has not been possible to include more disciplines in this study due to several barriers, such as lack of interest from other tutors, overcrowded curricula, and incompatible study programmes. These are common barriers in the implementation of IPE courses [18, 27, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37].

IPE courses may occur in various settings such as in a classroom, in a clinical setting, or online [32]. IPE courses in geriatric care are often conducted in nursing homes, which provide many advantages, such as prolonged residents' stays and complex needs, exposing students to a variety of cases [7, 18].

Preparatory students' training through seminars, webinars and delivery of online material was considered crucial, although it required considerable time from educators. It encompassed both discipline‐specific as well as transversal skills training. These activities also improve understanding of other team members and offer guidance on working within interprofessional teams [18]. It was noticed that adequate preparation reduced the length of the introductory session and allowed more time for the actual IPE activity.

During the development of the course, several challenges were encountered as previously described [7, 33]. Due to the crowded curricula and the incompatible timetables, the IPE course could only be implemented for one morning per week over 5 weeks. Tutors faced increased workload in designing and implementing the IPE activity, without prior formal training and without adequate managerial support. On the other hand, enabling factors included the protected time for outreach training in geriatric care in both curricula, the shared competences in geriatric care, the nursing home as common training venue, the strong collaboration with the nursing school administration and staff, the enthusiasm of the educators, and the positive feedback from both students and residents regarding their participation in these sessions.

The challenges met in the development of this course highlighted the importance of institutional support, flexible curriculum design, formal IPE staff training and resource allocation to overcome barriers and enable the successful implementation of effective IPE programmes in healthcare education. Moreover, the lower scores of dental students regarding perceptions of IPE and IPCP compared to nursing students emphasised the need for curricular interventions to improve transversal skills throughout dental studies.

The convenience sampling and the participation of only two professions in the IPE course restricted the generalisability of findings. Moreover, the short duration of the IPE activity did not allow observations of long‐term changes in students' perceptions of IPE and IPCP, or any effects on patients' satisfaction and health outcomes.

Conclusion

The present IPE course on older people care for undergraduate nursing and dental students was a positive experience for both students and educators. Both professions positively evaluated the activity and demonstrated improvement in their IPE and IPCP perceptions. Institutional support and flexible curricula are necessary prerequisites to implement these courses.
