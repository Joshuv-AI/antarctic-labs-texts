Longitudinal Associations Between Child and Family Factors With Korean Children’s Subjective Happiness Before and During COVID-19 in Late Childhood
Authors: Jungtae Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study investigated the trajectories of subjective happiness among children aged 8–13, assessed the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, and identified child- and family-level predictors of variations in happiness.MethodsDrawing on 5855 person-years of data from the nationally representative Panel Study on Korean Children, we employed longitudinal multilevel modeling to examine trends, including those during the pandemic period, and to estimate the influence of child- and family-level predictors. Sex differences were analyzed using interaction terms and stratified analyses.ResultsThe study revealed a significant negative temporal trend in children’s subjective happiness from ages 8 to 13 years, with a sharp decline associated with the onset of the pandemic in 2020 (b=-1.524, p<0.001). Regarding child-level predictors, male sex, sleep duration, subjective health, and number of friends were positive predictors. Maternal education was a positive family-level predictor, whereas maternal stress was a negative predictor. In terms of sex differences, the happiness levels of both males and females remained significantly below the pre-pandemic levels. Overweight/obesity, sleep duration, maternal education level, and paternal subjective health were significant predictors among females but not among males.ConclusionAlthough we found a decline in happiness with age, we confirmed that COVID-19 had a significant impact on children’s subjective happiness. Our findings underscore the need to identify the key predictors of happiness and to consider proactive strategies to prepare for future infectious disease outbreaks.Keywords: Happiness, COVID-19, Korean children, Multilevel analysis

INTRODUCTION

Over the past two decades, children’s happiness has been increasingly recognized as a crucial component of healthy development because it influences mental health outcomes, including the risk of suicide attempts, ideation, and depression [1-3]. Notwithstanding its importance, Korean children, particularly elementary school children, showed the lowest life satisfaction among 22 Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development countries in 2021, according to a report from the Korean Children and Youth Well-Being Index Survey [4]. This result suggests that paying closer attention to enhancing children’s well-being is essential for promoting healthy development.

A large body of literature has identified a significant association between child and family factors and children’s subjective happiness, especially among preadolescents (ages 9–12), who are still heavily dependent on family members, actively engage with peers, and experience dynamic physical changes [5-7]. Studies focusing on child factors have found that sex, health status (e.g., body mass index [BMI]), sleep, and peer relationships are substantial predictors of happiness [8-10]. In other studies, parents’ education level, health status, stress, and household income were also considered as indicators of children’s subjective happiness [5,11,12].

Although previous studies have found significant associations between child and family factors and children’s subjective happiness, happiness is an under-researched area, particularly how happiness changes in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. Previous research has reported that the pandemic has significantly impacted children’s mental health and developmental outcomes, emphasizing the need to investigate this period. Given that children’s happiness tends to decline naturally with age [13,14]—especially during the transition to adolescence—analytical strategies that fail to account for the pandemic’s effects may overestimate or misinterpret the pattern of children’s happiness. Additionally, including the pandemic variable in the analytical model allowed us to identify which child- and family-level factors continued to show significant associations with children’s subjective happiness, independent of the pandemic effect.

In this regard, the primary aim of this study was to assess the impact of the pandemic on children’s subjective happiness using longitudinal, multilevel data spanning both the pre-pandemic and pandemic years. Furthermore, we aimed to identify child- and family-level predictors that significantly influenced children’s happiness over time, while controlling for the pandemic variable. Finally, we examined sex differences, including whether male and female children experienced differential changes in happiness during the pandemic.

METHODS

Data and participants

This study used public data drawn from the Panel Study on Korean Children (PSKC), a nationally representative birth cohort of infants born in 2008. In 2008, PSKC recruited 2150 newborn households nationwide with stratified, multi-stage sampling. The survey has been conducted annually to investigate a wide range of factors related to growth, nurturing, and development, including child development, parental and family characteristics, early childhood education, and childcare experiences.

We analyzed data from waves 9–14 (2016–2021; ages 8–13 years) covering late childhood. Notably, Waves 13 and 14 were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic (July–December 2020 and 2021), with data gathered online or by phone in accordance with public health guidelines (e.g., wearing masks and maintaining at least 1 m of distance between respondents and interviewers) [15]. After excluding 50 participants with missing data, the final sample comprised 5855 person-years. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Sungkonghoe University (IRB No. 2508-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB because the study used de-identified secondary data.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was children’s subjective happiness, measured with six items that asked participants about their feelings: “How do you feel about studying at school?” “What do you think about your appearance?” “How do you feel about your family?” “How do you feel about your friends?” “How do you feel about the school you attend?” and “How do you feel about your day?” The response options ranged from “I am not happy at all (1)” to “I am very happy (4).” In prior studies, this scale has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity among children [16,17]. This study summed item scores, with possible scores ranging from 6 to 24, with higher scores indicating greater happiness (α=0.91).

Explanatory variables

We included two domains of explanatory variables: children and families. Child-level variables included sex (0=female, 1=male), number of friends, and parent-rated subjective health (0=very unhealthy, 4=very healthy). The average sleep duration was calculated from the reported bed and wake times during the previous week. BMI was computed as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2) and categorized using age-specific cutoffs from the 2017 Korean National Growth Charts as follows: underweight (<5th percentile), normal (5th–84th percentile), overweight (85th–94th percentile), and obese (95th percentile) [18].

For the family domain, we included monthly household income (logged), household debt (logged), parents’ educational levels, subjective health (rated from 0=very unhealthy to 4=very healthy), and daily stress levels (rated from 0=never stressed to 3=very stressed).

Statistical analysis

To investigate longitudinal changes in children’s subjective happiness and the influence of child and family predictors, we used multilevel models, nesting the time (years) variable within individuals. This approach accounts for the non-independence of repeated measurements within the same child, enabling simultaneous modeling of both within-child and between-child differences. The outcome variable was the children’s subjective happiness, measured annually from 2016 to 2021. To account for unequal sample sizes across study years, the year variable was centered on the weighted mean (2018.51), yielding centered values ranging approximately from -2.51 to +2.49. Additionally, we included a variable for the pandemic period (0=pre-pandemic [2016–2019], 1=during pandemic [2020], 2=during pandemic [2021]) to examine pandemic-related changes. The formula for the multilevel model used in this study was as follows:

At Level 1, children’s annual happiness scores were modeled as a function of time (Year, β1j, that is, the rate of change in happiness over time), pandemic period (β2j), and individual deviation (or residual error, rij). At Level 2, individual differences in intercept, slope, and pandemic effect were modeled as a function of child sex (Sexj) and other child- or family-level factors (Wj). γ00, γ10, and γ20 are fixed effects for the intercept, time trend, and pandemic effect, respectively. γ21 represents the interaction between the pandemic and sex. A random intercept (u0j) was included to account for individual baseline differences in happiness, and a random slope (u1j) was specified for each year to allow for individual variability in happiness trajectories over time. The pandemic effect was modeled with a fixed slope (β2j) and allowed to vary by child sex via cross-level interaction.

The model fit was evaluated using the -2 log-likelihood, Akaike information criterion, and Bayesian information criterion, with lower values indicating a better fit. To explore sex-specific patterns, stratified analyses by sex were conducted to examine the differences in predictor significance, magnitude, and direction. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.2; https://cran.r-project.org/bin/windows/base/), with a two-sided significance level of 0.05.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 5855 person-years were analyzed between 2016 and 2021, with boys comprising 51.5% of the sample (Table 1). Mean happiness scores were relatively high in the pre-pandemic (19.54–20.23) but declined during the pandemic (17.91–17.78) (p<0.001). Even before the pandemic, happiness declined slightly with age (from 20.12 in 2016 to 19.54 in 2019). Other child-level indicators also declined over time, including sleep duration (from 9.57 to 8.48 hours), subjective health (from 3.24 to 3.11), and number of friends (from 8.50 to 6.07), with significant differences between the pre-pandemic and pandemic periods (p<0.001). Regarding family background, approximately 74% of mothers and 75% of fathers held college degrees or higher, a proportion that remained stable over the years.

Multilevel models predicting children’s subjective happiness from 2016 to 2021

As shown in Table 2, which presents the multilevel models predicting children’s subjective happiness from 2016 to 2021, Model 1 revealed that a one-year increase in time (slope) was associated with a decrease in happiness (b=-0.485, p<0.001), controlling for other predictors. Moreover, in Model 2, children’s subjective happiness dropped significantly in both 2020 (b=-1.524, standard error [SE]=0.101, p<0.001) and 2021 (b=-1.464, SE=0.127, p<0.001) compared to the pre-pandemic period. Model 3, including the interaction term and showing a better fit than Models 1 and 2, exhibited that during the pandemic years, children’s subjective happiness was significantly lower by about 1.718 points on average in 2020 (b=-1.718, SE=0.128, p<0.001) and 1.656 points on average in 2021 (b=-1.656, SE=0.155, p<0.001) compared to the pre-pandemic period (Fig. 1). Regarding child factors, female children reported being relatively happier than males (β=-0.12, p<0.01). Sleep duration (β=0.03, p<0.01), subjective health (β=0.05, p<0.001), and number of friends (β=0.05, p<0.001) were statistically significant. The interaction between the pandemic period and child sex was statistically significant (b=-0.384, SE=0.154, p<0.05 for 2020 and b=-0.367, SE=0.167, p<0.05 for 2021). This result indicates that the negative impact of the pandemic on subjective happiness was significantly stronger for males than for females (Fig. 2).

Multilevel models predicting children’s subjective happiness by sex

To better understand sex-related differences, we ran multilevel models separately for male and female children (Table 3). For males (Model 4), the baseline slope over time was not statistically significant (b=0.035, SE=0.046), suggesting no notable decline in happiness. In contrast, female children (Model 5) showed a significant downward trend with age (b=-0.355, SE=0.045, p<0.001). When the pandemic variable was added, both groups experienced significant declines in happiness, with a larger effect observed among males (b=-1.726, SE=0.146, p<0.001 for 2020 and b=-1.857, SE=0.180, p<0.001 for 2021) than females (b=-1.282, SE=0.137, p<0.001 for 2020 and b=-1.012, SE=0.178, p<0.001 for 2021). This result shows that the pandemic’s negative impact intensified from 2020 to 2021 among males, with a slight improvement in 2021 compared to the acute drop in 2020 among females.

For both male and female children, subjective health (male: β=0.04, p<0.05 vs. female: β=0.05, p<0.01), number of friends (male: β=0.06, p<0.01 vs. female: β=0.04, p<0.05), and maternal stress level (male: β=-0.04, p<0.05 vs. female: β=-0.04, p<0.05) were significant predictors of happiness. DISCUSSION

This study investigated the trajectories of children’s subjective happiness between the ages of 8 and 13 years, assessed the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, and identified childand family-level predictors associated with variations in happiness. Using a longitudinal sample of Korean children, we identified several predictors of children’s subjective happiness and assessed the impact of the pandemic.

Specifically, we found that children’s subjective happiness declined with age, with a sharp decrease during the pandemic, even after disaggregating the pandemic periods (2020 and 2021). This result aligns with prior research, which shows that the pandemic has had profound detrimental effects on children’s well-being [19,20]. One possible explanation is that children are particularly vulnerable to disruptions in their routines and social lives, which affect their overall happiness [19]. Several Korean studies have highlighted the fact that school closures, social distancing, and home confinement may limit children’s opportunities for interpersonal engagement with peers and teachers. These changes are likely to increase feelings of loneliness, boredom, and stress, leading to lower overall happiness [4,21]. However, it is also important to note that the decline in happiness may not be solely attributable to the pandemic; rather, it may be confounded by early adolescent transitions, as our study has shown. This result suggests that unobserved time-varying factors may also play a role, as children often experience increasing academic pressure, reduced free time, growing emotional complexity, and hormonal changes as they mature, all of which may contribute to a natural decline in happiness over time [2,21].

Interestingly, our analysis also revealed that female children’s happiness notably decreased as they grew older, even before the pandemic, whereas male happiness remained more stable. This result is consistent with prior research, but points out the complexity of sex differences in happiness, such that changes in physical health, body image, social relationships, family, and psychological factors are intertwined in their happiness when they mature. Specifically, female children may be more vulnerable to these changes because their earlier experience of puberty may make them more sensitive to such changes, which could contribute to poor mental health [22,23]. During these changes, their experiences with the pandemic may be additional adverse events that influence their mental health, such as loneliness and anxiety [24,25].

However, this does not indicate that female children may be more adversely affected than their male counterparts when a pandemic breaks out. As our results showed, for males, the pandemic effect appeared cumulative, with happiness scores declining further from 2020 (b=-1.726) to 2021 (b=-1.857). Conversely, female children showed a pronounced drop in 2020 (b=-1.282) and a slight moderating trend in 2021 (b=-1.012). This result suggests that vulnerability cannot be determined by prior status based on sex, especially in the context of a substantial adverse event, such as a pandemic. Although we were unable to fully explain why such different trends occurred due to limited information, it may be that the year 2021, when social gatherings began to be partly allowed, may be a recovery time for females compared to males, remaining at the same level as the onset of the pandemic in 2020, which might worsen their happiness. However, given that their natural growth and situational characteristics may also confound the effect of the pandemic, a detailed investigation that encompasses a broader range of situational factors and their dynamic interactions before and after the pandemic may broaden our perspective on sex differences in happiness.

We also found that both healthy male and female children who reported longer sleep duration and more close friends tended to report higher levels of happiness. Specifically, this finding is consistent with prior research showing that children who sleep enough experience greater life satisfaction [26,27]. Research suggests that moderate sleep (approximately 9–11 hours for school-aged children) can improve emotional regulation and reduce stress, thereby increasing children’s happiness [27]. In the context of social relationships, previous studies have confirmed that having friendships is beneficial for mental and physiological health among children, wherein individuals with active social interactions may have a high chance of obtaining emotional support, information, and social resources; these forms of social support are critical elements in promoting happiness and health [28,29].

Regarding family factors, maternal education and stress levels were significant predictors of children’s subjective happiness. It is possible that mothers with higher education have better social resources and parenting knowledge, which help them create a supportive environment for their children and may contribute to their children’s happiness [30]. Additionally, high levels of maternal stress indicate that mothers may be less patient, responsive, and experience parent-child conflict [31]. In prior research using hair cortisol concentration, a valuable biomarker of chronic stress, maternal stress was positively associated with children’s stress levels [32]. Therefore, our findings and prior research suggest that mothers play a critical role in their children’s happiness. Thus, supporting their needs and helping them manage stress may be crucial for children’s overall well-being and happiness.

We also found sex differences in the predictors of happiness. First, females who were overweight or obese were less happy than those with a normal weight, and this was not significant among males. This result may be explained by evidence suggesting that a positive body image and a healthy lifestyle are essential elements for the happiness of female children. Indeed, previous studies have found that during late childhood, female children often become more conscious of their body image and may face more social pressures related to weight than male children, which can affect their self-esteem and happiness [22]. Second, sleep duration was a significant predictor for female children but not for males in our analysis. Prior research has revealed that the beneficial effects of longer sleep durations on behavioral and developmental outcomes are more pronounced in females than in males [33]. Another study found that female children were more emotionally affected by insufficient sleep than male children [34]. Third, the finding of paternal subjective health was supported by prior research, which found that the emotional closeness and psychological influence of father–daughter relationships during late childhood and early adolescence were particularly substantial for female children who perceive a father’s emotional and physical engagement as stable and supportive, which can reduce their distress [35,36].

Despite these findings, this study had several limitations. First, while the multilevel model accounted for between-person variability via random intercepts, it could not fully control for time-invariant confounding, as in fixed-effects models, raising the possibility of omitted-variable bias. Second, self-reported measures of happiness and other variables may have been subject to recall bias or misreporting. Third, due to data constraints, we used the number of friends as a proxy for social connectedness. However, prior research has found that the degree of intimacy should also be a measurement tool when estimating relationship quality [37,38]. Further research examining the role of intimacy in estimating happiness is warranted. Fourth, we were unable to estimate how children’s happiness changed following the pandemic due to data limitations. Fifth, panel attrition bias may have occurred over six years. Although multilevel models can accommodate unbalanced data, families who dropped out of the study or missed the follow-up surveys may differ systematically from those who remained. Sixth, due to limited data, we were unable to fully explain the divergent trends in happiness observed between the sexes during the pandemic. Future research should investigate sex-specific levels of social activity, as these may provide a critical indication of why female children exhibit a transitional recovery trend, whereas male children experience a cumulative decline. Finally, the findings may not be fully generalizable beyond South Korea, where unique cultural factors, such as intense academic pressure and a collectivist family environment, may influence children’s happiness. Future longitudinal studies across diverse cultural contexts are recommended to explore happiness trajectories and predictors, and to compare findings with those of the Korean population.

CONCLUSION

Despite these limitations, we provided meaningful insights from a longitudinal analysis of children’s subjective happiness, particularly before and during the pandemic, as well as from the association between child and family factors and happiness among Korean children of both sexes. Our findings suggest that the pandemic has had a significant impact on children’s happiness, and several child and family factors were identified as important predictors of happiness. These results underscore the need for public health policies that support children’s emotional well-being, consider child and family factors, and emphasize the importance of proactive strategies to prepare for future infectious disease outbreaks.
