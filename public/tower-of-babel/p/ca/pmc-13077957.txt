The mediating role of social networks and safety perceptions in the frailty–quality of life relationship among older people in rural and urban regions of Ghana
Authors: Gideon Dzando, Paul R Ward, Lillian Mwanri, Solomon Alhassan Yabila, Justice Moses K Aheto, Rachel C Ambagtsheer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFrailty is a major public health concern among older people, particularly in resource-limited settings where it significantly reduces Quality of Life (QoL) and increases vulnerability to poor health outcomes. While social and environmental factors influencing frailty have been studied in high-income countries, evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa remains scarce. This study examined the relationship between frailty and QoL in Ghana, focusing on the mediating roles of social networks and perceptions of safety across rural and urban settings.MethodsWe analysed nationally representative data from the World Health Organisation’s Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health (SAGE) in Ghana. The data included older people (N = 2077), aged 60 years and above. Frailty was measured using a 30-item Frailty Index, and QoL was assessed using a composite score from the WHOQOL-BREF. We conducted bootstrapped parallel mediation analysis to examine the indirect effects of social networks and perception of safety on the frailty–QoL relationship, with subgroup analyses comparing urban and rural settings.ResultsThe overall frailty prevalence was 39.7%, with slightly higher rates in rural (43.1%) than urban areas (35.8%). Frailty was significantly associated with lower QoL across both rural and urban settings. The total effect of frailty on QoL was strong and significant in both rural (effect = − 5.44, 95% CI: − 6.00, − 4.88) and urban areas (effect = − 5.97, 95% CI: − 6.65, − 5.29). In rural areas, perception of safety was found to be a significant mediator in the association between frailty and QoL (indirect effect = 0.08, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.15), while in urban areas, social networks were the key mediator (indirect effect = − 0.22, 95% CI: − 0.45, − 0.02). The combined total indirect effect was not significant in both urban (–0.24, 95% CI: − 0.48, 0.00) and rural (–0.05, 95% CI: − 0.24, 0.15) areas.ConclusionContext specific interventions are required to improve the well-being of older people in rural and urban regions of Ghana. Strengthening community safety and cohesion in rural areas and enhancing social connectedness in urban settings may help reduce the impact of frailty, as well as improve QoL among older people in Ghana.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Frailty, Ghana, Neighbourhood safety, Quality of Life, Rural, Social Network, Urban

Introduction

Ghana, like many countries in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), is experiencing rapid socio-cultural and demographic changes, with the population of older people (aged 60 years and above) projected to increase from about 2 million in 2021 to 6.3 million by 2050 [1]. While this increase reflects advancement in public health, it is accompanied by rising age-related health challenges [2, 3]. Among these, frailty has emerged as a critical concern due to its association with functional decline, increased healthcare utilisation, falls, and mortality [4]. Frailty is a multidimensional geriatric syndrome characterised by decreased physiologic reserves and increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes [5, 6].

Despite global attention on frailty, research in SSA remains scarce, with limited evidence on the unique socio-cultural factors shaping frailty in this region, particularly between urban and rural populations [7, 8]. In Ghana, rapid urbanisation and persistent rural-urban inequalities create distinct challenges for older people, especially concerning access to healthcare, social support, and safe living environments [9]. Understanding these context-specific drivers of frailty is essential for designing appropriate policies and services for older people.

Frailty is closely linked with Quality of Life (QoL), with a growing body of research identifying a bidirectional relationship between both constructs [10]. Frailty has been noted to reduce physical and mental well-being, increase social disengagement, and lead to loss of independence, while poor QoL has also been reported to accelerate frailty progression [10, 11]. Drawing on the disablement process model and social determinants of health perspectives, frailty can be understood not only as a biological condition but as a state of vulnerability that shapes older people’s capacity to interact with their social and physical environments, with downstream consequences for well-being and QoL. In this framework, the effects of frailty on QoL are expected to operate partly through intermediate social and environmental pathways rather than exclusively through direct health-related mechanisms.

Additionally, recent evidence from Europe and Asia demonstrates that strong social networks and broader environmental factors such as place of residence play an important role in promoting healthy ageing [10, 12–15]. These factors may help reduce the negative impact of frailty on QoL.

However, the extent to which social and environmental factors operate as mechanisms linking frailty to QoL has received limited empirical attention in SSA, with most existing studies, including those from Ghana, treating frailty and QoL as separate constructs [16–18]. For example, Alaazi et al. [16] found that neighbourhood characteristics in Accra significantly influenced older people’s QoL, while Attafuah et al. [17] identified associations between QoL and sociodemographic factors in urban slums. Similarly, Oduro [19] examined psychological and physical factors related to social integration, and Oduro and Oduro [20] explored how emotional, psychological, spiritual, and physical well-being predict life satisfaction among older Ghanaians. None of these studies incorporated frailty as a determinant. Although Asiamah et al. [21] investigated frailty and functional difficulty among older adults in Ghana, their analysis did not extend to QoL or psychosocial outcomes. As a result, the pathways through which frailty may influence QoL, particularly via social and environmental mechanisms, remain poorly understood in this context. Specifically, existing studies do not test whether social networks and perceived safety operate as mediating mechanisms linking frailty to QoL, nor whether these pathways differ between urban and rural settings.

In Ghana, older people in rural communities often continue to engage in labour-intensive work into old age, leading to considerable physical strain. This phenomenon is often exacerbated by inadequate healthcare provision, including shortages of healthcare workers and poor infrastructure, which leaves older people in particularly precarious circumstances [9, 22]. Conversely, urban areas, despite offering more health services, present challenges such as overcrowded housing, rising crime rates, and the weakening of traditional family support systems [17, 23]. These differences highlight the need for context-specific strategies to address the unique challenges faced by older people in urban and rural areas in Ghana.

Against this backdrop of rural–urban differences in living conditions and social organisation, perceived safety and social networks represent two key pathways through which frailty may affect QoL. Safety concerns, whether due to crime, traffic hazards, or poor infrastructure, can limit mobility, social participation, and access to services, particularly among physically vulnerable older adults, thereby contributing to poorer QoL [24, 25]. Social networks, in contrast, provide emotional support, social connectedness, and practical assistance that may buffer the adverse effects of frailty and support well-being [12, 26]. These dynamics are especially salient in Ghana, where concerns about personal safety and the erosion of extended family support systems have been documented in recent ageing studies [27, 28]. Accordingly, perceived safety and social networks were conceptualised as potential mediators in the association between frailty and QoL, rather than as simple correlates. These pathways may also operate differently across urban and rural settings, given contextual differences in infrastructure, social organisation, and exposure to risk. Consistent with the disablement process model, viewed through a social determinant of health perspective, we expected that higher levels of frailty would be associated with poorer QoL, partly through reduced perceived safety and weaker social networks, and that these pathways would differ between urban and rural settings.

Consequently, this study has two main objectives. First, to examine the relationship between frailty and QoL, focusing on the potential mediating roles of perceived safety and social networks in Ghana. Second, to explore potential urban–rural differences in these pathways to understand the distinct challenges that older people face in different geographical settings. It is expected that our findings will provide insights that may inform the development of appropriate and context-specific services to support older people in both rural and urban settings of Ghana.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a secondary cross-sectional analysis using data from Wave 2 of the World Health Organisation’s Study on Global AGEing and Adult Health (SAGE) in Ghana [29]. SAGE is a nationally representative survey conducted through multistage cluster sampling strategies. Sampling covered all the 10 (now expanded to 16) administrative regions of Ghana. SAGE Wave 2 collected data in both rural and urban enumeration areas to ensure national representativeness. The survey is part of a multi-country longitudinal study that collects data to complement existing ageing data sources to inform policy and programmes. The World Health Organisation (WHO) and the University of Ghana Medical School, through the Department of Community Health collaborated to implement SAGE Wave 2 in Ghana. Although SAGE is longitudinal in design (with data collected every 4 years), the present study uses data from Wave 2, conducted in 2014/2015 [29]. Wave 2 was the most recent publicly available nationally representative dataset on the health and wellbeing of older people in Ghana at the time of this analysis. Although a subsequent wave (Wave 3) has been conducted, it is not yet publicly available. Moreover, the study focuses on structural relationships between frailty, social networks, perceived safety, and QoL, which are unlikely to have materially changed over this period in Ghana, making Wave 2 appropriate for the study objectives. Participants were interviewed regarding their chronic health conditions and health services coverage; subjective well-being and QoL; healthcare utilization; risk factors and preventive health behaviours; perceived health status; socio-demographic and work history; social cohesion; and household characteristics. Detailed information on the study tools and data collection procedures for SAGE is published elsewhere [29]. This analysis focused on participants aged 60 years and above. Participants were included in the analysis if they had complete data on frailty, quality of life, perceived safety, social networks, and covariates. Cases with missing responses on any of these variables were excluded using a complete-case approach.

Study setting

Ghana is a West African country with a population of approximately 30.8 million people, according to the recent Population and Housing Census [1]. The country shares borders with Togo to the east, Côte d’Ivoire to the west, Burkina Faso to the north, and the Gulf of Guinea to the south. Ghana is a constitutionally democratic country and has witnessed peaceful transitions of power between political parties following successful elections since the early 1990s. Administratively, the country is currently divided into 16 regions, which are further subdivided into 261 Metropolitan, Municipal, and District Assemblies (MMDAs). Ghana has a diverse population comprising multiple ethnic groups. Approximately 57% of the population resides in urban areas, while 43% live in rural communities [1]. The number of older people (aged 60 and above) is estimated to be over 2.5 million, representing about 7% of the total population [1]. Life expectancy in Ghana is estimated at 65.41 years [30]. Family size in Ghana has been gradually decreasing, especially in urban areas, contributing to weaker intergenerational caregiving relationships, while rural areas tend to maintain larger family networks and stronger traditional caregiving practices [31]. Socio-economic status in Ghana is marked by pronounced inequalities, with rural residents generally experiencing higher poverty rates, lower educational attainment, and more limited access to healthcare and social services compared to urban populations. Ghana’s healthcare system operates under a mixed public-private model, with significant disparities in healthcare access between rural and urban areas. Urban centres benefit from better healthcare infrastructure and specialist services, whereas rural populations often face challenges such as inadequate medical facilities and limited geriatric care services [22].

Measures

Frailty

We measured frailty using a 30-item self-reported Frailty Index (FI) based on the deficit accumulation approach [32, 33]. The FI was constructed as a composite score that included responses to items covering key well-being domains such as activity limitation, pain, cognition, mental health, mobility restriction, and participation in community activities. We assigned equal weight to all items in the index. Although the FI as a frailty screening instrument has not been formally validated in Ghana, it has been applied in several low- and middle-income country (LMIC) settings. The FI relies on general health deficits that are broadly applicable across diverse cultural contexts. The selected items capture common physical, functional, and psychosocial deficits relevant to older people in Ghana.

We applied a standard threshold to categorise frailty: individuals with scores > 0.21 were classified as frail, scores between > 0.1 and ≤ 0.21 as pre-frail, and scores ≤ 0.1 as non-frail [34]. The full list of items included in the FI is presented in Appendix 1. We used frailty as a continuous independent variable in the mediation analysis [35].

Quality of Life (QoL)

We assessed QoL using 10 items from the World Health Organisation’s Quality of Life-Brief (WHOQOL-BREF) instrument, which provided a composite score reflecting participants’ overall QoL [36]. Participants were asked about their: (i) satisfaction with their health, (ii) ability to perform daily activities, (iii) satisfaction with personal relationships, (iv) satisfaction with the conditions of their living place, (v) overall satisfaction with current life, (vi) energy for everyday life, (vii) ability to meet daily financial needs, (viii) satisfaction with themselves, (ix) perceived control over important life aspects, and (x) self-rated overall quality of life. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, where 1 indicated ‘’very satisfied’’ or ‘’completely’’ and 5 indicated ‘’very dissatisfied’’ or ‘’none at all’’. For analysis, we recoded all items so that higher values consistently reflected better QoL [35]. The items were then summed and rescaled to form a continuous composite score ranging from 0 to 10, with higher scores indicating better overall QoL. The 10-item QoL scale demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.88) [37]. We used QoL as a continuous dependent (outcome) variable in the mediation analysis.

Social network

We assessed social networks using a 10-item scale, consistent with measures previously employed in studies conducted in other low income settings [38]. Participants were asked how often in the past 12 months they had: (i) attended public meetings discussing local or school affairs, (ii) met personally with community leaders, (iii) attended group, club, society, union, or organisational meetings, (iv) worked with others in their neighbourhood to fix or improve something, (v) had friends over to their homes, (vi) visited friends in other neighbourhoods, (vii) socialised with co-workers outside of work, (viii) attended religious services (excluding weddings and funerals), (ix) participated in social meetings, activities, or events, or visited relatives, and (x) communicated with their closest friends. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (‘’never’’) to 5 (‘’daily’’). Responses were summed and rescaled to form a continuous index ranging from 0 to 10. Higher scores represented stronger and more frequent social networks. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) [37]. We used the social network score as a continuous mediator in the mediation analysis.

Perception of safety

We assessed perception of safety using a 2-item safety scale. Study participants were asked (i) ‘how safe from crime and violence they feel when they are alone at home’ and (ii) ‘how safe they feel when walking down their street al.one after dark’. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 = ‘’Completely safe’’ to 5 = ‘’Not safe at all’’. The responses were reverse coded so that higher values indicated greater perceived safety. The two items were averaged to create a composite safety perception score ranging from 0 (completely unsafe) to 1 (very safe). Higher scores represent stronger perceptions of safety. The items used for our perception of safety scale are part of a broader neighbourhood safety perception scale commonly used to assess individual-level safety [39, 40]. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) [37]. The perception of safety scale was used as a continuous mediator in the analysis.

Covariates

Age, gender, and marital status were included as control variables in all analyses based on previous studies [41–43].

Statistical analysis

Assumption tests and bivariate analysis

We analysed the data using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.2 and employed descriptive statistics to present the demographic characteristics of the study participants. We first assessed whether the data met the assumptions of linear regression in the general population. We repeated these tests separately for both rural and urban populations. Specifically, we evaluated normality, linearity, homoscedasticity, and multicollinearity. Normality was assessed using histograms and normal probability plots. Linearity and homoscedasticity were evaluated using residual scatterplots. To test for multicollinearity, we reviewed tolerance values and Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with VIF values below 10 indicating acceptable levels. We found no major assumption violations, ensuring the reliability of the mediation analysis.

We also conducted bivariate correlation analyses to explore the direct relationships among the key variables: frailty (X), QoL (Y), perception of safety (M₁), and social networks (M₂). We calculated Pearson’s correlation coefficients to determine the strength and direction of these associations. Our decision to proceed with the mediation analysis was based on both statistical results and theoretical justification. We hypothesized that the mediating role of social networks and perceived safety in the frailty–QoL relationship might vary by geographical location. Specifically, we expected stronger indirect effects among urban residents, who may rely more on external social, and neighbourhood supports due to weaker family-based caregiving. Conversely, we anticipated that in rural settings, stronger traditional family ties and community-based caregiving would reduce the strength of these indirect pathways.

Mediation analysis

Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was applied to test whether social networks and perception of safety mediated the relationship between frailty and QoL. To assess the mediation effects, we employed a bootstrapping approach with 5,000 resamples. This method provides robust estimates of indirect effects by constructing confidence intervals without assuming normality in the sampling distribution. The bootstrap analysis quantified the indirect effect of frailty on QoL through perception of safety and social networks. Significance was determined using 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals, with effects considered significant if the confidence intervals did not include zero. The analysis was conducted in two stages. First, we tested the relationships and pathways at the entire population level. General population level relationships and mediation pathways

Regarding frailty status, using survey-weighted analyses, 39.7% (95% CI: 36.1–43.4) of the participants were classified as frail, 23.5% (95% CI: 20.6–26.4) as pre-frail, and 36.8% (95% CI: 33.1–40.5) as non-frail. When stratified by place of residence, the population-weighted prevalence of frailty was higher in rural areas (43.1%; 95% CI: 38.4–47.7) than in urban areas (35.8%; 95% CI: 30.1–41.5), while the proportion of non-frail individuals was higher in urban (40.3%; 95% CI: 34.6–46.0) compared with rural settings (33.8%; 95% CI: 28.9–38.6). However, these differences were not statistically significant (survey-adjusted Rao–Scott χ² test, p = 0.118).

The mean QoL score was 6.25 for the total sample, with urban participants reporting significantly higher QoL than rural participants (6.42 vs. 6.15, p < 0.001). Social network scores were slightly higher among rural participants (4.25) compared to urban participants (4.10), though this difference was not significant (p = 0.122). In contrast, perception of safety score was significantly higher in rural areas (0.80) than in urban areas (0.77, p = 0.001).

The effects of the demographic covariates (age, sex, marital status) were minimal and did not substantially alter the main direct and indirect relationships between frailty, the mediators, and QoL.

The results of the combined rural and urban population-level analyses are presented in Table 2. In the total sample, frailty was significantly associated with lower QoL (effect = − 5.53, BootSE = 0.19, 95% CI: − 5.91, − 5.14). Social networks were found to mediate the association between frailty and QoL (indirect effect = − 0.16, BootSE = 0.08, 95% CI: − 0.31, − 0.01), suggesting that better social connections may attenuate the association between frailty and lower QoL. Perception of safety did not mediate the association between frailty and QoL (indirect effect = 0.02, 95% CI: − 0.01, 0.06). The total indirect association through both mediators was not statistically significant (indirect effect = − 0.14, 95% CI: − 0.29, 0.00). The total effect of frailty on QoL (combining direct and indirect paths) was strong and significant (effect = − 5.67, BootSE = 0.21, 95% CI: − 6.07, − 5.26).

In rural areas, the association between frailty and QoL was partially accounted for by perceived safety (indirect effect = 0.08, BootSE = 0.03, 95% CI: 0.02, 0.15), suggesting that feeling safe may helped buffer the negative effect of frailty on QoL. However, social networks were not found to significantly account for the association between frailty and QoL (indirect effect = − 0.12, 95% CI: − 0.32, 0.07).

In urban areas, perception of safety was not a significant mediator (indirect effect = − 0.02, 95% CI: − 0.08, 0.02), but social networks significantly mediated the frailty–QoL relationship (indirect effect = − 0.22, BootSE = 0.12, 95% CI: − 0.45, − 0.02), suggesting that stronger social connections may help reduce the negative association between frailty and QoL.

The combined total indirect effect was not significant in both rural (indirect effect = − 0.05, 95% CI: − 0.24, 0.15) urban (indirect effect = − 0.24, 95% CI: − 0.48, 0.00) areas.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between frailty and QoL among older people in Ghana, focusing on the mediating roles of social networks and perceptions of safety across rural and urban settings. Our findings show a strong negative association, where higher frailty levels are consistently linked to lower QoL. This emphasizes frailty as an important determinant of well-being and highlights its multidimensional impact on physical health, social and psychological well-being.

Frailty is increasingly recognised as a major geriatric concern in SSA, with prevalence rates exceeding global estimates [7, 45]. In this study, high levels of frailty were observed across both rural and urban settings in Ghana, despite well-documented disparities in healthcare access and social services between these areas [22, 46]. This finding contrasts with patterns commonly reported in High-income Countries (HICs), where frailty is typically more prevalent in rural areas due to limited healthcare infrastructure and greater physical demands [47, 48]. The comparable prevalence of frailty across rural and urban settings suggests that healthcare access and social services alone may not fully explain frailty patterns in Ghana. Instead, this may reflect a complex interplay of socioeconomic, cultural, and environmental factors that transcend geographic location, highlighting the need for targeted, context-specific interventions. Further research is needed to explore how these factors interact to shape frailty pathways in Ghana.

The strong direct negative effect of frailty on QoL aligns with global evidence indicating that frailty reduces independence, increases psychological distress, and limits social participation [10, 11]. In LMIC settings like Ghana and most parts of SSA, these effects may be compounded by economic insecurity, high burdens of infectious and chronic diseases, and limited age-friendly services [7, 45, 49]. Higher frailty levels may be associated with greater dependence, loss of autonomy, and reduced social engagement, all of which contribute to poorer overall well-being.

Our mediation analyses provide contextually important insights into the mechanisms linking frailty and QoL. In rural areas, perception of safety showed evidence of a significant indirect pathway linking frailty and QoL, suggesting that feeling secure may help frail older people maintain social participation and a sense of independence, which may support their QoL [50, 51]. In contrast, in urban areas, social networks showed evidence of a significant indirect pathway. Strong social ties may be associated with a weaker negative association between frailty and QoL by providing emotional support, practical assistance, and a sense of belonging [13]. The lack of a significant mediation effect of perception of safety in urban areas may reflect higher crime rates, social fragmentation, and weakened community trust, which can diminish the protective role of perceived safety.

The absence of significant social network mediation in rural areas suggests that support may rely primarily on informal, family-based systems that may not be fully captured by standard social network measures. Although the total indirect effects were not statistically significant, this does not necessarily indicate the absence of meaningful social or environmental processes linking frailty and QoL. In cross-sectional data, indirect effects are often attenuated due to measurement error, limited temporal ordering, and the simultaneous operation of multiple pathways. The findings therefore suggest that perceived safety and social networks may operate as context-specific correlates rather than definitive causal mechanisms. These results should be interpreted as indicative of potential pathways that warrant longitudinal investigation, rather than as evidence of mediation in a causal sense.

Beyond individual-level implications, our findings have important implications for policy. With over 60% of older Ghanaians in this study classified as either frail or prefrail, there is a need for early-stage interventions to prevent further decline and support healthy ageing trajectories. In rural areas, it is important for policymakers to prioritize improving safety perceptions through better infrastructure, community-based security initiatives, and age-friendly public spaces. In urban settings, strengthening social networks through community centres, peer support groups, and intergenerational programs can help reduce the impact of frailty on QoL. Addressing broader socioeconomic inequalities and supporting intergenerational living arrangements can further reduce frailty risks and improve QoL.

Importantly, the findings from this study emphasise that frailty is not solely a clinical condition but is deeply shaped by social, cultural, and environmental factors. Socioeconomic disparities, evolving family support systems, and neighbourhood environments all influence how frailty affects older peoples’ daily lives and well-being.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it provides a comprehensive examination of the relationship between frailty and QoL among older people in SSA, an underexplored region where socio-cultural and economic factors uniquely shape ageing experiences. By incorporating social determinants such as social networks and perceptions of safety, our study extends beyond individual health indicators to explore broader structural influences. The use of robust analytical approaches, including mediation analysis and bootstrapping, ensures methodological rigor. Additionally, the study’s comparative perspective, examining urban and rural differences, offers valuable insights into how geographical and socio-environmental factors influence ageing.

Nevertheless, this study also has some limitations. All measures were self-reported, which may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Its cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between frailty, QoL, and the social determinants examined. Furthermore, other potentially relevant variables such as economic status, education level, occupational demands, and additional chronic conditions were not included as covariates because they were conceptualised as upstream determinants or components of frailty. Future longitudinal studies could model these factors to further examine their role in the frailty–QoL relationship. Finally, while the study provides valuable insights, findings may not be fully generalisable across all SSA countries due to differences in cultural norms, healthcare systems, and socio-economic conditions. The study was conducted in Ghana, a relatively peaceful country with comparatively stable socio-cultural structures. Further research is recommended in conflict-prone or more diverse settings across SSA to gain a deeper understanding of these mediation pathways. This can inform the development of context-specific policies and programs.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a strong and consistent negative association between frailty and quality of life among older people in Ghana. The findings further suggest that the pathways linking frailty to QoL differ by context, with perceived safety showing evidence of an indirect pathway in rural areas and social networks showing evidence of an indirect pathway in urban settings. These results highlight the importance of considering social and environmental contexts when examining the impact of frailty on well-being. While causal inferences cannot be drawn, the findings indicate that context-specific social and neighbourhood factors may shape how frailty relates to QoL among older people in Ghana. Future longitudinal research is needed to confirm these pathways and to inform interventions tailored to rural and urban settings.
