Acute kidney injury in a cardiac ICU in a middle-income country: risk factors and outcomes from Thailand
Authors: Supassara Nimkulrat, Nitcha Suphatheerawatr, Punnawit Laungchuaychok, Ukrit Prajantasen, Sathit Kurathong, Wanjak Pongsittisak, Solos Jaturapisanukul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute kidney injury (AKI) is associated with significant morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. However, data on AKI in coronary care units (CCU) remain limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This study aimed to determine the incidence, etiology, risk factors, and outcomes of AKI in a CCU setting in Thailand.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we analyzed 209 patients admitted to a tertiary hospital CCU between January 2019 and September 2020. We collected data on baseline characteristics, comorbidities, medications, clinical parameters, and outcomes. Risk factors were assessed using multivariable logistic regression analysis.ResultsAKI occurred in 30.6% of patients, with multifactorial etiologies being the most common (42.2%). AKI was associated with significantly higher mortality at both 30 days (35.9% vs. 3.4%, p < 0.001) and 90 days (43.8% vs. 6.9%, p < 0.001). Independent risk factors for AKI included pre-existing chronic kidney disease (adjusted OR 3.47), reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (OR 3.19), mechanical ventilation (OR 3.08), and vasopressor use (OR 2.84). Independent predictors of kidney replacement therapy were mechanical ventilation (OR 11.26) and vasopressor use (OR 5.61).ConclusionsAKI is a frequent and severe complication among CCU patients in a low- to middle-income country, significantly increasing short-term mortality and hospital stay. Early identification of high-risk patients may improve outcomes through timely intervention and monitoring.Keywords: Acute kidney injury, Coronary care unit, Heart failure

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) refers to a state of rapid deterioration in renal function, potentially resulting in fluid overload, electrolyte imbalances, uremic manifestations, and metabolic acidosis. The incidence of AKI varies between populations and the diagnostic criteria used for AKI, ranging from 1% to 66% [1]. The incidence and severity of AKI tend to escalate, particularly among critically ill patients. Hospitalized patients with AKI typically experience longer stays, higher healthcare costs, and increased morbidity and mortality [2]. In addition, compared with the general populace, individuals with underlying cardiac conditions, such as heart failure or myocardial infarction, are at a higher risk of developing AKI.

Although there is a growing body of literature on AKI in various clinical settings, including intensive care units (ICU), data on its incidence in coronary care units (CCU) remains limited. AKI is a common complication among CCU patients, and these units provide specialized care to patients with cardiac diseases such as acute coronary syndrome, congestive heart failure, and cardiac arrhythmias. However, the prevalence of AKI and its impact on patient outcomes in this population are still not well understood [3–6].

In addition, most research on AKI has been conducted in high-income nations, whereas few studies have focused on low- and middle-income nations (LMICs), where the burden of AKI is frequently higher due to limited access to health care resources and delays in presentation [7, 8]. Despite growing interest, studies specifically evaluating AKI in CCU settings in low- and middle-income countries remain scarce.

To address this gap in knowledge, we aim to investigate the incidence and impact of AKI in the CCU of a single-center tertiary care hospital in Thailand, a middle-income country. This study includes analyzed patient data to identify potential risk factors for AKI in CCU patients as well as the assessment of AKI’s impact on patient outcomes, including length of stay and mortality rates. The findings of this study will contribute to the growing body of literature on AKI in CCU, particularly in LMICs and provide valuable insights for improving patient care and outcomes in these settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We retrospectively reviewed the records of all patients older than 18 years who were admitted to the CCU for more than 48 h between January 2019 and September 2020 at Vajira Hospital, a tertiary care facility in Bangkok, Thailand. The CCU is a 10-bed unit at Vajira Hospital for seriously ill cardiac patients. Patients who had undergone postoperative cardiac surgery received care in a separate cardiovascular surgical ICU. To lower the risk of survival and treatment biases related to CCU readmission, we reviewed only the data from each patient’s first CCU admission. Patients who were admitted to the CCU prior to January 1, 2019; patients with prior kidney replacement therapy (KRT) or kidney transplantation; patients admitted for conditions other than cardiac problems, such as sepsis or non-cardiac operations; and patients with incomplete medical records were excluded.

Data collection

We reviewed electronic medical records to collect baseline characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and underlying diseases such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD) with staging, ischemic heart disease, atrial fibrillation, and stroke/transient ischemic attack.

Medication data included the use of renin–angiotensin system inhibitors (RASi), calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid antagonists, diuretics, sodium glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), statins, anti-platelets, and anticoagulants (warfarin, enoxaparin, heparin, and non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants). We also collected information on blood pressure at admission, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), mechanical ventilator use and duration, vasopressor use (norepinephrine, dobutamine, dopamine, and adrenaline), intra-aortic balloon pump use, contrast media volume, and surgical procedures. Laboratory data included complete blood count (CBC), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), electrolytes and liver function test. Imaging studies such as chest X-ray and electrocardiogram were also reviewed. The total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was calculated by at the time of CCU admission [9].

Two independent investigators reviewed and compiled the electronic medical record database. In cases of discrepancies regarding AKI etiology, both investigators assessed the diagnosis together to reach a consensus.

AKI diagnosis and staging

Patients were monitored daily for AKI until CCU discharge. AKI was diagnosed and staged based on serum creatinine and urine output criteria, following the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 guidelines [10]. The serum creatinine criterion for the diagnosis of AKI was defined as an increase in serum creatinine greater than or equal to 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h or an increase in serum creatinine greater than or equal to 1.5 times baseline or urine output less than 0.5 mL/kg per hour for at least 6 h. The lowest serum creatinine value recorded during hospitalization or within 1 month of admission was considered the baseline value. We calculated the eGFR using The 2009 CKD-EPI (Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration) creatinine equation [11]. We determined the urine output rate (mL/kg per hour) by dividing the urine output by patient body weight or we used the 24-hour cumulative urine output if available.

Two investigators independently diagnosed the etiology of AKI according to these criteria. The diagnosis of acute cardiorenal syndrome adhered to the study by Bart et al. [12], which included the following diagnostic criteria: AKI within 10 days after heart failure together with clinical heart failure of at least 2 of 3 of edema, engorged jugular vein, and evidence from imaging. Contrast-associated AKI is characterized by a decrease in kidney function that occurs within 24–72 h after the intravascular administration of iodinated contrast material [13]. All patients with AKI at Vajira hospital underwent renal ultrasound to exclude obstructive uropathy as well as urinalysis to detect evidence of glomerular disease. The decision to investigate the etiology of AKI, including performing a kidney biopsy, depended on the attending medical team’s clinical judgment. In the case of a discrepancy in the diagnosis of AKI etiology between the two investigators, they assessed the diagnosis together.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of AKI in patients admitted to the CCU. Secondary outcomes included 30-day and 90-day mortality, as well as length of stay, risk factors for AKI and KRT in CCU patients with AKI.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was estimated based on an expected incidence of AKI in CCU patients of 11.3% as reported by Tan et al. [14], with a 5% margin of error and 95% confidence level. A minimum of 155 patients was calculated to be sufficient to achieve 80% power. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, or as median and interquartile range (IQR 1–3) if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables. Survival analysis for 30- and 90-day all-cause mortality was conducted using Kaplan–Meier curves and log-rank tests. Risk factors for AKI were assessed using univariate and multivariable logistic regression. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in univariate analysis were included in multivariable models. The total SOFA score was not included in the multivariable logistic regression to avoid multicollinearity, as its components overlap with other covariates such as vasopressor use, mechanical ventilation, systolic blood pressure, and baseline kidney function. Results were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. We report the 95% confidence interval (CI) along with the OR and HR. Stata, version 15.0 STATA/IC Software, version 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA) was used to analyze all statistics.

Results

In this study, a total of 467 patients were admitted to the CCU at Vajira Hospital, Navamindhadhiraj University, between January 2019 and September 2020. Of these patients, we excluded 258 from the study due to various reasons, including 60 patients with preexisting end-stage kidney disease requiring KRT, 60 patients with noncardiac conditions leading to admission in the CCU and 138 patients with incomplete medical records. A total of 209 patients met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.

Of these patients, AKI was found in 30.6% (64 patients). Among them, 37 (57.8% of all AKI) cases were classified as AKI stage 1, 7 (10.9%) cases as stage 2, and 20 (31.3%) cases as stage 3, according to KDIGO staging.

Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the 209 patients included in the analysis. Of these, 112 (58.4%) were male, with a mean age of 68 ± 12.8 years and a mean BMI of 24.2 ± 5.0 kg/m². Ischemic heart disease was present in 69.4% of patients, while type 2 diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease were observed in 46.9% and 45.9%, respectively. The reduced left ventricular ejection fraction was noted in 92 patients (44.2%). The most common reasons for CCU admission were acute coronary syndrome (95 patients, 45.5%), acute decompensated heart failure (38, 18.2%), and cardiac arrhythmias (26, 12.4%). Patients who developed AKI were older (71.1 ± 11.5 vs. 66.7 ± 13.2 years, p = 0.022) and had lower systolic blood pressure on admission (123.1 ± 33.7 vs. 134.7 ± 29.9 mmHg, p = 0.020). Female sex (54.69% vs. 35.86%, p = 0.011), chronic kidney disease (73.4% vs. 33.8%, p < 0.001), atrial fibrillation (37.5% vs. 22.8%, p = 0.027), cerebrovascular disease (18.8% vs. 6.9%, p = 0.010), and reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (62.5% vs. 35.9%, p < 0.001) were more prevalent among AKI patients. Prior use of renin–angiotensin system inhibitors was associated with a lower AKI incidence (48.4% vs. 67.6%, p = 0.009), whereas diuretic use was more common in the AKI group (84.4% vs. 46.9%, p < 0.001). Indications for CCU admission differed significantly between groups (p = 0.023), with acute decompensated heart failure and post–cardiac arrest being more frequent among those who developed AKI.

Supportive interventions were more common in AKI patients, including mechanical ventilation (78.1% vs. 31.7%, p < 0.001), vasopressor therapy (62.5% vs. 24.1%, p < 0.001), and intra-aortic balloon pump support (15.6% vs. 1.4%, p < 0.001). In contrast, contrast exposure was not significantly associated with increased AKI risk (50% vs. 52.41%, p = 0.748). Radiographic findings, such as pulmonary congestion (84.4% vs. 39.3%, p < 0.001) and cardiomegaly (92.2% vs. 72.4%, p = 0.002), were more common in the AKI group. In addition, the mean total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score was significantly higher among patients with AKI (11.4 ± 2.7 vs. 8.1 ± 3.2, p < 0.001).

Etiology of AKI

The most common etiology of AKI in this cohort was multifactorial, observed in 27 patients (42.9%). Acute cardiorenal syndrome was the second most frequent cause, affecting 16 patients (25%). Prerenal AKI due to hypovolemia and sepsis-associated AKI were each identified in 8 patients (12.5%). Contrast-associated AKI accounted for 4 cases (6.25%), and one case (1.56%) was attributed to other causes. Most multifactorial cases involved a combination of acute cardiorenal syndrome, sepsis, and nephrotoxic medication exposure. Among these, nephrotoxic antibiotics—particularly colistin—were commonly used for infections caused by carbapenem-resistant gram-negative organisms, highlighting the rising burden of antimicrobial resistance in this setting. Only two patients underwent kidney biopsy to determine the underlying cause of AKI; both were diagnosed with acute tubular injury due to multiple contributing factors. Mortality and length of stay

At both 30 and 90 days, AKI was associated with higher all-cause mortality rates (Table 2). Mortality also increased with AKI severity (no AKI: 3.45%, AKI stage 1: 21.62%, AKI stage 2: 57.14%, AKI stage 3: 60%; χ² for trend, p < 0.001; Fig. 2). At 30 days, the overall mortality rate was 35.95% (23/64 patients) in those with AKI, compared to 1.38% (5/145 patients) in those without AKI (p < 0.001). At 90 days, the mortality rate was 43.75% (28/64 patients) in AKI patients, compared to 6.9% (10/145 patients) in non-AKI patients (p < 0.001). Mortality also increased with AKI severity at 90 days (no AKI: 6.89%, AKI stage 1: 27.02%, AKI stage 2: 71.42%, AKI stage 3: 65%; χ² for trend, p < 0.001; Fig. 2). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed significant differences in survival between non-AKI and each AKI stage (log-rank, p < 0.001; Fig. 3; Table 3).

Factors associated with AKI in the CCU

In univariate logistic regression, factors associated with AKI (p < 0.20) included older age, female sex, lower systolic blood pressure, CKD, atrial fibrillation, absence of RASi use, prior diuretic use, on mechanical ventilation, vasopressor therapy, reduced LVEF, and chest X-ray findings of pulmonary congestion and increased cardiothoracic ratio. After multivariable adjustment, the following were independently associated with AKI: CKD (adjusted OR 3.47, 95% CI 1.58–7.92, p = 0.002), reduced LVEF (OR 3.19, 95%CI 1.36–7.86, p = 0.009), on mechanical ventilation (OR 3.08, 95% CI 1.20–8.26, p = 0.021) and use of vasopressor (OR 2.84, 95% CI 1.21–6.81, p = 0.017) (Table 4).

Factors associated with KRT in the CCU

In our study, kidney replacement therapy (KRT) was required in 18 patients, accounting for 8.6% of the total cohort and 28.1% of those with AKI. Intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) was the most commonly used modality, employed in 12 cases. Four patients received continuous kidney replacement therapy (CKRT) alone, while two received both CKRT and IHD. No patients underwent acute peritoneal dialysis.

In univariate logistic regression, factors associated with the need for KRT (p < 0.20) included lower systolic blood pressure, preexisting CKD, atrial fibrillation, absence of RASi use, prior diuretic use, mechanical ventilation, vasopressor therapy, lower hemoglobin levels, and chest X-ray findings of pulmonary congestion and increased cardiothoracic ratio. After adjusting for covariates in the multivariable analysis, mechanical ventilation (adjusted OR 11.26, 95% CI 1.33–302.84, p < 0.001) and vasopressor use (OR 5.61, 95% CI 1.14–42.52, p = 0.04) remained independently associated with KRT (Table 5).

Discussion

AKI is a significant complication in critically ill patients, and it can be caused by a variety of factors, including drug toxicity, ischemia and sepsis. The incidence of AKI varies significantly among different patient populations and the risk factors for developing AKI can differ depending on the underlying disease. The incidence of AKI varies significantly based on the patient population under study—from 1% to 66% [1]. The incidence of AKI is increasing in ICU patients (around 20–50%) depending on the population and the diagnostic criteria for AKI [15]. For example, Fuhrman et al. [16] found that the incidence of AKI was 52.6% in the critical care unit. In Thailand, Srisawat et al. [17] found that the incidence of AKI in medical and surgical patients admitted to the critical care unit was 52.9% and the major etiologies of AKI were sepsis (47%) and cardiac disease (24.3%), respectively. Trongtrakul et al. [18] studied AKI in a surgical critical care unit in Vajira Hospital and found an AKI incidence of 47.3%. In their study, AKI stage 3 occurred most frequently (39.7%) and was associated with a statistically significant increase in the 28-day mortality rate (HR 7.75 [95% CI, 1.46–41.2], p = 0.02). However, data on AKI in the CCU is limited [1, 3–6, 19].

CCU provide specialized care to patients with various cardiac diseases, including acute coronary syndrome, acute decompensated heart failure and post–cardiac arrest [3, 20]. Due to factors such as reduced cardiac output, decreased renal perfusion, exposure to nephrotoxic medications and the use of contrast agents in cardiac imaging procedures, these patients are at an increased risk of developing AKI [3]. In patients with acute decompensated heart failure, the development of AKI is often related to acute cardiorenal syndrome, which represents a complex interaction between the heart and the kidneys [21]. Additionally, patients with acute coronary syndrome are at increased risk of contrast-associated AKI due to the use of iodinated contrast agents during coronary angiography. Other common causes of AKI in the CCU setting include cardiogenic shock, sepsis, over-diuresis, and exposure to nephrotoxic medications [20].

Our study found an AKI incidence of 30.62% among CCU patients—within the range reported in prior studies (11.3% to 51%) [4, 6, 14, 19, 20]. Multifactorial causes were the most common etiology, followed by cardiorenal syndrome, hypovolemia, sepsis, and contrast-associated AKI (CA-AKI). Notably, AKI from multiple causes often involved overlapping mechanisms, such as acute cardiorenal syndrome combined with sepsis and drug-induced nephrotoxicity. Sepsis-induced AKI is a particularly important contributor in LMICs, including Thailand. In our setting, common sources of sepsis included pneumonia, catheter-related bloodstream infections, and catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI). Importantly, Thailand has a high prevalence of multidrug-resistant organisms, especially carbapenem-resistant gram-negative bacteria, which often necessitates the use of nephrotoxic antibiotics such as colistin [22]. This antimicrobial challenge further increases the risk of AKI and complicates management in critically ill patients.

Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) use, vasopressor therapy, and mechanical ventilation were more frequent in the AKI group. These interventions are often required in patients with hemodynamic instability, which may contribute to renal hypoperfusion and ischemic injury. Interestingly, contrast-associated AKI (CA-AKI) accounted for only 6.25% of cases in our study, consistent with the 4.5% incidence reported in the PRESERVE trial [23]. In addition, most patients with CA-AKI in our cohort developed only stage 1 AKI, which was typically mild and reversible, suggesting a more favorable clinical course compared to other AKI etiologies.

Among all patients with AKI, AKI stage 1 occurred in 57.81%, AKI stage 2 in 10.94% and AKI stage 3 in 31.25% of cases. The incidence of KRT was 8.61% and the mortality rate at 90 days of patients with AKI was 43.75%, whereas patients without AKI had a mortality rate of 6.9%. This is consistent with previous studies, which found AKI to be associated with a higher mortality rate [5, 15, 17, 18].

Multivariable analysis identified pre-existing CKD (OR 3.47), mechanical ventilation (OR 3.08), vasopressor therapy (OR 2.84), and reduced LVEF (OR 3.19) as independent risk factors for AKI. CKD is a well-established risk factor, likely due to reduced renal reserve and impaired repair capacity [24–26]. The need for mechanical ventilation and vasopressors likely reflects hemodynamic instability and greater illness severity, contributing to pre-renal AKI and ischemic acute tubular injury. Additionally, lung–kidney crosstalk, involving gas exchange disturbances, hemodynamic changes during ventilation, and proinflammatory mediator release, may underlie ventilator-associated AKI in critically ill patients [27]. Reduced LVEF has been linked to increased AKI risk in several studies [3, 28, 29], potentially through mechanisms such as venous congestion, sympathetic overactivation, and activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [7, 21].

The association between sex and AKI remains debated. While some studies report higher AKI risk in males [30–32], others suggest increased risk in females [33, 34]. In our study, female sex was associated with AKI in univariate analysis but did not remain significant in the multivariable model.

In addition, multivariable analysis identified mechanical ventilation (OR 11.26) and vasopressor use (OR 5.61) as independent predictors of KRT requirement, both reflecting the severity of illness and the need for hemodynamic and respiratory support. The lack of statistical significance for other factors in the multivariable model may be attributable to the limited number of KRT cases in our cohort.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically explore the epidemiology, etiology, and outcomes of AKI in a CCU setting within a low- to middle-income country. Our findings highlight the substantial burden of AKI in this population and suggest that risk stratification and early intervention strategies are crucial to improving outcomes.

This study has several limitations. First, the absence of kidney biopsy in most patients limited our ability to accurately determine the etiology of acute kidney injury, particularly for conditions such as acute interstitial nephritis or glomerulonephritis. Second, although we excluded patients admitted for non-cardiac conditions, AKI etiologies remained mixed, reflecting the complex and multifactorial nature of AKI in CCU patients. Third, the relatively small sample size of KRT patients may have reduced statistical power, with wide confidence intervals observed for variables such as mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use, likely due to sparse data in these subgroups. Finally, as a single-center retrospective study, the findings may not be generalizable to other settings or populations. Further multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and improve generalizability.

Conclusions

This study highlights that AKI is a common and clinically significant complication among patients admitted to the CCU in Thailand, a middle-income country. With an incidence of 30.62%, our findings underscore the burden of AKI in cardiac-critical care settings, where multifactorial causes—especially overlapping cardiorenal syndrome, sepsis, and drug-induced nephrotoxicity—were the most frequent etiologies. Risk factors independently associated with AKI included preexisting CKD, reduced LVEF, vasopressor use, and mechanical ventilation—consistent with global data, but magnified in this context by higher baseline comorbidity and limited resources. Our findings suggest the need for region-specific strategies for early AKI detection and management in CCU settings. Future prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate these findings and guide tailored interventions in similar healthcare systems.
