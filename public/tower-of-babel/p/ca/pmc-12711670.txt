Modeling antithymocyte globulin-induced microvasculopathy using human iPSC-derived vascularized liver organoids
Authors: Shuntaro Kawamura, Yosuke Yoneyama, Norikazu Saiki, Yunheng Wu, Chiharu Moriya, Rio Ohmura, Mari Maezawa, Yoshihiro Shimada, Yicheng Wang, Kensaku Mori, Noriki Okada, Yasuharu Onishi, Yukihiro Sanada, Yuta Hirata, Yasunaru Sakuma, Takanori Takebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryAntithymocyte globulin (ATG) is a widely used immunosuppressive agent, yet its off-target vascular effects remain a clinical challenge in part due to a lack of relevant human models. Here, we uncover a biphasic mechanism of ATG-induced microvasculopathy using a humanized liver organoid platform derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs). We show that ATG triggers a rapid, complement-dependent phase of injury restricted to human iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (iLSECs), leading to C3 deposition and acute thrombosis specifically in human but not in mouse vessels. Transcriptomics reveals a delayed transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) pathway-driven proinflammatory program that coincides with neutrophil recruitment and degranulation, while pharmacological TGF-β blockade attenuates thrombosis and flow disturbance. Our findings reveal a pathogenic sequence of complement and TGF-β pathway activation and establish a translational platform for dissecting human liver-specific microvasculopathy in vivo.

Introduction

Antithymocyte globulin (ATG) is a widely used second-line immunosuppressive agent for graft rejection and graft-versus-host disease in solid organ transplantation, including the liver.1,2,3,4,5,6 The major clinical side effects include thrombocytopenia and hypercoagulability,2,7,8,9 consequences of complement activation on platelets.10 Case reports also describe acute hepatotoxicity during ATG treatment, such as severe acute transaminase and bilirubin elevation after ATG administration.11,12,13 Because the occurrence of liver injury varies with the regimen of ATG and concomitant medications, the mechanism of ATG-induced hepatotoxicity remains elusive.

Clinical studies indicate that ATG can engage endothelial activation across organs, from microvascular thrombosis to pulmonary capillary leakage14 and ischemic liver injury,11 underscoring ATG’s vascular impact. In vivo, ATG preferentially binds to post-capillary venules in both monkey and human muscles,15 suggesting that venous-derived microvascular endothelium is more susceptible to ATG-mediated damaging effects than arterial and other large-vessel endothelium. Despite the liver’s extensive venous-derived sinusoidal network, how ATG affects hepatic microvasculature has not been directly evaluated, leaving a critical gap in understanding its vascular effects in liver microenvironments.

Liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) line liver sinusoids and regulate exchanges between blood and liver tissue, while maintaining hepatic vascular homeostasis through coagulation control, immune cell recruitment, and barrier function.16 Toxic insults such as chemotherapy, irradiation, or high-dose immunosuppressants can disrupt LSECs, triggering inflammatory and thrombotic syndromes such as sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS).17,18 In particular, complement activation is a hallmark of endothelial injury that promotes platelet adhesion and coagulation.19,20,21,22

To gain further mechanistic insights, an in vivo human liver-specific vasculature is needed to capture microvasculopathy under physiologically perfused conditions, integrating human endothelium with blood flow containing platelets and immune components, including complement.23 Human liver bud organoids (HLBOs) are generated by self-organizing of human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived hepatic endoderm, mesenchyme, and endothelial progenitors, and form functional and vascularized liver-like tissue after transplantation.24,25 Incorporation of iPSC-derived LSEC (iLSEC) progenitors into HLBOs further yields liver sinusoid-like molecular and functional features in vivo, including selective uptake of low-density lipoprotein and permeability to low molecular weight dextran.26 Building on this platform, we established an intravital imaging system by transplanting HLBOs in immunodeficient mice, enabling real-time visualization of perfused human hepatic microvasculature and dynamic analysis of endothelial responses under flow, including immunothrombotic interactions. Using this system, we aimed to delineate how ATG induces microvasculopathy in a liver-specific context.

Results

ATG induces thrombosis and subsequent vascular disruption in human iLSEC-lined vessels

To develop a stable intravital imaging system for perfused human hepatic microvasculature, HLBOs incorporated with LSEC progenitors (iLSEPs)26 were transplanted under a cranial window (CW) in non-obese diabetic/severe combined immunodeficient (NOD/SCID) mice (Figure 1A). More than 1 month after transplantation, LYVE1 expression was observed in Ku80+ human cells of HLBO grafts (Figure 1B). LYVE1 expression was lower in larger-caliber vessels and higher in smaller-caliber vessels: LYVE1low vessels had an average diameter of 28.5 μm, whereas LYVE1high vessels were narrower, averaging 12.4 μm (Figures 1C and 1D). CD32B was expressed in a similar pattern: small, LYVE1high vessels showed strong CD32B expression, while large, LYVE1low vessels exhibited minimal CD32B signal (Figure 1E). The venous marker COUP-TFII was expressed in nuclei of both small and large vessels (Figure 1F). To evaluate the response of iLSEC-lined microvessels to ATG, ATG was administered intravenously into recipient mice after vascularization of transplanted HLBOs. Perfusion loss and bleeding-like appearance were observed in vascularized HLBOs from 6 h to 24 h after ATG injection. In contrast, non-immunized normal rabbit IgG (rIgG)-treated controls showed no such abnormalities (Figure 2A). Intravital microscopy revealed that ATG induced CD41+ platelet accumulation in human iLSEC-lined vessels (Figure 2B). Platelet-occupied area in human vessels increased from 0.5 to 6 h after ATG, but not in the rIgG group (Figure 2C). Platelets accumulated progressively in iLSEC-lined vessels during this phase (Video S1 and Figure S1A). Thrombi grew even with transient decreases in size (Figure S1B). In parallel, fibrin(ogen) deposition, indicative of coagulation activation, was increased in iLSEC-lined vessels with platelets 3 h after ATG (Figure 2D). Fibrin(ogen)-positive area in iLSEC-lined vessels was higher with ATG than with rIgG (Figure 2E). Platelet and fibrin(ognen) accumulation were minimal in mouse vessels after ATG administration (Figure S1C). In the ATG group, the platelet-occupied area was greater in human than in mouse vessels (Figure S1D). Surface-bound ATG and CD62P (platelet activation marker) expression were not increased on platelets in ATG-treated mice (Figure S1E). Spatial mapping and time course analysis of thrombus-associated vascular segments showed upstream dilation over time and relative downstream constriction (Figure S1F). These changes in vessel diameter coincided temporally with platelet accumulation (Figure S1G), prompting evaluation of dynamic vascular responses. To investigate the temporal relationship between thrombus formation and blood flow occlusion, we used 2,000 kDa fluorescently labeled dextran, which does not pass through intact endothelium.27 The dextran-perfused region in iLSEC-lined vessels decreased from 3 h to 24 h after ATG injection, in parallel with thrombosis progression, while extravascular dextran leakage occurred (Figure 2F). Intravascular perfusion area and extravascular dextran signal were quantified across multiple fields of view (Figure S2A). In the rIgG group, blood perfusion area in iLSEC-lined vessels remained stable from 3 to 24 h, whereas it declined markedly with ATG (Figure 2G). In this period, the perfusion area changed by only 0.15% in the rIgG group but decreased by 24.5% in the ATG group (Figure S2B). These perfusion deficits were absent in mouse vessels (Figure S2C). The extravascular dextran signal was comparable at 3 h but was markedly higher with ATG at 24 h (Figure 2H), while the intravascular dextran signal did not differ between groups at either time point (Figure S2D). In this interval, the rIgG group showed a 6.45% decrease of the extravascular dextran signal, whereas the ATG group exhibited a 12.9% increase (Figure S2E). We next assessed the change of vessel diameter and found that the mean vessel diameters reduced at 24 h compared to 3 h with ATG, whereas it did not change with rIgG (Figure S2F). At 3 h post-injection, vessel diameters positively correlated with extravascular dextran signal in the ATG group but not in the rIgG group (Figure S2G). Consistently, representative fields containing vessels with relatively larger diameters (Figure 2F) showed more pronounced dextran leakage in the ATG group than fields with smaller-caliber vessels (Figure S2H), suggesting that early subtle vascular leakage following ATG treatment preferentially occurs in vessels with relatively larger diameters, even in microvessels. These data collectively indicate that ATG leads to early thrombosis in iLSEC-lined vessels, followed by perfusion deficits with vascular disruption by 24 h.

Complement activation on human iLSECs drives early thrombosis after ATG

We next sought to examine the ATG binding to human iLSECs and subsequent complement activation. ATG localized along human vessels in HLBOs dissected at 1 h post-injection (Figures 3A and S3A), whereas no ATG accumulation was detected in mouse vessels (Figure S3B). C3 deposited on human iLSEC surfaces at 3 h post-injection (Figure 3B). Continuous imaging also showed surface-localized C3 on human iLSECs (Video S2). C3 signal in human vessel was higher with ATG than with rIgG (Figure 3C). C3 deposition was not detected in mouse vessels (Figure S3C). To assess the activation of the classical pathway, C4 deposition was examined in iLSECs exposed to ATG in the presence of normal mouse.28 Four hours after treatment, C4 deposition was promoted with ATG at 4 h (Figure 3D). C4 signal was markedly higher with ATG than with rIgG (Figure 3E). We next challenged HLBO-transplanted mice with cobra venom factor (CVF) to deplete C3,21 resulting in no apparent vessel occlusion and hemorrhage in HLBO-derived vessels compared with vehicle (Figure 3F). Consistently, CVF reduced ATG-induced C3 deposition and thrombosis (Figure 3G), with lower C3 at 6 h post-ATG injection (Figure 3H) and a smaller platelet-occupied area in iLSEC-lined vessels than with vehicle at 24 h (Figure 3I). To explore surface epitopes that mediate ATG engagement, we first evaluated the baseline expression of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) on iLSECs, given that ATG can recognize HLA among other leukocyte and endothelial antigens.29 HLA-A/B/C (HLA-I) was weakly expressed on iLSECs, whereas HLA-DR/DP/DQ (HLA class II) was undetectable (Figure S3D). To determine whether surface HLA-I contributes to ATG engagement and complement activation, an HLA-deficient (HLA-KO) iPSC line30,31 was differentiated into iLSECs and exposed for 4 h to ATG. ATG binding on HLA-KO iLSECs was comparable to that in iLSECs derived from a donor-matched wild-type (WT) iPSC line, and rabbit IgG binding was not detected in either cell line (Figure 3J). Although the ATG signal did not change between WT and HLA-KO (Figure S3E), the signal of C3b (C3 products) was modestly decreased in HLA-KO compared to WT (Figure 3K). Additionally, we investigated FcγRIIb (CD32B), which serves as one of the canonical binding sites for the Fc portion of IgG, as a potential mediator of ATG attachment.10 Over 95% of iLSECs were ATG-positive 1 h after treatment (Figure S3F), and pre-treatment with an anti-CD32B antibody reduced surface ATG signal in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S3G). These results suggest that CD32B is one of the ATG-binding targets on iLSECs, while multiple surface antigens, including HLA-I, likely mediate the complement activation.

ATG-associated hepatic vascular injury features sinusoidal thrombosis with complement activation

To explore the potential clinical relevance of ATG administration and hepatic vascular injury, we analyzed a prospective multicenter cohort dataset comprising 80 pediatric patients who underwent hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT).32 Cases were stratified based on their GVHD prophylaxis regimen, and SOS incidence was higher in patients who received ATG (40.0%) than in patients who received other immunosuppressive agents (7.02%) (Figure S3H). Additionally, a case of liver transplant rejection treated with ATG was identified (Table S1). Blood biochemistry revealed an elevation of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), accompanied by a decline in peripheral platelet counts after ATG initiation (Figure 4A). Total bilirubin levels similarly increased during this period (Figure 4B). Levels of fibrin degradation product (FDP) and prothrombin time-international normalized ratio (PT-INR) also increased, consistent with coagulopathy and activation of the fibrinolytic system (Figure 4C). Doppler ultrasound at day −10 (before ATG), day 0 (immediately after ATG), and day 98 (post-recovery) revealed a drop in portal vein flow speed and volume after ATG, with restoration at post-recovery (Figure 4D). To clarify the anatomical site of vascular injury in the same patient diagnosed as ATG-associated SOS, we performed histopathological analyses of liver biopsy specimens collected before (day −13), during (day 12–18), and after (day 100) the episode. Hepatocellular necrosis was detected in zone 3 (centrilobular region) at day 12, whereas zone 1 (periportal area) remained relatively preserved (Figure 4F). C4d (C4 products) deposition was predominantly in zone 3 at day 12 (Figure 4G). Platelet accumulation was observed primarily in zone 3 during the injury phase (day 12–18), where platelet-rich structures were present within the liver sinusoids, while portal vein regions lacked overt thrombus formation (Figure 4H). In zone 3, platelet aggregation increased during day 12–18, which resolved by day 100 (Figure 4I). At day 12, Doppler ultrasound demonstrated reduced portal vein flow speed and volume (Figure 4D). These findings indicate that thrombosis within the zone 3 sinusoids contributed to impaired hepatic perfusion during ATG-associated SOS. These observations align with our vascularized organoid model, supporting that ATG can contribute to transient but severe liver dysfunction characterized by sinusoidal thrombosis with complement activation.

iLSEC-lined microvessels are susceptible to ATG-induced thrombosis independent of complement

To evaluate the cell type-specific susceptibility of iLSECs to ATG-induced thrombosis and vascular injury, iLSECs were compared with iPSC-derived arterial endothelial cells (iAECs) differentiated from the same iPSC line.33 Transcriptomics showed venous/LSEC programs in iLSECs (NR2F2, APLNR, CD36; STAB1, LYVE1, and FCGR2B), whereas iAECs expressed higher pan-endothelial and arterial markers (PECAM1, CDH5; DLL4, HEY2, and NRP1) (Figure 5A). For in vivo comparison, HLBOs, incorporating iAEC (in place of iLSEPs) were generated and transplanted under a CW. iAEC-based HLBOs exhibited lower expression of LSEC markers (CD32B and LYVE1) in the endothelium compared to iLSEC-based HLBOs (Figure 5B). Six hours after ATG injection, both iAEC- and iLSEC-lined vessels exhibited intraluminal C3 deposition, but blood flow impairment and extravascular dextran leakage were pronounced in iLSEC-lined vessels (Figure 5C). The reduction in the blood perfusion area from −1 h to 6 h post-ATG injection was more extensive in iLSEC-lined vessels (Figure 5D). Extravascular dextran signal was higher in iLSEC than in iAEC (Figure 5E). Although smaller-caliber vessels exhibited a lower C3 signal in the lumen than larger-caliber vessels (Figure 5G), the extravascular dextran signal was higher in smaller-caliber vessels (Figure 5H). ATG induces delayed proinflammatory responses in iLSECs characterized by neutrophil recruitment and luminal degranulation

To elucidate the complement-independent effects of ATG on iLSECs, we performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of iLSECs exposed to ATG or rIgG for 4 or 24 h in serum-free conditions. Principal-component analysis showed minimal differences at 4 h but clear separation at 24 h between rIgG- and ATG-treated cells (Figure S4A). At 24 h, 322 genes were upregulated with ATG, including chemokines CCL2, CXCL1, and CXCL5 and prothrombotic mediators SPP134,35 and SERPINE1,36,37 with downregulation of the vasoprotective NOS338 (Figure 6A). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified enrichment of “overview of proinflammatory and profibrotic mediators” in ATG-treated iLSECs at 24 h compared to rIgG controls (Figure 6B). In line with transcriptomic changes, ATG treatment for 24 h increased the secretion of chemokines, including neutrophil chemoattractant CXCL139 and CCL240 (Figure S4B). ATG significantly upregulated CXCL1 secretion at concentrations ≥500 μg/mL (Figure 6C) and CCL2 at ≥150 μg/mL (Figure S4C). The secretion of PAI-1 (SERPINE1), which inhibits fibrinolysis, was also increased by ATG (Figure S4D). Consistent with a previous study,41 surface expression of classical adhesion markers (ICAM1 and SELE) remained unchanged after ATG stimulation (Figure S4E). Intravital imaging exhibited increased Ly6G+ neutrophil recruitment at 24 h post-ATG injection (Figure 6D). Counts of neutrophils are comparable between the rIgG group and the ATG at 6 h, whereas the ATG group only exhibited an increase in the count at 24 h (Figure 6E). This neutrophil recruitment was not observed in ATG-treated mice without HLBO transplantation at 3 or 24 h (Figures S5A and S5B). To assess ATG-induced vascular injury following this inflammatory phase, we evaluated early apoptosis in HLBOs at 24 h after ATG injection. Cleaved caspase-3 signal was enhanced in the human VAP1+ endothelium but minimal in the E-cadherin+ parenchyma of the HLBO graft from ATG-treated mice (Figure S5C). In human VAP1+ areas, this apoptotic signal was higher with ATG than with rIgG, whereas E-cadherin+ areas were unchanged (Figure S5D). Furthermore, plasma ALT levels at 24 h post-injection did not differ between sham, rIgG-, and ATG-treated mice with HLBO-transplantation (Figure S5E). These data suggest that cell injury at 24 h after ATG was restricted to human iLSEC-lined vessels, rather than affecting adjacent parenchymal cells and the host mouse liver.

To probe mediators of cellular injury selectively in iLSEC-lined vessels, we further characterized recruited neutrophils, focusing on 6 h post-injection, when the blood flow is relatively intact. Neutrophil elastase (NE), a marker of neutrophil degranulation and NETosis that promotes platelet aggregation and cell injury,42 was detected adjacent to neutrophils and deposited luminally along iLSEC-lined vessels after ATG (Figure 6F). In the rIgG group, the NE signal was minimal, and neutrophils were either flowing within the lumen or migrating in the perivascular space (Video S3). In contrast, luminally adherent neutrophils colocalized with NE in the ATG group (Video S4). Luminal NE signal was higher in the ATG group than in rIgG group (Figure 6G). An increase in rabbit IgG signal was detected on peripheral-blood neutrophils collected from the ATG-treated mouse, but surface expression of CD11b (neutrophil activation marker) and CD63 (degranulation marker43) did not change compared to PBS- or rIgG-treated controls (Figure S5F). Consistent with these in vivo results, liver histology from the ATG-associated SOS patient showed extracellular NE staining in zone 3 at 12 and 18 days after ATG administration (Figure 6H). In contrast, this NETosis-like pattern was absent in zone 1 (Figure S5G). NE+ neutrophils increased at day 18 and declined by day 100 (recovery-phase) (Figure 6I). These data suggest that luminally adherent neutrophils undergo degranulation on vascularized iLSECs after ATG, thereby contributing to vascular injury and propagation of sinusoidal thrombosis.

To further test the causative contribution of TGF-β signaling, iLSECs were co-treated with the TGF-β receptor inhibitor SB431542. SB431542 dose-dependently suppressed the secretion of PAI-1 (Figure 7G), CXCL1 (Figure 7H), and CCL2 (Figure S6A), while no effect was observed under rIgG conditions. ATG-induced CXCL1 secretion in tube-formed iLSECs was also suppressed by SB431542 (Figure S6B). SB431542 was then administered to HLBO-transplanted mice, and intravital microscopy focused on smaller-caliber vessels (diameter <50 μm). In the SB431542-treated group, ATG-induced neutrophil recruitment and dextran leakage were not suppressed (Figure 7I). Neutrophil counts (Figure S6C) and extravascular dextran signal (Figure S6D) did not change at 6 and 24 h post-ATG injection. By contrast, thrombosis and blood flow disruption were attenuated with SB431542 (Figure 7I). Platelet-occupied area was decreased with SB431542 than with vehicle at 24 h (Figure 7J). The reduction in blood perfusion from −1 h to 6 h was mitigated in the SB431542-treated group (Figure 7K). Together, these findings suggest that ATG augments the thrombotic responses of iLSEC-lined vessels via TGF-β pathway activation.

Discussion

Our study reveals a biphasic sequence in which ATG first induces complement-dependent thrombosis, followed by a delayed inflammatory phase characterized by TGF-β pathway activation in hepatic microvessels. In the acute phase, ATG triggered C3 deposition and platelet aggregation selectively in human iLSECs, not in murine vessels. C3 depletion effectively prevented platelet accumulation and vascular occlusion in our humanized vasculature model, underscoring the essential role of C3 in ATG-mediated thrombosis. This endothelial response is consistent with the clinical observation that ATG binds to endothelium.15 ATG also induced C4 deposition on iLSECs, and the liver from the ATG-associated SOS patient showed C4d deposition after the liver injury. Cumpelik et al. demonstrated that ATG induces platelet activation through the complement classical pathway,10 supporting a model in which complement classical pathway activation at the endothelial interface initiates a thromboinflammatory cascade. We further show that ATG amplified the TGF-β pathway-mediated prothrombotic and proinflammatory program in iLSECs by 24 h. In line with this, Xu et al. demonstrated that LSEC-reactive autoantibodies can trigger the secretion of CCL2 and TGF-β in LSECs, leading to capillarization and inflammation in autoimmune liver injury.53 Importantly, TGF-β pathway activity in iLSECs was not only inducible by ATG but also detectable at baseline, indicating that TGF-β pathway contributes to iLSEC susceptibility to ATG-induced endothelial dysfunction.

Prior studies in the rodent model of SOS implicated myeloid infiltration and activation in endothelial injury,54,55 but the spatiotemporal sequence of events remained undefined. In this study, we observed a sequence in which C3 deposition on human iLSECs preceded degranulation of luminally adherent neutrophils at 6 h post-ATG, with iLSEC injury at 24 h. While direct causality could not be established, the vascular injury mediated by neutrophils attached with C3/C4 was consistent with a study by Stark et al. that C3/C4-opsonized platelets trigger NETosis via CR2 in a murine deep vein thrombosis model.56 Moreover, the terminal complement complex (membrane attack complex [MAC]) can induce intercellular gap formation in endothelial cells.57 In parallel, Liu et al. reported that endothelial permeability can be exacerbated by NETosis of neutrophils bearing MAC, which are acquired via transfer from MAC-decorated endothelial cells.58 However, in NOD/SCID mice, we are unable to fully account for MAC contribution due to a loss-of-function mutation in the C5 gene.59 Together, our findings indicate that ATG induces C3 deposition followed by neutrophil activation within the human hepatic microvasculature, highlighting the immunothrombotic nature of SOS-like pathology.

Our intravital imaging system enabled real-time observation of human vessels in vivo, capturing sequential vascular events after ATG administration. We resolved that platelets accumulate within 6 h, followed by endothelial disruption with neutrophil by 24 h after ATG injection. Our findings collectively highlight the value of our preclinical model in dissecting the temporal dynamics and cellular specificity of ATG-induced microvasculopathy. While abdominal window with orthotopic transplantation would provide a more native microenvironment, such approaches are highly invasive and currently lack the reproducibility and optical stability for fine-scale dynamic analyses.23 In contrast, CW transplantation allowed us to perform repeated, long-term intravital imaging of the same microvascular fields without motion artifacts from respiration or peristalsis.24,25 This stability was essential for resolving the process of immunothrombosis within the same vascular segment. The use of engineered human blood vessels in this platform is uniquely poised to interrogate the organ-specific vascular vulnerabilities triggered by the perturbagens in patient-relevant contexts.

In summary, our findings uncover the direct mode of action of ATG on microvascular endothelium beyond its immunosuppressive function. Our preclinical investigational strategy, coupled with intravital imaging, offers a powerful translational platform for studying human liver-specific vascular injury and exploring potential therapeutic targets. Further investigation is warranted to validate these findings in clinical settings and to explore strategies to mitigate endothelial complications associated with ATG therapy.

Limitations of the study

First, although our vascularized liver organoid model enabled high-resolution imaging of human-derived endothelial responses in vivo, the contribution of the iPSC-derived multicellular components, especially hepatocytes, has not been fully validated. Specifically, CW transplantation lacks the native hepatic microenvironment and portal venous architecture present in orthotopic settings. Further exploration of hepatocyte-derived complements and pro-inflammatory proteins will be necessary to more precisely model liver-specific endothelial dysfunctions. Second, the use of NOD/SCID mice, which lack functional T cells, may not fully capture the potential contribution of ATG-mediated T cell injury to the observed vascular pathology. As ATG primarily targets human T cells, this limitation should be considered when applying our findings to immunocompetent settings. Finally, although the clinical cohort analysis revealed a higher incidence of SOS in ATG-treated pediatric HSCT patients, the small sample size and potential confounding factors limit definitive interpretation. These clinical observations should thus be considered exploratory and require validation in larger, well-controlled patient populations.

Ethics approval and clinical samples

The human specimen study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All experiments using human iPSCs and clinical specimens were performed in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Science Tokyo (G2019–003, M2021-162) and Jichi Medical University (20-003).

Clinical data and specimens from a liver transplant recipient who developed SOS following ATG administration were obtained with written informed consent and institutional ethical approval at Jichi Medical University. The patient was a 20-year-old male who underwent living-donor liver transplantation due to biliary atresia and refractory cholangitis. Preoperative assessments indicated an MELD (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease) score of 12 and a Child–Pugh score of 7. The living donor was his 43-year-old mother, and their ABO blood types were compatible. A right-lobe graft was used for transplantation. Tissue and blood samples were collected and analyzed for research purposes under approved clinical protocols.

hiPSCs

HiPSC lines 1383D6, 625A4, and 511-3E were obtained from the Center for iPS Cell Research and Application (CiRA), Kyoto University. The 511-3E line, which expresses mCherry introduced using the CRISPR/Cas9 system, was derived from 201B7.61,62 The 625A4 line was established by introducing GFP and luciferase into the 1383D6 line through a non-viral method.63,64 An HLA-deficient (HLA-A/B/CIITA −/−, HLA-KO) iPSC line (19A77) and the parental cell line (19A76) were provided by CiRA Foundation (CiRA F, Japan). The 19A77 line was established from the Ff-XT28s05_cont iPSC line (19A76), which is HLA homozygous and derived from a male donor (DRXT) with the third most frequent HLA haplotype in Japan, by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated disruption of the HLA-A, HLA-B, and CIITA (class-II transactivator) genes.30,31 All hiPSC lines used in this study were confirmed to have normal karyotypes. All cell lines were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination, and all tests were negative throughout the study. hiPSCs were maintained in StemFit AK02N medium (Ajinomoto, Tokyo, Japan) on culture dishes coated with laminin-511 E8 fragment (iMatrix-511; Nippi, Tokyo, Japan).

Method details

Induced liver sinusoidal endothelial progenitor induction

ILSEP differentiation was established as previously described.26,64,65 Briefly, undifferentiated PSC colonies were first disseminated onto laminin 511 E8-fragment-coated 10cm dishes in StemFit AK02N with 10 μM ROCK inhibitor (Y-27632; CultureSure, 034–24024) at a density of 300–600 clusters (depending on the cell line). When an individual colony grew to approximately 700–800 μm in diameter, differentiation was started by replacing it with Essential 8 medium (Gibco) containing 80 ng/mL BMP4 (R&D, 314-BP), 80 ng/mL VEGF-A (Invitrogen, PHC9393), and 2–4 μM CHIR99021 (Cayman, 13122) for 2 days. On day 2, a micropatterned colony including the Primitive Streak and Yolk Sac Mesoderm was formed, and the medium was replaced with Essential 6 medium (Gibco) containing 80 ng/mL VEGF-A, 50 ng/mL SCF (R&D, 255-SC), 25 ng/mL bFGF (FUJIFILM Wako, 060–04543), and 2 μM TGFb inhibitor SB431542 (FUJIFILM Wako, 033–24631) for hemangioblast induction. On day 4, the medium was replaced with StemPro-34 serum-free medium (Gibco) containing 2 mM glutamax (Invitrogen), 50 ng/mL VEGF-A, 50 ng/mL SCF, 10 ng/mL Flt-3 ligand (Peprotech, 300-19), 10 ng/mL IL-3 (R&D, 203-IL), and 5 ng/mL TPO (R&D, 288-TPN) for transition to hemogenic endothelial lineage. On day 6, CD34+ endothelial cells were formed at the outer edge of colony and the medium was replaced with StemPro-34 containing SCF (50 ng/mL), IL-3 (10 ng/mL), TPO (50 ng/mL), Flt-3 ligand (10 ng/mL), IL-6 (10 ng/mL), IL-11 (5 ng/mL), IGF-1 (25 ng/mL) and EPO (2 units/mL) for LSEP induction.

Liver sinusoidal-like endothelial cell (iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial cell) induction

For sinusoidal-like endothelium induction, on day 8, adherent CD34+ iLSEPs were dissociated and stained with CD34 MicroBead Kit (Milteny Biotec). After staining, cells were sorted using QuadroMACS Separator with LS Columns (Milteny Biotec), then sorted CD34+ cells were disseminated onto 12-well plate or 24-well plate coated with Matrigel Growth Factor reduced (Corning) at 1:50 dilution and cultured in s inusoidal endothelial differentiation media (SEDM) consisting of a mixture of endothelial cell growth medium (EGM, Lonza) and hepatocyte culture media (HCM, Lonza) at rate of 1:1 (EGM, HCM) with 2.5% FBS Gold (MP Biomedicals, 2916554), 10 μM Y-27632 and 5 ng/mL VEGF-A, and 10 ng/mL costatin M (OSM) (R&D, 295-OM) at a density of 4.4 × 104 cells/cm2. After 24 h, the medium was replaced by the same differentiation medium without Y-27632. The cells were cultured at 37°C, with 5% CO2 and 5% O2.

Arterial-like endothelial cell (iPSC-derived arterial endothelial cell) induction

For differentiation toward iAEC, the differentiation protocol described33 was modified as follows. Briefly, undifferentiated PSCs were first disseminated onto laminin 511 E8-fragment-coated 10cm dishes in StemFit AK02N with 10 μM Y-27632 at a density of 10,000 cells/cm2. On day 1, the medium was changed to DMEM/F-12 (Invitrogen) with 1% Glutamax, 1% B27 Supplement (Invitrogen), 25 ng/mL BMP-4, and 8 μM CHIR99021 to induce mesoderm for 3 days. On day 4, the medium was changed to StemPro-34 serum-free medium supplemented with 2 μM Forskolin (Cayman, 11018) and 200 ng/mL VEGF-A to induce differentiation into endothelial-like cells at 37°C with 5% CO2 for 3 days. On day 7, cell aggregates were removed by PBS wash, and endothelial cells were passaged on 10cm dishes coated with 1 μg/cm2 Fibronectin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 33016-015) at a density of 50,000 cells/cm2 in arterial endothelial expansion medium (AEEM) consisting of StemPro-34 supplemented with 50 ng/mL VEGF-A. AEEM was replaced every other day.

Formation of Human liver bud organoid by inverted multilayered air-liquid interface 3D (IMALI) culture

Directed differentiation methods for liver bud cells, including hepatic endoderm (iHE) and septum mesenchymal cells (iSTM), are described previously.33 The multilayer gradient gel culture system, as previously described26,66,67 was modified as follows. Hanging cell culture inserts (polyethylene terephthalate membrane, pore size of 8.0 μm, Millipore) were placed upside-down in a sterile plastic box. For a multilayering gel technique, Matrigel diluted 1:1 with HLBO differentiation media containing a mixture of EGM and HCM at a rate of 1:1 (EGM, HCM) with 2.5% FBS Gold, 0.1 mM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, D2915), 10 ng/mL OSM, and 20 ng/mL HGF (STEMCELL Technologies, ST-78019) were sequentially applied to the bottom membrane surface of the insert. First, to construct the gel basement, a 5 μL empty gel drop was applied, and the gel drop was solidified after 15 min incubation at 37°C. Next, the first cell layer consisted of a 3 μL drop with day-10 liver bud cells and CD34+ purified day-7 iLSEP or day-10 iAEC, at a total cellular concentration of 44 million cells/mL and a ratio of 10:7:1 (iHE, iLSEP/iAEC, iSTM) was placed on top of the previous drop. The gelation process was accomplished after 15 min of incubation at 37°C. Finally, the second cell layer consisted of an 8 μL drop with iLSEP or iAEC and iSTM at a final cellular concentration of 16.5 million cells/mL, with a ratio of 7:1 (iLSEP/iAEC, iSTM). This second cell layer was placed on top of the previous two drops. The gelation process was concluded after 20 min at 37°C. Matrigel was always kept on ice to avoid undesired solidification. An inverted hanging cell culture insert with a multilayered gel-embedded cell was placed in an Ultra-Low attachment 6-well plate, and 2 mL of culture medium was added to each well.

Intravital microscopy of human vascularized Human liver bud organoids under a cranial window

The CW model was established as previously described.25,68 Briefly, both male and female, 8- to 14-week-old mice were used for CW preparation. After the mice were anesthetized with a mixture of three anesthetic agents, the skull was exposed and drilled to a 7-mm diameter circular shape using a micro drill (Ideal). After the hollowed skull was removed, the dura mater was gently removed by ultra-thin forceps from the surface of the brain cortex. The exposed cortex was covered with a circular 7-mm coverslip (Matsunami) fixed with a biocompatible glue, consisting of a mixture of a cyanoacrylate glue and bone cement. 1 week after CW preparation, HLBO transplantation was performed. After the coverslip was carefully removed with an 18G needle, three HLBOs (day 3) containing fluorescent protein (GFP or mCherry) expressed in iLSEC/iAEC were placed on the brain surface, evenly spaced, and then covered with the glass again. Macroscopic monitoring was conducted regularly until the graft exhibited apparent and stable vascularization of human vessels.

4-7 weeks after the HLBO transplantation, intravital microscopy experiments were performed. Both sexes were included for all imaging sessions. Mice were anesthetized, and fluorescent tracers were administered via tail vein injection. Tetramethylrhodamine- (2,000,000 MW; Thermo Fisher Scientific, D7139) or FITC-conjugated dextran (2,000,000 MW; Sigma, FD2000S) was used to visualize mouse blood flow. Platelets were labeled with Alexa Fluor (AF) 647-conjugated anti-mouse Integrin alpha 2b/CD41 antibody (R&D Systems, FAB41181R). Fibrin deposition was visualized using AF647-conjugated fibrinogen (Invitrogen, F35200), and complement activation was assessed using Brilliant Violet 421-conjugated anti-mouse C3/C3b/iC3b antibody (BD Biosciences, 752718). Neutrophils were stained using BV421-conjugated anti-mouse Ly6G antibody (BioLegend, 127628). NE localization was detected using AF 647-conjugated NE monoclonal antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 79565). Following baseline imaging, antithymocyte globulin (ATG; Sanofi, 4912095) or an equivalent dose of non-immunized normal rabbit IgG (rIgG; FUJIFILM Wako, 148–09551) was administered via tail vein injection. We administered a dose of ATG at 1.5 mg/kg body weight, which falls within the clinically relevant range based on prior therapeutic protocols for liver transplantation.5,6 rrIgG was used as a control. Confocal imaging was performed continuously for 0.5 h post-injection under anesthesia. The mice were then allowed to recover, and follow-up imaging was conducted at 3 or 6 h and 24 h post-injection. Before each imaging session, dextran was re-administered via tail vein injection. To assess the role of complement activation in ATG-induced vascular responses, Cobra venom factor (CVF; 2 mg/kg body weight; Quidel, A600) was administered intraperitoneally at 24 h before and 5 h after ATG injection for systemic complement depletion. For TGFβ inhibition experiments, mice received intraperitoneal injections of the TGFβ receptor inhibitor SB431542 (2.0 mg/kg body weight; FUJIFILM Wako, 033–24631) or vehicle control (DMSO) at 1 h before and again at 4 h and 8 h after ATG administration. SB431542 and DMSO were diluted in PBS with 30% PEG-300 (Selleck, S6704) before administration. Imaging was performed using an FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus) or an AX-R confocal microscope with a high-speed resonant scanning system (Nikon). The excitation wavelength and laser power were optimized for each fluorescent probe in every experimental setting.

Analysis of intravital microscopy images

Intravital confocal microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji (NIH). All images were converted to 8-bit before quantification, and Gaussian denoising was applied when necessary to reduce background noise. Quantification was performed on maximum intensity projection images generated from z stack acquisitions under comparable laser and acquisition settings across all samples. Image quantification and plotting were performed using Python (v3.10.1) with NumPy, OpenCV, and Matplotlib libraries. Data were averaged across ROIs per field and per animal as applicable, and statistical analysis was performed as described in each figure legend and the Quantification and Statistical Analysis section.

For quantification of platelet and fibrin/fibrinogen area in vessels, at least 10 individual vessel segments (≥50 μm in length) were segmented manually as ROIs per view field. The percentage of the CD41+ or fibrin/fibrinogen+ area within each segmented vessel ROI was calculated, and each ROI was treated as an individual data point. Thrombosis was also evaluated by calculating the CD41+ platelet area, corrected for the GFP+ or mCherry+ vessel region, within the view field. For blood perfusion analysis in human vessels, perfusion rate was defined as the percentage of mCherry+ vessel area occupied by dextran signal. In mouse vessels, the perfusion rate was calculated as the proportion of the total field area occupied by the dextran signal. Vascular permeability was assessed by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of extravascular 2,000 kDa dextran in extravascular regions. Vessel diameter was measured as the distance between one vessel wall and another, which was selected manually. The mean vessel diameter of the view field was calculated based on more than 10 ROIs. For C3 and NE deposition, the MFI of C3b/iC3b and NE signal was quantified within the GFP+/mCherry+ vessel area. Neutrophil infiltration was quantified by counting Ly6G+ cells in each view field.

Plasma alanine aminotransferase measurement

Mouse peripheral blood was collected from HLBO-transplanted mice 24 h after intravenous injection of rIgG or ATG, or from sham-operated mice, via the inferior vena cava into a heparinized syringe. Plasma was separated by centrifugation and analyzed using a DRI-CHEM NX500 clinical chemistry analyzer (FUJIFILM, Japan) with DRI-CHEM slide GPT/ALT-PIII (FUJIFILM).

Processing of mouse whole blood for flow cytometry

Peripheral blood was collected from male non-transplanted NOD/SCID mice (8–12 weeks old) 3 h after intravenous injection of PBS, rIgG, or ATG (1.5 mg/kg body weight) via cardiac puncture into a syringe containing 3.8% sodium citrate (1/10 volume of collected blood). For platelet analysis, blood was processed at room temperature (RT) to obtain platelet-rich plasma (PRP). Whole blood was centrifuged at 200 × g for 10 min, and the PRP supernatant was transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL tube. The residual blood fraction was mixed with an equal volume of Ca2+-free Tyrode’s buffer and centrifuged again (200 × g, 10 min) to collect additional PRP, which was pooled with the first fraction. Prostacyclin (Cayman; 18220) was added to the pooled PRP at a final concentration of 0.1 μg/mL, followed by centrifugation at 1,000 × g for 10 min. The platelet pellet was resuspended in Ca2+-free Tyrode’s buffer containing 0.1 μg/mL prostacyclin. PRP samples were diluted in FACS buffer (PBS containing 2% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM EDTA) for flow cytometry analysis of platelets. The residual blood fraction after PRP removal was kept on ice and subjected to RBC lysis using chilled 1× RBC Lysis Buffer (Invitrogen, 00-4333-57). The final cell pellet containing leukocytes was resuspended in cold FACS buffer for flow cytometry analysis of the neutrophils.

Whole-Mount immunostaining and optical clearing of in vitro cultured Human liver bud organoids

Day 18 HLBOs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 2 h at 4°C. Immunofluorescent staining and optical clearing were performed by using SCALEVIEW-S (FUJIFILM Wako, 299–79901) according to the AbScale protocol supplied by the manufacturer, with minor modifications. Briefly, samples were sequentially incubated in Sca/eS0–S3 solutions at 37°C for 30 min each, followed by de-scaling in PBS at 4°C for at least 24 h. HLBOs were blocked with 1% donkey serum in PBS for 2 h at RT, then incubated with primary antibodies in AbScale solution (0.33 M urea, 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS) overnight at 4°C. Anti-CD32B (abcam, ab151497; 1/500), anti-LYVE1 (R&D, AF2089; 1/100) were used as primary antibodies. After washing in AbScale solution, samples were incubated overnight at 4°C with species-specific secondary antibodies in AbScale solution containing DAPI (DOJINDO, 340–07971; 1:1000). HLBOs were post-fixed in 4% PFA/PBS for 1 h at RT, washed in PBS, and cleared in Sca/eS4 at 37°C for 2 h. Cleared samples were mounted in Scal/eS4, and images were acquired using LSM 880 with Airy scan (Zeiss).

Whole-Mount immunostaining and optical clearing of CW-transplanted Human liver bud organoid grafts

Mice were anesthetized using a combination of three anesthetic agents, after which either rIgG or ATG was administered via tail vein injection. To visualize platelets and endothelial components, the following fluorescently labeled antibodies were intravenously injected: BV421 anti-mouse CD41 (BioLegend, 133912) for platelet labeling, AF488 anti-human CD31 (BioLegend, 303110) for human iLSEC identification, and AF647 anti-mouse CD31 (BioLegend, 102516) for mouse endothelial visualization. 1 h after antibody and ATG/rIgG injection, the CW cover glass was carefully removed, and the brain tissue containing the transplanted HLBOs was excised. The collected tissues were immediately fixed in 4% PFA in PBS and stored at 4°C for 3–4 days. Tissue clearing and immunofluorescence staining were performed using SCALEVIEW-A2 (FUJIFILM Wako, 193–18455) according to the AbScale protocol provided by the manufacturer. To detect the localization of rIgG or ATG, samples were further stained with AF555 anti-Rabbit IgG (Invitrogen, A31572). Following the clearing process, whole-mount imaging was conducted using an AX-R confocal microscope.

Immunohistochemistry in frozen sections from CW-transplanted Human liver bud organoid grafts

CW-transplanted HLBO grafts, which were harvested 46 days after transplantation, were fixed in 4% PFA and embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetek). Cryosections (10 μm thickness) were prepared using a CM3050 S cryostat (Leica Microsystems) and mounted on MAS-coated slides (Matsunami). Sections were air-dried for 30 min at RT and washed with PBS and blocked in PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 and 5% normal donkey serum (Millipore) for 1 h at RT. Primary antibodies used included anti-LYVE1 (R&D Systems, AF2089; 1/500), anti-CD31 (abcam, ab28364; 1/50), anti-CD32B (abcam, ab151497; 1/500), and anti-COUP-TFII (Novus Biologicals, PP-H7147-00; 1/100). Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C in blocking solution. After three washes with PBS-T, sections were incubated for 1 h at RT with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated with AF488, AF555, or AF647 (Invitrogen; 1/1000) and DAPI for nuclear counterstaining. Fluorescence images were acquired using LSM 880 with Airy scan or NanoZoomer (Hamamatsu Photonics). Quantification of vessel diameter was performed using ImageJ/Fiji.

Immunohistochemistry in FFPE sections from CW-transplanted Human liver bud organoid grafts

CW-transplanted HLBO grafts were harvested after 24 h post-injection of rIgG or ATG and fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4°C. Samples were processed for paraffin embedding and cut into sections (4 μm thickness) on glass slides. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by antigen retrieval in HistoVT One (Nacalai Tesque, 06380-05; pH 7.0) at 90°C for 20 min. After cooling to RT, sections were blocked in PBS containing 0.25% Triton X-100 and 5% normal donkey serum for 1 h. Serial sections were processed for dual immunofluorescence staining with anti-cleaved caspase-3 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 9664; 1/200) together with either anti-human VAP1 antibody (RayBiotech, 119–13209; 1/400) or anti–E-cadherin (R&D, AF748; 1/40). Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4°C in blocking solution. After three washes with TBS-T, sections were incubated for 1 h at RT with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated with AF488, AF555, or AF647 and DAPI. Images were acquired using an LSM 880. Quantification of cleaved caspase-3 mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was performed separately for VAP1+ and E-cadherin+ regions using ImageJ/Fiji on representative fields.

H&E staining and immunohistochemistry of clinical FFPE liver tissue

Paraffin-embedded liver tissue sections (4 μm thickness) were deparaffinized and rehydrated before staining. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed using standard protocols to assess histopathological changes.

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), antigen retrieval was performed using G-Activate pH9 solution (Genostaff, ARSE9-01), followed by blocking with G-Block (Genostaff; GB-01). Sections were then incubated with one of the following primary antibodies: anti-CD41 (abcam, ab134131; 1:2500) or anti-neutrophil elastase (NE) (R&D Systems, MAB91671-100; 1:100), each applied to separate slides. For CD41 staining, sections were treated with biotin-conjugated anti-rabbit Ig (Dako, E0432), followed by incubation with streptavidin-labeled peroxidase (Nichirei, 426062). Signal detection was performed using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB) solution, and the specimen was counterstained with hematoxylin. For NE staining, after incubation with the mouse primary antibody, slides were washed and treated with SignalStain Boost IHC Detection Reagent (HRP, Mouse; Cell Signaling Technology, 8125S). Color development was achieved using SignalStain DAB Substrate Kit (Cell Signaling Technology; 8059), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining.

Sections were imaged using a BZ-X fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE). Negative controls were prepared by substituting the primary antibody with normal rabbit IgG (Dako). Quantification of CD41-positive area and NE-positive cell counts was conducted using high-power field images (×20) by ImageJ/Fiji. Staining of C4d (BIOMEDICA, BI-RC4D) was performed on pre-stained specimens provided by Jichi Medical University.

Complement deposition assay of monolayer-cultured iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial cells

ILSECs were subjected to ATG treatment on day 15 of differentiation, 7 days after MACS-based reseeding on day 8. Cells were incubated with either ATG or rIgG (500 μg/mL) in culture medium supplemented with 10% normal mouse serum (FUJIFILM Wako, 146–06551) or normal human serum (Biopredic, SER018050) for 4 h at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Both WT and HLA-KO iLSECs were analyzed in parallel under identical stimulation conditions. After the reactions, cells were fixed with 4% PFA solution. Rabbit IgG binding and the deposition of mouse C4 or human C3b on the cell surface were assessed using immunocytochemistry.

Antithymocyte globulin treatment in monolayer-cultured iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial cells

ILSECs were used between days 15 and 21 of differentiation (i.e., 7–13 days after magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS)-based reseeding on day 8). During ATG stimulation, cells were maintained in SEDM as described in the above section. Cells were stimulated with ATG or rIgG at concentrations and durations appropriate to each downstream assay. For bulk RNA sequencing, iLSECs were treated with 100 μg/mL of ATG or rIgG for either 4 or 24 h. For other assays (e.g., multiplex chemokine assays, ELISA, and flow cytometry), cells were treated with 50 or 500 μg/mL rIgG or ATG for 24 h. Dose–response experiments were conducted using a concentration range of 5–500 μg/mL. To investigate the involvement of TGFβ signaling, iLSECs were treated for 24 h with rIgG or ATG (100 μg/mL) in the presence of the TGFβ receptor inhibitor SB431542 (FUJIFILM Wako, 033–24631) at final concentrations of 1 or 10 μM. After stimulation, cell culture supernatants or cells were collected and subjected to downstream analyses as described in the respective assay sections. For CD32B blocking experiments, iLSECs were pre-incubated for 30 min at 37°C with either a control antibody (25 μg/mL; Santa Cruz, sc-2028) or a blocking anti-CD32B antibody (5 or 25 μg/mL; NSJ Bioreagents, R34966) in SEDM. Cells were then stimulated with ATG (500 μg/mL) for 1 h under standard culture conditions.

Antithymocyte globulin treatment in tube-formed iPSC-derived liver sinusoidal endothelial cells and iPSC-derived arterial endothelial cells

For tube formation, day 8 iLSEPs and day 11 iAECs were subjected to CD34+ MACS and reseeded onto 48-well plates pre-coated with 80 μL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel at a density of 80,000 cells/well in their respective differentiation media (AEEM or SEDM) supplemented with 10 μM Y-27632. Cultures were maintained at 37°C, 5% CO2. On day 1 after reseeding, half of the medium was replaced with ROCK inhibitor-free medium, and from day 2 onward, complete medium exchange was performed daily without ROCK inhibitor.

For ATG stimulation, tube-formed cells were cultured in EGM supplemented with 5 ng/mL VEGF. For direct comparison between iLSECs and iAECs, cultures were treated for 24 h with either rIgG or ATG (500 μg/mL). For TGFβ inhibition experiments in iLSECs, cells were stimulated for 24 h with ATG (100 μg/mL) in the presence of SB431542 (10 μM), added simultaneously at the start of treatment. After stimulation, supernatants were collected for ELISA.

RNA isolation and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 2D-monolayer cultured iAECs and iLSECs using NucleoSpin RNA Plus XS (MACHEREY-NAGEL, 740990). A cDNA library was generated from 10 ng of total RNA, using a SuperScript VILO cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11754050). Amplification, primer digestion, and adapter ligation were performed using an Ion AmpliSeq Transcriptome Human Gene Expression Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A31446). The cDNA library was purified using Agencourt AM-Pure XP Reagent (Beckman Coulter, BC-A63880) and quantified using the Ion Library TaqMan Quantitation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4468802). It was then diluted to 75 pM with water and pooled in equal amounts. Eight samples per pool were sequenced simultaneously using an Ion 540 Chip Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A27766) with the Ion Torrent S5 XL (Life Technologies) and Ion Chef instrument systems (Life Technologies) and the Ion 540 Kit Chef (Life Technologies, A30011). All procedures were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Raw sequencing reads obtained from the Ion Torrent platform were initially processed using the ampliSeqRNA plugin (hg19 AmpliSeq Transcriptome ERCC v1) implemented in Torrent Suite Software v5.2.1 to generate gene-level count data aligned to the hg19 reference genome.

Bulk RNA-seq data analysis

For the analysis comparing day 21 iAECs, day 21 iLSEC, and primary LSECs, RNA-seq data for iAECs and iLSECs generated in this study were combined with publicly available primary LSEC datasets deposited in GEO (accession numbers GSE217649 and GSE152447). All samples were processed using the same RNA isolation and sequencing protocol (Ion Torrent S5 XL platform). For this dataset, read counts were normalized using reads per million.

For the analysis comparing iLSECs treated with vehicle (0 h), rIgG (4 h, 24 h), and ATG (4 h, 24 h), differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (version 1.44.0) in R (version 4.4.1), employing the median of ratios method for normalization.69 Genes with |log2 fold change| > 0.5 and adjusted p-value <0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing) were considered significantly differentially expressed.

For both datasets, normalized expression values were transformed into z-scores and plotted using heatmaps generated with the pheatmap package for visualization purposes. Unsupervised clustering of genes and samples was conducted using correlation distance and the average linkage method. Over-representation analysis and GSEA were performed using the clusterProfiler package. Gene sets were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) via the msigdbr package, including Gene Ontology and WikiPathways collections. Enrichment plots were generated using the enrichplot and ggplot2 packages.

Multiplex chemokine assay and ELISA

Chemokine concentrations in the culture supernatants of iLSECs were measured using the LEGENDplex Human Proinflammatory Chemokine Panel 1 (13-plex; BioLegend, 740984), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences), and cytokine levels were quantified using LEGENDplex Data Analysis Software.

For the individual quantification of specific soluble factors, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) were performed using commercially available kits for CCL2 (R&D, DCP00), CXCL1 (Proteintech, KE00133), and PAI-1 (Abcam, ab269373). All assays were conducted according to the manufacturers’ protocols, and optical densities were measured using a Luminex 200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Immunocytochemistry

Fixed cells were blocked in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 and 3% BSA, and the primary antibody was added and incubated at 4°C overnight. The next day, wells were washed with PBS and incubated with species-specific secondary antibodies conjugated with AF488, AF555, or AF647 (Invitrogen), and Hoechest 33342 (DOJINDO, 346–07951) or DAPI in blocking solution at RT for 1 h, followed by 3 times washes with PBS. For the complement deposition assay, anti-mouse C4 antibody (Invitrogen, MA1-40047: 1/50) or anti-C3b/iC3b antibody (Hycult Biotech, HM2286; 1/100) was used for primary labeling, and AF647-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Invitrogen, A31573) was used for secondary labeling. For the characterization of tube-formed iAEC and iLSEC, anti-CD31 antibody (abcam, ab24590; 1/50) and anti-LYVE1 antibody (R&D, AF2089; 1/100) were used for primary labeling. Fluorescent images were observed using BZ-X or FV3000 confocal microscopy. Quantification of rabbit IgG binding and the deposition of C3b and TCC were conducted using ImageJ/Fiji, and the mean fluorescent intensity was normalized to the nucleus count to standardize measurements.

Flow cytometry

Mouse platelet suspension was incubated with fluorescent-conjugated antibodies for 15 min at RT in dark. BV405-conjugated anti-mouse CD41 antibody (R&D, FAB41181V) and PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 antibody (BioLegend, 103105) were used to identify platelets (CD41+ CD45−). AF488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Invitrogen, A21206) and APC-conjugated anti-mouse CD62P antibody (BioLegend, 148304) were used to assess rabbit IgG binding and platelet activation, respectively. After staining, the platelet suspension was diluted 10-fold with FACS buffer without washing, and then analyzed to prevent cell loss due to centrifugation.

Mouse leukocyte suspension was stained with fluorescent-conjugated antibodies for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. BV421-conjugated anti-mouse Ly6G antibody (BioLegend, 127628) and PE-conjugated anti-mouse CD45 antibody were used to identify neutrophils (CD45+ Ly6G+). AF488-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG antibody, AF647-conjugated anti-CD11b antibody (BioLegend, 101218), and AF647-conjugated anti-mouse CD63 antibody (BioLegend, 143922) were used to assess rabbit IgG binding and platelet activation, respectively. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer.

For the analysis of iLSECs, Single-cell suspensions were washed with PBS and blocked in FACS buffer for 10 min on ice. Cells were then stained with fluorescent-labeled antibodies in FACS buffer for 30 min at 4°C in the dark. After staining, cells were washed and resuspended in FACS buffer. The antibodies used to identify the iLSEC population were as follows: BV421 anti-CD34 (BD Biosciences, 568861), BV421 anti-CD31 (BD Biosciences, 564089), BV786 anti-CD31 (BD Biosciences, 740973), anti-LYVE1 (R&D, AF2089) conjugated to AF647 by Lightning-Link (abcam, ab269823), anti-CD43 antibody (BD Biosciences, 744662; for the negative gating). BV421 anti-HLA-A/B/C (BD Biosciences, 567861) and BV510 anti-HLA-DR/DP/DQ (BD Biosciences, 740173) were used to evaluate the surface expressions of HLA molecules. APC anti-ICAM1 (BD Biosciences, 559771) and APC-conjugated SELE (BioLegend, 336011) were used to evaluate the surface expressions of adhesion markers. AF647 anti-rabbit IgG antibody was used to assess the rabbit IgG binding on cells.

Flow cytometry was performed on a BD LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10). Dead cells were excluded using DAPI staining.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.0.2), R (version 4.4.1), and Python (version 3.10.1). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Boxplots show median, interquartile range, and minimum–maximum values. Sample sizes and statistical tests used for each experiment are specified in the corresponding figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired t-tests assuming equal variances were used. When comparing multiple groups across a single factor (e.g., treatment groups at a single time point), one-way ANOVA was followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test when all possible pairs were compared, or by Fisher’s LSD test when comparisons were limited to pre-specified pairs after a significant ANOVA result. For experiments involving two independent variables (e.g., treatment and time), two-way ANOVA was performed, and Sidak’s multiple comparisons correction was used to adjust p-values. p-values or adjusted p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.
