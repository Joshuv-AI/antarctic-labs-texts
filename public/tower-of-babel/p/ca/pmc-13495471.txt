Phytohormone dynamics in tomato leaves due to in-situ continuous wearing of sensors
Authors: Mingji Wei, Shuai Lu, Fei Lyu, Kai Zou, Yongxian Song, Guoxiao Li, Tianbao Wang, Ning Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tomato has become an increasingly important functional food crop due to its high nutritional value and short growth cycle. However, its production is highly susceptible to compound environmental stresses, leading to substantial yield losses. Currently, in-situ continuous monitoring using wearable sensors offers a novel approach for early warning of stress in tomatoes. Yet, the physical properties of these sensors may trigger leaf stress responses during wear, resulting in distorted hormone monitoring data. To address above problems, this study systematically investigated the effects of key sensor properties including surface hardness, breathability, light transmittance, invasion depth and weight on the dynamic changes of six hormones in tomato leaves: abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), ethylene (ET), auxin (IAA), and gibberellin (GA). By designing polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) sensor simulators with varying parameters and employing enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for multi-time-point hormone detection, the results demonstrate that sensor physical properties significantly disrupt hormonal balance. Results show that higher hardness induces sharp accumulation of JA and SA, inadequate breathability promotes ethylene and ABA synthesis, and light shading significantly suppresses GA while activating ABA-mediated senescence pathways. This study provides a theoretical and experimental basis for optimizing sensor biocompatibility and improving the accuracy of in-situ monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13007-026-01552-3.

Introduction

Tomato is rapidly transitioning from a traditional vegetable crop to a “functional food crop” due to its high nutritional value (characterized by rich contents of lycopene, vitamins, and minerals), substantial biomass output, and short growth cycle [5, 23, 43]. However, compound environmental stresses such as diseases, insects, and weeds can cause up to 70% yield loss in tomato production [15, 25, 36], posing a serious threat to stable and high yield. Therefore, establishing an accurate and real-time early warning system for environmental stress in tomato crops, capable of effectively detecting and promptly addressing potential biotic and abiotic risks, is essential for ensuring yield security.

The phytohormone network acts as a “molecular switch” in response to environmental stresses, and its dysregulation often occurs before visible phenotypic changes [1, 3, 9]. Developing a precise and timely early warning model for environmental stress in tomatoes requires in situ and continuous monitoring of hormonal networks using wearable sensors [20, 34]. Compared to root and stem tissues [4, 7, 11], leaves offer advantages such as morphological adaptability, metabolic isolation, and signal fidelity, making them more compatible with sensor deployment [19]. Nevertheless, a critical paradox remains: while these devices are designed to monitor external environmental stresses, the sensors themselves may act as abiotic stressors due to their physical presence, potentially confounding the very signals they are meant to detect. However, inherent physical properties of leaf wearable sensors, including surface hardness, breathability, light permeability, invasion depth and weight, can trigger stress responses in tomato plants during prolonged in-situ monitoring. This leads to distortion in monitoring data of key hormones such as abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), salicylic acid (SA), ethylene (ET), auxin (IAA), and gibberellin (GA) [8, 34]. Currently, the mechanisms underlying this stress response caused by continuous wearable sensor attachment remain unclear. Thus, investigating how the intrinsic properties of wearable sensors influence the dynamic changes in the tomato hormone network is a critical scientific challenge for improving the accuracy and real-time performance of environmental stress early-warning models.

In the field of precision agriculture, wearable sensors have emerged as a promising tool for crop phenotyping and environmental stress monitoring [6, 18, 38]. In the year 2023, Zhang’s team [38] predicted that long-term sensor wear could disrupt the dynamic balance of plant hormone networks through mechanical pressure or alterations in the micro-environment [31]. This prediction was experimentally validated in 2025 by Wen et al. [30], whose study revealed a JA-ET synergistic stress response mechanism in plants induced by mechanical damage from sensors. Although the academic community widely advocates developing non-invasive and low-interference wearable devices [2], inherent sensor properties may still interfere with endogenous hormone networks through multiple pathways. Research from Lee’s team [20] further suggested that wearable sensors may indirectly affect hormone networks due to localized changes in environmental factors such as light intensity and humidity. Additionally, Xiao et al. [31] explored the macroscopic effects of sensor weight, gas permeability, light obstruction, and surface hardness on the growth of clover and pothos plants, contributing significantly to the design and biocompatibility optimization of plant wearables. However, their work did not deeply investigate the influence of sensor properties on plant hormonal networks.

In summary, while previous studies have confirmed that continuous in-situ sensor wear can disturb plant hormone networks, the specific impact of the inherent properties of wearable sensors on the dynamic hormonal changes in tomato leaves remains unclear. To address the limitation of data distortion caused by in-situ stress responses in tomato leaves, this study aims to elucidate the regulatory effects of the inherent physical characteristics of wearable sensors on the hormone network in tomato leaves.

Materials and methods

Materials and instruments

Susceptible Micro-Tom tomato seeds were procured from Nanjing Zhongding Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Six-week-old tomato plants at the flowering stage were used for all experiments. Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) base and curing agent (Sylgard 184) were obtained from Dow Corning (USA) to simulate wearable sensors with tunable physical properties [31]. Sodium diethyldithiocarbamate, phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) tablets, and HPLC-grade methanol were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (USA) and Thermo Fisher Scientific (USA). Plant hormone enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for quantitative analysis of SA, ABA, JA, IAA, GA₃, and the ethylene precursor 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC) were acquired from Agrisera (Sweden).

Hormone extraction and immunoassays were performed using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader (Agilent Technologies, USA) for absorbance measurements at 450 nm. Centrifugation was carried out with an Eppendorf 5430 R centrifuge (Eppendorf, Germany), and sample concentration under nitrogen was achieved using a TurboVap LV evaporator (Biotage, Sweden). Plant growth took place in controlled-environment chambers (Model RXZ, Ningjiang Jiangnan Instrument Co., China) maintaining daytime temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, nighttime temperature of 18 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 65 ± 5%, and a 16 h/8 h light/dark cycle. All mass measurements were conducted with ME104E precision electronic balances (Mettler Toledo, Switzerland), and liquid nitrogen samples were stored in CBS-1000 containers (Chongqing Bingyang, China).

Design of wearable sensor simulator and experimental protocol

The objective of this study is to quantify the effects of various interference conditions including surface hardness, respiration/sunlight obstruction, invasion depth, weight, and wearing duration. Those conditions above were imposed by PDMS sensor simulators on the tomato leaf on the concentration dynamics of six hormones: IAA, ABA, GA, ET, JA, and SA. PDMS was selected as the simulator material due to its highly tunable physicochemical properties [28, 31, 44]. We independently modulated five key sensor characteristics by adjusting specific fabrication parameters: (1) Surface hardness was controlled by varying the base-to-curing agent ratio (from 18:1 to 3:1); (2) Breathability was regulated by the perforation density (0% to 100% vent area); (3) Light transmittance was adjusted using transparency levels (0% to 100%); (4) Invasion depth was defined by microneedle arrays (0 to 50 μm). The dimensions of these microneedles were strictly controlled by high-precision photolithography master molds. Given the high replication fidelity of PDMS casting (sub-micron resolution), the geometry of the cured sensors corresponded accurately to the mold design, ensuring precise penetration depths; and (5) Weight was determined by the sensor mass (0.2 to 100 g). This factorial design allowed us to isolate the specific impact of each physical property on the tomato leaf hormonal network.

As outlined in Table 1, greenhouse-cultivated tomato plants were selected to ensure genetic uniformity. Two types of controls were defined to rigorously evaluate sensor effects: (1) Blank Control: leaves without any patch, serving as the absolute biological baseline; and (2) Sham Control (e.g., 100% vent, 100% transmittance, or 0.2 g weight): used as the reference group in normalization to isolate the specific physical variable of interest from the general effect of patch attachment. For each of the five interference conditions (surface hardness, respiration/sunlight obstruction, invasion depth, and weight), one set of different PDMS patches was attached to non-adjacent leaves of the same plant. Depending on the specific experimental group (e.g., hardness or weight), 4 to 5 patches were applied per plant. This within-subject design was chosen to minimize the genetic variability and physiological heterogeneity between individual plants. Although systemic signaling is a potential factor, the use of non-adjacent leaves minimizes direct crosstalk, ensuring that the observed hormonal changes primarily reflect the local stress response induced by the specific physical properties of the sensor. Each experimental group underwent a wearing period of 20 days, with measurements taken at 11 time points: 0, 0.02, 0.04, 0.5, 1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 15, and 20 days. Each condition was replicated across five plants, resulting in a total of 1 × 5 × 11 × 5 = 275 experimental units. Here, an “experimental group” is defined as the set of leaves subjected to a specific sensor interference condition (e.g., a specific weight or hardness level), distinct from the control groups. All plants were maintained under controlled environmental conditions: daytime temperature of 25 ± 2 °C, nighttime temperature of 18 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 65 ± 5%, and a photoperiod of 16 h light/8 h darkness. Plants were maintained under consistent irrigation with Hoagland solution every 2 days to avoid additional stress, and all plants were grown uniformly to the flowering stage (approximately 6 weeks old).

Hormonal network detection methods

In the study of the stress response in tomato leaves induced by the in-situ continuous wearing of sensors, the simultaneous detection of six key phytohormones—SA, ABA, GA, IAA, JA, and ET—is essential for elucidating their synergistic or antagonistic interactions (e.g., the well-characterized JA-SA antagonism) in immune responses. Given the need to process a large number of samples for time-series and biological replicate analyses, ELISA was selected as the primary tool for screening and quantitative analysis of the hormone network due to its high throughput, operational simplicity, and cost-effectiveness [29]. This study employed an indirect competitive ELISA protocol based on specific polyclonal or monoclonal antibodies. SA, ABA, IAA, JA, and GA were directly detected using 96-well microplates, while the gaseous hormone ET was measured indirectly via its precursor, ACC. ACC was generated through alkaline hydrolysis of tomato leaf extracts and quantified using antibodies specific to ACC. The direct extraction and quantification of ethylene (ET) from frozen tissue homogenates is extremely challenging due to its highly volatile gaseous nature, especially for high-throughput, multi-time-point screening. Therefore, 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), the immediate and biochemically stable precursor of ethylene, was utilized as a reliable proxy. Because ACC accumulation directly drives in vivo ethylene biosynthesis via ACC oxidase, and its water-soluble nature prevents loss during extraction, quantifying ACC via specific antibodies provides a robust and widely validated reflection of ethylene dynamics under sensor-induced stress.

Tomato leaf tissue (100 mg), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, was extracted with pre-chilled 80% methanol containing 1 mM sodium diethyldithiocarbamate under light-protected conditions at 4 °C for 16 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, concentrated under a stream of nitrogen using a TurboVap LV evaporator to prevent oxidation, and reconstituted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). Commercial ELISA kits or laboratory-coated plates were used according to competitive immunoassay procedures: hormone standards or sample extracts were incubated with specific antibodies, followed by the addition of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies and color development [42]. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. A standard curve was generated by four-parameter logistic (4-PL) regression and used to calculate the actual concentrations of hormones in the samples [40]. This method allows rapid simultaneous detection of multiple hormones with sensitivity at the picogram level. Both intra- and inter-batch coefficients of variation were controlled within 10%, demonstrating the high technical reproducibility of the ELISA assays required for reliable large-scale sample screening.

Although ELISA may exhibit certain cross-reactivities and its accuracy is slightly lower than that of chromatographic-mass spectrometric techniques, matrix effects can be effectively mitigated and quantification reliability ensured through strict sample dilution, validation, and the use of matrix-matched standard curves. In conclusion, ELISA provides an efficient and reliable preliminary screening and quantitative analysis tool for in-depth investigation of the dynamic changes in the hormonal network during stress responses in tomato leaves.

To ensure the analytical rigor and reproducibility of the ELISA-based quantification, comprehensive method validation was conducted. Spike-and-recovery experiments were performed by adding known concentrations of hormone standards to the blank tomato leaf matrix prior to extraction to evaluate recovery efficiency and matrix effects. The limits of detection (LOD), limits of quantification (LOQ), dynamic calibration ranges, and mean recovery rates for all six targeted phytohormones are detailed in Table S1. To ensure uniformity, all solid-tissue phytohormone concentrations (ABA, JA, SA, IAA, GA) are standardized and reported in ng/g (fresh weight). The only exception is ethylene (ET), which is intrinsically a gaseous hormone and is therefore reported in parts per million (ppm) to accurately reflect its headspace equivalent concentration.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD) of five biological replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. Differences between multiple experimental groups were evaluated using One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post-hoc test. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In the figures, different letters indicate significant differences between treatments. Results and discussions

Tomato leaf stress response induced by surface hardness

The in-situ application of continuous wearable sensors introduces mechanical stress at the sensor-plant interface that may interfere with internal phytohormonal signaling pathways, particularly those involving SA, IAA, ABA, and JA [21, 24, 39]. As illustrated in Fig. 1a, persistent mechanical loading from sensor attachment can activate plant mechanoperception systems, mimicking physical damage or herbivory patterns. This pseudo-wounding response initiates defense mechanisms characterized by rapid JA and SA accumulation, while IAA redistribution exhibits delayed kinetics. ABA fluctuations demonstrate circadian rhythm-dependent oscillations independent of immediate mechanical stimulation.

Mechanical stress-induced phytohormonal responses follow three distinct temporal phases: acute (0–72 h), adaptive (3–10 d), and steady-state (10–20 d) periods. During the acute phase, transient calcium waves and reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts precede rapid hormonal fluctuations. The adaptive phase shows stabilization through repair mechanism activation, while the steady-state phase reveals baseline hormonal level alterations or amplified periodic oscillations under chronic stress.

Our investigation of PDMS sensors with varying base-to-curing agent ratios (18:1, 10:1, 8:1, 3:1) demonstrates material hardness-dependent phytohormonal responses (Fig. 1b). Lower curing agent ratios (e.g., 3:1) produce stiffer substrates that induce greater mechanical stress, resulting in a statistically significant two to threefold elevation in SA levels within 2–4 h (P < 0.01) through phenylpropanoid pathway activation, followed by partial recovery unless tissue necrosis occurs. Comparative analysis reveals that stiffer sensors (3:1) maintained significantly elevated SA baseline levels (1.8-fold of the control, P < 0.05) versus softer variants (18:1), which showed no significant difference from the control (1.1-fold, P > 0.05) during the steady-state phase.

As can be seen from Fig. 1c, JA dynamics exhibit distinct biphasic patterns with initial burst amplitudes and subsequent pulsatile fluctuations showing positive correlation with substrate hardness. Notably, surface stiffness exerts more pronounced effects on JA than SA regulation, suggesting differential sensitivity in defense signaling pathways.

Our comparative analysis of mechanical (PDMS 8:1) and biological (herbivory) stresses reveals distinct phytohormonal signatures (Fig. 1d). Herbivory induces stronger JA responses with sustained pulsatility, while concurrently suppressing SA levels. Mechanical stress demonstrates moderate SA elevation without JA-SA antagonism. Combined stresses show non-linear interaction effects, particularly in JA accumulation kinetics.

These findings suggest that conventional wearable sensors using rigid substrates (PDMS ≤ 8:1) may significantly alter plant defense status through mechanostimulation, potentially confounding biotic stress detection. For minimal phytological interference, soft substrates (PDMS ≥ 10:1) are recommended, whereas stiffer materials (PDMS ≤ 8:1) could serve as defense response amplifiers in phytopathological studies.

Notably, our extended investigation incorporating herbivory data under PDMS 8:1 condition reveals that mechanical pre-conditioning alters insect-induced JA/SA dynamics, demonstrating the importance of considering sensor-induced priming effects in plant phenotyping applications. This interaction between sustained mechanostimulation and biotic stress responses highlights a critical challenge in developing biologically neutral plant wearables.

Tomato leaf stress response induced by breath impediment

As demonstrated in Fig. 2a and b, the imposition of respiratory impedance by the PDMS sensor simulators significantly triggered ethylene accumulation (P < 0.05) through hypoxia-triggered stress responses that activate ethylene biosynthesis. The most rapid ethylene accumulation occurred within 0–0.5 h, exhibiting an inverse correlation between PDMS vent area and ethylene concentration (R2= 0.96, P < 0.01). Specifically, the 0% perforation density (complete sealing) yielded maximal ethylene accumulation (25 ppm at 20 h), which was significantly higher than the control group (P < 0.01). In contrast, increasing the vent area to 10% resulted in a significant 42% reduction in ethylene levels compared to sealed conditions (P < 0.05). The 100% vent group (control) maintained baseline ethylene concentrations (1.3 ppm at 20 h) representing natural metabolic production. Temporal dynamics followed a triphasic sigmoidal pattern: rapid accumulation (0–1 h, dC/dt = 4.2 ppm/h), decelerated growth (1–10 h, dC/dt = 1.8 ppm/h), and stabilization (> 10 h, dC/dt < 0.5 ppm/h). Vent area increments of 10% enhanced ethylene efflux by 15% nonlinearly, with diminishing marginal effects beyond 60% perforation density.

Tomato leaf stress response induced by sunlight impediment

The attachment of sensors to the leaf surface may partially block sunlight, thereby affecting crop responses [22, 27, 31]. In wearable sensors mounted on tomato leaves, varying levels of light transmittance lead to changes in ABA concentration. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, shading significantly promotes ABA accumulation (P < 0.05), with lower transmittance resulting in statistically higher ABA increases. Specifically, the 0% transmittance group exhibited the most pronounced ABA accumulation compared to the control (P < 0.01). The control group with 100% light transmittance showed no significant change in ABA concentration, providing a baseline that excludes physical interference from the sensor. The rate of ABA variation under different transmittance levels varied across time points. For instance, complete shading (0% transmittance) induced a rapid increase in ABA within a short period, whereas partial shading (e.g., 20% transmittance) resulted in a more gradual upward trend. Prolonged shading may lead to sustained ABA accumulation, though a plateau may be reached, or a decline may occur due to plant adaptation. In the short term (< 1 day), shading is hypothesized to inhibit phytochrome B (phyB) activity, which may activate the ABA biosynthesis pathway (via NCED3 gene expression), and triggers stomatal closure to reduce water loss. During the medium term (1–7 days), impaired photosynthesis likely reduces sucrose signaling, and ABA may remodel carbon metabolism priorities through SnRK2 kinase—inhibiting growth and enhancing stress resistance [12, 16]. Over the long term (7–20 days), carbon starvation and ROS accumulation initiate senescence programs, wherein ABA acts synergistically with ethylene and SA to accelerate leaf abscission [10].

As shown in Fig. 3b, light serves as a crucial environmental signal for GA synthesis. Prolonged shading may suppress GA production, thereby inhibiting stem elongation and leaf expansion [32, 35]. Light, particularly red and blue spectra, regulates GA-related gene expression via phytochromes. When sensor attachment reduces light transmittance, GA synthesis is inhibited, leading to decreased GA concentrations. Different transmittance levels correspond to varying degrees of suppression: lower transmittance causes more substantial declines in GA. Under high-transmittance conditions, the effect is minimal, and GA levels remain close to those of the control group (100% transmittance). Using the 100% transmittance group as a reference, GA concentration exhibits a non-linear decay pattern. Statistical analysis indicated that each 20% reduction in transmittance significantly accelerated the GA decline rate (P < 0.05). For example, 0% transmittance resulted in GA levels dropping to 45% of the control by day 20 (P < 0.01), while 80% transmittance led to an 80% retention, which was significantly different from the full-shading group (P < 0.05). The decrease in GA under 80% transmittance can be partly attributed to local stress responses induced by slight mechanical damage from sensor attachment. Short-term shading (e.g., 0.02 days) may cause a slight reduction in GA, with the decline becoming more pronounced over time, especially under low-transmittance conditions. Long-term shading (e.g., 20 days) may lead to significant GA reduction and even trigger senescence-related mechanisms that further suppress GA biosynthesis.

Additionally, the potential influence of mechanical pressure on GA should be considered. Although sensor attachment may cause minor damage, the primary effect remains attributable to light reduction. Thus, under high-transmittance conditions (e.g., 80%), GA changes are minimal and approach control levels, though a slight decrease may still occur due to the presence of the sensor, even with adequate light penetration.

Tomato leaf stress response induced by invasion depth

Although current wearable sensor designs often emphasize non-invasiveness or minimal invasiveness, precise monitoring of hormonal changes undoubtedly requires invasion into leaf tissues [14, 20]. Therefore, this section focuses on investigating the influence of sensor microneedle invasion depth on hormone levels in tomato plants.

The invasion depth of wearable sensors on tomato leaves involves physical damage or physiological interference to plant tissues, thereby triggering hormonal responses such as JA, ET, and ABA [33, 39]. Deeper invasion causes more severe mechanical damage, potentially disrupting plant tissue structure and inducing hormone reactions similar to biotic or abiotic stress [13]. The invasion depth of microneedles not only affects the extraction efficiency of interstitial fluid but may also modulate the plant local stress response. For example, solid microneedles tend to cause more significant mechanical damage, whereas longer microneedles penetrate deeper into tissues, eliciting stronger stress responses and notably influencing JA levels.

Leaf damage or pathogen infection typically upregulates the expression of genes related to JA biosynthesis [26]. For instance, treatment with the phytotoxin COR enhances the expression of JA synthesis genes in tomato leaves, leading to JA accumulation. If sensor invasion induces similar stress, it may affect JA levels through the same mechanism, as shown in Fig. 4a. Figure 4b displays the normalized concentration changes, obtained by dividing all data by the values of the control group (0 μm). The variation in JA concentration is positively correlated with microneedle length (i.e., invasion depth). Over time, damage-induced JA synthesis gradually increases, reaching a peak before potentially declining or stabilizing. For example, with no invasion (0 μm), the JA concentration remains largely unchanged or at a very low level; invasions of 5 μm and 10 μm caused only a non-significant increase (P > 0.05), while a 50 μm invasion led to significantly elevated JA levels (P < 0.01) due to greater damage, particularly at early time points.

Regarding the temporal response of JA, mechanical damage generally induces a rise in JA within hours, with the peak potentially occurring between several hours and several days, followed by a gradual decline. Under no invasion (0 μm), JA concentration remains at baseline levels (e.g., 20 ng/g). With a 5 μm invasion, JA may begin to increase within 0.5 h, peak between 3 and 5 days, and then gradually decrease.

In summary, the dynamic response of JA can be divided into three phases: within 0–1 day after invasion, JA synthesis initiates rapidly, and the response intensity increases with greater invasion depth (e.g., a 50 μm invasion can raise JA to 105 ng/g within 1 day). At 3–5 days, JA accumulation reaches its peak, after which the plant initiates negative feedback regulation mechanisms, such enhanced activity of JA metabolic enzymes. Between 7 and 20 days, JA concentration gradually declines but remains above baseline levels when invasion depth is significant (e.g., the 50 μm invasion group maintains JA at 105 ng/g by day 20).

Ethylene is typically involved in processes such as senescence, fruit ripening, and mechanical damage. Mechanical damage can induce ethylene synthesis, but its accumulation and degradation dynamics differ from those of JA. In the ethylene biosynthesis pathway, ACC synthase and ACC oxidase are key enzymes. Mechanical damage rapidly activates these enzymes, leading to a swift increase in ethylene within a short period. Due to ethylene’s short half-life, its concentration may decline rapidly within hours, as shown in Fig. 4c and d. A 50 μm invasion may cause more severe damage, resulting in a more pronounced increase in ethylene concentration. However, ethylene peaks earlier and declines more rapidly as the plant initiates repair or adaptation mechanisms.

Under no invasion (0 μm) conditions, the PDMS sensor only adheres to the leaf surface without causing mechanical damage, and the ethylene concentration remains consistently at baseline levels (1.2 ppm).

Tomato leaf stress response induced by sensor weight

This section investigates whether the weight of wearable sensors attached to tomato leaves affects internal hormone levels in the plant and which hormones are influenced [20, 37]. A sap flow sensor developed by Zhejiang University weighs only 0.24 g, highlighting its minimal impact on plants and negligible influence on hormone levels [41]. This suggests that lightweight sensors (e.g., 0.2–1 g) may have little effect on IAA concentration changes, whereas heavier sensors may induce mechanical stress, leading to hormonal variations—particularly in GA or IAA.

This study examined the effects of sensor weight [0.2 g (control), 1, 10, 50, and 100 g] on the IAA in tomato plants, as shown in Fig. 5a and b. The 0.2 g sensor was used as the control. The results indicate that heavier sensors (e.g., 100 g) exert greater mechanical pressure, potentially promoting local IAA accumulation and facilitating cell wall remodeling to counteract stress. Due to the flexible PDMS substrate, lightweight sensors (0.2 g) cause minimal tissue damage, resulting in no statistically significant fluctuations in auxin levels (P > 0.05) compared to the blank control. In the initial stage (0.02–0.5 days), mechanical stress triggers rapid signal transduction, leading to a short-term increase in IAA concentration associated with plant stress response. During the intermediate phase (3–10 days), sustained pressure may inhibit polar auxin transport, causing persistently elevated local auxin concentrations. In the long term (15–20 days), plants may partially restore homeostasis through adaptive regulation, such as cell wall loosening or hormone redistribution. Meanwhile, the hydrophobicity and biocompatibility of PDMS reduce physical interference with plant tissues, so the impact of lightweight sensors (≤ 1 g) on auxin is considerably lower than that of conventional sensors.

While heavier sensors promote auxin accumulation, GA responds differently due to the distinct behaviors of hormones under stress. Mechanical pressure may inhibit GA synthesis, as GA is generally associated with growth promotion, whereas stress conditions often reduce its concentration. Changes in GA levels may oppose those of auxin, since plants under stress may suppress growth-related hormones and activate stress hormones such as ABA or ET. As shown in Fig. 5a and b, GA typically promotes cell elongation, but mechanical stress is hypothesized to activate DELLA proteins (repressors of GA signaling), potentially leading to downregulation of GA biosynthetic genes (e.g., GA20ox) and ultimately reducing active GA levels. Heavier sensors trigger stronger mechanical signals, resulting in statistically significant decreases in GA concentration (P < 0.05). For instance, the 100 g sensor group exhibited a 30% reduction in GA levels compared to the control (P < 0.01).

In terms of temporal dynamics, the rapid response phase (0–1 day) is characterized by the swift transmission of mechanical signals via calcium ion oscillations and the suppression of GA metabolic enzyme activity within hours. This is followed by a sustained inhibition phase (3–7 days), during which mechanically induced miRNAs such as miR159 are thought to suppress the expression of key GA biosynthesis enzymes. Finally, during the homeostasis recovery phase (10–20 days), plants initiate adaptive adjustments; however, heavy sensors (≥ 50 g) continue to maintain low GA levels. Additionally, the flexible PDMS substrate (0.2 g sensor) adapts to leaf growth and deformation, reducing persistent mechanical stimulation, thus resulting in only a slight decline in GA concentration (1% decrease after 20 days). In contrast, rigid sensors may continuously suppress GA signaling by impeding tissue expansion.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the stress responses of tomato leaves induced by the in-situ continuous wearing of sensors, with a specific focus on the influence of key physical properties of wearable sensors on the dynamic changes of major phytohormones. Our findings demonstrate that these sensor attributes significantly disturb the hormonal network (ABA, JA, SA, ET, IAA, and GA), leading to distorted monitoring data and potentially inaccurate environmental stress warning. Notably, softer PDMS substrates induced minimal interference, whereas stiffer sensors provoked pronounced JA and SA accumulation, mimicking biotic stress responses. Furthermore, respiratory obstruction triggered ET and ABA accumulation through hypoxic stress, while light reduction strongly suppressed GA synthesis and promoted ABA-mediated senescence pathways. Invasion depth and sensor weight were also identified as critical factors affecting JA, IAA, and GA levels via mechanostimulation and tissue damage.

These results highlight an inherent trade-off between measurement accuracy and sensor-induced plant stress, emphasizing the need for biocompatible sensor design in future plant wearables. However, this study has certain limitations. First, the experiments were conducted under controlled greenhouse conditions; field environments with fluctuating abiotic factors (e.g., wind, rain, variable light) may induce more complex sensor-plant interactions. Second, while we observed distinct hormonal phenotypes, the specific molecular mechanisms (e.g., gene expression changes) were inferred from established literature rather than direct molecular assays. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating these mechanistic insights to other species without further validation. Third, to minimize baseline genetic and physiological variability, multiple sensor treatments were applied to non-adjacent leaves of the same plant. Although this spatial separation was employed to reduce direct crosstalk, we cannot fully rule out the influence of systemic hormonal signaling, such as the long-distance transport of JA or ABA between treated leaves. Therefore, the observed hormonal dynamics may represent a superposition of localized sensor-induced stress and systemic background signals. Future studies utilizing single-leaf treatments on isolated individual plants are necessary to completely decouple local responses from systemic hormonal crosstalk. Future research should focus on developing novel sensor materials with enhanced biocompatibility and optimized physical characteristics to minimize plant stress responses. Additionally, integrating multi-omics approaches with real-time sensor data could help decouple sensor-induced artifacts from genuine stress signatures. Furthermore, while the ELISA methodology employed in this study is highly efficient and suitable for large-scale, high-throughput screening of hormonal dynamics, we explicitly acknowledge that future validation using more precise analytical techniques, such as High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) or Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), will be necessary to further improve the absolute accuracy and reliability of the phytohormone quantification. The construction of a sensor-plant interaction model that quantitatively correlates sensor properties with hormonal dynamics will be essential for improving the reliability of in-situ monitoring systems. Finally, extending this research to other crop species and field conditions will be critical for advancing sustainable and accurate precision agriculture applications.
