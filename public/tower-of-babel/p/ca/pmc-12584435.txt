Development and initial evaluation of a digital service package for community-dwelling older females with pelvic floor dysfunction
Authors: Ning Jiang, Qingyang Hu, Jiabei He, Weiwei Zhou, Jianlin Zhuang, Qi Chu, Yan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPelvic Floor Dysfunction (PFD) is common, especially among older people, causing physical and psychological issues and high costs. Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is a first-line, non-surgical treatment; however, resource limitations pose challenges. Digital interventions show potential, yet there’s a lack of a comprehensive digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD. This study aims to develop such a package, guided by the BCW model and a remote digital health studies framework, and initially evaluate its implementation quality to benefit patients with PFD and primary care.MethodsThis study’s design was based on the MRC Framework, human-centered design, and co-design. It involved a two-stage process: developing a digital service package for community-dwelling older PFD females and initially evaluating its implementation qualities. The development used a three-step procedure: a rapid literature review, patient interviews, and a two-round Delphi survey among experts. Guided by relevant models, the initial package was constructed and refined. Established procedures and success criteria for digital packages were adopted in the evaluation stage.ResultsDeveloping a digital service package for community-dwelling older PFD females resulted in a final version with three main sections. It includes content and form, use mechanisms, and an application platform. The content and form of the digital service package include evidence-based science popularization on pelvic floor anatomy, disease mechanisms, preventive measures, and rehabilitation skills, delivered through diversified formats such as visual materials, follow-up videos, and expert lectures, with fragmented knowledge systematically integrated into a structured framework. It also emphasizes interpersonal interaction via patient support communities, doctor-patient counseling channels, and family involvement, alongside personalized home training programs tailored to individual needs, rehabilitation goals, and daily habits. The mechanisms of use incorporate material support like urination pads and self-management guides, as well as social support through the involvement of healthcare providers, peers, and family members; regular reminders via text or voice calls reinforce training compliance, while monitoring systems and feedback loops enable dynamic evaluation of progress, and incentive mechanisms reward patients for achieving stage-specific rehabilitation goals. The application platform leverages WeChat’s high usability among older adults, integrating group chat functionalities with mini-programs and AI-driven autoreplies for basic queries. It features separate portals for patients and administrators to support long-term follow-up and evaluate the quality of implementation. An evaluation with 18 participants showed it met all pre-defined success criteria for practicality, attitudes, acceptability, usability, and cost.ConclusionThe digital service package innovatively realizes three core functions: reinforcing early intervention, supporting remote monitoring, and enhancing patients’ self-management ability. It has a scientific content structure, dynamic personalized management, and a multifunctional platform. This digital service package can be used to improve the adherence to rehabilitation training of community-dwelling older females with PFD during the rehabilitation process due to external factors such as heavy financial burden and insufficient time for health management. The initial evaluation shows its high implementation quality, marking an essential digital chronic disease intervention for older people. Future efforts should focus on technology upgrades, long-term tracking, and cross-domain cooperation to boost primary healthcare effectiveness and elderly healthcare equity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13574-z.Keywords: Digital service package, Initial evaluation, Digital health, Healthcare, Pelvic floor dysfunction, Older females, Delphi method

Background

Pelvic Floor Dysfunction (PFD) is a series of conditions that occur due to the natural degeneration or damage of the pelvic floor support tissues and the inability of atrophied pelvic floor muscles to maintain the normal position of the pelvic organs [1]. This results in the displacement or dysfunction of the pelvic organs, including urinary incontinence (UI), pelvic organ prolapse (POP), urination and defecation disorders, etc [1–3]. The prevalence of PFD increases with the aging of the population. Studies have shown that the prevalence of any UI ranges from 25% to 45% [4]. Among women aged 70 years and older, over 40% of the female population is affected by UI [5].

PFD triggers a series of physical and psychological problems that significantly reduce the quality of life of patients [6]. For example, the constant friction of the prolapse and frequent urine loss and leakage can cause persistent irritation of the patient’s pubic skin, triggering redness, itching, bleeding, and secondary urological disorders, such as urinary tract infections, inflammation, and stones [7, 8]. Its unspeakable symptoms can also cause patients to feel a sense of shame, affecting their perception of their overall body image and manifesting negative emotions such as embarrassment, anxiety, and low self-esteem, which can seriously affect their everyday interpersonal interactions and quality of life, and lead to depression, loneliness, and other psychological problems [9]. In addition, the treatment of PFD (mainly surgical treatment) requires many medical resources and economic costs, bringing a heavy financial burden to individuals and society. Studies have estimated that the annual outpatient cost of PFD in the United States exceeds $300 million [10], and the direct cost of prolapse surgery alone exceeds $1 billion annually [11]. Globally, the surgery cost for PFD is estimated to be over $10 billion per year, excluding non-surgical and conservative treatments [12].

Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is the crux of first-line nonsurgical therapy for treating PFD (mainly UI) in women, regardless of age [13]. PFMT is used alone or in conjunction with electromyographic biofeedback [14]. Although supervised individual PFMT is recommended by clinical practice guidelines [15], inadequate human and financial resources limit the delivery of PFMT, especially in primary care [16]. Previous research has attempted to provide group-based rather than individual-based interventions to overcome economic and human resource barriers and encourage positive self-management by promoting peer support [17]. In recent years, digital technologies have shown great potential in healthcare, presenting unprecedented opportunities to enhance early intervention, remote monitoring, and self-management of patients [18]. For patients with PFD, digital interventions have also shown potential in detecting pelvic floor muscle movement, guiding users through various exercise routines, and helping to improve patient compliance [19]. Studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of mobile applications for PFMT in controlling symptoms and improving quality of life [20], especially for women who are confident in independently managing their condition [21]. However, the existing literature has mainly focused on the design of new devices or mobile applications and the role of digital technology in health information delivery and patient management.

There is a lack of research on developing a comprehensive digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD to provide multi-component supports and services to promote their behavior change and improve implementation in primary care settings through digital technology. The digital service package in this study refers to a range of digital health services and supports. It belongs to the family of digital health, which is “a broad umbrella term encompassing e-health, information and communication technologies (ICTs) as well as developing areas such as the use of advanced computer sciences, including the topics of ‘big data,’ genomics, and artificial intelligence.”

The Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) model [22] and the framework for remote digital health studies [23] provide theoretical guidance for this study in developing a comprehensive digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD. BCW offers a systematic approach for selecting and designing behavior change interventions for specific behaviors, settings, and populations. Our study focused on the proposed nine BCW model intervention functions: education, persuasion, incentivization, coercion, training, enablement, modeling, environmental restructuring, and restrictions. These nine intervention functions guided our development of multi-component supports and services. The framework for remote digital health studies [23] suggests that three aspects should be considered in planning remote digital health studies, including the motivation profile of participants and offered incentives or nudges, the complexity of tasks required from participants, and the scientific requirements of the study. Guided by this framework, our study can benefit from targeting specific participant profiles, providing incentives and nudges, and reducing study complexity to improve study outcomes.

This study aimed to develop a comprehensive digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD and initially evaluate its implementation quality from the perspectives of providers and relevant parties.

Methods

The design was based on the Medical Research Council Framework (MRC) [24], human-centered design [25], and co-design [26]. This study included a two-stage process: development of a digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD and initial evaluation of implementation qualities of the digital service package. The specific method is shown in Fig. 1. A three-step procedure was used for the development of a digital service package, which included (1) a rapid review of related literature, (2) interviews with older female patients with PFD in the community, and (3) a Delphi technique with two survey rounds among selected experts. Rapid reviews have emerged as an efficient solution to support health policy-making and health systems strengthening by providing high-quality evidence in a timely and cost-effective manner [27]. Interviews with community-dwelling older female patients with PFD are critical to involve end-users to facilitate human-centered design and co-design. Under the guidance of the Behaviour Change Wheel (BCW) model [22] and the framework for remote digital health studies [23], combining the results of rapid review and patient interviews, we constructed initial candidate content items of the digital service package. We used the Delphi technique [28], a commonly used consensus method in research and content development, to modify the initial digital service package. In the initial evaluation stage of the digital service package, we adopted procedures and success criteria described elsewhere to evaluate digital packages [29, 30].

Flowchart for the digital service package for community-dwelling older females with pelvic floor dysfunction

The study obtained ethics review approval from the Institutional Review Board at the Centre for Disease Control and Prevention, Changning District, Shanghai, China (IRB number: 2023-18). The study was conducted between November 2023 and July 2024 following the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all participants signed an informed consent for participation.

Stage 1 - development of the digital service package

The research team reviewed a large amount of research literature. Based on a rapid review, the digital service package was initially completed by combining interviews with community-dwelling older female patients with PFD. Using the Delphi method, experts in the fields of gynecology, gerontology and geriatrics, primary health care, rehabilitation, digital health, and health education were invited to rate the importance and feasibility of the digital service package. Adjustments and modifications of the digital service package were completed according to the experts’ opinions and suggestions. After two rounds of expert panels, the essential content of the digital service package was finalized when a final consensus was achieved.

Step1. Rapid reviews

A rapid review was used to extract evidence from previous studies on each of the following three aspects: (i) behavioral change techniques (BCTs) for PFMT, (ii) BCTs in digital interventions, and (iii) BCTs for community-based interventions. In a rapid review, sources are limited due to the time constraints of searching; however, transparent and reproducible search methods are still used [31]. To date, there is no typology of the methods for rapid reviews, and the main difference between our rapid review and a systematic review is that our review is limited to the literature published in one database as described in previous research [32]. This method was used due to the urgent need to develop a digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD. We searched for literature published before November 6, 2023. Two reviewers (NJ and QH) participated the rapid review. The database Web of Science Core Collection was searched. Table 1 shows the summary of the rapid review searches. The three rapid reviews retrieved 40, 156, and 31 articles. After removing duplicates and screening by title and abstract, 129 articles were finally included for further analysis (see Table S1 in the supplementary). Based on the rapid review results, the draft of a digital service package was proposed.

Step2. Interviews

Study setting. We recruited participants from Xianxia Community Health Service Center of Changning District, Shanghai, China, which plans future digital service interventions for community-dwelling older females with PFD. Semi-structured interviews were conducted between Dec 2023 and February 2024. Eligible participants were initially selected based on their health record information with the assistance of family doctors. Potential participants were approached during their clinic visits at the Community Health Service Center, and two researchers then scheduled the interviews, obtained written consent, and facilitated these one-on-one in-person interviews, with an average interview time of 40–50 min each.

Participants. The inclusion criteria were: (a) age between 60 years and over with a history of childbearing; (b) the duration of PFD is 6 months to 10 years; (c) meet the clinical diagnostic criteria for mild to moderate severity of stress UI or meet the clinical diagnostic criteria of POP and the degree of prolapse is stage I and II; the criteria used to determine UI, POP and prolapse were based on the family doctors’ diagnostic information in the health records; and (d) possess everyday communication and comprehension skills. The exclusion criteria were individuals: (a) have a history of pelvic floor surgery in the last six months; (b) suffering from chronic wasting disease or acute psychiatric exacerbation; or (c) suffering from central nervous system diseases or serious cardiopulmonary diseases.

We planned to include 12–14 participants and considered data saturation (i.e., no new information emerged) and the concept of information power [33] (i.e., a lower number of participants is needed if the sample holds information that is relevant to the study aims). A purposive sampling technique was used to select participants.

Data collection and analysis. Researchers developed an interview guide based on information from the literature and their expertise in this area to facilitate the discussion. Example questions included: Please describe what digital health technologies you use daily. What challenges have you encountered in the long-term process of pelvic floor rehabilitation and behavior change? What resources and support do you need to design a digital package promoting pelvic floor rehabilitation and self-management at home? We used these questions as a topic guide, and prompts, examples, and follow-up questions were used during the interview to ensure the interviewee understood the topic, gained more information, and ensured the smooth conduct of the interviews. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service provider, and data were kept securely in a de-identified format. We used a reflexive thematic analysis [34] to organize, code, and sort the data.

Step3. Expert panel

A Delphi method was used in this study. Based on the results of rapid reviews and interviews with community-dwelling older females with PFD, a preliminary indicator pool (see Table S2 in the supplementary) was developed to create an expert consultation questionnaire. A pre-survey was conducted to test the inquiry questionnaire’s simplicity and ease of understanding. Between March 2024 and April 2024, two rounds of Delphi surveys were conducted via e-mail with 25 experts. After each round of expert consultation, adjustments were made based on expert opinion and the boundary value method [35]. After two rounds of expert panel discussions, all quantitative indices of the digital package achieved consensus; however, five experts provided additional qualitative comments. Therefore, further online expert focus group interviews were conducted among the five experts to reach a consensus. The definition of “consensus” was based on previous studies [28], and the threshold for consensus was set at a 75% agreement rate.

Participants. We used purposive sampling to select the panel of experts [36]. The criteria for the inclusion of experts in this study were: (a) with a master’s degree or above; (b) with an associate or above professional title; (c) with more than 10 years of work experience in their respective fields of specialization; and (d) a profound understanding of PFD, community care and digital care. We recruited participants from the Shanghai Community Health Association, Shanghai Pelvic Floor Health Center, and workshops related to digital health and primary care. The number of experts surveyed in previous studies has ranged from five to thousands [37], and data saturation was used to determine the size [38]. In line with the established inclusion criteria and available resources, we recruited 25 experts spanning seven disciplines: gynecology, geriatrics, gerontology, primary healthcare, rehabilitation, digital health, and health education. To ensure disciplinary diversity and balance, we invited 3 to 4 experts from each field, forming a multidisciplinary panel for this study.

Instrument, data collection, and analysis. The questionnaire consisted of four main parts: a survey description, basic information about the experts, the body of the questionnaire, and the expert self-evaluation about the basis of decision-making and familiarity with the content. The importance and feasibility of each entry in the first draft of the digital package were scored on a 5-point Likert scale of “very good, good, fair, poor, and very poor” with values of 5, 4, 3, 2, and 1, respectively. The reliability of the clinical practitioner’s opinion on the digital package was expressed in terms of the degree of authority, which was assessed by the authority coefficient (𝐶𝑟). The authority coefficient is calculated from the clinical practitioner’s basis of judgment (𝐶𝑎) and familiarity with the issue (𝐶𝑠) when scoring, and is calculated as 𝐶𝑟 = (𝐶𝑎 + 𝐶𝑠)/2. An authority coefficient of ≥ 0.7 indicates that the clinical practitioner has a high level of authority, and the meeting opinion is more reliable. Correspondence questionnaires were issued in the form of e-mails. After each round of expert consultation, team members will enter the questionnaire data into the database, analyze the experts’ feedback and suggestions, and discuss and modify the digital service package to form the next round of questionnaires. Items were screened and included based on the following criteria: importance of mean value ≥ 4.00, coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 0.25 [39], and full score ratio ≥ 20%.

Stage 2 - initial evaluation of the digital service package

After we had completed developing the digital service package, we invited relevant experts to discuss its implementation and solicit suggestions for further improvement. After the workshop, we distributed evaluation forms and analyzed the collected data.

Step4. Survey after experts workshop

Setting and participants. The parent study held an expert workshop to discuss further implementing the digital service package. The parent study, registered at the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration Number: ChiCTR2400088675), is an intervention program that aims to implement digitally supported PFD rehabilitation for community-dwelling older females through family doctors in community health service centers, and to evaluate the effects of the intervention. The parent study consisted of two parts: the development of the digital service package and an initial evaluation from the perspectives of providers and relevant parties (e.g., technical experts, managers), as well as the pilot study and intervention. This study is the first part of the parent study.

This workshop was conducted in July 2024 at the Xianxia Community Health Service Center in Changning District, Shanghai. At the workshop-seminar, a member of our study team presented the digital service package, inviting community health officials, clinical professionals (including family doctors and community nurses), experts from gynecology, gerontology, and geriatrics, digital health professionals (including technologists), and primary healthcare providers to participate. We recruited 20 participants from the workshop to assess the implementation qualities of the digital service package. At the end of the workshop, members of our study team distributed evaluation forms and analyzed the data collected.

Instrument and data collection. Two research team members developed the evaluation form based on previous research [30], and a senior researcher subsequently reviewed it for accuracy. The evaluation form included general information about the participants (sex, age, education, job title, main field of work, and years of work experience) and an evaluation of the implementation qualities of the digital service package, which included assessing the practicality, attitude towards the digital service package, acceptability, usability, and cost [29]. Items included several dichotomous and 1–10 scale ratings. The research team introduced the digital service package in the workshop and distributed 20 evaluation forms afterward. We used cluster sampling. Excluding the two experts who did not participate in the entire workshop, the evaluation forms completed by the remaining 18 experts who agreed to participate were included.

Step5. Data analysis

Implementation qualities included practicality, attitude, acceptability, usability, and cost, and we pre-defined the criteria for “success” based on previous research [30].

Practicality was defined as the usability of the digital service package in resource-limited settings. The evaluation included two items: (i) whether any community health workers could use the digital service package (yes/no), and (ii) the level of burden associated with using it, rated on a scale from 1 (zero burden) to 10 (highest burden). Success was defined as achieving more than 75% positive responses for the first item (i) and an average burden score of less than 6 for the second item (ii).

Attitude was defined as one’s perception of the digital service package’s availability, rated on a scale from 1 (very negative) to 10 (highly positive). Success was defined as achieving an average score greater than 6.

Acceptability was defined as the extent to which the digital service package suits the target population. The evaluation included three items: (i) whether the digital service package meets patients’ information needs (yes/no), (ii) whether it contains meaningful information (yes/no), and (iii) the perceived usefulness of the package, rated on a scale from 1 to 10.

Usability is the ease of operation when using the digital service package. In this study, we assess the usability from the perspectives of providers who use this digital service package to interact with patients. Success is pre-defined as achieving an average score greater than 6.

Cost is defined as the perceived human cost implications for community health workers, who must allocate time to provide necessary resources and maintain the digital service packages. We used a single item to assess whether these cost implications were acceptable (yes or no). Success is pre-defined as over 75% of respondents report acceptable cost implications.

Results

In this section, we report on the findings from the final version of the digital service package and the initial evaluation of its implementation qualities.

The digital service package

The development process resulted in the final version of the digital service package for community-dwelling older females with PFD (the full version is presented in Table S3 in the supplementary). The framework of the digital service package is shown in Fig. 2. As this study aimed to develop and evaluate the digital service package, we did not show the full results of the rapid review, interviews, and the Delphi method. Instead, we presented the initial candidate items and reference sources for the digital service package (Table S2 in the supplementary) based on the results of rapid review and interviews.

The framework of the digital service package for community-dwelling older females with pelvic floor dysfunction

The digital service package contains three main sections: content and form, mechanisms of use, and application platform, subdivided into several parts.

The content and form of the digital service package contain four parts.

Science popularization content

It consists of a series of contents proven by experts or research, covering a wide range of topics from theoretical knowledge such as those related to the anatomy and physiological function of the pelvic floor, causes of diseases, preventive measures and considerations for daily life, to practical skills such as rehabilitation training such as pelvic floor muscle training, diet and exercise management methods, and self-management. For patients with a low understanding of the condition or superstitious beliefs in biased prescriptions, the science popularization content will help to improve their cognitive level and promote better recovery.

Forms of access

Due to the specificity of the patient population, a single promotional channel will not work. For patients to better accept the knowledge and skills, it is necessary to provide diversified channels, such as transforming boring and difficult-to-understand words into picture delivery, making training steps into follow-up videos, or offering some lectures by authoritative experts. At the same time, the digital service package is designed with several strategies for popularizing science. This includes but is not limited to the fact that it will integrate fragmented knowledge, build a framework, and deliver it systematically. It will not provide all the knowledge to each patient indiscriminately but will give it accurately according to their needs or preferences.

Interpersonal interaction

Recognizing the importance of human connection in the rehabilitation journey, this section emphasizes the significance of interpersonal relationships. It establishes a vibrant platform for patients to share their experiences, creating a support community. The digital service package facilitates meaningful counseling sessions between doctors and patients while providing a space for emotional support from family members. Additionally, it introduces inspiring role models who can share their success stories in pelvic floor rehabilitation, fostering a sense of hope and enhancing patients’ self-identification with their rehabilitation journey.

Personalization

The digital service package allows for assessing the patient’s needs, understanding the rehabilitation goals the patient wishes to achieve, and evaluating the patient’s self-efficacy. An individualized home training program is developed on this patient - oriented basis. Appropriate training methods, tasks, and goals are customized according to the needs, tasks are set in stages, and relevant supportive measures are provided. It is worth noting that the training program needs to be developed in conjunction with the patient’s previous living habits so as not to increase the patient’s burden.

The mechanisms of use of the digital service package contain four parts.

Support offerings

Digital service packages provide material and social support. Physical support such as urination pads, record sheets, and self-management guides help solve the fundamental problems of patients in rehabilitation training. Social support, such as the participation of doctors, patients, and family members, can help motivate patients in their rehabilitation training and improve their compliance.

Regular reminders

Suppose the patient fails to complete the rehabilitation exercise according to the plan due to some subjective or objective factors. In that case, the digital service package can remind the patient via text message or voice call of the parts she struggles with, including the repetition of core skills and knowledge points, the essentials of movement, and the negative consequences of non-compliance.

Monitoring and feedback

This digital service package provides a feedback monitoring service. The supervisor can be a physician, family member, or patient. Depending on the supervisor, different monitoring tools can be selected. For example, patients can self-monitor by choosing a personal weekly treatment diary and a knowledge test. Depending on their condition and goals, they can choose different monitoring indicators, including the type of exercise, frequency, and duration of a single exercise session. If supervised by a physician, the patient’s recovery can be monitored by selecting symptom and behavioral scales. At the end of a cycle, they can provide feedback, such as the patient’s feelings and results during the exercise process and the physician’s evaluation and recommendations for recovery.

Incentives and rewards

If the patient’s rehabilitation exercise is very effective and meets the goals set for the current stage, she will be able to receive a reward. Rewards come in various forms and can be chosen according to the patient’s preference.

The application platform of the digital service package contains three parts.

Platform composition

After one-on-one interviews, we identified WeChat Group Chat as the vehicle to realize the functions of the digital service package due to its high popularity and usage among the elderly population. Using the WeChat group chat as the main body, we expanded the WeChat applet and used the group chat AI to reply to some basic FAQs automatically. If the problem still cannot be solved, it will be answered and updated by real administrators, and in addition, some tasks that the AI cannot handle will also be completed by them.

Platform functions

The patient portal and the administrator portal offer different sets of features tailored to their users. The patient portal primarily includes self-assessment and resource navigation, which help patients learn and train effectively. The administrator portal is designed for monitoring, data analysis, and feedback. This enables doctors to gain a dynamic understanding of each patient’s training progress and condition, allowing them to provide timely input and communicate effectively to enhance treatment plans.

Patient follow-up

Researchers can conduct regular and long-term follow-up surveys on patients through the digital service package to understand patients’ exercise compliance, knowledge of the disease, and quality of life. They can also conduct more rigorous controlled studies further to assess the quality of the digital service package implementation. Meanwhile, investigating patients’ opinions, suggestions, and satisfaction with the digital service package can promote further improvement.

Initial evaluation of the implementation qualities of the digital service package

Eighteen participants completed the survey (18/20, 90% response rate). The results of the implementation qualities are shown in Table 2. According to Table 2, all the pre-defined success criteria were met for implementation qualities (practicality, attitudes, acceptability, usability, and cost). Regarding practicality, 100% of stakeholders agreed that community health workers could use the digital service package. The mean score of the level of burden was 4.18, with a sample standard deviation of 2.28. This suggests that using digital packages may increase the workload of CHWs, but the level of burden is within acceptable limits. Regarding attitude and usability, 100% of stakeholders scored a mean of 8.71 and 8.06, respectively, with a sample standard deviation of 1.21 and 1.35. This data indicates that the digital service package is very available and usable. Regarding acceptability, 100% of stakeholders felt that the digital service package met patient needs and contained meaningful information. The mean score for the usefulness of the digital service package was 8.88, with a sample standard deviation of 0.96. These data indicate that the digital service package has a high level of usefulness. In terms of cost, we used a single item to assess whether these cost implications were acceptable (yes/no). Success is pre-defined as over 75% of respondents report acceptable cost implications. 100% of stakeholders considered the workforce cost of the digital service package to be within an acceptable range.

Discussion

Digital intervention is a significant aspect of the rehabilitation of pelvic floor dysfunction (PFD) in community-dwelling females. Developing a digital package is the fundamental and crucial step in implementing a digital intervention. In this study, we constructed a digital health service package for PFD in community-dwelling older females and invited experts in related fields to evaluate its implementation quality.

This study innovatively combined BCW with a framework for remote digital health studies to address the technology acceptance barriers faced by older users specifically. This integration allowed for a more precise matching of intervention functions to the abilities of the target population. Previous studies using the BCW for developing interventions, such as Beleigoli et al.’s research [40] on healthy weight management and Nelligan et al.’s study [41] on exercise adherence for knee osteoarthritis, highlight both theoretical consistency and divergent integration approaches. Similar to these studies [40, 41], our study is based on BCW’s intervention functions and BCTs, focusing on health management adherence by translating theoretical constructs into practical tools—online platforms, SMS systems, or WeChat-based service packages. Notably, the core distinction lies in how BCW integrates with technological platforms. Nelligan et al. [41] focus on single-technology SMS, simplifying BCW’s intervention functions into text-message triggering logic to emphasize direct “theory-to-behavior” mapping. In contrast, this study leverages the WeChat ecosystem to embed BCW’s “environmental restructuring” and “social influence” functions within community interactions, such as real-time doctor-patient communication and peer experience sharing, forming a socially embedded multifaceted intervention model.

The digital service package we have developed offers unique advantages, in addition to convenience and flexibility, in everyday digital services. There are similarities between our study and the MyHealtheBladder project conducted by Burgio, K. et al. [42], in which an eight-week urinary incontinence rehabilitation program was delivered to female military veterans with the help of mHealth technologies and devices, and through the translation of a web-based mobile platform. Compared to it, our digital package program offers access stability and usage continuity, and its component modules are more abundant, which helps optimize the problems of insufficient feedback channels and lack of personalization to a certain extent.

Firstly, our digital package is distributed through a WeChat group chat. Given the compatibility of the WeChat software, any mobile device can access this digital service package, which guarantees its stability.

Secondly, there is no time limit for accessing our digital service package, allowing it to be used at any stage, including throughout the patient’s recovery process, as per the patient’s wishes.

Moreover, personalized information, real-time feedback, and access to expert consultation when needed are features that users highly value [43]. Therefore, we have focused on the design of the usage mechanism for the digital service package, with the “regular reminders” and “monitoring and feedback” modules being the most important and effective. During the interview process, we learned that most patients are family caregivers. They usually don’t have large chunks of free time for rehabilitation exercises in their daily lives and may also forget to do them. Therefore, the regular reminder function of the digital service package is one of the essential means to improve patient compliance, which is consistent with the research findings of Wessels et al. [44] The digital service package can provide various reminder methods. Patients can choose their preferred method, set the reminder frequency, and schedule a time that suits them. Playing a reminder role will not give patients a sense of oppression, and patients can take control of their rehabilitation behaviors. With the clock-in function of the WeChat program, after the administrator’s settings, patients can upload their confusion, exercise content, duration, and other details in text, audio, and video formats. Doctors can give evaluations and suggestions based on the patient’s feedback. This function helps doctors understand patients’ rehabilitation situations and effects dynamically, and it can also promptly resolve patients’ confusion and improve efficiency.

Finally, the most significant advantage is that the digital service package has prominent personalized functions. The available evidence indicates that, in certain instances, personalized interventions improve physical activity and other behaviors and are more effective compared to non-personalized approaches [45]. The research team and doctors will establish a resource library regarding knowledge acquisition. Patients can obtain resources purposefully according to what they want. The resource library contains most of the widespread scientific knowledge required by patients, and these resources will be enriched and continuously updated according to the problems raised by patients online. Regarding rehabilitation programs, doctors set different tasks and goals according to varying programs and the patient’s situation. For example, some patients have problems with their waists and cannot perform several movements in the rehabilitation exercises. Then, they can do other movements with similar effects instead.

Furthermore, considering the age factor, the ease of comprehension of the content and the simplicity of the digital service package’s operation have reached the maximum extent that can be achieved. Therefore, after further improving the classification and management functions of the digital service package, the user group can be expanded to include postpartum women or even patients with other chronic diseases.

The development of this digital service package represents an innovative shift in the traditional rehabilitation model, integrating online, remote, personalized rehabilitation training with a single offline physical therapy session. This initiative is a significant advancement in digital healthcare. Additionally, the application of telemedicine technology enables the creation and delivery of innovative services [46]. In addition, telemedicine service technology can create and deliver innovative services. The development of this digital service package provides patients in low-resource or economically disadvantaged areas, who may not have access to physiotherapy and thus miss the opportunity to improve their condition, with an alternative option and the ability to contribute to the improvement and refinement of interventions.

The dissemination of this digital service package will not only raise awareness of both familiar and lesser-known conditions related to pelvic floor dysfunction but also help mitigate the disease’s impact on patients’ lives. Moreover, it presents a vital opportunity to address the challenges faced by older women, a demographic often overlooked by society, thereby enhancing their quality of life. Looking ahead, there may be potential to advocate for health insurance coverage that includes clinically validated pelvic floor rehabilitation programs, ultimately reducing out-of-pocket expenses for older women and improving access to essential rehabilitation services.

An initial evaluation of the digital service package revealed a high level of implementation quality. The ratings for practicality, usability, acceptability, usefulness, and cost were particularly favorable. It is worth noting that the standard deviation of the level of burden was slightly higher than the predicted value, indicating a wide variation in stakeholder evaluations of the level of burden on CHWs when using the digital software package. This suggests that further research should focus on understanding the working patterns of CHWs when using digital service packages and on improving adaptive digital service packages to empower CHWs.

Implications for clinical practice

Our research findings offer practical implications for primary care workers, enabling them to leverage digital tools to transition their services from offline to online and from one-to-one to one-to-many. By using digital service packages to connect Community Health Service Centers with homes, the pressure on the medical resources of community health centers can be alleviated to some extent. This digital transformation also empowers community-dwelling older females with PFD to access health services more flexibly. Overall, these findings provide empirical evidence and practical guidance for the future large-scale implementation of digital service packages, filling existing research gaps, enhancing the intervention efficiency, and improving the quality of life for community-dwelling older females with PFD. Additionally, the study serves as a valuable reference for digital health interventions in other areas of health management, ultimately benefiting primary care providers.

Limitations of the study

First, this study is confined to Xianxia Street in Changning District, Shanghai. This may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, we conducted an initial evaluation of the implementation quality of the digital service package from the perspectives of providers relevant parties (e.g., technical experts, managers) and with a relatively small sample size; we did not include the end users, which opinion could be more relevant regarding acceptability, usability; further study will be essential to evaluate the implementation and effects of digital service intervention from the end users of older females with PFD and to understand the long-term viability of applying the digital service package more widely. Third, we did not conduct a contextual analysis during the development of the digital service package. We encourage future studies to incorporate contextual analysis across all phases of development, implementation, and evaluation processes.

Conclusion

The digital service package builds an innovative solution for rehabilitating PFD in community-dwelling older females through a scientific content architecture, a dynamic and personalized management mechanism, and a multifunctional technology platform. The initial evaluation verified its outstanding advantages in terms of practicality, acceptability, and cost-effectiveness, making it an essential attempt at digital intervention for chronic diseases in the elderly. In the future, it is necessary to unleash its strategic value further to enhance primary healthcare’s effectiveness and improve healthcare equity for older people through technology iteration, long-term effect tracking, and cross-domain synergy.
