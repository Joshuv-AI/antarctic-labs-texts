Women's Satisfaction With Quality of Abortion Care, Associated Factors, and Barriers in Public Health Facilities of South Gondar Zone, Ethiopia: A Mixed Study
Authors: Tesfalem Ketema, Zemenu Shiferaw Yadita, Dabere Nigatu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsClient satisfaction is a key indicator of abortion care quality, influencing both service use and outcomes. While Ethiopia has expanded access to comprehensive abortion care, limited evidence exists on client satisfaction in conflict‐affected areas like South Gondar Zone. This study aims to assess perceived quality, identify associated factors, and explore barriers to accessing abortion services in public health facilities in South Gondar, Amhara Region, Ethiopia, in 2024.MethodsA concurrent mixed‐methods study was conducted from November 1 to December 31, 2024, using cross‐sectional and phenomenological designs. A multi‐stage sampling was used for selection. Quantitative data were collected from 477 women using a structured, pre‐tested questionnaire and analyzed using SPSS version 25. A binary logistic regression used to identify factors. Additionally, 15 purposively selected participants took part in in‐depth interviews, with qualitative data thematically analyzed using Open Code software.ResultsSatisfaction with abortion care quality was 38.4% (95% CI: 34.1%–42.8%). Women aged 15–24 (AOR = 3.25, 95% CI: 1.28–8.26), 25–34 (AOR = 2.49, 95% CI: 1.11–5.56), urban residence (AOR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.24–0.82), family size (< 5) (AOR = 0.32, 95% CI: 0.16–0.63), partners with no formal education (AOR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.18–0.74), receiving care at health centers (AOR = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.19–0.68) or primary hospitals (AOR = 0.21, 95% CI: 0.10–0.44), and male providers (AOR = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.27–0.69) were significantly associated client satisfaction. Qualitative findings revealed key barriers to satisfaction, including stigma, lack of privacy, supply shortages, and provider reluctance.ConclusionClient satisfaction with abortion care quality in South Gondar Zone is low compared to national standards and is associated with age, residence, family size, partner's education, facility type, and provider gender. Improving satisfaction requires expanding service access, promoting respectful, client‐centered care, upgrading facility conditions, training providers—especially males—and addressing stigma and privacy concerns.

Introduction

Abortion refers to the disruption of pregnancy before fetal viability. Even though the criteria of viability differ worldwide, most developed countries have set it at before 20 weeks of gestation, whereas developing countries, such as Ethiopia, count pregnancies less than 28 weeks or birth weights below 1000 g as abortions if the last normal menstrual period (LNMP) is unknown [1, 2]. Induced abortions are legal or illegal, safe or unsafe, and performed with medical or surgical methods. Safe methods have been endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) [3, 4].

The WHO describes quality of care as the degree to which care for health adds desired outcomes in health and reflects evidence‐based standards [5]. The WHO model categorizes quality in the areas of safety, effectiveness, timeliness, efficiency, equity, integration, and people‐centeredness [6]. Technical and interpersonal factors both fall under the umbrella of quality abortion care [7]. Client satisfaction is known to be a vital indicator of healthcare quality [8]. Clients' perceptions, like selection of service, informed decision‐making, and respectful care, are helpful performance indicators to evaluate abortion services [9, 10].

Abortion was legalized in Ethiopia in 2005 under some conditions, such as rape, incest, congenital fetal abnormality, or in case the life of the mother was in danger [11]. Despite this legal provision, access to high‐quality and safe abortion services remains low in the majority of the regions, including in the study site [12, 13]. In an effort to improve access, the Federal Ministry of Health in 2014 downgraded its guidelines in favor of task‐sharing through the allowance of first‐trimester abortions at health centers [13].

About 60% of unintended births and 30% of all births are ended by induced abortion worldwide, and an estimated 45% of these abortions are unsafe [4, 14]. In the region of sub‐Saharan Africa, an unsafe abortion prevalence is approximately 33 per 1000 women aged 15–49 years, which contributes to nearly 10% of maternal deaths in this area [15, 16]. The Ethiopian abortion prevalence is 28 per 1000 women, reaching close to 620,300 induced abortions annually [17]. Despite the availability of abortion methods from a medical perspective, access remains limited [18] [9], Dangerously performed abortions remain with severe public health consequences, thus the need for quality available abortion care [19].

The WHO emphasizes the importance of client experience when evaluating healthcare quality, as it is also related to clinical effectiveness, safety, and system efficiency [20]. However, despite international and local recommendations, there are limited systematic tools and studies to assess abortion care quality from clients' perspectives, especially in low‐ and middle‐income countries like Ethiopia [21]. More than one study depends upon a single method that is mostly quantitative, omitting key client‐centered factors and experiences. Numerous systemic and contextual barriers to client satisfaction with abortion care remain unexplored [22, 23, 24, 25]. Determinants of quality of care also include institutional and provider‐level determinants like availability of providers, client participation in decision‐making, availability of pain medication, cleanliness of facilities, and respect for clients [22, 24, 26, 27]. This study aimed to assess client satisfaction, associated factors, and barriers to safe abortion care at public health facilities in the South Gondar Zone, Amhara Region, Northwest Ethiopia. The goal was to help fill the existing evidence gap and support efforts to improve and expand access to quality abortion services.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Period

This study was conducted in South Gondar Zone public health facilities, Amhara Regional State, Northwest Ethiopia. The zone comprises 21 districts (13 rural and 8 urban) with an estimated 2,650,351 population and an estimated 536,166 reproductive age women. Health services available in the zone comprise a single referral hospital, nine primary hospitals, 94 health centers, and 362 health posts, though services for abortion care were available in only 10 hospitals and 18 health centers. A concurrent mixed‐method design was utilized, comprising a quantitative institution‐based cross‐sectional study and a qualitative phenomenological design. The study was conducted from November 1 to December 31, 2024.

Population

All women who received abortion care services in public health facilities across the South Gondar Zone were the source population. For the quantitative study, the study population included women who received abortion care at randomly selected public health facilities across the zone during the study period. Women with prior experience of abortion care and the healthcare providers and facility managers in public health facilities were part of the qualitative study.

Inclusion Criteria: Women receiving abortion care services at the randomly selected public health facilities of the South Gondar Zone during the data collection period were included.

Exclusion criteria: Women who received abortion care but were not permanent residents in the locality (remaining for 6 months or less or visiting temporarily) were excluded. Abortion care providers, MCH focal persons, and heads or directors of public health facilities with under 6 months of work experience were also excluded.

Sample Size Determination

For the quantitative component, the sample size was calculated using both the single population proportion formula and the double population proportion formula based on selected factors. However, the largest sample size resulted from the single population proportion formula, which was adopted for this study. The calculation was based on the following assumptions: a proportion of 25.1% derived from a previous study in Northwest Ethiopia [22], a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error, a 10% non‐response rate, and a design effect of 1.5. This gave a final sample size of 482 participants. For the qualitative part of the study, in‐depth interviews were conducted with clients, service providers, and MCH officials. The number of interviews was guided by the principle of data saturation, with interviews continuing until no new information emerged.

Sampling Procedure

A multistage sampling technique was employed in this study. Abortion services were available in 10 hospitals and 18 health centers across the zone. Following the rule of thumb, 50% of these service‐providing facilities, specifically, 9 health centers and 5 hospitals, were selected using a simple random sampling method (lottery technique). Baseline totals of the number of abortion clients received at each of the facilities chosen to take part in the 2 months leading to data collection were used to proportionally allocate the total sample size among the facilities. Finally, study participants were selected using a systematic random sampling technique; every 2 (k th = N/n) clients were included in the study. from each facility until the desired sample size was reached. For the qualitative component, participants were selected using a purposive sampling method, considering clients' place of residence (urban or rural) and educational status. This approach was used to ensure maximum variation among respondents.

Measurements and Variables

The dependent variable: Clients' satisfaction with the quality of abortion care (Satisfied or Unsatisfied).

The independent variables include Socio‐demographic characteristics, Reproductive health‐related characteristics, Facility and service provider‐related characteristics, and Service‐related Characteristics.

Abortion service: A service provided for a client presenting with abortion and declared by the provider in charge as having an abortion regardless of the cause and type [3].

Client satisfaction with quality of comprehensive abortion care service: Women were asked to assess their level of satisfaction with the quality of abortion services using a 13‐item Likert scale question with five response categories, including very satisfied [5], Satisfied [4], neutral [3], dissatisfied [2] and very dissatisfied [1]. The individual mean satisfaction score was computed, with a mean satisfaction score of 2.58, with a minimum score of 1.08 and a maximum score of 5.0. Respondents who scored below the individual mean score were considered “not satisfied” and those who scored equal to or above the mean score were coded as “Satisfied” [28].

Data Collection Tool and Procedure

For the quantitative survey, a semi‐structured questionnaire was developed from the literature [23, 27, 29]. It contained socio‐demographic data, health facility and service provider characteristics, reproductive health features, and client satisfaction. The questionnaire was translated into the local language (Amharic) and back translated into English by a different translator for consistency. The data were collected by fourteen BSc midwives through face‐to‐face interviews, and four public health officers served as supervisors. For the qualitative portion, in‐depth interviews were carried out by principal investigators and experienced data collectors using a semi‐structured interview guide. The interviews were tape‐recorded, and some notes were taken to supplement the recordings. Both providers and clients were included as participants to get differing opinions on the same issues.

To ensure data quality, data collectors and supervisors underwent a 2‐day training on the data collection tools, ethical concerns, and data integrity processes. Pretesting on 5% of the sample (24 women) was conducted at Alember Health Center. Tool reliability was checked with Cronbach's alpha and was 0.84. Supervisors conducted random checks for consistency and completeness.

To enhance the credibility of qualitative data, triangulation was achieved by conducting interviews with both clients and service providers, enabling the experience of abortion care to be understood more completely. Auditability was maintained by maintaining complete records during the exercise of data analysis, enabling a clear audit trail. Transferability was supported by presenting comprehensive descriptions of setting and participant characteristics, allowing readers to determine the applicability of the results to similar settings. Conformability was achieved by providing detailed findings supported by verbatim quotes from participants, allowing for transparency and credibility of the analysis.

Data Processing and Analysis

For quantitative data, the data gathered were cleaned, coded, and entered EpiData version 4.6 and exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis. Descriptive statistics computed frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations. Binary logistic regression was conducted since the outcome variable was dichotomous to identify variables that were associated with client satisfaction. Those variables with a p‐value of < 0.2 in the bi‐variable analysis were entered into the multivariable logistic regression. In the multivariable analysis an adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI) were computed, and a p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The model fit was assessed by the Hosmer‐Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, and the model fit was confirmed (p‐value > 0.05). Multicollinearity was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF), and no multicollinearity was confirmed (VIF < 10).

For the qualitative data, interviews were transcribed word for word in Amharic and subsequently translated to English. The transcripts were read again to become familiar with the contents. The translated documents were copied into plain text format and transferred to OpenCode version 4.03 for analysis. Themes and codes for the facilitators and barriers of abortion services were developed through thematic analysis. The responses were categorized under the respective themes. The interpretation was drawn based on these themes.

Ethical Approval and Consent to Participate

Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of Bahir Dar University (Reference No./003/), and an official approval letter for data collection was secured from the Amhara Public Health Institute (APHI) Research Directorate (Reference No. ROD/89/2017). All participants were provided with a detailed information sheet explaining the purpose, procedures, and ethical considerations of the study. They were informed of their right to withdraw from the interview at any time. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before the interview, and participation was only voluntary.

About 78.8% of respondents resided in rural areas, and 88.1% identified as followers of Orthodox Christianity. Regarding educational status, 41.1% had no formal education. Factors Associated With Women's Satisfaction With the Quality of Abortion Care

Binary logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with women's satisfaction. Initially, a bivariable logistic regression was performed, and sixteen variables with a p‐value less than 0.2 were included in the multivariable analysis, while there were 183 cases with the relevant outcome variable (satisfaction). This indicates that the events per variable were above 10, which exceeds the standard threshold for logistic regression models and suggests that overfitting is unlikely to be a concern.

In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, six variables were found to be significantly associated with client satisfaction at p < 0.05: age, residency, family size, partner's education level, type of health facility, and sex of the service provider.

Women aged 15–24 years (AOR = 3.25; 95% CI: 1.28–8.26) and 25–34 years (AOR = 2.49; 95% CI: 1.11–5.56) had significantly higher odds of being satisfied compared to those aged 35–49 years. Urban residents had 55% lower odds of reporting satisfaction than rural residents (AOR = 0.45; 95% CI: 0.24–0.82). Women from households with fewer than five members had 68% lower odds of satisfaction compared to those from larger households (AOR = 0.32; 95% CI: 0.16–0.63). Partner's education was also a significant factor—women whose partners had no formal education had 64% lower odds of satisfaction than those whose partners had secondary education or higher (AOR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.18–0.74).

The type of health facility was also significantly associated with satisfaction. Clients who received services at health centers (AOR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.19–0.68) and primary hospitals (AOR = 0.21; 95% CI: 0.10–0.44) had 64% and 79% lower odds of reporting satisfaction, respectively, compared with those served at specialized hospitals. Lastly, the sex of the service provider was significantly associated with satisfaction. Women attended by male providers had 57% lower odds of reporting satisfaction compared to those attended by female providers (AOR = 0.43; 95% CI: 0.27–0.69) (Table 5).

Qualitative Results

Characteristics of the Interviewee

Fifteen in‐depth interviews were conducted in 2024 in public hospitals in South Gondar Zone, Northwest Ethiopia, involving five clients, seven service providers, and three MCH officials, with participants representing both urban and rural settings, varying education levels, ages, and professional backgrounds.

Thematic Analysis Results

The thematic analysis identified four major themes. First, access to abortion services was constrained by financial barriers, unawareness, stigma, and geographic inaccessibility. Second, the quality of services reflected issues related to availability, provider competence, facility readiness, and adherence to medical standards. The third community and professional attitudes captured prevailing social, cultural, and provider perspectives toward abortion. Lastly, systemic challenges encompassed structural and policy‐related barriers, including resource constraints, legal restrictions, and administrative delays affecting service delivery.

Theme 1: Access to Abortion Services

Lack of information and service availability are major barriers to abortion access, particularly in rural areas. Limited awareness of the legal conditions for abortion, with stigma and fear of judgment, discourages women from seeking safe services and enhances unsafe procedures.

An 18‐year‐old urban service user with formal education described, “Those who live far away from health centers struggle to access services.”

A 32‐year‐old MCH case team leader described, “Professionals largely avoid providing induced (safe, legal) abortion services either on religious or personal objection grounds.”

A 34‐year‐old service provider described, “Abortion services are not being easily provided safely. They avoid facilities as they do not receive good treatment.”

Another provider aged 32 explained as follows, “Most safe abortion clients do not meet the legal requirements, and knowledge about Ethiopia's abortion law (Article 551) is extremely low.”

Theme 2: Quality of Abortion Services

Despite some positive outcomes, service quality is compromised by inadequate staffing, limited post‐abortion counseling, shortages of essential supplies, lack of privacy, and provider reluctance, resulting in delayed and substandard care.

A 20‐year‐old provider stated, “Patients have not been sent home unserved, but the quality typically does have a shortcoming, and so they do become dissatisfied.”

A 34‐year‐old provider commented regarding facility limitations: “Patients sometimes end up in a “social room” with only three beds for abortion services, due to a lack of private rooms.”

A 34‐year‐old MCH case team leader added that there is inadequate trained manpower and incomplete service provision, such as the lack of post‐abortion counseling and basic drugs.

A 32‐year‐old MCH team leader said, “Quality issues are widespread, including poor community awareness, provider shortages, insufficient time for professionals, inadequate training refreshers, and unwillingness to provide abortion care; often due to religious beliefs or inadequate training.”

Another provider, 34 years old, said, “There is a lack of lab services needed for safe abortion services. The war has caused ambulance shortages, equipment, and transportation, and also many women not having their blood type known or unable to afford transport.”

A 30‐year‐old provider stated, “Shortage of equipment and private rooms makes it difficult to provide clean abortion services and maintain confidentiality of clients.”

Another provider at 30 commented, “Most of the unwanted conceptions are among young people, yet there is no private room or safe place for them.”

Theme 3: Community and Professional Attitudes Towards Abortion

Attitudes towards abortion are strongly shaped by religious and cultural beliefs, leading to stigma, shame, and provider refusal based on personal or religious convictions, which further limit access to services.

A 32‐year‐old rural client with no formal education said, “Abortion is not a proper procedure within society unless it is a problem in its own.”

A 26‐year‐old urban citizen with formal education said, “In religion, a very serious offense; a sin would be considered the murdering of a soul, and it's not something we discuss lightly.”

A 32‐year‐old urban woman with formal education said, “Women get angry with some of these professionals, and hence they seek traditional medicine or private clinics.”

A 34‐year‐old MCH Case Team Leader commented, “Most health professionals have been trained on abortion, but some are not willing to provide induced abortion care.”

A 22‐year‐old service provider said, “Professionals are uncertain regarding clients’ eligibility under abortion law; mothers can give reasons like being related, but professionals fear legal repercussions.”

A 26‐year‐old urban service user with no education stated, “Unless it is due to a medical condition, the community regards it as an evil and sinful practice.”

A 32‐year‐old MCH Case Team Leader stated, “Most professionals say their religion forbids abortion—I'm Orthodox Tewahedo, and abortion is forbidden.”

Theme 4: Systemic and Policy‐Related Challenges

Structural and policy barriers, including inadequate training, supervision, resources, and weak policy enforcement, undermine abortion service delivery and allow unsafe practices to persist without stronger government commitment.

A 30‐year‐old District MCH Officer stated that the government is not interested in developing the skills and capacity of the professionals who graduate from college or university.

A 38‐year‐old Hospital MCH Case Team Leader responded, “Trained workers nowadays work in shifts with minimal monitoring and supervision.”

A District MCH Officer aged 34 said, “Support of government and NGOs for abortion capacity building and behavior change among health workers is limited. Follow‐up training is poor.”

A 32‐year‐old Team Leader of MCH said, “The relationship with the districts is defective and poor referrals.”

A 38‐year‐old MCH Team Leader also added, “Health Extension Workers (HEWs) have access to most of the mothers, but information and referral behavior to abortion is poor.”

Discussion

This study assessed client satisfaction with the quality of abortion care and its associated factors among women receiving abortion services in public health facilities of South Gondar Zone, northwest Ethiopia. Overall, 38.4% (95% CI: 34.1%–42.8%) of the respondents were satisfied with the service received. This finding is comparable to reports from Tigray, Ethiopia (40.6%) [30], But substantially lower than satisfaction levels reported in Addis Ababa (46%) [31], Mojo Town (56.5%) [24], North Shewa (65%) [28], Jimma Town (76.3%) [32], Oromia and Amhara regions (79.6%) [33], Guraghe Zone (83.5%) [34], and Gambella (87.1%) [23]. Conversely, the satisfaction level observed in this study is higher than reports from Mexico City public facilities (24%) [35], mainland and Zanzibar, Tanzania (20%) [36].

These variations may be explained by differences in study setting, service organization, measurement approaches, and sociocultural contexts. In particular, some previous studies included both governmental and nongovernmental facilities, where service quality and client experience may be higher [22, 37]. Moreover, the ongoing conflict in South Gondar Zone is likely to have disrupted health service delivery through infrastructure damage, shortages of essential supplies, and reduced availability of trained providers, all of which negatively affect the quality of care and client satisfaction [38].

In addition to stigma and material shortages, the qualitative findings identified limited knowledge of Ethiopia's abortion law (Article 551) among both clients and healthcare providers as a major systemic barrier. Providers reported fear of legal repercussions and uncertainty about eligibility criteria, which discouraged them from offering induced abortion services even when legally permitted. Similar findings have been reported in other Ethiopian settings, where inadequate legal awareness among providers has constrained service provision despite a relatively liberal legal framework [39]. Women's limited awareness of the legal conditions under which abortions are permitted also delayed care‐seeking and contributed to late presentation or resort to unsafe and traditional abortion practices, as documented in prior studies from Ethiopia and other low‐resource countries [22, 39]. These findings underscore the need to strengthen legal and policy literacy among both providers and communities as a core component of quality abortion care.

Regarding demographic factors, younger women aged 15–24 and 25–34 had a lower odds of reporting satisfaction than women aged 35–49, consistent with studies from Nepal [40] and central Ethiopia [28]. Notably, the crude association between age and satisfaction was weak, whereas the adjusted analysis revealed a strong and statistically significant relationship. This marked shift from crude to adjusted estimates suggests that confounding factors such as facility type, residence, partner education, and providers' characteristics substantially influenced the unadjusted association. The high likelihood of younger women to be satisfied may reflect that they focus on terminating unwanted pregnancies to avoid social stigma, potentially with less awareness of treatment standards or associated risks.

Urban residents had significantly lower odds of reporting satisfaction than rural residents, consistent with findings from central Ethiopia [28]. Urban women may have higher expectations regarding privacy, timeliness, and providers' communication [41], while urban facilities often experience higher patient volumes, longer waiting times, and reduced provider‐client interaction [29]. Family size was also associated with satisfaction, with women from households of fewer than five members reporting lower satisfaction. Similar findings have been reported in northwest Ethiopia [22]. Larger household size may be associated with stronger social support networks, shared responsibilities, and reduced psychosocial stress, which can positively influence health experiences [42, 43]. This study and evidence from Tanzania and Nigeria showed that partners' education level was the strongest predictor of satisfaction: women whose partners had no education were less satisfied than women whose partners had some education [36, 41, 44].

The type of facility was a strong determinant of satisfaction. Outpatients in health centers and primary hospitals had a lower odd of satisfaction than those in specialized hospitals. Similar evidence from England shows that lower sites are short of resources, have reduced specialist staff, and longer waiting times, with negative effects on satisfaction [45]. Better facilities and trained staff of specialized hospitals contribute to better quality and patient satisfaction [46]. Finally, provider gender also influenced satisfaction: female patients who were treated by male providers had lower odd of satisfaction than patients treated by female providers; evidence from central Ethiopia confirms [28]. Female providers are generally the first choice for touchy services such as abortion due to patient‐centered communication, indicating that provider gender should be taken into account while providing abortion services [47].

Strengths and Limitations of the Study

A key strength of this study is its mixed‐methods design, combining quantitative and qualitative data to capture both statistical associations and contextual insights on abortion care satisfaction. Conducted across the South Gondar Zone, the wide geographic scope enhanced sample representativeness and generalizability to public health facilities in the area.

Despite these strengths, the cross‐sectional design limits causal inference, and the study only included women receiving formal health services, excluding those using traditional or unsafe methods. Using the sample means as the cutoff for satisfaction may have introduced classification bias. Although design effects considered in the sample size estimation and proportional allocation of the sample were applied, the analysis did not explicitly account for clustering, and that residual intra‐facility correlation may have resulted in some underestimation of standard errors and confidence intervals. Future research should include non‐facility users and consider longitudinal designs to better assess access, quality, and satisfaction over time.

Conclusions and Recommendations

This study found that only 38.4% of the women indicated they were satisfied with the quality of abortion care services, showing wide gaps regarding expected standards. Young women aged 15–24 and 25–34 years were more satisfied than women in the age group 35–49. On the contrary, determinants with negative effects on satisfaction were urban residence, household size of fewer than five individuals, partners with no education, receiving care in health centers or primary hospitals compared to specialized centers, and encountering male providers. The qualitative results also highlighted important barriers such as stigma, privacy, shortages of medical supplies, and refusal by providers to offer abortion services. Improving women's satisfaction requires equitable access to respectful, client‐centered abortion care, strengthened facility infrastructure and supplies, targeted provider training, and community outreach to reduce stigma and raise awareness.
