The role of pictograms in improving patients’ medication adherence: a systematic review
Authors: Yawen Liu, Jiayi Sun, Wanqiong Zhou, Chengrui Zhang, Ying Xing, Ying Wang, Yan Zhang, Jie Shen, Wei Luan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoor medication adherence remains a significant public health issue. Although pictograms are established as intuitive visual aids for medication guidance, a current evidence synthesis is lacking, particularly regarding their role in the rapidly advancing digital health landscape.ObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the potential effect of pictograms on patients’ medication adherence and exploring implications for the digital health era.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, VIP Network, and CBM databases for relevant studies on the effect of pictograms on patients’ medication adherence, from the inception of each database to April 27, 2025.ResultsA total of 21 articles were included, comprising 19 English and 2 Chinese publications, all of which were randomized controlled trials. These studies varied in terms of study background, sample size, and drug regimens tested. All studies had methodological limitations, with pictographic interventions differing in complexity, duration, and adherence outcomes measured. Twelve studies (57.1%) reported statistically significant effects of pictographic interventions on patient adherence.ConclusionPictograms serve as an effective adjunct to enhance medication adherence, particularly among patients managing complex medication regimens or possessing limited health literacy. Future efforts should focus on developing standardized, evidence-based pictogram libraries and integrating them into multimodal medication education systems. Incorporating pictograms into digital health platforms, such as those using AI and smart devices, represents a promising direction for enhancing their reach and impact.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14988-z.

Introduction

Medication adherence refers to the extent to which a patient’s medication-taking behavior aligns with the prescription recommendations of a healthcare professional, and it is a critical factor influencing the effectiveness of disease treatment [1–4]. However, many patients fail to follow treatment recommendations for various reasons. According to the World Health Organization, the long-term adherence rate for chronic disease treatment in developed countries is only about 50%, with even lower rates reported in developing countries [3, 5]. Non-adherence leads to higher hospitalization rates and increased healthcare costs. Previous studies have shown that 25% to 90% of patients who are non-adherent experience treatment failure, and some require hospitalization, imposing a substantial economic burden on national healthcare systems, estimated to exceed $100 billion annually [6]. Additionally, research indicates that non-adherence increases the risk of death by approximately 35% on average [7]. The factors influencing patient medication adherence are complex and multifaceted, primarily including patient-related factors, treatment-related factors, and healthcare system factors. The World Health Organization (WHO) identifies five “dimensions” that affect adherence: patient, treatment, health system, condition, and socioeconomic environment [3]. Among these, inadequate health literacy is considered a significant barrier to medication errors and non-adherence. This issue is particularly pronounced among the elderly, patients on polypharmacy, and populations with lower educational levels [8–11].

Medical information supports medication adherence by promoting proper medication use, enhancing understanding, and fostering positive treatment attitudes [3]. Traditional medication education primarily relies on verbal counseling by healthcare professionals (HCPs), supplemented by written information provided by drug manufacturers in the form of patient information leaflets (PILs) [12]. However, these methods have notable limitations. Standard medication guides often use technical terminology, require a high reading level, and lack personalized design, making it difficult to meet the diverse comprehension needs of different patients [13–15]. This challenge is further exacerbated in multicultural and multilingual environments, where language barriers impede effective communication of medication information [16]. To address these limitations, visual aids are increasingly being introduced in healthcare. Pictograms, as a visual communication tool that transcends language and cultural barriers, offer unique advantages in conveying medical information [17, 18]. Studies have reported that incorporating pictograms into patient consultations can reduce administration errors related to liquid drug use, with the most effective study demonstrating a reduction in error rates of over 80% [19]. Additionally, pictograms combined with oral and/or written drug leaflets have been shown to enhance visual attention, comprehension, and recall of the information provided [11, 19, 20]. A study by Katz et al. also found that patients are more inclined to use pictograms in combination with written or verbal information [11]. Therefore, pictograms, as an intuitive and cross-cultural communication tool, hold significant potential and value in improving patient medication safety and adherence.

Based on the form of intervention and the level of technological integration, the pictogram-based interventions used in existing studies can be broadly classified into three types. Simple interventions involve only static pictograms, for example by adding them to medication labels or leaflets without any additional education or technical support. Composite interventions combine pictograms with other strategies such as structured patient education, pharmacist counseling, or feedback methods. Technology-enhanced interventions go further by integrating pictograms with electronic monitoring devices, mobile health applications, or real-time feedback systems. Understanding these different types helps to frame the analysis of their effects on medication adherence.

Currently, numerous studies have employed pictograms to examine patients’ medication adherence. However, due to notable limitations across these studies, applying the evidence to clinical practice remains challenging. First, most evaluations of pictogram effectiveness focus primarily on knowledge improvement, with relatively little research addressing their impact on actual behavioral change [21]. Second, inconsistencies and contradictions exist among research findings, and there is a lack of systematic synthesis. This situation creates multiple decision-making dilemmas and operational challenges for clinicians, who must navigate uncertainty when using this evidence to guide practice and make clinical decisions [9]. Third, the methodologies used in different studies vary, with no standardized implementation protocols or consistent target user groups for pictographic interventions, complicating the selection and application of these methods [22]. Therefore, the optimal implementation strategies for pictograms, their appropriate target populations, and their effectiveness in specific clinical settings require further clarification [11, 19, 20].

Based on the research background outlined above, this study employed a systematic review method to comprehensively search for and analyze randomized controlled trials examining the effect of pictograms on patients’ medication adherence. By systematically evaluating the existing evidence, the study aims to verify the effectiveness of pictographic interventions in improving medication adherence, as well as to summarize best practice strategies, applicable scenarios, and influencing factors. This will provide evidence-based guidance for clinical practice and highlight directions for future research. The results of this study are highly significant for enhancing patient medication safety, optimizing medication education strategies, and promoting health equity. This systematic review fulfills a dual function. Firstly, it provides a critical update to the existing literature by incorporating studies published up to 2025, thereby presenting the most current state of research on pictograms. Secondly, it situates its findings within the context of the rapidly evolving digital health landscape. Over the past decade, healthcare delivery has undergone a significant transformation through the integration of advanced technologies, including artificial intelligence-driven tools and smart medical devices. This shift necessitates a reconsideration of traditional instruments such as pictograms, raising questions regarding their adaptation for digital platforms and their potential for personalization when combined with AI. Although primary research in this specific area remains nascent, this review establishes a foundation for understanding the evolution of pictograms from static visuals to dynamic, intelligent elements in contemporary patient education. In doing so, it offers a forward-looking perspective intended to inform both current clinical practice and future technological innovation.

Methods

Search strategy

PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform, VIP, and CBM were searched for studies on the effect of pictograms on patients’ medication adherence. The search period spanned from the inception of each database to April 27, 2025. The literature search combined subject headings and free-text terms. The main search terms included: Pictorial Work, Symbolism, Audiovisual Aids, pictogram*, visual aid*, graphic label*, icon*, symbol*, pictorial instruction*, Medication Adherence, Patient Compliance, Treatment Adherence and Compliance, Drug Adherence, Drug Compliance, Medication Compliance, Medication Non-Adherence, among others. Literature inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study subjects are patients who are taking medication. The research topic is the effect of pictograms on patients’ medication adherence. The study design was a randomized controlled trial. To be included, studies must report raw data and be published in peer-reviewed journals.

Exclusion criteria

The literature includes only abstracts, research plans, or reports; literature for which the full text is not available; conference papers; duplicate literature; and works not in Chinese or English.

Literature screening and data extraction

The retrieved literature was imported into EndNote 20 software. After deduplication, two researchers independently reviewed the titles and abstracts to eliminate duplicate publications. Literature screening was conducted according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, with cross-checking performed to ensure accuracy. In cases of disagreement, a third party was consulted to make a final decision regarding the inclusion of studies. The included studies were independently analyzed by two researchers, who extracted data to present key features of the intervention studies and identify differences that may affect the interpretation of pictographic interventions. The following variables were extracted: study setting, study design, sample size, brief description of participants, type of intervention emphasizing pictogram contributions, adherence effect measures, adherence measurement timing, and adherence outcomes.

Literature quality evaluation

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were assessed for risk of bias by two researchers using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias 2 (ROB 2) tool [23]. When discrepancies arose between the two researchers’ evaluations, a third researcher was involved in the discussion to reach a consensus on the overall quality of the article. The assessment criteria included: (1) bias arising from the randomization process; (2) bias due to deviations from intended interventions; (3) bias from missing outcome data; (4) bias in outcome measurement; and (5) bias in selective reporting of results. Due to heterogeneity among studies in terms of patient populations, pictographic interventions, and adherence outcome measures, meta-analysis was not feasible. Therefore, the results of this systematic review are presented descriptively.

Data synthesis

The synthesis focused on the following aspects: Intervention characteristics: Type of pictogram and intervention complexity, which we categorized into three levels: (1) simple (pictogram alone), (2) composite (pictogram + other educational/behavioral strategies), and (3) technology-enhanced (pictogram + digital/electronic tools), delivery method, and involvement of healthcare professionals. Population subgroups: Effects stratified by health literacy level, age, and cultural background. Outcome measures: Comparison of subjective (e.g., self-report scales) and objective (e.g., pill counts, electronic monitoring) adherence metrics. Temporal trends: Short-term vs. long-term adherence outcomes. Results were synthesized in tables and narrative summaries to highlight patterns and inconsistencies in the evidence.

Results

We registered this review with PROSPERO (CRD420251147819) and adhered to the PRISMA reporting guidelines [24].

Literature search results

After the preliminary search, 7,471 articles were identified, and 6,275 articles remained after removing duplicates. By screening the titles and abstracts, 46 articles were initially selected. Following a full-text review, 21 articles were ultimately included. The flowchart of the literature search is presented in Fig. 2 for further details.

Fundamental characteristics of the reviewed literature

The 21 included studies were published between 1997 and 2022 and were conducted in various countries: China (n = 2), the United States (n = 8), Kurdistan (n = 1), South Africa (n = 3), Malaysia (n = 1), Malta (n = 1), India (n = 1), Thailand (n = 1), Australia (n = 1), Cameroon (n = 1), and Nigeria (n = 1). The study sites comprised hospital outpatient clinics (n = 11), clinics (n = 8), primary care centers (n = 1), and community mental health centers (n = 1). All studies were randomized controlled trials (n = 21). The study durations ranged from seven days to two years. Sample sizes varied from 30 to 632 patients, with a total of 3,698 patients included across all studies. The study populations differed in age, clinical conditions, treatment regimens, and levels of health literacy. Additionally, there was heterogeneity in the selection of interventions and control groups. The basic characteristics of the included studies are detailed in Table 1.

Study population

The study population was heterogeneous. It varied in disease type, treatment regimen, age group, health literacy, and cultural background. Chronic non-communicable diseases included hypertension [9, 25], heart failure [26], coronary heart disease [27], and diabetes [9, 28]. Respiratory diseases comprised asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [29, 30]. Ophthalmic conditions such as cataracts were also represented [31]. Chronic diseases in elderly populations frequently involved multimorbidity and polypharmacy [10, 32]. Infectious diseases studied included HIV/AIDS [8, 33–35], malaria [36], and bacterial infections [37, 38]. Additionally, mental health disorders [39], acute illnesses, and postoperative management [40] were addressed. The most commonly targeted drug therapies were antiretroviral therapy (four studies) and cardiovascular drugs, either alone or in combination with antidiabetic agents (four studies). Other studies focused on hypoglycemic agents (two studies), antimicrobials (two studies), asthma medications (two studies), chloroquine syrup (one study), cataract treatments (one study), and standard liquid formulations. Detailed data distribution is presented in Fig. 3.

Age-stratified analysis revealed that the study encompassed patients across all age groups. For pediatric patients, interventions primarily involved parents or caregivers, with medication adherence reliant on caregiver administration; pictograms were employed to facilitate correct medication use. Adolescents and adults constituted the majority of research subjects, particularly in studies related to HIV, antibiotics, and chronic diseases. Elderly patients were predominantly studied in the context of chronic disease comorbidities. The analysis of health literacy levels reveals that numerous studies have either specifically targeted individuals with low health literacy or included participants from the general health literacy population, while some studies did not explicitly screen for health literacy levels.

Regarding cultural background, the geographical distribution of research is extensive, encompassing the application of pictograms across diverse cultural contexts and healthcare systems. In high-income countries or regions, such as the United States, Australia, and Malta, studies frequently utilize technology-assisted methods, including electronic vials, online generation tools, sensors, and employ more complex and sophisticated interventions. Conversely, research conducted in low- and middle-income countries or regions—such as China, South Africa, Malaysia, Thailand, India, Cameroon, and Nigeria—primarily addresses challenges related to the transmission of basic medical information, illiteracy, limited healthcare resources, and brief doctor-patient communication times. In these settings, pictograms serve as a pivotal tool to overcome language and cultural barriers, with most studies reporting significant positive outcomes. Successful pictographic interventions underscore the importance of “localized design,” wherein symbols must correspond to local cultural norms and understandings to prevent potential misinterpretations.

Interventions

In all included studies, pictograms were incorporated into interventions related to pharmacological treatment; however, substantial variability existed across four key dimensions: intervention complexity, healthcare personnel involved, intervention duration, and adherence outcomes measured. Regarding intervention complexity, simple interventions [9, 25, 31–33, 35, 38, 41] involved the provision of pictogram labels, manuals, or cards without additional structured education or technical support. The effects of these simple interventions were mixed, with significant improvements observed primarily among individuals with low health literacy and uncomplicated medication regimens [31, 32, 38]. Composite interventions [8, 10, 26, 28, 36, 37, 40, 42], which combined pictograms with at least one other strategy—such as structured education, feedback, or pharmacist-led examples—generally yielded positive outcomes, with most studies reporting significant adherence improvements [10, 28, 36, 37, 40, 42]. High-technology interventions [29, 34, 39], integrating pictograms with electronic monitoring devices, real-time feedback, or personalized digital tools, demonstrated mixed results. For instance, the visual feedback therapy examined by Yoriko Kozuki et al. [39] produced significant effects, whereas the use of software-generated personalized cards and sensor monitoring methods, as reported by Monroe et al. [34] and Lisa Hynes et al. [29], did not yield significant differences between groups.

Regarding healthcare personnel involvement, pharmacists predominantly led the interventions [10, 25, 26, 42], serving as the primary designers and implementers. In contrast, other studies featured physician or nurse leadership [8, 27–29, 34, 36, 40, 41], wherein pictograms were integrated during clinical consultations. With respect to intervention duration, short-term interventions and follow-up periods [31, 32, 37, 38, 40, 41] demonstrated highly positive results, with nearly all studies reporting significant improvements and clear immediate effects of pictograms on adherence. Mid-term follow-ups [10, 25, 28, 29, 33, 34, 36], commonly employed in randomized controlled trials, yielded mixed outcomes. Long-term evaluations [8, 9, 26, 27, 39, 42], which assessed the sustainability of intervention effects, generally showed a marked attenuation of benefits over time. Notably, studies by Yoriko Kozuki et al. [39], Kalichman et al. [8], and Natalie Zerafa et al. [42] reported sustained significant effects, whereas Murray et al. [26] observed that intervention effects diminished in later stages.

Adherence effect measurement

The studies included in this review employed a diverse array of methodologies to assess medication adherence, encompassing self-reported adherence scales, pill counts, bottle volume measurements, electronic pharmacy refill records, blood drug concentration assays, viral load assessments, interview-based self-reports, and the utilization of drug event monitoring systems (e.g., MEMS). Consequently, definitions of adherence and reported outcome measures lack uniformity across studies. Among the 21 studies analyzed, twelve utilized subjective self-report questionnaires [9, 10, 25, 26, 28, 32, 33, 35, 36, 38, 41, 42] to evaluate patients’ medication-taking behaviors, beliefs, and challenges through interviews or standardized scales. For instance, Wei Hong et al. [25], Reza Negarandeh et al. [28], and Huan-Keat Chan et al. [9] employed the 8-item Morisky Medication Adherence Scale (MMAS-8), while R. Dowse et al. [28, 33] used the HIV Treatment Adherence Self-Efficacy Scale (HIV-ASES).

These instruments define adherence according to the specific scale criteria rather than by percentage adherence. Natalie Zerafa et al. [42] introduced a novel, self-developed questionnaire entitled “Assessing Patient Adherence”, which reported average adherence percentages per patient, calculated from the ratio of correct to incorrect responses. In studies by Ros Dowse et al. [38], adherence was determined via pill counts or volume measurements of antimicrobial tablets or suspensions; patients also completed questionnaires, with total adherence scores converted into percentages. Conversely, Lucy N. Ngoh et al. [37] measured adherence solely through pill counts, reporting adherence rates accordingly. One study demonstrated that pharmacist-led medication counseling combined with written information sheets incorporating pictograms significantly improved patient adherence compared to standard care [18]. Long-term adherence was evaluated in studies by Monroe, A.K. et al. [34], Michael D. Murray et al. [26], and Kripalani et al. [27] using medication dispensing records to calculate metrics such as the medication possession ratio (MPR) or continuous measure of gaps (CMG). Similarly, electronic monitoring devices—including MEMS, cDEM™ bottle caps, and Propeller Health inhaler sensors—were employed by Yoriko Kozuki et al. [39] and Lisa Hynes et al. [29] to record precise times of bottle openings or medication dispenses. Indirect adherence assessments were conducted in studies by Yoriko Kozuki et al. [39] and Kalichman, Seth C. et al. [8] through measurement of drug concentrations or disease-related biomarkers in blood samples. Overall, six studies utilized mixed-method approaches to adherence measurement [8, 32, 35, 36, 38, 39].

Outcome

Among the 21 studies included in this review, 12 (57.1%) reported a statistically significant impact of pictographic interventions on patients’ medication adherence. All studies were conducted within hospital, clinic, or consultation settings. Variations existed across studies regarding the type of drug therapy, the number of medications prescribed, and the duration of treatment (short-term versus long-term). Concerning population characteristics, individuals with low health literacy, limited literacy, or illiteracy derived the greatest benefit from pictograms [8, 28, 31, 35–37], whereas patients with high health literacy experienced limited additional benefit. Older adults showed significant improvement attributable to the concise visual cues provided by pictograms, which helped mitigate challenges related to cognitive decline, visual impairment, and polypharmacy [10, 26, 32]. In pediatric populations, adherence depends largely on caregivers, and pictograms effectively assist parents in administering medications correctly [36, 40]. The utility of pictograms is particularly pronounced in managing complex medication regimens, such as those for HIV, antipsychotic treatments, multidrug therapies, or precision medicine approaches [31]. Simple pictogram interventions demonstrated inconsistent effects, whereas composite interventions—combining pictograms with structured education, feedback methods, and pharmacist counseling—yielded more positive and reliable outcomes [10, 28, 39, 40]. Notably, pharmacist-led interventions exhibited a higher degree of consistency in improving adherence [10, 25, 26, 42].

Risk of bias assessment of the included literature

All included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were assessed using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias 2 (ROB2) tool [23]. The findings of this assessment are presented in detail in Figs. 4 and 5. Specifically, Fig. 5 displays cumulative results as stacked bar charts, illustrating the relative frequency of studies classified as having low, unclear, or high risk of bias across five domains. The analysis indicates that every RCT included in this review exhibited a high risk of bias in at least one domain. Notably, a significant source of bias stemmed from established interventions, primarily due to insufficient blinding of participants and/or personnel. In three studies [8, 26, 30], researchers were blinded to mitigate the challenges associated with blinding participants and caregivers when employing pictogram-based interventions. Although this approach represents an improvement over complete lack of blinding, the awareness of participant assignments by both participants and care providers remains a concern, potentially contributing to a high risk of bias related to established interventions. Another prominent source of bias was inadequate blinding of outcome assessment, with five out of 21 studies rated as high risk and eleven as having some risk in this domain. Additionally, eighteen studies were identified as having some risk of selective outcome reporting, and two studies were at high risk due to missing outcome data. Furthermore, ten studies exhibited some risk related to the randomization process.

Discussion

This study conducted a comprehensive search of the relevant literature concerning the impact of pictograms on patients’ medication adherence. After screening, a systematic review was performed on studies meeting the inclusion criteria, with particular attention to the basic characteristics of the included studies, study populations, interventions, adherence outcome measures, and results. The pertinent findings are summarized as follows.

The time frame of the studies included in this review spans from 1997 to 2022, demonstrating sustained scholarly interest in this research area. Early investigations primarily addressed fundamental issues, such as the utility of pictograms among populations with low literacy levels, whereas more recent studies have examined the integration of pictograms with technological tools. Geographically, nearly half of the studies originated from developed countries, notably the United States [8, 26, 27, 29, 34, 39–41], Australia [30], and Malta [42], reflecting heightened research activity in these regions. Concurrently, research conducted in low- and middle-income countries—including China [10, 25], South Africa [33, 35, 38], India [31], and Thailand [32]—underscores the recognition of pictograms as cost-effective interventions adaptable to diverse resource settings. Regarding study settings, outpatient and clinic environments were predominantly utilized, serving as primary contexts for patients to receive medication guidance and thus representing optimal sites for pictographic interventions. All included studies employed randomized controlled trial (RCT) designs; notably, nine studies implemented three- or four-arm designs, thereby enhancing the robustness of their findings by enabling the assessment of specific contributions attributable to pictograms [8, 9, 27, 28, 30, 31, 35–37]. The duration of the studies varied considerably, ranging from brief periods of 3 to 5 days to extended follow-ups of up to 24 months. Short-term studies predominantly targeted acute conditions, such as antibiotic therapy, whereas long-term studies focused on chronic disease management. Sample sizes ranged from 30 to 632 participants, with a mean sample size of 176. Larger sample studies generally concentrated on prevalent chronic diseases, including HIV [8, 33, 35]and hypertension [25], while smaller sample studies tended to focus on specific populations, such as individuals with mental disorders [39].

The study populations across the 21 included studies were clinically and demographically heterogeneous, a characteristic that both enhanced the comprehensiveness of the evidence and introduced challenges in result interpretation. This diversity indicates a broad potential applicability of pictograms as a universal communication tool; however, their effectiveness may vary according to disease characteristics and treatment requirements. Notably, seven studies specifically targeted individuals with low health literacy or limited education, among which five reported positive outcomes—an observation of significant clinical relevance. For these patients, pictograms offer an alternative mode of information processing through visual coding, thereby reducing cognitive load and overcoming text-based barriers. Conversely, pictograms appear to provide limited additional benefit in populations with adequate health literacy, where standard written instructions may already suffice. Elderly patients consistently demonstrated positive outcomes [10, 26, 32]. Given that older adults frequently experience vision impairment, cognitive decline, and polypharmacy, the salient visual features of pictograms can effectively mitigate these age-related challenges and serve as intuitive medication reminders. Similarly, studies involving pediatric patients suggest that pictograms indirectly enhance adherence by guiding caregivers in the correct administration of treatments [30, 36, 40].

This study demonstrates the critical influence of intervention design on the effectiveness of pictogram-based strategies. Simple interventions showed mixed effectiveness, depending heavily on the quality of the pictogram design (e.g., cultural appropriateness, clarity of the visual metaphor, use of colors, and inclusion of explanatory text) and on the patient’s capacity for autonomous use [17, 20]. Composite interventions exhibited the most substantial and consistent positive impact, likely because they combine pictograms with professional guidance (e.g., from pharmacists or nurses) that addresses both comprehension and behavioral reinforcement. Technology-enhanced interventions yielded mixed results: visual feedback therapy demonstrated significant effects [39], whereas software-generated personalized cards and sensor-based monitoring did not [29, 34]. These mixed outcomes suggest that technology is not a panacea but a promising direction requiring further validation. The inherent complexity of these multifaceted interventions, particularly composite ones, constrains our ability to isolate the specific contribution of pictograms to the observed adherence outcomes [28]. For example, Kalichman et al. [8] combined pictograms with adherence counseling and adherence tools to improve antiretroviral therapy outcomes. Although the study reported a statistically significant effect on adherence, it was not possible to isolate the specific contribution of pictograms to the observed outcomes. For instance, in the study by Reza Negarandeh et al. [28], a nurse-led diabetes medication education intervention was combined with either Isakari or pictograms, with participants divided into two separate intervention groups. Both intervention groups were compared to standard care, and a significant difference in medication adherence was observed between the combined intervention groups and the control group; however, no significant difference was found between the two intervention groups themselves [28]. In contrast, L. E. Mansoor et al. evaluated patient information leaflets (PIL) in two intervention groups—one with pictograms and one without—compared to standard care. In this study, adherence to antiretroviral therapy (ARVT) was significantly improved in the group receiving pictographic PIL compared to the other two groups [35]. To accurately assess the effects of pictograms, study designs must enable direct comparison between two interventions that differ solely in the inclusion of pictograms.

Significant variability existed in the methods employed to measure adherence across the studies, which directly influenced the interpretation of the intervention effects. Subjective measures, predominantly utilizing various self-report questionnaires, were more likely to yield positive outcomes; notably, 10 of the 12 studies employing these methods reported favorable results. However, such approaches are vulnerable to recall bias and social desirability bias, particularly when patients are aware that they are receiving specialized interventions, potentially leading to overreporting of adherence. In contrast, objective measures more accurately reflect actual medication-taking behavior but tend to report a lower proportion of positive findings. Among these, electronic monitoring is regarded as the most rigorous method, although it is costly and may alter patients’ usual behavior. While tablet counting is an objective measure, it does not provide information regarding the timing or regularity of medication intake. Biomarkers offer the most objective efficacy endpoints but are limited to specific drugs and diseases. Methodologically robust studies often employ multiple measurement methods to assess adherence from diverse perspectives, thereby providing more comprehensive evidence [8, 39]. Nevertheless, the majority of studies rely on a single measurement method, which constrains the reliability of their findings [43]. In this review, 6 of 21 studies employed mixed methods to assess adherence [8, 32, 35, 36, 38, 39], with 5 reporting significant effects on adherence. The analysis and interpretation of results become increasingly complex when multiple measurement methods are applied to the same outcome. Consequently, it is essential to select complementary rather than potentially conflicting measures; for instance, combining subjective and objective assessments while considering their relevance to the study context, participant characteristics, and other practical factors. A substantial number of studies included in this systematic review utilized subjective self-reported adherence measures to evaluate pictorial effects. We recommend the use of consistent and validated questionnaires to facilitate statistical comparisons across studies. Self-reported adherence scales are valuable for assessing medication-taking behaviors and identifying barriers to adherence or patient beliefs related to adherence [44]. The selection of the most appropriate questionnaire is critical in any intervention study measuring adherence, and the timing of measurement also significantly influences the interpretation of findings.

The findings of this study should be interpreted in the context of healthcare settings that are progressively shaped by digitalization and artificial intelligence (AI). The studies included in this review, primarily conducted prior to the widespread adoption of advanced AI technologies, demonstrate the effectiveness of pictograms within traditional and early digital frameworks, such as electronic labels and basic digital reminders. Nevertheless, the rapid advancement of intelligent health technologies presents new opportunities for the application of pictograms. AI has the potential to transform pictogram use by generating personalized, context-aware visual instructions tailored to individual patient profiles, languages, and cultural backgrounds. Furthermore, smart packaging and connected devices may incorporate dynamic pictograms that offer real-time feedback and adherence monitoring. Although the evidence supporting these advanced integrations remains limited, the fundamental principle that well-designed visual aids enhance comprehension and adherence is well established. The relatively few AI-focused studies included in this analysis reflect the current state of the published literature rather than a limitation of the review, underscoring the need for further research in this area. This study connects the established evidence base for traditional pictograms with the emerging wave of digital innovation, suggesting that the future of pictograms lies not in their replacement by technology but in their synergistic enhancement through it.

Implications for Digital Health and a Proposed Future Roadmap. While the evidence synthesized in this review is predominantly derived from studies utilizing analog pictograms, it offers invaluable insights to guide their evolution within the rapidly advancing digital health landscape. The consistent finding that pictograms are most beneficial for managing complex regimens, among populations with limited health literacy, and when delivered as part of a multi-component intervention, provides a critical foundation for their optimized implementation in digital platforms. To bridge the gap between traditional use and future potential, we propose a strategic roadmap centered on three foundational pillars for the development of “Smart Pictograms.”

(1) AI-Driven Personalization and Dynamic Generation. The future of pictograms lies in moving beyond static, one-size-fits-all images. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning algorithms can harness individual patient data—including health literacy level, cultural context, specific medication regimen, and sensory abilities—to dynamically generate or select the most appropriate pictographic content. For example, a patient initiating a complex anticoagulation therapy could be provided with a personalized visual schedule, generated in real-time by an AI model. This model would be trained on principles of visual communication and could integrate the schedule with the patient’s unique daily routine, thereby enhancing relevance and comprehension. (2) Seamless Integration with Smart Devices for Real-Time Feedback. The mixed outcomes observed in early technology-enhanced interventions underscore the necessity for more cohesive integration into the patient’s ecosystem. Pictograms should be natively embedded within the user interfaces of smart pill dispensers, mobile health applications, and other connected devices. Beyond passive display, they must be functionally linked to adherence monitoring systems. For instance, if a smart medication bottle registers a missed dose, the paired application should not only issue a reminder but also proactively display the corresponding pictogram for the specific medication and dosage. This creates a closed-loop, interactive feedback system, transforming pictograms from static informational aids into dynamic components of an adaptive adherence strategy. (3) Interoperability and Standardization for Scalable Implementation. To achieve widespread adoption and impact within digital health infrastructures, pictograms must overcome the limitations of proprietary, isolated systems. A critical priority is the development of standardized, machine-readable pictogram libraries. Utilizing scalable vector graphics (SVGs) or establishing a formal ontology would facilitate seamless integration across diverse digital platforms, including Electronic Health Records (EHRs), pharmacy management systems, and consumer-facing health applications. Such interoperability would ensure that a pictogram selected during a clinical encounter within an EHR is automatically propagated to a patient’s personal health record and medication adherence app, guaranteeing consistency, reducing cognitive load, and minimizing implementation barriers for healthcare providers and patients alike.

The limited number of studies in our review that incorporated digital elements [24, 29, 34] represent formative steps in this direction. Their varied outcomes highlight that technology, in isolation, is not a panacea. Success will be determined by how effectively technology is leveraged to augment the core strengths of pictograms—simplicity and intuitive clarity—while introducing new capabilities through intelligence and interactivity. Consequently, future primary research must prioritize the design, development, and rigorous evaluation of these “Smart Pictogram” interventions to establish a robust, contemporary evidence base for the digital age.

Based on the findings from 21 studies, 12 (57.1%) reported that pictograms significantly improved adherence. However, this apparent predominance of positive outcomes should be interpreted with caution, as these effects are primarily observed in specific contexts: individuals with low health literacy, older patients, multimodal interventions administered by healthcare professionals, and studies with short-term follow-up. In these scenarios, the efficacy of pictograms is most evident. Conversely, negative findings offer valuable insights, indicating that pictograms often provide limited additional benefit when interventions target individuals with average health literacy, involve simple interventions, utilize objective adherence measurements, or assess long-term outcomes. Many studies reported no significant effect on patient adherence, which is largely attributed to insufficient sample sizes. Measuring adherence has proven challenging in numerous cases, underscoring the need to standardize reproducible adherence metrics. Among the included studies, self-reported adherence via questionnaires or interviews was the most consistent method for detecting differences in adherence. Nonetheless, other methodological factors may also contribute to the absence of significant findings in several studies. Importantly, the systematic review provides sufficient evidence to support the hypothesis that pictogram-based interventions can enhance patient adherence to medication.

Although this review identified the potential value of pictograms, the methodological foundation of these findings warrants cautious interpretation. All included studies exhibited a risk of bias in at least one domain, most frequently performance bias arising from the inability to blind patients and implementers, as assessed by the Cochrane ROB2 tool. Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies regarding interventions, population characteristics, outcome definitions, and measurement time points, which constrained the feasibility of conducting a meta-analysis. These methodological limitations underscore the limited robustness of the current evidence concerning the effects of pictograms, highlighting the need for additional high-quality research to yield more definitive conclusions.

Future research should therefore emphasize: (1) methodologically rigorous, long-term follow-up studies employing objective outcome measures; (2) the investigation of these novel “Smart Pictogram” paradigms, combining pictograms with AI-driven personalization and smart device integration; (3) the development of evidence-based standards for pictogram design and validation within digital frameworks; and (4) examinations of the cost-effectiveness and implementation feasibility of these advanced pictographic interventions to guide policy decisions.

Limitations

The aforementioned studies exhibit several limitations. First, despite conducting comprehensive searches across multiple Chinese and English databases, it was not possible to capture all relevant grey literature. The systematic review encompassed studies published between 1997 and 2022, spanning a 25-year period. Notably, the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) statement was introduced in 2010 [45], and earlier studies may suffer from methodological shortcomings. The recently updated CONSORT 2025 statement further delineates best practices for randomized controlled trial (RCT) reporting [46]. Of the 21 included studies, eight were conducted prior to 2010, and subsequent advancements in healthcare and patient information access may limit the applicability of these older studies. Second, the included RCTs demonstrated considerable clinical and methodological heterogeneity in terms of study design, interventions, participant characteristics, and outcome measures. This heterogeneity precluded the performance of meta-analyses to derive pooled effect estimates, restricting the analysis to descriptive synthesis and thereby somewhat diminishing the strength of the evidence. Finally, there is a paucity of long-term follow-up studies, resulting in limited understanding of the sustained effects of object mapping interventions. Furthermore, as previously noted, the emerging domain of AI-generated content and intelligently adaptive pictograms remains underrepresented in the existing corpus of randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Therefore, our review was unable to formally evaluate the effects of these advanced technologies. This gap highlights a critical and timely avenue for future research. Future research should address these limitations by employing standardized measurement instruments, more rigorous study designs, and extended follow-up periods to generate more robust and reliable evidence.

Conclusion

The findings of this study hold significant implications for clinical practice and policy development. Pictograms represent a cost-effective communication strategy, particularly in resource-constrained environments and when addressing the needs of vulnerable populations. Healthcare organizations should consider establishing a repository of culturally appropriate, standardized pictograms and integrating them into routine pharmacy service protocols. Critically, this review demonstrates that to remain relevant and maximize their impact, pictograms must evolve from passive visuals into active components of the digital health infrastructure. We conclude that the future lies in the development of “Smart Pictograms”—AI-personalized, seamlessly integrated with smart devices for real-time feedback, and built upon interoperable standards for scalability. This evolution will enable pictograms to transition from universal, static images to dynamic, personalized, and context-aware digital assets.

In conclusion, pictograms are not a universal solution but rather a context-specific tool whose successful implementation depends on precise targeting, integration within multifaceted educational strategies, and a decisive transition into the digital health landscape. This systematic review provides updated empirical support for the use of pictograms and delineates their evolving role, moving from static visuals to dynamic, intelligent components of patient education. The future lies in the development and rigorous evaluation of “Smart Pictograms” to fully realize their potential in enhancing medication adherence and promoting health equity.
