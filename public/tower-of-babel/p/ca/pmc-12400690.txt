Knowledge and experience of local emergency care staff on stroke recognition and acute care in the United Arab Emirates

Introduction

Cerebrovascular diseases place a significant burden on patients, the healthcare system, society, and carers especially if inadequately treated [1, 2]. Each minute of ischemia during an acute stroke results in the impairment of 1.9 million neurons, causing irreversible neuronal damage that may lead to lifelong disability or mortality [3]. In the United States of America (USA), stroke mortality accounts for approximately 1 in every 6 deaths from cardiovascular disease, with nearly 160,000 deaths each year [4]. However, stroke mortality has significantly reduced as a result of systems improvement in primary prevention, and acute stroke care. Similar improved outcomes have been reported in Europe with lower mortality rates in Western Europe compared to Central and Eastern European Countries [5]. Differences in the reported results are a reflection of the maturation of the stroke network which includes development in stroke primary prevention, improved community awareness of stroke symptoms, efficient emergency medical services, and easy access to hospital hyper-acute stroke care. The success of a stroke system depends on the strength of the links between the individual components in the pathway [6]. Awareness and recognition of stroke symptoms in the community, access to emergency medical services, and hospitals with the necessary expertise are crucial for a successful stroke system. In large hospitals, emergency personnel typically manage stroke patients in conjunction with an on-call neurologist, who may be present in person or accessible via a remote telecommunication system. However, this may not be so in smaller EDs and those located in low-resource communities where a policy of ‘drip and ship’ is more pragmatic.

In the United Arab Emirates (UAE), cerebrovascular disease is among the top three causes of disability and death [2, 7]. Previous studies on stroke in the UAE reported a substantial rise in stroke prevalence over time, increasing from 483.2 per 100,000 people in 1990 to 2,168.4 per 100,000 in 2019 [7]. To promote stroke awareness and improve outcomes, we had a stroke conference in 2021 followed by a stroke workshop in 2023. Furthermore, the Abu Dhabi stroke network was established in 2022 with the sole purpose of reducing the burden of stroke diseases in the Emirate of Abu Dhabi, the UAE. Despite anecdotal evidence of improved stroke outcomes since the establishment of the network, there remain some challenges such as delayed patient contact with the EMS, deficiencies in the identification of stroke patients in the emergency departments, and over-activation of the stroke team. Improving the emergency department staff’s understanding of stroke symptoms, stroke risk factors, and treatment modalities may mitigate some of these challenges. Furthermore, ED staff’s understanding and familiarity with local stroke protocols and pathways are important in identifying areas that may need quality enhancement with the resultant improvement in stroke outcomes [8–10].

This study aims to assess the experience, knowledge and attitude of emergency department staff about acute stroke care and to identify areas for educational and training interventions that may improve outcomes in stroke patients in Alain City, the UAE.

Methods

Ethical approval and consent

The study was conducted in compliance with the ethics declaration of Helsinki and it received approval from the Abu Dhabi Health Research and Technology Ethics Committee. (Ref: DOH/CVDC/2023/836). All participants in the study gave their written informed consent to use their data for research.

Study setting

Al Ain City, is in the Al Ain Region of Abu Dhabi Emirate, the UAE. Al Ain City has a population of 1,009,735 inhabitants and is served by seven healthcare facilities that provide emergency or urgent care services [11]. One of the hospitals (Tawam Hospital) was awarded the comprehensive (highest) stroke centre accreditation by the American Heart and Stroke Association in 2023. This means it must provide regular stroke update training for its staff to maintain the status.

Population

The study population was medical and nursing staff who work at the ED or urgent care centres of the seven healthcare facilities in AlAin region. We approached all the seven healthcare facilities of which three government hospitals (Tawam Hospital, Alain Hospital, and Al Wagan Hospital), and one private (Al Khaleej Hospital) agreed to take part in the study. The three hospitals that did not participate were private hospitals with limited neurology services that would normally refer their stroke patients to government hospitals.

Study design

This is an observational cross-sectional study of the experience, knowledge, attitude and practice of acute stroke care among emergency care staff at four healthcare facilities in Al Ain City. The study was completed between 4th August 2023 to 15th October 2024.

We collected study data using an electronic questionnaire survey administered in person to all responders during multiple visits to the hospital EDs and to locations where staff typically convene for meetings. During these visits, we introduced the study, explained its objectives, and invited staff to participate by scanning a QR code that linked directly to the survey. For those who preferred a paper-based option, a hard copy was provided; responses from the paper copies were subsequently entered into the survey software. We aimed to recruit a representative sample of the nursing and medical staff that work in the participating hospitals.

Inclusion criteria: All emergency physicians, internal medicine doctors, and nurses working in the participating hospitals. Exclusion criteria: All other physicians such as surgeons, neurologists, paediatricians, intensive care, and cardiologists.

At the time of the survey, there were a total of 393 doctors and nurses working at the study sites which comprised 232 nurses and 161 doctors. We used standard sample size estimation for a finite population, with the following assumptions; a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and maximum variability (p = 0.5). This gives a sample size of 195.

The study survey tool

We developed the survey questionnaire after reviewing and adapting multiple published acute stroke care research questionnaires (see Supplementary Materials. Study questionnaire). The content and face validity of the survey were performed by the study investigators and through feedback from six healthcare professionals from outside our study sample. To evaluate the validity and reliability of the survey, we performed repeated pilot studies and iterated the questions based on the feedback until we achieved a Cronbach’s alpha score of > 0.7.

We administered the survey questionnaire electronically using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tool, hosted at the United Arab Emirates University [12]. The web-based platform is secure offering features such as customizable survey instruments, validated data entry interfaces, real-time data validation, and audit trails for tracking changes. The survey questionnaire consists of a consent section followed by six information domains, including socio-demographics, country of educational training, knowledge of stroke symptoms, stroke risk factors and therapies, care process measures, attitudes, and beliefs about acute stroke care. Participants were recruited through multiple in-person visits to the hospital EDs and to locations where staff typically convene for meetings. During these visits, we introduced the study, explained its objectives, and invited staff to participate by scanning a QR code that linked directly to the survey. For those who preferred a paper-based option, a hard copy was provided; responses from the paper copies were subsequently entered into the survey software. The results were analyzed together so we could appreciate the team learning gap and design a training intervention based on the educational needs of the team as a whole. This will enhance inter-professional training and teamwork. This approach is similar to that recommended by the American Heart/Stroke Association (AHA) for Advanced stroke life support (ASLS) and Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS).

Statistical analysis

We reported categorical data as frequencies and percentages, while numerical data were reported as means and standard deviations. We used inferential statistics to explore the differences in the stroke knowledge scores between groups. Unpaired t-tests and one-way ANOVA were utilized to compare the knowledge scores and their components. Although the knowledge scores did not follow a normal distribution, no substantial differences were observed in the p-values between parametric and non-parametric tests. All analyses were done using the statistical software IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. A test was regarded as significant if the p-values were below 5%.

Results

We enrolled 215 participants and 178 (83%) completed the study. Table 1 shows the participants’ demographics, country of primary qualification and experience. Just over half (56%) of the responders were physicians while the remainder were nurses. The majority of the responders (43.1%) had their primary medical and nursing degrees from the UAE followed by India and the Philippines (13% each) demonstrating the multi-national characteristics of the UAE workforce. Two-thirds (66.1%) of the responders work in a tertiary care centre and about a third 49 (32.7%) have personally cared for more than 10 stroke or TIA patients in the past three months, while 25 (16.7%) did not care for any stroke or TIA patients in three months.

Knowledge of stroke symptoms and risk factors

Figures 1 and 2 show overall recognition of stroke symptoms and identification of stroke risk factors. 75% or more of the responders identified the triad of symptoms of facial droop, unilateral arm or leg weakness and slurred speech, which are the components of the face-arm-speech (FAST) screening test, as indicative of an acute stroke. Facial or arm numbness and imbalance were recognised as stroke symptoms by just over 65% of the responders while less than 50% identified vertigo, severe headache and double vision as stroke symptoms. This indicates that the responders were more familiar with symptoms of anterior circulation stroke but less so with posterior circulation stroke symptoms of balance and visual disturbances. The majority of the responders (62.4%) considered dizziness as not indicative of stroke while 40.6% and 33.1% of the responders identified loss of consciousness and seizures respectively as non-stroke symptoms. Over two-thirds of the responders identified hypertension, diabetes mellitus, smoking and dyslipidemia as risk factors for stroke while only 61% recognised atrial fibrillation as a risk factor. Other factors such as chronic kidney disease, alcohol use and obstructive sleep apnoea were less recognised as stroke risk factors by responders.

Table 2 shows comparisons of stroke and knowledge scores by categories. Participants who work at tertiary centres were significantly more able to identify the stroke symptoms of sudden arm weakness (Chi-square df 6.297, p = 0.043) and slurring of speech (Chi-square df 7.395, p = 0.025) compared with those who work at secondary or primary centres. Doctors identified loss of consciousness as an atypical stroke symptom significantly more than nurses (Chi-square df 4.373, p = 0.037). Doctors also demonstrated significant higher ability to identify stroke risk factors of diabetes (Chi-square df 5.483, p = 0.019), dyslipidemia (Chi-square df 2.95, p = 0.086), atrial fibrillation (Chi-square df 16.561, p < 0.001), smoking (Chi-square df 3.027, p = 0.082), chewing tobacco (Chi-square df 3.952, p = 0.047) and chronic kidney disease (Chi-square df 4.156, p = 0.041) compared with nurses.

Stroke disease prognosis

In response to the statement: ‘Stroke patients do not do well overall and are disabled for the rest of their lives’ significantly more nurses (14.7%) strongly agreed compared with 3.2% of doctors (P = 0.044). Similarly, the response to the statement “IV tPA and mechanical thrombectomy have a great impact on patient recovery” showed a significant difference by place of work. Primary care centre (55.8%), Secondary centre (68.8%) and Tertiary centre (77.4%) (Chi-Square df 7.17, P = 0.028).

Eighty-four percent 84% (84%) of the responders agreed intravenous tPA is available at all times for the treatment of acute stroke at their workplaces and 70% of the participants have given IV t-PA in the past. There is a significant difference by profession in the likelihood of providing IV tPA for eligible stroke patients with doctors being more inclined to provide the treatment compared with nurses. Discussion

This study aimed to assess the knowledge, experience and practice of emergency department staff about acute stroke care in Alain City, the UAE. The results show that being a nurse, working in a primary or secondary care setting and having < 2 years of experience have a significant negative correlation with the knowledge scores of acute stroke.

Participants in our study showed significant variations in their ability to recognise signs and symptoms of acute stroke. There was a limited ability of responders to recognise vertigo, headache and double vision as symptoms of stroke. There was also a gap in the ability of responders, especially nurses and responders with < 2 years of experience, to identify risk factors for stroke. The results are similar to that of a cross-sectional study in the Emirate of Dubai, UAE that reported that only 68% of the participating emergency medical service staff were able to precisely recognise the two main stroke types. However, there was a significant improvement following educational intervention [13].

In our study, physicians scored higher in the knowledge domain than nurses. This aligned with a study from Cyprus in which physicians scored 15.7/28 on stroke management questions compared to 12.6/28 for nurses [14]. A similar trend was reported in Iran where a multivariate regression model revealed that knowledge of stroke signs and the attitude toward stroke care were significant predictors of acute stroke management [15]. In our setting, patient triage is carried out by nurses. Thus, targeted stroke training for nurses is likely to improve stroke identification in the ED. Furthermore, those working at primary and secondary care centres are at a disadvantage due to reduced exposure rates and a lack of acute stroke care teams. These challenges can be overcome by organising the stroke service as a hub and spoke system with the EMS given the right to bypass primary and secondary care facilities and go directly to the tertiary stroke facilities. A similar model was planned for the UAE when in July 2022, the Abu Dhabi Department of Health classified stroke treating facilities into three categories: Comprehensive (tertiary), Primary (Stroke units), and Stroke-Ready (Drip and Shift) centres, based on their geographical locations and ability to provide acute treatment. The arrangement ensures that all patients within the Emirate have access to treatment that is both safe and timely [16]. However, a significant proportion of our stroke patients still self-present to the primary and stroke-ready centres without calling for an ambulance and their prompt treatment will still depend on the knowledge and experience of staff at these centres. Thus staff working at these centres need bespoke regular training in stroke care.

The level of participants’ experience correlated with stroke knowledge, with those with < 2 years of experience performing worse compared with others. The exposure level and seeing stroke patients daily is equally important as reported in a study from Malaysia which concluded that healthcare professionals with limited experience in stroke management should be given priority in stroke education to ensure that a greater number of stroke patients receive timely and advanced care [17].

The assessment of healthcare professionals’ attitudes about stroke prognosis and treatment impact revealed significant variations by profession and workplace setting. Nurses, particularly in non-tertiary settings, were more likely to perceive poor prognoses, potentially due to limited experience as reported in similar studies [18, 19]. Studies have shown that this gap can be addressed by implementing a structured and tailored competency-based program that focuses on nurses’ knowledge, skills, and attitudes toward stroke care [19, 20].

The workplace setting of our study cohort significantly influenced responses to the question on the impact of IV tPA and mechanical thrombectomy on patients’ recovery. With healthcare professionals in tertiary centres exhibiting a positive correlation. This can be explained by their exposure to routine use of reperfusion treatments and witnessed improved functional outcomes of stroke patients [18, 19, 21, 22].

About 11% of the responders were concerned about the value of intravenous tPA. This may be a reflection of their limited experience with stroke patients who have received tPA. A study from the United States reported that non-tertiary healthcare professionals may overemphasize the bleeding complications of IV tPA due to the limited capability to manage complications in such settings [21]. Furthermore, the ANGEL-ACT Registry (Endovascular Treatment Key Technique and Emergency Work Flow Improvement of Acute Ischemic Stroke) study in China reported that non-tertiary hospitals often skip IV tPA due to multiple factors including patient or family refusal, cost concerns, or physician hesitancy, leading to lower success rates and higher scepticism [23]. Some of these factors may also have influenced the responses of our cohort.

Limitations

First, three of the seven eligible hospitals declined to participate in the study. However, we think this bias is mitigated in that the participating hospitals represented the three layers of our stroke network. Furthermore, our high response rate, the robustness of our questionnaire design and administration gives us confidence in the validity of our results. Second, our results are potential influenced by training variation across study sites as the tertiary care centre with a comprehensive stroke service and the introduction of mandatory stroke update training may have contributed disproportionately to higher knowledge and attitude scores compared to facilities with fewer patients exposure. However only a few staff from the tertiary centre would have benefitted from such regular training. Nonetheless, these limitations should be considered when interpreting inter-facility comparisons and generalizing our findings to the broader healthcare setting.

Conclusions

This study identified significant knowledge gaps among staff working in our stroke care system which were not addressed by previous stroke conferences and workshops. To further improve the outcomes for stroke patients in our setting, there is a need for regular educational updates for front-line staff especially those working at secondary centres with limited experience of daily stroke care.
