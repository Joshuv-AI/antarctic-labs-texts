Reconsidering Molecular Docking Practices in Aptamer Research
Authors: Yachen Xie, Juewen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMolecular docking is increasingly used to infer aptamer–target interactions, yet most studies rely on computationally predicted aptamer structures rather than experimentally determined ones. Using a benchmark set of aptamers with known high‐resolution structures, we show that commonly used modeling approaches, including RNAComposer and AlphaFold3, fail to reliably reproduce aptamer conformations, particularly at the binding sites critical for molecular recognition. Key limitations include the use of A‐form RNA models to represent B‐form DNA structures, the prediction of ligand‐free rather than ligand‐bound conformations, and the scarcity of experimentally determined aptamer structures for training machine‐learning models. Using the theophylline aptamer, for which high‐resolution structures are available in both DNA and RNA forms, we systematically evaluated each step of the standard docking workflow. We found that structure‐prediction errors generate incorrect binding pockets, docking scores fail to distinguish theophylline from caffeine despite a 250,000‐fold difference in affinity, and molecular dynamics simulations do not overcome these shortcomings. Together, these results reveal fundamental weaknesses in current aptamer docking workflows and caution against using docking‐derived models to infer binding mechanisms in the absence of experimental structural data.Keywords: aptamer, binding affinity, docking, specificity

Introduction

Aptamers are frequently regarded as nucleic acid counterparts of antibodies, while offering unique advantages such as precise chemical synthesis, facile site‐specific modification, programmable molecular interactions through base pairing, and direct integration with amplification and sequencing technologies [1, 5]. Despite the thousands of reported aptamers, high‐resolution structural characterization remains a major bottleneck in the field. To date, only about a dozen DNA aptamer structures have been experimentally resolved, compared with more than 240,000 protein structures available in the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [6].

Against this backdrop, the past decade has seen a sharp rise in the use of molecular docking in aptamer research, reaching 106 publications in 2025 alone (Figure 1). Strikingly, only ~10% of these studies utilized experimentally determined aptamer structures, while most relied on structures predicted directly from sequence. This trend likely reflects the success of modern structure‐prediction tools such as AlphaFold and the increasing accessibility of web‐based docking platforms [7]. Consequently, docking is now widely used to infer aptamer–target binding modes, guide sequence optimization, and even perform in silico SELEX, often in the absence of experimental structural validation.

Growth of aptamer‐related molecular docking studies from 2015 to 2025 (blue) compared with the much smaller number of studies using experimentally determined aptamer structures from NMR or X‐ray crystallography (red; with PDB IDs reported).

However, the reliability of molecular docking depends critically on the quality of the input structure. Docking predicts ligand poses and binding energies assuming that the receptor adopts a reasonably accurate, binding‐competent conformation [8]. While this assumption is often justified for proteins, where high‐resolution structures are abundant, it is far more problematic for aptamers, for which experimentally determined structures remain exceedingly rare. As a result, uncertainties in aptamer structure may dominate the overall prediction accuracy.

Upon examining the current workflows, we identified several fundamental issues, including intrinsic structural differences between DNA and RNA, discrepancies between ligand‐free and ligand‐bound conformations, limitations in secondary‐structure prediction tools, and the general absence of experimental validation. Here, we critically evaluate the commonly used docking workflow step by step, using solved aptamer structures as benchmarks, and offer recommendations for the appropriate and responsible use of docking in aptamer research.

Materials and Methods

Literature Survey

Web of Science was searched on 2 January 2026 using the query ALL=(“docking” AND “aptamer”) for publications from 2015 to 2025. Only primary research articles were retained; reviews and conference papers were excluded, yielding a total of 464 articles. For each article, cited PDB IDs were checked individually against the Protein Data Bank. An article was counted as using an experimentally determined (real) nucleic‐acid structure only if (i) the cited PDB entry was a nucleic‐acid (RNA or DNA) aptamer structure and (ii) that structure was actually used in the subsequent docking or computation. Articles that cited a PDB entry and docked a predicted aptamer onto it were recorded as using a predicted aptamer structure.

Secondary Structure Prediction

The secondary structures of the aptamers were predicted using mFold with default parameters at 25 °C and 150 mM Na+ and 5 mM Mg2+ [9].

Three‐Dimensional Structure Generation

An experimentally determined aptamer structure was defined as a small molecule, or metal‐ion‐binding aptamer with a deposited PDB structure determined with NMR or X‐ray crystallography. Protein‐binding aptamers, DNAzymes, and modified aptamers were excluded from this analysis. All experimentally determined aptamer structures were obtained from the Protein Data Bank.

RNAComposer structures were generated using RNAComposer from mFold predicted secondary structures [10]. RNA structures were converted to DNA by replacing ribose with 2′‐deoxyribose and uracil with thymine. This conversion was performed in VMD [11] using the CHARMM36 force field [12]. AlphaFold3 structures were predicted using the AlphaFold Server with sequences shown in Table S1 [7].

Structures were superimposed in ChimeraX 1.9 [13] with Matchmaker using the Nucleic matrix and the Smith–Waterman algorithm. Because each predicted model and its experimental reference share the same sequence, residues were matched 1:1. Fitting and RMSD used one point per residue (the C4′ atom), and RMSD was reported for the backbone over all residues. All the sequences used in this work were taken from their corresponding PDB structure, and are listed in Table S1.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was performed using Autodock Vina [8]. Ligand structures for theophylline and caffeine were obtained from crystal structures (PDB IDs: 8K0W and 9XUA) [14]. Docking was carried out on the holo crystal conformation of the theophylline aptamer with the target theophylline removed. The binding site was defined with a grid box of 26 × 24 × 24 Å centered at the loop region of the aptamer. Each docking run generated nine poses, ranked by ΔG.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) Simulations

For each of the four docking systems (crystal–theophylline, crystal–caffeine, predicted–theophylline, predicted–caffeine), three representative poses were selected from the docking output. The poses with lower binding free energy (ΔG) were prioritized. When ΔG values were comparable, the pose with more hydrogen bonds was chosen. This resulted in 12 independent MD simulations. The experimentally determined crystal complex structure (PDB ID: 8K0W) was also simulated as a reference baseline, yielding 13 simulations in total. Each aptamer compound was simulated by the same MD cascade as below. An aptamer complex was solvated in an 8 Å water box, with 0.15 M NaCl and 0.005 M MgCl2 by the VMD program [11]. Then, this system was minimized, annealed, equilibrated and produced using the NAMD program [15] with the all‐atom CHARMM36 nucleic acid force field [12]. The theophylline and caffeine force fields were generated by the Swiss Param online server [16].

In all MD cascades, bonds to all hydrogen atoms were constrained by the SHAKE algorithm [17], and the time step was 2 fs. The system was simulated in periodic boundary conditions, with full electrostatics computed using the PME method [18] with grid spacing on the order of 1 Å or less. Short‐range nonbonded terms were evaluated every step using a 12 Å cut‐off for van der Waals interactions and a smooth switching function. The system was first minimized with all heavy atoms of the aptamer–ligand complex positionally fixed, allowing water molecules and ions to relax, followed by a second minimization of the entire system without any restraints. Harmonic positional restraints were then applied to all heavy atoms of the aptamer and ligand (force constant: 30 kcal/mol/Å2) during canonical ensemble (NVT) heating from 0 to 368 K (100 ps) and subsequent cooling from 368 to 298 K (100 ps), while water molecules and ions remained unrestrained throughout. The restraint force constant was reduced to 10 kcal/mol/Å2 during the first 2 ns of the isothermal–isobaric ensemble (NPT) equilibration at 298 K and 1 atm, after which all restraints were removed for the remaining 4 ns of NPT equilibration and the subsequent 40 ns production dynamics. The temperature and pressure were maintained by the Langevin piston Nosé–Hoover method [19].

All the MD data were based on the MD trajectory of production dynamics. VMD was used to analyze the MD trajectories.

Results and Discussion

The Current Docking Workflow and Limitations

Molecular docking requires a three‐dimensional aptamer structure as its starting point. Because experimentally determined aptamer structures are rarely available, two computational strategies are commonly used. In the first, thymine residues are replaced with uracil, an RNA structure is generated using tools such as RNAComposer, and the resulting model is then converted back to DNA by replacing ribose with deoxyribose and U with T [20]. This convoluted workflow exists because no broadly validated methods are currently available for accurate de novo prediction of folded DNA aptamer tertiary structures directly from sequence. This approach suffers from two fundamental limitations. First, it does not reproduce typical DNA conformations. RNAComposer generates A‐form RNA helices, whereas DNA predominantly adopts the B‐form, which differs in sugar pucker (C3′‐endo vs. C2′‐endo), backbone geometry, base stacking, and groove dimensions. Simple substitution of ribose and uracil cannot convert an A‐form structure into a B‐form one, leading to systematic geometric distortions that may compromise docking results. These challenges are further exacerbated by fundamental differences in tertiary structure formation, where RNA often derives structural complexity from global backbone folding, whereas DNA frequently relies on localized noncanonical motifs [21]. Second, the resulting structures represent ligand‐free conformations. Unless binding follows a strict lock‐and‐key mechanism, the unbound and bound states can differ substantially. Structural studies, including NMR titration experiments, have shown that many aptamers undergo induced‐fit binding, in which ligand recognition reorganizes local base‐pairing and noncanonical interaction networks [22]. Consequently, ligand‐free models often fail to capture the functional binding conformation required for reliable docking predictions.

In the second strategy, aptamer structures are predicted directly from sequence using AlphaFold3 or related platforms [7]. Although target molecules can theoretically be included during modeling, current servers support only a small set of predefined ligands and ions, excluding most aptamer targets. Consequently, most docking studies still rely on predicted structures of target‐free aptamers. While this approach avoids the geometric artifacts associated with RNA‐to‐DNA conversion, its accuracy is fundamentally limited by the small number of experimentally determined aptamer structures available for training. As a result, despite the success of AlphaFold in protein structure prediction, the reliable prediction of aptamer tertiary structures and ligand‐bound conformations remains a major unresolved challenge.

To illustrate these fundamental limitations, we used the theophylline aptamers as an example to examine each step of the current docking workflow. High‐resolution crystal structures are available for both the DNA and RNA theophylline aptamers, and their well‐established ability to discriminate between theophylline and caffeine (differing by only a methyl group) provides an ideal benchmark for assessing docking reliability [14, 23].

Comparison of Predicted and Crystal Structures

In the commonly used RNAComposer workflow, construction of a three‐dimensional DNA aptamer model begins with secondary‐structure prediction. The predicted secondary structure serves as the primary input for tertiary‐structure generation, meaning that any errors introduced at this stage propagate throughout the subsequent modeling and docking process. Figure 2A shows the secondary structure of the theophylline DNA aptamer derived from its crystal structure (PDB ID: 8K0W), whereas Figure 2B shows the mFold prediction [9]. Although mFold correctly identifies the Watson–Crick stem–loop, it fails to capture the critical three‐tiered stacking motif that forms the binding pocket. This is because mFold infers structure solely by minimizing pairwise base‐pairing ΔG terms [9], and therefore cannot account for the extensive noncanonical interactions that stabilize many DNA aptamers.

A three‐dimensional structure was then generated from the mFold‐derived base‐pairing pattern using the DNA→RNA→DNA procedure via RNAComposer and VMD [10, 11]. Root‐mean‐square deviation (RMSD) is the standard metric for evaluating structural prediction accuracy. When overlaid with the crystal structure (Figure 2C), the RNAComposer model gives an overall RMSD of 5.7 Å, which may suggest reasonable agreement. However, because it averages deviations across all aligned atoms, a low RMSD may conceal significant local errors, particularly at ligand‐binding sites. We therefore complemented the RMSD analysis with residue‐level metrics such as the pseudorotation phase angle to assess local conformational accuracy. Analysis of the pseudorotation phase angle (P) shows that every residue in the predicted structure adopts an A‐form sugar pucker (C3′‐endo, P ≈ 0°), whereas the crystal structure exhibits the canonical B‐form DNA geometry (C2′‐endo, P ≈ 150°) (Figure 2D). Therefore, despite a moderate RMSD, the predicted model fails to capture a fundamental structural characteristic of the aptamer.

This A‐form/B‐form discrepancy remains after chemical conversion. In addition, the crystal structure contains a well‐defined three‐tier stacking architecture involving eight nucleobases, with A17 and T22 forming direct hydrogen bonds to theophylline (Figure 2E). By contrast, the corresponding region in the predicted docking structure is poorly organized, and the key binding residues are misoriented (Figure 2F). As a result, the predicted model fails to recapitulate the experimentally observed binding pocket and cannot serve as a credible starting structure for docking calculations.

To assess the generality of these observations, we extended the structural comparisons to ten additional DNA aptamers with experimentally determined structures in the PDB (PDB IDs: 1AW4, 2ARG, 1DB6, 6J2W, 7W9N, 8FHV, 8IF5, 8GZJ, 9KTJ, 9HIO [21, 24, 32]). In every case examined (Figure S1), the RNAComposer‐generated structures (blue) differed markedly from the corresponding experimental structures (orange; targets, water molecules, and ions removed), yielding RMSD values between 7.5 and 23.7 Å (Table 1). The dopamine aptamer exhibited the largest deviation, consistent with its complex architecture involving extensive noncanonical base pairs (Figure S2). These results highlight the difficulty of accurately predicting aptamer tertiary structures, particularly when recognition depends on noncanonical interactions.

We further examined the RNA theophylline aptamer. While it does not have the DNA‐to‐RNA conversion problem, the predicted model still fails to recover the true binding pocket. Its predicted binding‐site region shows substantial displacement relative to the crystal structure (PDB ID: 8D28 [33]), with an RMSD of 6.8 Å (Figure S3).

We next evaluated the AlphaFold3‐based workflow by generating structures for all of the aptamers in our dataset. The predicted models were aligned to the corresponding experimental structures (targets, water molecules, and ions removed), and their accuracies were assessed by RMSD (Table 1 and Figure S4). Interestingly, the performance depended strongly on whether the experimental structure had been released before AlphaFold3's training cutoff date (September 30, 2021). For the three structures available before this date (1AW4, 2ARG, and 1DB6), the mean RMSD was ~2.5 Å, whereas the eight structures released afterward yielded a mean RMSD of ~9 Å. The apparent success on the earlier structures therefore likely reflects their inclusion in the training set rather than true predictive capability of the model. For newly discovered aptamers that lie outside the training data, AlphaFold3 currently does not provide reliable structural predictions. This limitation is not unique to our benchmark. In the blind CASP16 assessment, nucleic acid structure predictions were found to capture overall global folds reasonably well but frequently failed in the noncanonical structural elements and ligand‐interaction regions that define functional binding sites. Consequently, even when the overall topology appears correct, the local structural inaccuracies most relevant to molecular recognition can severely compromise docking reliability [34].

Taken together, current modeling workflows often fail to generate structures that accurately represent aptamer binding conformations. Consequently, docking calculations performed on such models are unlikely to provide reliable mechanistic insight and may instead produce misleading interpretations of aptamer–target recognition.

Docking Score Analysis

After generating the structures, we next carried out the docking step of the workflow using Autodock Vina [8] for the theophylline DNA aptamer. When docking was performed using the experimentally determined crystal structure, all predicted poses clustered within the authentic binding pocket, even without constraining the search space to the known binding region (Figure 3A). However, the lowest‐energy pose did not correspond to the experimentally observed binding mode; instead, the best agreement was obtained for pose 3 (Figure S5A). This result highlights an important limitation of docking: even when an accurate receptor structure is available, the scoring function may fail to rank the correct binding mode as the most favorable. Thus, energy‐based scoring alone should not be considered sufficient for identifying the true aptamer–target interaction geometry.

The overview of the top nine docking poses and energies (in kcal/mol) for the following four systems: theophylline docked to (A) the crystal structure and (B) the predicted structure; caffeine docked to (C) the crystal structure and (D) the predicted structure.

We then turned to the predicted aptamer structure. Since the predicted structure provides no indication of a plausible binding pocket, we employed a large grid box encompassing the entire loop region for docking. The docking scores (−5.7 ± 0.2 kcal/mol) were nearly identical across all poses (Figure 3B). In addition, the predicted interacting residues did not correspond to those in the true binding pocket, and theophylline was modeled to interact mainly through one or two hydrogen bonds, with minimal contribution from base stacking (Figure S5B).

Together, these results demonstrate that docking scores do not reliably reflect true binding affinity or binding geometry. Even when an experimentally determined aptamer structure is available, the correct binding mode is not necessarily identified as the top‐ranked pose. More importantly, in the absence of prior knowledge of the binding pocket, docking alone cannot reliably recover the authentic aptamer–target interaction. These limitations become even more pronounced when predicted rather than experimental structures are used as the docking input.

Selectivity Analysis Using Caffeine

Taking advantage of the remarkable >250,000‐fold selectivity of the theophylline DNA aptamer [23], we further evaluated the selectivity aspect of docking. This test is directly relevant to the use of docking for SELEX, where discriminating binders from nonbinders is essential. Notably, caffeine also docked to the aptamer crystal structure with favorable scores, yielding interaction energies only 0.9 kcal/mol weaker than those for theophylline (Figure 3A,C), despite the experimental observation that caffeine shows no detectable binding even at 10 mM. Docking evaluates binding based on geometric shape complementarity (Figure S5D) and calculated interaction energies [35], rather than on the specific biochemical interactions, such as noncanonical base contacts and ligand‐induced conformational changes, that govern actual aptamer‐target recognition. Indeed, docking has been noted to be ineffective at comparing relative binding affinities between compounds [36].

For the predicted structure, the energy of binding between theophylline (Figure 3B) and caffeine (Figure 3D) was nearly identical, confirming that selectivity cannot be predicted either. Therefore, caution needs to be exerted when using docking for aptamer screening.

MD Validation of Docking Results

The majority of aptamer docking studies terminate at the docking stage without further validation. In some cases, MD simulations were employed as a postdocking tool to evaluate the stability of the predicted complexes. To examine the extent to which MD can validate docking results, we performed simulations on four systems: theophylline and caffeine docked to both the crystal structure and the predicted aptamer structure. As a benchmark, we first simulated the experimentally determined theophylline‐bound crystal complex. Throughout the 40 ns production run, the complex remained close to its native conformation: the two key recognition bases, A17 and T22, maintained nearly constant distances to theophylline (Figure S6), while the backbone RMSD stabilized at ~6 Å. These results confirm that the simulation protocol is capable of preserving a native aptamer–ligand complex. Representative structural snapshots provide additional insight into the stability of the binding interface (Figure S6).

Starting from the same crystal receptor but with the docking‐predicted ligand poses, both theophylline and caffeine remained in the cavity for the entire 40‐ns trajectory, close to their initial docked conformations (Figure 4A,B). Because the aptamer does not bind caffeine experimentally, the persistence of the caffeine pose shows that the simulation did not reproduce the known selectivity: MD neither expelled the nonbinder nor drove the docked poses toward the native binding mode, and therefore could not validate the docking results in this very classical system. This outcome likely reflects two inherent limitations of the workflow. First, once a ligand is artificially placed into a binding pocket, equilibration can kinetically trap the complex regardless of whether the pose is correct. Second, a 40 ns simulation is generally insufficient to sample ligand dissociation or large‐scale conformational rearrangements. Regardless of the underlying cause, the implication is the same: even when starting from an experimentally determined crystal structure, docking followed by MD simulation cannot reliably distinguish correct from incorrect aptamer–ligand binding modes. Consequently, the apparent persistence of a docked pose during MD should not be taken as validation of its biological relevance.

Starting from the RNAComposer‐predicted structure, the simulations did not converge to a stable, biologically meaningful binding mode, with large RMSD fluctuations (~10 Å; Figure 4C,D) and the ligand leaving the predicted pocket. These results demonstrate that when the starting structure is incorrect, short MD simulations started from the predicted structures did not converge to a stable, biologically meaningful binding mode and therefore could not validate the docking results.

Conclusions and Recommendations

Molecular docking, as currently practiced in aptamer research, cannot reliably evaluate aptamer–target interactions. Using the theophylline aptamer as a benchmark along with a few other aptamers with high‐resolution structures available, we demonstrate that predicted structures often deviate substantially from experimentally determined conformations, docking scores fail to distinguish true binders from nonbinders, and subsequent MD simulations do not overcome these limitations. The commonly used workflow is built upon assumptions that are frequently invalid for aptamers, resulting in conclusions that often bear little relationship to actual binding behavior in either DNA or RNA aptamers. Based on this analysis, we offer the following recommendations:

1. Avoid using docking to infer binding sites in the absence of experimental structures. Without an experimentally determined three‐dimensional structure, predicted binding pockets rarely reflect the true binding interface and should not be used to guide aptamer design or mechanistic interpretation. The widespread practice of generating DNA structures using RNA‐based prediction tools introduces an additional and often underappreciated source of error. While improved computational methods will undoubtedly emerge, the limited structural data available for training and the highly adaptive nature of aptamer binding make it unlikely that these fundamental limitations will be fully overcome in the foreseeable future.

2. Experimentally validate all docking‐guided aptamer discoveries. When docking is used for candidate selection or in silico SELEX, the resulting sequences should be validated using quantitative binding assays [37, 39], such as isothermal titration calorimetry, fluorescence‐based strand‐displacement assays, or other homogeneous solution‐phase methods, before biological conclusions are drawn.

3. Treat docking as a hypothesis‐generating tool, not a validation tool. Docking may generate plausible binding models, but these should be treated strictly as hypotheses rather than evidence. Experimental validation is essential. If a docking workflow cannot reliably discriminate between a known binder and an experimentally verified nonbinder, it lacks predictive power for that aptamer system and should not be used to infer binding mechanisms. Such workflows are even less appropriate for novel aptamers, where the absence of experimental benchmarks makes erroneous predictions difficult to recognize.

Ultimately, the growing reliance on docking reflects a critical gap in aptamer structural biology. Progress will require both a much larger collection of experimentally determined aptamer structures and the development of aptamer‐specific prediction tools capable of modeling noncanonical interactions, conformational adaptability, and ligand‐induced folding. Only then can computational modeling become a reliable tool for understanding aptamer recognition.
