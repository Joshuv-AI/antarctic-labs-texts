Abiotic stress effects on polyunsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis in Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp.
Authors: Nidhi Chaudhary, Monalisha Mishra, Alok Kumar Yadav, Nand K. Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroalgae play a crucial role in the biogenesis of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), essential bioactive molecules widely used in the pharmaceutical industry for their therapeutic efficacy and health-enhancing benefits. These microorganisms, due to their rapid growth and resilient nature, make an efficient source for PUFA production. The biogenesis of PUFAs in microalgae involves a series of enzymatic reactions catalyzed by elongases and desaturases that convert precursor fatty acids into PUFAs, including omega-6 and omega-3. This study explores cost-effective, and environmentally friendly strategies to enhance PUFA production in microalgae for use in the food and feed industries. The present studyevaluates the influences of salinity, pH, and low temperature on the nutraceutical properties of Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp., with a focus on PUFA production. Among the conditions tested, 10 mM NaCl yielded in the highest specific growth rates for Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp., reaching 0.142 ± 0.0010, 0.122 ± 0.0016 day−1, respectively. Under the same conditions, biomass yields peaked at 6.89 ± 0.22, 7.06 ± 0.35 g/L. The highest PUFA content of linolenic acid (29, 7.7%), eicosapentaenoic acid (47, 41%), and docosahexaenoic acid (56, 47%) was recorded at 15 °C. The activities of superoxide dismutase and malondialdehyde increased in response to elevated salinity, low temperature, and pH, indicating oxidative stress adaptation. In conclusion, low temperature had a significant effect on the enhancement of PUFA content in Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp., making these microalgae potential strains for PUFA production. These findings offer valuable insights for optimizing cultivation practices and enhancing the productivity of these economically important microalgae.Key points• Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. were studied for PUFA production.• Salinity, pH, and low temperature have a marked effects on the PUFA profile of microalgae.• The highest specific growth rate occurred at 10 mM NaCl.• The highest PUFA content was observed at 15 °C.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-026-13754-9.

Introduction

Microalgae hold great potential as contenders for the commercial production of PUFA, pigments, single-cell proteins, and a variety of cosmetic products. They have attracted significant attention as an alternative PUFA source, due to their accelerated growth rate, rich nutrient profile (lipids and carbohydrates), adaptability to seawater and wastewater environments, and superior photosynthetic efficiency compared to land-based crops (Suparmaniam et al. 2023; Suhaimi et al. 2024; Sivakumar & Ghosh 2025). Microalgae arise as an eco-friendly, sustainable, and vegan sources of PUFAs (Morales-Sánchez et al. 2020). PUFAs are essential in the human diet and are considered key elements of functional foods. Most people around the world consume diets that are deficient in PUFAs. Although fish oil is the main source of omega−3 fatty acids, such as alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), for human consumption, its production faces several drawbacks, including unpleasant odors, resource overexploitation, heavy metal contamination, and negative environmental effects like marine habitat degradation. Interestingly, fish accumulate PUFAs by consuming microalgae within the food chain (Mitra et al. 2015; Morales-Sánchez et al. 2020).

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are also gaining significant attention in the pharmaceutical and nutraceutical sectors due to their health benefits for animals and humans (Morales-Sánchez et al. 2020; Suhaimi et al. 2022). During pregnancy, they are necessary for the neurodevelopment and cognitive growth of the fetus. In addition, PUFA are crucial for neural system development in adults, supporting proper brain and retinal function (Keddar et al. 2025; Perdana et al. 2021). The World Health Organisation (WHO) and Food and Agriculture Organisation (FAO) recommend a minimum daily consumption of 0.25 g of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), while the American Heart Association (AHA) suggests a higher intake of up to 0.5 g/day for healthy adults. Humans and animals lack the enzymatic capability to synthesize long-chain unsaturated fatty acids, including DHA and EPA, and thus must obtain these nutrients through their food. Microalgae possess significant advantages over plant-based food sources, offering photosynthetic efficiency and surface-area yield up to tenfold higher than land-based crops. They can be cultivated year-round on non-arable land in open pond systems, with limited nutrient requirements (Kavitha et al. 2016; Perdana et al. 2021). The terrestrial plants predominantly synthesize ALA; microalgae can additionally produce EPA and DHA, thereby strengthening their importance as a sustainable and nutritionally rich source of omega-3 fatty acids (Adarme-Vega et al. 2012; Thurn et al. 2022). Researchers have extensively studied the production of PUFA in microalgae. Despite this, large-scale production faces significant obstacles, primarily due to the difficulty in optimizing culture conditions to enhance PUFA yields. Several factors must be considered when using microalgae as a source of PUFA for human consumption, including strain safety, high biomass output, lipid yield, and PUFA content (Perdana et al. 2021).

Microalgal metabolism is highly influenced by external conditions; even small changes in environmental or cultivation conditions can change the synthesis of important bioactive compounds with commercial potential (Kumar et al. 2019). Cultivating microalgae under abiotic stress conditions not only influences total lipid accumulation but also modifies the fatty acid composition, notably affecting PUFA levels (Nezafatian et al. 2024). Several strategies have been utilized for microalgal cell growth and PUFA yield in microalgae; low temperatures stimulate the formation of unsaturated FAs such as Chlorella, Spirulina platensis, Chlorella vulgaris, and Botryococcus braunii (Hu et al. 2008; Sushchik 2003), and low pH favors the production of unsaturated FAs, e.g., Chlorella sorokiniana (Qiu et al. 2017). High salt concentrations also enhance the unsaturated fatty acids content in Dunaliella salina, adapting to increased intracellular glycerol levels under high salinity conditions (Azachi et al. 2002). A study identified 20 °C as the optimal temperature for Nannochloropsis sp., producing 29% EPA of the total lipid content (Willette et al. 2018). Temperature is a critical factor influencing total fatty acid and PUFA content, with significantly higher PUFA levels observed at temperatures between 10 and 20 °C. These conditions reduce stress on microalgae during cultivation and enhance the fatty acids desaturation. Low temperatures enhance the accumulation of PUFAs, which support proper cell membrane fluidity. Such traits make microalgae suitable for industrial cultivation, where variable temperatures and salinity are common (Suzuki et al. 2019; Stuart et al. 2025). Limited reports are available on the effects of salinity, pH, and low temperatures stress on the accumulation of linolenic acid, EPA, and DHA fatty acid in Chlorococcum oleofaciens. However, no such studies have been documented for Leptolyngbya sp.

The objective of this study is to enhance the PUFA (linolenic acid, EPA, and DHA) content by an abiotic factors approach. This study analyzed the effect of abiotic factors, including salinity, pH, and low temperature, on the specific growth rate, biomass, oxidant and antioxidant activities, and PUFA content of Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. It could be shown that abiotic factors have a substantial impact on cell-specific growth rate and FA composition in microalgal sp. It will serve an important role in minimizing costs and scaling up the utilization of microalgae for PUFA synthesis.

Materials and methods

Microalgal strain and cultivation condition

A native freshwater microalgal strain, identified as Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. (NCBI accession no. SRR27110443 and SRR27110516), and previously reported by Chaudhary and Singh (2024), was isolated from the Ganges River and used in the present study. Microalgal cultures were grown in 500-mL conical flasks in BG-11 medium at a controlled temperature of 28 ± 2 °C with white fluorescent light at an intensity of 100 µmol photons m⁻2 s⁻1, following a 12L:12D h cycle. All analyses were conducted on microalgal cells cultured in BG11 medium, and the culture purity was consistently monitored through light microscopy. To maintain proper aeration, the culture was shaken manually three times per day (Chaudhary and Singh 2024; Yun et al. 2021).

Experimental setup and flask cultivation

Microalgae strains were cultured to evaluate the influence of pH, low temperature, and salinity, on their growth and fatty acid (PUFA) content. Experiments were carried out in triplicate using 250 mL of sterile BG11 medium in 500 mL conical flasks at NaCl concentrations of 0.1, 10, 30, and 50 mM, with a NaCl-free medium serving as the control. The pH effects were studied at pH values of 6, 7, 8, and 9, with pH 7.5 as the control. The temperature influence was evaluated by maintaining cultures at 15, 20, and 25 °C, with 28 °C as the control temperature (Sang et al. 2012). Control conditions were selected based on the usually reported optimal growth conditions for microalgae, as described in previous studies (Suarez-Montes et al. 2022; Yun et al. 2021).

Inoculum was derived from freshly prepared cultures in the exponential phase. The microalgal cultures were centrifuged at 7000 rpm at 25 °C for 15 min, and the resulting pellet was rinsed 3–4 times to eliminate salts and debris. The initial cell density was adjusted to approximately an OD 680 nm ∼0.04. Experiments were performed for 20 days, and at the end of cultivation, each culture’s samples were analyzed for biomass, antioxidant test, and fatty acid profile.

Microalgal culture and biomass productivity

A double-beam UV–Vis spectrophotometer was used to measure optical density at 680 nm every alternate day to evaluate microalgal strain growth. The calculations for specific growth rate (μ, day⁻1) and doubling time (T2) followed the methods outlined by Monod (1949) and Levasseur et al. To evaluate biomass production, a 50 mL sample was collected from the flask, rinsed with 1% NaCl, and then dried in an oven to measure the total dry biomass (Kozlova et al. 2017).

Lipid content and fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) analysis

Microalgal lipid extraction followed Bligh and Dyer (1959) method. Microalgal biomass was homogenized in a (2: 1 v/v) Methanol: Chloroform solution for lipid extraction. The resulting mixture was agitated for 10 min at RT, briefly vortexed, and centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 10 min. This process separated the solution into three layers; the upper and middle layers were removed, and the lipid-containing chloroform phase at the bottom was concentrated using a rotary evaporator.

The total lipids were converted into fatty acid methyl esters via transesterification. Microalgal pellets and a heptadecanoic acid internal standard were transesterified using 1% H2SO4 in methanol. FAMEs were analyzed using GC–MS (Agilent), and Helium was used as the carrier gas. Fatty acids were identified by matching the retention times with those of known standards (Liu et al. 2015; Mitra et al. 2015).

Stress biomarkers

Antioxidant stress biomarkers

The activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD) was assessed by Giannopolitis and Ries (1977) method by evaluating the reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT). For this, a 6 mL reaction mixture was prepared, containing 4 mM riboflavin, 100 mM PPB (pH 7.6), 26 mM methionine, 150 mM NBT, 200 µL enzyme extract, and 0.2 mM EDTA. The reaction mixture was placed in 5000 mmol photons/m2/s light intensity for 20 min to initiate the reaction, then moved to the dark to finish the reaction. Additionally, the absorbance was noted at 560 nm.

A single unit of superoxide dismutase activity corresponds to the enzyme quantity necessary to cause fifty percent inhibition of NBT reduction under the defined light parameters. Enzyme activity was reported in U/mg Pro, where U denotes enzyme activity units, and Pro represents the protein content.

Oxidative stress biomarkers

Assessment of lipid peroxidation was carried out by applying Heath’ and Packer (1968) method, and malondialdehyde (MDA) quantification was used to determine oxidative damage. A ten-milliliter aliquot of microalgal cells was collected after centrifugation at 10,000 x g for 15 min at 4 °C. The pellet was placed in another tube and mixed with 0.25% TBA in 10% TCA. The mixture was heated in a water bath at 95 oC for 30 min. The resulting supernatant was collected, and its optical density (OD) was determined at 532 nm, 450 nm, and 600 nm. Statistical analyses

The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analysis was analyzed with one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, which was conducted to evaluate significant differences (p < 0.05).

Figure 1A illustrates the effect of varying NaCl concentrations on the growth of microalgal cells. During the first 3 days, all salt-stressed cultures exhibited growth rates similar to the controls (without NaCl). On the fourth day of incubation, the growth of Chlorococcum oleofaciens cultured at 30 and 50 mM NaCl was reduced compared to the control culture. However, in Leptolyngbya sp. cultured at 30 mM of NaCl, growth declined after 10 days; and at 50 mM of NaCl, growth declined after the 3rd day compared to the control. It then gradually decreases up to the last day of observation. On the other hand, cultures of Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. stressed with 0.1 and 10 mM of NaCl showed a significant (p < 0.05) improvement in growth relative to the control culture starting on the fourth day. This outcome shows that Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. can tolerate salt at a particular concentration range. The microalgae specific growth rate shows high variability under various low temperature conditions, and the results of this study are shown in Table 1 and Fig. 1C. In Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp., the maximum specific growth rate was found in growth culture at control 28 °C (0.135 ± 0.001, 0.117 ± 0.003 day−1). It is evident that microalgal growth showed no significant variation (p > 0.05) across temperatures of 15, 20, and 25 °C. Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. minimum growth rate of 0.112 ± 0.0010, 0.100 ± 0.002 day−1 were found at 15 °C. The results exhibited that microalgae growth was boosted at higher temperatures. In Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp., the lowest doubling times were observed at the control temperature of 28 °C (5.128 ± 0.038, 5.892 ± 0.167 day−1) and the highest at 15 °C (6.173 ± 0.055, 6.887 ± 0.175 day−1). Among the three abiotic stresses evaluated, salinity increased the specific growth rate, whereas both pH and low temperature stress led to a reduction in specific growth rate.

Effect of salinity, pH, and low temperature on microalgal cell biomass and lipid content

(4.60 ± 0.30 and 4.64 ± 0.25 g/L, respectively) were recorded at 50 mM NaCl, as shown in Fig. 2 and Table S1. The biomass of microalgae Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. was significantly (p < 0.05) reduced with the increasing salt concentration, exhibiting a negative growth rate at 30 and 50 mM NaCl. In Leptolyngbya sp., MUFA, SFA, and PUFA fatty acid (myristic acid (C14:0), palmitic acid (C16:0), arachidic acid (C20:0), lauric acid (C12:0), hexadecatrienoic acid (C16:3), eicosapentaenoic acid EPA (C20:5), docosahexaenoic acid DHA (C22:6), and lignoceric acid (C24:0)) content was decreased with increased concentration of NaCl in comparison to control. Whereas limited SFA and UFA fatty acids (linoleic acid (C18:2), oleic acid (C18:1), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), and stearic acid (C18:0)) were improved with enhanced NaCl concentration in comparison to the control.

In Chlorococcum oleofaciens, linolenic acid (C18:3) content increased significantly (p < 0.05) to 9.25 ± 0.008, 9.54 ± 0.016, and 9.3 ± 0.163% with the addition of 0.1, 10, and 30 mM NaCl, respectively, corresponding to increases of 3.4, 6.7, and 4.0% compared with the control. Similarly, EPA (C20:5) concentrations rose to 2.20 ± 0.059, 2.83 ± 0.016, 2.66 ± 0.036%, showing 4.26, 34, and 26% increases under the same NaCl treatments. For Leptolyngbya sp. PUFA fatty acid, linolenic acid (C18:3) was increased up to 10.69 ± 0.028, 11.39 ± 0.14, and 10.83 ± 0.038% under 0.1, 10, and 30 mM NaCl, showing respective increases of 2.4, 9, and 3.7%, respectively. The linolenic acid concentration was significantly (p < 0.05) greater in the 10 mM NaCl enriched culture relative to the control culture.

In this study, the FA composition was influenced by the pH levels (6–9) alongside the control pH of 7.5 in the BG11 medium, as shown in Tables 2 and 3. Lipid accumulation was affected by pH 6 and 9 for SFA, MUFA, and PUFA; however, the most significant FA accumulation occurred at pH 7 and 8 treatment. The polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) levels increased to 9.05 ± 0.02% (1.23%) linolenic acid (C18:3) at pH 8 and 2.5 ± 0.10% (18.4%) EPA at pH 7 in Chlorococcum oleofaciens compared to the control (7.5 pH). In Leptolyngbya sp., linolenic acid, EPA, and DHA were increased by 11.56 ± 0.04 (10.7%), 1.6 ± 0.05 (12.6%), and 1.13 ± 0.02 (10.78%) % at pH 7 compared, respectively, compared to control (7.5 pH). Different pH ranges have shown a significant effect on FA profiles (p < 0.05).

Under different temperature stresses for Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp., the quantity of PUFA enhanced significantly (Tables 2 and 3). Various SFA and MUFA, including myristic acid (C14:0), arachidic acid (C20:0), stearic acid (C18:0), palmitoleic acid (C16:1), and palmitic acid (C16:0), content, were enhanced significantly when the temperature was enhanced to 15–25 °C (p < 0.05). The oleic acid (C18:1) content increased significantly as the temperature decreased from 25 to 15 °C. Concentration of PUFA such as linoleic acid (C18:2) were also significantly higher (p < 0.05) within the same temperature range.

In both microalgal strains, decreasing temperature significantly enhanced (p < 0.05) PUFA accumulation. At 15 °C, Chlorococcum oleofaciens exhibited the highest linolenic acid (C18:3) content (11.56 ± 0.016%), showing a 29% increase over the control, whereas Leptolyngbya sp. showed 11.25 ± 0.021% (7.7%). EPA (C20:5) levels in Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. reached 3.12 ± 0.012 and 2.00 ± 0.023%, corresponding to 47 and 41% increases, respectively. Similarly, DHA (C22:6) content rose to 2.35 ± 0.020 (56%) in Chlorococcum oleofaciens and 1.50 ± 0.021% (47%) in Leptolyngbya sp. compared to the control. The most significant stimulatory effect on linolenic acid, EPA, and DHA was observed at 15 °C in both strains. Low temperature significantly promoted polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) synthesis in Chlorococcum oleofaciens (20%) and Leptolyngbya sp. (11.2%). These results confirm that lower temperatures favor the accumulation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

Microalgal growth is significantly influenced by salinity, pH, and low temperatures, which serve as major limiting factors. This study found that Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. exhibited significant increase in growth and PUFA content at moderate pH, salinity, and low temperatures. To study the effects of different culture conditions on microalgal cell growth, antioxidant tests, biomass, and PUFA (linolenic acid, EPA, and DHA) production, three distinct culture parameters (salinity, pH, and low temperature) were studied independently.

Growth enhancement under low NaCl concentrations has been reported in microalgae. For instance, growth of Chlorella sorokiniana has been reported to be enhanced by low NaCl concentrations in earlier studies (Zhang et al. 2018). In another study, microalgal cell growth was observed exclusively at NaCl concentrations of 20 and 10 g/L. At salinity concentration of 30 g/L or higher, the cells underwent lysis slowely (Kim et al. 2021b). Under NaCl toxicity, the decline in microalgal growth rate could result from membrane shrinkage, exosmosis of turgid cells, and ionic imbalance (Verslues 2017). Salt-induced growth retardation results from disrupted membrane transport and nutrient depletion in the cells (Singh et al. 2018). In addition to pH, another key environmental factor governs microalgal growth. Ismaiel et al. (2016) found that S. platensis growth is pH-dependent, with the highest growth observed at pH 9. A pH below 8.5 or above 9.5 caused a significant reduction in growth. A similar outcome was observed in Nannochloropsis sp., which showed optimal growth at pH 2 (Peng et al. 2020). Microalgal cell growth was significantly suppressed at pH 5. Temperature has also been shown to play a critical role in regulating microalgal growth. In a culture of P. pyrenoidosus, the maximum cell density of (6.4 × 106 cell m/L) at the temperature of 28 ℃ (Sang et al. 2012). P. tricornutum grew optimally at 20 °C, but its growth diminished at temperatures both above and below this level, showing almost no growth at 30 °C (Jiang and Gao 2004). Temperature influences microalgae growth by altering cellular metabolic activities and the function of key enzymes, including ribulose 1,5-bisphosphate (Rubisco), which plays an essential role in photosynthesis (Chaisutyakorn et al. 2018; Wei et al. 2015). In contrast, Chaisutyakorn et al. (2018) found that the microalgal growth rate decreased at higher temperatures (above 30 °C), and increased at 25 °C, which was attributed to the rise in respiration.

Previous studies have reported that adding salt to the growth medium enhances microalgal (Chlorella vulgaris and Acutodesmus obliquus) biomass production, with the highest yield recorded after fifteen days of incubation in the medium containing 0.1 M NaCl (Pandit et al. 2017). Salinity contributes to sodium ions, which are crucial for microalgal nutrient absorption, alkalinity tolerance, regulation of intracellular pH, and photosynthesis (Salama et al. 2013). In K. antarctica, the maximum dry weight ranged from 2.6 to 2.9 g/L at salinity levels between 0 and 16%. However, the maximum biomass concentration reduced to 2.14 g/L at 32% salt concentration (Suzuki et al. 2019). A previous study reported that microalgae grow best at an optimum pH of about 8.2–8.7 (Havlik et al. 2013). In an experiment with Scenedesmus abundans, the maximum biomass productivity was observed at pH 8.0 (Mandotra et al. 2016), whereas the pH of the growth medium influenced the growth of S. platensis, with optimal growth at pH 9.0 (Ismaiel et al. 2016). In contrast, the highest biomass productivity in Chlorella sorokiniana was observed at pH 6 (0.14 g/L-day), which did not differ significantly (p > 0.05) compared to the biomass productivity at pH 5.8 (Qiu et al. 2017). In order to maintain pH homeostasis within the cell, microalgae actively absorb H⁺ ions from the surrounding environment for neutralization reactions (Farkas et al. 2023). At low pH, the increased chemical gradient leads to a higher influx of H+ ions, necessitating active transport mechanisms to regulate internal pH stability for proper metabolism (Visviki and Santikul 2000).

The optimal biomass of microalgae was observed at moderate temperatures, for instance, in a study reported that at temperature range of 25–30 °C, the biomass was significantly higher (12.1–12.5 g/L) than at 15 and 40 °C (Safdar et al. 2017). In another study, the biomass of P. tricornutum at 20 °C was significantly greater compared to that at 25 °C (p < 0.05), but there was no statistically significant variation compared to 15 °C (p > 0.05) (Qiao et al. 2016). The biomass of Nannochloropsis sp. and Chaetoceros sp. both declined at high temperatures (Chaisutyakorn et al. 2018). Microalgal biomass decreased as the temperature dropped below 35–20 ℃ (Peng et al. 2020). In the present study, variations in culture conditions markedly influenced the lipid content of the studied microalgae. In a study, at 10 °C temperature, microalgal lipid content was marginally greater than the lipid content recorded in microalgae exposed to varying temperatures (20 °C, 25 °C, and 30 °C) (Lu et al. 2017). Maximum lipid accumulation (37 ± 3%) was achieved by C. mexicana 25 mM NaCl. Likewise, S. obliquus showed comparable lipid content (34 ± 2%) at the same NaCl level (Salama et al. 2013). In a different study, the lipid content in the microalga increased with pH in the range of 6–9 and was significantly influenced by pH. The maximum lipid content (32.42 ± 0.51 mg 100 mg 1 DW) was observed at pH 9 (Santhakumaran et al. 2020).

Variations in culture conditions also markedly affected the fatty acid profile of the examined microalgae. In a different study, S. obliquus showed an increase of up to 30% in α-linolenic acid (C18:3n3) at 25mM NaCl, respectively (Salama et al. 2013). Cells enhance unsaturated fatty acid production under salinity stress to maintain membrane fluidity and defend cells from damage. In a different study, elevated salinity (10 mM) increased linolenic acid, a key PUFA fatty acid, in the total lipids of the microalgae Chloroidium saccharophillum (Santhakumaran et al. 2020). Earlier research has shown that salt stress can enhance lipid accumulation in Chlorella, possibly due to changes in membrane structure and regulation of lipid metabolism by osmotic pressure. But excessive salt concentrations can disrupt homeostasis once a critical threshold is reached (Farkas et al. 2023). In another study, the highest EPA yield of 9.13 mg L−1 day−1 was observed at 20 g L−1, enhancing to 30 g L−1 and then remaining constant at 40 g/L in N. gaditana (Mitra et al. 2015). The PUFA and EPA contents in P. pyrenoidosus reached their highest 31.37% and 17.82%, respectively, at salinity 30, but declined to their lowest values, 2.62% and 8.42%, at salinity 35 (Sang et al. 2012). PUFAs are widely utilized in the aquaculture industry, as they are essential for properly developing fish larvae. Furthermore, these fatty acids are vital sources of omega−6 and omega−3 polyunsaturated fatty acids for human diets, commonly obtained from fish and vegetable oils (Elloumi et al. 2020). Results obtained in this study are also consistent with the work performed on microalga S. abundans, where linolenic acid (C18:3) was observed at pH 7.5 (Mandotra et al. 2016). The microalgae Porphyridium cruentum exhibits higher production of PUFAs, including EPA and DHA, at pH 7.0 (Kim et al. 2021a). When pH decreases, carboxylate ions are protonated, forming neutral carboxyl groups. This neutralizes the negative charge on cells, disrupting dispersion stability and causing aggregation and sedimentation (Liu et al. 2015). pH is an essential abiotic factor that influences cell surface characteristics and alters the membrane ion permeability (Safdar et al. 2017). Modifying the pH of the medium in microalgal cultures affects metabolite production and cellular physiology (Almutairi et al. 2020). In a different study, linolenic acid and EPA were found at higher concentrations in Nannochloropsis salina at pH 7 and 8 (Bartley et al. 2014). Microalgae FA production is highly temperature-dependent (Sang et al. 2012; Thompson et al. 1992). In the current study, as temperature increased, the percentage of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) also increased (Tables 2 and 3). This is possibly due to alterations in the fluidity of phospholipid bilayers in cell membranes, influenced by FA unsaturation levels (Harwood 1998; Sang et al. 2012). The findings also indicated that a temperature of 15 °C resulted in the greatest accumulation of PUFAs, including linolenic acid, DHA, and EPA. Lower temperatures cause membrane lipid bilayers to become more rigid, reducing ion permeability (Cossins et al. 2002; Chua et al. 2020). To counteract this effect, cells increase PUFA synthesis, as these fatty acids enhance membrane fluidity (Jiang and Gao 2004; Sang et al. 2012). In P. pyrenoidosus, the highest EPA (20.83%) and PUFA (39.16%) concentrations were obtained when the microalgae were cultivated at 20 °C (Sang et al. 2012). In another study, it was demonstrated that Nannochloropsis sp. cultures grown at lower temperatures contained higher levels of EPA and PUFAs (Hu and Gao 2003). Similarly, reducing the temperature from 34 to 22 °C in Synechococcus sp. led to a tenfold increase in desB desaturase gene transcription, suggesting its role in maintaining membrane fluidity under cold stress (Sakamoto et al. 2006). The enhanced PUFA production at low temperatures may be linked to desaturase gene activation triggered by a sensor in the cytoplasmic membrane (Jiang & Gao 2004). Zhu et al. (2022) found that transcriptome analysis discovered that low temperatures predominantly influenced the photosynthetic apparatus. An analysis found that the cells enhance PUFA production to maintain the integrity of the photosynthetic apparatus under cold stress (Chua et al. 2020).

Different environmental stresses can trigger the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in microalgae, leading to oxidative stress (Yuan et al. 2023). The SOD activity in C. humicola and C. vulgaris increased by 46.8% and 56.79%, respectively, under 500 mM NaCl compared with control cultures (Singh et al. 2018). Similarly, Verma et al. (2019), exhibited the highest SOD induction at 500 mM NaCl, representing a 152% increase over the control. Research shows that oxidative stress leads to a gradual accumulation of ROS in macroalgae (Zhang et al. 2013). This can damage the photosystems, resulting from protein denaturation, DNA mutations, lipid peroxidation, and deterioration of the membrane structure (Leshem et al. 2007). Excessive ROS accumulation in cells is counteracted by various antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, APX, and catalase etc. (Chokshi et al. 2015). The microalgae, A. obliquus and C. vulgaris, exhibited significantly (p < 0.05) elevated MDA levels significantly (3.16 and 2.5 μM g−1) at 0.4 M NaCl, compared to their control values (Pandit et al. 2017). SOD activity was significantly higher (p < 0.05) in the 50 mM NaCl-enriched culture (0.052 μM g−1) relative to the control. An additional increase in its level was observed, reaching 0.081 μM g−1 in cultures grown with 200 mM NaCl and 0.1 μM g−1 in those enriched with 400 mM NaCl (Pancha et al. 2015). In a different study, 70.8% increase in SOD activity was observed on day 7 at pH 6.5 compared to the control. Conversely, S. platensis exhibited the maximum SOD activity at pH 10.5 and the lowest at pH 8.5 (Ismaiel et al. 2016). Significant enhancement in antioxidant enzyme activity under acidic and oxidative conditions demonstrates their effectiveness in combating acid-induced stress (Song et al. 2018). As the first line of antioxidant defence in plant cells, SOD transforms ROS into H2O2 and O2. This signalling pathway may influence intracellular ROS accumulation (Song et al. 2018). In a similar study, in Spirulina sp. SOD concentration peaked at 797.19 Ug−1 under 30 °C, while a lower level of 410.04 Ug−1 was observed at 20 °C (Rehman et al. 2025). In a different study, when algal thalli were treated at 23 °C, superoxide dismutase activity increased significantly in K. striatum and K. alvarezii thalli, reaching 2.8 and 2.1-fold increases relative to the groups (Li et al. 2016).

Malondialdehyde (MDA) is a widely used biomarker of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage in microalgal cells (Wu et al. 2025). In a study, MDA levels indicated the extent of lipid peroxidation, which was highest under 500 mM NaCl treatment, suggesting that salt enhances lipid peroxidation (Verma et al. 2019). Increased MDA concentration under stress indicates oxidative stress in microalgal strains. As a product of lipid peroxidation, MDA is capable of oxidizing sulfhydryl (-SH) groups, resulting in damage to membrane integrity and protein structure. Hence, the amount of MDA present indicates the level of lipid peroxidation occurring in the membrane. The elevated MDA levels under NaCl stress suggest that ROS production exceeded the antioxidant system’s ability to maintain cellular balance. The accumulation of ROS subsequently disrupted the membrane integrity, leading to an increased MDA content (Zhang et al. 2013). A study, it was shown that MDA concentration peaked on day 21 at pH 5.5, with a 120.0% increase compared to the control (Song et al. 2018). In another study, it was found that MDA content was highest (21.445 μM) at 30 °C, and decreased to 0.903 μM at 20 °C (Rehman et al. 2025). In K. striatum thalli, 17 °C temperature treatment resulted in an MDA concentration 2.2 times higher than the control, indicating oxidative damage; however, this response was not observed in K. alvarezii thalli (Li et al. 2016). These results show that the salinity stress is effective in enhancing cell specific growth rate and cell biomass accumulation, but is not very favorable for polyunsaturated fatty acid production, whereas low temperature caused a significant enhancement in polyunsaturated fatty acid synthesis. These results indicate a trade-off between biomass production and polyunsaturated fatty acid quality, suggesting that stress-specific or sequential cultivation strategies may improve overall productivity.

Future studies should focus on optimizing cultivation conditions to achieve a balance between biomass yield and polyunsaturated fatty acid production, including the use of two-stage or sequential stress strategies. In addition, research focusing on metabolic and molecular regulation would help clarify the regulatory mechanisms governing stress-induced polyunsaturated fatty acids. In addition, evaluating scale-up feasibility, process stability, and cost-effectiveness will be essential to assessing the biotechnological potential of the microalga under study for sustainable fatty acids and PUFAs production.

Conclusion

This study aimed to assess the impact of abiotic stress (salinity, pH, and low temperature) on the productivity of PUFA in Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. Thus, our results showed that various environmental conditions significantly influence the specific growth rate and biomass, and fatty acid composition. A salinity of 10 mM concentrations yielded the highest biomass and specific growth rate in Chlorococcum oleofaciens and Leptolyngbya sp. Among all three abiotic stress conditions, the maximum PUFA content was observed under low-temperature stress at 15 °C. The polyunsaturated fatty acids enhanced include linolenic acid (C18:3), EPA (C20:5), and DHA (C22:6), which are enhanced compared with the control (28 °C). The SOD and MDA activities were enhanced at 30 mM NaCl for Chlorococcum oleofaciens and at 50 mM NaCl for Leptolyngbya sp. as compared to control cultures. In both microalgal species, SOD and MDA contents were elevated at pH 6 and 25 °C, indicating an enhanced oxidative response under these conditions. These findings suggest that under optimized low-temperature stress, these microalgae could serve as promising sources of PUFAs and feed ingredients. Moreover, the results of this study indicated that abiotic stresses can alter the FA composition. Therefore, the using abiotic stressors can be a promising strategy to enhance PUFA levels in selected microalgae. These fatty acids have various applications in nutritive products for human consumption, coloring agents, aquaculture, and animal feed.
