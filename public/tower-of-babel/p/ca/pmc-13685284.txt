Stakeholder perceptions of antimicrobial resistance governance in livestock production: a cross-sectional study from North-Central Nigeria
Authors: Jones Soladoye Akinbobola, Kelvin Olutimilehin Jolayemi, Abdullahi Olawale Bello, Mujeeb Adetayo Adegbenro, Ruth Ifeoluwapo Akinbobola, Amina Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in food animal production poses a significant public health and agricultural challenge in Nigeria, driven by antimicrobial misuse, weak regulatory enforcement, and limited veterinary infrastructure. This cross-sectional study was conducted January-July 2025 across seven North-Central Nigerian states, assessed self-reported awareness, self-rated knowledge, perceptions, and implementation of AMR interventions among 800 stakeholders across five professional groups. Data were collected via online and in-person questionnaires and analysed descriptively with chi-square tests. Awareness of AMR was high (90.5%; n = 724), with 95.4% (n = 763) recognising it as a public health concern; however, only 17.9% (n = 143) self-rated their knowledge as excellent, significantly associated with profession (χ²=12.52, df = 12, p = 0.01) and education level (χ²=10.27, df = 9, p = 0.02). The most frequently perceived drivers of AMR were poor biosecurity (75.5%), weak veterinary oversight (75.0%), counterfeit drugs (73.9%), and antimicrobial overuse (72.9%), all varying significantly by profession (χ²=6.81–10.69, df = 4, p < 0.05). Education/awareness campaigns were most frequently selected as the most effective intervention (24.4%), with intervention rankings differing significantly by profession (χ²=13.62, df = 16, p < 0.01) and state (χ²=21.02, df = 24, p < 0.01). Awareness of the Federal AMR Committee varied markedly by profession (57.9% overall; χ²=167.36, df = 4, p < 0.001), highest among veterinarians (89.0%). Overall, 60.5% (n = 484) considered current regulations insufficient, differing significantly by profession (χ²=5.03, df = 8, p = 0.01). These findings suggest that while national-level AMR governance structures are recognised and valued, their perceived impact is limited by gaps in sub-national capacity and implementation. Strengthening local-level resources, regulatory enforcement, and targeted training may help translate policy into practice, addressing gaps in awareness, knowledge, and implementation capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05755-7.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a growing global crisis, threatening both human and animal health and projected to cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050 if unchecked [1, 2]. The proliferation of AMR in food animal production represents a growing public health and agricultural crisis in Nigeria [1, 2]. Food animals such as poultry, cattle, swine, and aquaculture species are increasingly implicated in the emergence and dissemination of resistant pathogens, facilitated by widespread misuse and overuse of antimicrobials in veterinary settings [3, 4]. Despite global efforts to mitigate AMR through coordinated, cross-sectoral frameworks, Nigeria’s progress remains constrained by systemic challenges including weak regulatory enforcement, insufficient veterinary infrastructure, limited surveillance capacity, and fragmented intersectoral coordination [2, 5].

A central research problem lies in the disconnect between national-level policy commitments and implementation realities at sub-national levels. Although Nigeria adopted its National Action Plan on AMR (NAP-AMR) in 2017 in alignment with the Global Action Plan (GAP) of the World Health Organization, progress has been uneven [6]. The federal government of Nigeria has initiated AMR governance structures and multisectoral committees, yet the extent to which these interventions are perceived as effective by front-line stakeholders like veterinarians, livestock farmers, animal health workers, and researchers remains poorly understood [7, 8].

Existing Nigerian studies on AMR awareness have largely focused on single professional groups in isolated location such as veterinarians [9], cattle handlers in one state [10], or poultry farmers in the northwest [11] leaving a gap in multi-stakeholder, cross-sectoral data, and in particular in how front-line actors perceive the effectiveness of AMR governance structures across tiers of government. This gap is especially pronounced in the North Central zone, where rising livestock production is not matched by improvements in veterinary services or stewardship. Multiple studies from this region reveal systemic gaps; over 90% of smallholder poultry farmers lack knowledge of withdrawal periods, drug-residue risks, or regulatory oversight [12]; 58% of pastoralists self-prescribe antimicrobials, with poor knowledge and weak enforcement contributing to misuse, including improper dosing and the widespread use of antimicrobials for prophylaxis or growth promotion in poultry, driven by cost constraints and lack of oversight [13]. Although veterinarians recognize the value of AST, only 4.4% routinely request it, limited by laboratory access and client affordability [14]. Across sectors, diagnostic infrastructure is sparse where just 34.7% of laboratories perform AST, forcing empirical prescribing patterns [15]. These practices contribute to drug-resistant infections in animals and heighten zoonotic risks to human populations through direct contact and contaminated food chains [16–18]. Given these concerns, the justification for this study is rooted in the urgent need to evaluate how AMR control measures are perceived, adopted, and challenged in real-world practice. Without this understanding, national AMR policies may fail to translate into meaningful change at the grassroots level. Additionally, understanding the role of each tier of government (federal, state, and local) is important for effective coordination and resource allocation [19, 20].

This study aims to assess the perceived effectiveness, implementation, and limitations of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) interventions in food animal medicine across North Central Nigeria, based on the perspectives of key stakeholders in the veterinary and livestock sectors. It investigates the level of awareness and knowledge of AMR among professionals, identifies which control measures have been implemented and how they are perceived in terms of effectiveness, examines the roles and coordination dynamics of different tiers of government in AMR control, and explores the barriers hindering successful implementation and policy uptake. By generating evidence from a diverse range of stakeholders, the study seeks to inform more grounded and context-specific approaches to AMR governance in Nigeria, ultimately bridging the gap between top-down policy formulation and bottom-up implementation practices.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted across seven states in the North-Central region of Nigeria: Benue, Federal Capital Territory (FCT), Kogi, Kwara, Nasarawa, Niger, and Plateau (Fig. 1). These states were selected due to their high concentration of livestock farming activities, diversity of food animals reared, and the presence of multiple categories of animal health stakeholders. The North-Central region is characterised by Guinea Savannah vegetation and a tropical climate with distinct dry (November-April) and wet (May-October) seasons. Average annual rainfall ranges from 1,100 to 1,600 mm, with maximum temperatures reaching 37 °C during the peak dry season. The region supports poultry, pig, fish, and ruminant production, making it an ideal setting for assessing antimicrobial resistance (AMR) awareness, perceptions, and practices in food animal medicine.

Study design

A descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and July 2025 to obtain a snapshot assessment of awareness, knowledge, perceptions, and practices regarding AMR among stakeholders in livestock health and production. This design allowed for the identification of associations between demographic and professional characteristics and AMR-related outcomes, although causal relationships could not be inferred.

Study participants

The study targeted veterinarians, animal health workers, livestock farmers, pharmacy/drug store operators, and researchers actively engaged in livestock health, production, antimicrobial distribution, or AMR-related research. These professional groups were chosen to capture perspectives from both prescribers/dispensers and end-users of antimicrobials in food animal production.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Participants included in the study were residents or active practitioners in any of the seven study states during the survey period who were directly involved in livestock health, food animal production, or the prescription, sale, or administration of antimicrobials; were adults aged 18 years and above; and provided informed consent to participate.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they declined to provide informed consent, or if they provided incomplete (> 10% missing) or internally inconsistent responses that could not be resolved during data cleaning.

Sample size determination

The minimum required sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula [21] for an infinite population:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm n=\mathrm Z^2\times\mathrm p\times\mathrm q/\mathrm d^2$$\end{document}

where Z = 1.96 (corresponding to a 95% confidence level), p = 0.5 (expected proportion, set at maximum variability in the absence of comparable prior estimates), q = (1 − p) = 0.5, and d = 0.05 (5% margin of error). This resulted in a minimum requirement of 384 respondents. To ensure adequate statistical power for planned sub-group comparisons across the five professional categories within each of the seven study states, this minimum was substantially exceeded: a target of 150 respondents per state (1,050 in total) was set.

Study population and sampling technique

The accessible study population was identified through professional associations, veterinary networks, livestock extension officers, and market leaders. A multistage sampling technique was employed. In the first stage, the North-Central geopolitical zone of Nigeria comprising Benue, Kogi, Kwara, Nasarawa, Niger, and Plateau States, and the Federal Capital Territory (FCT) was purposively selected. In the second stage, stakeholders involved in animal health and antimicrobial use were stratified into five categories: veterinary practitioners, livestock farmers, animal health workers, animal health product outlet operators, and researchers. In the third stage, respondents were recruited from each stakeholder category within the selected states using a combination of purposive and convenience sampling. Although a target of 150 respondents per state (1,050 total) was proposed, a total of 800 completed and validated questionnaires were ultimately retained after data cleaning (76.2% of target), comprising 181 veterinarians, 165 animal health product outlet operators, 157 animal health workers, 153 researchers, and 144 livestock farmers distributed across the seven states.

Data collection

Data were collected using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire (Supplementary File 1) comprising five sections. The Demographic information section captured profession, education level, years of experience, state of practice, and primary food animal species handled. The Knowledge and Awareness of AMR section captured exposure to the term AMR, self-rated knowledge level (Excellent/Good/Average/Poor; a single-item self-rating rather than an objective knowledge test), perception of AMR as a problem, and perceived major causes. The effectiveness of AMR interventions section captured which intervention, among five listed options, respondents considered most and least effective; whether respondents had personally observed a reduction in AMR cases in their own practice, a self-reported perception rather than laboratory-confirmed surveillance data, hereafter referred to as a perceived AMR trend; implemented control measures; awareness of the Federal Government AMR Committee; and the tier of government perceived to have the most/least effective AMR coordination committee. The limitations and challenges section captured barriers to implementing AMR interventions, including gaps in awareness, surveillance, infection prevention, funding, stewardship, and governance. The policy and recommendations section captured whether respondents considered existing antimicrobial-use regulations in food animals sufficient (Yes/No/Not sure) and recommendations for strengthening AMR control.

The questionnaire included both closed-ended and multiple-response items, allowing respondents to select more than one option where applicable. It was pre-tested among 30 respondents in Kaduna State, North-West Nigeria (a state not included in the main study), proportionally reflecting the five target professional categories. Adjustments were made to improve clarity and contextual relevance based on this pre-test, and pre-test responses were not included in the main analytic dataset.

Data collection used a dual approach. Online administration was conducted via Google Forms, distributed through professional networks and shared in WhatsApp groups of veterinary and livestock professional associations by state veterinary officers and association leaders. Face-to-face administration of printed questionnaires was conducted by trained enumerators at veterinary clinics, livestock markets, and feed stores for respondents without internet access, with responses later double-entered for verification. Questionnaires were administered in English and, where necessary, orally translated into local languages by trained enumerators to ensure respondent comprehension.

Data analysis

Responses from physical questionnaires were checked for completeness, coded, and entered into Microsoft Excel. Data from the Google Forms were downloaded and merged with the physical responses into a single dataset. Analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarise variables. Chi-square (χ²) tests were applied to assess whether the distribution of AMR-related perceptions and practices (e.g., self-rated knowledge, perceived drivers of AMR, intervention rankings, perceived regulatory sufficiency) differed significantly across profession and, separately, across state, with profession or state entered as the independent grouping variable and the AMR-related outcome as the dependent variable in each test. Multiple-response items (e.g., questions where respondents could select more than one option) were converted into binary variables prior to analysis. For each possible response option, a new variable was created and coded as “1” if the option was selected and “0” if it was not selected. This allowed inclusion of these items in frequency summaries and cross-tabulations with other categorical variables. Degrees of freedom (df) and p-values were reported, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Results are presented in tables and figures for clarity.

Results

Demographic characteristics of respondents

A total of 800 respondents participated in the study, representing a balanced distribution across the seven states in the North-Central region of Nigeria (Fig. 1). The largest proportion were veterinarians (n = 181, 22.6%), followed by pharmacy/drug store operators (n = 165, 20.6%), animal health workers (n = 157, 19.6%), researchers (n = 153, 19.1%), and livestock farmers (n = 144, 18.0%) (Table 1). Educational attainment was generally high, with 63.9% (n = 511) holding tertiary qualifications and only 5.8% (n = 46) reporting no formal education. More than half (51.1%; n = 409) had between 1 and 10 years of experience, and geographic representation ranged from 13.0% (n = 104) in Benue to 15.4% (n = 123) in Nasarawa. Poultry was the most common livestock type handled (28.9%; n = 231), followed by porcine (24.8%; n = 198), ruminants (24.0%; n = 192), and fish (22.4%; n = 179) (Table 1).

Awareness and knowledge of antimicrobial resistance

Awareness of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) was high, with 90.5% (n = 724) reporting they had heard of the term and 95.4% (n = 763) recognising it as a major public health issue (Table 2). Self-rated knowledge levels varied, with the largest proportion rating their own knowledge as good (42.0%; n = 336), followed by average (30.9%; n = 247), excellent (17.9%; n = 143), and poor (9.3%; n = 74). More than half (56.4%; n = 451) perceived a reduction in AMR cases in their own practice (a self-reported observation rather than a laboratory-confirmed or surveillance-based measure) while 57.9% (n = 463) were aware of the Federal Government AMR Committee (Table 2). Self-rated knowledge was significantly associated with profession (χ²=12.52, df = 12, p = 0.01) and education level (χ²=10.27, df = 9, p = 0.02) (Table 3). Pharmacy/drug store operators reported the highest proportion rating their knowledge as good (48.5%), while researchers had the highest proportion rating their knowledge as excellent (20.9%). Respondents with tertiary education reported higher self-rated knowledge than those with no formal education (Table 3).

Perceived causes of antimicrobial resistance

Overuse of antimicrobials was the most frequently perceived cause of AMR (72.9%; n = 583); the proportion citing overuse as a driver differed significantly by profession (χ²=6.81, df = 4, p = 0.04) (Table 4), being highest among pharmacy/drug store operators (76.4%; n = 126/165) and animal health workers (75.8%; n = 119/157), and lowest among livestock farmers (66.7%; n = 96/144). Poor biosecurity measures were also widely perceived as a driver (75.5%; n = 604), with the proportion citing this driver differing significantly by profession (χ²=5.98, df = 4, p = 0.01); pharmacy/drug store operators (80.6%; n = 133/165) and veterinarians (77.9%; n = 141/181) had the highest proportions. Poor veterinary oversight was perceived as a driver by 75.0% (n = 600) of respondents, with significant differences by profession (χ²=10.69, df = 4, p = 0.03) (Table 4); animal health workers (80.3%; n = 126/157) and pharmacy/drug store operators (79.4%; n = 131/165) were most likely to report this issue. Counterfeit or poor-quality drugs were also frequently cited as a perceived driver (73.9%; n = 591), though the proportion citing this driver did not differ significantly across professions (p = 0.30).

Perceptions of AMR control interventions

Perceptions of the most effective intervention differed significantly by both profession (χ²=13.62, df = 16, p < 0.01) and state (χ²=21.02, df = 24, p < 0.01) (Table 5); overall, education to increase awareness was the most frequently selected option (24.4%; n = 195), most favoured by animal health workers (27.4%), veterinarians (24.9%), and respondents from Benue (26.9%). Optimal antimicrobial prescription was prioritised by researchers (26.8%) and pharmacy/drug store operators (25.5%), particularly in Niger State (27.1%). Perceptions of the least effective intervention showed no significant difference by profession (p = 0.10) but differed significantly by state (χ²=31.54, df = 24, p = 0.03) (Table 6); investment in AMR research was most frequently considered least effective, particularly by veterinarians (39.2%) and respondents from Benue (34.6%) and Plateau (37.2%) (Table 6).

Perceived AMR reduction and implemented measures

Perceived reductions in AMR were not significantly associated with any single self-reported control measure, including restricted antimicrobial use (p = 0.41), biosecurity (p = 0.90), vaccination (p = 0.20), probiotics or other treatments (p = 0.57), and public awareness or education (p = 0.60) (Table 7). As both the perceived reduction and the control measures were self-reported rather than independently verified, this finding is best interpreted as suggesting that respondents attribute perceived AMR reductions to multifactorial influences rather than any single intervention, rather than as evidence of an actual causal effect.

Awareness of the Federal Government AMR Committee

Awareness of the Federal Government AMR Committee varied markedly between professions (57.9%; n = 463 overall). Veterinarians reported the highest awareness (89.0%; n = 161/181), followed by researchers (63.4%; n = 97/153) and pharmacy/drug store operators (61.2%; n = 101/165), while livestock farmers had the lowest awareness (19.4%; n = 28/144) (χ²=167.36, df = 4, p < 0.001) (Table 8). Figure 2 shows respondents’ views on the tier of government with the most and least effective multi-sectoral AMR coordination committees. The federal tier was most frequently reported as the most effective (68.4%), followed by the state tier (21.6%), while the local tier was least often identified as most effective (10.0%). Conversely, the local tier was most frequently cited as the least effective (64.2%), followed by the state tier (24.3%), with the federal tier least often regarded as ineffective (11.5%).

Perceived effectiveness and sufficiency of AMR regulation

Perceptions of intervention effectiveness showed no significant differences by profession (p = 0.35) or state (p = 0.12) (Table 9). Across all respondents, 34.8% (n = 278) rated interventions as effective, 31.5% (n = 252) as somewhat effective, 20.4% (n = 163) as not effective, and 13.4% (n = 107) as very effective. In contrast, perceptions of AMR regulation sufficiency differed significantly by profession (χ²=5.03, df = 8, p = 0.01) and state (χ²=8.91, df = 12, p = 0.01) (Table 10). Overall, 60.5% (n = 484) of respondents considered existing regulation insufficient, 21.8% (n = 174) were unsure, and 17.8% (n = 142) felt it was sufficient. The perception of insufficiency was highest among pharmacy/drug store operators (63.0%) and respondents from the Federal Capital Territory (67.2%) (Table 10).

Priority areas for strengthening AMR control

Strong support was observed for four priority areas: stricter antimicrobial regulation, increased farmer education, more research on alternative treatments, and better enforcement of biosecurity measures (Table 11). Overall, 72.5% (n = 580) of respondents supported stricter antimicrobial regulation. By profession, support ranged from 65.4% among researchers to 79.4% among pharmacy/drug store operators, with veterinarians (72.4%), livestock farmers (71.5%), and animal health workers (73.2%) in between. State-level support ranged from 70.1% in Niger to 75.4% in Kwara. Profession was significantly associated with support (χ²=7.96, df = 4, p = 0.04), as was state (χ²=6.67, df = 6, p = 0.04) (Table 11). A total of 75.3% (n = 602) endorsed increased farmer education. Researchers showed the highest support (81.7%), followed by pharmacy/drug store operators (76.4%), veterinarians (74.0%), livestock farmers (72.2%), and animal health workers (72.0%). State-wise, support was highest in Nasarawa (78.9%) and lowest in Kwara (71.2%). Profession (χ²=5.28, df = 4, p = 0.02) and state (χ²=6.35, df = 6, p = 0.03) were both significantly associated (Table 11).

Discussion

This study provides an extensive assessment of self-reported awareness, self-rated knowledge, perceptions, and the implementation of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) interventions among veterinary and livestock stakeholders in North Central Nigeria, revealing a multifaceted picture of strengths and gaps in the national AMR response. Although over 90% of respondents reported awareness of AMR, fewer than one in five self-rated their own knowledge as excellent. This agrees with previous reports in Nigeria among veterinarians, livestock farmers, and drug vendors, where awareness levels have consistently exceeded 85% [9–11]. Such widespread recognition likely reflects the influence of sustained professional sensitisation and veterinary training, including town-hall engagements by NAFDAC with drug vendors and livestock stakeholders, stewardship workshops and guideline dissemination through the NAP-AMR framework, and high-visibility communication initiatives such as the youth-led African Youths for AMR communication campaign, which reached millions of Nigerians [6, 14, 22]. However, as in previous research in Abuja and Oyo State, this broad awareness did not translate into a uniformly high proportion of respondents self-rating their knowledge as “excellent” [23, 24]. This discrepancy points to a persistent challenge in AMR control in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs); while awareness campaigns have increased recognition of AMR as a public and animal health threat, insufficient emphasis on context-appropriate technical training may mean that many stakeholders lack the detailed understanding required to inform evidence-based practices, though, as a self-rating rather than a tested knowledge measure, this finding reflects respondents’ own confidence in their knowledge rather than an objectively verified deficit.

The dominant perception of AMR drivers like overuse of antimicrobials, poor biosecurity, weak veterinary oversight, and circulation of counterfeit or substandard drugs reflects well-documented systemic vulnerabilities in Nigeria’s veterinary and livestock systems. Previous national and sub-regional studies have shown that inappropriate antimicrobial use, including prophylactic and growth-promotion applications of critically important antimicrobials in poultry and aquaculture, is entrenched in production systems due to economic incentives, limited access to professional veterinary services, and weak enforcement of prescription-only regulations [25–27]. Similar trends have been reported in Ghana, Uganda, and Ethiopia, where over-the-counter sales and unsupervised use of antibiotics persist despite the presence of formal regulatory frameworks [28–30]. The prominence of counterfeit and poor-quality drugs in stakeholder perceptions is equally concerning and aligns with WHO and FAO reports across Africa [31]. An estimated 22.6% of medicines are substandard, particularly antibiotics (≈ 44.6%), with the continent accounting for 42% of globally detected cases of poor-quality medical products [32, 33]. These failures expose pathogens to sub-therapeutic drug concentrations, a recognised driver of antimicrobial resistance.

Perceptions of intervention effectiveness in this study varied by professional group and state, with veterinarians and animal health workers prioritising increased awareness and education, while researchers and drug store operators placed greater emphasis on optimal antimicrobial prescription. This agrees with previous findings by Adekanye [9] but disagrees with findings from East Africa, where prescribers often focus on stewardship protocols and rational use guidelines, whereas regulators and public health officials prioritise compliance monitoring and public sensitisation [34, 35]. The relatively low prioritisation of research investment by respondents in certain states may indicate a disconnect between academic outputs and field-level application; a gap previously identified in LMIC AMR governance reviews, where research often fails to influence local decision-making due to poor translation into actionable interventions [36–39].

The absence of significant associations between perceived AMR reduction and individual self-reported control measures such as antimicrobial restriction, vaccination, or biosecurity may reflect the inherently multifactorial nature of resistance emergence and containment, although this interpretation should be treated cautiously given the self-reported nature of both variables. This finding is broadly consistent with prior evidence from LMICs, which suggests that integrated, multi-component strategies combining stewardship, surveillance, infection prevention, and community engagement tend to be more effective in reducing AMR prevalence than isolated measures [38, 40, 41]. This is consistent with the broader One Health principle that sustainable AMR mitigation requires coordinated action across human, animal, and environmental health domains.

In terms of governance, the perception that the federal tier is the most effective in AMR coordination, contrasted with the widespread view of local-level ineffectiveness, is consistent with Nigeria’s centralised public health policy structure. Federal AMR coordination bodies, such as the NCDC-led AMR Technical Working Group and the National One Health Steering Committee, benefit from international partnerships, dedicated funding, and political visibility [6, 42]. However, as seen in similar LMICs, the presence of well-structured federal institutions does not necessarily ensure effective local implementation if sub-national entities lack operational resources, technical capacity, and decision-making authority [43–45]. The findings here suggest that without deliberate investment in state and local AMR committees, the translation of national strategies into local action may remain limited.

The perception by over 60% of respondents that current AMR regulations are insufficient, despite the existence of statutory controls, points to a gap between policy formulation and enforcement. This is not unique to Nigeria; comparable observations have been made in Ghana, Uganda, and Zambia, where comprehensive national AMR policies exist but enforcement mechanisms are inconsistently applied [15, 46, 47]. Respondent priorities for strengthening AMR control including stricter regulation, enhanced farmer education, improved biosecurity enforcement, and research into alternative treatments align closely with African Union and FAO recommendations. Regional strategies emphasise harmonised legislative frameworks (e.g., the AU Model Law on Medical Products Regulation and continental oversight through the proposed African Medicines Agency), stakeholder capacity building via surveillance networks and workforce training (e.g., AU Task Force, AMRSNET, Africa CDC’s MAAP initiative), and innovation in diagnostics, vaccines, and non-antibiotic, One Health-oriented interventions [48, 49].

Overall, the findings of this study suggest that while federal-level AMR governance structures in Nigeria are recognised and valued, their perceived impact is constrained by fragmented vertical integration, limited sub-national resources, and insufficient stakeholder engagement at community level. Addressing these gaps would likely require sustained political commitment, dedicated funding for local implementation, operational capacity building for state and local AMR committees, and systematic translation of research into contextually relevant interventions. The strength of this study lies in its broad geographic coverage and inclusion of diverse stakeholder groups, providing a nuanced understanding of AMR perceptions across multiple professional groups. Nevertheless, three limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, knowledge and perceived AMR reduction were each assessed using a single self-reported item rather than a validated, multi-item, or objectively measured instrument; these findings should therefore be read as stakeholder perceptions rather than as evidence of actual knowledge levels or measured AMR trends. Second, the cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference regarding the relationships observed between demographic/professional characteristics and AMR-related perceptions. Third, although a face-to-face arm was included to reach respondents without internet access, the dual online/face-to-face recruitment strategy may have disproportionately captured more literate, urban, and better-connected stakeholders, as reflected in the high proportion of respondents with tertiary education (63.9%); this may limit generalisability to less-educated or more remote segments of the target population. Future studies should incorporate validated knowledge-testing instruments and microbiological or surveillance data alongside longitudinal follow-up to determine whether shifts in awareness, knowledge, and governance perceptions are associated with measurable changes in AMR prevalence.

Conclusion

This study indicates that while awareness of antimicrobial resistance among veterinary and livestock stakeholders in North-Central Nigeria is high, substantial gaps remain in self-rated knowledge depth, in the translation of awareness into reported practice, and in the perceived effectiveness of AMR governance at sub-national levels. Federal AMR coordination structures were perceived as more visible and effective than state or local structures; addressing this gap in perceived sub-national capacity may benefit from targeted investment in local implementation. Perceived drivers of AMR (antimicrobial overuse, weak biosecurity, and counterfeit drugs) mirror patterns reported elsewhere in sub-Saharan Africa, pointing to a need for multifaceted, context-specific interventions. These findings, drawn from stakeholder perceptions rather than microbiological or surveillance data, point to priority areas stakeholder-tailored education, strengthened regulatory enforcement, and sustained investment in sub-national AMR committees that future studies incorporating objective knowledge measures and longitudinal or microbiological data could help validate.
