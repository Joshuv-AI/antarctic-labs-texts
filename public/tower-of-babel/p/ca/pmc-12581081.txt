Risk factors for and outcomes of intraoperative desaturation in neonates undergoing non-cardiac surgery: a retrospective study
Authors: Chunyi Jin, Jialian Zhao, Bin Bin Cai, Hao Xie, Cuicui Sun, Lingli Yu, Yaoqin Hu, Yue Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to determine the prevalence, outcomes and risk factors for intraoperative desaturation in neonates undergoing non-cardiac surgery.MethodsThis retrospective study included neonates who underwent non-cardiac surgery between April 2020 and October 2022. Patients were classified as normal or reduced oxygenation based on a review of their medical records and whether they experienced intraoperative desaturation. The incidence of intraoperative desaturation, as well as postoperative outcomes and other characteristics, was recorded.ResultsA total of 1005 neonates were included, of whom 118 (11.74%) experienced intraoperative desaturation. Intraoperative desaturation was significantly associated with increased postoperative mortality (16.95% vs. 4.62%, p<0.001) and postoperative outcomes including pulmonary complications, acidosis, anemia, thrombocytopenia, unplanned surgery and intubation along with prolonged hospital and intensive care unit stay (all p<0.05). After conducting both univariate and multivariate analyses, 10 preoperative and surgical factors were identified to be significantly associated with intraoperative desaturation. These included six preoperative disease and treatment factors (pneumonia, pulmonary arterial hypertension, anemia, acidosis, pulmonary surfactant treatment and oxygen therapy), two surgical factors (duration of surgery and thoracic surgery) along with the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status and low weight.ConclusionsThe incidence of intraoperative desaturation among neonatal patients is relatively high, and it may be associated with a multitude of serious adverse complications. Several perioperative factors are significantly associated with the occurrence of intraoperative desaturation.Keywords: Intensive Care, Neonatal; Anesthetics; Thoracic Surgery; Neonatology; Pulmonary Medicine

Introduction

Desaturation is an adverse physiologic state that poses a significant risk to patients during the administration of general anesthesia and surgical procedures.1 Recent studies have shown that perioperative desaturation events were observed in approximately 6.8% of adults aged 16 years and older, with severe desaturation events occurring in 3.5% of patients.2 Neonates have an increased risk of desaturation during general anesthesia compared with adults3 4 due to elevated metabolic demands, reduced functional residual capacity and susceptibility to alveolar collapse.5,9 Desaturation is associated with a number of adverse outcomes including arrhythmia, neurologic injury and cardiac arrest, as well as postoperative infection and impaired wound healing through various metabolic pathways.1 Moreover, neurologic tissue changes caused by desaturation are associated with impaired cognitive function.10 11 In addition, desaturation is a significant contributory factor for neurological injury in full-term infants during the perioperative phase.12

While there are studies and guideline documents on desaturation in both pediatric and adult medicine,2 13 there is a lack of relevant data identifying and analyzing risk factors for neonatal desaturation and its impact on morbidity and mortality. Therefore, the aim of this study was to describe the incidence, outcomes and risk factors for neonatal desaturation in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

Neonates aged 1–28 days who underwent general anesthesia for surgery in standard operating rooms from April 2020 to October 2022 were retrospectively included in this study. All included infants presented with baseline oxygen saturation levels of at least 95% on operating room admission. Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients undergoing anesthesia without tracheal intubation; (2) cardiac surgery; (3) cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD); and (4) loss of vital data (absence of essential intraoperative vital sign monitoring, particularly oxygen saturation and heart rate). This study adhered to the “Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology” (STROBE) guidelines.

Data collection and definitions

Demographic and intraoperative data were collected from the clinical information system (Ewell, Hangzhou, China) and institutional electronic Anesthesia Data System (Medical System, Hangzhou, China) of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Hangzhou, China. All patients were automatically monitored and recorded for heart rate, blood pressure and pulse oxygen saturation (SPO2) every minute throughout the anesthetic period by the electronic Anesthesia Data System. Demographic information included: gestational age (GA), gender and weight. Preoperative variables included the following: the American Society of Anesthesiologists classification of physical status (ASA physical status), respiratory diseases, CHD, preoperative anemia, preoperative sepsis, preoperative therapy (pulmonary surfactant, corticosteroids, mechanical ventilation, oxygen therapy, vasoactive drugs and blood transfusions), preoperative laboratory tests and whether the procedure was an emergency. Intraoperative variables included type of surgical, duration of surgery and anesthesia, fluid load, blood transfusion, vasoactive drugs, fluid load, blood loss, urine and intraoperative laboratory testing. Postoperative complications were also recorded, including postoperative intensive care unit (ICU) stay, postoperative length of hospital stay, postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) within 48 hours, any other complications and mortality within 30 days.

The primary outcome measure was the incidence of desaturation. Secondary measures were the clinical outcomes among desaturated neonates and the risk factors for intraoperative desaturation. Intraoperative desaturation was defined as a sustained decrease in pulse oxygen saturation (SpO2) below 85% for at least two consecutive minutes.14,17 All complications were diagnosed according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) 9/10 medical codes. PPCs were defined as the occurrence of a respiratory infection, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax, bronchospasm or aspiration pneumonitis.18 19 Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined as an increase of serum creatinine of 0.3 mg/dL or 50% relative to preoperative levels within 48 hours of surgery or the presence of an AKI diagnostic code within 7 days of surgery.20 Hyperlactatemia was defined as an arterial blood gas lactate level greater than 2 mmol/L.21 Sepsis was diagnosed according to the International Consensus Criteria for Pediatric Sepsis and Septic Shock.22 Thrombocytopenia was defined as a platelet count of less than 150×109/L.23 Neonatal anemia was defined as hemoglobin (Hb) levels below the fifth percentile.24 Acidosis was defined as a blood pH level below 7.35 accompanied by a serum bicarbonate (HCO3⁻) level below 22 mmol/L.25 Hypercapnia was defined as an elevated arterial partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), typically greater than 45 mm Hg.26

As noted above, SpO2 data were automatically recorded at 1 min intervals. If the pulse waveform was severely distorted due to positional changes or external pressure the SpO2 recording was considered erroneous.13 As the value of SpO2 decreases, especially when it falls below the threshold of 80%–85%, the reliability of pulse oximetry diminishes significantly. To ensure data accuracy, all collected measurements underwent manual verification by clinical staff prior to formal inclusion in the dataset. If the pulse rate from the pulse oximeter differs from that recorded on the electrocardiogram (ECG) by more than 20%, the oxygen saturation reading is considered as erroneous.27

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed and presented as mean±standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range (IQR)) after testing for normality. Comparisons between the two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using the χ2 test. After conducting univariate analysis and collinearity diagnosis, significant variables were further analyzed using binary logistic regression (forward, LR; entry level, 0.05; exclusion level, 0.1) to evaluate the potential risk factors for intraoperative desaturation. Missing data were excluded or imputed using Multiple Imputation. After performing imputation, we conducted analyses to evaluate its effectiveness. A comparison of the results before and after imputation revealed no statistically significant differences. All statistical tests were two-tailed and performed using SPSS V.22.0 software. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From April 2020 to October 2022, 1390 neonates underwent surgery at the Children’s Hospital. After excluding patients who received anesthesia without tracheal intubation (n=181), cardiac surgery cases (n=185), cyanotic CHD (n=9) and those with missing key data (n=10), a total of 1005 neonates met the study criteria following retrospective review, of whom 118 (11.74%) developed desaturation during surgery (figure 1).

The study cohort displayed an overall 30-day mortality rate of 6.07%. Of note, neonates experiencing intraoperative desaturation demonstrated significantly higher mortality (16.95% vs. 4.62%, p<0.001). Intraoperative desaturation was associated with longer ICU stay, longer postoperative mechanical ventilation time and longer postoperative hospital stay (all p values <0.05). In addition, intraoperative desaturation was associated with an increased incidence of unplanned reoperation (8.47% vs. 2.82%, p=0.002) and unplanned reintubation (5.08% vs. 1.58%, p=0.010). The proportion of patients requiring postoperative blood transfusion in the ICU also increased significantly in the desaturation group (55.08% vs. 35.85%, p<0.001). Furthermore, intraoperative hypoxia was associated with significantly increased odds of postoperative pneumonia (69.49% vs. 43.29%, p<0.001), pneumothorax (11.02% vs. 2.93%, p<0.001), acidosis (32.2% vs. 20.52%, p=0.002), hyperlactatemia (40.68% vs. 26.94%, p=0.001), hypercapnia (11.86% vs. 4.06%, p<0.001), thrombocytopenia (16.10% vs. 6.20%, p<0.001) and postoperative anemia (36.44% vs. 28.41%, p=0.013). Figure 2 shows the multivariate analysis of risk factors for intraoperative desaturation. All of the above perioperative risk factors were included in this analysis. Ten preoperative and surgical factors were identified to be associated with the incidence of intraoperative desaturation. These included six preoperative diseases and treatments (pneumonia: odds ratio (OR) 2.732, p=0.004; pulmonary arterial hypertension: OR=1.719, p=0.038; anemia: OR=3.771, p<0.001; acidosis: OR=2.277, p<0.001; pulmonary surfactant treatment: OR=0.311, p=0.006; and oxygen therapy: OR=1.726, p=0.044), two surgical factors (duration of surgery: OR=1.009, p<0.001; and thoracic surgery: OR 3.329, p<0.001), ASA physical status (OR=1.592, p=0.038) and low weight (OR=2.944, p=0.004). With the exception of the protective effect of applying pulmonary surfactant preoperatively, the other nine are risk factors for intraoperative desaturation.

Discussion

This retrospective study reported a high incidence of intraoperative desaturation in neonates undergoing general anesthesia with tracheal intubation; this was associated with increased postoperative mortality and complications. Ten preoperative and surgical factors were identified as being associated with the incidence of intraoperative desaturation. This study is of clinical value as previous studies have not investigated the risk factors associated with intraoperative desaturation in neonates, a unique surgical population. The findings of this study may provide a theoretical basis to reduce desaturation during surgery and enhance oxygenation management in neonatal patients.

Our study indicates that intraoperative desaturation in neonates is associated with prolonged ICU and hospital stays, as well as prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation. Failure to stabilize respiration and oxygenation after preterm birth is a recognized factor for adverse outcomes and prolonged hospitalization.28 Desaturation is significantly linked to an elevated risk of mortality, regardless of the patient’s age or the type of disease present.29 Neonatal desaturation is associated with conditions such as asthma,30 bronchopulmonary dysplasia,31 intraventricular hemorrhage and ultimately mortality. Moreover, desaturation impairs cardiomyocyte proliferation, differentiation and survival, contributing to myocardial ischemia.32 Prolonged exposure to low oxygen levels has been associated with delays in speech development, cognitive development and motor skills deficits at 18 months.33 In addition, hypoxia causes vascular smooth muscle cell dysfunction, leading to vasospasm and pulmonary hypertension, potentially creating a vicious cycle.34 Congenital pulmonary arterial hypertension induced by desaturation is characterized by impaired nitric oxide synthase activity; this can be alleviated by exogenous nitric oxide therapy.35 In addition, neonates are intolerant to hypoxia, susceptible to internal environmental disturbances and acidosis. Collectively, this may lead to acid metabolite storage, lactic acid elevation and ultimately poor prognosis.

This study identified preoperative pneumonia and oxygen therapy as significant risk factors for intraoperative desaturation. Previous pediatric studies have also reported a prior diagnosis of pneumonia36 or other pulmonary diseases37 as significant risk factors for intraoperative desaturation. These findings highlight the need for careful preoperative assessment and management to minimize respiratory complications and improve patient outcomes.38 A multidisciplinary approach involving surgeons, anesthesiologists and other healthcare professionals is essential to determine the optimal timing of surgery, ensure comprehensive patient evaluation and thereby improve surgical safety. Proactive management of pneumonia, including respiratory therapy, assessment of lung maturity, correction of acidosis and treatment of pulmonary hypertension, is critical.

Inadequate blood volume and hemoglobin levels, along with decreased total blood oxygen concentration, are factors contributing to the increased incidence of decreased arterial blood oxygen saturation during respiratory pauses.39 Furthermore, the increased metabolic demands and limited lung capacity of neonates may further increase the risk of acute and severe decreases in arterial blood oxygen saturation. Blood transfusion may serve as a potential intervention to mitigate decreases in oxygen saturation and reduce the effects of desaturation in preterm infants, especially when oxygen saturation repeatedly decreases before transfusion.40

In addition to the risk factors noted above, our study identified several other factors that have only been partially reported previously. For example, in children under 5 years of age undergoing rigid bronchoscopy for foreign body removal, intraoperative or postoperative desaturation is associated with duration of surgery.36 Furthermore, body weight37 has been identified as a risk factor for postoperative oxygen desaturation in pediatric patients undergoing tonsillectomy.

Pulmonary surfactant treatment is a protective factor for intraoperative desaturation. Surfactant deficiency increases alveolar surface tension, leading to atelectasis and ventilation-perfusion imbalance. This results in desaturation, hypercapnia and acidosis. Studies have shown that pulmonary surfactant therapy significantly reduces mortality along with risk of pneumothorax and pulmonary interstitial emphysema in infants.41 Therefore, surfactant therapy may be a feasible intervention for reducing the incidence of perioperative desaturation.

A retrospective study in children aged 0–16 years found a desaturation rate of 56% in neonates, which is higher than the rate found in the present study.13 The differences arise from the definitions used in the two studies. The previous study used SpO2 ≤90%, while the current study used SpO2 ≤85%. The previous study also included patients with cyanotic CHD, which affects baseline oxygen levels. In addition, there were fewer neonates in the previous study. An additional study reported that 40% of neonates and infants under general anesthesia and intubated experienced desaturation, defined as a SpO2 below 90% for at least 60 s.42

There is no standardized definition of neonatal desaturation. A European multicenter study found that more than 60% of desaturation interventions were triggered by SpO2 levels below 85%.17 Studies suggest that maintaining neonatal SpO2 between 85% and 94% reduces the incidence of retinopathy of prematurity, oxygen exposure, duration of mechanical ventilation and bronchopulmonary dysplasia.14 43 However, a meta-analysis by Askie et al. found no significant difference in death or major disability rates at 18–24 months between low (85%–89%) and high (91%–95%) SpO2 target ranges.15 Moreover, Castillo et al. suggested an SpO2 range of 85%–93% to maintain partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2) between 40 and 80 mm Hg.14 Therefore, our retrospective study defined perioperative desaturation as SpO2 below 85%. Of note, these studies focused on prolonged periods of SpO2 control from birth until hospital discharge. In contrast, our study focused solely on the intraoperative period and transient desaturation events during surgery. While our findings are consistent with those of the above studies, we should not equate intraoperative hypoxia directly with neonatal intensive care unit(NICU) SpO2 target strategies.

Limitations

The current study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective study and despite a thorough review of all medical records, there are inherent limitations associated with the accuracy of recorded variables. Furthermore, this study can only demonstrate associations, not causal relationships. Although we used both pulse rate and the ECG heart rate to exclude erroneous oxygen saturation measurements, inaccuracies remain. Second, our study based comparisons on a threshold value, without continuous variable of SpO2. As a result, it was not possible to obtain outcomes related to varying degrees of hypoxia. Third, the large number of comparisons relative to the small number of hypoxia events (n=118) raises the risk of a Type I error. Fourth, the study was conducted at a single institution, which increases the likelihood of selection bias and limits the generalizability of our conclusions to other populations. Therefore, future multicenter prospective studies to validate and strengthen the findings of this initial research should be considered.

In conclusion, the incidence of intraoperative desaturation is high in neonates, placing them at risk for a variety of significant postoperative complications. Early prediction and identification of desaturation is critical for the effective management of neonatal patients, allowing anesthesiologists to manage and ultimately reduce the occurrence of desaturation.
