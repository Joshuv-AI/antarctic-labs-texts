Investigating substrate specificity in arogenate versus prephenate dehydratases
Authors: Emily J Clayton, Megan Smith-Uffen, Travis W Tribble, Martin L Duennwald, Susanne E Kohalmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhenylalanine is an essential aromatic amino acid that can only be synthesized de novo by microorganisms and plants. In microorganisms, phenylalanine is synthesized through the prephenate pathway, requiring the activity of a prephenate dehydratase (PDT). In plants, phenylalanine is synthesized instead mostly through the arogenate pathway, requiring the enzyme arogenate dehydratase (ADT). In Arabidopsis, there is a family of six ADTs that catalyze this final step of phenylalanine biosynthesis. However, two of the AtADTs, AtADT1 and AtADT2, can also act as PDTs. All six AtADTs have a high sequence similarity, making it difficult to determine in silico which amino acids determine substrate specificity.ResultsHere, we use domain swapping, targeted mutagenesis, and pha2 yeast complementation to investigate amino acids that confer PDT activity in pha2 yeast. In addition, we established a novel in vivo test of ADT activity to determine how these amino acid changes affect ADT and PDT activity of the AtADTs.ConclusionsOur results demonstrate that a combination of amino acids in the regulatory ACT domain contributes to both ADT and PDT activity in the AtADTs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12915-026-02586-8.Keywords: Arogenate dehydratase, Prephenate dehydratase, ACAI assay, Screening by complementation, Sequence-function relationship, ACT domain, Arabidopsis thaliana, Saccharomyces cerevisiae

Background

Phenylalanine (Phe) is an essential aromatic amino acid for animals as they cannot synthesize it, and it must be obtained through diet [1]. Not only is Phe important for protein synthesis, it also serves in animals as a precursor to important molecules such as dopamine, serotonin, and vitamin E [1–4]. In plants, Phe serves as a precursor to many specialized metabolites that are required for biotic and abiotic defense, structural support, and pigment and scent formation [5–9].

Plants and microbes synthesize Phe de novo from chorismate, the product of the shikimate pathway [10, 11]. Starting from chorismate, there are two pathways to synthesize Phe, the prephenate pathway and the arogenate pathway (Fig. 1 A). Microorganisms typically use the prephenate pathway, while plants predominantly use the arogenate pathway [12, 13]. Both pathways have mechanistically equivalent enzymatic reactions, just in reverse order. In the prephenate pathway, prephenate is first decarboxylated/dehydrated into phenylpyruvate by a prephenate dehydratase (PDT [14]), which is subsequently transaminated into Phe by a phenylpyruvate aminotransferase (PPY-AT [14, 15]; Fig. 1 A). The arogenate pathway begins with the transamination of prephenate to arogenate by a prephenate aminotransferase (PPA-AT [16–18]), followed by the decarboxylation/dehydration into Phe by an arogenate dehydratase (ADT [11, 13, 19]; Fig. 1 A). In plants, there is often a small group of closely related genes encoding a family of ADT enzymes that catalyze this final, rate-limiting step of Phe biosynthesis (Fig. 1B). There are three subgroups within this plant ADT phylogeny named subgroups I, II, and III [20, 21]. Most plant species have at least one ADT isoform in each subgroup (Fig. 1B, Additional file 1: Table S1). In fact, many enzymes required for plant specialized metabolite synthesis at key regulatory steps exist as multiple isoforms [22, 23]. This is partially due to multiple whole genome duplications throughout angiosperm evolution, often leading to a large number of duplicate genes in one species [23, 24]. While duplications events are often followed by the loss of duplicates, they can also lead to neofunctionalization at the genetic and biochemical levels [25–27]. The retained isoforms can then contribute differentially to metabolism [28–30] either due to new tissue or subcellular expression patterns, or additional substrate recognition and processing [31–33].

The prephenate and arogenate pathways are similar and require the action of related enzymes. Both ADT and PDT enzymes catalyze a decarboxylation/dehydration reaction. In addition, their respective substrates, arogenate and prephenate, only differ in one amino group. In addition, ADTs have a similar domain structure to microbial PDTs. ADTs and PDTs share a catalytic domain responsible for the enzymatic reaction and a regulatory aspartate kinase-chorismate mutase-tyrA (ACT) domain involved in the allosteric regulation by both Phe and Tyr [35–37] (Fig. 1 C). The ACT domain is a highly conserved regulatory domain found in many enzymes that participate in amino acid biosynthesis [36, 38]. Given the shared domain structure and similarity of substrates, it is not surprising that ADTs and PDTs share a high degree of sequence similarity at the protein level and many key catalytic and regulatory motifs are shared between ADTs and PDTs. The TRF catalytic triad was identified as critical for catalysis in the Escherichia coli P-protein and Corynebacterium glutamicum PDT [39, 40] and is conserved in all six AtADTs and other plant ADT sequences [20, 21]. In the ACT domain, two key regulatory motifs, GALV and ESRP, facilitate the two conformational structures (open and closed) depending on the ligand bound [37, 39, 41]. These motifs are also well conserved in many plant ADTs [20, 21].

One major difference between plant and bacterial domain structures is the presence of a transit peptide domain in plant enzymes. This transit peptide localizes the proteins to the chloroplast, the site of aromatic amino acid biosynthesis in plants [10, 42, 43] (Fig. 1 C). While plants predominantly use the chloroplastic arogenate pathway to synthesize Phe, there is evidence that a cytosolic prephenate pathway exists as well [44, 45]. Accordingly, specific ADT isoforms from various plant species have detectable PDT activity [12, 20, 21, 29, 45]. Further, the ADT enzymes capable of recognizing prephenate as a substrate are generally found in subgroups I and II (Fig. 1B), whereas strict ADT enzymes are generally clustered in subgroup III [20, 21] (Fig. 1B). However, the sequence similarity between ADTs and PDTs makes it difficult to identify which amino acids specify arogenate vs prephenate substrate recognition.

Predicting protein function solely from protein sequence remains challenging, as only a few key amino acids in the sequence can define protein function. Enzymes appear to be especially sensitive to small sequence changes [46–49]. Function defining amino acids are often located in the active site of the enzyme [50–52], but can also be located outside of the catalytic domain [53–56]. A single amino acid altering enzyme function has been demonstrated for multiple plant enzymes involved in various metabolic pathways [51, 57–60].

Previously, it was demonstrated that a subset of ADTs in Arabidopsis have PDT activity sufficient to complement the Saccharomyces cerevisiae pha2 PDT knockout phenotype [20, 61]. Here, we investigate the sequences that are involved in PDT activity using sequence analysis, domain swapping, and pha2 complementation analysis. As part of our analysis, we developed a new in vivo assay: the ACAI assay, which allows us to test for ADT activity independently from PDT activity. Our work identifies three amino acids that contribute to PDT activity in the AtADT family.

Results

The ACT but not the catalytic domain is required for PDT activity

To determine if substrate specificity can be determined by structural differences between the different AtADTs, we used predicted structures from AlphaFold [62, 63] to compare the AtADTs, as to our knowledge no crystal structure has been determined for a plant ADT enzyme to date. However, as the transit peptide regions were modeled with low confidence, we removed the transit peptide sequences prior to comparison. No major differences were found to the overall conformation or the active site between any AtADTs (especially between the AtADT/PDTs and strict AtADTs), and we were unable to identify key residues determining substrate specificity by structure alone (Additional file 2: Fig. S1).

Therefore, to determine which sequences within an ADT are responsible for substrate recognition, we decided on a domain swapping approach. A domain from a prephenate-accepting AtADT (ADT/PDT) was exchanged with a domain from a solely arogenate-accepting AtADT (strict ADT) and the resulting proteins (referred to as chimeras) were tested for PDT function. PDT function was assessed using the pha2 yeast complementation assay, as described by Bross and colleagues [61]. Complementation of the PDT knockout phenotype of the pha2 yeast strain indicates the chimeric protein can use prephenate as a substrate and therefore has PDT activity. AtADT2 was chosen as a prephenate-accepting ADT/PDT as it displayed the highest Kcat/Km of the AtADTs for prephenate [20], and was shown to complement the pha2 strain [61]. AtADT4 and AtADT5 were chosen as solely arogenate-accepting AtADTs, as they do not have any detectable PDT activity [20, 61]. For AtADT2/AtADT4 swapping, six different chimeras were constructed using overlap extension PCR (Fig. 2, C1–C6), and seven AtADT2/AtADT5 constructs were generated in the same manner (Fig. 2, C7–C13). Each chimera was transformed into the pha2 yeast strain and expressed under the control of a GAL1 promoter [64]. Full-length AtADT2, AtADT4, and AtADT5 were used as positive and negative controls, respectively (Fig. 2). In addition, yeast transformed with an empty destination vector (Fig. 2, pDEST) and untransformed yeast cells (Fig. 2, pha2) were also used as negative controls. To ensure that all transformants were expressing AtADT proteins, total protein was extracted and probed with an anti-green fluorescent protein (GFP) antibody, as all AtADTs were expressed as enhanced cyan fluorescent protein (ECFP) fusions (Additional file 3: Fig. S2A). All yeast strains were tested for their ability to grow in presence or absence of Phe on media containing glucose to repress or galactose to induce the GAL1 promoter [64–66] (Fig. 2).

As expected, the untransformed pha2 strain was unable to grow regardless of Phe supplementation or carbon source, as it does not contain the vector. Similarly, yeast transformed with the empty destination vector were able to grow with Phe supplementation, but not in the absence of Phe (Fig. 2). AtADT2 wild type, as a dual ADT/PDT enzyme, can synthesize Phe from prephenate and thus complements the pha2 phenotype. As strict ADTs, AtADT4 and AtADT5 full-length wild-type proteins cannot synthesize Phe using prephenate and were therefore unable to complement the pha2 phenotype. Of the chimeric proteins tested, the AtADT2/ADT4 chimeras C2, C4, and C6 and the AtADT2/ADT5 chimeras C8, C10, C12, and C13 were able to grow in the absence of Phe, indicating that these sequences confer sufficient PDT activity to complement the pha2 strain (Fig. 2). These chimeras all share the ACT domain of AtADT2, specifically the N-terminal portion of the ACT domain (Figs. 2, 3).

In silico sequence analysis of AtADTs

Since all the chimeric proteins able to accept prephenate as a substrate contained the N-terminal ACT domain from AtADT2, we investigated this region in more detail. A sequence alignment was used to identify amino acid positions that differentiate between the prephenate-accepting and the solely arogenate-accepting ADTs with the following three criteria: (1) an amino acid is conserved between AtADT1 and AtADT2, (2) an amino acid is conserved between AtADT3-AtADT6, and (3) these two amino acids are different (Fig. 3). Six amino acids (A335, A337, V338, L341, and N345; all positions are numbered according to AtADT5 sequence) met these criteria. In addition, a motif unique to AtADT1 and AtADT2, 322-SLEEGPG-330, was identified (Fig. 3). We therefore generated another AtADT2-AtADT5 chimera to test this 322-SLEEGPG-330 motif, called AtADT2-SLEEG, which contains N-terminal AtADT2 sequences up to G330, and C-terminal AtADT5 sequences from V331–V425. As a control for an alteration outside the catalytic domain, we chose one amino acid in the C-terminal region of the ACT domain, position 397. While position 397 follows the amino acid selection criteria, it is located outside of the N-terminal ACT domain identified in the chimera analysis. We initially focused on amino acid substitutions at these positions in AtADT5, as AtADT5 is a solely arogenate-accepting ADT. The AtADT5 sequence at these positions was substituted with the AtADT2 sequences.

Multiple amino acids in the AtADT2 N-terminal ACT domain are sufficient to complement the S. cerevisiae pha2 strain

Each of these six targeted substitutions in AtADT5 was tested using the pha2 complementation assay to determine if these sequences are sufficient to introduce PDT activity (Fig. 4). Full-length wild-type AtADT2, AtADT4, and AtADT5 were again used as positive and negative controls, respectively (Fig. 4). The AtADT2-SLEEG (N-terminal AtADT2 sequences up to and including the 322-SLEEGPG-330 motif fused to the remaining C-terminal sequences of AtADT5), AtADT5-S345N, and AtADT5-V397L mutants were unable to support growth on inducing galactose medium (inducing condition) lacking Phe. However, AtADT5-V335A, AtADT5-S337A, and AtADT5-F341L mutants complemented the PDT knockout phenotype (Fig. 4). Therefore, we concluded that the F341L, V335A, and S337A substitutions in AtADT5 result in the introduction of PDT function to this otherwise strict AtADT.

To confirm the importance of positions 335, 337, and 341 in conferring PDT vs ADT activity, targeted substitutions were generated in all other AtADTs. In the strict AtADT sequences (AtADT3, AtADT4, and AtADT6), the position of interest was replaced with the amino acid found in the AtADT/PDTs (AtADT1 and AtADT2) and vice versa (Fig. 5). Thr and Trp were chosen as these amino acids were present in ca. 50% of the bacterial PDT sequences analyzed (13/26). These AtADT substituted proteins were expressed in the pha2 yeast strain for complementation analysis.

The strict AtADT proteins with the F341L substitutions were all able to utilize prephenate as a substrate and complement the pha2 strain, indicating this substitution conferred PDT activity to these strict AtADTs (Fig. 5). AtADT1 L341F did not complement pha2 and therefore lost PDT activity (Fig. 5); however, AtADT2 L341F did not lose PDT activity. Further, the AtADT5 F341T and F341W mutants both complemented the pha2 strain, suggesting it is indeed the lack of Phe at position 341 that confers PDT activity.

Amino acid substitutions at positions 335 and 337 in general had a similar effect on PDT activity as those at position 341. Introducing the dual AtADT/PDT amino acid (V335A) into the strict AtADTs at position 335 conferred PDT activity to AtADT3, but not to AtADT4 or AtADT6 (Fig. 5). However, the A335V substitution resulted in a loss of PDT activity in the dual AtADT/PDTs, AtADT1 and AtADT2 (Fig. 5). Therefore, this amino acid appears to be important for prephenate substrate recognition in AtADT1 and AtADT2, but is not sufficient to introduce PDT activity into all the strict AtADTs. Surprisingly, substitutions at position 337 affected the PDT activity of AtADT1, but not any of the other AtADTs (Fig. 5). We concluded positions 335 and 337 are involved in prephenate substrate recognition in some AtADTs, but they do not define PDT activity in the entire AtADT family.

The ACAI assay: ADT complementation of aro8aro9 in vivo

While we have demonstrated the effect of single amino acid substitutions on PDT activity, the pha2 strain cannot determine the effect these substitutions have on ADT activity. Determining the ADT activity of a protein currently requires in vitro analysis and isolation of the metastable substrate arogenate, as it cannot be purchased commercially. As we have been using the pha2 complementation system to assess PDT activity, we sought to develop a comparable in vivo method to assess ADT activity. Our ADT complementation assay uses a ppy-at knockout yeast strain (eliminating the prephenate pathway) into which we incorporated the two-step arogenate pathway using Arabidopsis genes (Fig. 1 A). This assay can detect ADT activity regardless of PDT activity. Therefore, it can distinguish between an ADT and a dual ADT/PDT when used in conjunction with the pha2 complementation assay. We have named this assay the ACAI assay: ADT complementation of aro8aro9 in vivo.

In S. cerevisiae, there are two genes encoding two PPY-AT proteins, ARO8 and ARO9 [68, 69]. To determine if ARO8 and ARO9 were redundant, we obtained both single knockout strains (Dharmacon) and cultured them with and without Phe (Fig. 6A, top). Both aro8 and aro9 single knockout strains were able to grow in the absence of Phe, indicating redundancy, and so a double knockout was required. The aro8 and aro9 single knockout strains were mated, and the heterozygous diploid was sporulated. We then separated the ascospores and identified putative aro8aro9 double knockout strains by replica plating on media containing or lacking Phe (Fig. 6A, bottom). We identified multiple independent Phe requiring strains and confirmed the knockout by PCR (Fig. 6B and C).

Assessing the ACAI assay strategy

To co-express the AtPPA-AT and AtADTs, we cloned the Arabidopsis PPA-AT sequence into a galactose inducible (and glucose repressible) expression vector with the GAL1 promoter. The AtPPA-AT expression vector contains the LEU2 selectable marker to distinguish it from the AtADT expression vector (HIS3; Fig. 7A). If only one vector is present in the aro8aro9 strain, the arogenate pathway is incomplete and no Phe is synthesized. As the aro8aro9 strain lacks the prephenate pathway, it is Phe requiring, and there is no growth without Phe supplementation, regardless of carbon source used. In the presence of galactose, GAL1 is active, and both the AtPPA-AT and the putative ADTs are expressed (Fig. 7A). The arogenate produced by the AtPPA-AT can only be converted to Phe by an enzyme with ADT activity, thus completing the arogenate pathway (Fig. 7A). Though the aro8aro9 strain still encodes a functional PDT enzyme (PHA2), the pathway cannot be complemented by an additional PDT, as the subsequent aminotransferase is not present.

All cultures were spotted on media selecting for the presence of vectors (lacking His for AtADT vectors, lacking Leu for the AtPPA-AT vector, or lacking both for double transformants; Fig. 7B, column V). As expected, the untransformed aro8aro9 strain was unable to grow on any media as it does not contain either vector. No growth for any culture was observed on glucose media lacking Phe. Cultures transformed with only one vector were also unable to grow on any media lacking Phe. Only yeast expressing both the AtPPA-AT and an AtADT (AtADT2 or AtADT3; Fig. 7B) complemented the aro8aro9 strain, indicating the arogenate pathway was reconstituted in this yeast strain. We thus successfully devised an assay that detects ADT activity of any protein of interest.

The ACT domain contributes to ADT activity of dual AtADT/PDTs

We next tested each of the AtADT substitution proteins with the ACAI assay (Fig. 8). The A335V substitution in the dual AtADT/PDTs enzymes abolished ADT activity, as it did PDT activity of these enzymes (Fig. 8). The reverse V335A substitution also abolished the ADT activity of AtADT4 (Fig. 8). Interestingly, the AtADT4 V335A mutant also lacked PDT activity. The V335A substitution did not affect the ADT activity of the other strict AtADTs, while it did confer PDT activity to AtADT5 (Fig. 8, controls in Additional file 4: Fig. S3).

Substitutions at position 337 had a mixed effect on the ADT activity of AtADTs. The A337S substitution abolished PDT activity in both AtADT/PDTs. When tested with the ACAI assay, this substitution also abolished the ADT activity of AtADT1, but did not affect the ADT activity of AtADT2 (Fig. 8). The reverse S337A substitution in AtADT3 and AtADT6 did not confer PDT activity, and also resulted in a loss of ADT activity in these AtADTs (Fig. 8). The ADT activity of AtADT5 was unaffected by the S337A substitution (Fig. 8).

In contrast, enzymes with a substitution at position 341 generally retained ADT activity. AtADT2 L341F, AtADT3 F341L, AtADT4 F341L, and AtADT6 F341L all retained ADT activity. Of note, the F341L substitution in AtADT4 grew more slowly, which could be explained by having a reduced affinity for arogenate, though only future binding affinity experiments will clarify this. AtADT1 L341F and AtADT5 F341L lost ADT activity (Fig. 8). In the case of AtADT5 F341L, this likely results from reduced expression or reduced protein stability of the protein in yeast (Additional file 3: Fig. S2B). While the ADT activity of AtADT5 F341T protein was unaffected, the AtADT5 F341W protein did not grow in the absence of Phe, indicating the F341W substitution abolished the ADT activity of the AtADT5 enzyme. While there is no particular trend, our results indicate that all three positions are involved in enzymatic activity, strongly suggesting that the sequence composition of the N-terminal ACT domain as a whole contributes to substrate specificity.

Discussion

Three amino acids confer PDT activity to AtADTs in pha2 yeast

We have shown that PDT activity can be introduced to the strict AtADTs with a single F341L substitution. Substitutions at position 335 introduced activity to AtADT3 and AtADT5, but not to AtADT4 and AtADT6. Further, we demonstrated that multiple substitutions (A335V and A337S) to the dual ADT/PDTs abolished prephenate substrate recognition. In short, we observed that it is easier to remove activity than it is to introduce it. All critical amino acids are located within the PDT activity-conferring (PAC) domain previously described by El-Azaz and colleagues [21]. The PAC domain is a 22-amino acid region in the N-terminal ACT domain that is present in all plant ADT enzymes belonging to subgroups I and II (Fig. 1B) and many amino acids in this region are conserved. In parallel with our study, El-Azaz and group identified the position equivalent to AtADT Ala335 as a key residue specifying PDT activity of Pinus pinaster ADTs. In contrast to our results, they demonstrated that mutations to this key PDT defining residue did not affect the ADT activity of the PpADTs. Though we identified L341 as another key residue defining PDT activity within the PAC domain for the Arabidopsis ADTs, this residue did not confer PDT activity to the PpADTs. Petunia × hybrida ADT2 and ADT3 were shown to have PDT activity in vitro [12], but only PhADT2 contains the key residues at both positions 335 (Ala) and 341 (Met, not a Phe). Plant ADTs are generally well conserved, but different amino acids within the PAC domain appear to define PDT activity in different plant ADT families, and the role of this domain needs further exploration. The effect of amino acid substitutions within the PAC domain on the structure and active site of ADTs is still unclear and will benefit from the structural analysis of an ADT with arogenate and/or prephenate present in the active site.

An important point to consider is how the substitutions introduced to the AtADTs may affect the feedback sensitivity of the enzymes. The AtADTs, like many characterized ADTs and PDTs, are feedback inhibited by Phe binding in the ACT domain [37, 41]. The residues identified here are all located in the ACT domain, specifically the PAC domain, and have been shown to affect the feedback sensitivity of PpADTs and other plant ADTs by El-Azaz and colleagues [72]. Per their analysis, AtADT1 and AtADT2 are type I enzymes which are strictly regulated by Phe allosteric regulation, while AtADT3-AtADT6 were largely insensitive to Phe, and this regulation is determined by the PAC domain found in the type I enzymes. The mutations we introduced into the AtADTs are all within the PAC domain and are likely affecting the allosteric regulation of the enzymes. However, it is not possible to investigate this possibility using the complementation assays described above. Rather, both of our complementation assays (pha2 and ACAI) can determine basic enzymatic activities but not their rates or efficacies. False positives due to altered allosteric regulation are unlikely, though false negatives are possible, specifically with mutant type II enzymes (AtADT3-AtADT6), if the introduced ADT or PDT activity was high enough to significantly alter cellular Phe concentrations. Even though we did observe that yeast transformed with various AtADTs grew at different rates, we can only speculate the difference is due to changes in allosteric regulation or substrate affinity. This is the main limitation of our complementation methodology: it is qualitative in nature. While we can quickly assess whether an enzyme has ADT or PDT activity, we cannot determine the affinity for the substrate(s), or the catalytic efficiency of the enzymes. However, our methodology can be useful to quickly triage large numbers of putative ADT/PDT enzymes for further biochemical characterization. Thus, our work is a prerequisite for future kinetic and biochemical analysis of the AtADT mutants presented here should be done in future studies to fully characterize the ADT/PDT activity, as well as sensitivity to Phe regulation.

Single amino acids defining substrate preference has been demonstrated before. For example, a single amino acid in legume prephenate dehydrogenase (PDH) enzymes have also been shown to define arogenate dehydrogenase (ADH) vs PDH activity [51]. This presents an interesting parallel to Phe and Tyr biosynthesis. Both Phe and Tyr can be synthesized via either an arogenate or prephenate route (Fig. 1A), using the action of either an ADT/PDT or an ADH/PDH [11, 51]. PDH1 was shown to exclusively use prephenate as a substrate and was insensitive to Tyr inhibition. However, a Asn222Asp substitution conferred ADH activity and Tyr sensitivity to GmPDH1 [51]. Further, they observed that multiple residues at position 222 confer PDH activity, while all ADHs have an aspartate conserved at that position. Importantly, all this agrees with our finding that PDT activity of AtADT is defined by the amino acid in position 341. At position 341, it is the lack of a Phe that defines PDT activity, while multiple other amino acids we tested (Leu, Thr, Trp) confer PDT activity.

In the shikimate pathway, shikimate dehydrogenase (SDH) and quinate dehydrogenase (QDH) enzymes are structurally similar, but prefer shikimate or quinate, respectively [11, 73]. A study by Gritsunov and group [58] defined four key residues specifying the difference between a SDH and QDH, critically a T381G substitution in SDH that introduced quinate substrate recognition. They also determined that a small side chain at this position is important to create sufficient space to accommodate quinate at the active site [58].

Substitutions outside of the active site can also have an impact on enzyme catalysis and substrate preference. In another study of the Spodoptera frugiperda β-glycosidase, random mutagenesis was employed to identify amino acids anywhere in the protein sequence that affect substrate specificity [54]. Ten single amino acids were identified, and eight of them were located outside of the active site. In bacterial aspartate aminotransferases, a cysteine (C191) outside of the active site is highly conserved, suggesting a functional role for this amino acid [53]. An analysis of C191 in the E. coli aspartate aminotransferase determined that while it is not directly involved in substrate binding, C191 is important for the binding of the pyridoxal phosphate (PLP) co-factor [53].

In agreement with other works, we have shown that three amino acids outside of the catalytic domain affect the ADT/PDT activity of the AtADTs when tested in S. cerevisiae pha2 and ACAI strains. Specifically, position 341 confers PDT activity without affecting the ADT activity of the enzyme. These findings demonstrate that the overall 3D structure rather than the amino acid sequence alone is crucial for substrate specificity. Our results contribute to the growing body of literature detailing the role of residues outside of the active site in substrate preference and enzyme activity.

The possible role of PDTs in planta

Although plants predominantly use the arogenate pathway to synthesize Phe, dual ADT/PDT enzymes have been identified within many plant ADT families [12, 20, 21, 29, 74]. However, until recently it was unknown if there was an active prephenate pathway in planta. Qian and colleagues [45] identified a cytosolic route of Phe biosynthesis in Petunia × hybrida and Arabidopsis that begins with a cytosolic isoform of chorismate mutase (CM). Further, an alternative transcriptional start site of PhADT3 produces a cytosolic localized isoform, and this isoform has PDT activity [45]. They also noted that there are alternate in-frame start codons within the AtADT1, AtADT2, and AtADT6 sequences. In Arabidopsis, AtADT6 had weak PDT activity measured in vitro, but this isoform does localize to the cytosol. AtADT1 and AtADT2 have not been observed in the cytosol to date, but use of the alternate transcriptional start site may be tightly regulated. The use of the arogenate or prephenate pathways may also be development or tissue specific, as the AtADT isoforms are differentially expressed [5, 75, 76].

The ACAI assay as a tool to determine ADT activity

The ACAI system we established here is the first in vivo system to test for ADT activity independently of PDT activity to our knowledge. Other complementation assays to test ADT activity have been used, but in a pha2 background [21]. Expressing AtPPA-AT in a pha2 background cannot identify ADT/PDT enzymes, as both ADT and PDT activity would complement the strain. As we have shown here, the ACAI assay can easily be used in conjunction with the previously designed pha2 PDT assay [61] to identify strict ADTs and dual ADT/PDTs. The ACAI assay is not quantitative, but it is a cheaper, faster, and simpler method than in vitro biochemical assays. In addition, the ACAI assay does not require large quantity of the purified enzyme to be tested, a significant drawback of in vitro enzymatic characterization [77]. Our assay also does not require supplementation of arogenate. Arogenate is a difficult substrate to use in in vitro enzymatic assays as it cannot be purchased commercially and must be synthesized in house, or extracted in sufficient quantity from an arogenate producing organism [78]. As we have demonstrated, the ACAI assay is a viable and efficient test of ADT activity. When used in combination with the pha2 complementation assay, many mutant ADTs of interest can be efficiently screened and triaged for follow-up biochemical characterization.

Predicting protein function

The gap between deposited sequences and deposited structures based on physical data is continuously widening as more genomes are sequenced [79]. We often rely on prediction programs to annotate gene or protein functions, but it is still difficult to predict protein structure and function based on sequence alone. Functionally important amino acids have been identified in different ways: by analyzing the stability of the structure [80] or by comparing amino acid properties and recurring structural motifs [81], to name a few. In addition, enzymes appear to be particularly sensitive to small sequence changes [46, 48, 51]. While the efficiency of these in silico studies has improved with the development of better software to predict a protein’s structure, wet lab experiments are still necessary to not only confirm the predictions of these programs, but to determine the effects of minute sequence changes on enzyme activity and structure. ADT structure determination, as well as the characterization of the effect of combinatorial mutations in the PAC domain in more plant species, will elucidate the role of this domain in enzymatic activity.

Conclusion

We have developed the ACAI complementation assay that can detect ADT activity in S. cerevisiae. The ACAI assay used in combination with the pha2 complementation assay can distinguish between strict ADTs, strict PDTs, and dual ADT/PDT enzymes. We identified three amino acids outside of the catalytic domain that are involved in ADT and PDT activity. Our work here demonstrates the importance of functional data to support in silico analyses to train computer programs in how subtle difference in sequence contributes to the function of proteins.

Methods

Strains and plasmids

E. coli and yeast expression strains

Escherichia coli strain DH5α (Invitrogen, 11319019) was used for the cloning and transformations of plasmid DNA. All E. coli cultures were grown at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm in LB media supplemented with appropriate antibiotic selection. The haploid pha2 knockout S. cerevisiae strain (Δpha2: MATα, his3Δ1, leu2Δ0, lys2Δ0, ura3Δ0, YNL316c::kanMX, Dharmacon, YSC6272-201,922,119) was used for the PDT assays. For the development of the ACAI assay, Δaro8 haploid yeast (MATα his3Δ1, leu2Δ0, lys2Δ0, ura3Δ0, YHR137W::kanMX, Dharmacon, YSC6272-201,920,671) and Δaro9 haploid yeast (Mata his3Δ1, leu2Δ0, met15Δ0, ura3Δ0, YHR137W::kanMX, Dharmacon, YSC6273-201,923,251) were used. All S. cerevisiae strains were grown at 30°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Cultures of untransformed Δpha2 and Δaro8aro9 were grown in YPDA media. All other yeast cultures were grown in SD appropriately supplemented media.

Genotyping of Δaro8aro9 double knockout

To confirm the aro8aro9 double knockout by PCR, we used three sets of genotyping primers (Fig. 6B): (1) a forward and reverse pair flanking the ARO8 locus, (2) a forward and reverse pair flanking the ARO9 locus, and (3) two reverse primers within the KanMX cassette that replaced the coding sequences of both ARO8 and ARO9 [70, 82] that can be used in combination with gene-specific primers. As ARO8 and ARO9 coding sequences are close in size (1503 bp and 1549 bp, respectively), the ARO8 forward primer was designed just outside of the coding sequence and the ARO9 forward primer approximately 400 bp upstream of the translational start site (TrSS) to allow distinguishing the two amplicons. Only strains with a successful replacement of either ARO8 or ARO9 with the KanMX knockout cassette will produce a PCR product when a locus-specific forward primer is used with either of the KanMX REV primers.

Cloning of AtPPA-AT and AtADTs

The full-length AtADT and AtPPA-AT genes were PCR amplified from previously cloned plasmid templates that carried sequenced confirmed cDNA sequences compared to those documented in TAIR (AtADT1: AT1G11790; AtADT2: AT3G07630; AtADT3: AT3G27820; AtADT4: AT3G44720; AtADT5: AT5G22630; AtADT6: AT1G08520; AtPPA-AT1: AT2G22250). Amplified sequences were recombined into the pDONR™221 vector (Invitrogen, 12,536–017), using Gateway® cloning [83, 84]. This vector contains the kanamycin resistance gene KanR for selection in E. coli. AtADT sequences were then recombined via Gateway® cloning into the pAG423GAL-ccdB-ECFP yeast expression vector [85] (Addgene, 14,149), containing the ampicillin resistance gene AmpR for selection in E. coli, and the HIS3 marker for selection in S. cerevisiae (Fig. S4). All AtADT genes are expressed as ECFP fusion proteins for detection via Western blot. AtPPA-AT was recombined into the pAG425Gal-ccdB-HA yeast expression vector (Addgene, 14,153) and expressed as a 3xHA fusion protein. This vector contains the ampicillin resistance gene AmpR for selection in E. coli, as well as the LEU2 marker for selection in S. cerevisiae.

Overlap extension PCR was used to generate targeted mutations in the AtADT sequence [86, 87]. These sequences with site-specific mutations were then cloned as described using Gateway® cloning. Sequence integrity and desired mutations were confirmed by DNA sequencing. A schematic and sequences of AtADT-ECFP fusion proteins can be found in Additional file 5: Fig. S4 and Additional file 6: Table S2.

Transformation procedures

DH5α E. coli cells were made chemically competent through a Rubidium Chloride Competent Cell procedure [88, 89] and plasmid DNA was transformed into the competent E. coli cells using the One Shot® TOP10 Chemically Competent E. coli transformation procedure (Invitrogen, C4040-03), or the heat shock method [88, 90]. Escherichia coli transformants were selected on appropriately supplemented LB media while incubating overnight at 37°C. Saccharomyces cerevisiae Δpha2 and Δaro8aro9 cells were made competent and transformed using the lithium acetate/single-stranded carrier DNA/polyethylene glycol method [91], or using the GBiosciences Fast Transformation Kit (GBiosciences, GZ-1). Yeast transformants were selected on appropriately supplemented SD media while incubating at 30°C for 2 days.

AtADT detection by Western blot analysis

To isolate total protein, two different methods were used. 1: Yeast cultures were grown in appropriately supplemented raffinose liquid media [92] for 24 h, then were harvested by centrifugation in mid-late logarithmic growth phase (~ 7 × 106–7 × 107). Cells were then grown in appropriately supplemented galactose liquid media [92] to induce expression of FL AtADTs or chimeric AtADTs. Cultures were incubated for approximately 24 h and cells were harvested at a cell density of 5 × 106 cells/mL. Total soluble protein (TSP) was quantified via BCA assay [93] (Pierce Cat No. 23227) and 15 μg of TSP from each sample was size separated on a 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Invitrogen, LC2000). 2: Yeast cultures were grown overnight in glucose liquid media and harvested by centrifugation. Cells were washed 3 × with water, and then grown in galactose liquid media to mid-late logarithmic phase (OD600 of 0.6–0.8). Total protein was extracted using the alkaline lysis method [94]. Equal volumes of total protein from each sample were size separated on an SDS-PAGE gel (6% stacking, 12% separating) and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Invitrogen, LC2000). Total protein was also visualized with Coomassie Brilliant Blue in parallel to ensure protein was successfully extracted from each yeast culture.

In both methods, the membrane was probed with primary monoclonal mouse anti-GFP antibody (1:1200 or 1:5000 dilution of Clontech, JL-8 632,380), followed by a goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L)-horseradish peroxidase conjugated antibody (1:3000 or 1:10,000 dilution of Bio-Rad, 170–6516). To visualize expressed AtPPA-AT, a primary monoclonal mouse anti-HA antibody (Sigma Aldrich B9183) was used, followed by the same goat anti-mouse IgG-HRP conjugated secondary antibody. An enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (GE Healthcare, RPN2109) was used for visualization. Western blots were exposed for 1 min and imaged using X-ray film, or visualized using the Clarity ECL kit (Bio-Rad, 1,705,061).

Yeast complementation assays

For both the PDT and ACAI complementation assays, yeast cultures of either pha2 or aro8aro9 were initially grown in appropriately supplemented liquid raffinose media (lacking His, Leu, or His/Leu) overnight using the standard growth conditions described above. Cells were collected by centrifugation and diluted in water to a final density of 1000 cells/μL. For both assays, SD solid media were prepared with different carbon sources (glucose or galactose), as all sequences are under the control of the GAL1 promoter. For the pha2 assay, plates of each carbon source were made either lacking His or His/Phe. The ability of AtADTs to act as PDTs was tested by growing transformants on solid media lacking Phe. For the ACAI assay, plates of each carbon source were made lacking either His, Leu, or His/Leu/Phe. Similarly, the ability of AtADTs to act as ADTs was tested by growing transformants on solid media lacking Phe. Images were taken 6 days after spotting for both assays, and either 10 or 13 days after spotting for the ACAI and PDT assays, respectively. A single representative image of each construct is shown.

Sequence analysis

Sequence annotation and alignments were performed using three programs: DNAMAN (Lynnon Biosoft), Benchling (2021), and MEGAX [34]. For the alignments, the transit peptide sequences were removed to only compare sequences representing the enzymatic protein of the ADTs. The beginning of the catalytic domain was defined by the RVAY motif as in many bacterial sequences this motif is only preceded by a start codon, and we have tested these sequences to ensure that they are catalytically active [61]. For the phylogenetic analysis, only plant species with fully sequenced genomes were chosen to ensure that all ADT sequences were included. DNAMAN was used to analyze primer sequences for primer dimers, annealing temperatures, and secondary structure. Sequencing results of entry clones were also aligned to a template using either the DNAMAN sequence assembly function or the Benchling multiple alignment function. MEGAX was used to generate alignments and phylogenetic trees. Sequences of interest used in alignments were obtained from TAIR [95], Phytozome [96] and GenBank [97].

Homology modeling

To model protein structures of AtADTs, predicted WT AtADT structures were obtained from AlphaFold [62] and trimmed to remove the transit peptide sequences. Structures were viewed and analyzed in UCSF Chimera X [98] using the Matchmaker function, or PyMOL [99] to superimpose structures using default settings.
