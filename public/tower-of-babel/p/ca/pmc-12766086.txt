Hypoxia-responsive Exatecan prodrug liposomes co-delivered with IR808 for synergistic chemo–photodynamic therapy of breast cancer
Authors: Li Wang, Hongjie Huo, Chunyun Xu, Yuan Liang, Ning Yang, Qingxuan Han, Zhuang Wang, Hongyang Zhang, Ning Wang, Lei Li, Lixue Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo overcome the poor solubility, rapid clearance, and systemic toxicity of Exatecan, we developed a tumor-activated liposomal delivery system co-encapsulating a hypoxia-cleavable Exatecan–squalene prodrug (EXA PRO) and the photosensitizer IR808. The nanocarrier integrates photodynamic therapy-induced hypoxia with bioreductive drug activation, enabling spatiotemporally controlled release within the tumor microenvironment. The liposomes, formulated with pH-sensitive lipids, exhibited uniform size (∼136 nm), high encapsulation efficiency, and dual responsiveness to acidic pH and reductive stress. Upon near-infrared laser irradiation, IR808 generated reactive oxygen species and aggravated intratumoral hypoxia, thereby triggering the cleavage of the azobenzene linker and facilitating targeted release of Exatecan. In vitro and in vivo studies confirmed enhanced cellular uptake, efficient intracellular drug release, potent tumor growth inhibition, and favorable biosafety profiles. This synergistic chemo–photodynamic strategy offers a promising platform for precise, tumor-selective drug delivery and represents a potential advancement in personalized cancer therapy.Keywords: Photodynamic therapy, Hypoxia, Reactive oxygen species, Exatecan, IR808

Introduction

Exatecan, a highly potent topoisomerase I inhibitor, belongs to the camptothecin class of chemotherapeutic agents. By stabilizing DNA-topoisomerase I complexes, Exatecan induces DNA damage and ultimately triggers cancer cell death. It has demonstrated broad-spectrum anticancer activity across various solid tumors, including breast and gastric cancers (Shaun et al., 2023; Jo et al., 2024; Jo et al., 2022). Despite its promising therapeutic potential, the clinical application of Exatecan is severely limited by its poor aqueous solubility, rapid systemic clearance, and significant off-target toxicities (Cheng et al., 2021; Sabnis, 2024). Although derivatives such as deruxtecan have been successfully employed in advanced antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), traditional formulations of Exatecan continue to face challenges related to inadequate tumor selectivity and bioavailability (Cheng et al., 2021; Sawant et al., 2024). These limitations underscore the urgent need for innovative strategies to optimize its pharmacokinetic profile, enhance its therapeutic index, and minimize systemic toxicity.

Hypoxia is a defining characteristic of the tumor microenvironment, driven by the rapid proliferation of cancer cells outpacing the oxygen supply provided by disorganized and dysfunctional vasculature. This hypoxic condition stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs), which activate a network of adaptive pathways that promote tumor survival, angiogenesis, and metabolic reprogramming, while simultaneously contributing to resistance against conventional therapies. These adaptations enable cancer cells to thrive in oxygen-deficient environments, making hypoxia a formidable barrier to effective treatment but also a compelling therapeutic target (Kumari et al., 2020; Xia et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021).

To overcome the challenges associated with tumor hypoxia, hypoxia-sensitive drug delivery systems have been developed. These systems exploit the unique biochemical features of hypoxic tumor regions, such as elevated levels of reducing agents (e.g., glutathione) and specific bioreductive enzymes (e.g., nitroreductase and DT-diaphorase), to achieve targeted drug activation. For example, hypoxia-sensitive prodrugs incorporating nitroimidazole-based linkers have demonstrated selective release of therapeutic agents within hypoxic tumors, thereby minimizing off-target effects while enhancing tumor specificity. Moreover, hypoxia-responsive nanoparticles have been shown to further refine delivery precision, enabling controlled drug release in response to the hypoxic microenvironment. These advances address key limitations of conventional therapies, such as poor tumor penetration and systemic toxicity, and pave the way for innovative approaches in cancer treatment (Chen et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019).

Squalene, a natural triterpene and a precursor in sterol biosynthesis, has emerged as a versatile material in drug delivery due to its biocompatibility and capacity for chemical modification. Its covalent conjugation with therapeutic agents, known as squalenoylation, enhances the solubility and stability of hydrophobic drugs while improving their pharmacokinetics and reducing systemic toxicity. These attributes make squalene an appealing choice for addressing the formulation challenges of chemotherapeutic agents (Mura et al., 2019; Allain et al., 2011; Lirussi et al., 2023).

In this study, we engineered a hypoxia-sensitive prodrug by conjugating Exatecan with squalene via a hypoxia-cleavable linker. To achieve a synergistic therapeutic effect, the prodrug was co-encapsulated with the photosensitizer IR808 into a liposomal formulation (Chen et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022; Chen et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2022; Jia et al., 2021). Upon intravenous administration, laser irradiation at the tumor site activates IR808, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and consuming oxygen. This process induces a hypoxic environment, triggering the cleavage of the hypoxia-sensitive linker and enabling the selective release of Exatecan at the tumor site. This dual-modality approach integrates photodynamic therapy (PDT) with chemotherapy, enhancing antitumor efficacy while mitigating the systemic toxicity associated with Exatecan. By combining the advantages of hypoxia-responsive prodrug design and liposomal delivery, this study presents a novel platform for tumor-targeted therapy with improved safety and efficacy (Fig. 1).

Preparation and optimization of drug-loaded liposomes

IR808 and EXA-PRO pH-sensitive liposomes (IR808/EP PSLs) were prepared by thin film hydration method. 0.13 mg IR808, 1 mg EXA-PRO, 7.5 mg lecithin, 7.5 mg DSPE-PEOZ2K, and 3 mg cholesterol (w:w) were accurately weighed and dissolved in 2 mL chloroform, then vortexed thoroughly to mix. In water bath, chloroform was removed in vacuum to form a uniform film on the wall of the round-bottom flask at 55 rpm and 37 °C. Furthermore, residual organic solvents in the flask was evaporated in a vacuum desiccator for 4 h. Addition of 2 mL PBS, the formed suspension was shaken for hydration for 1 h at 60 °C and 100 rpm, then probe-ultrasonicated for 10 min, centrifuged at 4500 rpm for 5 min to remove unencapsulated drugs. Finally, a light yellow milky IR808/EP PSLs solution was obtained with EXA-PRO concentration of 0.5 mg/mL. The preparation method of IR808 and EXA-PRO pH-insensitive liposomes (IR808/EP PNSLs) is the same as that of IR808/EP PSLs, except that DSPE-PEOz2K is replaced with DSPE-PEG2K, yielding IR808/EP PNSLs. The pH-sensitive liposome formulation IR808 and EXA in pH-sensitive liposome (IR808/E PSLs) was prepared by co-loading IR808 and EXA into DSPE-PEOz-based liposomes, whereas IR808 and EXA in pH-nosensitive liposomes (IR808/E PNSLs) was obtained by co-encapsulating IR808 and EXA in DSPE-PEG-based liposomes. C6 in pH-sensitive liposomes (C6 PSLs) and C6 in pH-nosensitive liposomes (C6 PNSLs) were prepared using DSPE-PEOz and DSPE-PEG, respectively.

In vivo antitumor efficacy and safety evaluation

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (2020). Female BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks old, weighing 18–22 g) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. Mice were housed under standard specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a 12 h light/dark cycle, temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity of 50–60 %, with free access to food and water.

Study design and ethics approval

Animals were randomly assigned into different experimental groups (n = 6–8 per group) using a random number table to ensure allocation concealment. All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Dalian Medical University (Grant No. AEE 22103).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Only healthy animals without signs of illness or abnormal behavior were included. No animals were excluded from the analysis.

Blinding

Investigators were blinded to the treatment allocation during tumor volume measurement and data analysis to minimize subjective bias.

Sample size justification

The sample size was determined based on previous similar studies and preliminary data to achieve sufficient statistical power to detect significant differences between groups.

Endpoints and humane considerations

Mice were monitored daily for signs of distress. Humane endpoints included tumor volume exceeding 1500 mm3, significant weight loss (>20 %), or severe ulceration, at which point animals were euthanized immediately by CO₂ asphyxiation followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death.

Experimental procedures

Tumor models were established by subcutaneous injection of 4 T1 cells (1 × 106 cells in 100 μL PBS) into the right flank of each mouse. When tumor volumes reached 80–100 mm3, tumor-bearing mice were randomly allocated into six experimental groups (n = 6–8 per group): (1) saline control, (2) free EXA, (3) EXA PRO, (4) IR808/EP PSLs, (5) IR808/EP PNSLs, and (6) IR808/EP PSLs (+) with laser irradiation. All groups received intravenous tail vein injections (200 μL) every other day for five total administrations. The laser-treated group underwent 808 nm irradiation (50 mW/cm2, 5 min) at tumor sites 18 h post-injection. Animals were euthanized 48 h after the final dose for comprehensive analysis of tumor progression and organ toxicity through histopathological examination.

The results of statistical analysis were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using one or two-way ANOVA. p-value <0.05 was considered significant and p-value <0.01 was considered highly significant.

Result and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of the hypoxia-responsive Exatecan prodrug

To address the poor solubility and systemic toxicity associated with free Exatecan (EXA), a hypoxia-responsive prodrug (EXA PRO) was synthesized by covalently conjugating EXA with squalene (SQ) through an azobenzene-based hypoxia-cleavable linker. The synthetic route is illustrated in Scheme 1. Briefly, the hydroxyl group of intermediate compound 2 was protected with TBDMSCl, followed by esterification with carboxylated squalene under EDCI/DMAP catalysis to yield compound 5. After deprotection of the silyl group using TBAF, the resulting intermediate was reacted with acyl chloride to generate key intermediate 7. Finally, 7 was conjugated with Exatecan in the presence of TEA to obtain the final prodrug, which was collected by precipitation and purified by ethanol washing and vacuum drying.

Synthetic route of the hypoxia-responsive Exatecan prodrug (EXA-PRO).

The chemical structure of the final product was confirmed by high resolution mass spectrometry (HR MS). A prominent peak at m/z [M + H]+ = 1071.5776 was observed (Fig. S1), consistent with the expected molecular weight of the EXA-SQ prodrug (calculated [M + H]+ = 1071.5754), indicating successful synthesis and desired molecular structure. The structures of the targets were determined by proton and carbon nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR and 13C NMR) (Fig. S2).

Preparation and optimization of dual-drug liposomes

To co-deliver the hypoxia-responsive prodrug (EXA-PRO) and the photosensitizer IR808, a pH-sensitive liposomal formulation (IR808/EP PSLs) was developed using the thin-film hydration method. The liposomes were composed of phosphatidylcholine, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEOz₂k in a molar ratio optimized for size and drug loading. The inclusion of DSPE-PEOz₂k imparted pH sensitivity to the formulation, enabling triggered release in the acidic tumor microenvironment (Abri Aghdam et al., 2019).

A series of formulations with varying drug-to-lipid ratios (EXA PRO: phospholipid: cholesterol = 1:5:1, 1:10:2, 1:15:3, and 1:20:4 w/w/w) were prepared and evaluated. The formulation with a 1:15:3 ratio exhibited optimal physicochemical characteristics, including a mean particle size of 135.8 ± 2.87 nm, a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.23 ± 0.01, and a zeta potential of −14.18 ± 1.64 mV (Fig. 2A, B). This formulation also achieved a drug loading (DL%) of 3.68 ± 0.20 % and an encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of 79.84 ± 3.83 % (Table S1), and was thus selected for subsequent experiments.

Further process optimization was conducted using a Box–Behnken design to evaluate the effects of incubation temperature, hydration time, and probe sonication time on particle size and encapsulation efficiency. The optimal conditions were determined to be an incubation temperature of 55 °C, hydration time of 1.8 h, and probe sonication for 12 min. Response surface plots and contour maps revealed significant interactions among the three factors (Fig. S3), confirming the validity of the mathematical model for predicting liposomal quality attributes.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the optimized IR808/EP PSLs possessed a spherical morphology with uniform distribution and good dispersion, consistent with the DLS measurements (Fig. 2C). As shown in Fig. 2D, the as-prepared IR808/EP PSLs and IR808/EP PNSLs had broad light absorbed ability in the range of 250–800 nm. Compared to the UV–vis absorption spectra of DSPE-PEOz, lecithin, and cholesterol, the IR808/EP PSLs exhibited a distinct characteristic peak at 808 nm, which matched that of free IR808. Additionally, peaks observed at 368 nm and 262 nm in the IR808/EP PSLs spectra corresponded well with those of EXO-PRO, further confirming the successful preparation of IR808/EP PSLs. A similar UV absorption profile was also detected in IR808/EP PNSLs. These results indicated that the drugs were successfully encapsulated within the liposomes. Forthermore, the liposomes maintained excellent colloidal stability in PBS containing 10 % FBS at 37 °C for 24 h, and also demonstrated good storage stability at 4 °C for up to 7 days (Fig. 2E, S4). These properties ensure suitability for intravenous administration and further biological evaluation.

In vitro release and stimuli-responsive behavior

To investigate the stimuli-responsive release behavior of the dual-drug liposomal formulation, in vitro drug release studies were performed under different environmental conditions mimicking the tumor microenvironment. As shown in Fig. S5A and S5B, IR808/EP PNSLs maintained a particle size of approximately 170 nm and a PDI around 0.2 in both pH 5.0 and pH 7.4 environments, with no significant changes over time. In contrast, while IR808/EP PSLs showed no notable variations in particle size or PDI after 12 h of incubation at pH 7.4, their particle size increased from 170 nm to 400 nm after 8 h at pH 5.0, with further increases in both size and PDI upon prolonged incubation. These results demonstrated that IR808/EP PNSLs remained stable under different pH conditions, whereas IR808/EP PSLs exhibit pH sensitivity, likely due to acid-triggered structural changes and liposome aggregation, which may lead to burst release in weakly acidic environments.

The release profiles of EXA from IR808/EP PSLs and control IR808/EP PNSLs were further evaluated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.4 and pH 5.0, as well as under reducing conditions using sodium dithionite (Na₂S₂O₄) as a chemical reductant. As shown in Fig. 2F, both liposomal formulations exhibited relatively low EXA release (∼30 %) over 24 h in pH 7.4 PBS, indicating good colloidal stability under physiological conditions. However, at pH 5.0, the IR808/E PSLs demonstrated a sharp burst release, with cumulative release reaching ∼60 % within 4 h. In contrast, the IR808/E PNSLs showed no significant change in release profile at either pH condition. These findings suggested that the DSPE-PEOz-based PSLs are pH-sensitive and prone to destabilization in acidic environments, thereby promoting rapid drug release.

To further validate the reduction-sensitive properties of IR808/EP PSLs, the drug release profile was evaluated in PBS containing 1 % SDS with different concentration of sodium dithionite as a reducing agent. As illustrated in Fig. 2G, drug release increased significantly with rising sodium dithionite concentrations. While EXA release was limited to <20 % over 24 h under normoxic conditions (0 mM sodium dithionite), it reached approximately 70 % at 40 mM, confirming that the azobenzene linker is cleaved efficiently under reducing/hypoxic environments. These findings clearly demonstrated the redox-responsive characteristics of the liposomal system and its capacity for selective EXA release under hypoxic conditions mimicking the tumor microenvironment. Collectively, the results establish that IR808/EP PSLs exhibit dual stimulus-responsive behavior, with sensitivity to both acidic pH and reductive conditions. This sophisticated design enables targeted drug release specifically at the tumor site while formulation stability is maintained under physiological conditions. Such spatiotemporal control of drug delivery offers significant potential for improving therapeutic precision, while off-target effects associated with premature systemic drug release are reduced.

Cellular uptake and intracellular release

To evaluate cellular uptake efficiency, 4 T1 and MCF-7 tumor cells were incubated with free coumarin-6 (C6), C6-loaded pH-sensitive liposomes (C6 PSLs), and pH-nonsensitive liposomes (C6 PNSLs). As shown in Fig. 3A–D, both C6 PSLs and C6 PNSLs exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity than free C6, indicating enhanced cellular uptake via nanoparticle-mediated delivery. Notably, 4 T1 cells exhibited higher overall fluorescence signals than MCF-7 cells, suggesting stronger endocytosis capability in 4 T1 cells. However, no significant difference was observed between PSLs and PNSLs, implying that pH sensitivity does not affect initial uptake efficiency. To further investigate whether the internalization of this lipid-based drug delivery system in cells was affected by hypoxic conditions, we examined the cellular uptake of C6 and C6-PSLs under both normoxic and hypoxic conditions (Fig. S10). The results demonstrated that the uptake efficiency of C6 PSLs remained largely unchanged under different oxygen levels, suggesting that hypoxia does not significantly influence the internalization of PSLs.

Intracellular release of Exatecan (EXA) from different formulations was further quantified using LC-MS/MS. Compared to EXA PRO and PNSLs, the IR808/EP PSLs under laser irradiation and hypoxic conditions [IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo)] achieved the highest intracellular drug concentration in both MCF-7 and 4 T1 cells. This enhanced release is attributed to the dual action of IR808-mediated oxygen depletion and subsequent cleavage of the hypoxia-sensitive linker. In contrast, minimal release was observed in normal HBE cells, indicating good tumor selectivity of the system (Fig. 3E–G).

To investigate the photodynamic behavior of IR808, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were detected using DCFH-DA staining. As shown in Fig. 3H, both IR808 and IR808/EP PSLs induced significant ROS production in 4 T1 cells upon 808 nm laser irradiation, with the PSL group exhibiting stronger signals due to improved cellular accumulation. In MCF-7 cells (Fig. 3I), the ROS increase was less pronounced, consistent with their lower uptake capacity.

Hypoxia probe staining further confirmed that laser-irradiated IR808/EP PSLs significantly exacerbated intracellular hypoxia levels in 4 T1 cells and MCF-7cells (Fig. 3J, K), supporting the proposed mechanism of oxygen depletion-triggered prodrug activation. In summary, the above experimental results demonstrate that upon light irradiation, IR808 consumes oxygen and exacerbates the hypoxic environment, leading to cleavage of the hypoxia-sensitive linker in the prodrug and subsequent release of a substantial amount of the active parent drug for therapeutic effects. The IR808/EP PSL system exhibits enhanced cellular uptake and tumor-selective, laser-amplified drug release via hypoxia activation.

In vitro cytotoxicity and mechanistic studies

The cytotoxicity of various formulations was evaluated using CCK-8 assays in 4 T1 and MCF-7 cells over 24 h and 48 h. As shown in Fig. 4A–B, free EXA exhibited the strongest cytotoxicity, followed closely by IR808/EP PSLs under combined laser and hypoxic conditions [IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo)], indicating successful activation and release of EXA from the prodrug in a tumor-mimicking environment. Notably, IR808/EP PSLs (−) and IR808/EP PNSLs (−) exhibited moderate cytotoxicity, while EXA PRO alone displayed the weakest effect, confirming the necessity of stimuli for effective activation. These results suggest that IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo) could achieve cytotoxicity comparable to free EXA, but with higher tumor selectivity and potentially lower systemic toxicity. It should be noted that the experimental results demonstrated that the enhanced cytotoxicity observed in hypoxic conditions is attributable to efficient hypoxia-triggered prodrug activation and intracellular Exatecan release rather than differences in nanoparticle uptake.

To visually validate cell death, Calcein-AM/PI live/dead staining was performed (Fig. 4C–F). Cells treated with IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo) exhibited extensive red fluorescence, comparable to the EXA group, indicating substantial apoptotic/necrotic cell death. Quantitative analysis further confirmed that IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo) achieved significantly higher cytotoxicity than other formulations, particularly under laser exposure. These findings reinforce the notion that the photodynamic effect of IR808 enhances tumor hypoxia, which in turn triggers accelerated EXA release from the prodrug.

To further investigate the tumor-specific activation of EXA, we evaluated DNA damage induced by Exatecan activation using γ-H2AX staining, a well-established and highly sensitive marker for DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). As presented in Fig. S11 of the revised Supplementary Information, we assessed γ-H2AX expression in 4 T1 cells across different treatment groups. The results revealed that the IR808/EP PSLs under hypoxic conditions with laser irradiation [IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo)] significantly increased γ-H2AX fluorescence intensity compared to other groups, including EXA PRO (p < 0.01). Importantly, the γ-H2AX signal in this group was comparable to that observed in cells directly treated with free EXA, confirming that under laser-triggered hypoxic conditions, efficient activation and release of Exatecan occurred from the prodrug, leading to substantial DNA damage. These findings provide robust evidence for the mechanism of tumor-selective prodrug activation and cytotoxicity, further validating the design rationale of our hypoxia-responsive system.

A scratch wound assay was also conducted to evaluate the anti-migratory effect of each formulation. As depicted in Fig. S6A—C, IR808/EP PSLs (+, hypo) markedly suppressed wound closure in both 4 T1 and MCF-7 cells, with inhibitory effects comparable to free EXA. These results indicate that the dual-sensitive liposomal system not only inhibits proliferation but also suppresses cancer cell migration, suggesting its potential for controlling tumor invasion and metastasis.

Although the IR808/EXA-PRO PSLs system demonstrated improved anticancer efficacy under hypoxic conditions, our cellular uptake studies indicated comparable internalization between PSLs and PNSLs. Therefore, the superior therapeutic outcome is primarily driven by hypoxia-triggered prodrug activation within tumor cells, highlighting the advantage of integrating microenvironment-responsive prodrug design with pH-sensitive liposomal delivery.

The in vitro data demonstrate that IR808/EP PSLs, particularly under laser and hypoxic conditions, effectively suppress tumor cell viability and migration via a synergistic mechanism involving photodynamic oxygen depletion and hypoxia-triggered prodrug activation.

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution in vivo

The pharmacokinetic behavior of EXA, EXA PRO, and IR808/EP PSLs was evaluated in SD rats following intravenous injection. Plasma concentrations of EXA were quantified using a validated LC-MS/MS method. As shown in Fig. S7 and Table 1, IR808/EP PSLs exhibited significantly improved pharmacokinetic parameters compared to both EXA and EXA PRO. Specifically, the area under the curve (AUC0–t) of IR808/EP PSLs was 1.98-fold higher than that of free EXA and 1.47-fold higher than EXA PRO. The mean residence time (MRT0–t) was prolonged, while the plasma clearance rate (CLz) was markedly reduced. The terminal half-life (t₁/₂) of IR808/EP PSLs reached 2.37 ± 0.28 h, representing a 6.68-fold extension over free EXA. These findings indicate that prodrug modification and liposomal encapsulation synergistically enhance systemic exposure and prolong circulation time.

In vivo antitumor efficacy and safety evaluation

To evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy of IR808/EP PSLs, a 4 T1 orthotopic breast cancer model was established in female BALB/c mice. Once tumors reached ∼80–100 mm3, mice were randomized into six groups: saline, free EXA, EXA PRO, IR808/EP PNSLs (−), IR808/EP PSLs (−), and IR808/EP PSLs (+). The latter group received laser irradiation (808 nm, 50 mW/cm2, 5 min) 18 h post-injection to activate the photodynamic component. All groups were treated via intravenous injection every 2 days for a total of five doses (Fig. 6A).

As shown in Fig. 6B–D, saline-treated mice exhibited rapid tumor growth, with tumor volume increasing by ∼11-fold over 10 days. Mice treated with free EXA or EXA PRO showed moderate tumor inhibition, while those treated with IR808/EP PNSLs (−) showed stronger suppression. Notably, IR808/EP PSLs (−) significantly reduced tumor growth compared to the nonsensitive formulation, highlighting the contribution of the pH-responsive carrier. The greatest tumor suppression was observed in the IR808/EP PSLs (+) group, with tumor inhibition rates exceeding 70 %. These results suggest that photodynamic activation further amplifies the antitumor effects of the prodrug liposomes, likely through enhanced hypoxia-triggered drug release.

In terms of safety, body weight changes, liver/kidney function indices, and histopathological evaluations were performed. As shown in Fig. 6E, only the free EXA group exhibited significant weight loss during treatment, while other groups maintained stable body weight, indicating reduced systemic toxicity. Serum biochemical analysis (Fig. 6F) revealed elevated ALT and AST levels in the EXA group, suggesting hepatotoxicity. In contrast, liver and kidney function remained within normal ranges in all liposomal groups. Histological examination of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, tumor) via H&E staining showed no overt tissue damage in any treatment group (Fig. S9), further confirming the biosafety of the liposomal formulations.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that IR808/EP PSLs exhibit potent antitumor activity in vivo while minimizing systemic toxicity. The combination of tumor-targeted accumulation, pH/hypoxia-responsive release, and photodynamic enhancement contributes to their superior therapeutic profile.

Recent studies have explored various strategies to enhance Exatecan delivery and therapeutic efficacy. For example, Cheng et al. developed a hypoxia-activated Exatecan prodrug strategy (Cheng et al., 2021), while Li et al. reported optimized liposomal formulations of Exatecan derivatives to improve pharmacokinetics and safety profiles (Jinbo Li et al., 2025). In contrast, our study uniquely integrates hypoxia-triggered Exatecan prodrug activation, photodynamic therapy-enhanced hypoxia, and pH-responsive liposomal release within a single nanoplatform, achieving a triple-synergistic effect. This design not only enhances tumor-selective drug release but also overcomes the limitations of single-modality therapies, resulting in superior antitumor efficacy and translational potential.

Conclusion

This study presents a rationally designed, stimuli-responsive liposomal system integrating a hypoxia-cleavable Exatecan prodrug and the photosensitizer IR808 for enhanced tumor-targeted chemotherapy–photodynamic therapy (chemo-PDT) combination. The proposed mechanism relies on a spatiotemporally controlled therapeutic cascade: (i) long-circulating liposomes preferentially accumulate in tumors via the EPR effect; (ii) the photosensitizer IR808 generates ROS upon near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation, consuming local oxygen and exacerbating intratumoral hypoxia; (iii) the resulting hypoxic microenvironment triggers cleavage of the azobenzene linker, releasing active Exatecan selectively at the tumor site.

Compared to traditional prodrugs or passive nanocarriers, this system addresses several key challenges in cancer therapy. First, the use of a bioreductive linker ensures that drug activation occurs only in hypoxic regions, minimizing off-target toxicity. Second, photodynamic pre-conditioning actively modulates the tumor microenvironment, converting weakly hypoxic or normoxic areas into responsive zones. This enhances prodrug activation and improves drug penetration. Third, the incorporation of a pH-sensitive lipid shell (DSPE-PEOz₂K) further facilitates intracellular drug release under endosomal acidic conditions, complementing the hypoxia-sensitive core (Kim et al., 2022).

The strategy offers multiple advantages: tumor-specific activation, minimal premature leakage, synergistic enhancement of therapeutic efficacy, and reduced systemic exposure. The in vitro and in vivo data clearly demonstrate that IR808/EP PSLs (+laser) produce superior tumor suppression without increasing systemic toxicity, representing a significant improvement over free Exatecan and traditional liposomes. Importantly, the dual-trigger design enables therapeutic control via external stimuli (laser) and endogenous tumor signals (hypoxia), laying a foundation for personalized, image-guided interventions.

In summary, we developed a dual-responsive liposomal delivery system co-encapsulating a hypoxia-cleavable Exatecan prodrug (EXA-SQ) and the photosensitizer IR808 to achieve spatiotemporally controlled chemo–photodynamic combination therapy. This platform leverages IR808-induced hypoxia to trigger selective prodrug activation, enabling precise tumor targeting and enhanced antitumor efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity. Both in vitro and in vivo results demonstrated efficient drug release, strong tumor inhibition, and good biocompatibility. This study highlights a promising strategy for tumor microenvironment-responsive drug delivery and provides a versatile platform for future personalized cancer therapy.
