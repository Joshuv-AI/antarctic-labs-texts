Development and validation of the intelligent digital learning student engagement influencing factor scale for medical students
Authors: Rong Hu, Xiao Zhang, Xinyao Zhang, Yixin Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate the Intelligent Digital Learning Student Engagement Influencing Factor Scale for nursing and other medical students.MethodsThis instrumental development study was conducted in two phases. Phase 1 involved scale development through theoretical modeling, synthesizing the Pedagogy-Social Interaction-Technology Model, the Model of Student Motivation and Self-Regulated Learning, and the Three-Dimensional Model of Student Engagement, followed by qualitative interviews with 25 medical students guided by this integrated framework, a two-round Delphi expert consultation with eight experts, and pilot testing with 30 nursing students. Phase 2 involved psychometric evaluation through a cross-sectional survey of 1,499 medical students, with nursing students constituting the largest subgroup (n = 673, 44.90 %), followed by clinical medicine (n = 616, 41.09 %), medical laboratory science (n = 132, 8.81 %), and preventive medicine (n = 78, 5.20 %). The sample was randomly divided into two subsamples for exploratory (n = 750) and confirmatory (n = 749) factor analyses. Construct validity, reliability, and item discrimination were assessed.ResultsThe final scale comprised 20 items across four factors: personal digital literacy, perceived school support, perceived technological environment, and perceived interpersonal interaction and support. These factors validated the integrated theoretical framework, reflecting learner-level psychological mechanisms, macro-level institutional support, and micro-level classroom factors encompassing technological and social interaction dimensions. Exploratory factor analysis explained 66.85 % of the total variance, with factor loadings ranging from 0.488 to 0.751. Confirmatory factor analysis demonstrated acceptable model fit (χ2/df = 4.587, CFI = 0.928, TLI = 0.968, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.054). Average variance extracted values ranged from 0.542 to 0.608, and composite reliability ranged from 0.846 to 0.890. The scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.920) and split-half reliability (0.918). Item discrimination analysis confirmed significant differences between the upper and lower 27 % of the sample (P < 0.01).ConclusionThis scale enables nurse educators to diagnose engagement deficits across institutional, technological, and interpersonal dimensions, guiding curricular improvements and providing a basis for precise interventions amid digital teaching transformation. Future studies should longitudinally examine its utility in nursing populations and its predictive validity for clinical performance.

Introduction

Rapid digital transformation has significantly reshaped medical education, influencing students’ acquisition of knowledge, development of clinical competencies, and engagement in academic activities [1]. In this evolving landscape, Intelligent Digital Learning (IDL), which integrates artificial intelligence (AI), adaptive systems, and intelligent tools, has emerged as a contemporary form of digital learning, distinct from generic digital learning or non-AI-driven smart environments [2,3]. Central to the success of such learning environments is student engagement, a key indicator of educational quality and a robust predictor of academic achievement and learning persistence [4,5]. Yet adapting nursing education to IDL is especially challenging, given the high-stakes nature of clinical and simulation training, students’ fragmented access to digital systems, and the enduring trade-off between procedural skill acquisition and deep clinical reasoning [6,7]. These intertwined challenges render fostering sustained student engagement in IDL a fundamental priority for nurse educators.

A growing body of research has explored these factors in the context of medical education. Studies have consistently shown that digital literacy, AI literacy, and self-efficacy are positively associated with learner engagement [[8], [9], [10]]. Beyond individual attributes, environmental factors also play a crucial role. School educational atmosphere and institutional support have been found to shape students’ engagement [11,12], while the quality of the technological environment, including system usability and resource functionality, significantly influences motivation for learning [13,14]. Interpersonal factors are equally important. Perceived teacher support affects engagement through technology acceptance and learning motivation [15], and peer relationships serve as protective factors against the negative effects of online education [16].

Despite the expanding literature, few studies have developed a theoretically grounded instrument that integrates multiple influencing factors specifically for health professions students in IDL. Few studies have explicitly accounted for the distinctive pedagogical requirements of nursing education, including high-fidelity skills laboratory training, clinical preceptor-ship, and the need to master digital documentation skills quickly. At the broadest level, a systematic review of 23 articles identified 17 distinct engagement instruments across higher education. Although all exhibited high reliability, 78 % were not applicable across all course modalities, and 61 % were unsuitable for all age groups, highlighting significant constraints on inclusiveness and adaptability [17]. Similarly, a broader synthesis of 29 scales revealed substantial variability in item counts and factor structures, alongside pervasive limitations such as self-report bias and limited cross-cultural invariance [18].

Focusing on health professions education, another review found that although self-report surveys predominate, the measurement of agentic and sociocultural dimensions remains limited, and comprehensive guiding principles for instrument development and selection in this context are not yet well established [19]. For instance, the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale-Student version (UWES-S) conceptualizes engagement as a positive psychological state comprising vigour, dedication, and absorption, but it was originally developed in organizational settings and fails to capture learning-specific behavioral or emotional dimensions [20]; the Online Student Engagement Scale (OSE) was designed for general online learning and has not been adapted to medical education’s unique demands [21]. The Student Engagement Questionnaire (SEQ), though developed for medical students during classroom activities, remains confined to traditional face-to-face instruction and lacks relevance to the digitally enriched, clinically immersive settings that define contemporary nursing curricula [22].

Given these limitations, a validated instrument that comprehensively captures medical students’ perceptions of the multiple factors that influence their engagement in IDL environments remains unavailable. We operationally define the IDL student Engagement Influencing Factor Scale (IDL-SEIFS) as a self-report instrument designed to systematically assess medical students’ perceptions of theoretically relevant antecedents that may facilitate or hinder their engagement in IDL contexts. Accordingly, the current study aimed to develop and psychometrically evaluate the IDL-SEIFS for medical students, with a particular focus on its applicability to nursing education contexts. While the validation sample spans multiple medical specialties to ensure broad psychometric robustness, the scale is designed as a practical diagnostic tool for nurse educators, providing a holistic assessment of how nursing students perceive the factors that shape their engagement in IDL environments.

Method

Research design

The development and psychometric evaluation of the IDL-SEIFS adhered to the established guidelines for scale development and validation, particularly following the practical principles outlined by Swan et al. [23], which provides an accessible, step-by-step guide based on the COSMIN framework. The process proceeded in two distinct phases: Phase 1 focused on constructing the initial scale through theoretical modeling, qualitative interviews, expert consultations, and pilot testing; and Phase 2 assessed the scale’s psychometric properties using data from a large-scale cross-sectional survey.

Phase 1: scale development

Theoretical framework

The conceptual foundation of the IDL-SEIFS rests on an integrated theoretical framework that prioritizes the determinants of student engagement. This framework draws on two complementary models to capture the multifaceted nature of these determinants. First, Wang’s Pedagogy-Social Interaction-Technology (PST) Model [24] emphasizes that effective technology-supported learning requires deliberate integration of pedagogical approaches, social interaction mechanisms, and technological features, all of which are conceptualized as potential influencing factors. Second, Pintrich and Zusho’s Model of Student Motivation and Self-Regulated Learning [25] provides a social-cognitive lens, highlighting how individual characteristics and motivational beliefs serve as proximal antecedents of engagement. As a theoretical outcome, we draw on Fredricks et al.’s Three-Dimensional Model of Student Engagement [5,6], which defines engagement across cognitive, emotional, and behavioral dimensions as being shaped by individual and contextual influences.

By synthesizing these perspectives, the integrated framework categorizes the determinants of engagement into two overarching domains: individual characteristics and contextual factors. The latter comprises macro-level institutional support and micro-level PST-based factors. These determinants are hypothesized to influence engagement through motivational and self-regulatory processes. This integrative framework (Appendix A) not only guided the generation of the initial item pool but also informed the qualitative exploration of factors affecting medical students’ engagement in IDL environments.

Development of the first item pool

We employed a descriptive qualitative design from June 2024 to September 2024 to capture medical students’ firsthand accounts of factors shaping their engagement in IDL environments, with reporting guided by the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [26]. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was used to recruit full-time undergraduate nursing and clinical medicine students from a medical university in Fujian Province who had completed at least one IDL course, excluding those with clinical internships. The sample size was determined by data saturation [27], which was reached after 25 interviews; two additional interviews confirmed no new insights, yielding a final sample of 25 participants (10 nursing students and 15 clinical medicine students).

A semi-structured interview guide informed by the integrated theoretical framework was developed following Kallio et al.’s five-stage process [28]. The core questions explored students’ experiences with intelligent digital technologies, confidence in and barriers to using such tools, school and teacher support, and interpersonal interactions via digital channels. Interviews lasted 40–60 min, were audio-recorded, and transcribed verbatim. Two researchers independently performed thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s guidelines [29] using NVivo, with iterative comparisons and consensus meetings to refine the themes. The analysis yielded four major themes—Personal Digital Literacy, Perceived School Support, Perceived Technological Environment, and Perceived Interpersonal Interaction and Support—which directly correspond to the four factors of the final scale. Appendix B presents each theme along with a representative quotation, its theoretical origin, and an example scale item. These qualitative findings enrich and validate the theoretical dimensions identified in the literature, thereby guiding the development of an initial item pool.

Primary questionnaire development

Delphi expert consultation

To establish content validity, a two-round Delphi expert consultation was conducted between October and December 2024. Eight experts were purposively selected based on the following predefined criteria: holding a master’s degree or higher, holding the academic rank of associate professor or professor, having more than a decade of professional experience, and demonstrating specialized knowledge in medical education. In the initial round, experts rated each item’s relevance to its intended dimension using a five-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant to 5 = extremely relevant). They were encouraged to provide qualitative feedback on clarity, appropriateness, and potential revisions. After a two-week interval, the revised scale was circulated to the same panel for a second evaluation. A 100 % response rate was achieved in both rounds. Content validity was assessed using the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI). An I-CVI below 0.78 is considered grounds for item revision or removal. Based on the expert feedback, several items were revised to improve clarity and relevance. For example, item 2 was reworded to “emphasize ethical adherence when using digital platforms for case discussions,” and item 14 was revised to “focus on the frequency of peer communication rather than its perceived outcomes.” Other items underwent similar adjustments to enhance the precision of wording.

Pilot study

A pilot test was conducted in January 2025 with 30 undergraduate nursing students from Fujian Medical University using the online survey platform Wenjuanxing. This preliminary phase served three purposes: to evaluate item clarity and comprehensibility through participant feedback on wording and format, supplemented by brief follow-up interviews with five students to detect ambiguous elements; to assess the scale’s internal consistency via Cronbach’s α coefficient; and to examine procedural feasibility, including average completion time and response patterns. The mean completion time was 13.6 min, which informed the criteria for identifying invalid responses in the main survey (completion time <7 min or evidence of logically inconsistent or patterned answers). The total scale achieved a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.86, with subscale α values ranging from 0.82 to 0.85, indicating satisfactory internal consistency. No items were deleted or substantially reworded based on pilot feedback; only explanatory notes for key terminology (e.g., “digital intelligent platforms” and “high-fidelity simulation”) were added to the survey instructions. The item-level mean scores ranged from 3.2 to 4.5 (on a 5-point Likert scale), with no floor or ceiling effects observed.

Phase 2: formal investigation

Participants and data collection

The data for this cross-sectional study were collected between February and June 2025 from undergraduate students at Fujian Medical University. Eligible participants were full-time medical students who had completed at least one IDL course and provided informed consent. The required sample size was determined following the widely recommended ratio of 15-30 participants per survey item for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [30]. Given that the scale comprised 20 items, a minimum of 300 to 600 participants was required. Trained research staff distributed the survey through a QR code linked to the Wenjuanxing platform. Of the 1,572 questionnaires distributed, all were returned (100 % response rate). After excluding 73 invalid responses (42 with a completion time <7 min and 31 with logically inconsistent or patterned answers), 1,499 valid questionnaires remained for analysis.

Instrument

The only instrument used during this phase was the IDL-SEIFS. Given the absence of an established criterion for the multidimensional construct of factors influencing medical students’ engagement in IDL environments, criterion-related validity was not pursued.

The IDL-SEIFS comprises 20 items that encompass a variety of factors relevant to medical students’ learning experiences in IDL environments. All items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), and all are positively framed. The total score thus ranges from 20 to 100, with higher scores denoting stronger perceived positive influences of the IDL environment on student engagement. The factorial structure of the scale was derived from exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses; the detailed results are presented in the results section.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using SPSS (version 26.0). Participants’ characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables, whereas continuous variables were described using either the mean (with standard deviation) or median (P25, P75), depending on their distribution. An α-level of 0.05 was adopted as the threshold for determining statistical significance.

Content validity

Content validity was assessed through a two-round expert consultation. Expert authority was evaluated using three coefficients: the familiarity coefficient, the judgment basis coefficient, and the authority coefficient. Expert agreement on item importance ratings was assessed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W); P < 0.05 was considered significant. Item-level content validity index (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) were calculated based on experts’ relevance ratings, with I-CVI ≥0.78 and S-CVI ≥0.90 considered acceptable.

Item analysis

Three analytical approaches were employed for the item evaluation. First, a critical ratio method compared scores between the upper and lower 27 % of respondents based on total scale scores; items with a critical ratio exceeding 3.0 and significant between-group differences (P < 0.05) were retained [31]. Second, item-total and corrected item-total correlations were examined, with values greater than 0.4 considered acceptable. Third, homogeneity was assessed: items were retained if their deletion did not increase the overall Cronbach’s α coefficient. Additionally, factor loadings (> 0.4) and commonalities (> 0.2) were required for the exploratory factor analysis [31]. Items that failed to meet these criteria were excluded.

Construct validity

To examine the construct validity of the scale, we randomly divided the total sample into two subsets for EFA and CFA, respectively, following established methodological practice [32]. For the first subset, an EFA was conducted using principal axis factoring with promax rotation. Before extraction, data suitability was assessed using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure (> 0.80) and Bartlett’s test for sphericity (P < 0.001). Factors were retained based on eigenvalues greater than 1, and items with factor loadings below 0.40 were considered for elimination [33]. For the second subset, CFA was performed using maximum likelihood estimation to test the factor structure identified in the EFA. The model fit was evaluated using multiple indices [34], with acceptable fit defined as RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.08, and CFI >0.90.

Convergent and discriminant validity

Convergent validity was assessed using average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) for each factor, with AVE >0.50 and CR > 0.70 indicating adequate convergent validity [35]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Fornell-Larcker criterion, which requires that the square root of each factor’s AVE exceed its highest correlation with any other factor [35].

Reliability

Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α coefficient for the total scale and its subscales, with values above 0.70 considered acceptable [36]. Split-half reliability was also assessed via the Spearman-Brown coefficient, with values above 0.70 deemed acceptable and values above 0.80 indicating good reliability [36]. These multiple reliability indices collectively supported the scale’s internal consistency.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Fujian Medical University (Approval no. [2024(NO.252)]. All participants were fully informed of the study purpose, procedures, voluntary participation, and confidentiality measures, and provided written informed consent before enrolment.

Results

Demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 1,499 medical students participated, with a mean age of 21.43 ± 2.41 years (65.64 % female, 34.36 % male). The grade levels ranged from first-year undergraduate (17.08 %) to doctoral students (3.60 %), with the largest proportion in the third (22.08 %) and fourth (18.08 %) years. The sample comprised four majors: nursing (44.90 %), clinical medicine (41.09 %), medical laboratory science (8.81 %), and Preventive Medicine (5.20 %). Over half of the participants (52.90 %) had more than three years of digital learning experience. The most frequently used platforms were Chaoxing (95.20 %), Chinese University MOOC (73.32 %), Bilibili (70.51 %), and AI tools (62.17 %). The weekly digital learning time was relatively balanced: ≤5 h (35.76 %), 6–10 h (35.62 %), 11–15 h (13.61 %), and ≥16 h (15.01 %).

Validity analysis

Expert consultation and content validity

All eight experts completed both the consultation rounds (100 % response rate). Across the two rounds, no items were deleted, as all met the retention criterion (I-CVI ≥0.78); five items were revised, and the remaining 15 were retained without modification. The final I-CVI values ranged from 0.85 to 1.00, with an S-CVI of 0.94. In the first round, the coefficients for expert familiarity, judgment basis, and authority were 0.945, 0.965, and 0.943, respectively. In the second round, these values were 0.934, 0.967, and 0.936, respectively, indicating consistently high expert authority throughout the process. The mean importance ratings across items ranged from 4.19 to 4.94 in the first round and from 4.12 to 4.87 in the second round. Kendall's coefficient of concordance was 0.113 (P < 0.05) and 0.132 (P < 0.05) for the two rounds, respectively, suggesting statistically acceptable agreement among the experts.

Construct validity

Exploratory factor analysis

Data suitability for factor analysis was confirmed using a KMO measure of 0.975 and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (χ2 = 18718.809, df = 190, P < 0.001). An EFA was conducted using principal axis factoring with a promax rotation. Examination of eigenvalues greater than 1 and inspection of the scree plot (Appendix C) supported a four-factor solution that accounted for 66.85 % of the total variance. The four factors were subsequently interpreted and named based on the thematic content of the items with strong loadings.

The first factor, Personal Digital Literacy, comprised six items with loadings ranging from 0.589 to 0.751, capturing critical thinking, ethical awareness, self-efficacy, information discernment, and task-completion confidence in intelligent digital environments, and explained 19.45 % of the variance. The second factor, Perceived School Support, consisted of five items (loadings 0.516–0.742) and accounted for 17.80 % of the variance, reflecting institutional support mechanisms, including resource integration with clinical practice, evaluation systems, and training opportunities. The third factor, Perceived Technological Environment, included five items with loadings ranging from 0.541 to 0.695, explained 15.70 % of the variance, and encompassed perceptions of resource timeliness, system usability, cross-device stability, simulation effectiveness, and intelligent-assistance functions. The fourth factor, Perceived Interpersonal Interaction and Support, comprised four items (loadings of 0.488–0.712) and explained 13.90 % of the variance, reflecting teacher feedback quality, peer collaboration, and the online discussion atmosphere. All factor loadings exceeded the minimum criterion of 0.40 (Appendix D).

Confirmatory factor analysis

Confirmatory factor analysis was performed using maximum likelihood estimation to validate the four-factor structure. The model showed a satisfactory to good fit: χ2/df = 4.587, GFI = 0.96, CFI = 0.928, NFI = 0.918, TLI = 0.968, RMSEA = 0.049, and SRMR = 0.054. All standardized factor loadings were significant (P < 0.01), ranging from 0.68 to 0.79. Convergent validity was supported by the average variance extracted (AVE) values of 0.574, 0.608, 0.542, and 0.578 for Factors 1 to 4, respectively, all meeting or approaching the 0.50 benchmark. Item discrimination

To assess the discriminatory power of the IDL-SEIFS, respondents were divided into high- and low-scoring groups based on the upper and lower 27 % of the total scores (n = 405 per group). The Mann–Whitney U test revealed significant differences between the two groups across all factors (P < 0.01), with Z-values ranging from 48.319 to 51.962. These findings confirm that the scale effectively discriminated between students with higher and lower total scores, demonstrating a strong discriminant capacity (Appendix E).

Reliability

The internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability. For the four subscales, α values ranged from 0.882 to 0.895: 0.890 (Personal Digital Literacy), 0.890 (Perceived School Support), 0.882 (Perceived Technological Environment), and 0.895 (Perceived Interpersonal Interaction and Support). The total scale achieved an α of 0.920, with a Spearman-Brown split-half coefficient of 0.918, substantially exceeding the 0.70 threshold and indicating excellent internal consistency. Corrected item-total correlations ranged from 0.589 to 0.729 (Appendix D), well above the 0.30 criterion, suggesting that each item contributed meaningfully to the overall construct. Factor mean scores ranged from 14.54 to 22.08, and inter-factor correlations were all positive and significant (r = 0.715–0.773, P < 0.01), further supporting the scale's internal structure.

Discussion

The final 20-item IDL-SEIFS comprises four distinct factors: Personal Digital Literacy, Perceived School Support, Perceived Technological Environment, and Perceived Interpersonal Interaction and Support, collectively explaining 66.85 % of the total variance and demonstrating excellent model fit indices and strong reliability.

The four-factor structure is consistent with, and provides empirical support for, the integrated theoretical framework. Personal Digital Literacy (19.45 % of the variance) captured students’ critical thinking, ethical awareness, self-efficacy, and information discernment in the IDL environment. This factor aligns with the learner-level psychological mechanisms postulated by Pintrich and Zusho’s social-cognitive perspective [25]. It supports previous findings that digital and AI literacy are positively associated with student engagement [8,9]. The inclusion of items on maintaining independent thinking when using AI tools addresses contemporary concerns about the erosion of critical thinking skills [37,38]. Network analysis has identified academic self-efficacy as a central node in engagement networks [39]. Perceived school support (17.80 % of the variance) reflected institutional factors such as resource integration with clinical practice, evaluation systems, training opportunities, and technical support. This factor confirms the critical role of educational institutions in fostering student engagement [11,12], and its items regarding clinical integration are particularly relevant for bridging the theory–practice gap in medical education [40]. Perceived technological environment (15.70 % of the variance) captured system usability, cross-device stability, simulation effectiveness, and intelligent assistance functions. The emergence of this distinct factor provides empirical support for the technological dimension of the PST model, confirming that students perceive technological quality as separable from the pedagogical and social dimensions, consistent with research on digital educational games [13] and online learning environment instruments [14]. The inclusion of simulation effectiveness reflects the growing role of simulation-based education [41]. Perceived interpersonal interaction and support (13.90 % of the variance) included teacher feedback, peer collaboration, and online discussions. This factor corresponds to the social interaction dimension of the PST model and the relatedness aspect of self-determination theory [42]. The significance of this factor aligns with the evidence that instructor support directly (56.9 %) and indirectly (43.1 %) influences cognitive engagement among medical students [43], and that digital interactive peer support significantly enhances clinical skill performance and retention at a 7-week follow-up [44].

Compared with existing instruments, the IDL-SEIFS offers several advantages. While the Digi-MEE focuses specifically on online learning environments [14], the present scale provides a broader assessment across personal, institutional, technological, and interpersonal factors. The Engagement in Learning Activities Scale for newly graduated nurses addresses engagement outcomes rather than influencing factors [45], and instruments for problem-based learning [46] and simulation-based education [38] similarly focus on engagement. The Comprehensive Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice Questionnaire represents a parallel effort to assess e-learning utilization among health profession students [47]; however, the IDL-SEIFS specifically targets medical students in intelligent digital environments, including items on AI tools, simulation-based learning, and intelligent assistance functions that reflect contemporary technological advancements [34,48].

Although the scale was validated across multiple medical specialties, its practical utility is particularly salient in nursing education. Nursing students often face high-acuity clinical placements and demanding simulation-based training, where the four identified factors—personal digital literacy, perceived school support, perceived technological environment, and perceived interpersonal interaction—are critical determinants of engagement. Nursing educators can utilize this scale to diagnose whether low engagement stems from inadequate technical support during virtual clinical simulations or from weak interpersonal bonds in ward-based learning, thereby enabling targeted resource allocation. For instance, students with low perceived school support may benefit from improved technical assistance and institutional digital integration. In contrast, those with low personal digital literacy would benefit from AI literacy training that cultivates critical thinking, ethical awareness, and hands-on competence [9,35,48]. The distinction between perceived school support and perceived technological environment suggests that nursing programs should address both systemic support and technical quality—integrating digital resources into clinical practice to bridge the theory-practice gap [37], while enhancing teachers' ICT support to improve perceived usefulness [49]. Meanwhile, the perceived interpersonal interaction factor confirms that teacher feedback, peer collaboration, and positive discussion atmospheres remain crucial even in technology-mediated nursing education, supporting intentionally designed collaborative activities [16,50] and blended approaches that optimize student engagement [51]. Furthermore, as nursing increasingly embraces intelligent learning tools, this scale offers a validated metric to monitor how technological shifts impact nursing students’ psychological and behavioral engagement over time, and can serve as an outcome measure to evaluate curricular reforms or technology upgrades [52,53].

This study had several limitations. The sample was recruited from Chinese institutions, limiting cross-cultural generalizability. However, including multiple medical specialties enhances overall generalizability; we acknowledge that nursing students’ engagement patterns may exhibit unique nuances. Nevertheless, the robust model fit across the entire sample, and the theoretical saturation achieved in our qualitative interviews support the scale's content validity for nursing contexts. However, future research should validate the scale across diverse educational and cultural settings and conduct multi-group CFA to test measurement invariance between nursing and other medical cohorts. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes examination of sensitivity to change or predictive validity, and reliance on self-report data may introduce bias; future studies should include objective indicators and adopt longitudinal designs to test causal pathways. Test–retest reliability was not assessed, so the stability of the constructs over time remains to be established. Moreover, future research should examine criterion-related validity with established engagement measures and outcomes, assess applicability across other health professions, and use the scale in intervention studies to evaluate programs that enhance IDL environments or digital literacy.

Conclusion

The IDL-SEIFS demonstrates robust validity and reliability as a theory-grounded instrument. Although validated across multiple medical specialties, it is particularly relevant to nursing education, where simulated and clinical training demand high engagement. Its four-factor structure enables nurse educators to systematically diagnose engagement barriers and tailor interventions, which is critical for addressing student attrition. As intelligent technologies advance, this scale provides a practical metric for optimizing learning conditions and fostering digitally proficient nursing professionals.
