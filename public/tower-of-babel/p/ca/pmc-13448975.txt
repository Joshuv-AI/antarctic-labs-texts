Research on the Prevention and Control of Blast Impact Pressure in Deep Holes and the Mechanism of Coordinated Extraction of Harmful Gases
Authors: Jinlong Men, Bibo Xiong, Zehua Wang, Zhenpeng Ye, Jingxiang Li, Deqi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uncontrolled carbon monoxide (CO) release after deep-hole roof pressure-relief blasting remains a safety problem because conventional shallow drainage often fails to match the strata where blast gases are generated and retained. Here, we clarify the permeability-contrast-controlled retention mechanism and propose an integrated blasting-sealing-extraction framework that couples field diagnosis at the A503 working face, dynamic fracture modeling, confined explosion tests, adsorption-diffusion analysis, and industrial validation. The method uses the critical distance between the fracture front and the coal seam to constrain charge length and places extraction boreholes in the roof gas-enrichment zone rather than only in the coal seam. The A503 measurements showed that the sandstone roof permeability (2.1 × 10–13 m2) was about 41 times lower than that of the coal seam (8.7 × 10–12 m2), creating a low-permeability CO retention zone. Numerical simulations gave an effective blasting influence radius of approximately 6.5 m and showed that the control hole extended the connected plastic zone to about 10 m. Field mass-balance data showed that the optimized scheme increased the CO extraction rate from 4.43% to 82.21%, reduced ventilation discharge from 11.96% to 3.91%, and lowered the residual CO fraction from 83.61% to approximately 13.9%. These results show that the mechanism-guided matching of fracture control, sealing, and targeted roof-strata extraction converts postblasting CO control from delayed ventilation dilution to active capture at the enrichment zone.

Introduction

The persistent growth in global energy demand has reinforced the critical role of coal as a foundational energy source. However, increasing mining depths, now often exceeding 800 m, create high in situ stress environments that significantly elevate the risk of geotechnical hazards such as rockburst. In this context, deep-hole roof presplitting blasting has become a key technology for rockburst prevention in Chinese coal mines. By inducing controlled roof collapse to release stress, it is now employed in over 82% of relevant operations.

While mitigating rockburst risks, this blasting technique introduces the safety hazard of uncontrolled harmful gas emissions. Postblasting releases of gases like carbon monoxide (CO) have become increasingly prevalent, with approximately 32% of coal mine gas accidents directly linked to blasting fumes. CO is the primary toxic component, constituting over 75% of these gases. Its uncontrolled emission significantly increases toxic and asphyxiation risks in return airways, posing a severe threat to miners’ lives. Statistics indicate a high average postblasting CO extraction residual fraction of 83.6% in China, coupled with a 17% annual growth rate of concentration exceedances in return airways. This issue is particularly acute in major coal-producing regions like Shanxi, Shaanxi, and Xinjiang. Due to the significantly lower permeability of coal seams compared to surrounding rock strata, harmful gases tend to diffuse into the more permeable roof and floor rocks, drastically reducing extraction efficiency.

The root cause lies in the complex interplay between blasting gas generation, fracture network evolution, and subsequent gas migration. For the tested grade-three emulsion explosive, the field CO calculation used a specific CO yield of 27.66 L/kg, while the combined quantified harmful gas yield (CO, NO x , and CH4) was 29.19 L/kg. The gas initially accumulates within the newly created fracture network. Subsequently, driven by concentration gradients and extraction pressure differentials, it undergoes heterogeneous migration across the coal-rock interface, governed by the permeability contrast. Although mine ventilation influences overall gas distribution, existing models fail to accurately capture the interaction between the ventilation flow field and the dynamically evolving fracture network.

To address these gaps, this study integrates field measurements, numerical simulation, confined explosion tests, and industrial-scale validation. The workflow first identifies the cause of CO retention through borehole monitoring and permeability testing, then develops a coupled blasting-damage and gas-seepage model to clarify how charge layout and control holes regulate fracture connectivity and the critical safety distance. Confined explosion tests are used to quantify CO generation. On this basis, a synergistic “interval blasting-targeted strata extraction” mitigation technology is proposed by optimizing the charge and control-hole layout, strategically placing extraction boreholes in roof gas-enrichment zones, and applying sustained negative-pressure extraction. This mechanism-guided framework connects gas source identification, fracture regulation, and targeted capture, thereby improving postblasting harmful-gas control in deep coal seams.

Study Site and Problem Characterization

The study was conducted at the A503 working face, a deep coal mine in China with a hard, thick sandstone roof. After conventional deep-hole pressure-relief blasting, CO concentrations in the return airway repeatedly exceeded the national safety threshold of 24 ppm. Borehole logging, SF6 tracer tests, and core-permeability measurements showed that the control failure was mainly caused by strong permeability heterogeneity: the roof sandstone fracture network had a permeability of (K s = 2.1 × 10–13 m2), approximately 41 times lower than that of the underlying coal seam (K c = 8.7 × 10–12 m2). This contrast formed a gas-trapping zone near the rock-coal interface after high-pressure blast gases entered the strata, making roof-retained CO difficult to capture by conventional extraction systems. From an engineering-practice perspective, the initial A503 layout used blastholes spaced 5 m apart and shallow parallel extraction boreholes (110 mm diameter, 8 m depth) located 2 m from each blasthole. This arrangement was suitable for local coal-seam extraction but did not directly target the roof-strata gas-enrichment zone identified by the permeability and tracer evidence. The A503 case therefore represents a typical failure mode in which the blast-induced migration pathway, the low-permeability retention zone, and the extraction-borehole target did not coincide.

Numerical Modeling of Fracture Network Development and Control

To quantify how blasting parameters affect the evolution of fracture networks within heterogeneous coal-rock systems and to define the critical conditions for fracture-seam interaction, a fully coupled numerical model incorporating dynamic damage and fluid flow was developed in this study using the FLAC3D platform.

Constitutive Model and Material Parameters

The nonlinear response of the coal-rock mass under explosive loading was simulated using an elastic-plastic damage constitutive model that captures coupled tensile and shear failure. The yield function F is expressed as 1 F=τ+σntan⁡ϕ−c(1−D) where τ and σn represent the shear stress and normal stress (positive in tension) on the potential failure plane, respectively; ϕ is the internal friction angle; c denotes cohesion; and D is a scalar damage variable (0 ≤ D ≤ 1).

The damage variable D evolves with the equivalent plastic strain ε̅ p : 2 D=1−exp(−αε‐p) where α denotes the damage-sensitivity coefficient. Tensile failure is assumed to occur when the maximum principal stress σ1 exceeds the dynamic tensile strength σtd of the material: 3 σ1≥σtd

The model was further extended to represent the orthotropic behavior of the coal seam, with the elastic-modulus ratio E x /E z = 1.2.

Modeling Explosive Loading and Control Hole Effects

The pressure-time history P(t) exerted on the borehole wall by explosive detonation was described using an exponentially decaying oscillatory load: 4 P(t)=Pmaxexp(−βt)[1+sin(ωt)],0−t≤td where P max is the initial peak pressure, β is the pressure-decay coefficient, ω is the angular frequency of load oscillation, and t d denotes the duration of the quasi-static pressure phase generated by detonation gases.

The presence of a control hole significantly modifies the local stress distribution. According to elastic-wave dynamics, when an incident stress wave (σinc) impinges on the control hole, dynamic stress concentration develops around its periphery. The dynamic stress-concentration factor K t,dyn on the sides of the hole perpendicular to the incident-wave direction can be approximated as: 5 Kt,dyn=2Γ1−ν|Z2−Z1Z2+Z1| where Z 1 = ρ1 V p1 and Z 2 = ρ2 V p2 are the wave impedances of the surrounding rock and air, respectively; Γ is a lumped empirical factor related to rock-mass properties and in situ stress; and ν is Poisson’s ratio. This formulation captures the role of the control hole as a stress-waveguide, where the induced tensile stresses enhance fracture-network connectivity, as schematically shown in Figure .

Schematic of control hole-induced fracture linkage.

For the A503 working face, the lumped factor Γ in eq was evaluated through a normalized field-consistency check based on the measured stress amplification around the control holes. The control/no-control peak-pressure ratio in the control-hole zone at 4–6 m was defined as A obs, with a measured value of 2.11. The observed pressure-amplification ratio was compared directly with the dimensionless stress-concentration factor in eq and expressed as A obs = K t,dyn ≈ 2ΓR Z/(1 – ν), where R Z = |(Z 2 – Z 1)/(Z 2 + Z 1)|. According to the roof-sandstone elastic parameters in Table , the Poisson’s ratio was calculated as ν = (3K – 2G)/[2­(3K + G)] = 0.199, and the rock-air impedance contrast term was R Z = 0.9997. Therefore, the normalized field-check value was calculated as Γnorm = A obs(1 – ν)/(2R Z) = 0.85. This value indicates a localized stress-amplification effect around the control holes, which is consistent with the measured pressure response and supports the interpretation that reflected and superposed stress waves promoted fracture linkage in this zone. Together with the FLAC3D plastic-zone results, the different fracture responses of the 22 and 36 m blastholes, and the effective influence radius of approximately 6.5 m, this field check provides quantitative support for the control-hole-assisted blasting design at the A503 working face.

Quantifying Fracture Network Efficacy: The Connectivity Index

To evaluate the effectiveness of the developed fracture network, a Fracture Network Connectivity Index (C f ) was defined: Cf=VeffVref 6 Veff=∑i=1NViH(Di−Dcr)H(ki−kcr) Vref=πRth2L where N is the total number of model elements; V i is the volume of the i-th element; D i and k i are its damage variable and permeability, respectively; D cr and k cr are the threshold values for identifying an element as an effective gas-conducting fracture; H(·) is the Heaviside step function; and V ref = πR th 2 L is the reference cylindrical volume calculated from the charge length L and the theoretical crushing-zone radius R th.

The derivation of the connectivity index follows a volume-normalization procedure. In eq , the numerator represents the effective connected fracture volume, V eff, obtained by summing the elements that satisfy both the damage and permeability thresholds and belong to the connected gas-conducting region. Dividing V eff by the reference cylindrical volume V ref gives the dimensionless connectivity index C f . This treatment prevents isolated damaged elements from being counted as continuous extraction pathways, so the index represents the efficacy of the connected fracture network rather than the total damaged volume alone.

By simulating fracture development under different charge lengths, a quantitative relationship was established among the fracture-network connectivity index C f , the charge length L, and the distance d from the advancing fracture front to the coal seam. The critical safety distance d cr is defined as the minimum separation between the blast-induced fracture front and the coal seam that prevents a continuous gas pathway while maintaining sufficient roof-fracture development, that is, when C f reaches the connectivity threshold. Rather than being prescribed empirically, d cr is obtained from the numerical simulation by identifying the shortest safe separation satisfying both fracture-control and gas-isolation requirements. This parameter is subsequently used as the geometric constraint for determining the critical charge length L c .

Experiments and Modeling of Harmful Gas Generation and Retention in Confined Environments

Confined Explosion Experiments

Confined explosion experiments were conducted to quantify the harmful-gas source term and the initial retention behavior under controlled porous-medium confinement. A 50 L high-strength pressure vessel was packed with standard quartz sand with a grain size of 0.5–1.0 mm and a porosity of approximately 0.35, providing a reproducible porous confinement medium around the explosive charge. Before initiation, the chamber was evacuated to ≤4.0 kPa at ambient temperature to minimize the contribution of preexisting air to the measured gas composition. A 110.0 ± 0.5 g charge of permitted grade-three emulsion explosive was initiated using an electric detonator. After detonation and cooling, the gas composition was analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and gas chromatography (GC). Repeated tests were performed under the same experimental conditions, and the resulting gas-yield statistics were used to quantify the CO source term for the subsequent retention and extraction analysis.

The chamber experiment was designed to determine the gas-generation boundary condition rather than to reproduce the full postblasting atmosphere of the A503 working face. The initial chamber pressure of ≤4.0 kPa was approximately 3.95% of standard atmospheric pressure, corresponding to about 2.0 L of residual air in the 50 L vessel; this condition reduced the contribution of preexisting air to the measured gas composition. In the field, blasting occurs in a fractured and moist coal-rock system at near-atmospheric pressure, where CO migration and retention are further influenced by moisture, air entrainment, adsorption, fracture flow, and ventilation. Therefore, the chamber-derived gas-yield data were used as the source term, while the subsequent adsorption-diffusion analysis and industrial-scale validation were used to evaluate gas retention, migration, and extraction behavior under A503 field conditions.

This model predicts elevated CO yield caused by incomplete carbon oxidation under confined conditions and is consistent with the experimental measurements.

Transient Adsorption-Diffusion Coupling Model

The rapid postdetonation cooling and adsorption of high-pressure gas within the porous debris were modeled using a nonequilibrium adsorption-diffusion coupling equation: 9 ∂C∂t=De⁡∇2C−1−ϕϕρs⁡∂q∂t where C is the concentration of free gas in the pores, q is the adsorbed-gas concentration, ρ s is the solid grain density, ϕ is porosity, and D e is the effective diffusion coefficient. Adsorption kinetics are represented using a first-order rate model: 10 ∂q∂t=ka(qeq−q) where k a is the adsorption rate constant. The equilibrium adsorbed concentration q eq is described by the Langmuir isotherm: 11 qeq=VLPPL+P where V L is the Langmuir volume constant and P L is the Langmuir pressure constant.

The kinetic parameters k a and D e were calibrated by fitting the adsorption-diffusion model to the experimentally measured pressure-decay curves. For the low-permeability sandstone, the fitted parameters indicated rapid gas adsorption but a low effective diffusion coefficient, suggesting that CO can be rapidly taken up by the rock matrix after blasting and then released slowly during the subsequent pressure-relief and ventilation stage. This behavior supports the proposed mechanism of rapid gas adsorption followed by delayed retention in the low-permeability roof sandstone matrix.

Results and Discussion

Mechanism of Blast-Induced Fracturing and Characteristics of Fracture Network Development

Numerical Model Setup and Parameters

Based on the geological conditions of the A503 working face, two FLAC3D numerical models were established with borehole depths of 22 and 36 m, respectively. The model parameters, detailed in Table , were assigned strictly according to field-measured data. A key feature incorporated into the model was the significant permeability contrast, with a ratio of 41, between the rock stratum and the coal seam.

All boreholes had a diameter of 100 mm. The sealing lengths were 10 m for the 22 m borehole and 16 m for the 36 m borehole. Grade-three industrial emulsion explosive with a linear charge density of 3 kg/m was used. Consequently, the calculated charge weights were 36 and 60 kg for the 22 and 36 m boreholes, respectively. Initiation was achieved using millisecond-delay electric detonators. To mitigate gas emission, multicomponent efficient water-based stemming was placed at both ends of the explosive charge. The borehole sealing employed a three-stage process utilizing “cement anchoring agent-loess-cement anchoring agent.” The detailed borehole structure is illustrated in Figure .

Schematic diagram of the borehole structure.

The numerical simulation comprised two sequential steps. The first step involved a static analysis to achieve in situ stress equilibrium and simulate borehole drilling. Subsequently, a dynamic analysis was performed by applying the explosive load to simulate crack propagation. This dynamic calculation was used to investigate the process of shock wave propagation and fracture network development.

Bidirectional dynamic loads were applied to simulate the blasting pressure on the borehole walls. The data in Figure elucidate the attenuation law of the stress wave. Within the monitored range of 1–7 m from the borehole, both peak stress and peak displacement attenuate nonlinearly with increasing distance, stabilizing beyond approximately 6–7 m. This pattern is consistent with the geometric attenuation characteristics of stress waves. The two types of boreholes exhibit similar attenuation trends. The difference in their effects on the coal seam is primarily due to the varying straight-line distances between the bottom of the charge column and the coal seam.

In summary, within the initial blasting design, the 36 m borehole demonstrated poor fracturing efficiency owing to its excessive distance from the coal seam. Conversely, while the 22 m borehole achieved effective fracturing, it consequently induced significant gas emissions.

(1) For the 22 m Deep Blasting Holes: The blast-induced fractures successfully reached the coal seam. A portion of the generated CO migrated directly into the coal seam and diffused through its fracture network. This led to elevated CO levels exceeding safety limits at the upper corner and in the return airway. The shallow extraction boreholes (BP1-BP4), terminated within the coal seam, were only able to intercept a small fraction of this migrating gas and failed to capture the main flow originating from the fracture zone.

(2) For the 36 m Deep Blasting Holes: The CO produced remained largely confined within the low-permeability sandstone roof layer where the blasting occurred. All associated extraction boreholes (BP5-BP7), with their limited 8 m depth, did not penetrate into this gas-enriched zone within the rock mass. Consequently, extraction from these holes was minimal (<15 L) and largely ineffective.

In conclusion, the conventional strategy of uniformly deploying shallow extraction boreholes proved ineffective. The fundamental reason for this failure is a mismatch between the extraction geometry and the actual gas migration pathways dictated by permeability contrasts. The harmful gases are not uniformly dispersed but accumulate in specific strata, particularly low-permeability roof layers. Therefore, gas extraction measures must be targeted toward these accumulation zones; otherwise, retained CO can be released slowly into the goaf and return airway, creating a persistent safety hazard.

Synergistic Optimization Technology of “Interval Blasting and Targeted Strata Extraction” and Industrial Validation

Based on the integrated analysis presented in Section , an improved procedure was developed and implemented, involving three key steps: (1) application of the charge optimization module (eq ) to adjust charge length; (2) utilization of control holes to enhance fracture network connectivity; and (3) implementation of extended drainage based on the extraction-duration optimization module (eq ). The underlying optimization framework is illustrated in Figure . The specific rationale and adjustments are detailed below:(1)Elimination of Shallow Drainage Holes: The previous practice of drilling extraction boreholes 2 m parallel to the blasting holes was discontinued. This decision was based on the finding that the majority of harmful blast gases are retained within the roof strata, a zone not accessed by the shallow (8 m) extraction boreholes, rendering them largely ineffective.(2)Charge Length Reduction for 22 m Holes: The charge length in the 22 m blasting holes was reduced to 10 m, increasing the stemming length to 12 m. This modification was necessary because the original charge column extended too close to the coal seam. Blasting under the original scheme fractured approximately 2.5 m of the underlying coal seam, providing pathways for harmful gases to migrate into the mine ventilation system via the coal fracture network, leading to CO exceedances in the return airway.(3)Implementation of Interval Blasting with Control Holes: An interval blasting pattern was adopted, where the middle hole in a series of three is left uncharged and used as a control hole. This design leverages the effective blasting radius (∼6.5 m), which exceeds the hole spacing (5 m). Continuous blasting of closely spaced holes creates overlapping influence zones, which is inefficient and increases costs. The empty control hole provides additional free faces, promoting better fracture development and directionality. Crucially, as this hole penetrates into the target roof strata, it can be repurposed as a highly effective extraction borehole postblasting, directly accessing the gas-enriched zone.

Mechanistically, the optimized scheme closes the control chain from fracture regulation to CO capture. Interval blasting and control holes first redirected fracture development toward the roof strata, the roof-strata extraction boreholes then intersected the CO enrichment zone identified from the permeability contrast, and the extended extraction period allowed the free-fracture gas and slower desorbed gas to be captured before release into the return airway. This coupling explains the simultaneous increase in extraction efficiency and decrease in both ventilation discharge and residual CO fraction.

Conclusions

This study investigated the uncontrolled emission of harmful gases accompanying deep-hole destress blasting in deep coal mines. By integrating numerical simulation, confined explosion experiments, adsorption-diffusion analysis, and field trials, the interaction between blast-induced fracture networks and permeability-contrast-controlled gas migration was clarified. The principal conclusions are as follows:(1)The effective blasting range and fracture-network development were quantified. Numerical simulations showed an effective influence radius of approximately 6.5 m. For the 22 m deep boreholes, fractures propagated about 2.5 m into the underlying coal seam, whereas the fracture network from the 36 m deep boreholes was confined to the roof strata. The introduction of a control hole induced stress-wave reflection and superposition, extending the connected plastic zone to about 10 m and improving pressure-relief performance without forming an uncontrolled coal-seam gas pathway.(2)The harmful-gas generation characteristics and the failure mechanism of the initial drainage scheme were quantified. Confined explosion tests showed a total gas yield of 825.55 L/kg from the grade-three emulsion explosive. CO was the dominant harmful component, with a specific yield of 27.66 L/kg; together with NO x and CH4, the combined quantified harmful gas yield was 29.19 L/kg. Under the initial A503 scheme, only 4.43% of the generated CO was extracted and 11.96% was discharged by ventilation, leaving 83.61% retained mainly within the roof strata and rock-coal interface.(3)The proposed interval blasting and targeted strata extraction technology improved both fracture control and CO capture. In the actual 90 kg optimized trial, 2046.48 L of the 2489.40 L generated CO was extracted, corresponding to an extraction efficiency of 82.21%; 97.32 L was removed by ventilation (3.91%), and 345.60 L remained as residual CO. When normalized to the 96 kg equivalent basis, the corresponding extracted, ventilated, and residual volumes were 2182.90, 103.75, and 368.72 L, respectively. These results indicate that matching the charge structure, stemming length, borehole interval, and roof-strata drainage position can substantially reduce the CO exceedance risk in the mine return airway.
