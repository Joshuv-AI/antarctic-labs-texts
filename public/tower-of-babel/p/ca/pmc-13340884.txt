Perioperative Antibiotics and Endoscopic Carpal Tunnel Release: A Trinetx Analysis
Authors: Brooke Allen, Keith Kuo, Joy Ha, Diala Burjak, Ethan Lester, Alvin Kwok, Jayant Agarwal
Published: 2026

Purpose: Carpal tunnel release (CTR) represents 90% of all surgically treated compression neuropathies, with around 500,000 procedures performed annually in the United States. Of these, roughly 25% are done endoscopically. While the open approach is more common, endoscopic CTR performs similarly in long-term outcomes, and it outperforms open CTR in post-operative surgical site infection (SSI) rates. SSIs after CTR are rare, occurring in up to 1% of patients. Guidelines from the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) and the American Association of Plastic Surgeons (AAPS) do not recommend prophylactic perioperative antibiotics for SSI prevention in CTR. However, these guidelines are based on underpowered studies, and up to 51% of hand surgeons report still prescribing prophylactic perioperative antibiotics for their CTR patients. Given this population’s size, evaluating the necessity of prophylactic perioperative antibiotics is important for reducing antimicrobial resistance, lowering costs, and preventing antibiotic-associated adverse events. To answer this question, analyses of large cohorts are needed. This study aims to retrospectively assess the risk of SSIs after endoscopic CTR in patients who did and did not receive prophylactic perioperative antibiotics.

Methods: A retrospective cohort study was conducted using the TriNetX Research Network, which is a database containing de-identified patient information. The cohorts comprised of adult patients (>18 years) who underwent endoscopic CTR (CPT: 29848) for diagnosed carpal tunnel syndrome (ICD-10s: G56, G56.00, G56.01, G56.02, and G56.03). Patients were separated into two cohorts: those with and those without perioperative antibiotic exposure. Exposure was defined as antibiotic use within 24 hours before and after endoscopic CTR. Cohorts were then 1:1 propensity matched on age, sex, race, obesity (E66), diabetes mellitus (E08-E13), and nicotine dependence (F17). Measures of association analyses were run that calculated the cohorts’ risk of superficial SSI (T81.41) and deep SSI (T81.42) in the thirty days following endoscopic CTR.

Results: The initial query of individuals who underwent endoscopic CTR with and without perioperative antibiotics yielded a total of 26,109 and 24,873 patients, respectively. After propensity score matching, both cohorts contained 22,031 patients without significant differences in matched variables. 90.1% of the antibiotic cohort received penicillin and beta-lactams within the defined exposure window. There was no significant difference in the risk of superficial SSI between the antibiotic-exposed cohort (n = 22 cases) and the unexposed cohort (n = 19 cases), with a risk difference of 0 (95% CI: 0 to 0.001, p = 0.638) and a risk ratio of 1.15 (95% CI: 0.63 to 2.14). There were insufficient deep SSIs for TriNetX to perform a comparison between cohorts.

Conclusions: There was no increased risk of superficial SSI between those who did and did not receive perioperative prophylactic antibiotics for endoscopic CTR. These findings validate AAPS and AAOS clinical guidelines and strengthen their level of evidence, as these results were observed in large cohorts with sufficient statistical power. Future research should focus on high-powered analyses of SSI risk after open CTR to validate antibiotic guidelines for all CTR procedures.
