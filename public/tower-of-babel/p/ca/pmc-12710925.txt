Cancer targeting carfilzomib nanomedicine: a comprehensive review of delivery vehicles and efficacy
Authors: Matthew Molinaro, Dipanjan Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCancer therapy is an ever-changing landscape in constant demand of innovative approaches. Carfilzomib (CFZ) is a tetrapeptide epoxyketone covalent proteasome inhibitor currently approved for the treatment of refractory multiple myeloma. CFZ has a litany of anti-cancer biological effects lending itself to the treatment of a broad number of malignancies. Like many anti-cancer agents, CFZ is marred by severe offsite toxicities that limit applications of the drug. As a result, many nanomedicine approaches have been explored to improve the therapeutic index of CFZ-based treatments. Nanoparticle mediated delivery of CFZ has emerged as a leading candidate. CFZ can be encapsulated in a diverse array of nanomedical formulations including lipid-based, polymer, inorganic, and nanocrystalline vehicles. Each vehicle subtype has unique properties allowing for opportunities for enhanced delivery as well as multimodal therapy. In this review, we will categorize and summarize CFZ nanomedicine methods while demonstrating the potential of CFZ in the treatment of cancer.

Introduction

The proteasome presents as an intriguing target for cancer therapy. The initial clinical application of proteasome inhibitors was focused on combating muscle wasting disease [1,2]. However, early experiments demonstrated high toxicity of proteasome inhibiting compounds. The focus then shifted to cancer therapy. The earliest clinically studied proteasome inhibitor PS-341 had significant cytotoxicity on a broad range of malignant cell lines [3]. The drug was found to be particularly potent on human multiple myeloma cell lines when compared to healthy bone marrow stromal cells [4]. These findings spurred several phase I clinical trials mainly for hematological malignancy. PS-341, now known as bortezomib, was FDA approved in 2003 for the treatment of multiple myeloma. Bortezomib is also approved for use in mantle cell lymphoma [5]. Despite preclinical results demonstrating potential applications in a variety of solid tumors, bortezomib widespread clinical adoption never materialized. This is due, in part, to serious side effects such as peripheral neuropathy, gastrointestinal distress, and thrombocytopenia in addition to acquired resistance [6,7]. To combat the issues with bortezomib, 2nd generation proteasome inhibitor PR-171, later dubbed carfilzomib (CFZ) was developed. CFZ was derived from epoxomicin, a proteasome inhibiting molecules discovered in 1992 by Bristol Myer Squibbs [8]. Early optimization effort by the Crews laboratory at Yale University led to YU-101. Proteolix, a biotech company out of San Francisco, further improved YU-101 and ultimately synthesized the final form of CFZ [9]. CFZ is a covalent inhibitor of the proteasome which contrasts the reversible inhibition mechanism of bortezomib [10]. Furthermore, CFZ was found to have a high specificity for proteasome proteases when compared to bortezomib [11]. The first-generation bortezomib had high affinity for non-proteasomal proteases that are critical for neuronal cell growth [12]. CFZ was approved for the treatment of refractory multiple myeloma in 2013 [10]. Multiple myeloma has been the key disease clinically studied with CFZ. It is thought that since multiple myeloma cancers produce a high amount of immunoglobulin, they would be particularly sensitive to proteasome inhibitors. Though this appears to be true, there are many other cellular effects derived from CFZ exposure [13]. Unfortunately, CFZ is plagued with dose limiting side effects, namely hematological and cardiovascular toxicity [14]. The dose-limiting side effects rendered CFZ ineffective against solid tumors clinically [15]. CFZ is currently sold under the trade name Kyprolis™ via Amgen. Since the approval of CFZ, Ixazomib, an orally administered proteasome inhibitor, was FDA approved in 2015 [16].

CFZ presents distinct advantages to first-generation proteasome inhibitors, however current systemic administration will yield the same results. To unlock the potential of CFZ, it is necessary to develop improved strategies for drug delivery. Nanomedicine has sought to harness the properties of nanoscale systems to selectively deliver drugs to tumors while sparing healthy tissues throughout the body. Doxil™, a liposomal formulation of doxorubicin, was approved in 1996 and is utilized and investigated in a variety of malignancies both solid and hematological [17]. However, nanoscale systems go far beyond liposomal technology. In this review, we will categorize and summarize nano application of CFZ while offering insight into the outlook for CFZ in the developing landscape of nanomedicine.

Carfilzomib properties, mechanism, and biological effects

Physical and chemical properties

CFZ is a tetrapeptide epoxyketone molecule with an N-terminal morpholine group. The molecule, pictured in Figure 1, is an analog of the naturally derived epoxomcin [9]. The peptide portion is comprised of four hydrophobic amino acids noting a leucine group nearest the epoxyketone. This is a common trait of FDA approved proteasome inhibitors; however, bortezomib and ixazomib have leucine adjacent to boronic acid groups [18]. CFZ is highly hydrophobic with sparse aqueous solubility. The molecule possesses a pKa around 3.5 and will see a slight increase in solubility under acidic conditions [19,20]. Ashley et al. established the octanol/water partition coefficient of CFZ through the shake flask method. They found that the solubility of CFZ is over four orders of magnitude greater in the organic solvent compared to pH 7.4 phosphate buffered saline [21]. This presents a distinct pharmacological challenge in the administration of CFZ. Currently, commercial CFZ is prepared with sulfobutylether-β-cyclodextrin and citrate buffer to solubilize the drug for systemic administration [22]. Fifty times excess by weight of cyclodextrin is needed to solubilize the drug. A high excipient burden can prove toxic in some patients. A key advantage of nanoparticle delivery is the ability to increase the solubility of CFZ in aqueous media through encapsulation within biocompatible nanoparticles. Mechanism of action

CFZ directly interacts with the proteasome. The ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS) is the primary method in which cells control protein degradation. The complex is not a single entity but rather several different units acting in unison [23]. Ubiquitin, a small protein, is conjugated to a protein substrate via enzymes E1, E2, and E3 then is polymerized to form a polyubiquitin chain. The ubiquitin chain serves as a marker for degradation. The tagged protein is recognized by the proteasome and peptide bonds are cleaved [24,25]. The UPS is critical for not only clearing damaged and malformed proteins but also for regulation of key cell processes including cell cycle, DNA repair, and apoptosis [6]. The 26S proteasome facilitates most of the protein degradation in human cells. This complex is composed of the 20S catalytic core unit with regulatory subunits on the ends such as 19S serving as caps. The 20S core is built from an assembly of α and β heptamer rings. Two β rings make up the innermost portion with two α rings to the outside. The final 20S assembly resembles a cylinder in which the innermost portion of the β rings contain active sites for peptide hydrolysis [26]. The β5 (chymotrypsin-like), β2 (trypsin-like), and β1(caspase-like) subunits are all present. The β5 subunit is the typical target for proteasome inhibitor drugs [18]. CFZ selectively inhibits the chymotrypsin-like activity of the 20S proteasome through covalent binding to the β5 subunit [27]. The epoxyketone group on CFZ will form two covalent bonds with the N-terminal threonine [22]. The exact nature of the covalent modification is under debate with multiple proposed mechanisms [28,29]. An understanding of the precise mechanism of carfilzomib may help develop future covalent proteasome inhibitors as well as understand potential side effects from offsite reactivity. The chemical bonding of CFZ starkly contrasts the slow, reversible binding seen with bortezomib [27].

Biological effects

Once CFZ binds the β5 subunit of the 20S proteasome and the chymotrypsin-like proteasome activity halts, there are several pathways to cell death. Blocking of proteasome activity will lead to accumulation of misfolded proteins and precipitate the unfolded protein response and endoplasmic reticulum stress culminating in apoptosis [30]. CFZ can also directly induce apoptosis through phorbol-12-myristate-13-acetate-induced protein 1 (NOXA) activation or JUN N-terminal kinase (JNK) pathways [30,31]. Nuclear factor kappa B (NF-KB) is a key pro-survival pathway in many cells types especially those of hematological orgin [18]. Proteasome inhibitors block the degradation of the nuclear factor of the kappa-light polypeptide gene enhancer in B-cells inhibitor α(IKBα,) an endogenous protein inhibitor of NF-KB. When IKBα is not degraded, NF-KB cannot be activated and subsequently malignant processes such as proliferation, invasion, metastasis and angiogenesis will diminish [32]. Interestingly, Gupta et al. saw that in chronic lymphoid leukemia cells that CFZ does not explicitly shut down NF-KB but rather prompts an atypical activation void of malignant properties [33]. This study demonstrates that CFZ’s actions can be multifaceted and dependent on cell type. Regardless of an explicit mechanism, CFZ’s in vitro cell killing potential expands across a large variety of cancer types both hematological and solid tumor [34–38]. In addition to cell killing, CFZ appears to influence the immune system as well. With regard to cancer, it was demonstrated the CFZ modulates the tumor microenvironment by inducing M1 polarization of tumor associated macrophages [39]. Though not explicitly tied to CFZ, inhibition of the proteasome and ubiquitin depletion can be associated with alterations to critical cell processes including DNA repair, cell cycle regulation, and autophagy [40]. The litany of biological effects of CFZ including perturbation of cell cycle processes and DNA repair presents the opportunity for synergistic chemotherapy with a variety of cytotoxic agents. CFZ combination therapy has been investigated with agents ranging from cannabinoids to small-molecule inhibitors [41–43]. Specifically, a promising combination therapy may exist with histone deacetylase inhibitors such as vorinostat. It was found the CFZ and vorinostat synergize cytotoxicity against a variety of cancer cell types including leukemia, lymphoma, lung, and head and neck [44–46]. Potential mechanism includes potentiation of unfolded protein response apoptosis and increased reactive oxygen species.

Carfilzomib nanomedicine

CFZ exhibits distinct advantages in vitro including high specificity for chymotrypsin-like activity of 26S proteasome, a multitude of cytotoxic mechanisms, and high potential for synergistic therapy. However, the pharmacological properties and known systemic toxicity will continue to hamper widespread clinical adoption of the drug. Nanomedicine, specifically nanoparticle mediated delivery, may prove to be vital in establishing CFZ-based treatments. Nanoparticle delivery has several distinct advantages. Nanoparticles can be passively or actively targeted to tumors. Passive targeting relies on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect seen in tumors [47]. Due to leaky vasculature, nanoparticles can preferentially accumulate in solid tumors. Active targeting goes a step further and aims for cancer-specific receptors through targeting ligands on the surface of the nanoparticle [48,49]. Nanomedicine systems can also be designed to dynamically respond to the tumor microenvironment. Chemical or biochemical properties of cancerous tissue are used as stimuli for the release of drugs at the site of the tumor [50,51]. The concepts mentioned above have been reviewed in detail [50,52–54]. For CFZ, nanomedicine allows for the solubilization of the drug thereby enhancing systemic administration. Nanoparticle delivery boosts delivery to tumor, improving treatment efficacy while mitigating offsite effects in addition to providing opportunities for multimodal therapies. A wide variety of nanoparticle compositions are used in the delivery of CFZ including lipids, polymers, inorganic materials, and nanocrystals of the drug itself.

Lipid

Lipid-based nanoparticles represent the bulk of clinically available nanomedicine [53]. Beyond DOXIL™, there are several other liposomal formulation approved for treatment of cancer including DaunoXome™, DepoCyt™, and Marqibo™ [55]. Liposomes are composed of primarily phospholipids or other synthetic and biologically derived amphiphilic lipids. Cholesterol is often included to regulate the stability of liposomes, while a variety of surface modifications can be made to impart active targeting. Liposomes self-assemble into spherical bilayer structures containing an aqueous core and hydrophobic region within the lipid bilayer. Liposomes can traffic water-soluble drugs within the aqueous core and will often store hydrophobic drugs within the bilayer [56–58]. To date, liposomes are the most widely clinically used nanomedicine with seven formulations currently the FDA approved for the treatment of cancer [53]. Liposomes are not the only lipid-based drug delivery vehicles. Lipid core nanoparticles are another strategy for drug delivery. A surfactant molecule will coat a solid or oily lipid core. Hydrophobic drugs can be encapsulated within the lipid core, while hydrophilic species will require the formation of nanoemulsions within the lipid core. Overall, one of the key strengths of lipid-based nanoparticles is the high encapsulation of hydrophobic drugs [59,60]. Summary of lipid-based delivery of CFZ.

Liposomes are proven drug delivery vehicles and have been implicated in the delivery of CFZ. Ashley et al. developed the first published liposomal formulation of CFZ with the goal of targeted treatment of multiple myeloma [21]. They applied thin-film hydration followed by an extrusion technique to produce 70 nm phosphatidylcholine-based liposomes. The particles were decorated with a peptide targeted to VLA-4, an adhesion molecule overexpressed by multiple myeloma cells. In vitro, studies demonstrated the efficacy of the liposomes in terms of cytotoxicity and reduction in proteasome activity. In vivo efficacy was evaluated on a xenograft tumor model with NCI-H929 cells. Tumor growth was diminished by both targeted and non-targeted formulations. The unencapsulated CFZ control groups saw rapid weight loss resulting in the removal of the mice from the study. The liposomal formulation has a much higher therapeutic index when compared to freely administered CFZ. Ashley et al. expanded on their initial formulation with the inclusion of a doxorubicin prodrug within the liposome [61]. They found that CFZ and doxorubicin yield synergistic cytotoxicity on multiple myeloma cell lines when administered in a 1:1 stoichiometric ratio. Nanoparticle delivery gave them the ability to deliver this desired ratio. Systemic administration of free drug makes it exceedingly difficult to achieve desired ratios of drugs in a tumor due to vastly different pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters between the drugs. In vivo study on a xenograft model demonstrated improved efficacy in liposomal CFZ with embedded doxorubicin prodrug when compared to the two agents administered separately. A different formulation of sphingomyelin/Cholesterol/PEG-2000-PE (55/45/2.8, mol/mol/mol) with slightly larger particles of 110–130 nm was studied for the treatment of multiple myeloma [62]. The in vivo studies demonstrated that the liposomal CFZ increased treatment tolerability 5-fold when compared to standard administration of CFZ. Furthermore, they studied a combination therapy with vinblastine co-loaded within the liposomes. The combination treatment was far superior to other treatments evaluated in terms of both tumor response and survival.

The flexible chemical nature of liposomes allows for advanced functionalization.Liposomes that leveraged neutrophil migration for enhanced treatment of multiple myeloma were developed [63]. Neutrophils, a key component of the innate immune system, will express CXCR4 during their later stages of circulation. This receptor guides neutrophils back into the bone marrow, the sight of primary multiple myeloma. The microenvironment of multiple myeloma makes complete therapy challenging. Liposome designed with c (RGDfK) peptide (RLPs) bind mature neutrophils allowing for selective trafficking of liposome into the bone marrow as shown in Figure 3. The reason CFZ was included is not only to kill multiple myeloma cells but also to modulate tumor microenvironment. CFZ loaded liposomes were shown to increase natural killer cell mediated cancer cell death. This study is key in the development of CFZ therapeutics as it demonstrated the capabilities of the drug when delivered effectively. Simultaneously, liposomes were delivered with checkpoint inhibitor BMS-202. A lipid nanodisk formation of CFZ was developed and investigated for the treatment of glioblastoma. Through thin-film hydration, they synthesized a phosphatidylcholine and cholesterol lipid nanoparticles. The surface was functionalized with a heptapeptide DA7R which binds both vascular endothelial growth factor recptor 2 (VEGFR2) and neuropilin-1(NRP-1), surface receptors commonly over expressed on tumor cells and tumor-related endothelial cells. In vivo, they demonstrated enhanced tumor uptake of DA7R functionalized vs. solely PEGylated nanoparticles on a xenograft flank and intracranial model. Furthermore, targeted nanodisks proved superior to both PEGylated nanodisk and free CFZ in terms of tumor response and survival. This work is significant as it demonstrates how improving delivery of CFZ can open possibilities in the treatment of glioblastoma.

Polymer

Polymers are a broad category of biomaterials ranging from naturally derived collagens to synthetic block-copolymer [64]. The chemical flexibility presents the possibility to formulate polymeric nanoparticle with a wide variety of functions and properties [65]. Polymeric drug delivery vehicles can take a variety of structures including micelles, polymersome, dendrimers, and hydrogels [66]. CFZ, being a hydrophobic drug, is readily encapsulated within hydrophobic polymer materials. Summary of polymer-based delivery of CFZ.

A micelle is a nanoparticle formed through the self-aggregation of amphiphilic polymer molecules [77]. Polymers for micelle nanomedicine will typically be block copolymers with both hydrophobic and hydrophilic polymer subunits. The first instance of micelle-based delivery of CFZ in 2015 [37]. They tested a variety of block copolymers composed of varying molecular weight polyethylene glycol (PEG) and polycaprolactone (PCL). Micelles were created through thin-film hydrations with and without excipients. After establishing suitable formulations, they tested the nanoparticles on human cells lines H460 and RPMI-8226 finding comparable or improved potency when compared to free drug. These results inspired the same groups next study applying CFZ loaded PEG-PCL polymers in vivo [67]. The best composition from the previous study, 5–5.5kD PEG-PCL formulated with deoxycholic acid, was administered to a human non-small cell lung cancer xenograft mouse model. In vivo data suggests encapsulation of CFZ in PEG-PCL micelle did not improve therapeutic efficacy vs. unencapsulated drug. Furthermore, when comparing the proteasomal activity of whole-blood lysate, it was found that proteasome activity was equally reduced between encapsulated and unencapsulated drug, suggesting severe leakage prior to tumor uptake. The author notes that several factors may have influenced the results, including in vivo release of drug from nanoparticle. Specifically, dilution of micelles in blood lower than the critical micelle concentration leads to dissolution of the nanoparticle. It is critical that micellar drug delivery vehicles have high stability characterized by a low critical micelle concentration. This will ensure the integrity of the drug encapsulating micelle after dilution upon intravenous administration. Some strategies included incorporation of longer hydrophobic chains, use of aromatic monomers in hydrophobic region, as well as stabilizing additives such as cholesterol or cosurfactants [78,79]

Schiffelers et al. also developed and tested polymeric micelles with the key difference being the choice of polymer [68]. They applied PEG- b-poly (N-2-benzoyloxypropyl methacrylamide) (mPEG-b-p(HPMA-Bz) block copolymer as the micelle forming polymer. The nanoparticles were 55 nm in diameter and had an encapsulation efficiency of 88% for CFZ. Humanized bone marrow like scaffold xenograft model was used to present human multiple-myeloma cancer cells under the most accurate microenvironment possible. Biodistribution studies show significant accumulation of nanoparticles in the liver and spleen, not uncommon for similar nanoparticles [52]. Tumor growth assay showed little efficacy for unencapsulated CFZ and micelle encapsulated CFZ, demonstrating that this nanoformulation lacked the ability to overcome fundamental challenges with multiple myeloma tumor microenvironment.

Micelle compositions can include a variety of hydrophobic matrix material to increase the stability of the nanoparticle. Pluronic F127-based oil in water nanomicelle encapsulating CFZ were developed [69]. The nanoparticles were composed of Pluronic F127 amphiphilic block copolymer, sodium caprylate, ethyl butyrate, and CFZ. Nanomicelles were 7–20 nm in diameter and demonstrated steady release of CFZ in vitro. Cytotoxicity assays on Hela, MCF7, and Shy-SY5Y cancer cells showed F127 micellar CFZ to be more potent than free CFZ. In vivo studies were limited to biochemical and histopathological analysis, however, an increased safety profile for micellar CFZ compared to free CFZ was evident. To explain the high encapsulation efficiency of F127 CFZ micelles, computer simulations were developed. The simulations suggested that hydrogen bonding between CFZ and F127 stabilize the drug within the nanoparticle, explaining both the high encapsulation and steady-release kinetics.

Polymer chemistry allows for the incorporation of diverse functional groups for cancer specific targeting. Disulfide crosslinked micelles were synthesized for the delivery of CFZ to multiple myeloma via A6 peptide ligand as demonstrated in Figure 4 [70]. Glutathione (GSH) is a redox molecule commonly present in elevated levels in cancerous tissues. When a disulfide bond is exposed to excessive GSH, the disulfide bond will be reduced and break down into two separate sulfhydryl groups. The Zhang et al. micelle was built with poly (ethylene glycol)-b-poly(dithiolane trimethylene-co-ε-caprolactone) (PEG-P(DTC-co-CL)). DTC contains a disulfide bond that remains stable under ambient conditions. However, when exposed to elevated levels of GSH, the bond will reduce to two sulfhydryl, thereby destabilizing the core of the micelles and triggering release of CFZ. In vitro studies demonstrated burst release of CFZ in response to GSH with little release in buffered saline. Anti-tumor efficacy was evaluated in a xenograft tumor model. The in vivo study showed decreased tumor growth and increased survival in the mice treated with the A6 targeted micelle compared to all other experimental groups. However, there was no control for the utility of the disulfide bonds in this study, and it remains unclear if the GSH responsiveness of the micelles plays a role in therapeutic efficacy. Jun et al. synthesized CFZ loaded PLGA nanoparticles conjugated to quininic acid for targeting of peritumoral vasculture [71]. Nanoparticles were 200 nm in diameter and illustrated the enhanced stability of CFZ when exposed to whole blood and liver homogenates. An in vivo model with BALB/c mice with orthotopic 4T1 tumors demonstrated the efficacy of nanoparticle delivery. However, there was no improvement in tumor response when compared to the clinically available standard cyclodextrin CFZ formulation. Polymer chemistry allows for complex structures and possibilities for additional features and functionality. Reichel et al. took advantage of this with the development of tethered polymer nanoassemblies for delivery of CFZ [72]. They synthesized a complex polymer that is composed of a cationic polymer scaffold with both hydrophilic (PEG) and hydrophobic (polyethylene) polymers tethered through covalent bonds. The charged scaffolds were directly modified via capping of primary amines to reduce charge. Hydrophobic polymers served as the core of the nanoassembly and the site of CFZ encapsulation. Six combinations were synthesized with different combinations of cationic scaffold and amine capping. The highest encapsulation efficiency was seen in poly-L-lysine scaffold with charge fully reduced with acetic anhydride. These results suggest that positive charge discouraged CFZ entrapment within the polymer tether. In terms of drug release kinetics, each of the six polymer formulations were able to prevent a burst release of the drug. The slowest release kinetics were observed in the poly-L-lysine scaffolded polymer. This cationic scaffold held only primary amines were reacted thoroughly with charge reducing agents thereby making a more neutral core that more strongly bound hydrophobic CFZ. Cytotoxicity and proteasome assays confirmed that tethered polymer nanoassemblies delivered bioactive CFZ. There was no in vivo experiment performed and the safety and efficacy at an animal scale remains unknown.

Metastatic cancer is one of the biggest challenges in oncology. It can be difficult to not only identify but also eradicate metastatic tumors. Kang et al. developed a neutrophil mimicking poly glycolic lactic acid (PLGA) nanoparticle for the delivery of CFZ to premetastatic lesions [73]. Mouse neutrophils were collected and purified then exposed to lipopolysaccharide to induce inflammatory phenotype. Neutrophil-derived membrane vesicles (NMVs) were then isolated from homogenates of the activated neutrophils. CFZ loaded PLGA nanoparticles were mixed with NMV’s to form the final functionalized nanomedicine. A metastatic breast cancer model was used with 4T1 cells on Balb/C female nude mice. Metastasis occurred in the lungs of the mice. NMV functionalized nanoparticles were shown to decrease the metastatic burden on both focal lung metastatic tumors as well as circulating tumor cells. In vivo luminescence and infrared imaging showed that NMV coated nanoparticles collect at the site of metastatic lesion with minimal accumulation in other tissues.

Polymers serve as an excellent basis for complex multi component nanoparticle systems. For example, biomacromolecules can be used to functionalize nanoparticles. Ternary polypeptide nanoparticles for the encapsulation of cafilzomib were developed [74]. The core of their system is CFZ entrained in heptakis(6-amino-6-deoxy)-β-cyclodextrin(hepta-hydrochloride). Cyclodextrin inclusion complex formed a supramolecular assembly with azido-poly(ethylene glycol)-block-poly(L-glutamic acid sodium salt). Nanoparticles could be functionalized with Anti-EpCAM antibodies for targeting tumors. Nanoparticles complexes were roughly 50 nm in diameter and demonstrated steady drug release in vitro. Cellular uptake was confirmed with DLD-1 colon cancer cells. Cytotoxicity assays showed that ternary polypeptide nanoparticles loaded with CFZ were more potent than free CFZ. The same group also investigated the effects of various organic acids on drug loaded and drug release [75]. Citric acid and lactic acid were added to nanoparticle formulations under various ratios. Organic acids support small particle size as well as easy reconstitution from lyophilized solid. The highest drug retention rate was seen in 75% citric acid, 25% acid lactic acid formulation. There were minimal differences observed in drug loading. In a separate study, they also tried to improve their formulation by including amine substituted cyclodextrin [76]. They hypothesized that cationic polymers could neutralize charges within the nanoassembly. Cyclodextrin molecules would garner a positive charge from amine modification and polyglutamic acid polymer would yield a negative charge. They suspected that drug release could be controlled via pH due to alternative charges on polycationic polymers. They created formulations with Poly-L-Lysine (PLL), α-Ω-bis-(amino)-terminated poly(L-histidine) (PLH), and linear polyethyleneimine (PEI) and saw high entrapment efficiency with all groups. The differences came in drug release properties. Each cationic polymer demonstrated pH dependent release kinetics. Under physiological pH of 7.4, minimal release was observed. However, at pH 5, there was significant burst release, especially with the PLH formulation. Cytotoxicity assays on DLD-1 cells demonstrated comparable potent compared to free CF with PLH and PLL formulations with PEI being less potent. To date, no in vivo study on ternary polymer peptide complex encapsulating CFZ has been published.

Inorganic

Inorganic nanoparticles are key biomedical materials often studied for drug delivery [81–84]. A nickel-based metal organic framework (MOF) for the delivery of CFZ was synthesized [85]. The crystalline porous network was developed using a bottom-up hydrothermal synthesis of nickel sulfate, 4,4’-bipyridne, and pyridine. The final product is a highly porous nanoparticle 240 nm in diameter. A high drug encapsulation efficiency of 84.2% was observed and a steady in vitro release suggests the system avoids burst release of the drug. Cytotoxicity assays on a variety of cells lines demonstrate the biocompatibility of unloaded nickel MOF as well as the potency of CFZ loaded MOF. The in vivo studies were limited to the analysis of serum biomarkers and histopathology. The data suggest that the Nickle MOF are generally well tolerated but exhibit nephrotoxicity and hepatoxicity in a dose dependent manner.

Inorganic nanoparticles can possess a diverse array of chemical and physical properties as a function of composition, size, and morphology. For example, Figure 5 shows TiN nanoshells loaded with CFZ for dual phothermal and chemotherapy [80]. Intratumoral injection of CFZ loaded TiN nanoshells coupling with infrared light irradiation demonstrated superior tumor control to TiN nanoshells alone with irradiation and all other controls. Furthermore, in a cohort of animals receiving surgical excision and photothermal therapy, they observed a significantly decreased number of lung metastasis in mice treated with CFZ loaded Ti nanoshell photothermal therapy. Computer simulation is a common tool used in the design of drug delivery systems [86]. Molecular dynamics simulations can be used to study ZnO nanoparticles and functional units such as CFZ and oleic acid in various environments. The simulations suggested that in an aqueous environment, oleic acid will bind to the surface of the nanoparticle and the drug will tend to migrate from the solution to the lipids coating the particle. They also note that particle assemblies are stable in ethanol. In the same group’s next publication, they expanded their simulations and calculations to reactive molecular dynamics and density functional theory [87]. The new simulations suggest that CFZ can bind both oleic acid coating materials as well as bare ZnO. Specifically, they note that the carbonyl oxygens and epoxyketone groups on CFZ can adsorb to the (0001) surface of ZnO. The strength of this interaction suggests effective entrapment of the drug. Despite the progress on ZnO simulations, there are no experimental results published for such a system.

Nanocrystals

Nanomedicine for drug delivery typically applies a biocompatible delivery vehicle to encapsulate drugs. Nanocrystals shift the paradigm as the active agent becomes the bulk of the nanoparticle. Chemotherapy-based nanocrystals are solid particles composed of drug as well as stabilizing agents for improve colloidal stability and drug retention. Nanocrystals have similar benefits of typical nanoparticle-based drug delivery but with the added benefit of exceedingly high drug loading [88].

CFZ nanocrystals with an albumin protein coating have been synthesized [89]. The particles made by precipitating CFZ in a solution containing F127 amphiphile and then coated the resulting particle in human serum albumin. The coated nanocrystals had a rod-like morphology and a broad size distribution with an average length of 352 nm and a width of 58 nm. Nanocrystals were readily taken up by a variety of cancer cells and cytotoxicity is comparable to free CFZ. In vivo tumor model of murine breast cancer demonstrated improve tumor response in CFZ nanocrystals when compared to cyclodextrin stabilized CFZ. However, pharmacodynamic studies demonstrated the nanocrystals accumulated in the liver, lung, and spleen likely due to mononuclear phagocytic system. In response to undesirable biodistribution, efforts were made to optimize the size of nanocrystals to improve tumor accumulation and safety profile. Gadalla et al. synthesized albumin coated CFZ nanocrystals with an average diameter of 168 nm and width of 38 nm, significantly smaller than the first-generation formulation [90]. In vivo studies of the same model showed that the smaller nanocrystal had a significantly better tumor response. Critically, accumulation in the liver, lung, and spleen greatly diminishes and tumor accumulation increased.

A nanoformulation of CFZ comparable to a nanocrystal has been developed [91]. In lieu of having a core explicitly composed of CFZ they included tannic acid and Fe3+ to improve stability. The nanocapsule synthesis involved combining CFZ with tannic acid and Fe3+ in an ethanol/water mixture to precipitate nanoparticles. The tannic acid complexes with Fe3+ and polymerized to give more stability to the particles. The unmodified particles are then coated with albumin for improved pharmacological properties. The nanocapsules hydrodynamic diameter is 164 nm and yields a zeta potential of −27 mV. Nanocapsules lacking albumin coating exhibiting a burst release, while albumin coated particles had greater drug retention. Several in vivo models were investigated. The first was a standard single flank tumor models of murine melanoma cells B16F10 on immune competent C57BL/6 male mice. This model demonstrated that intratumorally injected albumin coated nanocapsule CFZ decreased tumor growth far more than cyclodextrin CFZ. A second model includes the introduction of a contralateral injection of B16F10 cells about 7 days after the first injection. The purpose of this study is to determine the effects on the growth of tumors remote to the intertumoral injection site. For the mice injected with albumin coated nanocapsules, distal tumor growth was delayed by 2 days when compared to saline control and cyclodextrin CFZ. This suggests the activation of systemic T-cell immunity, which they later support with immunophenotyping studies. Overall, this publication not only illustrates a nanoformulation of CFZ that can surpass clinically approve cyclodextrin formulation but also demonstrates the utility of CFZ in multimodal cancer therapy.

Conclusion

Cancer research has unveiled many targets for effective therapy with incredible in vitro and in vivo results. However, when scaling up to the complexity of a human being, many treatments fall short. Often the primary issue lies in the pharmacology of the treatment. CFZ is victim of the short comings of traditional chemotherapy with poor biodistribution and impassable toxicity. For treatments such CFZ to ever reach their clinical potential, more effective delivery mechanisms must be applied. Nanomedicine has emerged as a potential solution to the ailments of systemic cancer treatments including CFZ. As a necessity, it is critical that there is progress in this sector of research.

Future perspectives

CFZ requires more advanced nanomedicine preclinical trials to determine what formulations have the best potential to succeed in clinical trials and be adopted in clinical treatments. A variety of approaches have been taken so far including liposome, lipid nanoparticles, a variety of polymer formulations, inorganic framework, and nanocrystals. It is difficult to predict which of these has the greatest clinical potential; however, the studies do illuminate several key factors in CFZ nanomedicine. The first is the retention of drug within the nanoparticle. Premature and burst release is a common issue in nanoparticle mediated drug delivery. If too much drug leaks out prior to tumor uptake, the treatment is destined for failure. The second is the stability of CFZ in the nanoformulation. CFZ contains a reactive epoxy ketone group, and it is critical to limit exposure to reactive substrates during processing of the nanoformulation. The final key point is tumor delivery efficacy. A landmark meta-analysis in 2016 showed that on average, less than 1% of injected nanoparticles will reach to the tumor [92]. Therefore, it is important that every measure is taken to ensure maximum delivery to the tumor while minimizing to peripheral organs such as the liver. In terms of CFZ many steps have been taken including active targeting, immune cell membrane incorporation, and size optimization.

As previously stated, nanomedicine will be critical to widespread clinical adoption of CFZ-based therapies. However, resistance to single-drug treatment is a perpetual problem in chemotherapy and CFZ is no different [93,94]. A common method to combat resistance is combination therapy, which includes using multiple chemotherapeutic agents or multiple-cancer treatment modalities, including immunotherapy. Nanomedicine is a primary method for multimodal therapy as nanoparticles can be used as a vehicle of multiple agents at once. For example, CFZ can be combined with other drugs such as doxorubicin or vinblastine within the same liposome [61,62]. CFZ also possesses immunomodulatory properties for the potential of effective immunotherapies [39].
