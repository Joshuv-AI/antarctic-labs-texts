Effects of different acidifiers on the growth performance and diarrhea score of weaned piglets under commercial pig farm conditions
Authors: Mingfang Hu, Qing Gao, Wen Ren, Anping Li, Ming Huang, Liangqiang Che, Daiwen Chen, Aimin Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBenzoic acid (BA)-based acidifiers are widely applied in nursery diets; however, formulation can shape their functional outcomes on intake, host robustness, and gut microbial ecology. This study assessed a new-type benzoic acid (NBA) strategy under commercial farm conditions. A total of 210 21-day-old DLY (Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire) weaned piglets, with equal numbers of males and females and similar body weight (average 6.03 kg ± 0.01 kg), were randomly assigned to 5 groups with 6 replicates per group and 7 piglets per replicate. The control group was fed a basal diet, while the four experimental groups were fed the basal diet supplemented with 0.5% benzoic acid (BA), 0.3% new-type benzoic acid (NBA), 0.25% controlled-release-coated benzoic acid (CBA), or 0.5% compound acidifier (CA) powder, respectively. The trial lasted 42 days. The results showed that compared to the control group, the average daily feed intake (ADFI) from days 15 to 28 was significantly increased (P < 0.05) in the BA, NBA, and CA groups, indicating improved voluntary intake during the mid-nursery period. The diarrhea rate was significantly reduced (P < 0.05) during days 0–14 in the BA, NBA and CBA groups compared to the control group. Compared to the control group, serum total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) was significantly elevated (P < 0.05) in the BA, CBA, and CA groups, and catalase (CAT) levels increased significantly in the NBA group. White blood cell (WBC), lymphocyte (Lym), and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) increased significantly in the BA group compared to the control group, while MCHC levels decreased significantly in the NBA, CBA, and CA groups. Notably, compared with the control group, 0.5% BA, 0.3% NBA, and 0.25% CBA increased the richness and diversity of the intestinal microbiota. Specifically, these treatments promoted the growth of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus while inhibiting the proliferation of Escherichia coli. The results of this study indicate that adding 0.5% BA, 0.3% NBA, and 0.25% CBA all enhance piglets’ antioxidant capacity, reduce diarrhea incidence, and enhance gut microbial diversity. Among these, 0.5% BA and 0.25% CBA demonstrated superior efficacy compared to 0.3% NBA, offering potential economic benefits by improving feed efficiency, thereby enhancing overall production performance.

Introduction

The weaning stress syndrome in piglets frequently leads to intestinal dysfunction, increased incidence of diarrhea, and growth retardation, resulting in significant economic losses in the swine industry. Following the ban on antibiotics in feed, how to effectively alleviate this syndrome has become a major research focus in the industry. With the widespread application of acidifiers in livestock farming, benzoic acid (BA)—an organic acid additive—has increasingly attracted attention due to its significant broad-spectrum bacteriostatic and bactericidal properties (Ren et al. 2025). Additives such as organic acids, antimicrobial peptides, enzymes, and probiotics can all improve animal growth performance and immune function to varying degrees, thereby promoting animal health. Among these, organic acids, as a class of organic compounds with acidic characteristics, are widely available and serve as effective antibiotic alternatives (Ricke 2003).

BA is the simplest aromatic carboxylic acid. It is widely available and possesses broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. Furthermore, as a commonly used feed additive, BA can enhance animal growth performance by improving feed palatability and increasing feed intake. Therefore, adding benzoic acid to diets has become a common strategy in practical production to optimize piglet performance. Studies indicate that BA can promote growth performance in piglets, reduce the number of pathogenic bacteria such as intestinal Escherichia coli, and increase beneficial bacteria like intestinal Lactobacilli and Bifidobacteria (Zhai et al. 2017). However, other research shows that adding BA to diets has no significant effect on piglet growth performance (Øverland et al. 2007; Shu et al. 2016), and may even reduce the number of beneficial bacteria like intestinal Lactobacilli (Guggenbuhl et al. 2007). BA can improve intestinal morphology to enhance nutrient absorption and utilization in piglets (Choi and Kim 2024). It also modulates enzyme activity and redox status within piglets to optimize gut microbiota composition, thereby boosting immunity and reducing diarrhea incidence (Mao et al. 2019; Diether et al. 2022). BA exhibits potent inhibitory effects against fungi, yeasts, and bacteria in acidic environments. Its mechanism primarily involves disrupting cell membrane structure and function, depleting cellular energy, lowering intracellular pH, and suppressing specific enzyme production (Chipley 2020). Dietary supplementation with organic acids can alter microbial community composition, thereby regulating intestinal microbial homeostasis (Øverland et al. 2008). BA exhibits potent inhibitory effects against E. coli (Knarreborg et al. 2002). Research indicates that adding 5 g/kg BA to diets increases Bifidobacteria and Bacilli counts while reducing E. coli and Enterococcus counts in piglets’ intestines (Diao et al. 2014). Adding BA mixtures to diets similarly reduces E. coli counts and increases Lactobacilli counts in weaned piglets (Wang et al. 2021a). However, one study found that adding 0.5% BA to the diet had no effect on Lactobacillus counts in the cecum and ileum of weaned piglets, while significantly increasing microbial diversity in the ileum. Furthermore, Lactobacillus, E. coli, and Enterococcus counts were higher in cecal contents than in ileal contents (Torrallardona et al. 2007). Compared to conventional BA, controlled-release BA exhibits higher gastric bypass rates in both in vivo and in vitro studies and enables sustained-release in the hindgut (Yousaf et al. 2017). In experiments by Morel et al. (2019), a comparative study using CBA versus conventional BA in the diets of growing pigs weighing 30–60 kg demonstrated that CBA significantly improved ADG, feed intake, and F/G.

BA is predominantly absorbed in the upper gastrointestinal tract (stomach and proximal small intestine), with over 90% entering systemic circulation to be metabolized into hippuric acid and excreted. Consequently, its concentration in the hindgut remains low, limiting its modulatory effect on the colonic microbiota—a key determinant of intestinal health (Ren et al. 2025). To overcome this limitation, industry efforts are increasingly directed toward developing engineered BA formulations, such as coated slow-release and composite preparations. These innovations aim to achieve targeted hindgut delivery, thereby enhancing efficacy, precision, and safety—a defining trend in modern feed additive development (He et al. 2025). Currently, there is limited comparative research within the industry on the application of conventional BA formulations versus controlled-release CBA formulations in weaned piglet production. This study systematically compared the effects of different BA formulations added to diets on growth performance, diarrhea incidence, serum biochemical parameters, and gut microbiota in weaned piglets. The aim is to provide data-driven evidence for the efficient and precise application of different benzoic acid formulations in weaned piglet production.

Materials and methods

All animal experiments in this study were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan Agricultural University (Chengdu, China, No.:20210111).

Animals, treatments, and experiment design

The experiment selected 210 healthy Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire (DLY) weaned piglets (21 days old), which were randomly divided into 5 groups balanced for sex and initial body weight (average weight 6.03 kg ± 0.01 kg). Each group had 6 replicates, with 7 piglets per replicate. The control group received a basal diet, while the four treatment groups were fed the basal diet supplemented with the following additives at conventional recommended dosages: 0.5% conventional benzoic acid (BA; 99% purity), 0.3% novel benzoic acid (NBA; 97% BA and 3% permeabilizing agent to enhance antimicrobial activity by increasing pathogen cell membrane permeability), 0.25% controlled-release coated benzoic acid (CBA; 97% BA with a specialized coating for targeted hindgut delivery), and 0.5% compound acidifier (CA). The basal diet was formulated according to NRC (National Research Council 2012) as a corn-soybean meal diet, with its composition and nutritional levels shown in Table 1. The experimental diets were supplied as meal feed throughout the trial without any antibiotic growth promoters or other drugs. The experimental period lasted 42 days.

Feeding management

The experiment was conducted at Jiangwan Pasture in Ebian Yi Autonomous County, Leshan City, Sichuan Province. Three pig pens were set up, each containing 10 individual pens with 7 piglets in each pen. Prior to the experiment, the pig pens underwent thorough disinfection. During the trial period, the pig houses were kept well-ventilated with temperatures maintained between 26–28 °C. Daily cleaning and disinfection of the pig pens were performed. Throughout the entire experimental period, all piglets had ad libitum access to feed and water. To maintain feed freshness and ensure an uninterrupted supply, fresh feed was added to the rectangular four-way troughs three times daily (at 8:00, 14:00, and 20:00). During these times, staff observed the feeding behavior and ensured that the residual feed never fell below 1/4 of the trough capacity. Nipple drinkers were used for the water supply. If any piglets died from disease or were culled during the trial, their weights and feed consumption were accurately recorded. All other daily management followed standard commercial operational procedures.

Determination indicators and methods

Growth performance assessment

All piglets were weighed on an empty stomach at 8:00 am on days 0, 15, 29 and 43 at the beginning of the experiment, and the weights of additions and residuals were recorded to determine the piglets’ productive performance throughout the period, including ADG, ADFI and F/G.

Diarrhea rate

Piglets were observed and scored for defecation at 9:00 am and 15:00 pm every day during the trial (Table 2).

Serum biochemical marker assay

On the morning of Day 42 of the experiment, one piglet was randomly selected from each replicate. After a 12-hour fast, 10 mL of blood was collected from the anterior vena cava. One milliliter of anticoagulated blood (using heparin sodium vacuum tubes for the whole blood analyzer) was taken, and the remaining whole blood was placed in standard vacuum tubes. Centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 15 minutes yielded serum, which was aliquoted and stored at −20 °C for analysis. Serum levels of GSH, T-SOD, CAT, MDA, and T-AOC were measured according to the instructions of the corresponding kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute. A fully automated hematology analyzer (Hitachi, Japan) was used to measure WBC, RBC, Lym, HGB, MCH, HCT, MCV, and MCHC.

Gut microbiota analysis

Piglet feces samples were sent to Chengdu Luoning Biotechnology Co., Ltd. for microbial 16S rRNA genomic detection and analysis. Sample DNA was extracted and amplified via PCR using 16S V3-V4 region primers (338F-806R). Following pooling and purification, libraries were generated and sequenced. Subsequent analysis involved filtering raw sequence tags using the QIIME pipeline (Version 1.9.1) and identifying/removing chimeric sequences via the UCHIME algorithm. Representative sequences were classified against the SILVA database using the RDP Classifier (Version 2.2). Processed sequence data were then archived in the Sequence Read Archive (SRA). Sequences with ≥ 97% similarity were clustered into the same operational taxonomic unit (OTU).

Data statistics and analysis

Experimental data were organized and analyzed using Excel for statistical convenience. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 27 software, employing one-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA) followed by Duncan-Waller multiple comparison tests. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (mean ± SEM). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software.

Alpha diversity indices are presented in Fig. 2B. The Chao 1 and Observed species indices reflect community richness, with higher values indicating greater species diversity. Compared with the control group, the BA group showed slight decreases in Chao 1 and PD whole tree indices, while Simpson and Shannon indices remained largely unchanged. Both the NBA and CA groups exhibited declining trends across all four indices, whereas the CBA group showed increases in all four indices (see Fig. 2B). Among these, the Simpson index exhibited significant differences in the BA, CA, and NBA groups, while differences in the other indices were not statistically significant. Beta diversity analysis based on the partial least squares discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) model revealed that samples tended to cluster internally within groups while showing significant separation between groups, indicating substantial differences in gut microbiota composition among the five groups (Fig. 2C).

The phylum-level microbial composition is shown in Fig. 2D. Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were dominate phyla in the piglet gut microbiota. Compared with the CON group, the relative abundance of Firmicutes increased in the NBA, CBA, and CA groups, whereas the BA group did not exhibit this trend. Notably, the NBA group had the lowest relative abundances of Bacteroidetes and Proteobacteria among all five groups. The genus-level differential microbiota analysis is presented in Fig. 2E. Relative to the CON group, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus showed an increasing trend in all treatment groups, whereas the relative abundance of Prevotella 9 exhibited a downward trend in the BA, NBA, and CBA groups. Interestingly, the relative abundance of the Prevotellaceae NK3B31 showed a downward trend only in the CA group. In summary, dietary supplementation with 0.5% BA, 0.3% NBA, and 0.25% CBA significantly improved the diversity composition of the intestinal microbiota in weaned piglets and increased the relative abundance of the beneficial Lactobacillus.

Discussion

Organic acid formulations—particularly those based on BA—exhibit potent feed-acidifying effects by lowering dietary pH and acid-binding capacity. In addition, these agents possess antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, contribute to energy metabolism, reduce viral loads, and mitigate oxidative stress (Yin and Huang 2022). Benzoic acid, a widely used acidifier, exerts its antibacterial effects primarily in the stomach and the anterior small intestine(Choi and Kim 2024). It is rapidly absorbed into the bloodstream by animals and conjugates with glycine in the liver to form hippuric acid, which is subsequently excreted in urine (De Azevedo et al. 2025). This rapid absorption and hepatic conjugation result in low BA concentrations in the hindgut, thereby limiting its impact on the complex microbial community that predominantly colonizes this region—a community whose health is equally critical to overall animal well-being (Kim et al. 2025). Conventional BA is extensively used due to its potent antibacterial effects and well-documented growth-promoting benefits (Weng and Ma 2005; Nie et al. 2015; Del Olmo et al. 2017), particularly in piglets and young pigs. However, its restricted site of action and potential adverse effects—including reduced palatability leading to decreased feed intake and gastrointestinal mucosal irritation at high dosages—represent drawbacks. Consequently, the industry is evolving toward upgrading BA products through technological innovations such as encapsulation for sustained release and compound formulations, aiming for greater efficacy, precision, and safety. This approach is becoming the mainstream trend in feed additive research and application. In practical production settings, the selection of an appropriate formulation should be based on a comprehensive assessment of specific production stages, health challenges, and expected outcomes.

Although BA is widely applied, its growth-promoting effects in weaned piglets remain inconsistent across studies. Zhai et al. (2017) reported that 0.3% and 0.5% BA increased ADG and ADFI in a dose-dependent manner; however, improvements in the F/G were observed only during days 0–14. Kluge et al. (2006) also observed that 0.5% and 1.0% BA increased ADFI and body weight, yet no clear dose-response relationship was evident. In our study, the supplementation of 0.5% BA significantly enhanced the ADFI from days 15 to 28 and improved the F/G from days 29 to 42, demonstrating a distinct advantage in feed utilization during the later stages. This benefit is likely attributable to the acidification of digesta by BA, which enhances nutrient digestibility and maintains intestinal integrity (Kluge et al. 2006; Diao et al. 2013). Overall, the discrepancies among studies may reflect differences in experimental conditions, basal diet composition, and the dosage or method of BA supplementation.

Weaning stress is frequently accompanied by the overproduction of ROS, leading to oxidative damage of cellular macromolecules and subsequent performance deficits (Anikó et al. 2007; Qin et al. 2015). Although BA is widely recognized for its intestinal benefits, literature regarding its systemic antioxidant efficacy remains equivocal. For instance, while Diao et al. (2016) reported enhanced mucosal antioxidant enzyme activities in piglets, systemic responses in other animal models have proven negligible (Dai et al. 2018). In the current evaluation, dietary supplementation with 0.5% BA, 0.25% CBA, and 0.5% CA significantly amplified serum T-AOC. Furthermore, the 0.3% NBA formulation uniquely elevated serum CAT levels, despite the absence of significant alterations in T-SOD, GSH, or MDA across treatments. This targeted enhancement of the serum antioxidant profile suggests a robust capacity of these acidifiers to scavenge excessive ROS and alleviate cellular oxidative stress. The reduction in systemic oxidative burden likely conserves metabolic energy for growth and maintenance, which is consistent with the improved F/G observed in the 0.3% NBA group. These findings underscore that while BA inherently possesses antioxidant-promoting properties, its systemic availability and functional efficacy are heavily dictated by formulation technology and applied dosage.

Post-weaning diarrhea represents a critical bottleneck in swine production, frequently driven by environmental and dietary shifts that compromise intestinal barrier integrity and disrupt microbial homeostasis (Wu et al. 2015). Although extensive research confirms that dietary supplementation with BA effectively alleviates diarrhea severity in piglets (Papatsiros et al. 2011; Pu et al. 2020; Wen et al. 2021), the efficacy of BA-based compounds remains debated, particularly when employed as alternatives to pharmacological zinc oxide (Diao et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2021b). Our current data reveal that both 0.5% BA and 0.25% CBA successfully mitigated diarrhea incidence during the highly susceptible 0–14 d post-weaning window. Importantly, the 0.25% CBA formulation extended this protective benefit across the entire 42-day experimental period, achieving clinical efficacy comparable to that of the higher-dose conventional uncoated BA. This sustained improvement in intestinal health is plausibly driven by the capacity of BA to modulate luminal pH and restrict pathogenic bacterial overgrowth, thereby stabilizing the gut microecology (Murphy et al. 2011).

Intestinal microbial populations are closely linked to gut health and the physiological resilience of weaned piglets (Isusi et al. 2025). Although previous studies have shown that 0.5% BA enhances ileal microbial diversity (Torrallardona et al. 2007), our α-diversity analysis revealed that a lower dosage of 0.25% CBA significantly enriched the gut microbial community. Furthermore, β-diversity profiling confirmed that BA supplementation fundamentally altered the overall microbial architecture. The modulatory effect of BA on gut microflora is largely attributed to its undissociated form in the gastrointestinal tract, which penetrates bacterial membranes to inhibit critical metabolic enzymes such as α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase (Bridges et al. 1970; Giannenas et al. 2014). Taxonomic profiling further substantiated these ecological shifts. At the phylum level, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes constituted the dominant microbiota. The Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio serves as a recognized biomarker of gastrointestinal homeostasis (Cheng et al. 2017). Given that weaning diarrhea is frequently characterized by a depletion of Firmicutes (Yang et al. 2017), the increased relative abundance of this phylum observed in the present trial provides a compelling microbiological basis for the significantly curtailed diarrhea incidence in the 0.25% CBA group (Gong et al. 2021). Concurrently, treatments utilizing 0.5% BA, 0.3% NBA, and 0.25% CBA displayed a collective trend toward suppressing Proteobacteria. This phylum includes major enteropathogens such as E. coli and Salmonella, whose overrepresentation typically triggers mucosal inflammation (Friedman et al. 2003; Shin et al. 2015). Ultimately, these findings demonstrate that the application of these targeted BA formulations effectively restricts pathogenic proliferation while fostering a eugenic microbial environment, thereby preserving intestinal integrity and supporting overall host health.

Conclusion

Under the experimental conditions, adding 0.5% BA, 0.3% NBA, and 0.25% CBA to the diet significantly enhanced the antioxidant capacity of piglets, improved their feed intake, and reduced diarrhea incidence. Both conventional and CBA demonstrated superior efficacy in enhancing antioxidant capacity compared to the NBA. CBA simultaneously exhibited significant inhibitory effects on E. coli and promoted the growth and proliferation of Lactobacillus.
