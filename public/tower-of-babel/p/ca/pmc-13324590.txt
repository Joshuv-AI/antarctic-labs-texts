Evidence-Based Strategies for Addressing Cancer- and Treatment-Related Cognitive Impairment: A Review
Authors: Suqin Wu, Yan Li, Xue Yan Wang, Huifeng Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) has become a notable long-term consequence for cancer survivors, especially among those receiving chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or targeted treatments. Despite the advances in the treatment of cancer, CRCI still disrupts the quality of life of patients, daily activities, and work performance, as well as emotional well-being. This review provides a summary of the existing information on the neurobiological basis of CRCI, including neuroinflammation, disturbed neuroplasticity, oxidative stress, and tumour-related factors. It analyses diagnostic methods, from cognitive screening to high-level neuroimaging, and reviews evidence-based interventions that play a significant role in providing highly disciplined, personalized care. The ongoing constraints on advancing machine learning-based CRCI interventions include persistent obstacles, a lack of long-term trials, and difficulties accessing data and achieving interpretability, among others. Other important risk factors are also identified in this review, as are the performance of different predictive models with respect to accuracy, generalization ability, and clinical utility. Moreover, it addresses the challenges of validating these models and biomarkers across different populations, as well as the need for further studies to enhance their validity and enable personalized interventions.

INTRODUCTION

Cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI), also commonly referred to as chemobrain, is a neurocognitive complication that is generally correlated with cancer therapies, especially chemotherapy, and is a major clinical problem across the globe (Kim et al., 2025). One of the most widely used modes of treatment of cancer is chemotherapy, which may have serious side effects on the central nervous system, whether acting alone or in combination with other treatment agents (Pace et al., 2024). GLOBOCAN 2020 reported on 19.3 million new cancer cases, including breast, lung, leukaemia, lymphoma, colorectal, ovarian, prostate, and testicular cancer globally, and this has become the focus of both clinical and research interest (Cao et al., 2024). CRCI is a disorder first described in the mid-20th century, as a mild to moderate disorder with symptoms including lapsing of memory, impaired executive functioning, difficulty concentrating, attention deficits and slower speed. Such cognitive disturbances usually last for long periods of time, often several months or even years after radiation therapy ends. Therefore, CRCI broadly disrupts cognitive abilities, impairing a patient’s capacity to continue daily-life activities (Toledano et al., 2025).

The pathogenesis of CRCI is dependent on different factors, including immediate effects of cancer, systemic interventions, co-morbidities, and co-occurring treatments (Ditchfield et al., 2025). Moreover, cytotoxic agents, which are associated with neuroinflammation, can also affect the central nervous system, hamper neurotransmitter homeostasis, impair neurogenesis, and disrupt the blood-brain barrier (Ho et al., 2025). Systemic therapy has been discovered to induce cognitive deficit in patients far more commonly than any of the chemotherapy treatments; the nature and accumulated dose of the chemotherapy are additional factors in the amount of impairment. The combination of prevalence rates and one-year post-cancer treatment types with objective assessment of CRCI ranges from 21% to 35%, with no time effect (Ni et al, 2025). The results suggest the uniformity of the long-term influences of the treatment of systemic cancer on cognitive ability.

The prevalence of CRCI varies substantially, with reported rates ranging from 15% to 75%, mainly due to differences in assessment methods and inconsistent diagnostic criteria across studies (Hu et al., 2025). Cancer is the second leading cause of death in the U.S., especially in people aged 60-79 years. Age is also a significant factor in the cognitive decline, which is more pronounced in the elderly who are affected by cancer survivors of all ethnicities (Albers et al., 2024). Besides, the younger cancer survivors (both men and women) tend to deal with their own challenges, such as work stress and anxiety, sleep disorders and fatigue, which lead to worsening cognitive dysfunction (Chan et al., 2024). The emerging cases of cancer survivors, especially in the older population, highlight the fact that more emphasis should be given to CRCI as a vital element in the post-cancer management process, and that there is a need to thoroughly monitor and provide personalized interventions to reduce cognitive impairment in at-risk populations (Lange et al., 2019).

Furthermore, cancer chemotherapy patients experience adverse reactions, including cognitive impairment (Zhou et al., 2024). Chemotherapeutic drugs can disrupt cognition through direct neurotoxic effects on neurons and supporting cells, as well as indirect mechanisms, such as increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines, causing DNA damage, and accelerating reductions in frontal lobe and hippocampal white and grey matter. However, emerging literature indicates that other cancer therapies can also lead to CRCI through distinct mechanisms, such as hormone therapy, targeted therapy, and radiation therapy (Yang and Von Ah, 2024). Hormone therapy, particularly anti-oestrogen treatment like aromatase inhibitors, may raise CRCI risk by disrupting oestrogen’s role in supporting neuronal growth, maintenance, and synapse development related to memory. After radiation therapy, theorists have proposed that immune system dysregulation may induce chronic oxidative stress and elevate inflammatory processes in the brain, leading to cognitive impairment (Sahu and Tiwari, 2025).

The aetiology of CRCI is multifactorial, with contributions from treatment-related mechanisms and patient-specific vulnerabilities. Genetic influences- especially APOE polymorphisms - could increase the susceptibility. In contrast, modifiable factors such as older age, psychological distress, and comorbid systemic conditions (e.g., anaemia, metabolic syndrome) combine to lower neurobiological resilience (Caceres et al., 2025). To diagnose CRCI, patients’ subjective self-reporting, traditional neuropsychological testing, and more technologically advanced neuroimaging, including functional MRI and positron emission tomography, play an important role in identifying changes in brain connectivity and metabolism. There is also clinical validation of the diagnostic status of high and low levels of inflammatory cytokines and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (Kesler et al., 2023).

Clinically, CRCI presents with a wide range of symptom severity and duration. Some individuals experience only short-term, mild cognitive symptoms, while others develop persistent and worsening cognitive impairment. Common symptoms include memory lapses, difficulty concentrating, slowed information processing, and challenges with executive functions. These symptoms frequently interrelate with those seen in fatigue, stress, anxiety, and impaired multitasking abilities, making differentiation and management challenging (Wang et al., 2024). Treatment strategies for CRCI utilize multidisciplinary approaches such as cognitive rehabilitation programs, pharmacological methods (methylphenidate, modafinil, donepezil), and maintaining a daily life routine (Gan et al., 2025).

The adverse effects of CRCI on patients’ quality of life include autonomy, functional independence, social relationships, and treatment adherence. This review explores the need for more targeted research into CRCI interventions. While some interventions have shown promise, there is still much to learn about their long-term effectiveness, the optimal timing of their implementation, and how best to integrate them into standard oncology care. Moreover, future research should explore the mechanisms underlying CRCI in greater depth, as a better understanding of these biological and psychological processes could lead to more effective treatments.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGICAL FOUNDATIONS OF CRCI

Neurophysiological mechanisms

Chemotherapy can disrupt cerebral metabolism and neuronal function by damaging white matter integrity in the prefrontal cortex. Neurological morphological injuries include abnormal dendritic spine deformations, reduced hippocampal volume, and changes in the structure and density of the anatomy (Oliva et al., 2024).

Neurotransmission is slowed in patients due to the loss of white matter and oligodendrocytes (Shelton et al., 2025). Studies have shown that both doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide cause behavioural defects, but cyclophosphamide is the primary driver of microglia-induced inflammation. In contrast, doxorubicin contributes less to this inflammatory process. Activated hippocampal microglia contribute to the memory deficits observed in rats, indicating that chemotherapy-induced neuroinflammation is significant (Das et al., 2020). Immunofluorescence analyses demonstrated that both cyclophosphamide and doxorubicin reduce neurogenesis by 90%, but do not differentiate between the magnitudes of their effects. Cyclophosphamide has been specifically investigated for its impact on passive avoidance learning, both independently and in combination with doxorubicin, whereas doxorubicin’s cognitive effects have been characterized primarily when used alongside other agents. Similarly, thiotepa, another chemotherapeutic agent, acts as a neurogenesis inhibitor and impairs memory-related performance (Li and Caeyenberghs, 2018). Finally, activation of the p38 Mitogen-activated Protein Kinase pathway and increased IL-6 levels in mice further support the role of chemotherapy, particularly cyclophosphamide, in inflammation and systemic effects (Fig. 1) (Gamal et al., 2024).

Platinum-based chemotherapy agents remain central to the treatment of several solid tumours, including those of the lung, ovary, and testes. Historically, it was thought that the platinum-based substances were incapable of crossing the blood-brain barrier (BBB) (Zhang et al., 2022). Nevertheless, studies have shown that it crosses the BBB in low dosages, and some of the most vulnerable neural populations include progenitor cells, oligodendrocytes, and hippocampus neurons (Parveen, 2022). It also contributes to mitochondrial damage of DNA; mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress. There is eventual impairment in neuronal integrity and cognitive functioning (Fig. 1) (Liang et al., 2025).

Antitumor 5-Fu disrupts the integrity of the myelin sheath and is associated with neurodegeneration and mitochondrial dysfunction in the hippocampus. The prolonged presence of rodents on cyclophosphamide or doxorubicin impairs spatial and episodic memory and neurogenesis in the hippocampus (Chen et al., 2022). Intrathecal methotrexate and cytarabine in mice with acute lymphoblastic leukaemia selectively affect dendritic architecture and hippocampus-dependent cognitive functions (John et al., 2021). Methotrexate is also linked to cognitive impairment, leading to disruption of the oligodendrocyte compartment and white matter integrity (Yang et al., 2025). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that chemotherapy drugs contribute to neuronal damage and functional impairment.

Biological pathways in cognitive decline

Novel biomarkers of cancer-related cognitive impairment: Genetic polymorphisms such as apolipoprotein E (APOE), catechol-O-methyltransferase (COMT), and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) have been associated with cognitive vulnerability (Gerra et al., 2024). The APOE ε4 allele, while strongly linked to Alzheimer’s disease, appears to increase susceptibility to CRCI rather than serving as a disease-specific marker (Boschetti et al., 2025). Similarly, the COMT Val158Met polymorphism influences dopaminergic signaling in the prefrontal cortex, where the Val allele is associated with reduced dopamine availability and greater risk of treatment-related cognitive dysfunction (Chan et al., 2023). Furthermore, variations in BDNF, particularly the Val66Met polymorphism, may further affect neuroplasticity, although evidence in CRCI remains limited (Bonetti et al., 2021). Overall, these genetic factors are best regarded as indicators of predisposition.

In parallel, blood-based biomarkers offer a practical approach for capturing dynamic biological changes associated with CRCI. Circulating markers such as neurofilament light chain, amyloid-β (Aβ), and tau reflect neuronal injury, whereas inflammatory mediators and C-reactive protein indicate chemotherapy-induced neuroinflammatory responses (Dong et al., 2025). In addition, routinely measured parameters such as hemoglobin, platelet indices, and electrolyte levels have been linked to cognitive outcomes and may support large-scale screening due to their accessibility (Halicki et al., 2023). Despite their clinical potential, these markers lack specificity for CRCI, and no single biomarker currently provides reliable diagnostic accuracy. Consequently, integrating genetic susceptibility with circulating indicators may offer a more comprehensive approach to understanding and monitoring CRCI (Oppegaard et al., 2022).

Role of cytokines: During chemotherapy, chemotherapeutic agents increase the secretion and production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as small proteins that help regulate immune responses), including IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in stimulated peripheral macrophages, which are immune cells. Doxorubicin induces cytokine-mediated inflammatory cascades by activating the MAPK/p38 and c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) signaling pathways, leading to the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the release of active IL-1β from its inactive precursor, proIL-1β (Wang et al., 2021). The presence of IL-1β in the hippocampus interferes with the brain’s ability to process place-specific memories. Overproduction of IL-1β is also reported to affect neuron development in the hippocampal area. Neurotoxic symptoms are triggered by the depletion of the proliferating cells in the hippocampus (Singh et al., 2025).

Recent reports indicate that IL-12 influences neurogenesis by inducing engagement of the kynurenine pathway via up-regulation of important enzymes, including IDO (Indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase), KMO (kynurenine-3-monooxygenase), and kynureninase. With IL-1β, the microglial cells transform tryptophan to glutamate (NMDA) receptor agonist end product quinolinic acid (QA) (Mollaei et al., 2021). Neurotoxicity is further augmented by elevated levels of QA and NMDA receptor activity. Certain studies regarding depressive conditions have linked the reduction of one of the NMDA antagonists, kynurenic acid, which is the result of astrocyte loss (Sumneang et al., 2023).

Emerging evidence supports that IL-10 may have context-dependent effects on neurocognitive outcomes, especially by modulating microglial features (Garcia et al., 2017). In Alzheimer’s disease (AD) models, IL-10 has also been shown to inhibit microglial activation and impair amyloid-beta (Aβ) clearance, which stimulates cognitive decline (Bai et al., 2018). Consistent with this, IL-10 genetic ablation in APP/PS1 mice led to a significant reduction in the burden of the amyloid plaques, not only in the cortex, but also in the hippocampus, plus a significant reduction in the brain plaques called cerebral amyloid angiopathy (Ramadan et al., 2022). Furthermore, this IL-10 dual role may be extended to the cognitive impairment associated with chemotherapy (CRCI), where overactive anti-inflammatory signalling may impair microglial functional activities, synaptic homeostasis, and impair the brain repair mechanisms (Bagnall-Moreau et al., 2019).

One other important pro-inflammatory cytokine is IL-6. In vitro analysis reveals a direct relationship between IL-6 in blood, as well as between these cytokines, TNF-alpha and the level of sickness and neuroendocrine stimulation in the brain. It resulted in impaired learning mediated by the hippocampus and in its volume shrinkage, due to the IL-6 and TNF-alpha used in the clinical trial of cancer survivors (Bourgognon and Cavanagh, 2020). Though pro-inflammatory mediators in the nervous system are harmful, adequate levels of these mediators are essential for normal neuronal function. Illustrated in preclinical experiments using rodent models of IL-6 and TNF knockout, a deficiency of either cytokine can influence deteriorating hippocampal activity (Dos Santos et al., 2021). These findings highlight the importance of maintaining a balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines for normal neuronal function.

Role of oxidative stress: The brain’s high oxygen use makes it more prone to oxidative and nitrosative damage than other organs. This happens because the balance between the brain’s pro- and anti-oxidant systems breaks down, leading to more oxidative stress markers (ROS and RNS) (Teleanu et al., 2022) (Fig. 1). Local cytokines can trigger the NOS pathway, causing oxidative stress and DNA damage. When toxic free radicals rise beyond what the brain’s antioxidants can manage, tissue damage starts. This harms lipid membranes, changes and breaks down proteins, and leads to neurological disorders (Fleming et al., 2023).

The cognitive impairment process evolves based on the loss of neurons of the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus. About 40% of cancer chemotherapeutic drugs that are used clinically are known to cause oxidative stress, which leads to cellular damage with subsequent toxicities associated with treatment. The harmful incidents of this kind especially target neuronal cells located in the cerebral cortex, as well as oligodendrocytes (Binarelli et al., 2023). Oxidative stress disrupts mitochondrial calcium exocytosis and ionic balance, resulting in the loss of neuronal and synaptic plasticity. The ensuing influx of calcium depolarizes membranes and restricts DNA, thereby activating the caspase-3-mediated apoptotic process (Rick et al., 2024). Furthermore, cytosolic accumulation of ROS activates caspase-1, mediated via the NLRP3 inflammasome, which also influences mitochondrial apoptosis pathways. Therefore, oxidative stress and associated neuroinflammation can be considered key factors in the neurotoxicity of brain structures and functions that ultimately lead to cognitive impairment (Fig. 2) (Dash et al., 2025).

Comorbid factors

Patients with cancer often experience significant neuropsychological symptoms. These symptoms may result from cancer itself or from antineoplastic treatments (Xie et al., 2023). Neuropsychological effects impairing cognitive capacity are often worsened by comorbidities common in cancer patients. These comorbidities include: 1) fatigue and sleep disturbances, 2) mood disorders, 3) neuropathic pain, and 4) endocrine and nutritional dysfunction symptoms (Strander et al., 2025).

Orszaghova et al. (2021) reported that patients’ level of education and socioeconomic status might be factors that shape cognitive reserve. Nevertheless, their attitudes toward cancer treatment and the psychological changes that occur due to cognitive decline are also affected by these factors. Further, in a study cohort of survivors of testicular cancer (N=72), premorbid intellectual functioning as measured by IQ was found to be a statistically significant predictor of cognitive impairment.The IQ was detected to be lower in relation to the risk of cognitive impairment (OR=0.87; 95% CI: 0.810.95; p<0.01).

Pendergrass et al. (2018) demonstrated that in lymphoma survivors, fatigue and anxiety were linked to self-perceived cognitive difficulties, whereas pain was associated with poorer performance on objective cognitive measures. Subsequently, these findings underscore the significant role of fatigue in shaping patient experience and its broader impact on quality of life in cancer patients (Krolak et al., 2017).

CRCI SCREENING AND EVALUATION

The initial and most crucial step before treating any condition is diagnosing the disease or infection, which enables timely intervention and more effective treatment. Depending on several factors, including the age of the patient, the health professional (train nurse or neuropsychologist) that is available, and the time needed to conduct the assessment, the choice of the test depends on these factors (Simeoni et al., 2025). To diagnose the CRCI, the individual must be in a position to have pre-test availability on whether the individual had any symptoms prior to CT and their interconnection with other symptoms such as depression, anxiety, pain, fatigue or insomnia. Besides this, the blood tests and imaging might also be useful to exclude other potential causes that might lead to cognitive deterioration, such as dementia, hypothyroidism, vitamin deficiencies, chronic infections, and cardiopulmonary deficiencies (Luo et al., 2023).

Several tests may be used to identify cognitive dysfunction when CT is being used. An effort to standardize the diagnostic approach is underway, as the tests used to determine CRCI are heterogeneous across studies. The cognition screening tests included in the geriatric population, which were believed to have dementia the most, are the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and are only now being applied to other populations. The International Cancer and Cognition Task Force (ICCTF) identifies the impaired cognitive domains that need to be evaluated with the help of neuropsychological tests (Baghdadli et al., 2023).

To assess learning and memory, processing speed, and executive function, the ICCTF also suggests the Hopkins Verbal Learning test- revised (HVLT-R) and the Trial making test, as well as the controlled oral word association test of the Multilingual Aphasia Examination. ICCTF recommends the following criteria to be applied in defining cognitive impairment: 2 or more test scores that are lower than the normal mean (or an applicationally relevant control group) by 1.5 standard deviations or 1 test score that is lower than the normal mean (or an applicationally relevant control group) by 2.0 standard deviations. Cognitive impairment is considered to be a score of two or more tests on the above tests can be at or below the normative mean (or control group) or one of the tests can be at or below -2.0 SDs (Zhang et al., 2025).

Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy- Cognition (FA CT-Cog, version 3) is a 37-item self-report measure intended to reflect cognitive issues in cancer survivors in the domains of physical, social, emotional, and functional well-being. The scale has four subscales, each of which has 20, 9, 4 and 4 items, respectively, namely Perceived Cognitive Impairment (CogPCI; 20 items), Perceived Cognitive Ability (CogPCA; 9 items), Comments from others about Cognitive functioning (CogOth; 4 items) and Effect of Cognitive problems on quality of life (CogQoL; 4 items). An overall score (between 0 and 148) is obtained by the sum of all the responses to the items; the higher the score, the better his or her perceived cognitive functioning. Even though standardized cut-off scores have not yet been standardized, one study of 133 breast cancer survivors showed that an 18-item PCI sub-scale score of less than 54 (76% sensitivity, 82% specificity) and a 20-item PCI score of less than 60 (76-84% sensitivity and specificity, respectively) could be a sign of clinically significant cognitive impairment (Hajj et al., 2022).

In terms of diagnosis, neuroimaging has reported changes in the central nervous system, both structural and functional, after chemotherapy. Such alterations as shifts in the grey matter volume and density, white matter microstructure damage, diminished neural activity, and defects in network connectivity have been linked to poorer performances in neuropsychological tasks. However, such studies also revealed regions of hyperactivation and hyperconnectivity that can be considered as compensatory, which is an interesting fact and can cause an individual to be interested and occupied. Integration of clinical data, cognitive testing, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is an interesting sphere of research with potential to contribute to the diagnosis of CRCI. According to neuroimaging, the frontal, parietal, and temporal regions become less dense in grey matter among cancer patients (Yang et al., 2018).

In contrast to structural studies, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) not only measures the activity of the brain when performing a cognitive activity but also demonstrates dynamic deficit patterns (Williams and Henson, 2018). Mhanna et al. (2025) conducted a study that revealed consistent structural and functional alterations in the brain, including reduced activation of frontoparietal networks during executive functioning, attention, and working memory in cancer survivors after chemotherapy. Surprisingly, even those changes were found in other patients who never underwent chemotherapy, implying that brain functional modifications are caused not only by cancer but also by its treatments. These results emphasize that CRCI is evidence of a multifaceted impairment of the network of brain systems and could serve in the future as a tool of research that would use functional imaging to investigate cognitive changes in survivors of cancer in greater detail. Therefore, despite the serious challenge of diagnosing CRCI in clinical practice, the focus is on the patient’s clinical history, particularly whether a cognitive complaint existed before CT, and on excluding other contributing factors to cognitive impairment.

EVIDENCE-BASED INTERVENTIONS FOR CRCI

The majority of intervention studies concentrate on one of the major symptoms and track the improvement of the associated symptoms due to the intervention as shown in Fig. 2. Many non-pharmacological management and preventative strategies have been considered in the treatment and prevention of CRCI. These were generally divided into four general types as follows: cognitive training or rehabilitation, cognitive behavioral therapy, physical exercise, and intervention such as yoga, or mindfulness (Ramalingam et al., 2023).

Pharmacological strategies

The interest in interventions to manage chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) has grown during the last few years. Specifically, methylphenidate and modafinil are psychostimulant medications that have potential therapeutic value in the treatment of CRCI (Demos-Davies et al., 2024). In a systematic review of six randomized controlled trials published in 2019, three studies (involving 201 participants) assessed methylphenidate, and three studies (involving 146 participants) assessed modafinil. These treatments were evaluated for their effects on cognitive functioning and quality of life. Two studies with modafinil and one with methylphenidate reported enhanced cognitive performance. The action mode of stimulants is understood to involve neural-level signal processing that increases in concentrations of norepinephrine and dopamine in the prefrontal cortex (Table 1) (Karschnia et al., 2019).

A potential therapeutic agent to prevent CRCI during chemotherapy is memantine, an N-methyl-d-aspartate receptor antagonist. In patients with brain metastases receiving whole-brain radiotherapy, multicentre trials have shown that memantine reduces loss of executive function, memory, and processing speed, and it is now included in guideline recommendations (Folloso et al., 2022). Nakamura et al. (2023) contacted 126 patients, and only 56 (44%) enrolled in the study. Forty-five patients received 1 or more doses of memantine and completed pre- and post-assessments. 76% reported taking 90% or more of the scheduled doses. The mean age of participants was 56, White participants were 77 percent, and stage I participants were 57 percent. Sixty-four percent recorded a stable and better test score on delayed matching to sample. Faithful or enhanced cognition was realized in 87%-91% of interconnected measures of cognitive domains comprising the objectives. Sixty-six percent were self-reported to have stable or improved cognitive symptoms. The number of grade 3 or higher adverse events was 7, of which 2 were potentially related to memantine. Just 5% retorted that the administration of memantine interfered with their lives.

Furthermore, antidepressants have shown promise in managing CRCI. For example, methotrexate-induced memory impairment has been ameliorated by fluoxetine, and fluoxetine has been shown to reverse neurogenic deficits in mice (Daubner et al., 2021). Another study showed that the chemotherapeutic agent 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) significantly inhibits cell growth in the subgranular zone of the dentate gyrus, but combining 5-FU with fluoxetine prevented this reduction (Groves et al., 2021). Patients receiving combination therapy demonstrated better hippocampal-dependent spatial working memory than those on monotherapy. Collectively, these results suggest 5-FU impairs working memory and hippocampal neurogenesis, while fluoxetine may partially mitigate these effects (Sekeres et al., 2021).

Donepezil is an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that increases brain levels of acetylcholine by preventing its breakdown. Primarily used in the treatment of Alzheimer’s disease, it has attracted significant attention from researchers studying chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) due to its potential role as a neurotransmitter modulator (Pooladgar et al., 2022). In a clinical study, patients with CRCI symptoms from breast cancer received donepezil for 24 weeks. Compared to the placebo group, those in the donepezil group showed substantial improvement in memory, indicating an effect on the hippocampal memory system (Reeve et al., 2019).

Another mechanism that has been postulated to cause CRCI with chemotherapy is the central oxidative stress response. Research has revealed that oxidative stress and cognitive impairment that result from chemotherapy can be prevented by antioxidant therapy prior to and during chemotherapy. Short-term memory impairment that is the result of carmustine chemotherapy is preventable by treatment with the antioxidant, zinc sulphate (ZnSO4), prior to chemotherapy. Moreover, carmustine caused the death and inflammation of hippocampal cells, which were also reduced in ZnSO4-treated cells (Rummel et al., 2021). Kitamura et al. (2021) conducted a study to determine the effects of cyclophosphamide and Adriamycin on rats’ memory. As it turned out, cyclophosphamide in combination with doxorubicin resulted in memory impairment. However, the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine failed to impair short-term memory in rats when administered during chemotherapy.

Propofol is also said to have a neuroprotective effect of preventing inflammatory processes. It also alleviated oxidative stress and inhibited the subsequent rise in mitochondrial swelling of the neurons in animal models of nerve injury, such as ischemic stroke and traumatic brain injury (Gonullu et al., 2023). It has previously been determined that cognitive dysfunction induced by cisplatin is associated with the loss of synaptic mitochondria in the brain and that the peripheral nervous system has defective mitochondria. Propofol, therefore, may help slow cisplatin-induced memory impairment by deterring neuroinflammation, reducing oxidation, and mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction (Zhou and Qiu, 2019).

Cognitive rehabilitation approaches

Cognitive rehabilitation, which focuses on enhancing cognitive abilities impaired by injury or illness, plays a crucial role in this process (Besnier et al., 2021). This therapeutic approach includes a variety of interventions such as computer-assisted cognitive rehabilitation, which uses structured programs where patients practice cognitive tasks through computer-based platforms (Capetti et al., 2026). Studies have shown that this method improves working memory, perceived cognitive function, and cognitively related quality of life compared to control conditions (Vardy et al., 2023). Furthermore, domain-specific cognitive training exercises target particular cognitive domains like attention, memory, and executive functioning. These repetitive tasks aim to strengthen impaired cognitive processes and enhance overall cognitive performance (Sanchez et al., 2025). Alongside these, psycho-education helps individuals understand their cognitive challenges, offering strategies for managing difficulties and improving daily functioning. Collectively, these components work in synergy to address cognitive impairments and support individuals in regaining or compensating for lost cognitive skills (Bhutani et al., 2025).

Psychotherapeutic approaches

The psychoeducational and compensatory treatments can help patients cope with their cognitive problems in daily life by applying practical methods. One of the cognitive-behavioral interventions is the Memory and Attention Adaptation Training (MAAT), which involves the use of external memory aids, elimination of stress, and the pacing process to reduce cognitive constriction (Liu et al., 2025). A short, more group-based version of MAAT in combination with initial results was found to be viable and workable when utilizing survivors of breast cancer who showed higher scores in the two tasks of cognitive complaints and self-reported functioning. The Goal Management Training, which was initially meant to work with people with brain injuries, is successful in the population with cancer, as there is an improvement in executive functioning and performance on daily activities in the survivors of brain tumors. These types of low-level interventions are desirable in the clinical environment, where access to specialized cognitive rehabilitation services may be limited (Xia et al., 2024).

Ponsford et al. (2023) conducted a systematic review and found that rehabilitation training has a beneficial effect on cognitive functions, including memory, attention, executive functioning, and processing speed. Cognitive rehabilitation training can be of two types: computer-based training, which must be performed on a computer, and strategy-based training. Strategy-based training targets the rehabilitation of impaired cognitive and compensatory functions through psychological education and guided practice. In this approach, sessions typically last between 25 min and 2.5 h, occur over a period of 4 to 12 weeks, and are divided into two groups based on intervention type or participant characteristics. Most studies implementing cognitive rehabilitation after breast cancer showed sustained improvements in cognitive test results and long-term quality of life. Improvements were seen in executive functioning, working memory, attention, memory, processing speed, and visual-spatial skills following specific interventions (Table 1) (Josheghani et al., 2024).

In one study, a measurable EEG biomarker was identified, and rehabilitation was associated with enhanced global brain activity. People reported that cognitive rehabilitation interventions helped balance memory issues, and emotional issues such as anxiety and depression could also be successfully addressed with this intervention (Dashtipour et al., 2024).

Nutritional and supplement-based interventions

Nutrition is crucial for supporting brain function, as it affects brain activity in areas such as mood, cognitive functioning, neuroprotection, and neurogenesis. Some of the nutrients that help preserve the structural integrity and functional properties of neuronal membranes include the omega-3 fatty acids that are prevalent in fish (e.g., salmon and sardines), and also in plants (e.g., walnuts and chia seeds), such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) (Smolińska et al., 2024). Fatty acids are involved in synaptic plasticity, one of the significant processes underlying learning and memory. Moreover, omega-3s are known to increase production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which is key to neuronal growth, differentiation, and survival, and possess anti-inflammatory properties that could potentially reduce processes associated with neurodegenerative diseases (Table 1) (Wen et al., 2024). According to the most recent evidence provided by a systematic review and meta-regression of controlled trials, omega-3 supplementation is found to significantly increase the circulating levels of BDNF over the controls (pooled weighted mean difference: 1.01 μmol/L; 95% CI: 0.35167; p=0.003), with a more substantial positive effect in prolonged interventions longer than 10 weeks and lower doses of omega-3 than 1500 mg/day (Sohouli et al., 2023).

Vitamin E, also known as Alpha-Tocopherol, is another proposed fat-soluble antioxidant that may help prevent the formation of reactive oxygen species (Wen et al., 2025). Two articles examined its effectiveness on cognition. McCleery et al. (2018) reported improvements in executive function and verbal and visual memory in patients who took 1,000 IU of vitamin E twice daily for 1 year. In contrast, Zhao et al. (2022) ended their randomized, double-blinded, placebo-controlled study early due to low accrual and found no significant effect of vitamin E on cognitive impairment.

Physical activity as a neuroprotective measure

Physical activity interventions: Exercise has since been observed to be important for the general well-being of cancer survivors and for reducing symptoms. Exercise is a prescribed or official body movement that entails routine usage to improve or maintain cardiovascular stamina, muscle power, muscle endurance, flexibility, and/or body structure. CRCI is treatable using exercise interventions such as aerobic exercise, resistance training, yoga, qigong, or tai chi, which promote the quality of life of cancer survivors (An et al., 2024a). In one study, an experimental design to investigate the role of sedentary survivors of breast cancer, participants in a 12-week exercise program reported better processing speed and fewer cognitive symptoms 2 years post-diagnosis than a wait-list controlled group receiving general health issues (healthy eating, stress reduction, and overall brain well-being) emails (White et al., 2024).

In another recent systematic review of 26 studies, the authors added interventions of aerobic or resistance exercise and mindfulness-based exercise; there were more positive effects on cognitive performance in most trials (Miller et al., 2021). Second, there was also heterogeneity in the studies in terms of which no specific type of exercise or amount of exercise (duration, intensity, or frequency), or time frame to initiate exercise could be identified (Table 1) (Rodrigues et al., 2025). As such, additional research is required in order to establish the most appropriate exercise training prescriptions to be used in reducing CRCI in survivors of cancer.

Mindfulness-based practices: Mind-body interventions aim to reduce stress by encouraging participants to emphasize their own healing or restorative abilities. Additionally, there are mind-body interventions that can be useful in the treatment of CRI, such as guided imagery, meditation, neuro/biofeedback, acupuncture and restorative environments. Such approaches have yielded some good outcomes when applied to the treatment of CRCI (Table 1) (Pieczynski et al., 2020). Incidentally, MBSR was shown to increase participants’ subjective and objective working memory, and meditation was shown to enhance short-term memory and processing speed (Marciniak et al., 2020). Although the research is quite promising, it is relatively thin. The use of such mind-body interventions in terms of patient-reported and objective cognitive functioning needs to be studied further.

EMERGING TRENDS IN CRCI

Studies that use logistic regression to predict CRCI face challenges in preventing overfitting and ensuring the model’s generalizability to broader populations (Sahu and Tiwari, 2025). Gaps in the data are a frequent problem, and the traditional approaches to them (either deletion or simple imputation) may introduce bias; more sophisticated methods, such as multiple imputation, are required to achieve greater precision. Moreover, continuous variables can be dichotomized, which simplifies interpretation at the expense of predictive ability, as it loses essential information (Duan et al., 2025). To overcome these issues, new technologies, including machine learning and advanced data analytics, are improving prediction accuracy and enabling the handling of large, complex datasets, leading to more consistent models for managing CRCI.

Evidence-based advancements in CRCI management technologies

Machine learning has proved to be a strong method of CRCI risk prediction in the last decade, due to its ability to combine high-dimensional data, introduce intricate correlations, and model nonlinear associations (Aliferis and Simon, 2024). Machine learning algorithms are also data driven as opposed to traditional statistical methods which operate under predetermined assumptions about variable relating features, thus improving predictive accuracy, robustness, and clinical relevance (Pratama et al., 2025). In this regard, Mitra et al. (2025) created a three dimensional convolutional neural network using MRI data to extract credible biomarkers that measure brain ageing rate and aids risk analysis of mental deterioration. Parallel work has shown that EEG based biomarkers play a role in estimating the brain age, as well as are linked with consequent cognitive decline. Further than regional results, machine learning on structural covariance networks allows brain age prediction on a systems level base, with major networks comprised of the caudate nucleus, hippocampus, amygdala and cerebellar areas, which highlights the importance of network informed models in CRCI as summarized in Fig. 3.

The cross-sectional study, carried out by Xinran et al. (2023), aimed at estimating the risk of cognitive impairment in the samples of patients with advanced cancer and comparing the performance of various predictive models. This study involved 494 advanced cancer patients who had participated in the study and important clinical indicators were identified first through analysis. Two multivariate tests were conducted using two different models which were an artificial neural network model (ANN) model, a multi-layer perceptron model, and a logistic regression model. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was used to identify the predictive power of the models. Results established that there was significant risk of cancer duration, anxiety and age. It is also worth mentioning that ANN model performed better than logistic regression model since it was more accurate with higher AUC of 0.910. These findings are alternatively indicative that ANN model could be having additional capabilities of forecasting cognitive deficits in the subsequent cancer patients and therefore, facilitate clinical interventions. The research limitations are, however, the following; the research that was looked at and conducted in one region; the samples were not mixed and the research was cross-sectional thus hampering the study prospects of providing a causation effect (Li et al., 2025).

Chen et al. (2025) conducted a prospective longitudinal study to determine the risk of cognitive decline in patients receiving blood or marrow transplantation for hematologic malignancies to determine the predictive model size, encompass the genetic, clinical, and demographic factors, and evaluate the predictive models using AUC values and using a dissimilar group of BMT transplant survivors. The critical predictors were age, gender, total body irradiation (TBI), baseline cognitive reserve, and genes involved in DNA repair and the blood-brain barrier. The better-integrated model, including genetic data, had an AUC of 0.89 (95% CI 0.85703) in the discovery cohort and was superior to models that used only clinical and demographic data. This is more predictive, but because it results from genetic issues and variation in cognitive testing methods across cohorts, it raises questions about the model’s generalizability.

Challenges in CRCI prediction models

Despite advancements in the development of CRCI prediction models, their clinical integration remains constrained by multiple obstacles. There is no standardization or external validation across data sets, which reduces confidence in their robustness and generalization (An et al., 2024b). The problem with most CRCI prediction models is that the sample sizes are small, which is prone to over-fitting, and the models do not apply to a broader range of patients. Internal validation methods, such as cross-validation, are used in most studies, whereas only a few are externally validated in heterogeneous populations, limiting their application in clinical settings. (Martin et al., 2021). Moreover, the issue of missing data remains prominent, and most studies do not adequately address it or use straightforward imputation techniques that can be biased. More sophisticated methods, including multiple imputation and improved handling of continuous variables, can improve the model’s performance (Stangler et al., 2025).

Moreover, model calibration, despite its critical role in ensuring that predicted probabilities align with actual outcomes, is often inadequately addressed during development. Most models have strong discriminative power, as reflected in AUC scores, but calibration metrics are more specific to a clinical environment, as they reflect the model’s accuracy. Otherwise, predictive models can yield incorrect risk probabilities, thereby reducing their usefulness in practice, as discussed in Table 2 (Dhiman et al., 2023).

FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS

Based on the subject analysis, several areas of future research can be identified. First, greater conceptual clarity will be needed, and consistency is also demanded. While the recognition of CRCI has increased, diagnostic criteria and methods of assessment remain inconsistent. Future studies should develop agreement-based frameworks to identify, monitor, and categorize mental changes across diverse cancer populations and treatment cycles (Mulholland et al., 2024). Longitudinal studies that embrace sequential designs and pre- and post-intervention designs are highly requested. Most existing publications are cross-sectional or cover a very narrow time span, a limitation in our assessment of the process of cognitive alteration and its time-varying changes. Besides, it seeks to address cognitive side effects. Oncology clinical trials should also be performed regularly (Kesler et al., 2023). Cognitive impairment has far-reaching consequences for functional autonomy, therapeutic compliance, and the quality of existence, yet they are hardly among the key outcomes or are given low priority. Clinical research will be more effective and applicable as tested cognitive endpoints are incorporated.

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) with large genomic and multi-omics data sets has excellent potential to improve the prediction of cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI). AI systems analyze genetic variants, neuroimaging markers, and treatment variables to evaluate and refine predictive algorithms (Ozaki et al., 2024). In neuro-oncology research, AI has analyzed genomic data and protein interaction networks to predict cognitive outcomes (Mandala, 2025). By combining data from genomics, neuroimaging, and treatment history, individualized risk profiles for predicting CRCI can be created. The main advantages are improved prediction, new biomarker discovery, and real-time follow-up enabled by digital technologies such as wearables. The future of CRCI management is focused on big data, AI, and integration with clinical practice to deliver precision cognitive care in oncology (Gu et al., 2024).

Furthermore, the development of CRCI research that incorporates electronic health records (EHRs) offers revolutionary opportunities to enhance predictive modelling. EHRs provide a vast repository of clinical data, including demographics, treatment records, laboratory findings, imaging records, and long-term outcomes (Bandyopadhyay et al., 2024). Using these large datasets, scientists will be able to maximize the statistical power and extrapolatability of CRCI prediction models, which cannot be achieved with small samples and homogeneous populations (Zhang et al., 2024). Models can be generalized and used across various demographic groups and clinical settings because of the variety available in EHR data sets, and this approach will help tackle one of the most urgent problems in existing CRCI prediction studies.

CONCLUSION

CRCI remains one of the concerns of cancer survivors and affects their quality of life and cognitive abilities. Despite significant achievements in CRCI research, the findings are often conflicting, and the conclusions are contradictory. There are several agents involved in the cognitive impairment among cancer patients, such as cytokine imbalance, oxidative stress, hormonal alterations, fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive reserve, and mental state. The neurological complications, especially in those patients with extra-cranial cancers, may be very detrimental to their quality of life, tolerance to treatment and survival rate. Although a significant focus has been placed on the neurological effects of the anti-cancer therapies, cognitive and affective disorders in most cases tend to develop prior to the treatment and, as such, the entire aetiology of such complications cannot be placed on the cancer treatments. It has been noted that the inflammatory nature of peripheral tumour growth is a significant issue for the central nervous system (CNS). However, the scope of its effects on brain function remains limited.

Currently, diagnostic techniques rely on neuropsychological scales, and the sensitivity and specificity of web-based tools should be enhanced several-fold as well. Molecular processes underlying peripheral tumors that cause cognitive and affective disorders are crucial for improving treatment outcomes and cancer survival rates. Additionally, new specific neurotherapeutic interventions, as well as holistic non-pharmaceutical interventions, including mindfulness and meditation, that have demonstrated promise in the management of inflammation and stress in breast cancer patients, can provide further tools to use in CRCI management. However, many more studies are required to investigate how peripheral tumors interact with and influence the brain. The implementation of advanced technologies, especially machine learning and AI, has the potential to enhance the prediction and management of CRCI by providing more specific and personalized risk assessments, thereby facilitating more efficient interventions and better patient outcomes.
