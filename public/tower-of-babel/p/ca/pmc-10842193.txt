Experimental and mathematical approaches for drug delivery for the treatment of wet age-related macular degeneration
Authors: Eduardo A. Chacin Ruiz, Katelyn E. Swindle-Reilly, Ashlee N. Ford Versypt
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Several chronic eye diseases affect the posterior segment of the eye. Among them age-related macular degeneration can cause vision loss if left untreated and is one of the leading causes of visual impairment in the world. Most treatments are based on intravitreally injected therapeutics that inhibit the action of vascular endothelial growth factor. However, due to the need for monthly injections, this method is associated with poor patient compliance. To address this problem, numerous drug delivery systems (DDSs) have been developed. This review covers a selection of particulate systems, non-stimuli responsive hydrogels, implants, and composite systems that have been developed in the last few decades. Depending on the type of DDS, polymer material, and preparation method, different mechanical properties and drug release profiles can be achieved. Furthermore, DDS development can be optimized by implementing mathematical modeling of both drug release and pharmacokinetic aspects. Several existing mathematical models for diffusion-controlled, swelling-controlled, and erosion-controlled drug delivery from polymeric systems are summarized. Compartmental and physiologically based models for ocular drug transport and pharmacokinetics that have studied drug concentration profiles after intravitreal delivery or release from a DDS are also reviewed. The coupling of drug release models with ocular pharmacokinetic models can lead to obtaining much more efficient DDSs for the treatment of age-related macular degeneration and other diseases of the posterior segment of the eye.

Introduction

Several common diseases affect the posterior segment of the eye including age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, diabetic macular edema, viral retinitis, polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy, proliferative vitreoretinopathy, posterior uveitis, retinal vascular occlusions, and diabetic retinopathy. The incidences of these diseases are growing at an alarming rate, and most of these diseases can cause permanent vision loss if not treated on time [1]. Among these diseases, AMD is one of the leading causes of visual impairment globally [2]. In the US, the number of affected people is expected to double to 22 million by 2050 [3], and current annual costs of AMD to the healthcare system exceed $5 billion [3-5].

AMD is a chronic disease that progressively affects the macula, a light-sensitive pigmented tissue in the center of the retina that is composed of several layers of specialized cells such as the photoreceptors, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), Bruch’s membrane, and the choroid [6]. Depending on the stage of the disease, AMD can be classified in two types: non-neovascular atrophic (dry) AMD and neovascular (wet) AMD [7]. Aging of the eye involves a reduction in the ability of the RPE cells to detoxify, which produces a buildup of uncleared cellular debris (drusen) associated with photoreceptor outer segment turnover [8-10]. This ocular debris starts accumulating between the RPE and Bruch’s membrane, and it is the first visible sign of dry AMD [2]. Furthermore, with aging, Bruch’s membrane and the choriocapillaris get thicker, and permeability is decreased, reducing the diameter of the choroidal capillaries and causing hypoxia [8,9]. Wet AMD is caused by the growth of new and leaky blood vessels in the choroid layer as a result from this acute vascular compromise, which leads to an abnormally high presence of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) [8,11]. VEGF is a protein that induces both intracellular calcium increase and production of vasodilatory mediators [12,13]. Consequently, VEGF participates in the development, progression, and regulation of new blood vessels in the choroid [14,15]. These blood vessels break through the retinal layers and leak blood and lipids, which leads to fibrous scarring. This causes progressive atrophy in these tissues and eventual vision loss [16].

There is no cure for wet AMD, but early and recurring treatment can slow its progression and relieve the symptoms. Currently, the best wet AMD therapies are based on anti-VEGF drugs. The three most commonly used anti-VEGF drugs are ranibizumab, bevacizumab (off-label), and aflibercept [17]. Ranibizumab and bevacizumab biosimilars have also been studied, and some of them are approved for the treatment of wet AMD in the USA, Europe, and India [18]. Other anti-VEGF biologics that have been gaining interest include abicipar pegol, brolucizumab, conbercept, and faricimab [19,20]. These drugs improve visual conditions by inhibiting choroidal neovascularization [21]. Other studies have investigated the synergistic effect of sequential therapies. Agustin et al. [9] evaluated a triple therapy involving verteporfin photodynamic therapy, dexamethasone, and bevacizumab to attempt address the multifactorial pathogenesis of wet AMD. The therapy had similar effects to single bevacizumab injections for three months. Furthermore, proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) consisting of a ligand for the target protein (i.e., VEGF), a linker, and a ligand to recruit E3 ligase [22] could, in theory, induce degradation of VEGF and help treat wet AMD.

Different routes of administration for delivery of the anti-VEGF agents have been studied, with the intravitreal route being the most popular option [23]. However, the need for monthly intravitreal injections is associated with poor patient compliance, high costs, and possible complications [1]. To address this issue, several drug delivery systems (DDSs) that can be injected in the vitreous have been proposed in the literature to extend anti-VEGF release and consequently reduce the need for frequent injections [24-44].

The development of a DDS is a long task that usually requires performing many drug release experiments under controlled settings. Mathematical modeling of DDSs has arisen as an important tool to help predict drug transport and release rate from delivery systems [45]. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic models provide insight on the relationship between the administered drug concentration and the fate of the drug in vivo over time. Coupling these types of models can help reduce the number of experiments required, along with the overall costs.

In this review we describe the four main routes of drug delivery to the posterior segment of the eye with a focus on the intravitreal route due to its advantages over the others (Section 2). We review specific characteristics of a spectrum of polymer-based DDSs that have been studied for long-term drug release in the vitreous humor (Section 3). We describe relevant types of mathematical models that could be applied to predict drug release from the polymer-based DDSs (Section 4). Finally, we review ocular pharmacokinetic models that incorporate relevant aspects of ocular tissue behavior and the drug release from intravitreal polymer-based DDSs (Section 5). Even though the main focus of this paper is wet AMD, the information gathered from the literature might also be beneficial for treating other long-term retinal diseases, such as diabetic retinopathy and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy.

Routes of drug delivery to the posterior segment of the eye

The eye can be separated into the anterior and posterior segments. The anterior segment refers to the front-most one-third of the eye and includes the cornea, iris, ciliary body, and the crystalline lens. The posterior segment refers to the back-most two-thirds of the eye, including the retina, choroid, vitreous humor, and the optic nerve [23,46] (Fig. 1a). Routes for drug delivery to the posterior segment of the eye include topical, systemic, periocular, and intravitreal (Fig. 1b). Each of these routes is briefly described in turn, but more extensive reviews on drug delivery routes to the posterior segment of the eye have already been published [23,47-51].

Topical ocular drug delivery is usually administered via eye drops or ointments through the cornea. The topical route is the easiest and most convenient form of drug delivery in the eye, accounting for more than 90% of ophthalmic products in the market [52]. Nevertheless, delivery to the posterior segment of the eye using this route is inefficient, and only about 0.0001% of the drug is expected to reach the posterior segment [53]. This is due to factors such as: (i) limited volume of administration, (ii) fast clearance from ocular surfaces, (iii) anterior membrane barriers (cornea, conjunctiva, and sclera), (iv) aqueous humor outflow, and (v) long diffusional path [1]. Therefore, frequent topical applications of high drug concentrations are required to maintain a therapeutic concentration, but this could lead to systemic side effects [54]. Furthermore, large antibodies such as anti-VEGF therapeutics cannot overcome the transport barriers between the site of administration and the target tissue when administered topically [55].

In the periocular route, the drug is administered through the region surrounding the eye via subconjunctival, suprachoroidal, peribulbar, posterior juxtascleral, sub-Tenon’s, and retrobulbar injections [1] (Fig. 1b). Interest in suprachoroidal injections have been rising due to the potential of the suprachoroidal space for gene therapy and drug delivery for treating wet AMD [19,20,56-59]. The periocular route has the advantage that repetitive injections are possible without directly interfering vision [1]. Drugs delivered this route can reach the posterior segment of the eye through the conjunctiva-sclera-choroid-retina pathway [60]. Bioavailability achieved is 0.01–0.1% [1]. Nevertheless, the amount of drug that reaches the retina is not enough to be therapeutic due to the drug losses from factors including the blood-retinal barrier, periocular space, and choroidal circulation [61,62].

Systemic drug administration is accomplished by one of the conventional routes (oral, intravenous, or intramuscular) [23]. Systemic routes have advantages including high patient compliance and wide availability of pharmaceutical options [49]. However, the problem with the systemic routes is that physiological barriers such as the blood-aqueous barrier and the blood-retinal barrier hinder drug delivery, yielding drug bioavailability of less than 2% [49]. This reduced bioavailability makes it necessary to administer high concentrations of the drug, which, in turn, can lead to systemic toxicity and severe side effects because of drug non-specific absorption in other organs [49,62].

In the subretinal route, the therapy is injected in the subretinal space, which is between the RPE cells and photoreceptors. The subretinal route is one of the most invasive routes of delivery to the posterior segment of the eye. However, the advantage of this route is that the therapeutic comes in direct contact with the targeted RPE cells, allowing high drug concentrations. The subretinal route is mainly used for the delivery of gene therapies targeting retinal cells [63].

For the intravitreal route, the drug is injected directly into the vitreous humor through the pars plana [64], the flattest part of the ciliary body. It is the most common administration route for the treatment of posterior eye diseases [23] and usually the only option for treatment of retinal diseases due to its efficiency and straight-forwardness [50]. In contrast with other ocular delivery routes, intravitreal injections allow high drug concentration to be available in internal tissues such as the choroid and the retina [1]. Furthermore, systemic side effects are rare since exposure to other eye parts is minimal [50]. The commercial success of several formulations for intravitreal delivery reflects the acceptance of this route [65]. However, intravitreal injections are painful, and complications such as vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, cataract, endophthalmitis, and elevated intraocular pressure can arise as a consequence of frequent injections [1]. The remainder of this review will emphasize the intravitreal drug administration route due to its prevalence and advantages for treating the posterior segment of the eye.

Polymer-based drug delivery systems for intravitreal delivery

Several problems are associated with the treatment of wet AMD via intravitreal injections. Anti-VEGF therapeutics have a short half-life in the vitreous (6–9 days), and their bioactivity reduces over time [66,67]. Monthly intravitreal injections of drug solutions are associated with invasiveness, poor patient compliance, and high medical costs. Furthermore, each subsequent injection risks severe complications [68,69]. To overcome these challenges, localized intravitreal extended release delivery using polymer-based DDSs has been gaining traction [70,71]. Some of the approaches include the development of particulate systems, hydrogels, implants, and composite systems that can be injected intravitreally for long-term therapeutic release (Fig. 2). In this section we describe relevant characteristics such as preparation method, size, drug release profiles, and in vitro anti-VEGF bioactivity for several examples of the aforementioned DDSs. Common drug release profiles from DDSs include zero- and first-order drug release (Fig. 3), which will have different drug concentration profiles (Fig. 4). Ideally, an initial burst release followed by a zero-order drug release will allow for quickly reaching a therapeutic drug concentration in the eye and to keep it approximately constant until the DDS is depleted. Alternatively, usually a first-order release device will present drug release and drug concentration profiles that are proportional to the drug concentration on the DDS, but in general the therapeutic window is not maintained for as long as for a zero-order DDS [72]. For further details, readers are referred to other reviews of ocular DDSs available in the literature [1,46,50,62,70,71,73-81].

Particulate systems

Particulate systems can be classified in two categories: (i) nano/microspheres, where the drug is dispersed in the polymeric matrix, and (ii) nano/microcapsules, where the drug is surrounded by a polymeric layer [71]. Moreover, the term “nano” is applied if the diameter of the particle is on the order of 1–1000 nm, whereas the term “micro” applies when the diameter is on the order of 1–1000 μm [77]. Some of the challenges of particulate systems include: improving the encapsulation efficacy, controlling the particle size, and controlling the stability of molecules during manufacturing [82]. Furthermore, injections of particulate systems into the vitreous have been demonstrated to produce retinal damage and inflammation due to particle movement [83]. However, compared to drug solutions, nano- and microsized particles are less affected by clearance mechanisms in the vitreous. This resistance to clearance along with a controlled release of the drug extends the drug’s half-life in the vitreous [84]. Information on particulate systems is summarized in Table 1.

Poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is a biodegradable polymer that has been widely used in drug delivery since its approval for use in DDSs by the US Food and Drug Administration [85,86]. For instance, Zhang et al. [24] developed ranibizumab-loaded PLGA microparticles (MPs) for intravitreal injection. The preparation process involved coaxial electrospray of PLGA to encapsulate the ranibizumab. In vitro release in saline solution was studied using rhodamine-6G as a model drug and measured using an UV-VIS spectrophotometer. The release rate was almost linear with about 70% total release after 26 days. In vivo experiments in newly hatched leghorn chicks model showed that when the MPs were injected in low concentration, there was no significant short-term or long-term inflammation or retinal cell death. The procedure involved the enucleation of the eyes from the chicks on the 12th day after intravitreal injection of the MPs, and double-labeling was used to stain Draq5 and CD45 to determine microglia’s activity in the retina. Furthermore, assesment of fragmented DNA in dying cells was performed using the In Situ Cell Death Kit (TUNEL). Microscopic and photographic imaging were used to take photomicrographs. Additionally, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) tests showed that ranibizumab bioactivity remained above 80% after the preparation of MPs.

Another method for preparing PLGA DDSs is via a modified water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) emulsion/solvent evaporation. Sousa et al. [25] used this technique to prepare spherical PLGA nanoparticles (NPs) loaded with bevacizumab to inhibit VEGF interaction with its receptors. In vitro experiments in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at different pH were performed to determine the pH-dependent bevacizumab release profiles from the NPs using the Bradford protein kit assay kit. At pH 7.4 (close to biological conditions), an initial burst release was observed, followed by a sustained delivery that reached a cumulative release of around 18% after 7 days. It was shown that the bevacizumab encapsulation into the NPs led to some level of aggregation; however, the drug was capable of refolding upon release from the PLGA NPs. In vitro experiments with human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) involved cell toxicity assessment using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay and determination of bevacizumab bioactivity using bromodeoxyuridine (BrDU) and MTT. Both tests were performed on the cells after up to 72 h in presence of bevacizumab-loaded NPs. Results showed that bevacizumab bioactivity was not changed during the preparation process and that the NPs were not cytotoxic.

Similarly, Tanetsugu et al. [26] used the W/O/W emulsion/solvent evaporation technique to obtain a long-acting DDS for the treatment of wet AMD. The system consisted of a ranibizumab biosimilar loaded in spherical PLGA MPs. The release rate in PBS showed an initial burst release, and the complete release of the drug took approximately 45 days. The release data were obtained using a DC protein assay kit, and the absorbance of the solution was measured using a plate reader. By the end of this period of time, the MPs degraded completely. In vitro experiments with HUVEC and Matrigel revealed that the bioactivity of the ranibizumab biosimilar within the MPs was similar to that of free ranibizumab biosimilar.

Stabilizers, such as albumin, can be used to prevent protein aggregation during preparation of PLGA particles [87]. Varshochian et al. [27] developed bevacizumab-loaded albuminated PLGA NPs for the treatment of neovascularization in the posterior segment of the eye. The spherical NPs were prepared by a modified double-emulsion/solvent evaporation method. In vivo experiments were performed in New Zealand albino rabbit, and analysis of the samples by ELISA test showed that bevacizumab vitreous concentration following the NPs injection was maintained within the therapeutic range for about 8 weeks. Furthermore, ocular toxicity of the NPs was evaluated by electroretinography (ERG) and histology tests, both performed at 3, 21, and 56 days. No significant ocular toxicity was observed in this period. Additionally, coumarin-6-loaded NPs were used to confirm the persistence of the NPs in ocular tissues during the 8 weeks.

Poly(cyclohexane-1,4-diyl acetone dimethylene ketal) (PCADK) is a polymer that can help improve encapsulation efficiency and reduce inflammation after injection of PLGA microspheres. Liu et al. [29] investigated PLGA/PCADK microspheres for the purpose of intravitreal drug delivery of bevacizumab. The microspheres were prepared using either a solid-in-oil-in-water (S/O/W) or a W/O/W emulsion/solvent evaporation technique, and 0 or 20% of PCADK. In vitro experiments in PBS showed that the microspheres prepared by the S/O/W method had an initial burst release of about 9.5% after 1 h, and the cumulative drug release was between 59.6 and 75.7% after 50 days. Under the same conditions, the microspheres prepared by the W/O/W technique had an initial burst of around 20.5% after 1 h, and a cumulative drug release between 43.5 and 50% after 50 days. Irritability and intraocular pressure were tested in vivo in rabbits, and no undesirable effect was observed. Bevacizumab released from the PLGA/PCADK microspheres was able to reduce the percentage of blood vessels almost as much as a direct bevacizumab injection.

Polyethylene glycol (PEG) is a hydrophilic polymer that can increase the biocompatibility, solubility, and bioavailability of integrated drugs [55]. Tsujinaka et al. [28] studied biodegradable sunitinib-loaded PLGA-PEG MPs for the treatment of retinal vascular diseases. These MPs self-aggregated into a depot after intravitreal injection. The MPs were prepared by an oil-in-water (O/W) single emulsion/solvent evaporation technique. In vitro experiments with PBS showed that the MPs released sunitinib for around 85 days with minimal burst release. This was measured using UV absorbance. In vivo experiments were performed in mice, rabbits, and minipigs. These experiments showed that therapeutic levels were maintained in the RPE/choroid for a total of 6 months. ERG was used to study retinal function; there was no identifiable toxicity related to the MPs during a 40-week observation period, and bioactivity of the drug was maintained. This technology reached clinical trials under the name of GB-102. However, the 2 mg intravitreal injection was discontinued after presenting adverse events related to the particulate system [88].

PolyActive™ is a biodegradable polymeric drug delivery system composed of PEG and poly(butylphthalate) (PBT). Adamson et al. [30] studied six MP formulations based on PolyActive for prolonged release of anti-VEGF after intravitreal injection. The model drug used was a dimeric molecule that contains two different anti-VEGF domain antibodies (dual dAb). The double-emulsion/solvent evaporation technique was used for the preparation of the MPs. Depending on the formulation, the release lasted from 6 months (fast release) to 18 months (slow release) after an initial burst release in PBS. The released amounts were measured using electrochemiluminescense that determined functional activity of the drug. The DDS showed protection against laser-induced choroidal neovascularization in a Cynomolgus model over 6 months as measured by liquid chromatography mass spectometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). The released anti-VEGF maintained sufficient activity in vitro after 12 months.

Polydopamine (PDA) is a polymer capable of inhibiting acute inflammation by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS) [89]. Jiang et al. [31] developed protein-loaded PDA NPs for the treatment of wet AMD. The NPs simultaneously addressed the anti-angiogenesis and the anti-oxidization pathways by means of anti-VEGF release and PDA degradation, respectively. The NPs were prepared via a solution-oxidation technique. Cell apoptosis was studied on human retinal pigmented epithelial cells (ARPE-19), where the cells were incubated during 24 h in presence of different concentrations of PDA nanoparticles and evaluated by a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (MTS) assay. Furthermore, the cells were stained by live/dead cell assay and observed under fluorescent microscopy. An acceptable biocompatibility of the PDA NPs was obtained when the concentration of NPs was below 100 μg/mL. In vitro experiments showed that PDA NPs were biodegradable under physiological conditions and that ROS reduce following PDA degradation. An initial burst release followed by a more sustained delivery was observed for in vitro experiments in PBS and different hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) concentrations. After 3 months, it was shown that complete bevacizumab release was achieved for a 10 mM concentration of H2O2, but only 65% of the total load was released for the case with no H2O2. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) release was observed to be faster than bevacizumab for equivalent H2O2 concentrations, potentially due to molecular weight. FITC-BSA release was measured using a multi-mode microplate reader, and bevacizumab release was measured by ELISA.

Polycaprolactone (PCL) is a polymer that has shown good in vivo compatibility and slow degradation [90], and chitosan is a biocompatible and biodegradable polymer used in biomedical applications [91]. PCL and chitosan have been combined in various DDSs. For instance, Jiang et al. [32] developed biodegradable MPs composed of a drug-loaded chitosan core and an outer PCL shell. The drug-loaded chitosan particles were prepared by a W/O emulsion method, whereas the PCL coating was obtained by an O/W technique. The MPs had a smooth spherical shape. BSA as well as bevacizumab were used as model drugs. In vitro experiments showed that chitosan-PCL MPs had an initial burst release followed by a steady release, which lasted six months before depletion of the DDS. BSA release was measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit, whereas bevacizumab release was measured by ELISA. Furthermore, MTS assay was used to determine in vitro cytotoxicity on ARPE-19 cells. The cells were seeded, and after 1 day and 1 month, different concentrations of the microparticles and media were added to the cells. The particles presented minimal toxicity to ARPE-19 cells as well as good anti-angiogenic bioactivity and proper biodegradability.

Hydrogels

Hydrogels are hydrophilic networks of polymer chains that, when surrounded by water, undergo swelling without losing their structure. This swelling can cause hydrogels to have a rapid burst drug release and fast drug release if they are porous [92]. However, hydrogels are appropriate for delivery of drugs that are soluble in water [93]. A hydrogel can have a spontaneous sol-gel phase transition as a response to one or more stimuli like temperature, pH, light, and ionic changes. They can be formed by various natural or synthetic polymers. In terms of ocular drug delivery, most efforts have been placed on thermosensitive PEG hydrogels, but other thermoresponsive hydrogels have been studied [71]. Careful selection of the polymeric hydrogel is important since some of them have been shown to produce toxicity in both retina and lens [83]. Stimuli-responsive hydrogels are outside of the scope of this review, but they have already been reviewed by other authors [71]. A summary of the properties of a couple of non-stimuli responsive hydrogels for intravitreal drug delivery is provided in Table 2.

Lovett et al. [33] developed silk hydrogels for sustained drug delivery in the eye for the treatment of wet AMD. The hydrogels were prepared by mixing silk fibroin and bevacizumab and then sonicating the mixture. In vitro release experiments showed that the DDS presented an initial burst release followed by a sustained release. After 91 days, the standard dose and high dose hydrogels released approximately 40% and 62% of the total load, respectively. In vivo experiments in Dutch belted rabbits demonstrated the overall biocompatibility and proper biodegradability of the hydrogels. The ophthalmic evaluations included assessment of ocular lesions that were performed up to day 8 after hydrogel injection. Therapeutic concentrations in the vitreous were achieved for around 90 days, especially with the high dose gel. For determination of bevacizumab concentration, the ELISA method was applied as well as a gel permeation chromatography (GPC) method. Bevacizumab maintained its bioactivity during the experiments.

Another study involving bevacizumab-loaded hydrogels for the treatment of ocular disease was performed by Yu et al. [34]. This hydrogel was formed in situ after an intravitreal injection of chemically crosslinked vinylsulfone functionalized hyaluronic acid (HA-VS), thiolated dextran (Dex-SH), and bevacizumab. From experimental testing with ARPE-19 cells, the hydrogels showed cytocompatibility as determined by a live/dead assay performed after 24 h of cell exposure to the hydrogel. Additionally, in vivo experiments in New Zealand White rabbits showed that bevacizumab concentration was therapeutically relevant up to 6 months after injection. Bevacizumab concentrations were measured by the ELISA method. Moreover, the eyes of the rabbits were constantly checked using a digital binocular indirect ophthalmoscope, and retinal function was assessed by ERG for up to 12 weeks after injection.

Implants

Implants are solid devices usually in the millimeter scale that can be injected or surgically implanted into the vitreous [94]. They are large enough to guarantee long-term drug release, but they can be painful and difficult to implant. Current implants in the market for the treatment of eye diseases include release of dexamethasone (Ozurdex®), fluocinolone (Iluvien®, Retisert®), ganciclovir (Vitrasert®) [71], and ranibizumab (Susvimo®). Susvimo is the state-of-the-art for treating wet AMD. However, it requires surgical implantation and removal, refilling every 6 months, and leaving a port connected into the eye, and it can cause complications such as endophthalmitis and retinal detachment leading to voluntary recall. Implants approved for drug delivery are usually well tolerated in the vitreous, but this might be related to the fact that most of them release a steroid that might mitigate immune responses [83]. Burgalassi et al. [35] investigated hydroxypropylmethyl cellulose (HPMC), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), and polyacrylic acid (PAA) bevacizumab-loaded solid matrices implants for intravitreal administration. The implants were obtained by freezedrying followed by sterilization with gamma rays. Dextran and bevacizumab were used as model drugs, and around 5% in volume of bevacizumab was found to form aggregates. PAA formulations were too brittle to handle without breakage. Furthermore, initial in vitro experiments showed that both HPMC and PVA matrices were able to slow down dextran release, but PVP matrices had a release profile similar to the control. Subsequent in vitro experiments with bevacizumab showed that after 96 h the HPMC and PVA implants released approximately 1.9% and 3.5% of the total load, respectively. Finally, in vivo experiments using the HPMC formulation in New Zealand albino rabbits demonstrated that even after 12 weeks, bevacizumab was still present in the rabbits’ eyes in a concentration of around 4.72 ng/mL. Bevacizumab concentrations were obtained by the ELISA method. Indirect ophthalmoscopy was performed up to 1 week after injection, and no signs of inflammation were observed. Furthermore, retinal samples were evaluated using histological analysis up to 12 weeks after implant injection, and no retinal alterations were observed.

Pakdel et al. [36] developed a drug-loaded biodegradable implant for the treatment of wet AMD. The drug was obtained from a mixture of ranibizumab and dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) using the hydrophobic ion pairing technique. The homogeneous rod implants were prepared with poly (3-hydroxybutyric acid-co-3-hydroxyvaleric acid) (PHBV) as the base polymer and PEG as a plasticizer. The drug was added to the molten polymer. In vitro release in PBS showed a first-order release, with around 29.3% of the drug released after 32 days. Drug release was determined using the ELISA method. Toxicity was assessed in vitro by adding the drugs released from the implants after 32 days to HUVECs and then performing an MTT assay. The bioactivity of the drug was maintained, and the DDS showed no toxicity.

Similarly, Jiang et al. [37] encapsulated BSA and bevacizumab in the hollow interior of a bi-layered implant for the treatment of wet AMD. The device inner layer was made of chitosan, and the outer layer was made of PCL. The preparation process involved the electrospinning of chitosan and PCL fibers around a rod to create the hollow interior for neat drug loading and posterior salt leaching for tuning the porosity of the system. In vitro release in PBS during 9 months showed an initial burst release followed by a steady release. The cumulative bevacizumab release was between 25 and 45% depending on the salt concentration used during the salt leaching process, whereas BSA release was greater for the same time period. BSA was measured by BCA assay, and bevacizumab release was determined by microplate reader after determining the drug absorbance using UV–Vis spectroscopy. It was determined that the percentage of bevacizumab aggregates were slightly above 10%. In vitro cytotoxicity was assessed by the MTS assay conducted on ARPE-19 cells. The implants were immersed in media for 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks, and 1 month. Then the media was transferred to the ARPE-19 cell culture, where measurements were performed 24 h later. The device showed proper biodegradation, good cell viability, and maintenance of the potency of bevacizumab.

Annuryanti et al. [38] studied triamcinolone acetonide (TA)-loaded PCL implants for drug delivery in the posterior segment of the eye. The implants were obtained by semi-solid extrusion 3D printing and did not need to use organic solvents during preparation. Three implant shapes were studied: filament-like, rectangular, and circular. Additionally, different ratios of low to high molecular weight PCL and surface area to volume were tested. In general, in vitro experiments over 180 days in PBS showed that the circular implants had the slowest release, followed by the rectangular implants, and the filament-shaped implants presented the fastest drug release. The cumulative drug release for the different formulations was between 54 and 100%. This was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Furthermore, no aggregates were observed under scanning electron microscopy (SEM). A biocompatibility assay based on resazurin reduction showed that the implants were non-cytotoxic for ARPE-19.

McAvoy et al. [39] studied drug-loaded PEG diacrylate (DA) implants for sustained drug delivery in the eye. The PEGDA implants were obtained by photocrosslinking of PEGDA at two different molecular weights: 250 and 700 Da; followed by the use of a pore formation agent. TA and ovalbumin were used as model drugs. Cumulative drug release experiments were performed in vitro using PBS and measured using HPLC. For TA-loaded PEGDA 700 implants, complete drug release was reached in PBS after 3–4 weeks, whereas for TA-loaded PEGDA 250 the cumulative drug released was between 25.61 and 53.51% after 252 days. For ovalbumin-loaded implants, an initial burst release was observed during the first 24 h. Additionally, ovalbumin-loaded PEGDA 700 showed a cumulative drug release between 13 and 63% after 84 days; whereas for PEGDA 250 the cumulative release was between 16 and 46%. Bioactivity of the drugs was maintained, and all the formulations were non-cytotoxic. This was determined in vitro on ARPE-19 cells using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) fluorescent staining and MTS assay. The cells were tested after 24 h of direct and indirect methods. In the direct method, the cells were directly in contact with the implant. For the indirect method, the cells were in contact with release media collected up to 7 days from the implant.

Composite systems

Composite systems are made up of two or more drug delivery systems to combine the main advantages of each type [71]. Examples of composite systems include embedded nano- or microparticles in hydrogels or implants, and nanoparticles within microparticles. For instance, nano- and microparticles usually present an initial burst release, and including them within an implant or hydrogel will provide a more controlled and extended release since the latter will act as a barrier to sudden drug release. Nanoparticles within microparticles also help achieve good protein stability since the drug can be encapsulated in a nanoparticle made from an appropriate polymer. Furthermore, nanoparticles can be within a microparticles to achieve a more controlled drug release profile throughout the release period [71]. Information about composite systems for intravitreal drug delivery can be found in Table 4.

A composite system consisting of PLGA nanoparticles and injectable hyaluronic acid (HA) hydrogel was studied by Hsu et al. [40]. The model drug used was BSA, and some aggregation was reported to occur during the preparation process. In vitro experiments involved MTT assay on ARPE-19 cells after they were exposed for 48 h to extracts from the devices, which were taken at different time points up to 1 month. Results showed that some of the obtained particles presented toxicity at initial time points, but the authors attributed it to the organic solvent used to dissolve the PLGA in the synthesis process and proposed methods to decrease toxicity. BSA-release profile was evaluated in vitro and measured by BCA assay. The formulations presented an initial burst release during the first day followed by a steady state release. After 56 days, the cumulative drug release of the formulations averaged between 11.70 and 14.94%.

Another composite system consisting of an implant containing drug-loaded nanoparticles for the treatment of choroidal neovascularization was developed by Badiee et al. [41]. The chitosan NPs were prepared by ionic gelation, whereas the implant was prepared from HA and zinc sulfate (ZnSO4) as cross-linker. Bevacizumab was used as the model drug. The in vitro experiments used diluted vitreous with 1% BSA as the release medium, and drug release was measured by ELISA. The results showed little bevacizumab inactivation and a small initial burst (< 10%) in the first 24 h followed by a slowdown in drug release; 47% of the cumulative drug load was released after 60 days. It is possible that some aggregation occurred, but this was not further evaluated. The implant was assumed to be biocompatible since it is made out of chitosan, but more studies assessing cytotoxicity of the composite system needed to be done.

A common type of composite system consists of nanoparticles within microparticles. For instance, Shmueli et al. [42] investigated injectable biodegradable polymeric composite particles for long-term treatment of ocular neovascularization. For the preparation of the NPs, polymer 2-(3-aminopropylamino)ethanol end-capped 1,3-propanediol diacrylate-co-3-amino-1-propanol) (B3-S3-E6) was used. The drug, a serpin-derived peptide (SP6001), was mixed with polymer B3-S3-E6, a biodegradable cationic polymer, to form peptide-polymer NPs. Then, the NPs were encapsulated into PLGA MPs by a double emulsion technique. In vivo experiments in rabbits showed that the anti-angiogenic effect of the DDS was maintained at least 14 weeks with a single injection as measured by fluorescence microscopy. The release profile started with a linear release followed by a slower release. Additionally, SP6001 showed low toxicity on human retinal endothelial cells (HRECs) after these cells were cultured during 48 h in medium containing the peptide.

Yandrapu et al. [43] studied bevacizumab-loaded NPs within porous MPs for treatment of posterior eye diseases. Plain PLGA MPs and poly (lactic acid) (PLA) NPs were prepared using the emulsion/solvent evaporation method. The plain PLA NPs were coated with bevacizumab by lyophilization, mixed with plain PLGA MPs, and then exposed to supercritical carbon dioxide (CO2). Formulations with different bevacizumab concentrations were tested, and it was shown that < 1% of the drug forms aggregates. In vitro release in PBS showed that for the PLA NP formulation with 10% w/w bevacizumab, the burst release in 1 day was 21%, and after 4 months the cumulative release was 81% as estimated by micro BCA test. Moreover, in vivo delivery in rats after intravitreal injection was evaluated with ocular fluorophotometry, showing that bevacizumab maintained its VEGF binding activity.

Elsaid et al. [44] prepared chitosan-based NPs entrapped in PLGA MPs for the intravitreal delivery of ranibizumab. Chitosan-N-acetyl-L-cysteine (CNAC), CNAC/tripolyphosphate (TPP), chitosan/TPP, and chitosan/TPP-HA were incorporated in PLGA MPs by a double-emulsion/solvent evaporation technique. In vitro release was studied for 180 days using ELISA, and all formulations maintained their structural integrity. CNAC formulation presented the highest entrapment efficiency and no burst release, but its release rate was the slowest. CNAC/TPP had faster release rate, but it presented burst release and the entrapment efficiency was reduced. Chitosan/TPP-HA had the fastest and near complete release of ranibizumab. Cell viability was studied on ARPE-19 and HUVECs by MTS assay. The MPs were added to the cell culture during 24 h before performing the MTS assay. Furthermore, a live/dead assay was also performed after 24 h of exposure. No significant toxicity was shown by any of the formulations in low concentration.

Modeling of drug release from polymer-based drug delivery systems

A general limitation of DDSs is the need for a better understanding of how they perform in the human eye before they are able to reach the market. Traditionally, this information has come mostly from diverse experiments in different phases of clinical trials. A complementary approach for guiding experimentation is to explore drug release properties via mathematical modeling.

Mathematical models help to predict drug transport and release rate from drug delivery systems. There is no single, overall model that can describe every mass transport mechanism and chemical reaction that could arise in all DDSs. Rather, different theories and models have been developed to describe drug release from different systems [45]. Depending on the mechanism controlling drug release, models can be classified as diffusion-controlled, swelling-controlled, and erosion-controlled [95]. For ocular drug delivery purposes, an ideal device would be one that is biodegradable, biocompatible, and can carry a significant amount of anti-VEGF so that a therapeutic concentration of the drug can be maintained in the eye for as long as possible. Usually, a diffusion-controlled drug release device that does not degrade will need surgical removal after being implanted. Therefore, swelling-controlled and erosion-controlled devices can help prevent additional patient burden but might not be able to load as much drug. However, it is important to note that drug delivery systems can also have combinations of these mechanism-controlling behaviors that may shift over time. In this section, we consider some of the mathematical models that could be applied to describe drug release from the previously presented DDSs. Other authors [96- 98] have considered a different approach that involves the use of machine learning techniques to attempt to predict drug release from various formulations; however, this approach is not discussed in this review paper. For more reviews exploring mathematical models of drug release from polymer-based drug delivery systems, the reader is referred to [45,79,95,99-105].

Diffusion-controlled release

Diffusion is the process in which individual molecules of a substance (drug) travel from one part of the system to another due to random molecular motion and a concentration (or chemical potential) gradient acting as a driving force [100]. In a diffusion-controlled mechanism, drug diffusion through the polymer is the rate-limiting step, and the rate of drug transfer usually can be described by Fick’s law [45]. A summary of diffusion-controlled mathematical models for drug release from DDSs is provided in Table 5.

Two common types of DDSs considered for diffusion-controlled release: (i) reservoir systems, which are DDSs made of a barrier membrane that is surrounding a drug depot, and (ii) matrix systems, which are DDSs where there is no bulk physical separation between the drug and the membrane [103], meaning that the drug is dispersed in the membrane. Moreover, both types of DDSs can be sub-classified in two groups: (i) drug concentration is above the maximum drug solubility in the DDS, or (ii) drug concentration is below the maximum drug solubility in the DDS [103]. This classification along with the geometry of the DDS influences the mathematical modeling.

In theory, ideal systems can be modeled easily. Some assumptions are commonly made for diffusion-controlled release modeling: (i) constant diffusivity, (ii) perfect sink conditions in medium at all time, and (iii) device does not significantly swell or erode during drug release [104].

In the case of a reservoir system of any geometry with initial concentration above maximum drug solubility, the barrier membrane’s surface concentration stays constant with time while there is excess drug. This is because released molecules are being replaced continuously, resulting in zero order kinetics [106]. However, once the drug concentration in the reservoir is below maximum drug solubility, the barrier membrane surface’s concentration decreases with time, resulting in first order kinetics [106]. The equations for both types of behavior were derived by Baker [106].

In the case of a matrix system with initial concentration above maximum drug solubility, Roseman and Higuchi [107] and Roseman [108] derived implicit equations that can describe drug release from spheres and cylinders. These equations state that the fraction of total drug released is directly proportional to diffusion constant, drug solubility, and time and inversely proportional to radius and initial drug concentration.

Once drug concentration is below maximum drug solubility, Fick’s second law of diffusion can be solved for each of these geometries, allowing the calculation of cumulative amount of drug release as a function of time. Crank [109] presented the analytical expressions for drug diffusion for spherical geometries, whereas the expression for a finite cylindrical geometry was derived by Vergnaud [110]. Since infinite series of exponential functions are used in these equations, those authors have also shown approximate solutions for short and late times.

Often diffusion-controlled release models assume perfect sink conditions (infinite external medium). However, Ibarra et al. [111] developed a mathematical model for predicting drug release from single-layer cylindrical implantable devices that incorporated an exact volume of the external medium as a parameter. The model assumptions included unswellable and nonerodible polymer, homogeneous initial drug distribution, initial drug load greater than drug solubility in the device, constant diffusion coefficient, and pseudosteady state approximation. Fick’s second law described the drug diffusion process, and mass transport occurred in one dimension only. The model showed good agreement with data reported in the literature for drug release in a finite medium but also in the limiting case of drug release in an infinite release medium.

Lu and Chen [112] derived an analytical solution for the case of drug release from a core-shell microparticle. The model was solved using the dimensionless form so that the solution could be kept general for different cases (e.g., finite volume extraction medium, infinite volume extraction medium, and infinite diffusivity in matrix or coating). Some general model assumptions included constant diffusivities of drug in the matrix and coating film, initial drug in matrix only, and no initial drug on shell or medium. Interface boundary conditions also included partition coefficients. The set of equations is solved by using Laplace transforms. The model was used for studying the effects of the different variables (diffusivity ratio, volume of external medium, thickness of coating film) on drug release.

Hadjitheodorou and Kalosakas [113] derived an analytical solution for the diffusion-controlled drug release problem from composite spherical matrices. These DDSs were composed of a drug-loaded inner core and an outer shell. The model assumed constant and different diffusion coefficients in each layer, homogeneous initial drug concentration within the sphere, and perfect sink conditions. The model was solved using a Laplace transform approach. Furthermore, those authors also developed a stochastic model based on Monte Carlo simulations where a spherical region is considered inside a cubic lattice. This spherical region represented the composite sphere, which was 50% occupied by drug. A diffusivity ratio was introduced based on lower diffusion coefficient over higher diffusion coefficient. Diffusion was simulated by randomly selecting a drug particle and if this particle was in the highest diffusion area, it would move randomly to a near neighboring site. If the drug was in the lowest diffusion area it stayed in its position with a probability equivalent to the diffusivity ratio, otherwise or it tried to move to a neighboring site. Movement was only allowed if the destination site was empty. Both the analytical and stochastic models were compared against the fitted Weibull function and the models were in good agreement.

A model for predicting drug release from multi-layer spherical capsules was developed by Kaoui et al. [114]. The model assumed that drug dissolution was fast compared to drug diffusion. The drug diffusion in the core, surrounding layers, and release medium was described by Fick’s second law. Drug concentration was not necessarily constant at the core surface and not necessarily uniform in the release medium. At each interface, flux continuity was the applied boundary condition. Drug partitioning between adjacent layers was also considered. An external shell was considered as semi-permeable and of finite thickness, and it allowed for a jump in the concentration value. In this model the external medium was confined by a finite length beyond which the drug concentration was considered to remain sufficiently small and constant. Similarly, Carr and Pontrelli [115] derived the analytical solution for the case of a semi-infinite external medium. In this model the surrounding medium was considered to be several orders of magnitude larger than the sphere itself.

Furthermore, analytical solutions are not always possible since real conditions can differ significantly from ideal conditions, and the assumptions might not be acceptable. In these cases, it is necessary to turn to numerical methods to determine drug release from DDSs. For instance, Van Kampen et al. [116] used the finite element method to determine drug release from cylindrical devices loaded with drug. Fick’s second law was used to model the unsteady-state diffusion in multiple dimensions. The device’s geometry was designed for high payload and was not a solid cylinder with ideal boundary conditions; instead, it consisted of a hollow cylinder surrounded by a polymeric layer and a polymeric base. Furthermore, one end of the cylinder was set to be impermeable. The modeled DDS had drug release radially and along the axis but not through the impermeable end.

Swelling-controlled release

Swelling is related to polymers that need to absorb surrounding solvent before drug delivery can occur [99]. This produces non-Fickian diffusion since the release depends on factors such as polymer disentanglement and dissolution [101]. Semi-empirical and mechanistic models are considered to address drug release from swelling DDSs.

Models relying to some extent on observation and experimentation have been developed, e.g. [117-119]. Relevant aspects of these models in the case of swelling-controlled drug release can be found in Table 6. A semi-empirical equation to describe drug release from swelling polymers was developed by Peppas and Korsmeyer [117]. It assumed constant diffusivities, constant dimensions of the device during release, one-dimensional diffusional behavior, and perfect sink conditions. This power law established a relationship between drug release and time. Later, Peppas and Sahlin [118] proposed a modification to the model were the Fickian contribution and the relaxational contribution were considered separately. Additionally, another modified version of the Peppas-Korsmeyer equation was proposed by Kim and Fassihi [119] to include lag time and initial burst.

Mechanistic models based on the swelling phenomena have also been formulated, e.g. [120-125]. A summary of the important information regarding these models in the case of swelling-controlled drug release can be found in Table 7. Peppas et al. [120] derived a one-dimensional drug diffusion model for swelling polymers. This model was developed for the case of a homogeneously distributed drug within a polymer matrix. The drug was assumed to diffuse countercurrent to a solvent penetrating the polymer. Due to volume changes of the system, the problem was formulated as a moving boundary diffusion problem. Concentration continuity was the boundary condition used at the polymer/gel interface. Furthermore, the drug diffusion coefficients in the polymer and gel phases were assumed to be constant. Experimental results for concentration of water and potassium chloride within a HPMC matrix tablet agreed with the mathematical solution of the model.

A mathematical model to predict drug release from hydrophilic polymer matrices during swelling and dissolution was derived by Ju et al. [121,122]. Initially, two scaling laws were developed [121] considering the polymer disentanglement, and matrix dissolution in the medium. These scaling laws described the relationship between HPMC equivalent molecular weight and fraction of polymer released, and the relationship between HPMC equivalent molecular weight and fraction of drug released. Furthermore, the comprehensive model for predicting drug release from the hydrophilic polymer matrices was developed considering the previous scaling laws [122]. The moving boundary problem was transformed to a fixed boundary problem. The model assumptions included anisotropic polymer expansion, radial mass transfer of both polymer and drug, negligible polymer relaxation, much faster drug dissolution than drug diffusion, and diffusion coefficient that exponentially decays with polymer concentration. The model was validated with experimental data of adinazolam mesylate release from HPMC-based matrices.

Siepmann et al. [123] and Siepmann and Peppas [124] developed a mathematical model for predicting the drug release from hydrophilic matrices. The model [123] considered counter diffusion of water and drug, a moving boundary, concentration-dependent diffusivity, negligible dissolution processes, and homogeneous swelling. Mass transport was considered in both the axial and radial directions for a cylindrical geometry. This model assumed perfect sink conditions, no volume contraction upon mixing, much faster drug dissolution than drug diffusion, and volume increase proportional to relative surface area. Fick’s second law described water and drug diffusion. Parameters were known or fitted. Furthermore, the model was improved to incorporate inhomogeneous swelling, poorly water-soluble drugs, and high initial drug loading [124]. Ideal thermodynamic behavior of the system was assumed besides the previous assumptions. The model successfully predicted theophylline release from HPMC-based matrix tablets.

Borgquist et al. [125] developed a mathematical model to predict drug release from swelling and dissolving cylindrical polymer matrices. The mass transfer rate considered both diffusion and swelling (diffusion-induced convection). The expansion in the radial and axial directions were considered. Polymer dissolution started once the polymer entanglement strength was reduced due to an increase in penetrant concentration. The transition from solid to gel phase was assumed to be limited by the rate of solvent penetration into the solid phase. It was also assumed that drug transport only occurred in the solvent phase and that the effect of drug transport in solvent transport was negligible. The equations describing polymer dissolution, water mass balance, drug release, and polymer and drug fractions in the bulk were solved numerically by the finite volume method. The model was validated with methylparaben and saligenin release from a poly(ethylene oxide) matrix.

Erosion-controlled release

Erosion is the process in which material is lost from the polymer bulk. This material can be monomers, oligomers, and portions of polymer backbone or polymer bulk [102]. There are two main types: surface erosion and bulk erosion. In surface erosion the rate of degradation of the polymer is much faster than the rate of solvent uptake into the polymer bulk [102]; therefore, the polymer degrades from the exterior. In bulk erosion the intrusion of solvent into the polymer is much faster than the rate of degradation [102]; therefore, the polymer degrades throughout the whole bulk. Empirical and mechanistic models are considered to address drug release from eroding DDSs.

Models based on observations have been developed, and a summary of some empirical mathematical models for erosion-controlled drug release is provided in Table 8. For instance, Hopfenberg [126] assumed that the release rate was proportional to the surface area of the device and was characterized by a single rate constant. The overall release process had a zero-order release rate, meaning that it was irrelevant if the process corresponds to a single phenomenon or the superposition of several processes. This model can be applied to surface-eroding systems regardless of the geometry. Cooney [127] developed a similar model assuming that the release was proportional to the surface area and that the overall process was a zero-order release rate. In this model, two steps composed the dissolution of nonporous homogeneous solid. First, an atom, molecule, or ion detached from the solid surface. Then, it diffused to the bulk fluid through an adjacent stationary solvent. The model can be applied to surface-eroding systems of any geometry.

Models involving randomness of erosion events can also be found in the literature. Relevant aspects of mechanistic-probabilistic models in the case erosion-controlled drug release are summarized in Table 9. Zygourakis [128] and Zygourakis and Markenscoff [129] modeled drug release from bioerodible pellets as a random event, based on cellular automata and discrete iterations. The model [128] represented the polymer matrix in a 2D grid composed of pixels, and each pixel represented a small volume element of either drug, polymer, solvent, or void. Each pixel type was assigned a “lifetime” that started decreasing when in contact with solvent. The model simulation started with one end of the polymeric matrix in contact with solvent. As time went by, each pixel changed according to the expected behavior, e.g., after a drug pixel contacted the solvent, it dissolved and was replaced by a solvent pixel. It was assumed that the internal voids were not initially accessible to the solvent, but they opened up as the erosion front moved. The model also assumed that there was no diffusional resistance, and, therefore, drug dissolution rate equaled drug release rate. Another assumption was that surface detachment was the rate-limiting step, thus the model can be applied to surface-eroding systems. Later improvement of the model [129] allowed to model drug particles of different shapes or sizes, pores and polymer matrix, and crystalline and amorphous areas.

Another approach for stochastic modeling of eroding polymers was proposed by Göpferich and Langer [130,132] and Göpferich [131]. In general, the polymer matrix was represented in a 2D grid, composed of individual pixels that could be either crystalline polymers or amorphous areas. Amorphous pixels had higher likelihood to erode. Polymer pixels were assigned lifetimes according to first-order Erlang distributions. It was assumed that the erosion of a pixel could be described by a Poisson process of first order and the pixel degraded when the lifetime of the pixel expired. For surface-eroding polymers, once a polymer pixel was in contact with an eroded neighbor, it was assigned an individual life expectation that immediately started decreasing [130]. For bulk-eroding polymers, a polymer pixel was assigned an individual life expectation before the simulation started and was considered eroded after it came in contact with a pore [131]. After erosion proceeded, parameters such as porosity were calculated from the model and coupled to Fick’s law in 1D to predict drug release [132]. The drug only diffused inside of pores and not in non-eroded polymer.

Similarly, Siepmann et al. [133] modeled polymer degradation as a random process. A 2D grid was defined in which each pixel represented either polymer or drug, and all pixels had the same height but different widths. Also, each pixel possessed randomly distributed lifetimes, which started to decrease once in contact with water. Amorphous polymer pixels eroded faster than crystalline ones and, therefore, had shorter lifetimes. Ideal mixing and perfect sink conditions were assumed. At each time step, drug and water concentration were calculated at each grid point. Once the lifetime reached zero, the pixel was assumed to erode instantaneously. Knowing which pixels had eroded allowed calculation of the porosity in the radial and axial directions. Subsequently, the porosities were used to calculate the diffusivities of the drug in both directions and therefore Fick’s law was used to describe drug release. The model can be applied for sphere-shaped, bulk-eroding systems.

Deterministic models based on the erosion phenomena have also been formulated. Relevant information regarding mechanistic-deterministic models in the case of erosion-controlled drug release is summarized in Table 10. Two groups of mathematical models describing drug delivery from bulk- and surface-eroding porous polymeric microspheres were proposed by Zhang et al. [134]. The overall release process involved the simultaneous polymer erosion, drug dissolution, and drug diffusion. Three phases in the system were considered in the model: (i) liquid phase, (ii) virtual solid phase, and (iii) effective solid phase. For bulk erosion, an erosion term was incorporated into the dissolution and diffusion equation, and three erosion patterns were considered. Homogeneous erosion and variable diffusivity, porosity and tortuosity were assumed. The results from the bulk-eroding model were validated by comparison with experimental results of FITC-dextran release from PVA-g-PLGA and inmunoglobulin G from PLA. For surface erosion, a substitution was used to transform the moving-boundary problem to a fixed-boundary problem. Low absorbed solvent, constant diffusivity, and negligible drug diffusion in the solid phase were assumed. The results from the bulk-eroding model were validated by comparison with experimental results of BSA release from poly(trimellitylimido-L-tyro-sine-co-sebacic acid-co-1,3-bis(carboxyphenoxy)-propane). For both the bulk and surface erosion model, the physical properties of the DDS and drug must be determined experimentally for useful results.

Stiepel et al. [135] developed a mechanistic model to predict drug release from drug-loaded acetalated dextran NPs. Assumptions to the model included surface erosion as the degradation mechanism, and constant effective diffusivity. Two constitutive equations composed the mathematical model: Fick’s second law of diffusion in spherical co-ordinates and polymer radius decrease as a first order degradation process. Most model parameters and initial values were obtained empirically. Different drug release curves were fitted within the diffusion-erosion model. When compared to other drug release models, the diffusion-erosion model had high predictive capability at neutral and acidic pH. Furthermore, a neural network model was developed to predict effective diffusivity from drug release data of different drugs, and it was coupled to the diffusion-erosion model. This coupling successfully predicted drug release from acetalated dextran NPs.

Morozova et al. [136] developed a holistic continuum model for predicting drug release from a PLA cylindrical matrix. The model considered penetration of the solution into the DDS, drug dissolution, drug diffusion, polymer dissolution, and matrix collapse. A representative volume inside the DDS could contain solid drug, polymer, solution, and dissolved drug. Additionally, some fraction of the representative volume was occupied by voids or pores. Since the polymer was degrading, porosity was considered to be a function of position and time. Fick’s law was used to describe drug and solvent diffusion. It was assumed that there was no dissolved drug initially and that drug was released in the radial direction only. Furthermore, to get to the continuum scale, the drug particles were assumed to be spherical, identical, and evenly distributed in the matrix.

Batycky et al. [137] derived a mathematical model to quantify hydrophilic macromolecular drug release from bulk-eroding PLGA microspheres. The model considered the coalescing micropores and spatially evolving mesopores in the polymer using a combined random-chain scission and end-chain scission mechanism. Burst release of the macromolecule was controlled by the rate of macromolecule desorption from the MP surface and preexisting mesopores. The rest of the drug was released in a Fickian manner after the induction phase. Some of the assumptions included: (i) pseudo steady-state approximation, (ii) cylindrical and randomly distributed pores, (iii) instantaneous hydration in comparison to erosion, and (iv) constant effective diffusivity. The model was validated with experimental data of envelope glycoprotein GP120 release from PLGA microspheres.

Faisant et al. [138] developed a mathematical model to predict drug release from bulk-eroding spherical PLGA microparticles. The model considered polymer degradation, drug diffusion, and initial distribution of the drug within the system. Drug diffusivity was a function of molecular weight of the polymer. Erosion was considered to have pseudo first-order kinetics. The model neglected any polymer breakdown and autocatalytic effects. Unknown parameters were obtained by fitting the model with experimental data. The model results were compared with experimental data of fluorouracil release from PLGA microparticles, and the predicted release was in agreement with the experimental release.

Ford et al. and later Ford Versypt [139,140] proposed a reaction-diffusion model to predict drug release from PLGA microspheres that underwent autocatalytic degradation. The autocatalytic degradation was modeled as either a pseudo first-order or a second-order process. The developed model considered acid-catalyzed degradation kinetics, molecular weight distribution variation, and drug transport with varying diffusivity. The diffusivity increased as a function of void fraction in the polymer bulk. The kinetics mechanism was coupled to a diffusion and pore evolution model to incorporate spatial variations in the degradation rate for all species within the microspheres (drug, acid, small dissolveable oligomer fragments, and lumped non-diffusing large oligomers). The model assumed that reactions occurred throughout the polymer volume, full dissociation of carboxylic acid end groups, and constant concentration of excess water. This model was solved using the method of lines and finite differences. Ford Versypt et al. also derived an analytical solution for the model [140,141]. In this case, PLGA erosion was treated assuming that all carboxylic acid end groups had a uniform constant diffusion coefficient. The model was solved for the reaction-dominant and diffusion-dominant limit cases. Analytical solutions were compared to numerical solutions in the limiting cases, showing good agreement.

Similarly, Sevim and Pan [142] developed a mathematical model for predicting polymer degradation and drug release from PLGA microspheres. Three mechanisms of PLGA degradation were considered: hydrolysis reactions, autocatalytic effects of carboxylic acid end groups, and further acid catalysis based on drug dissociation. The model kept track of the concentration of ester bonds of short and long polymer chains, protons, and drug. Short polymer chains were assumed to be water-soluble and could diffuse to the medium. Additional assumptions included instantaneous acid dissociation of oligomers and drug and excess water concentration so that it did not affect the rate of polymer scission. Fick’s law was used to describe drug and short polymer chains diffusion through the DDS. Since polymer degradation increased the porosity inside the matrix, effective diffusivities were calculated. The model was validated by comparison with experimental data of ibuprofen release from a PLGA 50:50 matrix.

Ocular pharmacokinetic modeling of drugs released from intravitreal polymer-based DDSs

Predicting drug release from a DDS in an in vitro medium is not sufficient alone to predict its in vivo efficacy in maintaining a therapeutic drug concentration in the target region of the eye. Ocular pharmacokinetics (PK) models aim to describe the dynamics of drug concentration in the eye by incorporating the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion of a drug into the models [143]. For these models to be effectively implemented in the eye, information on the biophysical properties of the involved ocular tissues is important [144]. Experimental and analytical studies have been conducted to accurately measure diffusion coefficients of drugs and drugs surrogates, as well as hydraulic resistance, in the different ocular tissues [145-151].

Several papers have studied the PK modeling of drugs after being injected directly into the vitreous [152-162]. Other studies have addressed the PK modeling of drugs after being delivered directly or within a DDS through the periocular [163-169] or topical routes [170-175]. Here, this section covers papers that studied PK modeling of drugs released from DDSs after intravitreal injection. Two main types of PK models are considered in this review: classical compartmental models and physiologically based models (Fig. 5). The reader is also referred to other broader reviews on ocular PK [47,64,79,176-178].

Classical compartmental models

Classical compartmental models contain one or more compartments that represent an anatomical space as a well-mixed, spatially homogeneous unit. They typically assume a first-order material transfer between compartments [179]. Relevant aspects of compartmental models for intravitreal drug delivery are tabulated in Table 11.

A one-compartment model to describe drug concentration in the vitreous from microspheres was derived by Sarkhel et al. [180]. Experimental cumulative drug release data was fitted, and the obtained equation was then differentiated to describe the release rate with time. This experimental rate was used as the model input for each drug. For the output rate from the vitreous, anatomical volume of drug distribution and clearance values for drug elimination were obtained from literature. The input and output rate defined the amount of drug at a given time in the vitreous. Therefore, the concentration profile was given by the ratio of amount of drug over vitreous volume. However, the PK aspects of the model were not validated.

Another one-compartment model was developed by Del Amo et al. [181] to predict the PK behavior of drugs released from DDSs with zero- and first-order release kinetics after intravitreal injection. The authors developed a multivariate quantitative structure properties relationship (QSPR) model, which was then fitted for different drugs using data from literature. For the PK model, two main PK parameters were incorporated: intravitreal volume of distribution (Vss) and clearance (CLv). CLv was calculated from the QSPR model and used as input in the PK model, while Vss was obtained from the literature. The PK model was validated for drug solutions of heptanol and amikacin, and the predicted behavior was close to the observed behavior.

Rimpela et al. [182] included a second compartment besides the vitreous: the aqueous humor. This compartmental model was developed to describe drug concentration in the vitreous after release from a drug delivery device. Clearance by the vitreous was negligible, and the drug elimination pathway was through the anterior route. Parameter values were taken from the literature. Results from the model were compared to available experimental data, and concentration profiles of bevacizumab, ranibizumab, rituximab, conbercept, and aflibercept in the vitreous and aqueous humor were accurately described by the model. For the PK simulation of the DDS, it was assumed to be injected in the middle of the vitreous and to have a zero-order drug release profile.

A linear three-compartment model for drug release from an implant in the vitreous was proposed by Tamhane et al. [183]. The DDS was assumed to be placed in the vitreous, and the drug distributed bidirectionally to and from the aqueous humor and the retina. Drug elimination occurred only from the aqueous humor. Volume of drug distribution was fixed to the specific anatomical characteristic of the ocular tissues. Parameter estimates were obtained from fitting experimental data, and the model results were similar to experimental data of brimonidine concentration in aqueous humor, retina, and vitreous in monkeys after implant injection. Afterwards, the model predicted brimonidine concentration in human vitreous, aqueous humor, and retina by using the same fitted parameters but considering the anatomical volume/weight of these tissues in humans.

Dosmar et al. [184] suggested a three-compartment model to describe drug flow through the posterior ocular tissues following intravitreal administration of a vancomycin (VAN)-loaded DDS. The compartmental model for the vitreous included bidirectional flow between vitreous and retina and between vitreous and aqueous chamber. Each compartment also had its own elimination pathway. The fitted curve of experimental cumulative release of VAN from the DDS was used as input in the PK model. The rate constants were obtained from the literature, and all compartments were assumed to have the same surface area (1 cm2). Furthermore, drug bioavailability was assumed to be constant. However, the model was not validated in vivo due to incompleteness of data. Additionally, the authors also proposed compartmental models for subconjunctival, topical, and subretinal routes.

Physiologically based models

Ocular physiologically based models take into account the geometry of the eye. These models are an improvement on classical compartmental models. They associate compartments with specific tissues and incorporate their anatomical and physiological aspects [179]. These models allow simulation of the spatial concentration profiles in the different ocular tissues. A summary of the physiologically based models for intravitreal drug delivery is provided in Table 12.

Tojo and Isowaki [185] derived a mathematical model to predict elimination and distribution of drugs in the posterior segment of the eye. The model was based on Fick’s second law of diffusion. It assumed a cylindrical vitreous body in contact with the retina/choroids membrane, the lens posterior capsule, and the posterior aqueous humor, which all three acted as elimination pathways. Model parameters were obtained from experimental results. The drug source was either a reservoir-type or matrix-type delivery system. The model was validated in rabbits’ eyes by comparing experimental results from a ganciclovir-releasing device with the calculated results from the model.

A PK model using the 3D geometry of the rabbit eye was developed by Park et al. [186]. The purpose of the model was to simulate drug concentration in the eye after administration by intravitreal injection of a drug solution or implant for the treatment of retinal disease. The model considered twelve sections of the anatomy of the eye: sclera, retina, choroid, vitreous, lens, posterior chamber, anterior chamber, iris, ciliary processes, hyaloid membrane, Schlemm’s canal, and cornea. Two elimination pathways were considered: the aqueous outflow pathway and the vitreous outflow pathway. Both convection and diffusion terms were included in mass transport equations. The generation of aqueous humor was modeled as a source of fluid. The parameters were obtained from both literature and experimental data fitting. The model was validated with literature experimental data of fluorescein concentration in rabbit vitreous after drug injection.

Heljak and Swieszkowski [187] developed a model to simulate bevacizumab concentration in the rabbit eye after intravitreal injection of drug-loaded PLGA microspheres. The model was based on diffusive-convective drug transport. Drug release from the microspheres was modeled as a function of the surface area of the spheres, drug diffusion coefficient, drug concentration, and drug solubility. Drug diffusion coefficient and permeabilities were obtained from the literature. Perfect sink conditions were assumed on the microsphere surface. It was also assumed that the only way of drug clearance was through the trabecular mesh. Furthermore, all ocular tissues were assumed to be porous except for the aqueous body. The aqueous body was regarded as a well-stirred compartment. The results obtained from the model were in good agreement with experimental data of bevacizumab release from PLGA nano- and microspheres.

The 3D geometry of the human eye has also been used in PK models. For instance, Stay et al. [188] developed a 3D mathematical model for describing the vitreous drug concentration after release from an intravitreal controlled release device. The model geometry included the dimensions of the vitreous humor, aqueous humor, lens, hyaloid membrane, retina, and sclera. A square-root-of-time release form was chosen to be representative of the drug release profile from the DDS. Both diffusive and convective mass transports were taken into account. The vitreous was assumed to be an incompressible porous medium so that Darcy flow equations were applicable. The source term was a localized non-displaceable source, and drug degradation and metabolism were neglected. The model included both active and passive transport terms. Parameters were obtained from previous experiments. This model was missing PK validation.

Similarly, Jooybar et al. [189] proposed a mathematical model for predicting drug concentration in the posterior segment of the eye. The model was a 3D convective-diffusive transport model based on human eye geometry. Vitreous was assumed to be stagnant and incompressible; therefore, Darcy equations applied. Active transport by RPE was also incorporated. Parameters were obtained from either the literature or from fitting the model with experimental fluorescein data. The model prediction was similar to available experimental data of fluorescein concentration in the vitreous. The effect of the position of the implant on the concentration profile in the posterior segment of the eye was also evaluated.

Dosmar et al. [184] also developed a 3D human eye model for drug concentration in the posterior segment of the eye following intravitreal administration of the VAN-loaded DDS. Similar to the compartmental model, the fitted curve of experimental cumulative release of VAN from the DDS was used as input. The COMSOL model was obtained using three 2D work planes for each tissue. Within the work plane, a curve with the dimensions of the respective tissue was constructed and revolved around the central axis. A solid sphere represented the vitreous humor. The DDS was represented with the geometry of a standard contact lens. A couple of revolving layers were also used: one around the DDS to simulate the rate of loss, and a second one around the vitreous to simulate vitreal drug loss. Material and transport properties were specified in each layer, and initial drug concentration in these layers was set to zero. The model was not validated with in vivo human data. The authors also used the model to study drug concentration profiles for subconjunctival, topical, and subretinal routes.

Additionally, analytical and computational models for describing drug release from implants in the vitreous humor was formulated by Khoobyar et al. [190]. For the analytical model, a singular perturbation analysis was performed. DDS was assumed to be a sphere in the vitreous, and convective flow was described by Darcy’s flow equation in porous medium. Mass transfer was modeled with the convection-diffusion equation. Concentration far away from the DDS was taken to be zero. At the border of the DDS, there was a continuity of mass flux, but the concentration was allowed to jump across the membrane. The analytical solution was obtained for Péclet number (Pe)< 1, where diffusion pre-dominates over advection. The computational analysis was based on a 3D geometry of the human eye. The eye was assumed to be a spherical segment, excluding the lens portion. Mass transport was modeled with the convection-diffusion equation. Momentum-conservation was modeled with Brinkman flow model to keep the no-slip condition on the capsule surface. This geometric model can handle moderate Pe numbers. The model was validated comparing the results of the computational model with the results of the analytical model for Pe< 1.

Li et al. [191] developed a model to predict drug concentration in the eye after intravitreal injection of a hydrogel-based DDS. The geometry was based on the human eye, including the vitreous, retina, choroid, sclera, macula, ciliary body, cornea, lens, and hyaloid membrane. The posterior segment of the eye was assumed to be incompressible porous media and was governed by Darcy’s law. The vitreous was taken as an isothermal, uniform, and incompressible Newtonian fluid. Each region of the eye was described by the convective-diffusion equation, considering the respective transport properties of each region. Most of the parameters used in the simulations were obtained from the literature, but some diffusion coefficients were estimated. The model was validated with experimental results from the literature. The authors studied the effect of DDS position and hydrogel mesh size on the drug concentration profile in the retina.

Kathawate and Acharya [192] proposed a model for studying drug delivery in human vitreous chamber with a vitreous substitute. The governing equations were the continuity, momentum, and species transport equations. Silicone oil was considered as a vitreous substitute, and water was used to represent a liquefied vitreous. The geometry of the human eye included the lens, hyaloid membrane, vitreous body, retina, and sclera. A no-flux boundary condition was applied on the lens. The retina and sclera were both treated as porous zones with different permeabilities. The DDS was assumed to be located at the center of the vitreous chamber and was modeled as a source term. The authors considered a range of diffusion coefficients for the drug in the vitreous substitute, since there was no available data. Drug concentration at the outer of the retina was set to zero. Permeability of the retina and sclera was obtained from the literature. The drug concentration results obtained from the model were in agreement with experimental data.

Ferreira et al. [193] developed a mathematical model to predict drug concentration in different eye tissues following an intravitreal injection of an implant. The geometry included the sclera, choroid, retina, and vitreous humor. The hyaloid membrane, the RPE, and the blood-retinal barrier were considered as interface conditions. The authors considered a biodegradable implant, therefore, its diffusion coefficient was variable. The vitreous humor was regarded as a static, porous, and incompressible medium. The retina was considered a porous medium with a unidirectional pumping effect. The choroid was regarded as a highly porous medium. The sclera was assumed to have diffusion only. The diffusion-convection equation was applied for the different domains with their respective parameters. The parameters used were obtained from different animal models found in the literature. The model results agreed with the expected range of dexamethasone concentration in monkey vitreous after 2 and 7 months of release from an implant. The authors also studied the transscleral route. Subsequently, Ferreira et al. [194] expanded the model by considering the viscoelastic properties of the polymeric matrix and the influence of different factors on the biodegradation of the implant in the drug concentration profile.

Conclusions

Wet AMD needs to be treated with anti-VEGF drugs that are commonly intravitreally injected. However, due to poor patient compliance and possible complications, the use of polymer-based drug delivery systems is highly appealing to reduce the frequency of injections. Different types of drug delivery systems have been proposed for long-term anti-VEGF release, such as particulate systems, hydrogels, implants, and composite systems. The drug delivery systems’ polymer materials and preparation methods influence the encapsulation and loading efficiencies, as well as the drug release profiles.

Correct prediction of drug release from DDSs requires the selection of the proper mathematical model. The greater the predictive power and precision of the model, the more information about the device is needed (type of polymer, loaded drug, size, shape, composition, degradation mechanism, etc). However, simpler empirical models are also available, and they are particularly helpful when the mass transport mechanism is not known. Furthermore, mathematical modeling of drug delivery has the potential to help understand and predict complex delivery systems.

PK modeling allows predicting drug concentration in different tissues. Simpler models rely on the representation of an anatomical tissue as a “compartment”, whereas more complex models are based on the geometric features of the eye. By being able to predict the PK behavior of an anti-VEGF drug in different ocular tissues, the time and cost of experimentation is reduced. The main difficulty of PK modeling is that data of ocular concentrations in humans is scarce due to ethical issues, and the validation of the models rely on animal data.

Moreover, a persistent challenge of the field consists in combining the mathematical models describing drug delivery with the pharmacokinetic models describing drug concentration in the different tissues of the eye. In theory, this should allow for the quantitative prediction of both drug release and drug concentration time profiles. This is a complex type of modeling that could allow the development of much more efficient drug delivery systems for the treatment of wet AMD and other diseases that need treatments delivered to the posterior segment of the eye.

Future directions of the field involve enhancing long-term drug delivery to the target tissues in the posterior segment of the eye through advanced drug delivery devices, refining mathematical model predictions of in vivo release dynamics, and exploring sustained delivery of dual or triple therapies to address the multifactorial pathogenesis of AMD. Another promising direction is investigation of the use of PROTACs as a means to degrade VEGF. Furthermore, gene delivery is another pathway that is being explored. All these areas have the potential to reduce patient burden and improve wet AMD treatment.
