Immature Surfactant Protein Type-B Associates With Graft Patency in Patients Undergoing Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting
Authors: Sonia Eligini, Erica Gianazza, Fabrizio Veglia, Lisa Brocca, Laura Cavallotti, Giulia Giusy Papaianni, Daniele Andreini, Mauro Pepi, Gianluca Polvani, Elena Tremoli, Alessandro Parolari, Piergiuseppe Agostoni, Cristina Banfi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery is performed in patients with complex multivessel coronary artery disease, but long-term graft occlusion remains a major limitation. The immature surfactant protein type-B (proSP-B) has emerged as a predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes.ObjectivesThe purpose of this study was to examine the relationship between preoperative plasma proSP-B and graft occlusion 18 months post-CABG. The aims were to evaluate whether elevated proSP-B could predict graft occlusion and to assess its predictive value within a multivariable model for long-term graft patency.MethodsWe evaluated proSP-B in relation to graft occlusion in 40 patients with occluded grafts (cases) and 130 patients without occlusions (noncases), with 18-month graft patency assessed by coronary computed tomography angiography and/or invasive angiography. Logistic regression assessed the association between proSP-B and graft occlusion. Predictive performance was evaluated using receiver-operating characteristic curves and category-free net reclassification improvement.ResultsPreoperative proSP-B was significantly elevated in cases compared to noncases (24.0 [IQR: 19.8-31.0] AU vs 19.4 [IQR: 14.0-25.0] AU; P = 0.002). In a multivariable analysis, proSP-B associated with increased risk of graft occlusion, even after adjusting for factors like D-dimer, left ventricular ejection fraction, and extracorporeal circulation time. Including proSP-B in a reference model improved the area under the curve from 0.7204 to 0.7733, significantly enhancing patient classification (net reclassification improvement = 0.43; SE = 0.18; P = 0.02).ConclusionsElevated preoperative proSP-B is independently associated with 18-month graft occlusion following CABG. Including proSP-B improves existing predictive models, supporting its role as a novel biomarker for risk stratification in surgical revascularization.Key words: coronary artery bypass graft, graft occlusion, surfactant protein type B

Coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery is among the suggested therapies for patients with complex multivessel coronary artery disease and/or left main disease, diabetes, or reduced left ventricular function.1,2

Despite significant advancements in surgical techniques, graft design, and peri-operative care, graft occlusion is the “Achilles' heel” of this procedure, occurring in many CABG conduits. It has been shown that saphenous graft occlusion occurs in approximately 25% of patients within 18-month follow-up.3 Additionally, a recent meta-analysis of individual patient data from 7 contemporary CABG trials found that 33.7% of patients experienced at least one graft failure within 1 year after CABG.4 Graft failure is a multifactorial process influenced by patient-specific, procedural, and biological factors.5 Pathophysiological mechanisms contributing to graft occlusion include early thrombotic events, intimal hyperplasia, and, in the long term, the development of de novo atherosclerotic lesions. In this context, systemic biological processes such as oxidative stress, inflammation, platelet activation, and endothelial dysfunction are increasingly recognized as key contributors.5 Thus, the identification of reliable circulating biomarkers that reflect these biological pathways and are predictive of graft failure may improve preoperative risk stratification and guide personalized therapeutic strategies.

Surfactant protein-B (SP-B) is a member of the saposin-like protein family, required for the biophysical stability of pulmonary surfactant. It is synthesized as a 42-kDa precursor protein (proteoform of SP-B, proSP-B) and undergoes complex post-translational processing to its mature, functional form. While SP-B is primarily expressed in alveolar epithelial type II cells, its immature circulating proteoform, proSP-B, has consistently been linked to alveolar-capillary barrier dysfunction and poor clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure (HF).6, 7, 8, 9 We have previously demonstrated that circulating proSP-B holds potential as a theranostic biomarker, due to its capability of functioning as a diagnostic and therapeutic biomarker.8,10 Furthermore, we have shown that proSP-B's binding to human high-density lipoprotein particles impairs their antioxidant capacity,11 likely exacerbating oxidative stress, a well-known mediator in HF pathogenesis.12 Additionally, Cardner et al identified SP-B as the most significantly enriched protein in high-density lipoprotein among patients with coronary heart disease.13 Consequently, we hypothesized that elevated preoperative levels of proSP-B may be associated with an increased risk of graft occlusion following CABG surgery, owing to shared mechanisms such as oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and inflammation that underlie both alveolar and vascular damage.

In addition, previous findings from our group demonstrated an association between circulating mercaptoalbumin (HSA-SH) a reduced, antioxidant isoform of serum albumin, and graft patency following CABG.14 Given its relevance in redox balance, we included HSA-SH in this study as a secondary analysis to assess whether redox status might confound or modulate the relationship between proSP-B and graft outcomes.

Therefore, in search of biomarkers that may predict graft occlusion and possibly suggest strategies to reduce graft failure, we assessed preoperative levels of circulating proSP-B and its association with the risk of graft occlusion at 18 months in logistic regression models, including different sets of covariates, such as D-dimer, a well-known marker of activated coagulation that has recently been found to be associated with graft occlusion.15

Methods

Study population

In this study, we analyzed samples from a plasma biorepository of a cohort of 330 consecutive patients enrolled for elective primary surgical myocardial revascularization between November 2006 and February 2010 at Centro Cardiologico Monzino (CCM) IRCCS. This is a post hoc analysis of data and plasma samples collected within the framework of the prospective CAGE study (NCT00755248).16 The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of CCM (CCMS90/108) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Patients with other concomitant surgery, major end-organ dysfunction, coagulation disorders, serious undercurrent illness or infection, serum creatinine level >2 mg/dL, and atrial fibrillation were excluded.

The postoperative therapy followed guidelines for coronary artery disease and surgical myocardial revascularization including always at least single antiplatelet therapy, optimized blood pressure control therapy, and statin. All patients with graft occlusion underwent clinical and instrumental evaluation (echocardiogram, ischemia test, coronary angiography, etc) according to guidelines. All coronary computed tomography angiography and/or coronary angiography images were analyzed by 2 investigators and in case of disagreement the images were reviewed collegially, possibly with a third specialist. As described in Figure 1, coronary computed tomography angiography and/or coronary angiography were available for 179 patients within an 18-month follow-up for evaluating graft patency. Clinical outcomes at 52-month follow-up were assessed by telephone interview.

Study Enrollment Flowchart

This study is part of a broader biomarker investigation based on the same prospective cohort (NCT00755248). Preoperative plasma samples used for proSP-B analysis were collected and biobanked under the same protocol as those analyzed in previously published studies on D-dimer,16 microvesicles,17 and mercaptoalbumin.14 A comparison of key study parameters is provided in Supplemental Table 1 to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

Sample collection

Blood was collected from a peripheral vein for each patient, 1 to 3 days before their surgery, which served as the baseline. The collected blood was centrifuged at 1,500 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C within 30 minutes of the venipuncture. The plasma was divided into aliquots and then stored at a temperature of −80 °C until it was ready to be assayed.

Analysis of immature SP-B (proSP-B)

The analysis of the immature form of proSP-B was performed by Western blotting on plasma samples, as previously described.8,9 Briefly, the immature forms of SP-B, which are detectable in the 3 predominant forms, with molecular mass ranging from 17 to 42 kDa, were analyzed by immunoblotting on plasma samples, as described by one-dimensional Sodium Dodecyl Sulphate - PolyAcrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) on 15% polyacrylamide gels using a Tris-Tricine buffer system in no reducing conditions.18 Gels were then electrophoretically transferred to nitrocellulose at 60 V for 2 hours. After transfer, membranes were stained with MemCode reversible protein stain (Pierce Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer's instructions to ensure equivalent loading of protein. Immunoblotting on transferred samples was performed using a primary antibody against SP-B (rabbit anti-human SP-B F-2; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) diluted at 1:1,000 in 5% (w/v) nonfat milk in tris buffered saline-tween. Bands were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence using the ECL kit (GE Healthcare), acquired by a densitometer (GS800; Bio-Rad), and quantified using image analysis software (QuantityOne version 4.5.2; Bio-Rad). Data are reported as the ratio of band volume, after local background subtraction, vs the volume of the total proteins loaded and stained with MemCode. The values were also normalized vs the band volume of pooled plasma, loaded as a control on each gel, and they are expressed as arbitrary units (AU). The interassay coefficient of variation was 12.1 ± 2.9%.

Among the 179 patients included in the analysis, proSP-B values were available for 170 individuals (40 with occluded grafts and 130 with patent grafts). In 9 patients (3 cases and 6 noncases), proSP-B levels could not be determined due to insufficient plasma volume.

Quantitation of albumin isoforms by mass spectrometry

The relative composition of albumin isoforms in human plasma samples was assessed by directly infusing diluted (500-fold) plasma samples using the Xevo TQ-S micro triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer coupled with the ACQUITY UPLC M-Class system (Waters Corporation), as previously described.14

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were reported as mean ± SD or median and IQR according to their distribution. Spearman's correlation was used to find monotonic associations between variables. Categorical variables were compared between the 2 groups by the chi-square test or Fisher exact test when appropriate, and quantitative variables by Wilcoxon rank sum test. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess whether proSP-B measured before surgery was independently associated with graft occlusion. Covariates included in model A were selected among potential confounders, that is, baseline variables that were associated both to the dependent variable (graft occlusion) and the variable under investigation (proSP-B). A subset analysis was conducted on a subgroup of 71 patients for whom baseline HSA-SH measurements were available. To assess whether HSA-SH confounded the association between proSP-B and graft occlusion, a simplified logistic regression model was constructed including both variables.14 As a sensitivity analysis, we also included all variables significantly associated only with graft occlusion or only with proSP-B (model B), although, in this case, the analysis was potentially subject to overfitting. As a further caution against a possibility of confounding, we concomputed the ORs for pro-SPB sequentially adjusted for each of the covariates considered.

Category-free net reclassification improvement (NRI) was used to check the improvement in risk prediction by adding proSP-B to the reference model including all covariates in model A: D-dimer, right internal mammary artery (RIMA), use and extracorporeal circulation (ECC) time. Reclassification analysis was preferred to receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis because of its higher statistical power.19 For illustrative purposes, ROC area under the curves (AUCs) were also computed, as this methodology is widely used and the results could serve as a comparison of data reported in the literature. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value for single biomarkers or statistical models were assessed by logistic regression. The optimal cutoff points were identified by maximizing the Youden's index, which is computed as: sensitivity − (1 − specificity).

SAS statistical software version 9.4 (SAS Institute) was used for all analyses, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population and graft evaluation

After discharge from hospital, 306 patients were included in the study. At follow-up, 179 patients were enrolled. Among them, 9 had missing proSP-B values and were excluded from the statistical analysis. All analyses were therefore conducted on the remaining 170 patients with complete biomarker data. Information on the characteristics of excluded (n = 136) vs included patients (n = 170) in the analysis is shown in Supplemental Table 2. In the cohort of 170 patients from the CAGE study population, 478 grafts (166 left internal mammary artery, 19 RIMA, 5 radial artery [RAD], 288 great saphenous vein) were evaluated. Among them, 43 grafts occluded in 40 patients (10 left internal mammary artery, 5 RIMA, 1 RAD, 27 great saphenous vein) have been found. One hundred and seventy patients were divided into 2 groups according to graft patency at 18 months of follow-up and analyzed. Patients with graft occlusion (cases, n = 40) and patients with graft patency (noncases, n = 130), showed no significant differences in terms of age, sex, risk factors, and medications (Table 1).

The estimated increase in the odds of graft occlusion, for an increment of proSP-B equal to one SD, which corresponds to 7.2 AU, was 95% in Model A, and 114% in Model B. We also assessed the performance of proSP-B as a binary classifier by identifying the optimal threshold through Youden’s index. The best cutoff was 19.1 AU, which yielded good sensitivity and specificity (Supplemental Table 3).

For comparison, a 1-SD increase in D-dimer (442 ng/mL) was associated with a 71% and 79% increased odds of graft occlusion in Model A and Model B, respectively. We obtained similar ORs using log2-transformed variables, for example, OR for proSP-B was 3.51 (95% CI: 1.53-8.07), P = 0.003.

Supplemental Figure 1 shows the ORs sequentially adjusted for each of the covariates in Model B. As the variations are small, the possibility of major confounding is unlikely.

The potential nonlinear association between proSP-B levels and the odds of graft occlusion was assessed by calculating ORs for the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th quartiles relative to the first quartile. As illustrated in Supplemental Figure 2, the ORs increased progressively across quartiles, suggesting an approximately linear relationship. In a subset of 71 patients with available HSA-SH data, we assessed whether HSA-SH confounded the relationship between proSP-B and graft occlusion. In this sample, HSA-SH showed a weak nonsignificant inverse correlation with proSP-B (R = −0.19; P = 0.11). To assess whether HSA-SH has a confounding effect on proSP-B, we ran a simplified logistic model including only these 2 variables. In this analysis, proSP-B was independently associated with the risk of graft occlusion (OR: 1.75; 95% CL: 1.06; 2.90), whereas HSA-SH was not (OR: 1.04; 95% CL: 0.64; 1.69).

Reclassification and ROC curve analyses

In reclassification analysis, compared to a model that only includes D-dimer, RIMA use, and ECC time, the addition of proSP-B was able to better reclassify 11% of events (occlusions) and 33% of nonevents. The category-free NRI was 0.43 (SE: 0.18; P = 0.02), thus indicating that 43% of the subjects were better reclassified as cases and noncases with the new model also including proSP-B levels.

In ROC curve analysis, the AUC of 0.7204 (95% CI: 0.62-0.82) for a model including D-dimer, RIMA use, and ECC time (reference model) and an AUC of 0.7733 (95% CI: 0.68-0.86) for the same model after including also proSP-B (P = 0.10 for the 2 ROC curves comparison) (Figure 3).

Clinical outcomes during follow-up

During a median follow-up period of 52 months, graft occlusion was frequently uneventful. Acute myocardial infarction occurred in 2.79% of patients, with 80% of events classified as perioperative. Cerebrovascular events occurred in 2.79% of patients, including 20% transient ischemic attacks, 60% ischemic strokes, and 20% hemorrhagic strokes. All-cause mortality was 5.03%, of which 44% were cardiovascular deaths.

A total of 10.6% of patients underwent percutaneous coronary intervention, and in 58% of these cases, the indication was graft failure. These findings suggest that while graft occlusion is often subclinical, it may contribute to adverse cardiovascular events over the medium term.

Discussion

This study presents evidence for the first time that the preoperative plasma level of proSP-B was significantly higher in patients with occluded grafts than in patients with graft patency at the 18-month follow-up. Furthermore, preoperative proSP-B positively correlated with preoperative plasma D-dimer levels, tobacco smoke, RAD use, and ECC time. In a multivariable analysis, proSP-B was found to be associated with the risk of graft occlusion at 18 months in logistic regression models, including different sets of covariates, such as D-dimer, a well-known marker of activated coagulation that has recently been found to be associated with graft occlusion,16 left ventricular ejection fraction, and ECC time.

By assessing the capacity of proSP-B to properly classify patients undergoing occlusion, on top of other variables considered as confounders, a reclassification analysis based on the NRI revealed that proSP-B was able to better reclassify 11% of events (occlusions) and 33% of nonevents. Furthermore, to evaluate the predictive capability of different variables for 18-month follow-up graft patency, we carried out a ROC curve analysis. Our results showed an AUC of 0.7204 for a model comprising D-dimer, RIMA use, and ECC time (reference model), and an AUC of 0.7733 for the same model with the inclusion of proSP-B.

These findings underscore the potential utility of proSP-B in enhancing risk stratification before CABG.

Of note, previous analyses from this same cohort have also evaluated other circulating biomarkers, such as microvesicles, which reflect endothelial activation and vascular injury.17 While the present study did not include a direct comparison, the involvement of both markers in vascular homeostasis suggests that proSP-B and microvesicles may capture overlapping or complementary biological processes. Future studies might benefit from integrating these parameters into multimarker models. Although graft occlusion was often clinically silent, medium-term follow-up revealed a non-negligible incidence of adverse cardiovascular outcomes. Notably, 2.79% of patients experienced myocardial infarction, and 2.79% had cerebrovascular events, with a predominance of ischemic stroke. All-cause mortality reached 5.03%, nearly half of which was attributed to cardiac causes. Moreover, 10.6% of patients required percutaneous coronary intervention, with graft failure being the indication in over half of the cases. These data reinforce the clinical importance of identifying patients at risk of graft occlusion, even in the absence of early symptoms. A biomarker like proSP-B, if validated, could help stratify risk and guide follow-up intensity or preventive strategies. Although redo CABG was rare, the burden of nonfatal events highlights the relevance of improving long-term graft patency.

After CABG, many molecular pathways are activated, leading to increased inflammation and oxidative stress, activation of hemostasis, and an unfavorable endothelial environment. Thus, several biomarkers indicating the activation of these metabolic systems have been suggested as predictors of early and late graft occlusion.15, 16, 17,20 Indeed, identifying patients at risk of graft failure remains an urgent clinical need and a research challenge.

ProSP-B positively correlates with D-dimer, previously identified as a biomarker associated with graft occlusion.16 D-dimer is a marker of fibrin formation and breakdown, indicating both coagulation and fibrinolytic pathways.21 Whether D-dimer association with graft occlusions reflects changes in fibrinolytic capacity or an increased thrombotic state is still to be determined and requires further studies. The current uncertainty lies in determining which of these mechanisms predominates in the setting of graft occlusion after CABG. However, considering D-dimer's role as a predictor of graft occlusions, we hypothesize that patients with elevated preoperative D-dimer levels would benefit from therapies aimed at reducing thrombin generation in the perioperative period.

We previously demonstrated that the immature proSP-B, not the mature protein, is related to lung abnormalities, more precisely to the membrane component of DLCO (diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide).8,9

Furthermore, patients with severe HF, defined by NYHA classification, peak of oxygen consumption rate (VO2), ventilation/carbon dioxide production (VE/VCO2) slope, and b-type natriuretic peptide, all strong predictors of HF prognosis, showed, in all variables, higher plasma levels of immature proSP-B than those with less severe HF. Furthermore, the strongest correlation, at multivariate analysis, was found between DLCO and immature proSP-B.

In a larger cohort of stable HF patients, only immature proSP-B and peak VO2 were related to hospitalization. Interestingly, at a multivariable analysis, considering age, DLCO, peak VO2, VE/VCO2 slope, b-type natriuretic peptide, and creatinine, proSP-B overwhelmed the prognostic role of DLCO or VE/VCO2 slope, being both parameters of ventilatory efficiency recognized as strong and independent markers of HF prognosis.7

Graft occlusion is linked to lower quality of life and decreased long-term survival. Late graft occlusion is defined as occlusion occurring in the late phase (>12 months after grafting).22 Its mechanisms are different from early graft occlusion, this latter being primarily attributed to a technical failure that results in graft thrombosis and hyperplasia. Late occlusion is caused by the development of atherosclerosis, which impacts long-term clinical outcomes. Treating it is more challenging compared to early occlusion, and the specific risk factors for late graft occlusion remain unclear.

Previous studies have suggested that sex, diabetes mellitus, and off-pump surgery affect graft patency.23,24 However, there is no precise scoring model for late graft occlusion. Several risk prediction models for cardiac surgery have been developed, including the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) score,25 the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association score,26 the European EuroSCORE,27 and its modified version, the EuroSCORE II.28 However, these prediction models were primarily used for evaluating perioperative risk. For graft occlusion, the SAFINOUS score20 is a model for early saphenous vein graft (SVG) occlusion, with risk factors that include sex, diabetes, dyslipidemia, active smoking, and graft number. Moreno et al, Wezel et al, and Yazdani et al found that atherosclerosis and plaque rupture are the main causes of late vein graft failure.29, 30, 31 The formation of atheromatous plaques is promoted by predisposing factors for atherosclerosis (eg, high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking), and damage to the vein wall is induced by highly proliferative smooth muscle cells and the expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines.29

Even though we acknowledge that the sample size is relatively small and it is a single-center study, these findings offer a novel predictor of late graft occlusion. This is important because there is still a need to develop a risk score system that can help with clinical decision-making by calculating individual risk for late graft occlusion.

The assessment of the SVG risk score could enhance surgical strategy and aid in the development of personalized postoperative treatment plans. Proactive risk assessment and the associated treatment may also be particularly cost-effective by reducing SVG occlusion and cardiovascular events in patients after CABG.

The identification of proSP-B as a potential biomarker for graft failure opens new avenues for improving risk stratification before CABG surgery. If validated in larger prospective cohorts, proSP-B could be incorporated into predictive models to support personalized decision-making. Future studies should explore its integration with existing clinical scores and assess whether its use could guide targeted perioperative interventions aimed at improving graft patency and long-term outcomes. This study offers several novel contributions. First, it represents one of the few investigations exploring the clinical significance of circulating proSP-B in cardiovascular surgery, and to our knowledge, the first to link it to graft occlusion following CABG. Additionally, the use of a well-defined, prospectively enrolled surgical cohort with standardized follow-up and outcome assessment further enhances the internal validity of the study. These elements collectively strengthen the translational relevance of our findings and support the utility of proSP-B as a novel biomarker candidate for risk stratification in cardiac surgery.

Study limitations

We acknowledge that the relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power to detect more nuanced associations and increases the risk of false negative findings. Moreover, the post hoc nature of the analysis requires the results to be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, the consistent signal observed across multiple statistical approaches reinforces the biological plausibility of our findings. These results should therefore be considered exploratory but promising, and they warrant confirmation in larger, prospectively designed studies to validate the clinical utility of proSP-B as a biomarker of graft failure.

A further limitation concerns the relatively high number of subjects (n = 127) lost to follow-up after hospital discharge, due to loss to follow-up (n = 61), death before the 18-month time point (n = 7), or baseline exclusion criteria (n = 50, primarily due to atrial fibrillation or renal dysfunction). Although the overall dropout rate (∼23%) is in line with similar prospective observational studies, we performed a comparison of baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients (Supplemental Table 1) to assess potential selection bias. While excluded patients showed slightly higher logistic EuroSCOREs, they were less frequently hypercholesterolemic and had fewer diseased vessels. These modest differences suggest a limited impact on the validity and generalizability of our findings.

Finally, we reiterate that this study represents a post hoc analysis of data and samples collected within the framework of a prospective observational cohort. Although the analytical approach was rigorous, the retrospective formulation of the hypothesis limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Accordingly, the results should be considered hypothesis-generating and should be validated in independent prospective cohorts.

Conclusions

Preoperative levels of proSP-B could represent a new easily measurable circulating predictor of graft occlusion after CABG surgery (Central Illustration).

Immature circulating proSP-B is associated with cardiovascular disease. Elevated preoperative proSP-B levels are an independent predictor of graft occlusion 18 months after CABG. CABG = coronary artery bypass graft.
