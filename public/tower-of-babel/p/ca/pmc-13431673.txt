The spearmint extract L-carvone as a euthanasia agent in sheep
Authors: Robert J Brosnan, Monica Aleman, Lais Costa, Heather Knych, Adrien Dupanloup, Ujwala Vedavally, Chelsea M Unkel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractL-carvone is a natural monoterpene found in spearmint and other foods that can be administered intravenously to euthanize rats. We hypothesized intravenous L-carvone rapidly alters consciousness and produces death in sheep, resulting in tissue L-carvone concentrations that are commonly found in foods. Jugular catheters were placed in five adult Hampshire-Suffolk ewes, and a carotid arterial catheter was inadvertently placed in a sixth ewe. Animals were restrained in a seated position and instrumented with surface electroencephalographic (EEG) and electromyographic electrodes and a two-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) accompanied by pulse and respiratory rate and behavioral monitoring. A 50% solution of food-grade L-carvone administered as a rapid intravenous bolus at a dose of 0.5 mL/kg was followed by bilateral ocular strabismus, loss of responsiveness, shallow breathing, and a 50% reduction in heart rate within 6 ± 4 s (mean ± SD). In 4 sheep, myoclonus and increased muscle tone was present for 15–54 s after injection. Pulses were undetectable within 20 ± 9 s, and animals were apneic in 48 ± 14 s and without cardiac electrical activity 46 ± 19 s after injection. During this time, the EEG spectral edge frequency rapidly decreased, indicative of progressively altered consciousness and deepening anesthesia. Intermittent, large amplitude polyspike waves were observed on the EEGs of most sheep, consistent with seizure activity. Intra-arterial L-carvone injection yielded quantitatively similar results except for persistence of ECG electromechanical dissociation for several minutes after death. Sheep tissues collected after euthanasia were analyzed for L-carvone by liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy. For intravenous doses, L-carvone concentrations ranged from 283 ± 155 in the kidney to 2.2 ± 1.6 in the semitendinosus muscle. Intravascular L-carvone produces rapid onset of unconsciousness and irreversible death in sheep without contaminating the carcass with non-edible residues. However, increased muscle tone and EEG seizure activity make L-carvone imperfect for euthanasia when administered as a sole agent.Keywords: carvone, electrophysiology, euthanasia, isoelectric, mint

Introduction

Acceptable methods of euthanasia produce rapid, reliable, and irreversible unconsciousness and death with minimal distress to the animal (Leary et al. 2020). Pentobarbital, a barbiturate and the most commonly-used chemical euthanasia agent in veterinary practice, poses secondary risks both to animal food and to the environment. For example, pentobarbital persists in rendered animal tissues and has led to pet food contamination and recent manufacturer recalls (Bischoff and Rumbeiha 2018). Dogs or wild animals that feed on carcasses containing pentobarbital can also be poisoned (O’Rourke 2002; Jurczynski and Zittlau 2007; Kaiser et al. 2010; Bischoff et al. 2011; Viner et al. 2016; Wells et al. 2020). Even heated and buried, pentobarbital persists in the environment and can contaminate soil and water (Eckel et al. 1993; Bagsby et al. 2018). Nonetheless, rendering and burial of pentobarbital-euthanized livestock are still recommended as disposal methods by more than 23% and 54% of food animal veterinarians, respectively (Hess et al. 2023).

L-carvone is a naturally occurring terpenoid essential oil found in spearmint (Mentha spicata). Administered intravenously to rats, L-carvone produces general anesthesia (Brosnan et al. 2022). At higher doses, excessive modulation of γ-aminobutyric acid receptors, N-methyl-d-aspartate receptors, voltage-gated sodium channels, and likely multiple other anesthetic-sensitive ion channels and receptors would be expected to produce euthanasia as occurs following anesthetic overdoses with conventional drugs (Brosnan et al. 2022). Moreover, since L-carvone is considered a synthetic flavoring substance or adjuvant that is generally recognized as safe, it likely poses little risk to the environment or to animal health (Department of Health and Human Services 1989). It might even be possible to use food-grade L-carvone as a chemical euthanasia agent in food-producing animals without the necessity for withholding periods to avoid drug residues.

Accordingly, we hypothesized that L-carvone formulated for intravenous administration could produce rapid and reliable unconsciousness and death in adult sheep. Furthermore, we hypothesized that concentrations of L-carvone present in tissues of euthanized sheep did not exceed reported concentrations of this same essential oil extract naturally found in food.

Materials and methods

Animals

Six healthy, adult Hampshire/Suffolk crossbred ewes aged 3 ± 2 years-old (mean ± SD) and weighing 75 ± 3 kg were studied. All work was approved by the University of California, Davis, Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC #22625).

Sheep received a physical examination prior to the study, and wool over the neck and calvarium was shaved. After aseptic preparation of the neck, a 16-gauge, 8.9 cm jugular catheter (Angiocath, BD, Franklin Lakes, NJ) was placed, capped with an intermittent infusion plug, and sutured to skin. Sheep were restrained using a standard, low-stress method by tipping the sheep onto their hindquarters, keeping the body of the sheep against the legs of the handler, and the neck and forelimbs manually stabilized by the handler.

Electrophysiologic study

A Cadwell digital wireless electroencephalography (EEG) system (Cadwell ArcEEG, WA) with integrated video was used to obtain all standard EEG recordings. Continuous EEG with simultaneous video recording for the duration of the study for each animal was performed to monitor cortical activity before, during, and after the administration of the euthanasia solution. Disposable needle electrodes (Grass/Astro-Med, West Warwick, RI, USA) were placed subcutaneously in the scalp of sheep for the recording of the EEG. Electrodes nomenclature and placement were according to a modified human 10–20 system. Even and odd numbers for the electrodes denoted right and left side, respectively. Vertex was denoted as z. A transverse and rostral to caudal bipolar montage was used with the following channels: F3-Fz, Fz-F4, C3-Cz, Cz-C4, P3-Pz, Pz-P4, F3-C3, C3-P3, Fz-Cz, Cz-Pz, F4-C4, C4-P4, O1-O2, and Z; where F = frontal, C = central, P = parietal, and Z = ground electrode (Fig. 1). Frequency bands were defined as: gamma (γ, > 30 Hz), beta (β, 14 to 30 Hz), alpha (α, 8 to 13 Hz), theta (θ, 4 to 7.5 Hz), and delta (δ, > 0 to < 3.5 Hz) (Amzica and Lopes da Silva 2018). To minimize contamination of the EEG tracings by artifacts, the impedance was checked at the beginning of each recording and maintained < 10 kΩ. Two additional electrodes were placed at the left base and apex of the cardiac region for the concurrent recording of ECG.

The raw EEG recordings were reviewed with concurrent observation of sheep behavior from integrated video. For the analysis of quantitative EEG and determination of total spectral power/total power across frequency bands, various time points were selected, each one consisting of an epoch of 8 s. Time points were defined as: T0 = baseline (recording before euthanasia), T1 = administration of euthanasia solution, T2 = EEG changes (from predominant beta frequency to background slowing with high amplitude prior to profound contamination with movement artifacts from muscle rigidity), T3 = time to isoelectric EEG, and T4 = time to isoelectric ECG. Time at baseline consisted of 20 to 30 s of recording that allowed obtaining interpretable data and minimize stress to the animal. Heart rate and muscle tone (flaccid versus rigid) were recorded for each time point. Based upon visual inspection, unconscious state was defined when the animal was not responding or reacting to the environment or stimuli, not moving, and head and body had to be fully supported. EEG findings were reviewed when unconsciousness was observed to determine presence or absence of cortical activity.

EEG frequency total power and spectral edge

Baseline and other time points were visually examined using the transverse bipolar montage. Quantitative EEG (qEEG) analysis was performed selecting an epoch of 8 s relatively free of artifacts at each time point for each animal. Each epoch was processed with software using Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) analysis for calculation of peak frequency and power, and absolute power (μV2) in each frequency band (Hz) (Kochs et al. 1993). Definition of frequency bands followed that published in human medicine (Amzica and Lopes da Silva 2018). Time for total frequency power in μV2 was determined as described (Verhoeven et al. 2015; Williams et al. 2016).

Spectral analysis of digital EEG data was done using the EEG software (Persyst V 14 (Persyst, Solana Beach, CA 92075, USA). The analysis was performed on the frequency spectrum 1–25 Hz using the transparietal derivation P3-P4. Spectral analysis was performed over the selected EEG divided into successive epochs of 8 s each. The spectral edge frequency 90%, defined as the frequency below which 90% of the total spectral power is contained, was obtained for each epoch. The first 10 epochs of the EEG recording were used to calculate the mean SEF baseline and the standard deviation (SD) of the SEF at baseline. Following intravascular administration of L-carvone, the time required to reach a SEF reduction of 3 and 5 standard deviations below baseline (SEF-3SD and SEF-5SD, respectively) was recorded. A decrease by 3SD or 5SD encompass the lower 0.15% SEF tail or lower 0.0003% SEF tail, respectively, of the baseline EEG spectral edge distribution. Changes in SEF were used as objective metric of degrees of altered consciousness after L-carvone administration.

Euthanasia

Euthanasia solution was freshly prepared by mixing L-carvone (natural, food-grade, 99%, Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO), propylene glycol (natural, food-grade, > 99.5%, Sigma Aldrich, St Louis, MO), and undenatured ethanol (200 proof U.S.P., Kosher, Spectrum, New Brunswick, NJ) into sterile, multidose vials using sterile syringes in a 2:1:1 ratio, respectively, by volume. The drug dose was then drawn up into a new sterile syringe using a 0.22 μm filter unit (Millex, Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA) to ensure solution sterility.

Unsedated sheep were manually restrained in a seated position while baseline physiologic data were collected. Next, 0.5 mL/kg of the L-carvone euthanasia solution was rapidly administered as an intravenous bolus over 10–20 s (approximately equal to 3 mL per second). This dose was selected based on pilot data in which one sheep administered 50 μL/kg/min over 10 min developed unconsciousness based on observation of the animal and EEG recording followed by muscle rigidity, seizure, and cardiopulmonary arrest. A second pilot sheep administered this same dose as a bolus quickly became unconscious and developed muscle rigidity without seizure followed by cardiopulmonary arrest soon thereafter.

Thoracic auscultation, digital palpation of femoral arterial pulses, behavioral signs (including globe position, palpebral reflex, and muscle tone/myoclonus), and electrophysiologic responses were monitored and recorded continuously until EEG and ECG activity ceased. After death, the sheep were necropsied, and blood from the contralateral jugular vein, semitendinosus muscle, omental fat, liver, kidney, heart left ventricle, and brain were collected for measurement of drug concentrations.

Determination of L-carvone concentrations

Plasma sample analysis

Plasma calibrators were prepared by dilution of the L-carvone (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) working standard solution with drug free sheep plasma to concentrations ranging from 0.01 to 250 μg/mL. Calibration curves and negative control samples were prepared fresh for each quantitative assay. Quality control samples (sheep plasma fortified with analyte at three concentrations within the standard curve) were included as an additional check of accuracy.

Plasma samples (200 μL) were diluted with 150 μL of acetonitrile (ACN):1M acetic acid (9:1, v: v) and 50 μL of methanol to precipitate proteins. The samples were vortexed for 2 min to mix, refrigerated for 20 min, vortexed for an additional 1 min, and centrifuged (4300 rpm/3102 g) for 10 min at 4°C prior to injection of 10 μL into the liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system.

Quantitative analysis was performed on an LTQ XL Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) coupled with a Waters Acquity HPLC (Milford, MA). The system was operated at a resolution of 60,000 (M/ΔM, at full width at half maximum of the mass peaks) using positive electrospray ionization (ESI (+)). Detection and quantification were conducted using full scan accurate mass from 145 to 160 (m/z). The responses were plotted using a 20 ppm mass tolerance for L-carvone (151.11160 (m/z)). The spray voltage was 3500 V and the sheath and auxiliary gas were 45 and 20 respectively (arbitrary units). Chromatography employed an ACE 3 C18 10 cm × 1 mm 3 μm column (Mac-Mod Analytical, Chadds Ford, PA) and a linear gradient of ACN in water with a constant 0.2% formic acid at a flow rate of 0.1 mL/min. The initial ACN concentration was held at 1% for 0.4 min and ramped to 99% over 9 min before re-equilibrating for 14 min at initial conditions.

The precision and accuracy of the assay was determined by assaying quality control samples in replicates (n = 6). Accuracy is reported as percent nominal concentration and precision as percent relative standard deviation. For the L-carvone accuracy was 112% for 1.5 μg/mL, 106% for 10 μg/mL, and 114% for 100 μg/mL. Precision was 5% for 1.5 μg/mL, 2% for 10 μg/mL, and 2% for 100 μg/mL. The technique was optimized to provide a limit of quantitation of 0.01 μg mL−1 and a limit of detection of approximately 0.005 μg/mL for L-carvone.

Tissue sample analysis

Approximately 100 mg of tissue was weighed into 7 mL Precellys hard tissue homogenizing vials (Omni International, Kennesaw, GA). Tissue samples were homogenized at 4.5 m/s for 30 s in an Omni Bead Ruptor Elite tissue homogenizer (Omni International), with a 5-min cool down. Acetonitrile:1M acetic acid (1 mL; 9:1, v: v) and 50 μL of methanol was added and the samples were shaken a second time. Following homogenization, 500 μL of the homogenate was transferred to a microcentrifuge tube and 1 mL of hexane was added. Tubes were subsequently rotated for 10 min and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min and the hexane. Supernatant (300 μL) was transferred to an autosampler vial with insert and 10 μL injected into the LC-MS/MS system using the analytical method described above for plasma. Calibrators were prepared by adding the working standard solutions to the Precellys vials at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 30 μg/vial. For the L-carvone quality control samples, accuracy was 107% for 0.3 μg/vial, 100% for 2 μg/vial, and 101% for 20 μg/vial. Precision was 2% for 0.3 μg/vial, 1% for 2 μg/vial, and 1% for 20 μg/vial. The technique was optimized to provide an LOQ of 0.1 μg/vial and a LOD of approximately 0.01 μg/vial.

Data analysis

Data were summarized using mean ± SD. Heart rate was calculated as R-wave frequency over 1 min, and occurrence of bradycardia was defined as a > 50% decrease in heart rate. Apnea was defined as > 60 s without regular breathing, excluding agonal respirations. Cardiac arrest time was defined as time to absence of an auscultable heartbeat and pulse or onset of electrical asystole or ventricular fibrillation on the ECG, whichever was detected first. The total spectral power was analyzed using a one-way ANOVA (GraphPad Prism, Dotmatics, Boston, MA).

Results

Behavioral and cardiorespiratory responses

Before drug administration, sheep had a baseline heart rate of 133 ± 19 beats/min and respiratory rate of 64 ± 14 breaths/min while restrained in a seated position. Within seconds of the L-carvone bolus, sheep developed ocular strabismus, loss of palpebral reflex, loss of behavioral responsiveness to restraint or activity around them, shallow rapid breathing, and a 50% or more decrease in heart rate consistent with a Bezold-Jarisch reflex.

In 5 of the sheep, intravenous drug administration resulted in pulselessness in about 20 s followed by apnea and electrical silence on the ECG and EEG in less than 1 min on average (Table 1). During the initial 16-to-54 s, 4 of these sheep also had myotonus and small clonic activity in the legs and facial muscles, and these same animals exhibited pulmonary edema post-mortem. L-carvone concentrations in these animals were highest in the heart and kidney, but comparatively lower in all other tissues. L-carvone concentration in the semitendinosus muscle averaged only 2.2 µg of drug per gram of tissue (Table 1).

In the remaining 1 sheep, L-carvone administration was presumed to have accidentally been injected into the carotid artery rather than the jugular vein. This is evidenced by the extraordinarily high 4.2 mg L-carvone concentration per gram of brain tissue whereas all other tissues had drug concentrations that were approximately 3 or more orders of magnitude lower (Table 1). Nonetheless, this animal exhibited a Bezold-Jarisch reflex, myotonia, and progression to apnea and asystole over a similar time course as the sheep that received L-carvone intravenously.

Electrophysiologic findings

The electroencephalogram was recorded before, during, and after euthanasia. Sheep were awake upon restraint and minimal movement was recorded during baseline (T0, Fig. 2A). Within seconds from the beginning of the injection of L-carvone (T1), the raw EEG changed in its pattern, showing slowing of waves concurrently with apparent increased movement artifact as sheep displayed rigidity (Fig. 2A). After a median average of 31 s (range 6 to 193 s) after the IV administration of L-carvone, the EEG recording became isoelectric (Fig. 2B). Either shortly preceding or following EEG silence, an isoelectric ECG was noted after a median time of 44 s (range 24 to 72 s) (Fig. 2C).

Baseline and other time points were analyzed for power by frequency bands and total power (Fig. 3). The predominant frequency bands during baseline included beta and alpha (>95%) and a lower percentage (<5%) of theta and delta (Fig. 2A). As L-carvone was administered, a sudden change in EEG recording was seen as the result of alteration in the power spectrum transitioning to an increase power in theta and delta frequency bands in a background of beta and alpha frequencies (Fig. 3). This alteration correlated with the clinical observations where the animal clinically lost consciousness immediately prior to isoelectric EEG. The total spectral power sharply declined after EEG alterations were qualitatively observed in the raw recording (Fig. 2B), and total spectral power significantly decreased across the 5 different time points (p < 0.003). Despite using filters to avoid recording and inclusion of muscle artifacts in the calculation of the total frequency power, increased muscle tone and ECG artifacts were picked up prior to onset of an isoelectric EEG. Seizures were observed in 3 of the sheep; attempts at reviewing EEG during this period in affected animals were not possible since movement artifacts and loss of electrodes precluded the analysis.

For sheep administered intravenous L-carvone, the mean (±SD) baseline 90% SEF was 21.6 ± 1.3 Hz with a median (range) of 22.3 (19.6 to 22.5) Hz. Times at which the SEF decreased by at least 3SD and 5SD are shown in Table 1. After decreasing by at least 3SD, the mean ± SD SEF was 19 ± 3 Hz, and the median (range) was 20.8 (13.9 to 21.3) Hz. After decreasing by 5SD, the mean ± SD SEF was 17.2 ± 4.4, and the median (range) was 19.6 (10.1 to 20.5). In the one sheep administered intra-arterial L-carvone, the SEF had decreased by > 5SD within the first 8-second epoch used for the EEG analysis.

Drug concentrations

At necropsy, all sheep carcasses had a faint minty odor. For the intravenous injections, tissue drug concentrations (Table 1) were highest in heart and kidney tissues which were about 5-times higher than observed in plasma. In contrast, fat, liver and muscle all had tissue L-carvone concentrations that were only 4%-to-14% of plasma levels. Surprisingly, despite representing a vessel rich group with high tissue blood flow, brain L-carvone concentrations were only about one-third of plasma concentrations. In comparison to the IV injections, carotid arterial L-carvone resulted in plasma concentrations that were more than 4-times higher, and the sheep had brain concentrations that were 4.2 mg L-carvone per gram of brain tissue. All other tissue concentrations following the carotid injection were comparatively low.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study represents the first successful use of a plant-derived food-grade compound to produce chemical euthanasia by intravenous injection using a livestock model. Following injection, animals were rapidly behaviorally unresponsive and bradycardic, and animals were pulseless in about 20 s with EEG evidence of severely depressed consciousness or isoelectric EEG within 1 min after injection. Death rapidly followed.

In its 2020 Guidelines, the AVMA considered 14 different criteria when evaluating the acceptability of different euthanasia methods: unconsciousness and death with minimal pain and distress, time until loss of consciousness, reliability, irreversibility, compatibility with animal signalment and health status, personnel safety, effects on observers, animal/carcass use, impacts of carcass disposal to the environment and food web, equipment considerations, and legal requirements and abuse potential (Leary et al. 2020). For these last 2 considerations, L-carvone requires minimal equipment beyond what is necessary for routine vascular access, and as a food grade product, there are no specific legal or drug diversion issues surrounding the procurement and storage of L-carvone.

All sheep were euthanized by intravascular L-carvone, and death was irreversible at the dose administered. However, critical to the humane use of chemical euthanasia agents is the speed with which they sufficiently alter or ablate consciousness to minimize the experience of pain or distress. Behavioral signs—including irregular breathing and loss of eye reflexes and normal globe position—suggested that consciousness might be lost shortly after L-carvone administration, as these signs have been associated with unconsciousness in sheep administered propofol and in sheep slaughtered by exsanguination (Verhoeven et al. 2015). However, the literature offers no consensus on EEG methods to define awake-unconsciousness transitions for a novel CNS depressant agent. Published techniques include measuring loss of electroencephalographic coherence between brain hemispheres and brain regions across different frequency bands for inhaled anesthetics (Li et al. 2013), observing decreased EEG spectral entropy for propofol (Sleigh et al. 2004), and presence of oscillations between low- and high-frequency EEG waves for ketamine (Nicol and Morton 2020). In rats, L-carvone can produce general anesthesia, characterized by a loss of righting reflex and loss of movement in response to noxious mechanical stimulation (Brosnan et al. 2022), which by definition includes unconsciousness. In sheep, L-carvone is an analgesic at lower doses (Brosnan et al. 2023), and pilot data show higher intravenous infusions likewise can produce anesthesia, albeit accompanied by severe respiratory depression (unpublished data). For most conventional agents, EEG changes with increasing anesthetic depth are typically characterized by increased wave amplitude and a significant decrease in spectral edge frequency, although there is considerable overlap in the absolute SEF value between anesthetized and pre-arousal states (Drummond et al. 1991). In the present study, we therefore looked at the change from baseline as a multiple of the SD of baseline 90% SEF as an objective, subject-specific and non-behavioral indicator of altered consciousness. However, the relationship between SEF and consciousness is incompletely characterized and likely drug-dependent (Jildenstal et al. 2022). High pass filters necessary to remove low frequency activity associated with muscle activity and fasciculations may have resulted in decreased ability to recognize greater low-frequency delta power, thereby artificially increasing the calculated SEF, especially since EEG frequencies between 0.5 and 2 Hz may account for 60% of total power at a surgical plane of anesthesia (Schwilden and Stoeckel 1987). Collectively, times to onset of SEF-3SD, SEF-5SD, and isoelectric EEG activity in the present study might therefore represent an upper bound estimate to reach states of altered consciousness, unconsciousness, and brain death, respectively.

Euthanasia with injectable L-carvone was irreversible and reliable in the 6 animals studied here. However, much larger sample sizes would be necessary to confirm these findings if a smaller confidence interval were needed. In the absence of treatment failure, at least 60 animals would have to be studied to obtain a 95% confidence interval for failure between 0% and 5% (Ho et al. 2002). The animals studied here were all young adults and healthy and thus might presumably have a higher L-carvone median lethal dose (LD50) compared to sick or injured animals most likely to be euthanized. It therefore appears unlikely that signalment or health status would alter suitability assessments of L-carvone as a euthanasia agent. As an agent that is generally regarded as safe, the handling or administration of L-carvone would not appear to present significant personnel safety concerns (Department of Health and Human Services 1989), and there is no evidence to suggest that it may pose a risk of abuse potential.

An important flaw of L-carvone as a euthanasia agent is the muscle rigidity and myoclonus—accompanied by EEG seizure activity—that precedes onset of death. Even though animals are anesthetized and unresponsive, increased muscle tone and seizures are certainly undesirable and may create an unpleasant experience for an observer. For this reason, L-carvone cannot be considered an ideal injectable euthanasia agent in sheep. Although it is possible that prior administration of other centrally-acting muscle relaxants might prevent L-carvone muscle rigidity, this would defeat the original aim of identifying a single agent that could produce euthanasia without use of controlled pharmaceuticals or risk of exceeding drug residue limits in food.

L-carvone is one of the primary components of spearmint oil and is commonly used as a food flavoring up to 200 ppm (Morcia et al. 2016), well within the range of most of the sheep tissue concentrations measured immediately after euthanasia. Human exposure to L-carvone for high level users can range from 0.59 mg/kg/day to 1.87 mg/kg/day (European Food Safety Authority 2014). This means that a 70 kg person would need to eat 18.8 kg of muscle from sheep euthanized by intravenous L-carvone to reach this lower exposure, although less offal consumption would be required to reach these levels. It is unknown whether L-carvone would adversely affect meat quality, but given an obvious mint odor from the carcasses during necropsy, it seems likely that L-carvone would be detectable during human consumption. Whether a mint flavor could present a problem in sheep tissue used for animal feed also remains to be determined.

L-carvone offers important environmental benefits over barbiturates commonly used for injectable euthanasia. Even if consumed by wildlife, carcasses of animals euthanized using L-carvone seem unlikely to cause toxicity. Moreover, carvone is biodegradeable and thus unlikely to pose soil and water contamination risks that have been found with pentobarbital (Eckel et al. 1993; Bagsby et al. 2018; Li et al. 2022).

In summary, intravascular L-carvone produces reasonably rapid unconsciousness and reliable and irreversible death in sheep yielding a carcass absent of persistent contaminants within the food web or environment. However, L-carvone is not perfect. Transient muscle rigidity, myoclonus, and EEG seizure activity commonly occurred prior to death and make L-carvone a less-than-ideal euthanasia agent. Perhaps another chemically-related compound that lacks this feature may be the solution.
