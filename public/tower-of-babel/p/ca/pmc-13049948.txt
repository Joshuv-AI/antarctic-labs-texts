Back in stride: patient perspectives on operative and nonoperative treatment and recovery after acute proximal hamstring avulsions – a qualitative study
Authors: Anne-Mari N. Rosenlund, Jorunn Thaulow, Kjell Erling Kjellman, Charlotte Myhre Jensen, Lars Nordsletten, Kenneth Jonsson, Frede Frihagen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient-reported outcome measures indicate favorable results after both operative and nonoperative treatment of acute proximal hamstring avulsions. However, little is known about the patients´ experience with treatment and recovery. The aim of this study was to explore the patient perspective on proximal hamstring avulsions, spanning the trajectory from injury through treatment and rehabilitation to the final outcome.MethodsSemi-structured interviews were conducted in groups with participants treated either operatively or nonoperatively for a proximal hamstring avulsion. Participants in the “Proximal Hamstring Avulsion Clinical Trial”, a parallel randomized controlled trial and observational cohort, were invited to take part in this focus group study. One focus group included participants who experienced persistent disabling neurological symptoms. Interviews were conducted 22–50 months after injury. The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. The data were analyzed using Systemic Text Condensation.ResultsEighteen participants from 30–70 years of age were divided into five focus groups. The inductive analysis led to the following five themes: Concern and uncertainty in the initial phase, Surgical versus nonsurgical treatment, Motivational factors, Physical and psychological sequelae, and “When things do not turn out as expected”. The latter theme was related to participants suffering persistent disabling neurological symptoms. Participants were overall satisfied with their treatment and motivated to return to pre-injury activities and mostly succeeded in doing so. However, most participants experienced lingering discomfort even years after injury. For those who suffered persistent disabling symptoms, quality of life remained significantly reduced. Experiences were similar across treatments, though surgical patients felt reassured everything had been done, while some nonsurgical patients wondered if surgery might have led to better results.ConclusionThe findings of this study provide valuable insights for patient education and improving management strategies for proximal hamstring avulsions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09632-0.

Introduction

Avulsion injuries of the proximal hamstring tendons (PHA), are relatively rare, constituting about 10% of all hamstring injuries [1]. Unlike more common strains of the hamstring muscles often with good prognosis after rehabilitation, PHA is a more severe injury, potentially followed by serious sequelae [2–5]. Affected individuals are often middle-aged, and the injury is usually sustained either during sports or from a slip or fall during daily activities [6]. The typical mechanism involves an involuntary split where the hip is flexed, and the knee extended [1]. The diagnosis is made through clinical examination and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [7]. Recent studies reporting outcomes in middle-aged non-athletes have suggested that nonsurgical treatment is as effective as surgical treatment for this patient group [5, 8, 9].

In PHA trials, validated and non-validated patient reported outcome measures (PROMs), functional and strength tests and return to sports are common endpoints [8, 10, 11]. These outcome measures aim to provide objective results. However, despite good to excellent outcome scores, patients may have experiences that are not accounted for in quantitative clinical studies. Retrospective studies on PHA have reported that objective strength measurements do not correlate well with the patient´s perception of their own muscle strength. Some injured individuals have rated their leg as both weaker and stronger than the measured value [12, 13]. Even when strength and balance are objectively restored, the patient may not trust their injured leg. Complications are reported in clinical studies, but this may not convey how the complication is experienced by the affected individual. In addition, results after complications are rarely reported separately. Serious complications may lead to a very poor result but may not impact the mean scores for the groups. Surgeons and patients may have different perceptions of a good result, and some factors important to the patients may not be addressed in quantitative studies [14]. The scarcity of qualitative studies published in orthopaedic journals is reported by Mew et al. who emphasize the value of combining quantitative and qualitative methods to provide a broader understanding of the impact of treatment and patient care [15].

Obtaining knowledge on how patients perceive their treatment and rehabilitation phase may aid our understanding of the injury and improve how health care personnel inform patients [16, 17]. A qualitative systematic review on ankle and distal tibia fractures reported that patients were affected physically, emotionally and socially by the injury [17]. Tutuhatunewa et al. found that thorough information given to patients with clavicle fracture avoided unrealistic expectations and thereby dissatisfaction [18]. These are important factors to bear in mind for clinicians treating these injuries.

To our knowledge no previous publications describe the patients’ experiences related to treatment and recovery after a PHA. The aim of this study was to explore the longitudinal patient perspective of a PHA.

Methods

Design

This was a qualitative study conducted parallel to the Proximal Hamstring Avulsion Clinical Trial, PHACT, an on-going multicenter randomized controlled trial (RCT) and observational cohort study comparing operative and nonoperative treatment of proximal hamstring avulsions [5]. Patients in the observational cohort were not randomized but treated according to surgeon or patient preference. The in-hospital follow-up was identical in the RCT and the observational cohort (3, 6, 12 and 24 months) and the patients received the same rehabilitation protocol.

In the present study we conducted focus group (FG) interviews. The rationale for using focus groups were the advantages of a dynamic setting where the participants are stimulated to interact and discuss and where they feel comfortable to share experiences, opinions, and insights [19, 20]. The interviews were carried out during May and June 2022.

Participants and sampling

FGs consisted of participants who had suffered a PHA. We chose a convenience sampling strategy considering the already available participants in the PHACT study. Participants in PHACT at Oslo University Hospital were invited to take part [5]. Dropouts from the randomized controlled trial and participants who lived more than one hour drive from Oslo were excluded. PHACT included at least moderately active individuals aged 30 and 70, defined as those who regularly participated in strenuous work or sports and other recreational activities. Based on the diversity of the PHACT sample we expected to form FGs with both women and men with a spread in age, treated either operatively or nonoperatively, contributing to opinions and perspectives of a range of proximal hamstring injury patients. A small subgroup in our sample suffered persistent disabling neurological symptoms due to surgery or the injury itself believed to lead to marked limitations also post-rehabilitation. By including these participants in a separate FG, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of long-lasting disability after a PHA. Potential participants were invited by phone by the first author.

Conducting of focus groups

We planned a total of five FGs with at least 3 participants in each group; two groups with participants treated nonoperatively and two groups with participants treated operatively. These participants had a presumed normal recovery. Three participants with persistent disabling neurological symptoms were in a separate group. Data saturation was evaluated after five conducted FGs.

Two of the authors were present at the FGs (AMR and FF). AMR acted as the main moderator while FF as an assistant moderator. They are both orthopedic surgeons treating patients with proximal hamstring injuries. FF is an experienced researcher and AMR a PhD student. Each focus group interview began with a brief introduction about the study and its aims. We used a semi-structured interview guide with open questions (Supplementary material) [21]. The interview guide was organized into the following categories: the experience with the healthcare system, expectations, communication with health care personnel, received treatment, rehabilitation, and final function and complaints. The categories were selected based on the gaps in knowledge identified by the researchers, who formulated the questions based on their clinical experience and practice. The aim of the guide was to encourage the participants to reflect on and describe their experiences. The same interview guide was used in all five FGs. Questions were posed in an open manner, encouraging the participants to talk freely and to interact with each other. Before the commencement of the study, the interview guide was evaluated and deemed understandable by one of the RCT participants. Data saturation was reached after four FGs, as no new themes emerged. However, a fifth FG was conducted with three participants who experienced a complicated post-injury course. Given the small number of participants in this group, we cannot conclude that data saturation was achieved for this subset.

Data analysis

The interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim by one of the researchers (AMR) and an administrative assistant. AMR proofread the transcripts to ensure that there were no discrepancies between recordings and transcripts. Data were analyzed manually using the four steps of Malterud´s Systemic Text Condensation; Step 1) Total Impression – from chaos to themes, step 2) Identifying and Sorting Meaning Units – from themes to codes, step 3) Condensation – from code to meaning and step 4) Synthesizing – from condensation to descriptions and concepts [22, 23]. Step 1 was performed by AMR and FF. This implied reading the transcripts multiple times to become familiar with the content, noting patterns and preliminary themes. Independently the same authors pursued step 2, where significant phrases or segments of text related to the research question were extracted and divided into code groups. The two authors then discussed their findings and refined the preliminary themes until agreement was reached.

To enhance validity a third researcher was brought in to assist with categorizing the data, JT, an ear nose and throat specialist with qualitative methods experience. AMR and JT created a code tree to elaborate and visualize the code groups (Appendix). Based on the code tree AMR performed step 3, which involved creating artificial quotations (condensates) for each code group. Illustrative quotations from the empirical data were then sought out. This step led to a refinement of the code tree (Appendix). Subsequently step 4 was carried through by AMR. The text was reconceptualized and theme headings were adjusted. The quotes were translated from Norwegian to English by AMR. The analysis and presentation of results were conducted according to the principles of Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) [24].

Ethical considerations

This study was solely qualitative and involved no intervention. Identifiable data were removed from the FG-transcripts. The participants received written and oral information according to the WMA Helsinki Declaration [25]. They were cautioned not to share any information they were not comfortable disclosing in the group setting. Before each FG, participants were instructed in confidentiality and signed a consent form.

The study was approved by the Institutional Board and the Data Protection Officer at Oslo University Hospital (Ref: 22/07440) and presented to The Regional Committee for Medical Research Ethics Oslo, who determined that the study did not fall within their mandate (Ref: 394839).

Summary of findings

Concern and Uncertainty in the Initial Phase

Many participants described the injury incident and initial symptoms as dramatic, with intense pain and helplessness. Several were in remote locations, such as the woods or the mountains, when injured, relying on others for help, and experiencing significant pain in the first few days. Pain, massive swelling and bruising of the posterior thigh caused concern and fear. Most participants were unsure of their injury type, leading to hesitancy in mobilizing the first days post-injury. However, perceptions of injury severity varied widely, from “minor muscle strain” to “dislocated hip” or even “mortal danger”.

“And I was so scared, because it hurt so bad, I had no clue what it was and thought that now I will have to amputate the leg that hurts so much.”(P7)

“And I was so scared, because it hurt so bad, I had no clue what it was and thought that now I will have to amputate the leg that hurts so much.”(P7)

“At first, I thought it was just a regular strain but then I couldn´t walk and I started getting cramps at night. So, then I thought it might be a bit worse.” (P13)

“At first, I thought it was just a regular strain but then I couldn´t walk and I started getting cramps at night. So, then I thought it might be a bit worse.” (P13)

Participants experienced an uncertainty among health care personnel (HCP) they initially encountered. Few received a clear diagnosis after their first consultation with a physician. Multiple physicians or physiotherapists were often involved in diagnosing the injury. Some participants visited more than one health institution to confirm their diagnosis. While not much anxiety and frustration were reported, some participants expressed being "thrown back and forth". This uncertainty among HCPs also created doubt in the participants.

“I was at the Emergency department, where they did not in any way ascertain that this was a hamstring [avulsion injury]….so clearly, you also become uncertain yourself.” (P6)

“I was at the Emergency department, where they did not in any way ascertain that this was a hamstring [avulsion injury]….so clearly, you also become uncertain yourself.” (P6)

After receiving the correct diagnosis, the participants were presented with the treatment options. Being confronted with the possibility of both surgical and nonsurgical treatment and the fact that the experts did not know which treatment was superior, again led to uncertainty and concern. Many were surprised that such an injury could be treated without surgery.

“My uncertainty was that no one took responsibility and said you should have surgery, or you should not have surgery.” (P3)

“My uncertainty was that no one took responsibility and said you should have surgery, or you should not have surgery.” (P3)

“When I came to the hospital, I was convinced that this had to be repaired, so I was quite surprised that nonoperative treatment was an option.” (P14)

Surgical versus Nonsurgical Treatment

Most participants who underwent surgery were very satisfied with their treatment, finding it logical to reattach something that had ruptured. They felt that surgery was necessary to do everything possible to make the leg work again. Even those not fully satisfied with their final functional outcome post-surgery did not wish they had been treated nonoperatively, except one participant. Nonoperated participants were mostly content, though some expressed uncertainty, wondering if they would have had a better result with surgery. Concerns about how the muscles would behave without reattachment, was mentioned as having an impact on the rehabilitation.

“I think, and that is psychologically, that if I had felt that I am not 100% and I was operated, then it is okay. If I were not operated and had not become 100% good maybe I would have thought “damn it, why could they not have operated me.” (P4).

“I think, and that is psychologically, that if I had felt that I am not 100% and I was operated, then it is okay. If I were not operated and had not become 100% good maybe I would have thought “damn it, why could they not have operated me.” (P4).

Motivational factors

The participants understood early on that their own effort in the rehabilitation played a crucial role in achieving a good functional outcome. They spoke about being determined to do the recommended exercises or other forms of training for a long time to regain function and strength in their injured leg. However, some nonoperated individuals mentioned that the motivation would have been stronger if they had received surgical treatment. Similarly, some of the operated individuals underlined that they felt more motivated to exercise because of their surgical treatment.

Follow-up appointments at the outpatient clinic and with physiotherapists were mentioned as strong motivational factors. Participants felt motivated by the need to perform well in physical tests and the encouragement received during appointments. They spoke about how the feed-back they received made them more confident about their progress serving as an inspiration to continue exercising. They felt reassured by the consistent support from health care personnel and appreciated being able to seek information when needed. Group training was mentioned as a missed element in the rehabilitation. Meeting people with the same injury and doing exercises together would have helped during the recovery phase.

“And that follow-up every three months, to have that goal to get where you wanted, and the competitive instinct kicks in when you are supposed to jump and measure and hop and so forth. And to get here on those occasions, even though it meant taking time off work, to me that was very important. To feel seen and heard.” (P2).

“And that follow-up every three months, to have that goal to get where you wanted, and the competitive instinct kicks in when you are supposed to jump and measure and hop and so forth. And to get here on those occasions, even though it meant taking time off work, to me that was very important. To feel seen and heard.” (P2).

A desire to return to sports or other leisure activities was a strong motivational factor and was described as a goal they set from the start of the rehabilitation. When asked if they believed they would be nearly or fully restored again after treatment and rehabilitation, all participants affirmed this expectation. They anticipated resuming their active lifestyles and engaging in the same activities and sports as before. However, the ability to return to pre-injury levels of sport varied among participants. Some described a gradual return to sport, experiencing some impatience but eventually reaching pre-injury levels. Others faced more difficulties, experiencing setbacks and ultimately failing to reach pre-injury levels despite their efforts.

Running was highlighted as particularly challenging. Only one participant could run without difficulty one month post-injury. Others eventually managed to run but struggled with speed and stride initially. A few participants could not run properly after the injury, citing uneven leg strength and lack of explosiveness.

“It took a really long time before I could run, and even now I struggle with the feeling that my leg is a bit shorter when I run. Especially when I try to run fast, I feel that my leg can´t keep up.” (P15).

“It took a really long time before I could run, and even now I struggle with the feeling that my leg is a bit shorter when I run. Especially when I try to run fast, I feel that my leg can´t keep up.” (P15).

Return to cross country skiing was mentioned by many participants as important. The majority were able to ski again, albeit often with adaptations. Skiing downhill was described as particularly challenging and intimidating, leading many to choose less hilly terrain. While these adaptations enabled skiing, they also led to frustration at not being able to perform as before.

“I´ve become very cautious on downhill slopes. But that´s because, well, I am 61 years old and I´ve had some falls, and it always seems to happen to me on skis. So, I have decided that no, I am going to quit now [Laughter].” (P11).

“I´ve become very cautious on downhill slopes. But that´s because, well, I am 61 years old and I´ve had some falls, and it always seems to happen to me on skis. So, I have decided that no, I am going to quit now [Laughter].” (P11).

When discussing rehabilitation and return to sports several participants blamed themselves for a slow or insufficient recovery. Initially eager and diligent, some lost motivation over time and felt they did not continue exercising as planned. Achieving specific goals during rehabilitation sometimes decreased motivation for continued training and exercises perceived as monotonous were often neglected despite recognized benefits. As fitness centers closed during the Covid-19 pandemic, participants lost motivation to carry on rehabilitation independently. However, they did not attribute their reduced enthusiasm solely to the pandemic, but instead, taking personal responsibility for their declining engagement in exercise over time.

“Tighter there than in the right leg, but that is only my own fault, no one else´s. I think stretching is damn boring.”(P10)

“Tighter there than in the right leg, but that is only my own fault, no one else´s. I think stretching is damn boring.”(P10)

“I got through the hunting season brilliantly and then I thought: «I am done” [everyone laughs] and I lost part of, if not the motivation, but became at least less diligent to exercise. It is boring to keep doing those kinds of exercises.” (P6).

“I got through the hunting season brilliantly and then I thought: «I am done” [everyone laughs] and I lost part of, if not the motivation, but became at least less diligent to exercise. It is boring to keep doing those kinds of exercises.” (P6).

Physical and psychological sequelae

Most participants, except those in the group with persistent disabling neurological symptoms, expressed satisfaction with their final function, and some mentioned that they no longer thought about their hamstring injury. However, upon further questioning, the majority described lingering issues in their leg. Often mentioned was cramping in the injured leg, affecting both the thigh and calf, with some experiencing this frequently. Cramping could occur during activities like training or even at night. Additionally, complaints included stiffness in the back of the thigh and a sensation of muscle shortening. Nearly all the operatively treated participants described both cramping and stiffness. Some of the nonoperatively treated patients expressed a feeling of their muscle sagging lower in the thigh or that the muscles were no longer “in the right place”.

“In the beginning I had no cramps, but they came after a while, and I found that when I did more specific exercises the cramps came much more frequently.”(P9)

“In the beginning I had no cramps, but they came after a while, and I found that when I did more specific exercises the cramps came much more frequently.”(P9)

“Like when I am sitting here, I feel that lump because it is sagging, it has sunk some centimeters, so it is much more of a lump here than on the other side where it is at its full length. It does not matter, it does not hurt, it is not that, but it is a big difference in the legs.” (P7).

“Like when I am sitting here, I feel that lump because it is sagging, it has sunk some centimeters, so it is much more of a lump here than on the other side where it is at its full length. It does not matter, it does not hurt, it is not that, but it is a big difference in the legs.” (P7).

Several participants described increased caution in certain situations due to fear of re-injury, and some reported adjusting their activities accordingly. Some patients lacked confidence in using their injured leg, but they could not determine whether this lack of trust was caused by actual physical limitations (such as weakness or instability) or by psychological factors (such as fear or anxiety).

“A bit afraid to tear it again, I am not very careful, but a little more cautious. I don´t run so much in rough terrain.”(P8)

“A bit afraid to tear it again, I am not very careful, but a little more cautious. I don´t run so much in rough terrain.”(P8)

“When Things do not Turn out as Expected”

Participants with persistent disabling neurological symptoms experienced their post-injury life quite differently from the participants in the other four groups. They reported loss of function and/or intolerable pain, with neuropathic pain being the predominant issue. This pain hindered them in performing daily activities and sports. They described cramps affecting them both day and night and often causing them to avoid activities.

In addition to physical challenges, participants reported considerable psychological and social effects. Reduced quality of life was attributed to functional loss and pain, necessitating changes in lifestyle and work. They described diminished cognitive capacity, forgetfulness, and a sense of mental overload. Balancing work, social and family activities and training was difficult as they rarely had the energy for all. The participants mentioned sleep disturbances, caused by cramps and pain waking them multiple times per night.

“But it was the neuropathic pain that got me, but now they aren´t as bad anymore, now it is more the foot drop and no muscles on the front of the leg. And pins and needles, but not that horrific pain. That was very exhausting.” (P16).

“But it was the neuropathic pain that got me, but now they aren´t as bad anymore, now it is more the foot drop and no muscles on the front of the leg. And pins and needles, but not that horrific pain. That was very exhausting.” (P16).

“I have never experienced that my body is a stumbling block. Suddenly that is what it is, that is unfamiliar. It is difficult to accept.” (P18)

“I have never experienced that my body is a stumbling block. Suddenly that is what it is, that is unfamiliar. It is difficult to accept.” (P18)

“My memory is poor, I think having constant pain takes up a lot of space. I have got a bit of a “Teflon brain”. I can´t remember things. I forget things that I have said or promised.” (P16).

“My memory is poor, I think having constant pain takes up a lot of space. I have got a bit of a “Teflon brain”. I can´t remember things. I forget things that I have said or promised.” (P16).

Participants in this group described feeling judged by those around them for reduced work capacity, and reliance on social security benefits. They felt misunderstood by others, who did not recognize their challenges, and believed it would be easier if their pain were visible. They expressed frustration and surprise by their treatment by the Labor and Welfare Services.

“Are you kidding me? I have had employees my whole life and now I am supposed to do a course to learn how to write a CV? No…..” (P17)

“Are you kidding me? I have had employees my whole life and now I am supposed to do a course to learn how to write a CV? No…..” (P17)

Enjoyable activities had to be put on hold, leading to a sense of loss. However, they explained how they had learned to adapt and find other enjoyable activities or modifying the way they performed certain tasks. Some pushed themselves to continue previous sports for therapeutic reasons, despite facing exhaustion and more pain as a consequence.

“We have an ambition to find things we can do now, that we might not have done before. I have been canoeing for example, because fortunately I am able to sit.” (P16)

“We have an ambition to find things we can do now, that we might not have done before. I have been canoeing for example, because fortunately I am able to sit.” (P16)

Discussion

This focus group study showed that individuals who sustained a PHA experienced the incident and the initial phase as dramatic and worrying but reported an overall satisfaction with the treatment and a strong motivation to return to pre-injury sports and activities. However, nearly all participants experienced some form of discomfort, even two to four years post-injury. The patients with neurological sequelae reported that the injury significantly impacted their lives.

Successful treatment, but still….

Participants described their function after treatment and rehabilitation as good to excellent aligning with much of the available literature on treatment of PHA [1, 26]. The lingering discomfort reported by the participants ranged from minor annoyances during certain activities to more disturbing issues that affected their activity and sometimes prevented them from performing sports and other strenuous activities. Interestingly, these complaints are rarely addressed in papers reporting PHA results. The only validated patient-reported outcome measure for PHA, the Perth Hamstring Assessment Tool (PHAT), incorporates pain after a PHA but does not capture other potential sequelae [11]. In clinical settings, it is crucial for health care personnel to be aware of these sequelae to provide proper patient information.

Placebo effect of surgery?

Discomfort was reported by both operated and nonoperated participants. However, stiffness and cramps were commonly described among the operated individuals, while nonoperated participants often felt that their muscles were sagging in the thigh. Nearly all patients were content with their treatment type, though some of the nonoperated participants had ambiguous feelings. The perception that surgery is the best treatment option has been documented in previous studies. Davies et al. reported that when an injury is presented as a mechanical problem, patients expect surgery to be the logical choice [27]. Nonsurgical treatment may be perceived as inferior or not as actual treatment [18]. Our participants accepted nonsurgical treatment but struggled to understand how it would heal. In recent years nonoperative treatment has been demonstrated to be noninferior for several orthopedic injuries (Achilles tendon ruptures, clavicle fractures, distal radius fractures [28–30]). However, the feeling that everything possible has been done when surgery is performed may still be a driver for operative treatment. This may be linked to a placebo effect of surgery. Dettori et al. described the overall treatment effect of surgery as more than just the procedure by itself [31]. The patient expectations, how the surgery is being explained by the surgeon and the ceremony around the surgical procedure are factors contributing to the placebo response [31, 32]. In our study nearly all participants treated surgically were convinced of having received superior treatment. While there was no difference in patient reported outcome scores between operated and nonoperated patients in PHACT, there was a tendency of higher patient satisfaction in the operated group [5]. Nonoperative treatment may require more focus on patient information and explanation on healing of injuries when not surgically repaired.

Persistent disabling symptoms

sistent disabling symptomsA perspective from this study not captured in most quantitative studies is the experience of the “worst cases”. Sciatic nerve symptoms may be iatrogenic or caused by the PHA injury, and are underrecognized [33]. Our study showed that participants with persistent disabling neurological symptoms had a reduced quality of life due to both physical and psychological challenges, resulting in changes to work and social life. Although severe complications are uncommon, understanding their impact is crucial when counseling patients about treatment options and prognosis.

Rehabilitation and motivation

Return to sports and regular follow-up, primarily by physiotherapists, were the two main motivators for recovery in our study. Although the participants were recreational athletes, they had a strong wish to return to sports and activities they enjoyed before their injury. To have a clear goal to strive for has been reported as important for recovery in qualitative studies on ankle fractures and Achilles tendon ruptures [34, 35]. Studies also support our findings on the positive patient experience of seeing a physiotherapist regularly, boosting the participants’ confidence and encouraging further training [35, 36]. Alongside the desire for group training this highlights the psychological importance of social support in preventing loneliness during the rehabilitation process.

Uncertainty

We found that many participants did not receive a precise diagnosis at their first consultation with healthcare personnel. Delayed diagnosis or incorrect diagnosis is common in PHA [37]. For a trained physician or physiotherapist, PHA should be an easy diagnosis given the obvious signs such as swelling, bruising, reduced strength, and a palpable defect just below the ischial tuberosity. Our findings suggest a knowledge gap among healthcare personnel in diagnosing a PHA, as proposed by van der Made et al. [37].

Methodological considerations

The experience from the clinical work of the moderators of the FGs influenced the interview guide and how questions were posed in the interview setting. The preconceptions brought in by the researchers is a source of bias. However, we also considered it to be a strength to have a broad experience in the field, hopefully balanced by open questions and within group discussions. [38].

The researchers were known to the participants prior to the interviews due to participation in PHACT. This was seen as an advantage as trust was already established between the participants and the researchers presumably making them comfortable to share information in the interview setting. However, a result of such a relation between a patient and caregiver could lead to response bias where the patient wants to please the caregiver and does not respond truthfully [39]. Both response bias and selection bias based on sampling from an RCT and cohort study may have influenced responses such as satisfaction with treatment type.

We used investigator triangulation to enhance credibility. The data analysis was performed by two researchers (AMR and FF) with support from JT, contributing with competencies from a different clinical background and qualitative research.

A limitation of this study was the low response rate. Of the 34 invited participants only 18 took part in the FGs. It is likely that the individuals who declined participation differed from the study population regarding treatment, rehabilitation experiences, and outcomes. Having only three to four participants in each focus group was primarily due to logistical considerations. To maximize recruitment, participants were given multiple appointment options to accommodate their schedules. However, the overall number of participants was high, and the small groups may have contributed to a more secure environment for sharing experiences. Our participants were two to four years post-injury at the time of interview, and we considered they would be reasonably well rehabilitated by then but still able to remember their post-injury trajectory in detail. However, recall bias may be an issue and a longitudinal design could have reduced this.

Only one group with three participants with persistent marked neurological sequalae was included. We did not have access to more participants in this category. However, this group provided a valuable perspective, especially as a supplement to the findings on residual complaints from the participants with an assumed normal course of rehabilitation. The group was heterogeneous with both sexes represented with different symptoms and severity.

All participants were treated in the same hospital by only a few surgeons which may raise questions on transferability. However, our sample included a range of individuals with PHA, both operated and nonoperated, including men and women aged from 30 to 70 years, with different activity levels, experiencing normal and complicated rehabilitation courses. With this heterogeneity our data should be applicable to the treatment of hamstring injuries in similar settings to ours.

Conclusion

This focus group study found that individuals sustaining a proximal hamstring avulsion initially worried significantly but were generally satisfied with their treatment. Return to sports served as a key motivating factor during rehabilitation. Despite good functional outcomes, most participants experienced some sequelae, e.g. cramps and stiffness of the thigh and reduced activity, underreported in the literature. Participants with persistent disabling neurological symptoms suffered a substantial reduction in quality of life. The study also identified drivers towards surgical treatment. Taken together the findings of this study provide valuable insights for patient education and improving management strategies for proximal hamstring avulsions.
