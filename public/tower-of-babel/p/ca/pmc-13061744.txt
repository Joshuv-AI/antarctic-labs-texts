Age‐Related Differences in Sleep Measures Among Pediatric Patients With Obstructive Sleep‐Disordered Breathing
Authors: Matthew Zhang, Basir Mansoor, Stephen R Chorney, Yann‐Fuu Kou, Cynthia S Wang, Romaine F Johnson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo characterize age‐related differences in sleep measures among a cohort of pediatric patients referred for polysomnography (PSG) due to obstructive sleep‐disordered breathing (oSDB).MethodsA retrospective review of 303 children aged 1–18 years referred for PSG due to oSDB was conducted. Patients were categorized into four age groups based on American Academy of Pediatrics guidelines. Demographic, clinical, and polysomnographic differences were analyzed using linear regression and chi‐square techniques. Sleep parameters were further characterized using correlation analysis and multiple linear regression. A subset of 133 patients was analyzed post‐tonsillectomy.ResultsThe study included 6.6% patients aged 13 months to 2 years, 50.5% aged 2–5 years, 22.1% aged 6–11 years, and 20.8% aged 12–18 years. Obesity prevalence increased significantly with age, peaking at 75.8% in the 12–18‐year group (p < 0.001). The apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) showed a U‐shaped distribution, with the highest level in the oldest (28.8/h) group (p < 0.001). N3 sleep showed an inverse U‐shaped distribution, peaking in the 2–5‐year group (115.2 min, p < 0.001). Multiple regression analysis revealed significant negative associations between N3 duration and both age (β = −1.90, p < 0.001) and AHI (β = −0.41, p < 0.001), which persisted post‐tonsillectomy (age: β = −4.24, p < 0.001; AHI: β = −0.87, p = 0.001).ConclusionAge‐related differences in sleep parameters exist in children with oSDB and persist despite surgical intervention. These findings suggest that age and obesity continue to play crucial roles in sleep architecture, warranting further investigation.Level of Evidence3.Keywords: age, obesity, pediatric obstructive sleep‐disordered breathing, polysomnography, tonsillectomy

Introduction

Obstructive sleep‐disordered breathing (oSDB) encompasses a spectrum of sleep related disorders, ranging from primary snoring to its most severe form, obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), and affects approximately 3%–12% of all children [1, 2]. Untreated oSDB can lead to adverse neurological, cardiovascular, and developmental outcomes, highlighting the importance of early identification and management [3, 4].

Polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing oSDB and assessing its severity [4, 5]. In children, treatment typically involves adenoidectomy and/or tonsillectomy, as adenotonsillar hypertrophy remains the most common cause of pediatric OSA [6, 7, 8]. However, the reported success rate of tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy varies widely, ranging from 27.2% to 82.9% [9, 10]. While factors such as obesity and comorbidities are known to influence oSDB severity and treatment outcomes, the specific PSG characteristics and risk factors across different pediatric age groups remain underexplored [11, 12, 13]. Given that age‐related differences in sleep physiology and comorbidities may influence disease severity and treatment response, a more detailed, age‐based analysis is warranted.

Age has been associated with varying tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy outcomes: children under 3 years of age experience higher rates of postoperative respiratory complications, while older, obese children are more likely to experience persistent OSA [14, 15]. Although general age‐related trends in PSG parameters–such as declining total sleep time–have been documented, how these patterns present in children with oSDB, and how they change after surgery across different pediatric age groups, remain poorly understood [16, 17]. Furthermore, sleep undergoes rapid changes particularly in the first 2 years of life, as children shift from a polyphasic pattern to more consolidated nocturnal sleep [18, 19].

The primary aim of this study was to characterize age‐related differences in PSG parameters, with a focus on N3 sleep and AHI as key markers of sleep architecture and OSA severity. By identifying these differences across different pediatric age groups, we hope to inform more precise clinical decision‐making regarding surgical intervention and long‐term management of pediatric OSA. We hypothesize that AHI changes significantly among different age groups even when accounting for other factors.

Methods

We performed a retrospective cohort study at Children's Medical Center of Dallas of pediatric patients aged 1–18 who had tonsillectomy performed between January 2021 and July 2021. This time period was selected to capture a window following the resumption of elective surgical cases and PSG availability after COVID‐19‐related restrictions at our institution, balancing adequate sample size with a relatively uniform practice environment. At our institution, preoperative PSG is obtained for all patients being considered for tonsillectomy due to oSDB, consistent with AAP guidelines, and is not limited to patients with specific comorbidities. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at UT Southwestern and was exempt from consent due to its retrospective nature. Patients were excluded if they had a prior tracheostomy, underwent PSG solely with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), or if they had no preoperative PSG. A total of 303 patients were included, and 133 patients completed a follow‐up PSG, which was analyzed in a postoperative subgroup. Follow‐up PSG was generally recommended for patients with a preoperative AHI ≥ 5 events/h, those with significant comorbidities (e.g., obesity, Down syndrome, craniofacial anomalies), and patients whose caregivers reported persistent symptoms postoperatively. The median interval from surgery to follow‐up PSG was 104 days (IQR 73–147 days; range 41–820 days).

Demographic data collected include age at PSG, age at operation, body mass index (BMI), area deprivation index (ADI), sex, race, ethnicity, and insurance type. Patients were stratified into age groups based on AAP guidelines: 13 months–2 years, 2–5 years, 6–11 years, and 12–18 years [20]. Weight status was categorized based on BMI percentiles (underweight: < 5th percentile, normal: 5th–85th percentile, overweight: 85th–95th percentile, and obese: > 95th percentile). For patients with a BMI above the 95th percentile, BMI was expressed as a percentage of the 95th percentile value. Comorbidities documented include asthma, gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), Down syndrome, craniofacial disorders, prematurity, and allergies. Tonsillar hypertrophy, defined as tonsil size of either 3+ or 4+ before removal, was also noted. GERD was identified based on clinical diagnosis documented in the electronic medical record and may include cases diagnosed empirically without confirmatory pH probe or impedance testing. Patients with syndromic conditions (Down syndrome, craniofacial disorders) were included and reported separately; patients with primary neuromuscular disorders were rare in our surgical population and were not specifically excluded.

PSG parameters recorded were total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep latency, rapid eye movement (REM) latency, REM sleep duration, non‐REM (NREM) sleep duration, Stage 1 NREM sleep (N1) duration, Stage 2 NREM sleep (N2) duration, Stage 3 NREM sleep (N3) duration, arousal events per hour, obstructive apnea (OA) events, obstructive hypopnea (OH) events, AHI (apnea–hypopnea index), obstructive AHI (oAHI), central apnea index (CAI), time spent with oxygen saturation (SpO2) < 90%, lowest recorded SpO2 (SpO2 nadir), mean SpO2, peak end tidal carbon dioxide (CO2), average CO2 levels, percent of total sleep time with end‐tidal CO2 > 50 mmHg, and periodic limb movements of sleep per hour (PLMS). Sleep efficiency was defined as the percentage of time asleep out of total sleep time, sleep latency was the time to sleep onset, and REM latency was the time to first REM period. AHI, oAHI, and CAI were reported as events per hour of sleep. For patients with OSA, severity was determined by AHI and categorized as severe (AHI > 10/h), very severe (AHI > 24/h), or profound (AHI > 50/h).

All PSGs were performed as attended, in‐laboratory overnight studies at the Children's Medical Center of Dallas sleep laboratory, which is accredited by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM). Sleep stages and respiratory events were scored by registered polysomnographic technologists according to the AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events, using pediatric scoring rules for patients under 18 years of age. No home sleep apnea tests were included in this study.

Sample‐size calculations were performed assuming six age strata and a medium effect (Cohen f = 0.24, equivalent to an 8–10 events/h difference in mean AHI). A total of 239 participants (39 per group) provide 80% power at α = 0.05. Accounting for the uneven age distribution observed in our population, the required total sample size was estimated to be approximately 300.

A multiple regression analysis was performed to assess the effects of various parameters on sleep architecture. A correlation matrix was created to assess the association between sleep parameters, age, and BMI. p values were calculated using chi‐squared for categorical variables and linear regression for continuous variables when compared across age groups.

For our primary hypothesis and the relationship between AHI and age groups, we performed a one‐way analysis of variance. If statistical differences were found, we performed multiple comparisons using the Tukey–Kramer method. For secondary analysis of other sleep measures, we applied the Bonferroni correction. For all other comparisons, we considered them exploratory and reported the p values, but the findings should be held in caution and considered for future research hypotheses. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Statistical Software, Version 18, College Station, Texas.

Results

Of the 303 pediatric patients who met inclusion criteria for this study, 6.6% were aged 13 months to 2 years, 50.5% were aged 2–5 years, 22.1% were aged 6–11 years, and 20.8% were aged 12–18 years. Demographic data is listed in Table 1. The average age of the cohort at surgery was 7.2 (4.6) years old, and the average BMI of the population was 22.8 (9.0) kg/m2. Given the skewed age distribution, with more than 50% of patients in the 2–5 year group, the median age at PSG was 5.5 years (IQR 3.2–10.3), and median BMI was 20.0 kg/m2 (IQR 16.3–27.0). There was a significant increase in BMI percentile and z score with increased age (p < 0.001), with obesity prevalence peaking at 75.8% in the 12–18‐year‐old category (p < 0.001). Socioeconomic status, as measured by ADI, differed significantly across age groups (p = 0.044), with the highest ADI in the 6–11‐year group and the lowest in the 12–18‐year group. The cohort was majority male (60.5%) and predominantly white/Caucasian (62.0%) or black/African American (27.7%), with 54.3% of the total cohort identifying as Hispanic.

Comorbidity data collected is also summarized in Table 1. GERD and premature birth were significantly more prevalent in the 13 month to 2‐year group compared to the other age groups (GERD: 35.0%, p = 0.005; premature birth: 30.0%, p = 0.023). Other comorbidities and tonsillar hypertrophy prevalence were not significantly different between the age groups.

Preoperative PSG parameters are documented in Table 2. Total sleep time and NREM followed inverse U‐shaped patterns (p < 0.001), peaking in the 2–5‐year group, while REM sleep significantly decreased with age (p < 0.001). REM latency was lowest in the youngest group at 102.0 (82.6) min and highest in the 6–11‐year group at 140.8 (75.4) min (p = 0.004). Sleep latency and sleep efficiency did not differ significantly between age groups. N1 increased with age (p < 0.001), from 15.9 (11.0) min in the youngest group to 25.4 (14.8) min in the oldest group. N3 followed an inverse U‐shaped distribution (p < 0.001), peaking in the 2–5‐year group at 115.2 (30.0) min. Arousal events per hour, OA events, obstructive AHI, AHI, and time spent with SpO2 < 90% all followed U‐shaped distributions, peaking in the 12–18‐year group (p < 0.001, p = 0.010, p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p = 0.018, respectively). The prevalence of severe, very severe, and profound OSA were highest in the oldest group (p = 0.010, p = 0.017, and p < 0.001, respectively). OHs also followed a U‐shaped pattern, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.084). Mean SpO2 and average CO2 levels also featured inverse U‐shaped patterns (p = 0.004 and p = 0.013).

Correlations among key sleep parameters are presented in Table 3. Preoperative N3 time was moderately and negatively correlated with age (r = −0.435), BMI (r = −0.411), AHI (r = −0.383), and oAHI (r = −0.376). These relationships were confirmed in multiple regression analysis (Table 4), with age (β = −1.90, p < 0.001) and AHI (β = −0.41, p < 0.001) showing statistically significant effects. BMI showed a nonsignificant trend (β = −0.54, p = 0.071). These negative correlations strengthened in the postoperative cohort (age: β = −4.24, p < 0.001; BMI: β = −0.81, p = 0.027; AHI: β = −0.87, p = 0.001).

Additional correlations revealed significant positive correlation between preoperative N3 time and sleep efficiency (r = 0.332), total sleep time (r = 0.477), and REM sleep (r = 0.309). Age at PSG was positively correlated with both AHI (r = 0.232) and oAHI (r = 0.235) and negatively correlated with sleep efficiency (r = −0.152), total sleep time (r = −0.276), and REM sleep (r = −0.334).

Of the 303 patients, 133 (43.9%) completed a follow‐up PSG. A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients who completed follow‐up PSG and those who did not was performed to assess for potential selection bias. Patients who completed follow‐up had significantly higher mean preoperative AHI (24.6 vs. 9.3 events/h, p < 0.001) and were more likely to have severe OSA (78.2% vs. 25.3%, p < 0.001). Mean BMI was also higher in the follow‐up group (24.1 vs. 21.8 kg/m2, p = 0.028). No significant differences were observed in age at PSG (p = 0.57), sex (p = 0.45), race (p = 0.18), ADI (p = 0.60), or insurance type (p = 0.68). These differences indicate that the postoperative subgroup overrepresents patients with more severe preoperative disease, and postoperative findings should be interpreted accordingly.

Discussion

This study demonstrated significant differences in PSG parameters across age groups in a cohort of 303 children with oSDB. A U‐shaped pattern in AHI and other respiratory variables (including arousals, OA events, oAHI, and time spent with SpO2 < 90%) suggests differing mechanisms of disease severity across age groups. Increased BMI percentile with age likely contributed to higher AHI values in older children, reflecting the established association between obesity and OSA severity [12, 21]. Furthermore, we found an inverse U‐shaped pattern with N3 duration, highlighting distinct age‐related sleep parameter differences in pediatric patients with oSDB.

Normative studies in healthy, nonsurgical pediatric populations have documented age‐related declines in N3/slow‐wave sleep and total sleep time across childhood and adolescence [16, 17]. Our observed patterns are broadly consistent with these normative trends but appear more pronounced, suggesting that oSDB may amplify normal developmental changes in sleep architecture. Importantly, the U‐shaped AHI distribution observed in our cohort is not a feature of healthy populations and likely reflects the distinct pathophysiology of oSDB at different ages. Future studies incorporating nonsurgical control groups would help delineate the independent contributions of oSDB versus normal development to the age‐related sleep architecture patterns observed here.

Prior studies have demonstrated the link between obesity and OSA severity, as well as the risk of residual OSA post‐tonsillectomy in older, obese patients [12, 21, 22]. The persistence of elevated AHI postoperatively in the oldest age group in our study supports these findings. In contrast, the elevated AHI in the youngest group may be attributed to adenotonsillar hypertrophy and a greater prevalence of comorbidities such as prematurity and GERD, both of which have been previously associated with more severe OSA [23, 24]. These results emphasize the importance of considering age‐related factors, including BMI and comorbid conditions, in the evaluation and management of pediatric oSDB, and they suggest that obese pediatric patients may require additional interventions beyond tonsillectomy to achieve optimal resolution of OSA.

When comparing sleep architecture, REM sleep decreased with increased age, while NREM followed an inverse U‐shaped pattern, peaking in the 2–5‐year age group. Although N2 did not differ significantly, N1 increased with age, and N3 followed an inverse U‐shaped distribution. N3, or slow wave sleep, is associated with important development processes, including synaptic plasticity, insulin sensitivity, and autonomic regulation [25, 26, 27]. Reduced N3 sleep has been associated with adverse metabolic outcomes, including increased risk for hypertension and type 2 diabetes [25, 28, 29, 30]. Previous studies have noted general decreases in NREM and N3 duration with age in children, particularly during adolescence [31, 32]. Our findings extend these observations across a broader pediatric range and demonstrate a moderate negative correlation between N3 duration and both age and BMI. However, the drop in N3 time in the youngest group compared to the 2–5‐year group could suggest that OSA may be associated with greater changes in N3 sleep time in very young children. These findings imply that both developmental changes and OSA severity may contribute to sleep architecture changes across pediatric age groups and raise questions about the long‐term impact of oSDB on sleep architecture, given the important neurocognitive processes that occur during slow‐wave sleep.

Additional observations in the preoperative PSG data in Table 2 revealed statistically significant differences in REM latency, mean SpO2, and average CO2 between age groups. REM latency, defined as the first REM sleep after sleep onset, was longest in the 6–11‐year‐old group and shortest in the youngest group. Though there is limited research on REM latency and pediatric OSA populations, prematurity—most prevalent in the youngest group in our cohort—has been associated with shorter REM latency times [23]. While both mean SpO2 and average CO2 followed similar reverse U‐shaped distributions preoperatively, all group means (SpO2: 96.7%–97.7%, CO2: 41.6–44.7 mmHg) remained within normal PSG‐documented clinical values [33]. These observations indicate that while these physiologic differences exist across age groups in our cohort, their clinical relevance should be further explored.

Limiting factors for this study include its retrospective design and the lack of a non‐OSA control group, which restricts generalizability. The absence of a control group prevents clear differentiation between OSA‐related PSG changes and normal age‐related sleep development. Additionally, because all patients were referred for surgery, this cohort may overrepresent children with more severe oSDB. Unequal age group distribution, particularly the small number of patients in the youngest group, may have affected statistical analyses. We also observed significant differences in ADI across age groups, with higher values in the 6–11‐year group. This may reflect disparities in timing of referral or access to care, while the lower ADI in the oldest group could reflect a shift in indication driven by obesity and more severe AHI. However, these findings may not reflect the general pediatric population with OSA. The study period of January–July 2021 coincided with the COVID‐19 era, which may have affected case mix, access to PSG, and surgical indications; delays in referral during the preceding year may have resulted in a shift toward more severe presentations due to deferred care. GERD diagnoses were based on clinical documentation and may not have been confirmed by objective testing such as pH probe or impedance monitoring, which could overestimate GERD prevalence in our cohort. The duration of OSA prior to diagnosis is unknown and may differ across age groups; it is plausible that older patients had a longer duration of untreated disease, which could independently affect sleep architecture and treatment response. The inclusion of patients with syndromic conditions (Down syndrome, craniofacial disorders) may introduce heterogeneity in OSA phenotype, as these patients may have a higher likelihood of mixed or central apnea events. Only 133 of 303 patients (43.9%) completed follow‐up PSG, representing a selected subgroup that overrepresents patients with more severe preoperative disease (mean preoperative AHI 24.6 vs. 9.3 events/h, p < 0.001) and higher BMI; the results of this comparison are detailed in the Results section. Additionally, the median interval from surgery to follow‐up PSG was 104 days (IQR 73–147; range 41–820 days), and this variability in follow‐up timing may influence postoperative sleep parameters, particularly in younger children where sleep architecture changes rapidly with development.

While some postoperative findings were significant, only a subset of patients had post‐op PSG data, with only a few in the youngest group, limiting longitudinal conclusions and accurate comparisons with preoperative parameters. Future studies should include patients with both pre operation and multiple post operation PSGs, and ideally a control group, to better characterize persistence and clinical significance of age‐related PSG differences and investigate whether addressing sleep architecture could improve long‐term cognitive and metabolic health in pediatric oSDB patients.

Conclusion

This study highlights age‐related differences in PSG parameters in children with oSDB. We observed a U‐shaped pattern in AHI and an inverse U‐shaped pattern in N3 sleep. Obesity appears to be associated with increased OSA severity in older children, as well as residual OSA after tonsillectomy. In contrast, OSA may be associated with greater changes in N3 sleep in younger pediatric patients, though this relationship warrants further investigation given the limited postoperative sample size. These findings emphasize the need for age‐specific approaches to the diagnosis and treatment of oSDB and support further research into the long‐term effects of obesity, age, and sleep architecture on pediatric OSA outcomes.
