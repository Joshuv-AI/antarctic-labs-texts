Residential Energy Dataset with Electric Vehicles, Photovoltaic Generation and Tariff Variability in Ireland
Authors: Dimitris Avgoloupis, Vasilis Michalakopoulos, Elissaios Sarmas, Vangelis Marinakis, Mario Couto, Alessio Coccia, Mariana Jimenez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHousehold energy demand data are essential for designing load-shifting strategies, storage solutions and demand response programs. This is the first publicly available dataset that integrates multiple household-level energy and mobility metrics. These include grid imports and exports (30-minute intervals), rooftop photovoltaic (PV) production (30-minute intervals), EV charging sessions, and detailed journey logs (start time, end time, distance, and duration) from the same households of a pilot energy community located on the Dingle Peninsula, Ireland. The dataset includes four volunteer households, each equipped with a 2.1 kWp rooftop PV system, 5 kWh Sonnen battery, a Hyundai Kona Electric (64 kWh), a Pulsar Plus EV charger (7.4 kW AC), and a Mitsubishi Electric Ecodan air-source heat pump.While grid and PV data span three years, EV mobility logs cover February 2021 to January 2022. Battery telemetry and heat pump demand data are available for the final six to eight months of the observation period (mid-2021 to early 2022), enabling detailed whole-home analysis for that specific window.

Background & Summary

Household demand profiles are changing. The primary drivers are the electrification of vehicles and the adoption of new heating and cooling technologies, such as heat pumps1,2. The European Union’s (EU) long-term climate-neutrality targets require that by 2050 at least 75% of the total energy demand comes from Renewable Energy Sources (RES)3. Moreover, rooftop Photovoltaic (PV) systems are installed with fast rates across EU, driven by the cost reduction and subsidy schemes4. PV production can exceed household electricity demand, but is often misaligned with it. One way to counter this is through flexible scheduling of these loads, coupled with storage and smart tariffs5. These changes have created both challenges and opportunities, not only for system operators and suppliers but also for consumers6. One key challenge is the increase in peak load, driven by uncontrolled EV charging behaviour–often influenced by range anxiety. To make it clearer, a single Electric Vehicle (EV) charging session can match or exceed the old consumption profile of a house, which was dominated by lighting and appliances. Similarly, the replacement of fossil-fuel-based heating systems with heat pumps contributes to increased average demand and seasonal variability. Together, these factors can stress distribution networks and increase reliance on fossil fuels for electricity generation7.

Although these challenges and opportunities are significant for individual households, the difficulties become even more complex when many households come together to form groups, known as energy communities8. Historically, energy communities have been set up as grass root initiatives to accelerate the energy transition, while offering economic, environmental and social benefits to their members9. Here we focus on such a setting on the Dingle Peninsula, supported by ESB Networks through the Dingle Electrification Project10. The programme enrolled more than 30 homes to trial vehicle electrification, rooftop PV and flexibility options; five “ambassador” homes received a common technology package comprising an EV, rooftop PV with battery storage and a heat pump. The locations of the ambassadors on the Dingle Peninsula are shown in Fig. 1. In this data release we assemble multiple streams from four ambassador households: grid imports and exports from AS230 smart meters, PV production from Shelly energy meters, EV charging events from Wallbox Pulsar Plus chargers, and EV journey logs from the Rentalmatics telematics platform. In the original project, three flexibility products (SUSTAIN, SECURE and DYNAMIC) were considered. The flexibility products Sustain, Secure, and Dynamic were originally developed in the United Kingdom under the Open Networks programme, led by the Energy Networks Association (ENA))11. This initiative aimed to standardize the procurement of flexibility services across distribution network operators, enabling distributed RES to participate effectively in local flexibility markets. These products were later adopted by ESB Networks in Ireland as part of the Dingle Electrification Project to explore the role of smart technologies in building a resilient, low-carbon electricity network. Within this context, ESB trialed the use of these flexibility products to assess how residential-scale batteries, electric vehicles, and smart devices could support network operations through scheduled, conditional and real-time responses.

SUSTAIN refers to scheduled flexibility services that address predictable and recurring network constraints, such as seasonal demand peaks. SECURE ensures the availability of flexibility resources during periods of operational uncertainty, acting as a preventive measure against potential congestion or instability. DYNAMIC enables rapid, near-real-time activation of flexibility to respond to unforeseen events, including equipment failures or sudden fluctuations in renewable generation. These products collectively provided a structured framework for enhancing grid reliability and operational efficiency through localized, responsive energy services. For consistency, we adopt the SUSTAIN series here and extend it to cover the full observation window. We further integrate hourly solar irradiance and air temperature from Open-Meteo API to cross-check PV output against meteorological conditions. Existing open datasets illuminate parts of this landscape, but leave important gaps. The UK Power Networks dataset offers half-hourly demand for over 5.000 households, yet lacks EV charging and PV generation signals12. StoreNet contributes rich Irish traces, demand with PV and battery storage for homes and businesses, but does not pair these with EV charging or mobility13. Standalone EV datasets, such as Vicomtech d-EVD14 and Baek et al.15, characterise charging or travels in isolation from household context. To our knowledge, no prior open dataset links household demand, rooftop PV, EV journeys, EV charging and tariff time series within the same dwellings. Here we bridge that gap by aligning these signals at 30-minute resolution across four homes in a single energy community on the Dingle Peninsula.

The integration of these data streams offers researchers valuable opportunities to investigate household flexibility in the context of EV smart charging and enables the development of optimization models aimed at maximizing PV self-consumption. Moreover, since all households belong to the same energy community, there is potential to design innovative community-based strategies. For instance, how can members collectively minimize energy imports during peak hours? How much PV generation can be stored or shared within the community? Addressing these questions could significantly influence the future design and operation of energy communities.

Methods

The dataset originates from the Dingle Electrification Project in Ireland, a demonstration trial led by ESB Networks on the Dingle Peninsula. ESB Networks recruited 35 households to participate with some privileges, five of these, known as the program’s ambassadors, received the full technology package. Four of them had enough data to be included in this publication. Each ambassador’s home was equipped with a 2.1 kWp Rooftop Photovoltaic Panel with a 5kWh Sonnen Battery, a Hyundai Kona Electric 64kWh, a Wallbox Pulsar Plus smart EV charger and a Mitsubishi Electric Ecodan air-source heat pump. All of these technologies were coupled with advanced metering systems. The released datasets from four ambassador households provide multi-stream records, with smart-meter, PV production and tariff data available through early 2022 and EV charging and journey logs spanning February 2021 to January 2022. The data captures multiple dimensions of modern residential energy: household grid imports and exports, rooftop PV production, EV charging sessions, EV journeys, and tariff signals.

Data collection was conducted by the ESB Networks. For household’s demand, an AS230 smart meter was used, providing 15 minute records for both active power imports and exports expressed in kW. PV production was measured with a Shelly energy meter, this sensor provided measurements for half an hour periods in kWh. For continuity beyond mid-2021, PV production for the same households was obtained from an alternative dataset collected through the Greencom platform, as data from the original Shelly meters were no longer available. The two data sources were cross-checked for consistency in both magnitude and temporal alignment prior to merging with the earlier time series. EV charging sessions were recorded using Wallbox Pulsar Plus smart chargers, which provided data including start time, end time, and delivered energy. Additionally, vehicles were equipped with Rentalmatics telematics devices to capture EV mobility patterns. The telematics system recorded trip start and end timestamps, trip duration (minutes), and travelled distance (km). Hourly solar irradiance and temperature data were obtained from the Open-Meteo API16. These data were used to validate PV production and enable modelling of renewable variability under intense weather changes. The ambassador households were connected through the GreenCom Energy Management System (EMS), which coordinated data collection across metering, charging, and telematics devices. Each device–meters, chargers, telematics units–communicated with a household gateway via Wi-Fi or Ethernet. The data were subsequently transmitted to a cloud server operated by ESB Networks and its partners, where all measurements were stored and processed for research purposes (see Fig. 2). Researchers accessed anonymised CSV files containing time-stamped logs directly from the cloud platform.

Technical Validation

This section outlines the methods employed for validating the dataset. First, the consistency of each file was assessed to ensure that a unified dataset could be constructed for each user. In addition, the quality of the data was examined and cross-validation was performed using other datasets and the available literature. The most important aspect of technical validation is the quantification of per-household and per-stream coverage (Active energy import, PV production, and tariffs), and the visualization of event activity related to journeys and charging. Continuity is shown by most data streams, apart from small gaps (Fig. 5). The exact root cause cannot be determined retrospectively, such interruptions may be associated with transient communication issues, commissioning activities, or interactions between pilot-installed equipment and the existing smart metering infrastructure. According to the fact that, household (id02) is unaffected suggests that the interruption was not systemic, but rather dependent on household-specific conditions, such as local communication stability or equipment configuration. For EV journeys (Fig. 6) and charging (Fig. 7), sessions are observed to occur sporadically, which is especially pronounced in EV charging.

In the case of data derived from EV charging and trips, it was necessary to calculate the State of Charge (SoC) of the battery at the end of each charge to verify physical plausibility. Since the vehicle telemetry did not report continuous battery levels, we mathematically reconstructed the battery’s state using a specific average consumption of 0.168 kWh/km. The validation process began by identifying charges where the battery was fully charged (energy ≥ 95% capacity), at which point the estimated SoC was reset to 100% (64 kWh). From these charging events, we propagated the SoC forward and backward in time; for each interval, the estimated energy consumed by driving was subtracted, while the energy recorded by the wallbox charger was added. The algorithm enforced three precise physical constraints to ensure realism. First, an upper bound constraint ensured the reconstructed energy state never exceeded the vehicle’s usable battery capacity. Second, a non-negativity constraint was applied to ensure the battery state never dropped below zero. Third, and most critically, a look-ahead constraint verified trip feasibility by requiring that the SoC at the end of any charging session be at least equal to the energy required for the immediately following journey (SoCend ≥ Distancenext × Consumptionavg). If the calculated SoC was insufficient to satisfy this lower bound, the algorithm flagged a potential external charging event and adjusted the SoC upward to meet the energy requirement. These potential external charges are distinguished in red. The reconstructed state of charge is compared with subsequent driving distance in Fig. 8.

Correlation of estimated SoC with distance (id01).

To validate the solar PV data, satellite-derived radiance values were obtained from the Open-Meteo API. Iniatially, weather data were collected for all 5 Ambassador locations. Furthermore, correlation was used to match one weather file per user, but only two representative weather profiles were identified as optimal. The open_meteo_dingle2.csv file showed the highest correlation for id01 and id02, while the open_meteo_dingle1.csv file provided the best match for id03 and id04. This happened due to the larger spatial grid from open meteo than the distance between households. To validate the beginning of the process PV data were aggregated to daily totals, and half-hour values below 0.005 kWh were removed to avoid noise caused around sunrise, sunset, or by sensor errors. The relationship between daily PV production and both ALLSKY (actual cloud-modified radiation) and CLRSKY (clear-sky radiation) irradiance was tested. In both cases, a clear positive correlation was observed: for ALLSKY, the coefficient of determination was R2 = 0.713, whereas for CLRSKY, the correlation was weaker (R2 = 0.414), likely due to Ireland’s frequently cloudy conditions. Correlations between daily PV production and irradiance are shown in Figs. 9 and 10. These correlations could potentially be higher; however, since, weren’t use the exact locations of the users were not known, data for a random area in the Dingle Peninsula were used, which may have resulted in lower correlations.

Figure 12 presents the aggregated 24-hour activity patterns of all households, illustrating the temporal distribution of PV generation, grid imports, charging start times, and the initiation times of EV journeys. PV generation (Fig. 12a) was observed to coincide with the peak of solar irradiance, showing strong production around midday. In contrast, grid imports (Fig. 12b) were found to be minimal during daylight hours and to peak in the evening and early morning when PV generation was absent. The figures for EV events represent start times: charging sessions (Fig. 12c) were predominantly initiated in the evening, particularly between 8 p.m. and midnight, indicating that most charging occurred during periods of low PV production. Conversely, EV journey start times (Fig. 12d) were grouped around typical commuting periods, with heightened activity observed in the morning and afternoon, overlapping with hours of high PV generation. The evident temporal misalignment between PV production and EV charging initiation suggests potential for demand-side management and smart-charging strategies to shift charging activity and better align it with periods of abundant local renewable generation.

Potential Applications of the Dataset

The dataset can be used for research on energy systems, mobility and data-driven optimization. More specifically:

Energy forecasting: By using data on household imports and exports from the grid and solar energy production, researchers can develop models to forecast household demand and solar energy production. These models are important for designing demand response programs and evaluating the impact of flexible tariffs.

Smart charging and flexibility optimization: The dataset enables research into smart-charging algorithms and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) strategies, supported by detailed EV journey and charging records. Researchers can explore how charging schedules may align with community energy goals, tariff signals, or PV generation.

Ethics Statement

The dataset and accompanying analyses comply with the ethical standards of Scientific Data and the European General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). The datasets used in this paper were obtained within the scope of the ENPOWER project, through collaboration with ESB Networks, which is a project partner. ESB Networks acted as the data controller and provided the datasets exclusively for research purposes.

The research presented in this paper is based on fully anonymised datasets provided by ESB Networks. The original data collection, involving human participants, was conducted by ESB Networks as part of a prior pilot initiative, the Dingle Project. Ethical approval, participant recruitment, and informed consent procedures were managed by ESB Networks in accordance with their internal governance processes and applicable data protection regulations.As the authors did not directly recruit participants nor interact with human subjects, and as only fully anonymised data were used, no additional Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was required for this study.

All datasets provided to the researchers were fully anonymised prior to transfer, ensuring that no personal or directly identifiable information was accessible to the research team. Data were shared securely via SFTP, and access was restricted to authorised researchers involved in the ENPOWER project. No attempt was made to re-identify participants, and all analyses were performed on anonymised data only. No personal identifiers such as names, addresses, or vehicle registration numbers are included in the released dataset, and the platform(s)’ data redistribution policies are fully complied with.
