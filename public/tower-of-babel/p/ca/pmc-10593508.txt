Viewpoint: Could better understanding of risk factors for comorbidities pave the way towards personalized therapy in rheumatoid arthritis?
Authors: Judit Majnik, György Nagy
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn addition to joints, several organs can be affected in rheumatoid arthritis. Coexisting conditions with different pathomechanisms all contribute to disease activity, treatment efficacy, mortality and quality of life. The wide selection of treatment options makes it possible for rheumatologists to personalize treatment for their patients, which in present practice mainly includes the consideration of established comorbidities and contraindications. We suggest that further research can enable clinicians to take into account the individual risk of the future development of comorbidities, when making therapeutic decisions. Individual risk assessment could be mainly based on biomarkers and the better understanding of the patomechanism of different coexisting conditions, as we highlight with the examples of depression and interstitial lung disease. This biomarker-based person-centred therapy can lead not only to the treatment but ideally even the prevention of coexisting conditions, and can lead to better disease control, survival and quality of life in rheumatoid arthritis.

The terminology of these associated conditions is multiple, including extra-articular manifestations and comorbidities, reflecting their complex, often not fully elucidated origin. In addition to joints, several organs can be affected in RA, for example the lungs, heart, blood vessels, bone and the central nervous system [1]. As extra-articular manifestations, we mean conditions that have a clear association with the autoimmune processes of RA. The term comorbidity is widely used for coexisting conditions of different pathomechanisms, and we suggest that this term mainly refers to conditions that are fully or partially independent from RA. Coexisting condition seems to be an optimal term to cover all these pathologies that have different pathomechanisms and still can contribute to disease course and activity, treatment efficacy, mortality and quality of life. As rheumatologists are the specialists who manage RA, it is mainly their role to take into consideration all these coexisting conditions when considering the treatment options of individual patients.

As today a large selection of therapeutic options exist, by taking a multidisciplinary approach we can optimally offer a personalized treatment to our patients, taking into account all coexisting conditions (Fig. 1). It should be noted that clinicians have to make decisions about management options based on contraindications, largely driven by comorbidities, and therefore this kind of personalization is already used in the clinic. In present practice, rheumatologists mainly focus on established comorbidities that could, for example, contraindicate certain medications, but the potential for personalized treatment leads far beyond this approach. A better understanding of the pathomechanism of the different coexisting conditions enables the identification of special biomarkers for the estimation of the individual risk of each potential comorbidity and extra-articular manifestation. There is an unmet need for the identification of biomarkers that could be validated for clinical practice, so that optimally not only already established coexisting conditions, but also those with a high risk of occurrence can be taken into account when considering treatment options. This approach can lead to better disease control and quality of life, optimally even the prevention of serious coexisting conditions and thus better survival, and can also help to prevent treatment failure or non-adherence, typical factors leading to difficult-to-treat RA [2, 3].

The personalized treatment of RA should optimally include both pharmacological and non-pharmacological therapies, based on evidence of their efficacy in the individual setting of coexisting conditions. This needs better understanding of the pathomechanism of different comorbidities and extra-articular manifestations, helping to define the best targets of individual therapy. Intensive research is focused on the pathomechanism and therapeutic approaches of different coexisting conditions in RA, as we highlight below with the examples of depression, which is a coexisting comorbidity with some possible inflammatory underpinnings, and interstitial lung disease (ILD), which is an extra-articular manifestation immediately related to RA and part of the more challenging disease phenotype.

Depression is one of the major coexisting conditions in RA, and is present in 14–48% of RA patients [4]. There seems to be a bidirectional relationship between arthritis and depression; nevertheless, this bidirectional relationship could not be evidently demonstrated in a study based on meta-analysis with Mendelian randomization estimates [5]. It is a matter of debate whether depression or RA is the primary illness, or both originate from the same root, and there are data supporting each of these three options. Depression was shown to be associated with subsequent increased risk for seronegative RA, and depression can reduce compliance, remission rate and response to biological therapy in RA. On the other hand, arthritis activity and functional disability can lead to depressive symptoms [4]. The common origin of the two conditions is supported by data that show the elevation of several chemokines and cytokines (e.g. IL-1β, TNFα, IL-6, IFNα and IFNγ) both in RA and in depression independent from RA, and also the inflammation-induced alterations in the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis seem to be linked to depression, underlying an at least partly immune-mediated mechanism [4]. Some potential biomarkers associated with depression in RA are IL-6, CRP, fibrinogen and SAA, although the majority of these markers mainly reflect inflammation, which may not be specific enough to help in the decision of treatment choices. A recent work using bioinformatic analysis and machine learning-based methods identified several genes likely involved in the mechanism underlying RA with depression, and also indicated that RA combined with depression is related to six diagnostic markers (AURKA, BTN3A2, CXCL10, ERAP2, MARCO, PLA2G7) [6], which could represent candidate biomarkers but need to be validated in RA cohorts. Treatment of RA-associated depression seems to be different from other forms. In RA patients both tocilizumab and infliximab decrease depressive symptoms, but their effect on depression without RA is not evident [7]. Vice versa, in cases of depression in RA, antidepressants do not seem to be highly effective [6]. For depression in RA, many studies have shown beneficial effect of non-pharmacological treatments, for example there is some data regarding the efficacy of mind–body therapies like meditation, mindfulness or yoga [8].

Another condition for which in the last years new therapeutical options have arisen is ILD. For a long time, ILD and lung fibrosis in RA were primarily considered side effects of MTX. New data do not underlie the causal relationship between MTX therapy and ILD, seems to have rather a beneficial effect in the prevention and treatment of RA-ILD, and the pathomechanism of ILD in RA seems to lie in common immunological and environmental factors. This is underlined by a strong association with ACPA positivity and HLA haplotypes, neutrophil and fibroblast activation, and smoking [9, 10]. Also, some potential biomarkers for ILD risk in RA are under investigation, like mutations in the MUC5B gene, alterations of cytokines and other molecules (IL-33, IL-18, MMP-7, SP-D), and even a risk score for subclinical RA-ILD has been elaborated including sex, age at RA onset, 28-joint DAS-ESR and the MUC5B rs35705950 genetic variant [9]. In addition, Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease is another proven risk factor for ILD apart from smoking, which can be taken into account and treated adequately in RA patients. As for management of RA-ILD, there are accumulating data that abatacept, tocilizumab and rituximab could be superior to TNF inhibitors for the therapy of RA associated with ILD [10], and the antifibrotic therapy nintedanib is registered for progressive fibrotic ILD.

As shown by these examples, we propose that real personalized treatment of RA cannot occur without a better understanding of coexisting conditions. With the multidisciplinary approach in mind, and the accumulating knowledge of pathomechanisms, new biomarkers can be validated for clinical practice, to help in the estimation of the individual risk for different comorbidities and extra-articular manifestations in RA. These biomarkers could help rheumatologists to decide on optimal treatment regimens including both evidence-based pharmacological and non-pharmacological therapies. This biomarker-based person-centred therapy can lead not only to the treatment but ideally even the prevention of coexisting conditions, and thus may effectively contribute to the minimization of long-term consequences of chronic inflammation of various organs in RA. This holistic approach could result in better survival and better quality of life and well-being of patients with this complex chronic disease.
