Development and validation of a self-management ability scale for patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy (SMA-HNC)
Authors: Jianxia Lyu, Hao Zhang, Li Yin, Ping Cheng, Bin Li, Fang Li, Shichuan Zhang, Qing Yang, Aiping Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving self-management is essential for the health of patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. This study aimed to develop and validate a self-management scale at a preliminary level owing to the lack of standardized assessment tools. A cross-sectional study was conducted to develop the scale through three stages: item generation, item evaluation, and psychometric testing. Content validity was assessed using the content validity index (CVI). Construct validity was evaluated using exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Convergent and discriminant validity were examined using average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). Criterion validity was assessed by correlation with the Chinese version of the Strategies Used by People to Promote Health (C-SUPPH) scale. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α, split-half reliability, and test–retest reliability. After item analysis and factor analysis, the final scale consisted of 6 dimensions and 19 items. Exploratory factor analysis extracted six factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, explaining 71.78% of the total variance. Confirmatory factor analysis supported the six-factor structure with acceptable model fit indices (χ2/df = 2.939, CFI = 0.909, TLI = 0.887, RMSEA = 0.099), and the total variance increased to 79.13%. The scale demonstrated good criterion validity (r = 0.706, P < 0.001). The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.906, the split-half reliability was 0.711, and the test–retest reliability was 0.806. Introduction

Head and neck cancer (HNC) is among the most prevalent cancers in China, with an incidence rate more than ten times that of Europe and America1. HNC differs from other types of cancer based on the following characteristics: (1) The lesion site is the head and neck, with a relatively limited impact on limb and body movement. Therefore, patients and their families inevitably become the main bearers of self-management2. (2) The treatment approach is complex. HNC often involves a comprehensive treatment strategy that combines surgery and (or) various forms of chemotherapy (induction and neoadjuvant chemotherapies, among others), radiotherapy, concurrent chemoradiotherapy, and immunotherapy3,4, almost covering all existing treatment methods for cancer. (3) The burden of symptoms is high. It affects important tissues and organs, including the nose, pharynx, larynx, and oral cavity, as well as nerves, directly affecting vision, hearing, smell, taste, breathing, speech, and swallowing. Its symptoms and the toxic and side effects of treatment are significant5. In the early stage6, there may be postoperative pain, thick saliva, radiation-induced oral mucositis, radiation-induced skin damage, dry mouth, difficulty swallowing, dental-related issues, and reduced food intake due to compromised functions of diet-related organs, which can cause nutritional problems and further increase the risk of related complications such as infection and bone marrow suppression7. In the advanced stage6, patients may experience limited mouth opening, cervical lymphedema and fibrosis, head and neck dysfunction, and symptoms related to tumor metastasis and recurrence8. These symptoms encompass most symptoms and burdens associated with cancer treatments. (4) Reintegration into society is limited. According to studies, HNC causes the most emotional trauma9, which may be associated with treatment-related toxicity and side effects10, the important role of the head and neck in everyday activities and socialization11, and the complex and unmet survival demands of this population12. Additionally, patients with HNC are more likely to experience a higher risk of physical, emotional, and psychological disorders13 and have a harder time returning to work and struggling with self-management than patients with other cancers14. (5) Functional rehabilitation is challenging. Rehabilitation becomes extremely difficult after HNC treatment. Patients may experience xerostomia, difficulty in mouth opening, swallowing issues, and head and neck movement disorders, particularly impairment of language function, in patients with laryngeal and tongue cancer after surgery15. Long-term rehabilitation exercises (such as mouth opening exercises, swallowing training, speech and language training, and neck function exercises) are necessary, requiring high compliance and self-management by patients and their families16.

Self-management involves individuals engaging in preventive or therapeutic health activities under the guidance of medical staff. It helps manage medical roles and emotional aspects of health by using skills, including problem-solving, decision-making, resource utilization, patient-caregiver partnerships, action planning, and self-regulation. This approach has been effective in managing chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease. Cancer patients and their families are key players in self-management, as they transition from treatment to long-term rehabilitation as a result of medical advancements that have extended their lives.

Self-management is a situation in which individuals engage in preventive or therapeutic healthcare activities under the guidance of medical professionals17. Self-management encompasses individuals’ behaviors in response to their health conditions, including three self-management tasks, such as medical, role, and emotional management, as well as six self-management skills, such as problem-solving, decision-making, resource utilization, forming a partnership between patients and caregivers, action planning, and self-adjustment. It has been widely applied and achieved remarkable effects among patients with chronic diseases, including hypertension18, diabetes19, and cancer20.

The survival period of cancer patients has gradually increased owing to advancements in medical technology, which has shifted the focus from cancer treatment to long-term rehabilitation. Consequently, patients and their families inevitably become the primary subjects of self-management. Research demonstrates21 that self-management can improve the health of cancer patients and enable them and their families to actively participate in managing their symptoms and rehabilitation process. Domestic and international scholars have demanded greater attention to self-management among cancer patients and its integration into the field of cancer patient research. It has been found that improving self-management in cancer patients can effectively reduce the risk of emergency hospitalization and disease recurrence, lessen the impact of cancer on daily life, and improve the quality of life22.

There is a close relationship between self-management ability and quality of life in patients with HNC during radiotherapy and chemotherapy23. Consequently, assessing the level of self-management efficacy is essential to better understand the current state of self-management in patients with HNC undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy and to guide health professionals in providing personalized intervention measures to improve health outcomes. To date, no suitable assessment tools have been developed to measure the self-management effect of radiotherapy and chemotherapy in patients with head and neck tumors. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a self-report questionnaire to assess the self-management ability of patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and to evaluate its psychometric properties. The goal was to identify weak links in self-management for patients with HNC and provide a reference for developing targeted self-management intervention support.

Methods

All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Participants

A convenience sample of patients with HNC undergoing inpatient treatment (chemotherapy and radiotherapy, not surgery) at Sichuan Cancer Hospital was taken between January 2025 and August 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with a clinicopathological diagnosis of HNC, including oral, pharynx, larynx, nasal cavity, or large salivary adenocarcinoma (on a pathological or imaging diagnostic basis), who were currently undergoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy; (2) patients who could understand and respond to questions; (3) patients whose actual condition was known; (4) patients whose expected survival time was ≥ 3 months; (5) patients who provided informed consent to voluntarily participate in this study. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) Patients with cognitive impairment or mental illness, and (2) patients participating in additional psychology-related clinical trials.

The sample size was determined based on the general recommendation for scale development studies that the sample size should be at least five to ten times the number of items24. Considering the initial pool of 55 items, a minimum sample size of 275 to 550 participants was required. Finally, 400 patients were included.

The first phase of the study (item generation and revision) did not involve participants. This sample (n = 400) was used in the second phase (item evaluation and exploration) and the third phase (psychometric evaluation of the scale). In the third phase of factor analysis, samples were randomly assigned to two groups, one for exploratory factor analysis (n = 200) and the other for confirmatory factor analysis (n = 200). The sample size for exploratory factor analysis was considered sufficient, as previous methodological guidelines suggest that a sample size greater than 150 or at least five participants per item is acceptable. For confirmatory factor analysis, a sample size of 200 is generally regarded as adequate for structural equation modeling and model fit evaluation. Therefore, the sample sizes used in both analyses were deemed appropriate.

Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2025 to August 2025 to develop a clinical assessment tool. This process was divided into three stages: (1) Item generation; (2) item evaluation; (3) questionnaire evaluation. The scale development process is depicted in the following Fig. 1.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Cancer Hospital (No. SCCSMC-01-2024-122). All participants received detailed information on the study. Participants had the option to voluntarily accept or decline participation in the study at any time. Each participant signed an informed consent form, and their information was kept confidential.

Item development

The related literature on self-management among patients with head and neck cancer during radiotherapy and chemotherapy was reviewed. The initial items of the scale were derived from three main sources. First, the initial draft of the self-management questionnaire for patients with head and neck cancer (HNC) undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy was adapted from the Head and Neck Cancer Patient Self-Management Inventory (HNC-PSMI) developed by Professor Barbara A at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (with authorization)25. The original scale consists of 11 domains containing a total of 57 self-management tasks. These domains cover general functioning, medical care, nutrition, feeding tube care, tracheostomy care, oral care, speech and voice function, skin care, rehabilitation, psychosocial support, and symptom management. Each item corresponding to the self-management task is evaluated from two dimensions: whether the patient performs the task (yes/no format), and the difficulty level of task implementation adopting a 5-point Likert scoring method. Among them, 1 indicates not difficult at all, and 5 represents extremely difficult. The American Cancer Society HNC Survivorship Care Guideline was also referenced for item formulation and domain setting. Secondly, a meta-synthesis of qualitative studies on the self-management experiences of patients with head and neck cancer during radiotherapy and chemotherapy26 was conducted to supplement patient-derived data regarding self-management content and subjective perceptions. Subsequently, semi-structured interviews were conducted with eight patients diagnosed with head and neck cancer to explore their current status and needs related to self-management, which provided abundant empirical data for the development of the item pool and the initial questionnaire and further formed the final item list. Patients with head and neck tumors require self-management in multiple domains during radiotherapy and chemotherapy, including disease symptom management, daily life management, psychosocial adjustment, health information acquisition, and rehabilitation management.

Questionnaire development

A total of 20 experts were invited to revise the first draft of the SMA-HNC in two rounds. The inclusion criteria for experts were: (1) at least 10 years of working experience in the field of head and neck tumors; (2) at least an intermediate professional title; (3) voluntary participation in the study. The expert consultation questionnaire consisted of three parts. The first part introduced the research purpose and relevant background of the developed scale. The second part presented the dimensions and items of the preliminary scale. Experts were invited to evaluate whether the questionnaire covered all self-management dimensions of head and neck cancer patients undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and whether the item type design was reasonable. Meanwhile, experts were required to rate the importance and relevance of each item and provide revision suggestions and feedback. The third part investigated experts’ familiarity with the research topic and the basis for their judgments. The questionnaire response rate was used to reflect experts’ participation enthusiasm. The authority coefficient was calculated based on experts’ familiarity with the research field and the mean score of judgment criteria. Kendall’s coordination coefficient27 was adopted to evaluate the degree of agreement among expert opinions, so as to reflect experts’ professional judgment level and academic familiarity with the research field.

Eight patients with head and neck tumors who received radiotherapy and chemotherapy and matched the inclusion and exclusion criteria completed the questionnaire in a pilot study to assess its accuracy and readability. They were asked to comment on any unclear items. The item pool consisted of 55 items. The questionnaire adopted a five-level Likert format, with responses ranging from “strongly disagree” (1), “disagree” (2), “ Neutral “ (3), “agree” (4), to “strongly agree” (5). The self-management ability increased with increasing score.

Evaluation and exploration of the items

The quality of the items was evaluated using the critical ratio (CR) value, item-total correlation (ITC), and internal consistency during project analysis. CR ranked the total scores of the questionnaire from low to high, and the subjects were divided into low- and high-score groups based on the 27% critical score. Items with a CR value < 3 were eliminated using the t-test on the two independent samples28. Items with a Values less than 0.4 were eliminated using ITC analysis. Additionally, Cronbach’s α coefficient was calculated after deleting each item to determine the homogeneity of the items. If the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the entire questionnaire was significantly higher after deleting one item, it was considered for deletion. The suggested minimum coefficient loading was 0.4, and cross-loadings were not allowed. If any item did not meet any of these conditions, it was excluded from the pretest scale29.

Psychometric evaluation of the scale

Content validity

Nine experts were invited to evaluate the content validity of the scale. The experts were selected using the same selection criteria as the Delphi experts previously. A four-point Likert scale, ranging from one (irrelevant) to four (very relevant), was used to collect the responses of experts. The item content validity index (I-CVI) was calculated as the ratio of the number of experts who ranked an item as 3 or 4 to the total number of experts. The average of all item I-CVI scores was calculated for the scale content validity index (S-CVI). An I-CVI score of 0.78 or above and an S-CVI score of 0.90 or above were required to meet the standards of content validity.

Construct validity

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA) was conducted to uncover the underlying factor structure of the scale and establish consistency with the conceptual framework to assess its structural validity. The Bartlett’s sphericity test and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) value were used to determine the suitability of the data for EFA. The KMO value above 0.6 was a prerequisite, and the results were significant (P < 0.05). All common factors should account for more than 40% of the total variance30,31. Additionally, Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was used to validate the factor structure identified by EFA and theoretical hypotheses. The recommended goodness-of-fit indices are reported in the table32,33. Meanwhile, the structural validity of the scale was evaluated in terms of convergent validity and discriminant validity. The convergent validity was evaluated by calculating average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR) values. The scale indicates appropriate convergent validity when the AVE value is above 0.50, but values above 0.40 are also acceptable, and the CR value is above 0.7034. Discriminant validity was evaluated by calculating the square root of the AVE value and the correlation coefficients between factors. The square root of the AVE value must be greater than the correlation coefficients between the corresponding factors34.

Calibration validity

The Chinese version of the Cancer Patients’ Self-Management Efficacy Scale (C-SUPPH) was adopted as the standard tool for evaluating the self-management efficacy of patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy to verify the validity of the calibration. The Strategies Used by People to Promote Health (SUPPH) was developed by the American scholar Lev in 199635 to assess self-efficacy in self-care. The original scale consists of 29 items across four dimensions: coping, stress relief, decision-making, and life enjoyment. A 5-point rating scale is adopted, with scores ranging from 1 to 5 representing no confidence, slight confidence, moderate confidence, considerable confidence, and extreme confidence, respectively. Higher scores indicate a higher level of self-management self-efficacy. The original scale has a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.93, and the four dimensions together explain 81% of the total variance. The scale was translated, culturally adapted, and revised by Chinese scholars Qian Huijuan and the research team led by Professor Yuan Changrong36, resulting in the Chinese version of the Strategies Used by People to Promote Health (C-SUPPH). The C-SUPPH contains 28 items distributed into three dimensions: self-relaxation, self-decision-making, and positive attitude. The self-relaxation dimension includes 10 items, the self-decision-making dimension comprises 3 items, and the positive attitude dimension has 15 items. All items are scored on a 1–5 Likert scale according to the level of self-confidence. The total score of the scale ranges from 28 to 140 points, which is classified into three efficacy levels: low efficacy (≤ 65 points), moderate efficacy (66–102 points), and high efficacy (≥ 103 points). Qian Huijuan applied the C-SUPPH to evaluate self-management self-efficacy among 194 cancer patients. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.970 and the KMO value was 0.948, demonstrating good reliability and validity of the scale. The C-SUPPH has been applied in patients with breast cancer37, gynecological cancer38, thyroid cancer,39 and nasopharyngeal carcinoma40. It exhibits satisfactory effectiveness and reliability, and can well measure self-management self-efficacy and explore its influencing factors among cancer patients.

Internal consistency, reliability, and test–retest reliability

The reliability of self-management in patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy was evaluated through internal consistency reliability, test–retest reliability, and split-half reliability. Cronbach’s α is often used to assess the internal consistency of questionnaires. The Cronbach’s α and split-half reliability coefficients were calculated to evaluate the homogeneity of the items. Test–retest reliability reflects the stability of the questionnaire. The test–retest reliability was assessed by calculating the correlation coefficient between the two measurements after 25 patients completed the questionnaire again two weeks later. It was necessary that the Cronbach’s α coefficient, split-half reliability coefficient, and test–retest reliability coefficient must all be at least 0.7.

Data collection

A questionnaire containing informed consent and expert consultation content was sent to experts via email in the first stage. It briefly introduced the purpose and significance of this study. Experts were requested to provide feedback and suggestions within two weeks of receiving the questionnaire. Additionally, in-person interviews were conducted with eight patients with head and neck tumors who were undergoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy in the ward, and they offered language suggestions for the content of the scale. In the second stage, a questionnaire survey was conducted among 400 patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy or chemotherapy under the direction of oncology case management nurses. All participating patients signed the informed consent form after being briefed on the purpose, importance, resources, and anonymity principle of the study.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 26.0) and the Analysis of Moment Structures (version 29.0) software. The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the study population were described using frequencies and percentages. Item analysis was completed to evaluate and screen items, and expert consultation was used to assess the content validity of the questionnaire. Factor analysis was used to evaluate the structural validity, including EFA and CFA. Maximum variance rotation was used to investigate the potential factor structure in EFA. The consistency of the factor structure with theoretical expectations was verified using the structural equation model and the maximum likelihood method. The C-SUPPH scale was used as the calibration test questionnaire. The homogeneity and stability of the questionnaire were evaluated through internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and split-half reliability analyses.

Regarding monthly income, 42% of the participants earned less than 3000 yuan, and 47.8% of the participants had been ill for 3 to 6 months. Generation and revision of the items

A draft including 69 items for self-management of patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy was developed after a literature review and content analysis. After two rounds of Delphi expert consultation, the scale content was revised in accordance with expert opinions, and a pretest version consisting of 55 items across 5 dimensions was developed. The experts agreed to adopt a 5-point Likert rating scale. (see Appendix A). The response rate of the first round of expert consultation was 100%, with an authority coefficient of 0.890 and a Kendall’s coefficient of concordance of 0.250 (P < 0.5). The response rate of the second round of the survey was 100%, with an authority coefficient of 0.900 and a Kendall’s coefficient of concordance of 0.265 (P < 0.5), which was within the acceptable range.

Evaluation and exploration of the items

Project analysis was conducted using CR, DI, and ITC. Items 1, 2, 6, 13, 17, 28, 31, 42, 43, 46, 47, and 53 that did not approach significance were deleted using the independent sample t-test. Items 10, 23, 32, 39, 44, and 48 having DI and ITC less than 0.3 were also deleted.

Psychometric evaluation of the scale

Content validity

Nine experts who had not participated in the previous Delphi survey were invited to evaluate the validity of the content. The survey results revealed that the consultation questionnaire had a 1.000 response rate, an I-CVI range of 0.778–1.000, and a S-CVI of 0.941.

Construct validity

Items 9, 11, 14, 18, 19, 24, 25, 33, 35, 45, 54, and 55 were deleted after three rounds of EFA. The data were suitable for EFA as indicated by the KMO value of 0.866 and the significant results of Bartlett’s sphericity test (χ2 = 3739.743, P < 0.001). A total of six common factors with eigenvalues ≥ 1 were obtained using the Promax oblique rotation method, which accounted for 71.78% of the total variance (Fig. 2). The factor loadings of all items were greater than 0.4, and there were no cross-loadings.

Items 15, 16, 29, 34, 38, and 40 with smaller standardized loadings were deleted after the initial model was adjusted twice in the CFA based on the modification indices. The fit indices of the modified model were then examined (Fig. 3), and the model was drawn. The model fit indices were χ2/df = 2.939, RMR = 0.096, GFI = 0.826, AGFI = 0.759, IFI = 0.910, TLI = 0.887, CFI = 0.909, and RMSEA = 0.099. The model was acceptable. The selected fit indices indicated that the six-factor structure scale had an appropriate fit. The AVE values ranged from 0.591 to 0.760, and the CR values ranged from 0.743 to 0.930 in the convergent validity analysis. The discriminant validity analysis revealed that the square root of AVE ranged from 0.769 to 0.872, and each value exceeded the correlation coefficient of its respective factor. In addition, six common factors with eigenvalues ≥ 1 were extracted via Promax oblique rotation, and the revised factor model accounted for 79.13% of the total variance. Criterion validity

The Chinese version of the scale, C-SUPPH, served as the criterion validity. The findings of the study demonstrated that the total score of the C-SUPPH scale was highly positively correlated with the total score of the Self-Management Ability Scale for HNC Patients Undergoing Radiotherapy and Chemotherapy (r = 0.706, P < 0.001).

Reliability test

The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.906, and the Cronbach’s α coefficients of each dimension ranged from 0.739 to 0.928. The split-half reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.711. The test–retest reliability coefficient was 0.806 when 25 patients who had been readmitted for head and neck radiotherapy and chemotherapy were re-assessed four weeks later.

Item reduction process

The initial item pool consisted of 55 items following expert consultation. A systematic item reduction process was conducted using multiple analytical procedures. First, item analysis was performed using the critical ratio, item–total correlation, and internal consistency. Based on predefined criteria, 18 items were removed due to low discrimination or poor correlation with the total score. Second, exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the remaining items. After three rounds of factor extraction and refinement, 12 items were further removed due to low factor loadings or cross-loadings. Third, confirmatory factor analysis was performed to validate the factor structure. Based on model modification indices and standardized factor loadings, 6 additional items were deleted to improve model fit. Finally, a total of 19 items were retained, forming a six-dimension scale.

Discussion

There are a few specialized assessment tools to evaluate the self-management ability of patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy, despite an increasing number of studies demonstrating the importance of self-management in improving the health outcomes of such patients2,41. Consequently, it is crucial to develop appropriate tools to identify and evaluate the self-management ability of patients with HNC during chemotherapy. The distinctive feature of this scale is that it is specifically designed for patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy, thereby enhancing its clinical applicability.

The scale is based on the concept framework of self-management. The item pool was established using literature review and semi-structured interviews. The items were merged and refined to form the first draft of the scale after consulting Delphi experts. Pretesting was followed by the formation of the second draft. A total of 18 items were deleted after screening to form the second draft. Then, reliability, validity, and responsiveness were tested. In the CFA, 12 items were deleted, and in the CFA, 6 items were deleted. The self-management scale for HNC patients undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy is an acceptable and relatively reliable self-reported instrument for assessing self-management ability in this population. This scale can be used to evaluate symptom self-management status among patients with HNC and serve as a feasible assessment tool for self-management interventions in clinical practice, demonstrating good applicability (see Appendix C). Healthcare professionals can identify patients with low self-management ability and their weaknesses by evaluating the self-management effect of patients with HNC undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. This information provides a foundation for the subsequent implementation of effective intervention studies and related health education.

Multidimensional tasks are the core focus of self-management for patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. The main challenge faced by patients with head and neck tumors during radiotherapy and chemotherapy is the management of disease manifestations, which encompasses treatment-related symptoms, swallowing and speech function exercises, daily life management, psychological and social adaptation, and nutritional management.

According to research, patients with head and neck tumors may experience several symptoms during radiotherapy and chemotherapy41. Patients may develop oral mucositis after receiving radiotherapy for 1–2 weeks (10–20 Gy), which is often accompanied by dry mouth, changed taste, and thick saliva. The skin may exhibit erythema reactions, increased local itching, and a burning sensation and pain after two weeks of radiotherapy with a dose of over 20 Gy. Extensive peeling may happen by the fourth week of radiotherapy, when the dose exceeds 40 Gy. This damage may leave scars and pigmentation after healing. These symptoms become more severe when chemotherapy is administered concurrently, and additional symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, constipation, and diarrhea may also occur. Additionally, most patients receive radiotherapy and chemotherapy in outpatient settings, and basic and additional symptoms need to be managed by the patients and their families.

Functional exercises for the head and neck, swallowing, and speech training are unique and important self-management contents for patients with head and neck tumors. The head and neck region are densely populated with vital organs and has a complex structure. Tumors in various locations within this region can readily affect adjacent tissues. Adjacent organs are also exposed to radiation when the primary tumor is treated with radiotherapy. Various studies have demonstrated42 that about 15–30% of patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy experience grade 3 dysphagia. Research indicates that early inspiratory muscle strength training can improve dysphagia and enhance the safety of swallowing in patients with nasopharyngeal carcinoma after radiotherapy43.

Patients with HNC may exhibit a range of physical symptoms and functional and psychological challenges during the course of radiotherapy and chemotherapy, which can bring about difficulties and changes in their daily lives. Patients need to adopt a series of self-management strategies and measures to maintain a normal daily life26. Psychosocial adaptation is also an important component of self-management for patients with HNC. According to research44, patients with HNC experience a significant symptom burden during radiotherapy and chemotherapy. They experience a series of psychological distresses, such as shock, shame, decreased relationship satisfaction, lack of confidence at work, anxiety, and depression. Some patients will go through a long period of self-adjustment to adapt to this process45. Furthermore, dietary management is a crucial aspect of the self-management process for patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Research demonstrates 46 that patients with head and neck tumors experience a number of nutrition-related symptom clusters during radiotherapy and chemotherapy, including changes in taste and smell, oral mucositis, and dysphagia, which are also long-term issues that patients and their families must deal with.

The dimensions and items of the scale were first determined based on the literature review. A pretest version with 55 items was developed after two rounds of Delphi surveys. The Delphi surveys indicated that the experts revealed satisfactory enthusiasm, authority, and consistency towards these items. Therefore, the newly developed scale is considered scientifically reasonable based on the literature analysis and the Delphi surveys.

The critical ratios in the project analysis satisfied the reference standard values, supporting the appropriate distinction of scale. Projects 1, 2, 6, 13, 17, 28, 31, 42, 43, 46, 47, 53, 10, 23, 32, 39, 44, and 48 did not reach significance and were deleted. The applicability of the scale was supported by the moderately to high degree of correlation coefficient of the total project. The deleted projects were subsequently excluded from the initial EFA. The remaining projects demonstrated higher stability with the support of factor loadings. Finally, projects 9, 11, 14, 18, 19, 24, 25, 33, 35, 45, 54, and 55 were deleted from the project analysis based on the recommended standard values, leaving 37 projects overall.

A factor analysis was conducted regarding the structural validity. A total of 25 items and 6 dimensions were extracted from the EFA, which accounted for 71.78% of the total variance. After 6 items were deleted from the CFA, a well-fitting model was obtained, with all fit indices within acceptable ranges, indicating that the scale has appropriate structural validity and the total variance increased to 79.13%. Additionally, acceptable AVE and CR values, as well as square roots of AVE values being greater than the correlation coefficients, suggest that the scale has good convergent and discriminant validities30. In terms of calibration validity, the C-SUPPH scale was previously proven to have high correlation and was thus used as a calibration tool. There was a significant correlation between the scores of the C-SUPPH scale and those of the newly developed scale, supporting the appropriate calibration validity of the new scale. Overall, the self-management scale for patients with head and neck cancer undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy is an acceptable and relatively reliable instrument with sound scientific rationale and good validity.

This study also validated the internal consistency and test–retest reliability of the scale. The Cronbach’s α coefficient and the split-half reliability coefficient of the scale exceeded the recommended reference values in terms of internal consistency reliability, demonstrating it to be appropriate. Additionally, the test–retest reliability coefficient achieved an appropriate range after re-measuring the previously marked participants, verifying the measurement stability of the scale. Overall, the self-management ability of patients with HNC undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy scale has good reliability.

Limitations

The results of this study prove the good reliability of the questionnaire. Still, this study has some noteworthy limitations. First, bias is inevitable due to the inherent nature of convenience sampling. Second, there are only two items in each of the two dimensions in the EFA. However, they were retained after carefully considering their importance in patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Finally, the newly developed scale is mainly targeted at patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy. However, these validation analyses were mainly conducted in a university-affiliated specialized cancer hospital in the southwest of China, which, to some extent, weakens the applicability and scientific of the newly developed scale in patients with other cancers. In addition, some items show relatively high ceiling and floor effects; therefore, the Likert scoring system should be further optimized in future studies.

Consequently, although this scale was specifically developed for patients with head and neck cancer, future research is warranted to enroll more diverse sample populations, clarify differences in influencing factors across various clinical characteristics, explore longitudinal changes in study indicators over time, and examine responses to intervention strategies, all of which will help further validate and refine the scale. Future studies may also consider conducting comparative analyses between a simplified version of the SMA-HNC and the full-length scale. In addition, further validation evidence is needed to support its applicability in other healthcare settings, as well as its cultural adaptability for assessing self-management behaviors among ethnic minority populations.

Conclusions

In this study, a self-management ability scale was developed for patients with head and neck tumors undergoing radiotherapy and chemotherapy with 6 dimensions and 19 items, and its psychometric properties were verified. This scale can accurately and comprehensively assess the self-management ability of patients with head and neck tumors during radiotherapy and chemotherapy. In future research, this newly developed scale can be used to investigate the level of self-management and identify influencing factors during radiotherapy and chemotherapy for patients with head and neck tumors, thereby formulating targeted interventions.
