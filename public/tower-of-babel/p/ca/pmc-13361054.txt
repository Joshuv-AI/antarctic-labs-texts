Common genetic variants in vitamin D metabolism-related genes affect gut microbiota diversity in postmenopausal women
Authors: Adriana Becerra-Cervera, Rogelio F Jiménez-Ortega, Diana I Aparicio-Bautista, Tania V López-Pérez, Edgar Denova-Gutiérrez, Berenice Palacios-González, Alberto Hidalgo-Bravo, Samuel Canizales-Quinteros, Jorge Salmerón, Berenice Rivera-Paredez, Rafael Velázquez-Cruz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent studies have demonstrated that vitamin D (VD) influences gut microbiota (GM) by regulating epithelial barrier function and immune responses, thereby affecting microbial composition and diversity. Additionally, host genetic factors, particularly single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in genes related to VD metabolism, may influence GM composition. Postmenopausal women are a particularly relevant population to assess the impact of these SNVs, as this group experiences reduced hormonal variability and a higher burden of vitamin D deficiency (VDD), enabling clearer evaluation of the association between vitamin D-related genetic variants and gut microbiota composition.ObjectiveTo explore the relationship between SNVs in genes involved in VD metabolism and their impact on GM.MethodsWe genotyped nine variants: rs10741657 (CYP2R1), rs6013897 (CYP24A1), rs10877012 (CYP27B1), rs10783219 and rs4516035 (VDR), rs4588 and rs7041 (GC), rs4944957 and rs3794060 (NADSYN1). GM was characterized by sequencing the V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene. We compared taxon abundance at the genus level across host genotypes using the dominant model. Quantile regression was used to analyze non-mean diversity responses, and PICRUSt2 identified functional pathways. We also calculated the genetic risk score (GRS) using rs4516035, rs3794060, and rs4944957, which were associated with lower alpha diversity.ResultsAlpha microbial diversity differed significantly for the VDR (rs4516035) and NADSYN1 (rs3794060, rs4944957) variants, both for risk alleles and genotypes. Differential abundance analysis identified taxonomic disparities, notably in the genera DTU014, Fusobacterium, Negativibacillus, Pseudomonas, Peptococcus, and [Clostridium]_innocuum_group. PICRUSt2-predicted functional pathways for the rs4516035- C allele revealed significant glutamine-glutamate, folate, galactose, and fatty acid metabolism. A GRS was associated with lower alpha diversity indices and differences in the insulin signaling pathway and oxidative phosphorylation.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the role of SNVs in GM modulation and suggest potential implications for VD metabolism and host health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12263-026-00806-y.Keywords: Vitamin D, Gut microbiota, Genetic variants, Postmenopausal women

Introduction

Vitamin D (VD), a lipid-soluble vitamin absorbed in the proximal small intestine, is essential for bone health, metabolic function, and immune system regulation. Both endogenous and exogenous factors influence VD acquisition, including diet composition, sunscreen use, obesity, skin pigmentation, and limited sunlight exposure [1]. In circulation, VD is primarily bound to its specific vitamin D-binding protein, also known as the GC-group component (GC). VD undergoes initial hydroxylation by the enzyme 25-hydroxylase to form 25-hydroxyvitamin D3. In the kidneys, 1α-hydroxylase (CYP27B1) converts this inactive form into its active form. Subsequently, the renal enzyme CYP24A1 catabolizes the active form of VD into inactive metabolites. The vitamin D receptor (VDR), a conserved nuclear receptor expressed in the intestinal epithelium, mediates the primary physiological actions of VD, and plays a critical role in intestinal homeostasis by regulating mucosal immunity [2].

Accumulating evidence demonstrates that the gut microbial community is shaped by multiple factors, including host genetics [3–5]. A genome-wide association study identified host genetic determinants that influence gut microbiota (GM) composition, with significant associations observed between microbial diversity and the VDR gene [6]. Differences in the abundance of Parabacteroides and Butyricimonas have been reported in a healthy Spanish cohort [7]. Additionally, a VDR KO murine model showed increased susceptibility to Clostridium rodentium, suggesting that VDR expression regulates innate lymphoid cells and interleukin-22 production, which contributes to dysbiosis [8].

A recent meta-analysis identified vitamin D deficiency (VDD) as a significant public health issue, with the highest prevalence (18.2%) among adults aged 19–44 years, followed by those aged 65 and older (15.3%) [9]. Women were more affected than men, with prevalence rates of 17.8% and 13.6%, respectively. In a study of young adults aged 18 to 50 years (n = 155) from Mexico City, 58.1% were found to have VDD, with a significantly higher proportion in females (65.7%) than in males (43.4%) [10]. The median dietary VD intake was 2.8 µg/day, representing less than 20% of the recommended daily intake of 15 µg/day for adults in this age group [10]. Furthermore, VDD is also highly prevalent among postmenopausal women with metabolic disorders [11], who are already at increased risk of cardiovascular disease [12]. Together, these findings highlight the importance of studying postmenopausal women, as this group faces a convergence of elevated VDD risk, metabolic vulnerability, and increased cardiovascular burden.

Understanding the impact of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) on the GM may provide novel insights for the prevention and management of chronic conditions. This study analyzed common genetic variations in genes related to VD metabolism, including precursor biosynthesis (NADSYN1), VD transport (GC), hydroxylation/dehydroxylation (CYP27B1, CYP2R1, and CYP24A1), and the VD receptor (VDR), and assessed their effects on GM diversity and composition in the Mexican-mestizo population.

Materials and methods

Study population

The Health Workers Cohort Study (HWCS) is a prospective study with three follow-ups, designed to explore the impact of genetic and environmental factors on chronic diseases. The participants included employees of the Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social (IMSS) and their relatives residing in urban areas of central Mexico. A total of 1,050 participants were recruited between 2017 and 2019, and stool samples were collected as described previously [13]. Participants completed a questionnaire on sociodemographic characteristics (birth date, education level, medical history, medical history of relatives, medication use, dietary intake, physical activity, smoking status, and alcohol consumption) [14]. Individuals with malnutrition, renal damage, and VD supplementation were excluded from the study, as these conditions have been observed to compromise VD metabolism. All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the IMSS, and the participants provided written informed consent.

16S rRNA sequencing

Total genomic DNA was isolated from stool samples using either the QIAamp® DNA Stool Mini Kit or the PowerFecal Pro Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocols. DNA concentration and purity were measured by spectrophotometry (NanoDrop 2000c; Thermo Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). For microbial community profiling, the hypervariable V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers (515F and 806R) according to the Earth Microbiome Project protocol. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 250 bp paired-end configuration; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the Sequencing Unit of INMEGEN (National Institute of Genomic Medicine). Further methodological details have been reported previously [15].

Bioinformatic analysis

The raw FASTQ files were processed using QIIME2, an advanced bioinformatics pipeline for microbial ecology analysis [16]. Data preprocessing included removing adapter sequences, sequences with barcode mismatches, and filtering low-quality reads (Phred score < 30). Reads were processed using DADA2 (v1.20.0) for denoising and removal of chimeric sequences, generating Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs). Taxonomic annotation was performed against the SILVA v138-99 reference database. For phylogenetic analysis, ASVs were aligned with MAFFT, and a maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree. The ASV table, taxonomic assignments, phylogenetic tree, and metadata were imported into R using the qiime2R package (v0.99.34) and compiled into a phyloseq object. All data analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3). To account for uneven sequencing depth and minimize biases in diversity comparisons, we implemented a rarefaction procedure. Specifically, samples were rarefied to 10,589 reads per sample, based on the sample with the lowest sequencing depth that still retained sufficient coverage. This process allowed for the retention of samples for downstream alpha and beta diversity analyses.

Regarding the data structure, we observed high dispersion and sparsity, which are inherent characteristics of amplicon sequencing data. Alpha diversity metrics were measured using observed ASV counts, Chao1 richness, Shannon index, and Simpson index. For functional profiling, we predicted metabolic pathways using KEGG Orthology (KO) at level 3, leveraging the PICRUSt2 tool (v2.1.3-b) [17]. Differentially abundant gene families (DAGs) were further analyzed for functional enrichment using STAMP software [18], with Welch’s t-test as the default method for two-group comparisons.

Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentration measurement

Blood samples were collected after an 8-hour overnight fast. Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentrations were determined using the LIAISON 25OH Vitamin D Total Assay by Diasorin, Saluggia (VC), Italy. The assay demonstrated low variability, with intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation below 10% [19]. Based on serum VD concentrations, samples were divided into two groups: (1) the VDD group, with serum 25(OH)D concentration < 20 ng/mL, and (2) the VD non-deficient (VDND) group, with serum 25(OH)D concentrations ≥ 20 ng/mL, as previously reported [19].

Genotyping of SNVs

Peripheral blood DNA extraction was conducted utilizing the Puregene Blood Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Genotyping of variants involved in VD metabolism was performed using pre-designed TaqMan SNV. The rs10741657 (CYP2R1), rs6013897 (CYP24A1), rs10877012 (CYP27B1), rs10783219 and rs4516035 (VDR), rs4588 and rs7041 (GC), rs4944957 and rs3794060 (NADSYN1). Genotyping assays from Applied Biosystems (MA, USA) on a QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Automatic variant calling was performed using SDS software version 2.1. The analysis, design, and selection of SNVs have been described previously [20, 21].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for body measurements and biochemical parameters are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For pairwise comparisons, we applied either the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (non-parametric) or Student’s t-test (parametric). Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) for each SNV was evaluated using the Chi-square (χ2) test. Genetic associations were tested under a dominant inheritance model (AA + Aa vs. aa, where A is the risk allele). Linear regression was used for continuous outcomes (serum 25(OH)D), and logistic regression for binary outcomes (VDD status). Models were adjusted for age (years), sex, energy intake (kcal/d), leisure-time physical activity (h/wk), alcohol consumption (g/d), hormone replacement therapy (HRT, only in females), VD intake (defined by tertiles), and blood collection season. Sex-stratified analyses included the same covariates, with HRT included only in female models. Given the limited sample size of premenopausal women, which precluded statistically powered subgroup analyses, all subsequent analyses were restricted to the postmenopausal cohort to ensure robust and interpretable results, following the criteria predefined by Davis et al. [22].

Alpha diversity indices and taxon abundances at the genus level across genotypes were analyzed under a dominant inheritance model. Differential abundance at the genus level was then evaluated using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe v1.0) [23], as implemented in the microeco package [24]. Significance was determined using a threshold linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score of ≥ 1.5 and a P-value < 0.05. To ensure statistical robustness, a minimum subsampling threshold of 50 (lefse_min_subsam = 50) was also applied. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, with significance levels defined as FDR < 0.1. All visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package in R [25].

The association between SNVs, serum 25(OH)D concentrations, and microbial diversity indices (Observed, Shannon, Chao1, and Simpson) was evaluated via quantile regression in postmenopausal women. Genotypes were coded as factorial variables, accounting for a dominant inheritance model. For each diversity index, quantile regression models (quantreg package) were fitted at τ = 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75 percentiles. This approach enabled assessment of heterogeneity in associations across the distribution of each diversity index. A statistical model was implemented per SNV, including main effects and adjusting for VD–related variables (age (years), energy intake (kcal/d), leisure-time physical activity (h/wk), alcohol consumption (g/d), hormone replacement therapy, VD intake (defined by tertiles), blood collection season, and sequencing kit). The variation explained by categorical and continuous variables was assessed using the Bray - Curtis distance (the OTU and genus levels) with the envfit function of the vegan package.

The genetic risk score (GRS) was estimated using an unweighted additive approach, summing the number of risk alleles for genetic variants (rs4516035, rs4944957, and rs3794060) associated with alpha diversity. Each variant was coded according to an additive genetic model (0, 1, or 2 risk alleles), and the GRS represented the total number of risk alleles across the loci. Individuals with 3, 4, 5, and 6 risk alleles were combined into a single category (≥ 3 alleles) due to low frequency.

To identify microbial features associated with metadata variables, MaAsLin3 [26] was employed with centered total sum-scaling (TSS) normalization and logarithmic transformation (LOG) of the data at the genus level. MaAsLin 3 is a modeling framework that simultaneously identifies abundance and prevalence relationships. Models were adjusted for confounders, including age, BMI, and serum VD concentrations. All other parameters in MaAsLin3 were set to their default values. Significance was defined as FDR < 0.1.

Host genetics and microbial associations

Global alpha diversity analysis showed no significant differences in microbial diversity indices between the VDD and VDND groups in the overall population (194 vs. 495, P > 0.05; Supplementary Fig. 2A). Similarly, no significant differences were observed between sexes within VDD and VDND groups (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Due to the limited sample size in age-stratified categories and hormonal shifts (n = 143), the analysis focused on potential microbial changes in postmenopausal women. The subsample for microbiota and host genotype analysis included 466 postmenopausal women with a median age of 60.0 years (55.0–68.0). The median BMI was 27.2 kg/m² (24.6–30.9), with 42.3% classified as overweight and 28.5% as living with obesity (Table 1).

As shown in Supplementary Table 5, the SNV rs4516035-VDR showed nominal associations with lower microbial richness indices. Carriers of the C allele (TC + CC genotypes) exhibited lower observed species, Chao1, and Shannon indices compared to TT homozygotes; however, these associations were suggestive after FDR correction (PFDR = 0.082). For the NADSYN1 gene, the rs4944957 showed an association with microbial evenness and dominance. Carriers of the A allele (GA + AA genotypes) showed a suggestive association with lower Shannon diversity (PFDR = 0.0527) and a statistically significant association with Simpson index values (PFDR = 0.0489). Additionally, carriers of the C allele (CT + CC genotypes) of the rs3794060-NADSYN1 gene exhibited significantly lower microbial diversity in the Shannon (PFDR = 0.0131) and Simpson (PFDR = 0.0038) indices than TT homozygotes. Association analyses between SNVs and alpha diversity using quantile regression under a dominant model yielded no significant results after FDR correction (Supplementary Table 6).

Several covariates related to inter-individual variation in bacterial community composition were investigated. Among ten factors significantly associated with inter-individual microbial distance in postmenopausal women, six were environmental variables, including SNVs. Notably, BMI (r² =0.018, PFDR = 0.064), triglycerides (r² = 0.019, PFDR = 0.064), and the SNVs rs4516035 (r² = 0.006, PFDR = 0.073) and rs4588 (r² = 0.005, PFDR = 0.066) showed suggestive associations with microbiota composition at the genus level. Together, these factors explained 4.86% of the variance in microbial composition (Supplementary Table 7).

The association between the relative abundance of bacterial DNA taxa and the genotypes of VDR (rs4516035) and NADSYN1 (rs4944957 and rs3794060) variants was examined. Carriers with at least one risk allele in the NADSYN1 gene (rs4944957 and rs3794060), exhibited significantly higher abundance of Negativibacillus and DTU014 than those with the ancestral allele (Fig. 1A and B). Fusobacterium, Erysipelatoclostridium, Acidaminococcus, and [Clostridium]_innocuum_group were significantly enriched in carriers of the VDR- rs4516035 C-risk allele (Fig. 1C). When alleles were grouped by genotype (non-risk and risk) (Supplementary Fig. 3), increased abundance of Akkermansia, Adlercreutzia, Pseudomonas, and Anaerotruncus was observed in the rs4516035-CC genotype of VDR. The AA genotype of rs4944957 of NADSYN1 showed enrichment for the genus DTU014. In contrast, the CC genotype of rs3794060 of the same gene was associated with increased abundance of Peptococcus, and the Treponema group compared to the TT genotype (Supplementary Fig. 3).

After establishing that host genetics influence vitamin D‑related microbial taxa, the contribution of genetic variants to differences in vitamin D‑related microbial functions was examined. Carriers of the C allele of rs4516035-VDR exhibited significant enrichment in specialized pathways, including carbohydrate metabolism, such as galactose metabolism and the phosphotransferase system (Fig. 2). Impact of genetic risk score on gut microbiota composition and metabolic function

The combined effect of risk alleles associated with alpha diversity was evaluated. A GRS based on all eight genetic variants showed no significant associations; therefore, a restricted GRS was estimated using only the three variants (rs4516035, rs4944957, and rs3794060) associated with alpha diversity. Categories with 3, 4, 5, and 6 risk alleles were combined due to low frequency. Among women aged 47 years or older, carriers of > 3 risk alleles showed lower diversity indices than those carrying ≤ 3 risk alleles. Significant differences after FDR correction were observed for the Observed (PFDR = 0.042), Shannon (PFDR = 0.0384), and Simpson (PFDR = 0.0384) indices, whereas the association with Chao1 diversity was suggestive (PFDR = 0.064) (Fig. 3A). Five genera were significantly enriched in carriers with > 3 risk alleles, including Weissella, Negativibacillus, Victivallaceae, Peptococcus, and Paeniclostridum (Fig. 3B). Exploratory functional analyses identified a significant enrichment of the insulin signaling pathway among carriers with > 3 risk alleles (q = 0.031). In addition, suggestive enrichments were observed for pentose and glucuronate interconversions (q = 0.069) and oxidative phosphorylation (q = 0.075) (Fig. 3C).

Discussion

In the present study, no differences in alpha diversity metrics were observed between individuals with VDD and those with VDND status in the overall population. This finding aligns with previous reports suggesting that the relationship between VD and the GM may involve distinct or indirect mechanisms [27]. Consequently, the focus shifted to host genetic components as potential drivers of microbiota variation. Building on prior work demonstrating that common genetic variants in VD metabolism-related genes influence serum VD concentrations [20], it was hypothesized that these same SNVs might also shape GM composition. The present findings extend prior knowledge by showing that VDR (rs4516035) and NADSYN1 (rs4944957 and rs3794060) variants are associated with significant changes in GM composition, particularly in postmenopausal women. Beyond establishing these novel genotype–microbiota associations, specific taxa enriched in risk allele carriers were identified, including DTU014, Fusobacterium, Negativibacillus, and Pseudomonas. These variants were also linked to distinct microbial functional pathways, such as insulin signaling, glutamine-glutamate, folate, galactose, and fatty acid metabolism. Collectively, these results suggest that host genetic variation may be associated with differences in gut microbial ecology and its metabolic potential, particularly in the context of postmenopausal physiology.

The GM is a key determinant of health and disease, and recent studies have highlighted the numerous factors influencing its diversity [28]. Significant associations were observed between the VDR (rs4516035) and NADSYN1 (rs4944957 and rs3794060) variants and multiple facets of GM diversity. The concurrent decline in both diversity indices (Shannon and Simpson) may reflect homeostatic dysregulation [29]. A decrease in the Shannon index could suggest the loss of rare species or a generalized reduction in commensal taxa. In contrast, a decrease in the Simpson index might suggest relative overgrowth of dominant, potentially pathogenic fractions, which could influence host innate immunity. Together, these shifts suggest an ecosystem transitioning from a high-diversity, functionally redundant state toward a less balanced configuration. Further studies are required to determine whether these patterns represent a true dysbiotic state. In this study, the rs4516035‑C allele was associated with increased abundance of the genera Acidaminococcus, Anaerotruncus, Fusobacterium, and Pseudomonas. Genetic variants in the VDR gene may influence cellular differentiation during immune responses [6, 30, 31] and predispose individuals to overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria such as Pseudomonas and Fusobacterium [32–36]. A genome-wide association study (GWAS) performed in two cohorts from northern Germany (n = 1,812 individuals) identified significant associations for overall microbial variation and individual taxa at multiple genetic loci, including the VDR gene [37]. Additionally, analyses of alpha diversity in Caucasian Spanish individuals (n = 86) revealed significant differences between populations carrying distinct genotypes at the TaqI polymorphism of the VDR gene [7]. Although several Firmicutes genera (Acidaminococcus, Anaerotruncus, Clostridium, Erysipelatoclostridium, Fusobacterium, Terrisporobacter,and Pseudomonas) identified in this study have been previously linked to serum VD concentration [38–42], no correlation was observed between the VD-related SNVs and circulating VD concentrations in this cohort. Based on previous evidence and current results, a possible association between the GM and certain VDR genotypes is suggested. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require further investigation, as the functional consequences of the lead SNVs remain unknown. The role of these genera in host–microbe interactions in humans remains unclear. Future studies incorporating metagenomic sequencing, functional assays, and larger independent cohorts are needed to validate these observations.

The NADSYN1 gene (NAD synthetase 1) encodes an enzyme involved in the biosynthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD⁺), a critical coenzyme for cellular energy metabolism. In this study, both SNVs in the NADSYN1 gene (rs4944957 and rs3794060) were associated with enrichment of DTU014 and Negativibacillus in the minor-allele frequency group. In Chinese postmenopausal women, DTU014 has been positively correlated with high serum VD concentrations but negatively associated with osteocalcin and parathyroid hormone [39], suggesting a potential protective role against bone loss [43]. In contrast, Negativibacillus has been linked to metabolic dysfunction, including metabolic disorders, chronic inflammation, and aging [44, 45]. This association is supported by a digital nutrition cohort study (n = 1,014 participants), which reported positive correlations between Negativibacillus abundance and fast food consumption, as well as negative correlations with the Healthy Eating Index [46]. Collectively, these findings indicate a possible interplay among diet, host genetics, and GM. This is consistent with previous work suggesting that circulating host nicotinamide contributes to microbial NAD synthesis and may thereby influence GM composition [47]. Further studies are required to validate these observations and clarify the underlying causal mechanisms.

PICRUSt was used to predict the functional potential of the GM from 16S rRNA data. As a prediction tool, PICRUSt has inherent limitations, and its outputs should be considered exploratory rather than definitive. After FDR correction, some metabolic pathways showed statistically significant associations with VDR genotype. Notably, pathways related to glutamine-glutamate and folate metabolism appeared downregulated in carriers of the VDR rs4516035-C allele. These pathways have previously been linked to energy metabolism [48–50], as well as to T2DM and adiposity markers [20, 51, 52]. The GM’s capacity to synthesize folate appears to be independent of diet quality and blood folate concentrations [53], highlighting the need for further research into host–microbe interactions within the metabolic system to better understand dietary implications. Despite the predictive nature of this analysis, these findings are consistent with prior reports and suggest that VDR gene variants may contribute to the pathogenesis of these conditions.

An exploratory genetic risk score (GRS) was generated to assess the combined effect of VDR (rs4516035) and NADSYN1 (rs4944957 and rs3794060). This analysis revealed that the abundance of the taxa Weissella, Negativibacillus, Victivallaceae, Peptococcus, and Paeniclostridium was associated with high GRS values and potential involvement in the insulin signaling pathway and oxidative phosphorylation. Victivallis, another member of the Victivallaceae family, has been linked to adiposity, hepatic steatosis, and diabetes in animal models [54, 55]. Peptococcus is more abundant in patients with knee osteoarthritis and VDD, as well as in those with autoimmune and irritable bowel diseases [43]. While most Weissella strains exhibit beneficial probiotic properties, W. confusa is the most clinically relevant species due to its opportunistic pathogenic behavior, having been isolated from severe infections such as bacteremia, endocarditis, postoperative osteomyelitis, and meningitis [56]. The mechanisms underlying the observed associations among taxa (Weissella, Peptococcus, and Negativibacillus) and their potential to mediate metabolic dysfunction require further investigation. The possibility that the GRS participates in predicted microbial metabolism suggests new research directions. However, given the risk of overfitting due to variant selection within the same dataset, as well as the inconsistent findings observed across analyses, these results should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis generating rather than conclusive.

This study has several strengths. First, it involves 466 postmenopausal women who have been thoroughly phenotyped regarding health behaviors, a subpopulation particularly susceptible to metabolic changes resulting from decreased estrogen levels. Second, various potential confounders were included in the analyses. Third, the GRS provides an integrated risk model for decision-making. However, several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to determine causality or the direction of the associations found. Second, the study is constrained by taxonomic classifications based on 16S rRNA amplicon profiling rather than comprehensive shotgun metagenomic sequencing data. Moreover, the putative functional capacity predicted by PICRUSt2 should be considered exploratory and requires validation through metatranscriptomics, metabolomics, or targeted biochemical assays. Third, although previous large-scale studies have identified associations between SNVs and serum VD concentrations, the sex-stratified analysis did not replicate these findings in postmenopausal women. This lack of significance may be explained by reduced statistical power due to the smaller sample size after stratification [57]. Fourth, the use of different DNA extraction kits may introduce technical variability. Although previous evidence suggests that kit-induced effects are generally minor compared to biological signals, particularly in contexts with pronounced group differences such as those reported here, their potential influence cannot be completely ruled out. Fifth, while previous studies have reported a relationship between BMI and serum VD concentrations, this connection was not directly examined in the present study. Future research should investigate this interaction to clarify its underlying mechanisms and potential confounding factors. Additionally, findings may differ in other cohorts, as contextual environmental effects may reshape the interplay between host genetics and GM. Finally, given the large number of comparisons, the residual risk of false-positive findings cannot be excluded even after FDR correction.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that genetic variants in genes involved in VD metabolism are associated with differences in GM diversity and composition among postmenopausal women. Additionally, genotype-specific differences were identified in predicted microbial functional pathways, suggesting potential shifts in the gut microbiota’s functional capacity. As these pathway results were inferred using PICRUSt2, they should be interpreted with caution and considered as hypothesis-generating. Direct functional profiling studies are necessary to determine whether these inferred differences translate to altered microbial metabolic activity and host physiological outcomes. Further research is essential to clarify the mechanisms by which genetic variants in the VDR and NADSYN1 genes influence GM composition and function, thereby laying the groundwork for future research and potential therapeutic approaches. Improved understanding of the interactions between genetic variants and the GM may ultimately facilitate personalized medical approaches. Postmenopausal women with specific gene variants could benefit from targeted probiotic or dietary interventions to enhance gut health and address VDD.
