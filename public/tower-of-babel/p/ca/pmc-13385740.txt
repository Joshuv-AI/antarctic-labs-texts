Filamin–ETV4/5 acts as mechanosensor–mechanotransducer axis that drives cell competition-mediated elimination of transformed cells
Authors: Jiaying Wen, Kazuhito Sai, Suzuka Masutani, Yuki Aoshima, Kotaro Yumi, Tomoki Kyo, Junya Hasegawa, Toshiki Itoh, Ken Mizuta, Tomonori Nakamura, Yukihiro Yabuta, Mitinori Saitou, Satoshi Toda, Yasuyuki Fujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCell competition is a fundamental surveillance mechanism that preserves epithelial integrity by eliminating aberrant “loser” cells through interactions with surrounding “winner” cells. However, how winner cells sense and eliminate transformed neighbours remains poorly understood. Here, using a synNotch-based transcriptomic screen, we identify a mechanosensor–mechanotransducer axis linking the actin crosslinker filamin to the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway that mediates extrusion of oncogenically transformed cells through cell competition. Within an epithelial monolayer, transformed cells increase their volume, inducing membrane stretching. In adjacent normal cells, filamin senses the increased membrane tension and triggers activation of ETV4/5. Activated ETV4/5 then upregulate PRKG2, which promotes volume expansion of normal cells, consequently pushing back and driving the extrusion of transformed cells. These findings reveal that dynamic changes in cell volume can trigger mechanotransduction-driven cell competition, uncovering a fundamental pathway by which epithelial tissues maintain homoeostasis and defend against oncogenic transformation.Subject terms: Cell signalling, Mechanotransduction, Cancer

Introduction

Multicellular organisms are equipped with a variety of inherent mechanisms to maintain tissue homeostasis against various perturbations, such as genetic mutations and cellular stresses. Among them, cell competition is one of the most crucial mechanisms in which aberrant or dysfunctional “loser” cells within a tissue are actively eliminated by surrounding “winner” cells, thereby preserving tissue integrity1–8. Cell competition was originally discovered in Drosophila9 and is now recognised as an evolutionarily conserved phenomenon extending to vertebrates10. Recent studies using mammalian models have demonstrated that, at the earliest stages of carcinogenesis, newly emerging transformed cells with a single oncogenic mutation can be eliminated from epithelial tissues via cell competition11. For example, when oncogenic Ras- or Src-transformed cells arise within epithelia, adjacent normal cells recognise these transformed cells and actively extrude them toward the apical side12,13. During apical extrusion, filamin, an actin-binding protein, accumulates in adjacent normal cells at the interface with transformed cells, thereby generating the physical forces required for extrusion14. This process was termed Epithelial Defence Against Cancer (EDAC), representing a tissue-intrinsic anti-tumour mechanism that functions independently of the immune system.

A key question in cell competition is how winner cells recognise loser cells that are destined to be eliminated. Several ligand-receptor interaction-based models have been proposed to explain this process. In the Flower-Azot model, when cells expressing the winner isoform of the transmembrane protein Flower interact with cells expressing the loser isoform of Flower, the transcription factor Azot is induced in the loser cells, resulting in their death15,16. Another example in Drosophila is the intercellular interaction between Protein tyrosine phosphatase 10D (Ptp10D) and Stranded at second (Sas). The cell surface receptor Ptp10D, which is normally localised to the apical surface of epithelial cells, relocates to the lateral membrane upon loss of cell polarity. This relocalisation enables interaction between Ptp10D and its ligand Sas on the surface of adjacent cells, leading to the elimination of polarity-deficient loser cells17. While these findings have significantly advanced our understanding of the possible mode of winner-loser interaction, it remains to be investigated how prevalently these mechanisms play a role in the diverse contexts of cell competition. Moreover, not only chemical signalling, but also physical cues, may play a role in the recognition between winner and loser cells. Various morphological and physical changes occur non-cell autonomously during cell competition between normal and transformed cells, including increased height of transformed cells12 and alterations in membrane tension at the normal-transformed cell interface18. In addition, topological defects in epithelia often lead to the apical delamination of cells19, implying that physical imbalance within an epithelial layer could facilitate the process of cell extrusion. Nevertheless, the identity of the mechanosensing and mechanotransduction machinery during cell competition remains elusive.

In this study, we have identified the filamin–ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway, which is activated in normal cells in response to an increased volume of adjacent transformed cells. This pathway in turn induces the enlargement of surrounding normal cells, consequently pushing back and driving the extrusion of transformed cells. Thus, dynamic cell volume changes activate the mechanotransduction machinery in adjacent cells, thereby inducing cell competition.

Results

ETV4, ETV5 and PRKG2 are identified as cell competition regulators

In order to identify regulators of cell competition, we adopted a screening approach utilising the synthetic Notch (synNotch) system. The synNotch system is an engineered Notch signalling platform that enables user-defined gene expression in response to cell-cell interactions20. In this system, “sender” cells expressing a specific ligand (synNotch ligand) are co-cultured with “receiver” cells bearing a chimeric Notch receptor (synNotch receptor) composed of an extracellular domain recognising the synNotch ligand, the Notch juxtamembrane segment and an intracellular transcription factor domain. As in canonical Notch signalling, ligand binding to the synNotch receptor induces proteolytic cleavage at the juxtamembrane segment, leading to nuclear translocation of the transcription factor and activation of programmed gene expression20. This technology allowed us to specifically label and isolate normal cells directly contacting transformed cells during cell competition. We used EpH4 cells, a murine non-transformed mammary epithelial cell line, and established EpH4 cells stably expressing oncogenic RasV12 or cSrcY527F in a doxycycline-dependent manner. Cell competition-mediated elimination of transformed cells occurred in EpH4 cells, as previously observed in Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells12,13; when Ras- or Src-transformed cells were surrounded by normal cells, the transformed cells were apically extruded, whereas apical extrusion did not occur when transformed cells were cultured alone (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). We first established the sender (transformed) and receiver (normal) cell lines (Fig. 1a). The sender cells (EpH4-pTRE3G GFP-RasV12) expressed BFP and the synNotch ligand CD19, and the receiver cells harboured a UAS-mCherry reporter cassette and a synNotch receptor composed of an anti-CD19 antibody, the Notch juxtamembrane segment and the GAL4 transcription factor. When these cells were co-cultured, mCherry expression was induced in receiver cells directly contacting sender cells (Fig. 1b). We co-cultured sender and receiver cells at a 1:3 ratio in the absence or presence of doxycycline and isolated mCherry-positive receiver cells by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for bulk RNA-seq analysis (Fig. 1c). Dozens of genes were upregulated in mCherry-positive receiver cells contacting RasV12-expressing sender cells compared with those contacting non-transformed sender cells (Fig. 1d, red dots). Among them, we confirmed by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) analysis that the expression of Etv4, Etv5 and Prkg2 was significantly elevated in mCherry-positive normal cells that were in direct contact with RasV12-transformed cells, compared with those contacting non-transformed cells (Fig. 2a). A smaller upregulation was also observed in mCherry-negative normal cells that were not in contact with RasV12 cells (Fig. 2a). Comparable upregulation of Etv4, Etv5, and Prkg2 was also observed in normal cells contacting Src-transformed cells (Supplementary Fig. 2a). The Etv4 and Etv5 genes encode the ETS transcription factors ETV4 and ETV5, members of the PEA3 subfamily of the E26 transformation-specific (ETS) family, which regulate diverse biological processes including cell proliferation, cell motility and tumourigenesis21,22. The Prkg2 gene encodes the membrane-bound serine/threonine kinase PRKG2 (also known as PKG2/cGKII), a member of the cyclic GMP-dependent protein kinase (PKG) family23,24. Treatment with the ETS inhibitor YK-4-279 partially but significantly suppressed the upregulation of Prkg2 (Fig. 2b), suggesting that Etv4 and/or Etv5 act upstream of Prkg2, though other upstream regulators might be present. To monitor ETS transcriptional activity, we generated EpH4-pETS (ETS promoter) DD-tdTomato cells, in which DD-tdTomato expression is transcriptionally induced upon ETS activation. The destabilization domain (DD) causes rapid proteasomal degradation of tdTomato, which can be blocked by the stabilizing ligand Shield1. Thus, Shield1 administration enables temporal monitoring of ETS activity by fluorescence. A known activator of ETS, epidermal growth factor (EGF), induced tdTomato expression in this reporter cell line, which was suppressed by YK-4-279 (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Using this reporter system, we confirmed that ETS activity was increased in normal cells contacting Ras- or Src-transformed cells (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 2c). ETS activity was also elevated in normal cells at the second row from Ras cells, but to a lesser extent (Fig. 2c). Knockdown of E-cadherin in RasV12-transformed cells suppressed ETS activity in the surrounding normal cells (Fig. 2d), suggesting that upregulation of ETS is mediated through E-cadherin-based cell-cell adhesions.

To elucidate the functional role of the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway, we examined the effect of ETV4/5 or PRKG2 knockdown in normal cells on apical extrusion of transformed cells (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). When RasV12-transformed cells were surrounded by ETV4/5-double knockdown cells, apical extrusion was strongly suppressed (Fig. 3a). In addition, YK-4-279 treatment significantly reduced the frequency of apical extrusion (Fig. 3b). Similarly, apical extrusion of Src-transformed cells was diminished when surrounded by ETV4/5-knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig. 3c). PRKG2 knockdown in normal cells also suppressed apical extrusion of RasV12- or Src-transformed cells (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Fig. 3d). Furthermore, the PKG inhibitor KT-5823 or Rp-8-Br-cGMPS diminished apical extrusion of RasV12-transformed cells (Fig. 3d). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway positively regulates the cell competition-mediated elimination of transformed cells.

PRKG2 upregulation drives volume expansion

We next investigated the underlying mechanisms by which the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway promotes apical extrusion of transformed cells. Previous studies have shown that PRKG2 positively regulates cell volume by phosphorylating and regulating ion channels, leading to increased water influx through aquaporin channels25,26. We thus measured cell volume by analysing a stack of serial cross-sectional images of cells captured by confocal microscopy. Indeed, overexpression of PRKG2 significantly increased EpH4 cell volume (Supplementary Fig. 4a). We then examined changes in cell volume during cell competition. GFP- or GFP-RasV12-expressing cells were co-cultured with normal cells, and the volume of each cell type was analysed. At 24 h after doxycycline addition, normal cells directly contacting GFP-RasV12-expressing cells showed a significant increase in cell volume compared with those directly contacting GFP-expressing cells (Fig. 4a). We also found that RasV12-expressing cells had a 1.5-fold greater volume than GFP-expressing cells (Fig. 4a), consistent with the increased cell height in RasV12 cells under the co-culture condition (Supplementary Fig. 4b)12; the volume increase may provide a physical basis for the increase in cell height. The volume increase in RasV12 cells was detectable at 6 h after doxycycline addition, followed by a clear increase in the volume of surrounding normal cells at 16–24 h (Supplementary Fig. 4c). Comparable volume changes were also observed in Src-transformed cells and the surrounding normal cells (Supplementary Fig. 4d). We then examined whether PRKG2 is involved in these cell volume changes during cell competition. When PRKG2 was knocked down in normal cells, no increase in cell volume was observed in normal cells directly contacting RasV12 cells (Fig. 4b). Similarly, treatment with the PKG inhibitor Rp-8-Br-cGMPS also diminished the volume increase in normal cells (Fig. 4c), indicating that upregulated Prkg2 expression accounts for the increased volume in surrounding normal cells. In contrast, Rp-8-Br-cGMPS treatment partially reversed the volume expansion of RasV12 cells (Fig. 4c). Knockdown of ETV4/5 in normal cells also significantly suppressed volume increase in normal cells directly contacting RasV12 cells (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, treatment with the aquaporin inhibitor CuSO4 diminished the volume increase in normal cells directly contacting RasV12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4e) and significantly suppressed apical extrusion of RasV12 cells (Fig. 4e). Collectively, these data indicate that upregulation of Prkg2 expression induces an increase in the volume of normal cells surrounding transformed cells, thereby promoting cell competition.

ETS and PKG regulate extrusion of transformed cells in vivo

We further investigated the functional significance of ETS and PKG in the elimination of transformed cells in vivo using a cell competition mouse model (Fig. 5a)27,28. In the CK19-RasV12 mice, treatment with a low dose of tamoxifen induces mosaic expression of GFP and RasV12 in a variety of epithelial tissues. As a control, the CK19-YFP mouse was used in which expression of only YFP is induced. We have previously reported that newly emerging RasV12-expressing cells, but not YFP-expressing cells, are often apically extruded from the epithelium in the lung, intestine and pancreas27,28. We first analysed the cell size of YFP- or RasV12-expressing cells and the adjacent wild-type cells within the lung ductal epithelium three days after tamoxifen injection. Wild-type cells contacting RasV12 cells were significantly larger than those contacting YFP cells (Fig. 5b). We also found that the size of RasV12 cells was greater than that of YFP cells (Fig. 5b). Treatment with the PKG inhibitor KT-5823 suppressed the cell size change in wild-type cells, but not in RasV12 cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 5a), suggesting that PKG regulates the volume of wild-type cells contacting RasV12 cells, but not the volume of RasV12 cells. We then examined the effect of KT-5823 or the ETS inhibitor YK-4-279 on apical extrusion of RasV12-transformed cells in the lung epithelial tissues. When KT-5823 or YK-4-279 was administered, apical extrusion of RasV12 cells was significantly suppressed, and a larger fraction of RasV12 cells remained within the epithelium (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b). Hence, ETS and PKG positively regulate the cell competition-mediated elimination of transformed cells in vivo.

Cell swelling triggers extrusion through the ETS pathway

Since the size of RasV12-transformed cells increased prior to their apical extrusion, we next examined whether an increase in cell volume alone is sufficient to activate the ETS pathway in surrounding cells and thereby promote apical extrusion. First, we showed that transient overexpression of aquaporin 3 (AQP3) significantly increased cell volume as previously reported (Fig. 6a)29. We then established EpH4 cells stably expressing AQP3 in a doxycycline-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 6a, b) and confirmed that AQP3 overexpression increased cell volume in this cell line as well (Fig. 6b). In addition, as observed for RasV12 cells, the volume of cells in contact with AQP3-expressing cells was significantly increased (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, using the ETS reporter system, we demonstrated that ETS transcriptional activity was elevated in cells directly contacting AQP3 cells (Fig. 6c), indicating that ETS is activated when the volume of adjacent cells increases. Moreover, AQP3-expressing cells were often apically extruded when surrounded by normal cells, whereas apical extrusion did not frequently occur when AQP3 cells were cultured alone (Fig. 6d). Importantly, apical extrusion of AQP3 cells was strongly inhibited when surrounded by ETV4/5-double knockdown or PRKG2-knockdown cells (Fig. 6e). Thus, cell-autonomous volume expansion is sufficient to trigger non-cell-autonomous mechanotransduction via the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway in adjacent cells, thereby driving extrusion of the enlarged cells.

Membrane stretching upregulates Etv4/5 and Prkg2

We then investigated how cells sense and respond to an increase in the volume of adjacent cells. In a previous study, using Flipper-TR, we have demonstrated that membrane tension is increased in normal MDCK cells when they are co-cultured with RasV12-transformed cells18. Flipper-TR is a small molecule that intercalates into membranes, and membrane tension can be monitored through changes in its fluorescence lifetime; increased membrane tension leads to an elongation of the Flipper-TR lifetime30. First, we confirmed using EpH4 cells that the Flipper-TR lifetime was increased by hypo-osmotic treatment which induces membrane stretching (Supplementary Fig. 7a), validating the usage of Flipper-TR as a membrane tension sensor in EpH4 cells. As observed in MDCK cells, the Flipper-TR lifetime was elongated at cell-cell contact sites between normal and RasV12-transformed cells relative to those between monocultured normal cells (Fig. 7a). The Flipper-TR lifetime was also elongated between the first and second row cells, but to a lesser extent (Fig. 7a). Similarly, the Flipper-TR lifetime was elongated at the boundary between AQP3-expressing cells and adjacent cells (Supplementary Fig. 7b), indicating that membrane stretching occurs at cell–cell junctions in response to volume expansion. We then examined the effect of membrane stretching on the expression of Etv4/5 and Prkg2. Cells were incubated on a stretchable polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membrane, and a 10% stretch was applied. After 2-6 h of stretching, the expression of Etv4, Etv5 and Prkg2 was significantly elevated (Fig. 7b). Treatment with the ETS inhibitor YK-4-279 suppressed the upregulation of Prkg2 in response to stretching (Fig. 7c). Furthermore, hypo-osmotic treatment also increased cell volume and the expression of Etv4, Etv5 and Prkg2 (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Using the ETS reporter system, we confirmed that ETS transcriptional activity was promoted by hypo-osmotic treatment (Fig. 7d). These results indicate that the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway can function as a component of the mechanotransduction machinery triggered by membrane stretching. We next examined whether an increase in the volume of RasV12-transformed cells causes activation of the mechanotransducer ETS in adjacent cells. We previously demonstrated that the height of RasV12 cells was increased when surrounded by normal cells, a process regulated by Cdc4212. Consistently, co-expression of a dominant-negative mutant of Cdc42 (Cdc42N17) suppressed the increased volume of RasV12 cells surrounded by normal cells (Supplementary Fig. 7e). We then found that co-expression of Cdc42N17 in RasV12 cells strongly reduced the upregulated ETS activity in adjacent normal cells (Fig. 7e), suggesting that the increased volume of transformed cells triggers activation of the ETS pathway. Taken together, these results delineate a mechanistic sequence in which cell swelling induces membrane stretching, which in turn activates the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway in adjacent cells, leading to apical extrusion of the enlarged cells. This highlights a volume-sensing mechanotransduction machinery that enables epithelia to eliminate abnormally swollen or transformed cells.

Filamin is a mechanosensor acting upstream of ETS

Finally, we explored the identity of a mechanosensor responding to membrane stretching at cell-cell adhesions between normal and transformed cells and acting upstream of the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway. First, we examined the involvement of mechanosensitive calcium channels such as Piezo and transient receptor potential (TRP) C1 and C6 channels. However, treatment with GsMTx-4, an inhibitor of mechanosensitive calcium channels, did not significantly affect ETS activity in normal cells adjacent to RasV12-transformed cells (Supplementary Fig. 8a). In addition, we examined the involvement of α-catenin, a tension transducer at cell-cell adhesions, by using an antibody recognizing force-activated α-catenin31, but could not obtain evidence that α-catenin plays a role in cell competition as a mechanosensor (Supplementary Fig. 8b). We have previously reported that the homodimeric actin-binding protein filamin in normal cells positively regulates apical extrusion of adjacent transformed cells14. Filamin crosslinks and stabilizes cortical F-actin filaments and can function as a mechanosensor that responds to membrane stretching32. We first confirmed that filamin plays a positive role in apical extrusion of RasV12-transformed cells in the EpH4 cell culture system (Fig. 8a), as previously observed in MDCK cells14. We also demonstrated that filamin accumulation was promoted between cell-cell contact sites upon membrane stretching (Supplementary Fig. 8c), suggesting that filamin acts as a mechanosensor and accumulates at the stretched membranes. We then found that when RasV12 cells were surrounded by filamin-knockdown cells, ETS activation in the directly contacting normal cells was diminished (Fig. 8b and Supplementary Fig. 8d). Additionally, after 16 h of RasV12 induction by doxycycline, filamin accumulated in the adjacent normal cells at the boundary with RasV12 cells (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Furthermore, when ETV4/5 were knocked down, the accumulation of filamin was strongly suppressed (Supplementary Fig. 8f), indicating the presence of a feedback loop between filamin activation/accumulation and ETV4/5 activation. Moreover, filamin knockdown strongly suppressed membrane stretching-mediated activation of ETS (Fig. 8c). Collectively, these results suggest that filamin is a mechanosensor acting upstream of the ETS pathway during cell competition.

Discussion

Here, we demonstrate that dynamic changes in the volume of transformed cells can trigger cell extrusion through a mechanosensor-mechanotransducers axis composed of filamin and ETV4/5–PRKG2 (Fig. 8d). Increased volume of transformed cells enhances interfacial tension between transformed and surrounding normal cells. The resulting membrane stretching activates the filamin–ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway in adjacent normal cells, increasing their volume and, acting in concert, ‘pushing back’ to mechanically drive extrusion of the transformed cells. Mathematical modelling has predicted that mechanical alterations within epithelial layers can promote cell extrusion33–36. Consistent with these predictions, our findings indicate that changes in cellular physical properties, particularly cell volume, can act as a mechanical trigger for cell competition. Interestingly, increased cell volume is also observed in other types of loser cells, including Scribble-knockdown cells37,38. It will be of great interest to determine whether the filamin–ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway represents a conserved mechanism underlying cell competition across distinct cellular contexts. Our data using AQP3-expressing cells indicate that an increase in cell volume is sufficient to trigger apical extrusion through the ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway when AQP3-expressing cells arise in a mosaic manner. However, because the extrusion ratio of AQP3-expressing cells was lower than that of RasV12-transformed cells, cell-volume increase alone is unlikely to fully account for the efficient elimination of transformed cells. It is therefore likely that the volume-sensing mechanism operates in parallel with other mechanisms or factors implicated in cell competition, including ligand–receptor-mediated recognition pathways15,17 and metabolic and calcium-dependent pathways18,27,39 as well as physical properties of transformed cells such as increased compressibility40. Notably, homogeneous expression of RasV12 or AQP3 does not frequently induce apical extrusion. Thus, volume increase per se is not sufficient, but local mechanical imbalance generated at the interface between cell populations with different volumes induces non-cell autonomous changes and could thus be a critical determinant of extrusion, although it remains unclear whether this depends primarily on simultaneous volume increase in adjacent normal cells, temporal differences in volume change, or both.

Previous studies have shown that compressive forces generated by surrounding cells promote extrusion from the epithelial layer, a process termed mechanical cell competition41,42. In this context, mechanical compression is exerted not only by directly contacting adjacent cells, but also by cells that are not in direct contact. By contrast, the cell volume-mediated apical extrusion described in the present study appears to operate primarily through direct interactions at the interface between normal and transformed cells. Nevertheless, both processes share the feature that compressive forces contribute to cell elimination from the epithelial layer. It should also be noted that, in cell volume-mediated extrusion, the underlying mechanism may involve not only lateral pressure from surrounding normal cells but also additional mechanisms that sense and resolve morphological imbalance between neighbouring cells, for example, differences in cell height.

Filamin and ETV4/5 function as a mechanosensor and a mechanotransducer, respectively. Filamin is a homodimeric actin-binding protein that crosslinks orthogonal F-actin networks via its V-shape dimer structure. Mechanical deformation of the actin networks exposes cryptic binding sites on filamin, thereby altering its affinity for interacting partners32. This property enables filamin to act as a sensor of mechanical stress. How mechanical activation of filamin leads to the upregulation of ETV4/5 remains unclear. We found that the MEK inhibitor U0126 markedly suppresses the upregulation of Etv4/5 and Prkg2 during cell competition or upon stretching (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). However, FRET analysis revealed no detectable ERK activation in normal cells directly contacting RasV12-transformed cells (Supplementary Fig. 9c), suggesting that the basal ERK activity is required, but is not sufficient for ETV4/5 activation; additional pathways are therefore likely to be involved. Our data further indicate that membrane stretching upregulates ETV4/5 expression and activates the ETS pathway. In contrast, cell crowding has been reported to suppress ETV4 activity43. Thus, dynamic modulation of ETS activity may allow cells to adapt to diverse mechanical environments. Our newly developed ETS reporter system will be a valuable tool to monitor such mechanoresponsive behaviours. Regarding downstream of ETV4/5, our synNotch-based RNA-seq screening identified Mmp9 and Trib2 as other candidate target genes, whose expression was suppressed by the ETS inhibitor YK-4-279 (Supplementary Fig. 9d). Thus, together with PRKG2, MMP9 and TRIB2 may act as downstream effectors of the ETV4/5 pathway to orchestrate the extrusion response, although this possibility remains to be investigated in future studies.

Cell competition constitutes a fundamental homoeostatic mechanism that continuously removes defective or aberrant cells. However, pathological conditions such as inflammation, obesity and ageing have been shown to attenuate this process28,44. It will be important to clarify whether these conditions compromise the filamin–ETV4/5–PRKG2 pathway. Identification of pharmacological or nucleotide-based modulators that sustain or enhance the filamin/ETS axis could reinforce our intrinsic defence against transformed/aberrant cells, offering new avenues for preventive medicine.

Methods

Antibodies and materials

The following primary antibodies were used: anti-GAPDH (Merck Millipore, #MAB374), anti-Myc tag (clone 4A6, Merck Millipore, #05-724), anti-E-cadherin (clone ECCD-2, Takara, #M108), anti-Filamin A (Sigma-Aldrich, #F6682), anti-GFP (Abcam, #ab13970) and anti-α-Catenin (Sigma-Aldrich, # C2081). Anti-α18 antibody was a gift from A. Nagafuchi31. Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated phalloidin (Life Technologies) was used at 1.0 U ml-1. Alexa Fluor 405-, 488-, 568- and 647-conjugated secondary antibodies were obtained from Life Technologies. Hoechst 33342 (Life Technologies) was used at a 1:5000 dilution. 7-Aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; BioLegend, #420404) was used for FACS. CMAC (CellTracker Blue, #C2110) and CMTPX (CellTracker Red, #C34552) were obtained from Invitrogen and used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Chemical compounds, dissolved in either DMSO or water, were used at the following final concentrations: Rp-8-Br-cGMPS (10 μM; Calbiochem, #370677), KT-5823 (5 μM; Calbiochem, #420321), YK-4-279 (5 μM; Selleck Chemicals, #S7679), Copper (II) sulfate (150 μM; Sigma-Aldrich, #C1297), U0126 (20 μM; Promega, #V112A), GsMTx-4 (10 μM; PEPTIDE, #4393-s). Recombinant murine EGF (#315-09) was obtained from PeproTech. Type I collagen (Cellmatrix® Type I-A) was obtained from Nitta Gelatin and was neutralised on ice according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To construct pcDNA4/TO Myc-RasV12, the cDNA encoding Myc-HRasV12 was subcloned into the BamHI and ApaI restriction sites of the pcDNA4/TO vector. pcDNA4/TO was obtained from Invitrogen (Paisley, United Kingdom). To construct pEGFP-Cdc42(T17N), human CDC42 cDNA was amplified by PCR and inserted into the BamHI and SalI sites of the pEGFP-C1 vector (Clontech) containing a modified multiple cloning site. The T17N point mutation was introduced by PCR-based site-directed mutagenesis using specific primer sets. pCMV-PRKG2-Myc (#MR225140) was from OriGene.

Cell culture

Cells were maintained in DMEM (Wako, #045-30285) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma-Aldrich, #F7524), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, #15140-122) and 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, #35050-061) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For osmotic stress experiments, cells were cultured in either Hypo-osmotic or Iso-osmotic medium. The Hypo-osmotic medium was prepared by mixing 44% DMEM, 44% sterile water, 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 1% GlutaMAX. For the Iso-osmotic medium, sterile water was replaced with an equal volume of 330 mM mannitol solution. For transient gene overexpression, 3.5 × 105 or 7.0 × 105 EpH4 cells were seeded on Type-I collagen gel-coated coverslips in 12-well plates or 35-mm dishes, respectively, and transfected with the indicated plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, #L3000015) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Type-I collagen was obtained from Nitta Gelatin (Nitta Cellmatrix type 1-A) and was neutralized on ice to a final concentration of 2 mg ml−1 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Glass coverslips in 12-well culture dishes were coated with 0.5 ml of neutralized collagen and allowed to solidify for 30 min at 37 °C. For transient gene knockdown, siRNAs (listed in Supplementary Table 1) were transfected using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen, #13778150) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Knockdown efficiency was evaluated by qPCR, western blotting or immunofluorescence at 72 h post-transfection. For testing the effect of inhibitors on apical extrusion or cell volume, all inhibitors were added concurrently with doxycycline, except YK-4-279 and KT-5823, which were added during the final 12 h and 10 h, respectively, to minimize cytotoxicity. For the effect on gene expression in normal cells contacting transformed cells, all inhibitors were added concurrently with doxycycline. For the effect on stretching-induced gene expression, all inhibitors were added 1 h prior to the stretching. For the effect on EGF-induced ETS reporter activation, YK-4-279 was added 1 h prior to the addition of EGF.

Mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Care Committee of Kyoto University. Cytokeratin19 (CK19)-CreERT2 mice were crossed with R26R-CAG-loxP-STOP-loxP-eYFP or DNMT1-CAG-loxP-STOP-loxP-HRasV12-IRES-eGFP mice to generate CK19-YFP or CK19-RasV12-GFP mice, respectively28. Both male and female C57BL/6 mice aged 8–10 weeks were used. All mice were bred and/or maintained under a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle with food and water available at all times at the Institute of Laboratory Animals, Graduate School of Medicine, Kyoto University. Genotyping was performed by PCR using primers listed in Supplementary Table 1. To induce YFP or RasV12/GFP expression, mice were given a single intraperitoneal injection of tamoxifen (2 mg per 20 g body weight; Sigma-Aldrich) in corn oil and analysed at 3 days post-injection. For PKG inhibition, KT-5823 (Focus Biomolecules, #10-2083) was prepared as a 4.4 mg ml-1 stock in DMSO and diluted to a final working solution comprising 5% KT-5823 stock, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80 and 50% ddH₂O. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with the KT-5823 working solution (100 μl per 22 g body weight, 1 mg kg-1 of KT-5823) once daily for three consecutive days. For ETS inhibition, YK-4-279 (Selleck Chemicals, #S7679) was prepared as a 660 mg ml-1 stock in DMSO and diluted similarly to a final working solution comprising 5% YK-4-279 stock, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween-80 and 50% ddH₂O. Mice were intraperitoneally injected (100 μl per 22 g body weight, 150 mg kg-1 of YK-4-279) once daily for three consecutive days. DMSO was used as vehicle control for KT-5823 and YK-4-279. Mice were euthanized by CO₂ inhalation in accordance with institutional guidelines and subsequently transcardially perfused with PBS, followed by 1% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for tissue fixation.

Establishment of cell lines

EpH4 (Sigma-Aldrich Cat# SCC284), K562 (from S. Toda), MDCK (from W. Birchmeier) and Lenti-X 293 T (Takara Cat# 632180) cell lines were used in this study. To generate EpH4-pTRE3G GFP, EpH4-pTRE3G GFP-RasV12, EpH4-pTRE3G cSrcY527F-GFP and EpH4-pTRE3G Myc-RasV12 cells, the cDNA encoding GFP, GFP-HRasV12, cSrcY527F-GFP or Myc-HRasV12 was subcloned into the BamHI and EcoRI restriction sites of the pPB-TRE3G-MCS-CEH-rtTA3-IP vector27. EpH4 cells were subsequently transfected with the respective plasmid using nucleofection (Nucleofector Kit V, #VCA-1003, Lonza), and stable transfectants were selected in the medium supplemented with 5 μg ml-1 blasticidin (Invitrogen).

To establish sender cells (EpH4-TRE3G GFP-RasV12/CD19/BFP, EpH4-TRE3G cSrcY527F-GFP/CD19/BFP or EpH4- CD19/BFP cells), viral supernatants were produced by transfecting Lenti-X 293 T cells with pHR-EF1a-CD19-P2A-BFP (transfer vector)45, pCMV-dR8.91 (packaging vector), and pMD2.G (VSV-G envelope vector) using polyethylenimine (PEI) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The viral supernatants were subsequently used to infect EpH4-pTRE3G GFP-RasV12, EpH4-TRE3G cSrcY527F-GFP or EpH4 cells in the presence of polybrene45. Stable sender clones were established by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for BFP-positive cells and expanded from single colonies. To establish receiver cells (EpH4-anti-CD19-synNotch/UAS-mCherry), viral supernatants were similarly produced with pHR-UAS-mCherry-PGK-mycscFvCD19-Gal4VP64-synNotch (transfer vector), which was built by inserting DNA cassettes of PGK promoter and anti-CD19-synNotch (myc-scFvCD19-Gal4VP64-synNotch) into pHR vector containing UAS promoter and mCherry reporter20, and used to infect EpH4 cells. Stable receiver clones were selected by FACS based on mCherry expression after co-culture with CD19-expressing K562 cells20. To establish EpH4-pTRE3G AQP3-Myc/CD19/BFP cells, the cDNA encoding AQP3-Myc was amplified by PCR from pCMV-AQP3-Myc (OriGene, #MR203989) and was subcloned into the NheI site of pPB-TRE3G-MCS-CEH-rtTA3-IP. EpH4 CD19/BFP cells were then transfected with pPB-TRE3G-AQP3-Myc by nucleofection, followed by selection in the medium containing 5 μg ml-1 blasticidin. MDCK-pTRE3G Myc-RasV12 cells were established and cultured as previously described27. For all pTRE3G cell lines, gene expression was induced by 2 μg ml-1 doxycycline (Sigma-Aldrich).

The pETS-DD-tdTomato plasmid was generated by replacing the NF-κB enhancer element in the pNF-κB-DD-tdTomato vector (Takara, #631081) with synthetic oligonucleotides containing the ETS-binding site46,47. Specifically, the original NF-κB enhancer linked to the TA minimal promoter was excised by restriction digestion using NheI and HindIII. Subsequently, oligonucleotides carrying the ETS-binding sequence linked to the TA minimal promoter (Supplementary Table 1) were inserted into the corresponding restriction sites. EpH4 cells were then transfected with pETS-DD-tdTomato by nucleofection, followed by selection in the medium containing 800 μg ml-1 of G418 (Geneticin, Gibco).

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

For FACS, cells were suspended in phenol red-free DMEM (Wako, #040-30095) containing 5% FBS and 5 mM EDTA. Cells were then stained with 7-AAD (BioLegend, #420404), which labels dead cells, and filtered through mesh strainers (Falcon, #352235). The mCherry-negative and mCherry-positive receiver cells were isolated by FACS from the 7-AAD-negative/BFP-negative population using the SH800S cell sorter (SONY). Gating strategy for FACS isolation is demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 10.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

FACS-sorted cells were lysed, and total RNA was purified with the RNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, #74004) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis and library construction followed the SC3-seq protocol48,49. For each sample, 1 ng of total RNA was used for cDNA synthesis, and cDNA was amplified for 14 PCR cycles—the established cycle number for bulk RNA-seq48. Libraries were prepared from the amplified cDNA and sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 500/550 platform (High Output Kit v2, 75 cycles; Illumina, cat. no. FC-404-2005). Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38.p6) downloaded from the NCBI FTP site. Because the libraries capture the 3′ ends of transcripts, expression was quantified at the gene level (Entrez Gene IDs) rather than at the transcript level. Read trimming, alignment and quantification were performed with Cutadapt (v1.18), TopHat2 (v2.1.1) and HTSeq (v0.11.0), respectively. Bulk RNA-seq data were analysed in R (v4.3.2). Read counts were normalized to reads per million (RPM) and log2-transformed [log2(RPM + 1)]. We defined “all expressed genes” as those with log2(RPM + 1) > 4 in at least one sample48,49. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those exhibiting >2-fold differences between samples and log2(RPM + 1) > 2. The R script is available upon request.

For cell competition assay, EpH4-pTRE3G GFP, EpH4-pTRE3G GFP-RasV12, EpH4-pTRE3G cSrcY527F-GFP, EpH4-pTRE3G AQP3-Myc/BFP/CD19 or EpH4-pTRE3G Myc-RasV12 cells were mixed with EpH4 cells at the indicated ratio and plated onto collagen gel-coated coverslips in 12-well plates or 35-mm dishes, with a total of 3.5 × 105 or 7 × 105 cells, respectively. For immunofluorescence, cells were fixed with 1% PFA in PBS for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 15 min and blocked with 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 1 h. A total of 30 GFP-positive clusters, each containing 2-8 GFP-positive cells, were analysed. For AQP3-overexpressing cells, the same approach was applied by assessing BFP-positive cells. For analysis of apical extrusion of single-cultured RasV12, cSrcY527F or AQP3-overexpressing cells, unlabelled cells were co-cultured with CMTPX-labelled cells at a ratio of 50:1, and the apical extrusion ratio of CMTPX-labelled cells was examined. Primary antibodies were used at 1:500, and all secondary antibodies were used at 1:1000 for mouse tissues.

Cell size and height analysis

For the quantification of cell volume during cell competition, non-extruded GFP- or BFP-positive cells were randomly selected. Confocal microscopy z-stack images of the cells were acquired, with confocal sections collected at 1-μm intervals along the Z-axis. An approximate cell volume was calculated by multiplying the cell area in each plane by the interval (1 μm) and summing across all planes using the ImageJ software. Cell height was also measured using the same z-stack images. F-actin staining was used to define cell boundaries. For the quantification of the volume of PRKG2- or AQP3-overexpressing cells (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 4a), GFP- or Myc-positive cells were randomly selected, and cell volume was quantified as described above. For the quantification of cell volume in Hypo-osmotic treatment (Supplementary Fig. 7c), WT cells were randomly selected. For the quantification of cell area in the mouse tissues, non-extruded YFP-positive or GFP-positive (RasV12) cells were randomly selected. The cell area of YFP-positive, GFP-positive (RasV12) or directly contacting normal cells was quantified at the central plane of each cell, defined as the plane with the maximal nuclear cross-sectional area. E-cadherin staining was used to define cell boundaries.

Western blotting

SDS-PAGE gels were transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then incubated with blocking buffer (0.05% Tween20, 3% skim milk in Tris-buffered saline) followed by incubation with primary and secondary antibodies. Proteins were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescence. Primary and secondary antibodies were used at 1:1000. Western blotting data were analysed using ImageQuantTM LAS4010 (GE Healthcare).

Stretching assay

Stretching assay was performed using the STB-100 system (STREX) with polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) chambers (STREX, #STB-CH-04; 20 × 20 mm)51. PDMS membranes were coated with fibronectin (5 μg ml-1; Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C for 1 h prior to cell seeding. 5.0 × 10⁵ EpH4 cells were seeded onto an unstretched membrane and cultured for 24 h to grow to confluence. To apply mechanical stretch, one edge of the membrane was fixed, and the opposite edge was connected to a movable shaft. The membrane was extended from 20 mm to 22 mm to apply a uniaxial strain of 10%, which was maintained for the indicated time periods. Note that 10% stretching was selected because, under the co-culture condition, the height of RasV12 cells becomes 1.1- to 1.2-fold greater than that of the adjacent normal cells; accordingly, the cell-cell contact interface between normal and RasV12 cells is estimated to be elongated by approximately 10%.

For the analysis of Flipper-TR lifetime under hypo-osmotic treatment, EpH4 cells were cultured alone and, just prior to analysis, incubated in the M199 medium (Gibco, #11043023) supplemented with 1% penicillin–streptomycin and 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, #A2153). For Supplementary Fig. 7a, the lifetime of Flipper-TR in EpH4 cells was measured before and after hypo-osmotic treatment (1 ml of M199 medium + 2 ml of ddH₂O) for 10 min. For Fig. 7a, EpH4 cells were co-cultured with EpH4 or EpH4-pTRE3G Myc-RasV12 cells pre-stained with CMAC (blue) at a 3:1 ratio. After the monolayer formation, cells were incubated with doxycycline for 15 h, then the medium was replaced with the M199 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and doxycycline for 1 h, followed by Flipper-TR analysis. For quantification of W-W in the WT:RasV12 co-culture, Flipper-TR lifetime at cell-cell adhesions between WT cells at the first and second row from RasV12 cells was analysed. For Supplementary Fig. 7b, EpH4 cells were co-transfected with pPGK-BFP and pCMV-AQP3-Myc, and after 5 h, the medium was replaced with the M199 medium supplemented with 10% FBS for 1 h, followed by Flipper-TR analysis. For quantification of Flipper-TR lifetime at cell-cell junctions, regions of interest (ROIs) were manually selected at cell-cell contact sites from images showing plasma membrane-localised Flipper-TR signals using the ImageJ software.

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Invitrogen, #15596018) or RNeasy Micro Kit (QIAGEN, #74004), followed by reverse transcription with the QuantiTect Reverse Transcription Kit (QIAGEN, #205313). Quantitative PCR was performed using the GeneAce SYBR qPCR Mix (NIPPON GENE, #319-07683) on the StepOne system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Relative mRNA abundance of target genes was calculated using the ΔΔCt method, normalized to Gapdh mRNA expression. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism software. Depending on the experimental design, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test, or Chi-square test was used. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant and are indicated in the figures. All experiments were repeated at least three times. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized. The Investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
