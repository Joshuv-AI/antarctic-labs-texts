Contemporary evidence in protected percutaneous coronary intervention: a case-based review
Authors: Ovidio De Filippo, Jürgen Leick, Giuseppe Tarantini, Ee Ling Heng, Guido Tavazzi, Roberto Lorusso, Assad Haneya, Nikos Werner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-risk percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with markedly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) carries a substantial risk of haemodynamic collapse, creating an unmet need for strategies that allow complete, optimized revascularization without compromising safety. These cases often fall within the Class IIb–III indications of the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Chronic Coronary Syndromes and Revascularization Guidelines, which support PCI in patients with severe left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, complex or multivessel disease, and surgical ineligibility or refusal after Heart Team evaluation. For such individuals, protected PCI using percutaneous mechanical circulatory support (MCS), namely microaxial flow pumps (mAFP), may enable procedural completeness and improve outcomes. Herein, we present a case of a 66-year-old male that illustrates the expanding role of protected PCI in patients who meet Class IIb–III ESC Guidelines criteria, with severe LV dysfunction, multivessel or left main disease, and surgical refusal or ineligibility. Contemporary evidence supports the use of mAFP-assisted PCI to maintain forward flow and ventricular unloading during complex revascularization, thereby minimizing ischaemic and haemodynamic instability. Such an approach allows the operator to pursue meticulous lesion preparation, imaging-guided optimization, and bifurcation PCI techniques while preserving perfusion and procedural safety. In appropriately selected cases, Impella-supported high-risk PCI represents a safe and effective option. It enables complete, optimized revascularization and can promote early functional recovery, bridging a crucial therapeutic gap in contemporary revascularization practice.Keywords: High-risk PCI, Protected PCI, Haemodynamic support, Impella CP, IABP, VA-ECMO, Left main disease, Multivessel CAD, Reduced LVEF

Introduction

Patients with complex coronary artery disease (CAD) and severely reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) remain among the most challenging cohorts in contemporary cardiovascular practice.1 When coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is deemed prohibitive due to diffuse disease, frailty, or major comorbidities, these individuals fall within the Class IIb–III indications of the 2024 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines on Chronic Coronary Syndromes and Revascularization, which support percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients who refuse surgery or are surgically ineligible following Heart Team review.2 Such patients, often categorized as complex, high-risk in indicated patients, face elevated risks of peri-procedural hypotension and cardiogenic shock, ischaemia during prolonged balloon inflations or lesion modification, and incomplete revascularization when standard PCI strategies alone are applied.3 Consensus statements from European and North American societies increasingly recognize a structured role for percutaneous mechanical circulatory support (MCS) as part of a broader protected PCI strategy to mitigate haemodynamic hazards and enable procedural safety and completeness in carefully selected patients.1,3

Rationale for protected PCI

Protected PCI using percutaneous MCS, and especially microaxial flow pump (mAFP) devices, has emerged as an important option in this setting. Impella CPTM (Johnson & Johnson MedTech, Danvers, MA) provides continuous forward flow and left ventricular unloading, stabilizing coronary perfusion and systemic blood pressure during complex manoeuvres, thereby allowing operators to perform meticulous lesion preparation, intravascular imaging-guided optimization, and bifurcation stenting techniques with reduced risk of repeated ischaemic or haemodynamic instability-related episodes.4 Although the randomized PROTECT II trial in high-risk PCI did not demonstrate superiority of Impella over intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) for its primary endpoint, it showed haemodynamic advantages and provided foundational evidence that prophylactic support is feasible and safe in appropriately selected patients.5,6 Subsequent registry data and meta-analyses demonstrate improved procedural completeness and an acceptable risk profile when Impella is used for protected PCI in surgically ineligible patients who meet ESC Guidelines Class IIb–III indications.7,8

Defining the gap: who benefits and how much?

Despite growing adoption, uncertainties persist regarding which subsets of patients derive the greatest net clinical benefit from protected PCI and how the strategy should be executed across diverse anatomical and physiological risk profiles.1,9 For example, patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) are often undertreated due to concerns about contrast nephropathy and haemodynamic fragility. However, recent analyses suggest that, with contrast-sparing techniques and careful monitoring, outcomes in CKD can be acceptable, supporting patient inclusion rather than exclusion when benefits outweigh risks.10

Dedicated studies in older adults indicate that age alone should not be a barrier to protected PCI, provided goals of care are aligned and post-procedural recovery pathways are in place.11 Sex-specific analyses reveal meaningful differences in presentation, anatomy, and outcomes that should inform shared decision-making and consent.12 Right ventricular dysfunction (RVD) and significant mitral regurgitation (MR) pose further challenges for haemodynamic management and post-procedural recovery, and both have recently been characterized within protected PCI cohorts to guide monitoring and follow-up.13 ,14

In addition to coronary anatomical and patient-related criteria, the pre-procedural haemodynamic status of the patient must also be considered when deciding whether an mAFP would be beneficial. Baseline factors, such as severely reduced LVEF, decreased mean arterial blood pressure (MAP), and significantly increased left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP), all increase the risk of peri-interventional complications. However, there are currently no standardized haemodynamic criteria in elective cases to guide interventional cardiologists’ clinical decision-making.15 LVEDP may play a pivotal role as it reflects left ventricular wall tension and, when elevated, can signal impending haemodynamic instability, myocardial dysfunction, or the onset of cardiogenic shock. Elevated LVEDP has been linked to progressive myocardial ischaemia, acute kidney injury (AKI) or renal failure, and reductions in both MAP and cardiac output (CO). A study of more than 49 000 patients demonstrated that an LVEDP of ≥26 mmHg led to a 10.4% higher in-hospital mortality rate.16 In this context, a pilot study demonstrated that the use of Impella CP in elective protected PCI significantly reduced LVEDP levels compared with non-protected PCI.17 Nevertheless, LVEDP measurement is typically confined to the catheterization laboratory and thus is not readily available in elective settings. In everyday practice, echocardiographic surrogates, such as markedly reduced LVEF, significant MR, or evidence of impaired ventricular compliance, may serve as practical indicators of patients who could benefit from pre-emptive haemodynamic support.

Procedural completeness and safety balance

Completeness of revascularization remains a central determinant of symptomatic and functional recovery after PCI in patients with low LVEF, yet it must be balanced against peri-procedural risk.18 Multicentre registry data highlight the association between more complete revascularization and improved outcomes in Impella-supported cases, reinforcing the rationale for support when aiming for complex multivessel or left main strategies that require prolonged ischaemic time or advanced lesion modification.19,20 Still, the field lacks large, pragmatic randomized trials directly comparing protected PCI with unsupported optimized PCI in well-defined elective high-risk populations, leaving open questions about absolute risk reductions in hard endpoints and cost-effectiveness across subgroups.1

Technique and haemodynamic monitoring

Consistent outcomes require standardized technique. Adoption of ultrasound-guided large-bore femoral access, pre-closure strategies, and hybrid radial-femoral approaches have minimized vascular complications.1 Haemodynamic and echocardiographic monitoring are crucial throughout the procedure. In addition to ultrasound used to ensure correct pump position, invasive arterial line monitoring is required to assess patient-device interaction. If available, use of a pulmonary artery catheter (Swan–Ganz) is strongly suggested as it can provide continuous guidance for assessing haemodynamics at baseline and post-pump initiation, as well as guide titration of vasoactive therapy if needed.3 Moreover, device-specific telemetry with Impella SmartAssist allows real-time insights into purge function, motor current and pump position within the ventricle. However, standardized alarm-driven pathways and validated weaning criteria are still evolving and merit consensus definition.4 As such, emerging physiologic endpoints, including LVEDP dynamics during PCI and MAP profiles under support, may help personalize weaning and post-procedural care but require broader validation in prospective studies.17,21

Imaging and lesion preparation

Protected PCI enables complete anatomic optimization. Among the advantages of protected PCI is the freedom to perform thorough lesion preparation and imaging-guided stent optimization without recurrent hypotension.4 Associations between pre-procedural anatomic complexity (e.g. SYNTAX score) and outcomes in supported PCI underpin the emphasis on intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) or optical coherence tomography (OCT) to optimize stent expansion and minimize residual stenosis, particularly in diffuse in-stent restenosis, calcified segments, and bifurcations.22 Specialized techniques, such as cutting or non-compliant balloon preparation, atherectomy, and intravascular lithotripsy (IVL), may be deployed more confidently under haemodynamic support, with complex bifurcation strategies executed to achieve durable results when anatomically indicated.20

Safety and haemolysis surveillance

Although overall safety is favourable, vigilance remains essential in protected PCI with percutaneous MCS. Structured anticoagulation and haemolysis surveillance—tracking lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), haptoglobin, and plasma-free haemoglobin—are central to safe device use.4 Contemporary series have not shown a consistent excess of AKI with Impella-assisted high-risk PCI when contrast use is carefully managed, but vigilance remains essential. Haemolysis is a recognized device-related complication, typically associated with prolonged support, higher pump speeds, device malposition/suction events, or low preload, and should, be explicitly monitored (plasma-free haemoglobin, LDH, haptoglobin,) with prompt correction (repositioning, P-level reduction, volume optimization, purge troubleshooting). Early detection of suction events and standardized purge management help mitigate risk. Practical strategies for large-bore access and bleeding control are available and should be incorporated into institutional protocols.23,24

Patient-centred outcomes and recovery

The primary aim of protected PCI is to secure a haemodynamically stable and safe procedural course, reducing intra- and peri-procedural deterioration and allowing operators to complete and optimize revascularization. Functional improvement is an important downstream objective, contingent on the extent and quality of revascularization and subsequent medical therapy, and may not be immediate in all patients. The RESTORE-EF registry and subsequent analyses show meaningful post-PCI increases in LVEF and quality of life metrics, supporting the premise that haemodynamic stability enables optimized revascularization and myocardial recovery.23,24 Integration of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) for heart failure remains essential to consolidate long-term benefit.25

Health-system considerations and equity

Protected PCI requires systems-based approach that includes institutional readiness and multidisciplinary team involvement. Successful programmes depend on a trained multidisciplinary Heart Team, catheterization laboratories with large-bore access capability, intensive care unit (ICU)-level post-procedural monitoring, and established escalation and de-escalation pathways.3 Programmatic elements, such as proctoring, simulation training, and complication-response drills, likely contribute to the improved outcomes observed in contemporary datasets, yet their components and thresholds for competency have not been uniformly defined.7

Equitable access is another concern. Ensuring that women, the elderly, and patients with CKD or RVD are neither over- nor under-selected demands transparent criteria and shared decision-making frameworks that incorporate the latest subgroup evidence.10-13 Thus, the multidisciplinary Heart Team should include interventional cardiology, cardiac surgery, a heart failure specialist, anaesthesia/critical care, and imaging experts to support decision-making regarding protected PCI candidacy in patients with severely reduced LVEF, complex coronary anatomy, and frailty. The Heart Team remit is to weigh surgical feasibility, anatomical/physiological risk (including anatomic burden, RVD/MR, frailty, renal risk), patient preference, institutional capabilities for large-bore access support, and ICU care. In elective high-risk scenarios, consensus should explicitly address access planning, targets for completeness, concerns about futility, and the post-procedual pathway, including weaning, monitoring, and staged revascularization if needed. This structured process underpins consistent, patient-centred decisions and aligns with contemporary guidance.

What this review adds

This case-based review integrates clinical rationale, procedural principles, and system-level insights to inform contemporary protected PCI practice (Graphical Abstract). It defines patient selection in the context of surgical ineligibility, outlines procedural planning, including a hybrid radial–femoral access strategy, and proposes monitoring and weaning pathways informed by invasive haemodynamics and SmartAssist telemetry.1,3,4 The review highlights special populations, including patients with CKD, MR, RVD, advanced age, and female sex, for whom growing evidence can refine consent and follow-up, and underscores the importance of aiming for optimized revascularization when the risk profile favours support.9-14,18,19 Finally, it articulates priorities for future research, including pragmatic randomized comparisons with unsupported optimized PCI, standardized outcome measures that incorporate patient-reported metrics, validation of physiologic markers to guide weaning, and cost-effectiveness analyses across diverse health-system contexts.1,5,17,21

Case presentation

Patient characteristics, cardiac history, and surgical risk

A 66-year-old male with a long-standing smoking history of ∼5 cigarettes/day for 45 years and dyslipidaemia presented with recurrent ventricular arrhythmias on the in the setting of ischaemic dilated cardiomyopathy. His medical history was notable for coeliac disease and low-normal nutritional status (body mass index of 21.6 kg/m²). He reported New York Heart Association (NYHA) Class II symptoms—exertional dyspnoea without orthopnea or syncope—and was maintained on bisoprolol, amiodarone, furosemide, clopidogrel, rosuvastatin/ezetimibe, plus optimized GDMT.

The patient sustained inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in 2003, treated with thrombolysis and PCI of the right coronary artery (RCA). In 2004, he experienced a new STEMI with RCA in-stent restenosis (ISR), managed with plain balloon angioplasty and drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation in the obtuse marginal (OM) branch. In 2015, coronary angiography revealed critical left main (LM) and multivessel CAD with a severely reduced LVEF (∼25%). He underwent PCI with DES placement from the LM to the left anterior descending (LAD) and left circumflex (LCx) arteries and received a single-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) for ischaemic cardiomyopathy. Between 2022 and 2025, the patient experienced multiple ICD therapies for ventricular tachycardia (VT) and ventricular fibrillation (VF), including shocks, indicating a progressive arrhythmic burden. In 2025, due to increasing arrhythmia complexity, the device was upgraded to a dual-chamber ICD. Despite maximal medical therapy, ischaemic dilated cardiomyopathy persisted, with recurrent VT and LVEF of 34%.

Baseline cardiovascular assessment

The patient was referred for reassessment of coronary anatomy and stent patency before consideration of advanced ventricular arrhythmia management, such as percutaneous ablation. The most recent echocardiography (ECG) confirmed a dilated left ventricle (end-diastolic volume 60 mL/m²), LVEF of 34%, regional wall motion abnormalities consistent with a posterolateral scar, and mildly impaired right ventricular function (TAPSE of 11 mm). There was moderate functional MR, mild tricuspid regurgitation (TR), and mild pulmonary hypertension (PAP of ∼30 mmHg). Additionally, the ECG showed sinus rhythm with QRS duration of 160 ms and QTc of 475 ms. Laboratory results indicated preserved renal function (creatinine of 0.91 mg/dL), normal haemoglobin (13.8 g/dL), and elevated total cholesterol (180 mg/dL).

Baseline coronary angiography revealed chronic total in-stent occlusion of the RCA and OM branch, severe ISR (99%) of the ostial LCx, and diffuse ISR of the mid-distal LAD involving large septal and diagonal bifurcations (Figure 1). No right heart catheterization was performed. The Heart Team reviewed the case and considered surgical revascularization unsuitable owing to prior multivessel PCI, advanced ischaemic cardiomyopathy, frailty, and comorbidity burden. According to the 2024 ESC Guidelines, this corresponded to a Class IIb–III indication for PCI in patients who refuse surgery or are surgically ineligible.

Clinical decision and rationale

The patient expressed a clear preference for less invasive management aimed at symptom relief and arrhythmia stabilization. Although myocardial viability in the posterolateral scar was absent, the presence of viable myocardium in other territories justified a revascularization attempt. The Heart Team concluded that a protected PCI strategy using Impella CP support could safely enable complex LM–LAD–LCx intervention while minimizing haemodynamic collapse risk. This decision reflected a guideline-consistent, patient-centred approach that balanced procedural benefit, anatomical complexity, and surgical inoperability.

Intra-procedural course

An Impella CP device was successfully placed via the right iliac artery despite navigation challenges related to tortuosity, consistent with consensus recommendations to de-risk complex optimization in patients with low LVEF.1,3 The procedure proceeded a under local anaesthesia with anaesthesiology assistance, minimal sedation, no intravenous opioids, and oxygenation provided via nasal cannula. Peri-procedural anticoagulation was achieved with unfractionated heparin targeting an activated clotting time of ∼250–300 s, with the Impella purge maintained using a heparinized solution per protocol. Cangrelor was initiated as the second antiplatelet agent at the start of PCI, with a planned transition to oral therapy post-procedure. Vasopressor support was limited to intermittent vasopressor low-dose boluses during brief episodes of severe hypotension. No continuous vasopressor or inotrope infusions were required.

During the index procedure, the RCA was deemed a chronic total occlusion (CTO) and was not treated. The LAD had diffuse ISR of the mid-distal segment. Lesions were prepared with cutting balloon and non-compliant balloon pre-dilatation. Definitive treatment was achieved with implantation of an ultrathin DES (Figure 2A).26 The LCx had severe ISR (99%) at the ostium. Pre-dilatation of the ostium was followed by re-implantation of a DES. A TAP technique was performed with the LAD, finalized by a kissing balloon inflation (Figure 2B).27

Final PCI result with Impella CP. (A) Final PCI result of the left anterior descending artery and (B) the ostial left circumflex artery with Impella CP in place. PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention.

Next, planned CTO-PCI of the OM branch was attempted using an antegrade wire-escalation strategy. Multiple high tip-load guidewires supported by a microcatheter were trialed. Ultimately, a Gaia Third wire penetrated the proximal cap but caused perforation of a distal side branch, with minimal contrast extravasation. Prolonged intra-procedural echocardiographic monitoring confirmed no pericardial effusion. Given the procedure duration and this complication, the planned CTO PCI of the OM branch was deferred. However, this event provides further insight into the usefulness of upfront Impella positioning when complex PCI is being performed. Indeed, the device may provide the required haemodynamic support that enables physicians to manage such complications safely and effectively. On the other hand, observational data suggest that bailout positioning of percutaneous MCS is associated with a higher risk of in-hospital death, the occurrence of the slow reflow phenomenon, and all-cause mortality during follow-up.28

Here, Impella CP provided stable forward-flow support throughout the procedure. Several episodes of sustained intra-procedural hypotension were observed during the procedure. No malignant ventricular arrhythmia occurred once support was established. Moreover, severe iliac tortuosity complicated large-bore femoral access but was successfully managed via a pre-implanted single suture-based closure device. Radial access with a 7 Fr EBU catheter facilitated complex two-vessel revascularization under Impella support. The procedure involved treatment of multiple coronary bifurcation lesions requiring prolonged balloon inflation, as well as management of ISR lesions necessitating intensive lesion pretreatment.

Impella management

Due to the intermittent episodes of severe hypotension leading to peri-procedural haemodynamic instability, the Impella CP (Figure 3) was kept in place at the end of the procedure for haemodynamic support and as a bridge to stepwise weaning. An initial post-procedural myocardial stunning phase was observed on echocardiography, followed by progressive recovery in LVEF and clinical stability under GDMT. Continuous arterial pressure monitoring and comprehensive haemodynamic monitoring with a Swan-Ganz catheter inserted upon ICU admission guided Impella support P level adjustments and vasoactive therapy.3 In addition, Impella SmartAssist parameters, such as flow, motor current, and placement signal, were tracked alongside lactate, urine output, and haemolysis labs (LDH and haptoglobin).4

Haemodynamic monitoring with Impella SmartAssist. (A) Impella console snapshot captures loss of pulsatility on the Impella console, with low differential pressure and a blunted waveform. (B) Placement-signal time series shows the corresponding placement-signal waveform/trend (raw and smoothed), highlighting reduced pulsatile amplitude. (C) SmartAssist log export that shows the corresponding SmartAssist trends—decrease in placement-signal amplitude, flow variation, and motor current/speed modulation—consistent with device–patient interaction during titration.

Levosimendan was administered to facilitate device weaning (no bolus; continuous infusion at ∼0.1 µg/kg/min for 24 h). After stabilization of perfusion indices (MAP of ≥65–70 mmHg without vasopressors, CI of ≥2.2 L/min/m², lactate clearance, no suction alarms), Impella P-levels were reduced stepwise with close SmartAssist surveillance and invasive haemodynamic guidance (Figure 4). This involved gradual reduction in P-levels (2 P-levels at each step every 4–6 h) with haemodynamic monitoring-directed titration.3,4 On the day of explantation, transthoracic echocardiography showed overall improved cardiac function: LVEF of ∼38–40%, TAPSE of ∼14 mm, LV EDVi of ∼58 mL/m², easing of mild/moderate MR, mild TR, and echo-estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure of ∼28–30 mmHg.13,14 According to the haemodynamic and echocardiographic goals, the device was explanted. Vascular closure and limb perfusion were reassessed, and bleeding-risk mitigation strategies consistent with current Impella programme standards were implemented.29

Follow-up and outcomes

Following Day 1 in the ICU, no recurrent sustained arrhythmias or haemodynamic instability were observed. Moreover, renal function remained stable, and no major access-site complications were reported. After initial ventricular stunning, LVEF improved up to 40% prior to discharge,23 consistent with protected PCI cohorts reporting post-procedural LVEF recovery.7 In-hospital LVEF improvement can be a favourable signal of myocardial recovery and has been linked to better longer-term outcomes in several series;26,27 however, the relationship is associative and may reflect resolution of stunning and selection factors. The patient was discharged on optimized GDMT, including a beta-blocker, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system/angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor as tolerated, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor, statin/ezetimibe, proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 inhibitor, and dual antiplatelet therapy. A 90-day follow-up visit was scheduled to reassess LVEF, NYHA functional class, and therapy adherence, with consideration of staged CTO treatment based on symptoms and ischaemia testing.18,19

Discussion

Protected PCI aims to balance procedural completeness with haemodynamic safety in patients with severe LV dysfunction and complex coronary anatomy who are unsuitable for surgery or at high surgical risk.9 Contemporary European and North American statements endorse Impella-assisted PCI as a reasonable option when delivered by experienced teams within structured institutional frameworks.1,3 Mechanistically, active forward flow with LV unloading sustains coronary perfusion and systemic pressure during prolonged balloon inflations, myocardial ischaemia, lesion modification, intravascular imaging, and bifurcation stenting.4 Randomized evidence from PROTECT II, while neutral for the composite primary endpoint, demonstrated superior intra-procedural haemodynamics and the established feasibility and safety of prophylactic support in elective high-risk PCI compared with IABP support.5,6 Subsequent registries, including PROTECT III, have shown improved outcomes with better patient selection, standardized access, anticoagulation protocols, and imaging-guided optimization, supporting the approach applied in this case.7

Mapping evidence to the patient

This 66-year-old patient with ischaemic cardiomyopathy and multivessel ISR represents the typical high-risk percutaneous interventional procedure (CHIP) candidate for whom protected PCI offers a means to achieve complete revascularization when CABG is contraindicated or high-risk. Moreover, while mild right ventricular impairment and moderate functional MR added haemodynamic complexity, subgroup analyses from contemporary protected PCI registries indicate that such features are compatible with favourable outcomes when monitored appropriately.13,14

Role and rationale for mechanical support

Completeness of revascularization strongly correlates with symptomatic and functional recovery in patients with low LVEF and CAD, but it is often associated with longer ischaemic times, extensive lesion preparation, and bifurcation strategies that increase the risk of further cardiac contractile compromise and cardio-circulatory collapse.18 As such, registry data demonstrate improved outcomes with more complete revascularization using percutaneous MCS, particularly in anatomically complex cases.19,20 In this patient, diffuse ISR of the mid-distal LAD was treated after cutting and non-compliant balloon preparation, followed by implantation of an ultrathin DES, while severe ostial LCx ISR was managed with a TAP strategy and final kissing balloon inflation. These techniques, performed safely under stable Impella support, reflect the procedural freedom afforded by protected PCI.22,26,27

Access and timing: prophylaxis over bailout

Given the severely reduced LVEF and anticipated procedural complexity, prophylactic rather than bailout Impella implantation was chosen in this case. Despite marked tortuosity of the right iliac artery, large-bore access was achieved safely, and PCI proceeded via the right radial approach using a 7 Fr EBU guide. Observational data favours prophylactic MCS in elective high-risk PCI over bailout deployment, as early unloading mitigates ischaemic and pressure-related deterioration that might otherwise preclude full optimization.30,31

Monitoring and weaning

In the absence of formal guidelines, several expert opinions suggest the use of haemodynamic monitoring to guide percutaneous MCS allocation and management to achieve enhanced outcomes.32,33 Device-level telemetry, such as SmartAssist, provides real-time insights into machine performance (e.g. purge signals), patient-device interactions, and functional variations (e.g. positioning), complementing invasive metrics and guiding timely troubleshooting. In addition, emerging physiologic markers, such as dynamic LVEDP changes during high-risk PCI, may, among other parameters, help inform decisions on temporary device removal in the catheterization laboratory, although definitive proof is still lacking.4,17,21 There are indications in the literature that patients with a baseline LVEDP >19 mmHg may benefit from Impella insertion during pre-procedural planning.17

Although standardized weaning protocols for mAFP are lacking, general principles have been proposed.34 These principles emphasize minimizing support duration and ensuring haemodynamic and organ function stability prior to initiating a weaning trial, ideally with minimal or no vasoactive support. A stepwise reduction in support should be accompanied by comprehensive multiparametric evaluations In addition, combining invasive and noninvasive assessments at each step. Potentially informative parameters include, monitoring of with systolic blood pressure and MAP, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), pulmonary artery pulsatility index, and right arterial pressure, together with ventricular performance, valve function, and interventricular septal dynamics, although all require validation.

Clinical course and alignment with evidence

In the current case, haemodynamic support was maintained for three days post-procedure due to transient episodes of severe hypotension. Following an initial myocardial stunning phase, LVEF improved before discharge on optimized GDMT. This pattern parallels findings from RESTORE-EF and other contemporary studies demonstrating significant post-PCI recovery in LVEF and functional class among patients treated with protected strategies, particularly those requiring by a prolonged support after the planned procedure. Early in-hospital improvement in LVEF is a recognized indicator of favourable long-term outcomes. Moreover, meta-analyses suggest that, in patients with severely reduced LVEF, Impella-supported PCI achieves survival comparable to or better than surgical or unsupported PCI, though randomized validation is still needed.23-25

Programmatic and guideline implications

This case reinforces key institutional prerequisites for protected PCI, including Heart Team selection, operator proficiency in large-bore access, hybrid radial–femoral planning, standardized ICU monitoring, and weaning protocols. Predictors of recovery, such as baseline substrate, revascularization extent, and peri-procedural haemodynamics, should guide counselling and procedural planning.35 As emphasized by ESC and European Association of Percutaneous Cardiovascular Interventions (EAPCI) consensus documents, Impella use must reflect both clinical indication and operator readiness rather than device availability alone.33 Equitable access remains a broader consideration. Women, older adults, and patients with CKD or RV dysfunction are frequently underrepresented in studies; nonetheless, emerging data suggests that, when carefully selected, these patients may experience meaningful benefit. As such, this case underscores how protected PCI, integrated within multidisciplinary decision-making, can safely achieve complete revascularization, symptom relief, and early LV recovery in surgically ineligible patients with advanced ischaemic cardiomyopathy.
