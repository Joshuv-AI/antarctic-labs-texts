Corrections to the standard retrieval of total ozone column with a Dobson spectrophotometer operated at Belsk, Poland, since 1963
Authors: J. W. Krzyścin, B. Rajewska-Więch, J. Jarosławski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Monitoring of total ozone column (TOC) were carried out at Belsk (51°50′N, 20°47′E), Poland, using a Dobson spectrophotometer (DOB) for the period 1963–2024. Various combinations of DOB settings are applied in the TOC monitoring, including double wavelength pairs (AD or CD) and observation types: direct sun (DS), zenith blue (ZB), or zenith cloudy (ZC). Current DOB TOC retrieval uses the Bass-Paur (BP) ozone absorption cross-section (OACS), which is fixed at a temperature of − 46.3 °C. Recently, the Absorption Cross Sections of Ozone committee recommended changing from the BP OACS to the temperature-dependent Serdyuchenko-Gorshelev (SG) OACS. This paper incorporates the SG OACS into the DS TOC standard calculation, which is based on the Beer-Lambert law, and into the non-DS TOC retrieval, using an empirical approach to adjust TOC values obtained from the attenuation of zenith solar irradiance to the most reliable DS-AD TOC values. An empirical correction function is also proposed to reduce the effect of stray light (SL) on DOB TOC values. Using the SG OACS and SL correction reduces the winter-summer bias relative to the Brewer spectrophotometer to ± 0.5% for DS measurements. The difference between DOB and reanalysis/satellite TOC values decreases by approximately 2% in winter.

Introduction

Total ozone column (TOC) measurements using the Dobson spectrophotometers (DOBs) have been carried out for almost 100 years. First developed in the 1920 s, the DOB uses manual measurements of ultraviolet (UV) solar radiation intensity at two wavelengths, showing strong and weak ozone absorption, to infer total ozone column (TOC) values. The first TOC measurement, currently archived at the World Ozone and Ultraviolet Radiation Data Centre (WOUDC) in Toronto, Canada, was taken on 18 August 1924 in Oxford, GBR. Since then, approximately 200 stations have archived daily TOC values from DOB measurements at the WOUDC. One of these stations is Belsk, Poland (20.78°E, 51.84°N), where DOB84 began operating on 23 March 1963. The data from Belsk have undergone the strict QAQC procedures described in detail by Krzyścin et al.1.

The ozone absorption cross section (OACS) spectrum in the ultraviolet (UV) range is used to retrieve TOC with DOB. Many attempts have been made to update the OACS values using increasingly advanced laboratory techniques. Currently, WOUDC uses the OACS values calculated according to Bass-Paur (BP) measurements2, with an effective temperature (Teff) of − 46.3 °C applied year-round. Teff is the air temperature weighted by the vertical profile of ozone density. Kerr et al.3 observed that the DOB tends to underestimate TOC in winter when Teff is set to − 46.3 °C, which is much higher than the actual winter temperature. The BP coefficients were adopted as the WOUDC standard on 1 January 1992, with past WOUDC ozone data being recalculated to incorporate the new OACS values4.

The monitoring of TOC required many time-consuming manual steps, which limited the number of measurements that could be performed in a day. This means that the TOC value obtained may depend to some extent on the individual skills of the DOB operator. The need to automate DOB measurements resulted in the development of a fully automated instrument - the Brewer spectrophotometer (BRE). It was designed in Canada, and after improvements in the 1970s5,6 it was ready to participate in the global TOC monitoring. However, seasonal differences between the DOB and BRE measurements were found especially in winter with higher BRE measurements of approximately a few percent (e.g. Scarnato et al.7, Vanicek et al.8). This has also been confirmed in comparisons between DOB and satellite measurements9.

The need to reduce such differences prompted research into applying temperature-dependent OACS to TOC retrievals. Following the testing of various OACS sets and the use of Teff from the Tropospheric Emission Monitoring Service (TEMIS) retrieval, https://www.temis.nl/climate/efftemp/overpass.php, Redondas et al.10 and Grobner et al.11 recommended the use of the OACS proposed by Serdyuchenko et al.12 and Gorshelev et al.13, which is henceforth denoted as SG14. The Absorption Cross Section of Ozone Committee has finally recommended SG14 for use by the WOUDC network14. Voglmeier et al.15 presented information on the conversion of old (BP) WOUDC data using new temperature-dependent SG14 OACS data, which can be used to automatically update the entire WOUDC DOB network. To the authors’ knowledge, work on recalculating TOC for the DOB network is currently underway within WOUDC (https://www.bipm.org/documents/d/guest/rapportbipm2024-03).

The conversion from BP to SG14 for the DOB measurements, as proposed by Voglmeier et al.15, is formally correct for TOC values derived only from direct sun (DS) measurements for the AD and CD wavelength pairs. All-sky TOC measurements are especially needed for sites with frequent cloudiness throughout the day, such as Belsk, particularly during periods of the year with short daylight hours, when there is little opportunity for making DS observations with the highest possible accuracy16,17. It is also possible to calculate TOC using diffusive radiance coming from the zenith while the sky is blue (ZB) or cloudy (ZC). In these cases, the TOC retrieval is not as straightforward as that for the DS retrieval. For example, Rindert18 proposed the ZB&ZC retrieval, which is used to calculate TOC value from measurements of the zenith irradiance. This retrieval method was used when DS measurements were not possible at Belsk19, and it requires updating in line with the OACS change.

The objective of this paper is to incorporate the SG14 OACS into the current all-sky TOC retrieval method based on the BP OACS (see the Methods section). The new SG14 TOC values from all intraday measurements at Belsk between 23 March 1963 and 31 December 2024 have been recalculated, and the improvements to DOB performance resulting from this replacement are demonstrated (the Result section) by examining intraday, daily and seasonal (winter and summer) TOC datasets. Due to the many gaps in the daily DOB TOC series, particularly before 2000, alternative TOC sources to fill these gaps are presented in the Result section, taking various reanalyses and satellite observations into account. As stray light in the DOB leads to underestimation of TOC values at low solar elevation, an empirical approach is proposed to estimate the strength of this effect in the Belsk DOB with the DS setting. Finally, an empirical stray light correction function is proposed for use with all-sky SG14 TOC. The Discussion and Conclusion sections summarise the advantages of using the new SG14 DOB TOC values, and consider how the Belsk results can be generalised to other mid-latitude stations.

Methods

TOC measurements at Belsk

The Central Geophysical Laboratory of the Institute of Geophysics, Polish Academy of Sciences (IGF PAS), at Belsk (51.84 °N, 20.78 °E), Poland, has been using DOB84 since 23 March 1963. In Europe, longer time series of TOC are taken in Arosa since 192620 and Hradec Kralove since 196121. The Belsk’s TOC measurements have been carried out in all-sky conditions, with the exception of days where there was continuous rain or snowfall. Various efforts have been made to ensure the quality of Belsk’s data1,22–24.

The uniqueness of the TOC measurements at Belsk comes from their high frequency comprising all possible type of measurements including DS, ZB, and ZC measurements made with the AD (305.5 nm|325.0 nm and 317.5|339.9 nm) and CD (311.5 nm|332.4 nm and 317.5 nm|339.9 nm) double wavelength setup.

In total, 137,730 intraday manual TOC observations were made by 31 December 2024. Since start of the DOB84 measurements, the number of observations has been increased on selected days to determine the differences in TOC values obtained using different combinations of DOB84 settings, changing wavelength pairs (AD or CD) and observation types (DS, ZB or ZC) for adjacent observations. The DOB84 observation staff were informed about the importance of such comparison campaigns in assessing the accuracy of TOC measurements. On days when weather conditions were variable (clear skies alternating with cloud cover), more DOB84 measurements were taken during the day (up to 50) to enable almost simultaneous observations with different instrument settings. The number of such campaigns has increased in recent years (2000–2024).

DS-AD observations yield the most accurate TOC values16,17. Such observations are recommended when ozone air mass (µ) is smaller than 2.5. However, the yearly course of µ gives these observations not possible for mid- and high latitudinal sites during the late autumn–winter period. For example, at Belsk, the noon air masses above 2.5 appear in the period from 3 November up to 9 February and the yearly maximum air mass at noon on 23 December is 3.75. Therefore, DS-CD observations are recommended for these periods. If DS observations are not possible due to clouds obscuring the solar disc, the only alternative is less accurate ZB and ZC observations. In cases, with the clean zenith and its surroundings, ZB-AD/CD observations can be made. Two categories of ZC observations are considered, i.e. ZCs (with small and medium cloud opacity) and ZCl (with highly variable or large cloud opacity).

As shown in Table 1, DS observations (DS-AD and DS-CD) were the most frequent, accounting for approximately 42% of all observations. Due to the lower number of sunny days, ZC observations were most common in winter, accounting for around 54% of the total. These observations were less frequent in spring and summer, accounting for around 34% and 29% of total observations, respectively. ZB observations were taken at a comparable frequency of around 22% throughout the year. ZB-CD (approximately 15%) and ZCl-CD (approximately 35%) only dominated in winter, as CD observations are preferable under low solar elevation with µ > 2.5. It is also worth noting the high frequency of ZCl-AD observations (around 20–25%) in spring, summer, and autumn.

These percentages were influenced not only by the local meteorological and geographical conditions at Belsk (i.e. local cloudiness and day length), but also by the observational policy at the IGF PAS. This policy involved taking a variety of observations throughout the day to evaluate uncertainty in the DOB84 measurements under non-perfect conditions, particularly in winter. Throughout the entire period of the DOB84 measurements, this policy changed in line with manpower availability.

Since July 1992, TOC monitoring at Belsk has been extended to include collocated, fully automated measurements taken by a Brewer Instrument #64 (BRE64) Mark II with a single monochromator24. This instrument measures solar irradiance at six wavelengths in the UV range, from 303.2 to 320.1 nm. The four longest wavelengths are used for TOC retrieval5,6. Between 2010 and 2013, BRE Mark III (with double monochromator) with serial numbers 207 (BRE207) operated simultaneously at Belsk. Subsequently, the BRE207 was moved to Warsaw to monitor the TOC and UV spectrum25. The BRE calculates TOC value averaging five almost simultaneous (every 20–30 s) measurements. Here, we consider only reliable TOC data obtained with DS setting and standard deviation of TOC from these five measurements less than 2.5 DU. It appears that BRE retrieval is only slightly sensitive to effective temperature, so the assumption of using a fixed Teff of − 45 °C for OACS can still be applied to the Brewer network15.

The long-term stability of DOB84 has been supported by frequent intercomparisons with world standard instruments, almost every four years between 1974 and 1995, and afterwards with the European standard. The most recent calibration took place in 2019. Krzyścin et al.1 described the technical details of the QA/QC procedures applied to Belsk’s DOB84 observations. Calibrations of BRE64 measurements have been carried out regularly (at least every two years) against the international travel reference instrument provided by International Ozone Services Inc. (https://www.io3.ca/Calibrations).

Both DOB84 and BRE64 are susceptible to the influence of stray light, which affects the intensity of shorter wavelength radiation, as the optics do not completely filter out longer wavelengths. This leads to an underestimation of the TOC values, particularly in cases involving high slant-path TOC exceeding 1000 DU. Savastiouk et al.26 developed a physical correction for scattered light in BRE, which will be implemented for BRE64 measurements taken between 2023 and 2024, i.e. since the most recent BRE64 calibration. As the BRE207 instrument contains a double monochromator, the effect of stray light was negligible. TOC data from BRE64, with stray light correction applied, and from BRE207 will be used for further comparison with simultaneous DOB64 measurements. This will validate the proposed empirical method of stray light correction, which will then be applied to all-sky DOB64 measurements.

Other TOC data. We compare daily TOC values collected at Belsk with the corresponding reanalysis data obtained from the 5th generation of European Reanalysis (ERA5), Modern-Era Retrospective analysis for Research and Applications, ver.2 (MERRA-2), and Multi-sensor reanalysis, ver.2 (MSR-2). Additionally, we include daily TOC measurements made by the Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI) aboard the Aura satellite from 10 October 2004 to the present.

The ERA5 dataset is provided by the Copernicus Climate Change Service of the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF). ERA5 uses advanced physical models and data assimilation to extend results of historical measurements into a regular global grid with a latitude/longitude of 0.25°×0.25°. Since its release in 2019, ERA5 ozone data has been used in numerous global–scale TOC analyses27–29. The most recent version of the ERA5 data, “ERA5 hourly data on single levels from 1940 to the present” (10.24381/cds.adbb2d47) has replaced the previous version, ERA-Interim. This study uses this version, and the TOC values at 11:00 a.m. (i.e., around noon local time) for the grid point (20.75 °E, 51.75 °N) closest to the station location were used for comparison with the TOC values measured at Belsk.

MERRA-2 is provided by the Global Modeling and Assimilation Office of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA). Among many other meteorological and chemical variables it comprises the hourly mean TOC on the global grid with the resolution of 0.625° (longitude) x 0.5° (latitude) from 1 January 1980 up to now, https://giovanni.gsfc.nasa.gov/giovanni/#service=TmAvMp&starttime=&endtime=&dataKeyword=M2T1NXSLV%20total%20column%20ozone. The quality of MERRA-2 ozone were supported by numerous comparisons with satellite and ground-based observations30–32. TOC values at Belsk were selected as the hourly mean at 11 am from the closest grid point (20.625 °E, 51.5 °N).

The multi-sensor reanalysis (MSR) dataset, which was published by the Royal Netherlands Meteorological Institute (KNMI), was created using the methodology proposed by van der Allaart et al.33. The MSR data include TOC observations made with various ultraviolet-visible (UV-VIS) satellite instruments. These results have been corrected for instrumental drift, as determined by comparison with the WOUDC ground-based network. The MSR ver. 2 (MSR-2) database was created using the algorithm developed by van der A et al.34. The usefulness of these data for trend analysis has been confirmed in several publications35–37. The MSR-2 dataset currently contains daily TOC values from 1 January 1960 up to the present day, in a grid with a resolution of 1° x 1°. However, the quality of ozone data prior to 1979 (i.e. before the era of satellite observations) requires further verification. The time series of TOC values in Belsk at noon local time was taken from TEMIS station overpass data, which is available at https://www.temis.nl/uvradiation/UVarchive/stations_uv_msr2.php.

OMI aboard the NASA Aura spacecraft is a nadir viewing spectrometer measuring (since October 2004) the solar radiation backscattered by the atmosphere and surface. OMI provides complete daily global coverage. The OMI data are gridded at 0.25 × 0.25 degrees and archived at https://acdisc.gsfc.nasa.gov/data/Aura_OMI_Level3/OMDOAO3e.003/. The reasonable TOC agreement between the OMI observations and ground–based network has been found38–40. To retrieve the Belsk TOC time series we use a tool: https://www.esrl.noaa.gov/gmd/grad/neubrew/SatO3DataTimeSeries.jsp.

The daily TOC representative

The daily mean TOC representatives (dTOCR) are calculated from the intraday TOC values to facilitate the subsequent calculation of the monthly, seasonal and annual averages. There are many ways to calculate dTOCR by averaging selected types of TOC observations throughout the day, resulting in varying degrees of uncertainty in the dTOCR value.

Ideally, the dTOCR should be calculated by averaging only the DS observations, as these are considered the most reliable. If this is the case, dTOCR for day, d, is denoted in the text as < DS(d) >BP. or < DS(d) >SG14 when BP OACS or SG14 OACS is used in the TOC calculation, respectively. If there are no such observations, the average is obtained using ZB or ZC measurements (when ZB are unavailable).

The following alternatives of dTOCR are constructed for comparison purposes: < DSZB(d) >(…) which averages DS and ZB data, < ZC(d) >(…), which is firstly based on ZCs-AD and ZCl-AD observations. If such observations are unavailable, ZCs-CD and ZCl-CD TOC are used instead. Finally, < ALL(d) >(…) is built from the most reliable observations taken during the day. If DS and ZB observations are available on day d, then < ALL(d) >(…) = < DSZB(d) > (…). If such observations are unavailable, then < ZC(d) > (…) is used instead of < DSZB(d) >(…).

The monthly and seasonal TOC representatives

The monthly TOC representative (mTOCR), which is crucial for trend analysis, is calculated by averaging dTOCR values. However, these daily values are sometimes unavailable due to adverse weather conditions or DOB84 failure. This raises the question of how many daily TOCs are needed to create a reliable mTOCR and which TOC source can be used to fill the daily gaps.

At the beginning of the DOB84 measurements at Belsk, the TOC measurements were not as frequent as in the period when the ozone-hole problem first appeared in the early 1980s43,44. The representativeness of the mTOCR value for a given month depends on how many dTOCR values are used to calculate the monthly average. Ideally, dTOCR values would be available for every day of the month, but this is not always possible. In this section, we have set a minimum threshold of 10, 15 or 20 days of dTOCR data in order to obtain mTOCR values.

Previous trend analyses revealed that the TOC trends depend on season with the largest trend in the spring in the Northern Hemisphere mid latitudes37,45,46. Therefore, the mTOCR values were averaged over three-month blocks to calculate seasonal TOC representatives, sTOC, for winter (December–February), spring (March–May), summer (June–August) and autumn (September–October). Subsequently, these sTOCR were analysed for the subperiod 1963–1979, 1980–2004 and 2005–2024.

Trend differences between various clusters of DOB84 measurements

In this subsection, the impact of various types of TOC observations, including the DS, ZB and ZC categories, on the long-term TOC pattern is examined.

The mTOCR values can be obtained using the full (DS & ZB & ZC) and shortened (DS or DS & ZB) monitoring programmes. Thus, three subsets are considered: < DS(m)>, < DSZB(m) > and < ALL(m)>. The first subset consists only of DS-AD and DS-CD observations, which are the most common in the WOUDC network. The other observations are made rather infrequently, even though they are possible for many winter days at the mid-latitude DOB stations.

Difference between dobson and brewer measurements

This subsection presents a proposed method for reducing the effects of stray light in DOB84. This method has been validated using the intraday TOC values from BRE64 after applying the stray light correction proposed by Savastiouk et al.26 and BRE207 (nominally free of the stray light effect).

Underestimation of TOC occurs at low solar elevations due to an increase in stray light within the optical instrument when the slant-path TOC (STOC = µ × TOC) exceeds approximately 1,000 DU. The effects of stray light in Belsk’s spectrophotometers can be assessed using an empirical approach that analyses the differences between intraday TOC values and the corresponding daily baseline value. The latter is calculated as an average of observations with STOC values of less than 500 DU.

For STOC values greater than 1000 DU, the expected decline from the baseline is a few percent. These effects can be studied using DOB84 and BRE measurements carried out at Belsk: BRE207 between 2010 and 2013, and BRE64 between 2023 and 2024. The DOB84 (from the SG14 OACS) and the BRE64 values, corrected for stray light, will continue to be referred to as DOB_SG14_SC and BRE64_SC, respectively.

Discussion

It is well known that DS-AD observations provide the most reliable DOB TOC values. This is also supported by the results shown in Fig. 3. Many monitoring sites that archive DOB data in WOUDC focus solely on this type of TOC observation, disregarding the less accurate ZB and ZC observations that can be made in non-optimal weather conditions. Recently, there has been a need to implement temperature-dependent OACS in TOC retrieval, replacing the previously used temperature-fixed BP scale with the newest temperature-dependent SG14 scale.

We have extended the approach of Voglmeier et al.15, which was originally used for DS observations only, to all-sky DOB observations. Specifically, BP TOC values for other DOB setups are also multiplied by the ratio between the BP and SG14 ozone absorption coefficients. This approach is formally correct for the DS option of DOB measurements. We demonstrate that it also functions for other instrumental settings (i.e. ZB-AD(CD) and ZC–AD(CD)) when fixed, experimentally determined correction values are added, i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ECC}_{\lambda\:1\lambda\:2}^{\mathrm{OBS}}$$\end{document} constants, as defined in the Methods section. Analysis of the results shown in Table 4 indicates that agreement between TOC intraday values obtained with the DS-AD setup and those obtained with other DOB setups improves when the SG14 OACS is used instead of the BP OACS. Furthermore, daily TOC representatives calculated using the SG14 OACS increased by about 2–3% in winter (Fig. 4), resulting in closer agreement with other TOC sources (see Table 6).

The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ECC}_{\lambda\:1\lambda\:2}^{\mathrm{OBS}}\:\:$$\end{document}constants cannot be calculated if there is a lack of intraday TOC observations and only historical daily TOC data is available (as is the case for the TOC data archived in WOUDC). As a first approximation, these coefficients can be set to zero for a non-DS-AD instrument setup. This is in line with the low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ECC}_{\lambda\:1\lambda\:2}^{\mathrm{OBS}}\:$$\end{document}values obtained from the Belsk’s intraday TOC data, which are usually within ± 1% for typical DOB observations (see Table 1).

The monthly TOC averages, which are calculated by averaging the daily TOC values over the course of a month, are of particular importance as they are used to calculate trends and reveal long-term anthropogenic changes to the ozone layer. However, these calculations are sometimes based on a limited number of daily TOC data due to adverse weather conditions or instrument failure. The question of how many daily TOC observations are necessary to obtain a reliable monthly TOC mean remains unanswered. If the threshold for the minimum number of TOC observations required for monthly mean calculations is set too high (e.g. 20 daily means), more gaps will appear in the available monthly mean TOC data for trend detection (see Table 5).In such cases, however, daily TOC data from other sources can be used to fill the gaps.

Analysis of Belsk’s TOC data showed that using only DS and ZB types of DOB observations with changing frequencies between 1963 and 2024 would introduce trend uncertainty of approximately ± 0.3% per decade (see Fig. 5). For other DOB stations where TOC monitoring is limited to DS observations, data gaps can be filled using daily TOC values from the MSR-2 reanalysis up to 2004 and, subsequently, from OMI measurements aboard the Aura satellite. However, TOC values derived from these data sources must be corrected for biases detected when compared with DS-DOB observations before they can be used to supplement the DOB data.

Another issue that focused our attention was the underestimation of the DOB TOC values by a few percent for large slant-path TOC, which was caused by the presence of stray light in the instrument optics. We propose using an empirical method to estimate the effect of stray light on spectrometers by analysing the daily pattern of TOC values obtained from numerous intraday observations between sunrise and sunset. The steady decline in TOC values as the slant path increases enables us to construct an analytical formula that provides individual instrument parameterisation of the stray light effect. Applying this correction formula to new TOC retrievals using the SG14 OACS shows that the year-round difference between the DOB064 and BRE207 measurements, which have reduced stray light due to the double monochromator, diminishes to ± 0.5% (see Table 7). Figure 7 shows that the stray light correction function derived from DS observations can be successfully applied to all-sky SG14 TOC measurements over a wide range of STOC values (up to approximately 1,500 DU).

Savastiouk et al.26 developed an algorithm for stray light correction based on the physics of the BRE response to stray light. This algorithm was used here for the BRE64 with a single monochromator, as the effect of stray light is particularly pronounced in this type of BRE. For BREs with a double monochromator, such as our BRE207, the stray light is much lower. When comparing the differences between the DOB64, for which the stray light was removed using our empirical approach, and the BRE64, which was corrected using the Savastiouk et al.26 algorithm, an increase of over 1% was found between the two spectrophotometers for STOC > 1500 DU (see Fig. 8b). However, this overestimation was not evident for the BRE207 (see Fig. 6a) and the DOB84 (see Fig. 7). This suggests that the algorithm may overestimate TOC for extremely high STOC when applied to certain single monochromotor BREs. However, the number of measurements available for very high STOC was limited here, and a special measurement program focusing on Brewer observations at very low solar elevations (less than 10°, giving µ > 5) is needed to support this statement.

Surprising increases of about 1% point are observed when comparing the smoothed patterns of the relative BRE-DOB84_SG14_SC differences for small STOCs (Fig. 8a, b). At this stage, we hypothesise that this may be due to elevated temperatures inside the BRE affecting the performance of the optical and electronic components. This is because these small STOCs appeared at the warmest time of year, around local noon, when the BRE may have overheated. Fountoulakis et al.48 suggested that temperature gradients within the BRE could generate mechanical stresses that might affect the instrument’s optical properties. However, the overheating does not appear in DOB, as a manual temperature adjustment is applied to the instrument before each observation begins. Further analysis is required to determine the cause of this increase, which is beyond the scope of this article.
