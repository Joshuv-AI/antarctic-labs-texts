Molecular characterization of extended spectrum beta lactamase producing Escherichia coli in two different wastewater treatment plants in Hatay Province, Türkiye
Authors: Büşra Gülay Celil Özaslan, Cemil Kürekci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWastewaters are considered as hotspot for multidrug resistant bacteria and genes, especially for extended spectrum beta lactamase producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-EC), which pose significant public health concern. Conventional wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are not designed to remove such resistant bacteria before discharge. This study, therefore, aimed to evaluate and compare the performance of two municipal WWTPs in eliminating ESBL-EC in Hatay province, Türkiye. The isolates were further characterized by antimicrobial susceptibility testing, pulsed-field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) and resistance gene analysis. A total of 24 wastewater samples [influent (n = 12) and effluent (n = 12)] were collected from both conventional and advanced biological WWTP. A total of 66 ESBL-EC were isolated and confirmed as ESBL producer. The abundance of ESBL-EC was significantly more prevalent in the influent water samples (mean 3.86 ± 0.11 log cfu/ml), when compared to the effluent wastewater samples (mean 2.20 ± 0.27 log cfu/ml). According to the PFGE results (cut-off > 85%), 16 isolates were clonally related, whereas 49 isolates were singletons and one isolate could not be evaluated. Besides beta-lactam antibiotics, the isolates were highly resistant to tetracycline (63.63%) and fluoroquinolones (43.93%). The blaCTX-M gene was the most frequently detected gene in the isolates (86.36%), among which the blaCTX-M-15 type (86.36%) was the most frequently detected. At least two disinfectant resistance genes were also detected in each of the isolates. Even though both WWTP were found to be effective in reducing ESBL-EC counts, not well enough to complete elimination, showing the potential public health risk.Keywords: ESBL, E. coli, Wastewater treatment, Antibiotic resistance

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance (AMR) has been one of the most serious global concerns in recent decades, posing a considerable threat to human health as well as adding financial burden on health-care systems. Antibiotic misuse and overuse are thought to be key contributors to the evolution of resistance strains1, which has been linked to veterinary, medical, and agricultural settings including aquatic production. Many factors such as geographic location, socioeconomic status, climate, antibiotic consumption, and treatment regimens of infections have been shown to alter the abundance and dissemination of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) and antibiotic-resistant bacteria (ARB)1,2. Wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) recently attracted great attention enabling monitorization of hazardous materials including infectious agents. Although wastewater treatment comprises many steps and employ different methods (mechanical, biological, physical, chemical, and physical–chemical), for which technology is constantly improving, it is insufficient for total eradication of antibiotics, ARB, and ARGs3. Furthermore, the presence of antibiotics in aquatic niches at sub-inhibitory doses create selective pressure, with the likely promotion of other substances, such as pesticides, detergents, and heavy metals, in the sewage for the co-selection of resistant strains4. Zhuang et al. analyzed articles published in PubMed from 1990 to 2020 pertaining to reports of ARGs in the environment, especially in WWTPs, and found that the genes encoding beta-lactamases had the highest frequency all around the world5. In addition, Bréchet et al. estimated more than 600 billion of extended spectrum beta lactamase producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-EC) cells released into the receiving rivers daily in France6.

ESBLs are enzymes that hydrolyze a broad spectrum of beta lactam antibiotics, which confer resistance to penicillins, 3rd generation cephalosporins and monobactams. To date, many ESBLs have been identified, among which CTX-M-15 type represents the most frequently encountered one7. Notably, CTX-M-15 producing E. coli, especially those belonging to the ST131, have emerged as a significant cause of urinary and blood infections in humans and is frequently found in wastewater7,8. The gene blaCTX-M is mostly found on plasmids, which were also found to carry other resistance genes conferring resistance to fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and disinfectants, limiting the availability of effective therapeutic alternatives9,10. Public health authorities, including the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, have categorized ESBL-EC as serious and critical threats. The occurrence of ESBL-EC continues to increase globally despite the introduction of infection prevention and control strategies intended to prevent further spread in healthcare settings11. For instance, with 59,900 reported deaths worldwide in 2019 linked to 3rd generation cephalosporin-resistant E. coli infections, the associated morbidity and mortality probably increased with prevalence 12.

Many studies have been conducted on the diversity and composition of the bacterial communities in WWTPs (reviewed in1) and there have been reports of ESBL-EC bacteria in wastewater and rivers in Türkiye13,14. However, a comprehensive comparison of bacterial communities and AMGs in WWTPs treating different types of wastewaters is needed for the development of efficient and consistent treatment processes. Accordingly, the main objective of this study was to examine and compare the performance of two municipal WWTPs in the removal of ESBL-EC. These WWTPs differs in their fundamental modes of operation, for which the one operates under a conventional treatment process, while the other employs advanced biological processes, that embodies novel biological approaches, harnessing through newly identified microbial strains with enhanced nitrogen or phosphorus removal capabilities via enrichment or cultivation of specific microbial species15. The present study further aimed to investigate the molecular characteristics of ESBL-EC by determining antimicrobial susceptibility, ARGs including disinfectant and providing epidemiological relatedness.

Material and methods

Wastewater treatment plants and sample collection

Sampling was carried out from March to May in 2021 in two different WWTPs (Iskenderun advanced biological treatment and Antakya conventional treatment) in Hatay province, for which detailed information are provided below (Table 1). A total 24 water samples (250 mL each) were collected, comprising six influent and six effluent samples from each of the two treatment plants.

ESBL-EC count and bacterial isolation

For ESBL-EC enumeration, water samples were serially diluted (1/10) and cultured on Tryptone-bile X glucuronide agar (TBX) medium (Merck, Germany) containing ceftazidime [2 µg/ml, above the epidemiological cut-off value established by the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (https://mic.eucast.org/search/)]. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, blue-green colonies showing typical E. coli characteristics were counted in all petri dishes. For each sample, suspicious blue-green E. coli colonies (1–5 colonies) were selected and inoculated on blood agar (Merck, Germany) for purification. Finally, all bacterial isolates were stored at -80 °C in Brain Heart Infusion Broth with 20% glycerol.

Species level identification

Species level identification of the isolates was performed by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the uspA (universal stress protein A) gene specific for E. coli16. The primers used for the uspA gene region were F-5' CCGATACGCTGCCAATCAGT and R-5' ACGCAGACCGTAGGCCAGT. E. coli ATCC 25,922 DNA was utilized as the positive control, while DNA and RNA-free water was utilized as the negative control.

Antibiotic susceptibility testing and ESBL confirmation

Antibiotic susceptibility testing was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar, in accordance with Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines17 for the following antibiotics: amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (20 + 10 µg) cefoxitin (30 µg), cefotaxime (30 µg), cepefime (30 µg), cefuroxime (30 µg), cephalothin (30 µg), ceftiofur (30 µg), aztreonam (30 µg), ampicillin (10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), gentamicin (10 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), and fosfomycin (200 µg). The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. E. coli ATCC 25,922 was used as a reference strain.

Confirmation of ESBL production was done according to CLSI criteria via using double disk synergy and disk combination methods17.

Pulsed field gel electrophoresis (PFGE) and phylogenetic subtypes of ESBL-EC isolates

Clonal relationship of ESBL-EC isolates from wastewater were determined by PFGE using XbaI enzyme. The protocol specified by PulseNet (www.cdc.gov/pulsenet) was used for PFGE analysis. Band profiles analysis was performed using BioNumerics software 7.6 (Applied Maths, Gent, Belgium). Phylogenetic types of E. coli isolates (A, B1, B2, C, D, E and F) were determined by multiplex PCR using the protocol reported by Clermont et al.18. The primers (chuA, yjaA, TspE4C2, ArpAgpE, trpAgpC1 and aceK) were used and phylogenetic typing of the isolates was determined according to the combinations described by Clermont et al.18.

Detection of antibiotic resistance genes

ESBL-EC isolates were screened for the presence of the blaSHV, blaCMY-2, blaCTX-M and blaTEM genes by multiplex PCR using primers reported by Ahmed et al.19. DNA sequencing of amplicons was carried out by Sanger sequencing (Metsantek, Türkiye). The sequencing results were compared to those available in GenBank using the BLAST tool (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) to identify subtypes. Quinolone resistance genes (aac(6)-Ib, qnrA, qnrB, qnrC and qnrD) were screened by using PCR as reported by Cavaco et al.20. Disinfectant resistance genes (qacEΔ1, qacF/H/I, emrE, mdfA, sugE(c), sugE(p), ydgE, ydgF and oqxA) were also investigated by PCR in all isolates as reported by Zou et al.21 and Deus et al.22.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained in this study were analyzed by the SPSS 22.0 program and the effects of regions (Antakya and Iskenderun), point (influent and effluent) and their interactions in terms of CREC loads were analyzed by two-way analysis of variance. Multiple comparison tests with Bonferroni correction were applied in cases where significance was found. Significance level was taken as 0.05.

Results

ESBL-EC number and isolation

ESBL-EC was identified in 23 of the 24 collected water samples, except one effluent sample from advanced biological treatment plant. The total number of ESBL-EC in the influent water samples from two WWTPs was between 3.74 ± 0.16 and 3.97 ± 0.16 log cfu/ml (mean 3.86 ± 0.11 log cfu/ml), while this number was between 1.44 ± 0.35 and 2.71 ± 0.20 log cfu/ml (mean 2.20 ± 0.27 log cfu/ml) in the effluent water samples from two sides (Table 2). There was no statistically significant difference for the ESBL-EC counts in influent water samples in both facilities (p > 0.05), while effluent water samples from advanced biological treatment plant had lower number of ESBL-EC compared to these from conventional treatment (p < 0.05; Table 2).

A total of 66 suspected E. coli isolates were obtained on TBX agar and were confirmed by PCR using E. coli specific uspA gene. Thirty-seven isolates were obtained from conventional treatment plant (influent water 18 isolates and effluent 19 isolates) and 29 isolates were obtained from advanced biological treatment plant (influent water 21 isolates and effluent 8 isolates).

Determination of antibiotic resistance status and detection of ESBL-EC

As expected, all CREC isolates were found to be resistant to ampicillin and cephalothin and all CREC isolates were found to be ESBL positive according to double disk synergy and disc combination method. In addition, 65 isolates (98.48%) were resistant to cefotaxime and cefuroxime, 31 isolates (46.96%) to amoxicillin clavulanic acid and nine isolates (13.63%) to cefoxitin. While 60 isolates (90.90%) were resistant to ceftiofur, 38 isolates (57.57%) were resistant to cefepime. Among the monobactam group, 59 isolates (89.39%) were resistant to aztreonam. Among the aminoglycoside group of antibiotics, 12 isolates (18.18%) were resistant to gentamycin. Additionally, 20 isolates (30.3%) were resistant to levofloxacin, while 29 isolates (43.93%) were resistant to ciprofloxacin. Forty-two isolates (63.63%) were resistant to tetracycline antibiotic. All isolates were found to be susceptible to imipenem.

PFGE results and Phylogenetic grouping

No bands were obtained from one isolate in PFGE analysis and therefore this isolate could not be further evaluated. Upon analysis (cut-off value > 85%) of band profiles for 65 isolates, it was observed that 16 isolates were clonally related and 49 isolates were not (Fig. 1). The clusters that were clonally related contained two isolates and these isolates were mostly from the same wastewater plant. Notably, some strains (Ww21.25/Ww21.74 and Ww21.17/Ww21.20) isolated from both influent and effluent water samples within the same plant showed the same PFGE profile, suggesting poor removal efficiency for AMB. Additionally, only two isolates (Ww21.66 and Ww21.26) originated from wastewater plants of different cities displayed similar banding pattern.

According to the combinations described by Clermont et al.18, 33 (50%) ESBL-EC isolates belonged to group A, 13 (19.69%) to group B1, eight (12.12%) to group D, five (7.57%) to group C, four (6.06%) to group B2 and three (4.54%) to group E.

Determination of beta-lactamase types and subtypes

It was determined that 86.36% (n = 57) of the isolates carried the blaCTX-M gene. 85.96% (49/57) of the isolates were found to contain blaCTX-M-15, 8.77% (n = 5) blaCTX-M-55 and 1.75% (n = 1) blaCTX-M-1 (Fig. 1). Additionally, 3.5% (n = 2) of the isolates contained the blaCTX-M-3 gene. The blaTEM gene was found in 54.4% (n = 36) and the blaCMY-2 gene in 7.57% (n = 5) of the isolates. However, no blaSHV gene was detected in any isolate.

Findings on quinolone and disinfectant resistance genes

Five quinolone resistance genes (qnrA1, qnrB1, qnrC, qnrD and aac(6)-Ib) were analyzed and the aac(6)-Ib, qnrB1 and qnrA1 genes were found in 10.60% (n = 7), 4.54% (n = 3) and 3.03% (n = 2) of the isolates, respectively (Fig. 1). However, the qnrC and qnrD genes were not detected in any of the isolates.

Eight of nine disinfectant resistance genes (qacEΔ1, qacF/H/I, emrE, mdfA, sugE(c), sugE(p), ydgE, ydgF and oqxA) were detected in the isolates (except for the oqxA gene) (Fig. 1). The distribution of these genes in CREC isolates were ydgE (84.84%; n = 56), qacEΔ1 (81.81%; n = 54), ydgF (80.30%; n = 53), mdfA (33.33%; n = 22), emrE (30.30%; n = 20), qacF/H/I (7.57%; n = 5), sugE(c) (6.06%; n = 4), and sugE(p) (3.03%; n = 2).

Discussion

Several studies have reported the occurrence of ESBL-EC as a common public health related bacteria in the sewage and wastewater, indicating likely environmental and human health issues1,13. Results of this study presented high counts of ESBL-EC (3.74–3.97 log cfu/ml) in the incoming water of two cities. Adegoke et al. found similar values (1.8–4.8 log cfu/ml) in their study on a WWTP in South Africa23. Bréchet et al. also monitored urban wastewater samples in France and found ESBL-EC at concentration of 2.87 log cfu/ml6. Conversely, ESBL-EC was detected at higher concentration (approximately 5.34 log cfu/ml) in the influent water samples in another city in Türkiye14. It has been stated previously that the number of bacteria depends on the factors including the nature of the influent. For example, hospital wastewater has been reported to be heavily contaminated with ESBL-EC6. When the counts of CREC were determined in the effluent water samples of both plants, CREC number were found to range from 1.44 to 2.71 log cfu/ml in this study. The most substantial reduction (over 2 log cfu/ml vs 1 log cfu/ml) was obtained in the effluents collected from advanced biological treatment when compared conventional treatment plant, indicating the importance of treatment processes. Gündoğdu et al. also reported reductions of more than 2 log cfu/ml of ESBL-EC with conventional treatment14. Bréchet et al. reported the biological content degradation with chlorination procedure as effective method in WWTPs, removing 94% ESBL-EC6.

According to the phylogenetic classification, the most of the CREC isolates (n = 54) were in groups A, B1 and D. As mentioned in different studies on ESBL-EC, phylogenetic groups of E. coli strains vary depending on the sources and geographical regions24. Some studies have suggested that group A is the group that adapts best to different environments25. In a study conducted in Japan, Xie et al. showed that antibiotic-resistant phylogroup B2 isolates have a high survivability in treated wastewater, and these strains were found to be the most predominant strains in wastewater samples collected from both treatment plants in different areas of the same city26. There are major public health concerns regarding the survival of the phylogroup B2 strains in the treated wastewater. Extra-intestinal E. coli strains primarily fall into groups B2 and D, whereas intestinal pathogenic strains of the bacteria have been linked to groups A, B1, and E27. The diversity of phylogroups in this study is not surprising given that the wastewater treatment plant receives waste from a wide variety of sources, including environmental and biological wastes.

When the band profiles of isolates were analyzed in PFGE analysis, most of the isolates were not linked and a homogeneous distribution was observed. Since the wastewater coming to the WWTP comes from different sources, the results are not surprising. Similarly, a study done in Colombia in 2019 also revealed that ESBL-EC isolates were not clonally related according to PFGE analysis results28.

ESBL-EC isolates obtained in this study were found to be also resistant to tetracycline at high rate (63.63%), which is consistent with previous studies from different countries8,29. In addition, 43.93% and 30.3% of ESBL-EC were also found to be resistant to ciprofloxacin, and levofloxacin, respectively, in the current study. However, in previous study, much lower resistance rate (42%) was obtained for ciprofloxacin resistance among ESBL-EC isolates from WWTP and Orontes River in the same city13.

The blaCTX-M group, found in 86.36% of the isolates, was the most common ESBL gene among our isolates. Brecket et al. found that most of the urban wastewater isolates (215 of 243 [88%]) produced blaCTX-M type ESBLs. blaCTX-M-1 was the most common blaCTX-M type enzyme (50%), followed by blaCTX-M-15 (25%) and blaCTX-M-14 (10%)6. ESBLs are unevenly distributed in Europe, as previous studies have reported that blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14 are the most common ESBLs among ESBL-EC30–32. As in our study, among ESBLs, blaCTX-M-15 was found to be the most common type of enzyme in wastewater, as reported by studies from Türkiye and various countries8,14,28. For example, Amos et al. discovered that the majority ESBLs observed in E. coli from an English river were of the blaCTX-M-15 type, and this was associated with the high abundance of this type in the wastewater from a WWTP32. Similar findings about the widespread distribution of blaCTX-M enzyme were also found by Blaak et al.33, with blaCTX-M-15 being the most prevalent kind in Dutch recreational waters33.

Kim et al. have reported that quinolone resistance genes are also carried along with blaCTX-M genes34 and in 18.46% (n = 18) of the samples, one of the quinolone resistance genes and blaCTX-M were found together. This can be explained by both beta-lactams and fluoroquinolones stimulate the transcription of qnrB genes, and the existence of qnr is strongly associated with the use of cephalosporins in the past. When antibiotic selection pressure is present, co-localization of qnrB with ESBL may be advantageous for bacterial isolates34.

Several efflux pump genes, particularly ydgE and ydgF, were commonly detected in the ESBL-EC in the current study. Of the 60 ESBL-EC isolates from chicken meat, ydgE, ydgF, sugE(c), and mdfA were found in 100% of isolates but they did not detect the oqxA gene10. It was also notable that the qacEΔ1 gene was present in 81% of the isolates in our study. However, Sahin et al. reported much lower (20%) rate among ESBL-EC from chicken meat10. Likewise, when Zou et al. examined QAC resistance genes in E. coli isolated from the retail meats, they found that ydgE and ydgF had 100% detection rates, with mdfA (99.5%), qacEΔl (23%), and qacG (0.4%) having the next highest detection rates21. Chromosomally encoded sugE(c) mediates strong resistance to QAC disinfectant21 and was not very common in this study. qacEΔ1 was found in all samples of ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates obtained from the influent and effluent of three WWTPs in Romania35 and in a study of ARGs in sewage sludge, qacEΔ1 was one of the five most abundant ARG transcripts36. In our study, the qacEΔ1 gene was detected at a high rate (81.81%), indicating the selection of this kind of resistance in wastewater, most likely as a result of the strong selection pressure that disinfectants impose. The prevalence of the genes ydgE/ydgF (83–100%), mdfA (86–100%), and qac (0–18%) in E. coli isolates from various sources has also been observed by a number of studies21,22,37,38.

This study has potential limitations such as the lack of quantitative measurements of antibiotic concentrations in wastewater and the absence of real-time PCR analysis for the ESBL gene quantification. Furthermore, more in-depth genomic characterization using whole-genome sequencing and comparative analysis with previously identified dominant clones from human infections were not performed. However, these limitations could be addressed in future investigations with advance molecular techniques to provide detailed insights into the dynamics of ESBL-EC in wastewater environments.

Conclusions

The results of this study showed that treatment process of wastewater reduce the ESBL-EC, however significant number of bacteria still release into the receiving water bodies (river and sea). It is also noteworthy that the biological wastewater treatment plant reduced ESBL-EC logarithmically more than the conventional wastewater treatment plant. Importantly, the one with advanced biological treatment has more than 2 log cfu/ml reduction. The blaCTX-M-15 gene was the most prevalent type of ESBL in both of WWTPs and is an important finding for public health. According to our findings, there is a need to monitor antibiotic-resistant microorganisms in WWTPs and processes with higher treatment power are needed.
