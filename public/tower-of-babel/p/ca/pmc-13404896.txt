The mediating role of emotional regulation strategies in the relationship between the dark tetrad of personality and anxious–depressive symptomatology
Authors: Manuel Galán, Álvaro García del Castillo-López, Fátima Ruiz-Fuster, David Pineda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmotional regulation is crucial for psychological well-being, influencing how individuals manage their emotions and adapt their behavior in various situations. Emotional regulation strategies can function either as protective factors or as risks in the development of psychopathologies such as depression and anxiety. Individual characteristics—specifically, the traits of the Dark Tetrad (narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism)—are generally associated with emotional difficulties and an increased risk of psychopathology. The aim of this study is to examine how emotional regulation strategies mediate the relationship between these dark traits and emotional distress. A sample of 464 participants was assessed on Dark Tetrad traits, cognitive emotional regulation strategies, and emotional symptomatology. Four mediation models were developed to investigate whether emotional regulation strategies mediate the relationship between each Dark Tetrad trait and anxious–depressive symptomatology. The results indicate that psychopathy and Machiavellianism exhibit direct effects on symptomatology, suggesting partial mediation through maladaptive strategies, whereas sadism and narcissism show no direct association with symptoms. Overall, narcissism is associated with adaptive emotional regulation, while Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism are linked with dysfunctional strategies that heighten vulnerability to psychopathology. These findings highlight emotional regulation strategies as mediators and have important implications for designing interventions aimed at improving emotional regulation in individuals with high levels of dark traits.Keywords: Dark Tetrad, Cognitive Emotional Regulation Strategies, Anxiety, Depression

Introduction

Emotional intelligence has emerged as a key concept in both scientific psychology and popular discourse, serving as an important framework for understanding how individuals manage their emotions in personal and social contexts [1]. This ability involves not only the capacity to perceive, understand, and express emotions but also to regulate them effectively—an essential process for successful adaptation to various life situations [2].

Emotional regulation, a concept closely related to emotional intelligence, refers to the processes by which individuals influence which emotions they experience, when they experience them, and how they feel and express them [3]. While emotional intelligence encompasses the ability to manage both one’s own and others’ emotions, emotional regulation specifically focuses on modifying one’s own emotional states to achieve particular goals by modulating their intensity, duration, or manner of expression [4].

These regulatory processes are fundamental for maintaining emotional balance, which in turn facilitates cognitive, social, and physical functioning. Effective emotional regulation is associated with enhanced mental and physical health by reducing stress levels and improving resilience in adverse situations [5]. Moreover, these processes are critical not only for individual well-being but also for fostering positive interpersonal interactions and sustaining satisfactory relationships [4, 6].

Emotional regulation has been recognized as an essential component of psychological adaptation and emotional well-being [3]. Within this broad framework, cognitive strategies have a significant impact on mental health by acting as either protective or risk factors in the development of psychopathologies such as depression and anxiety [7, 8]. Strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and suppression have been extensively studied; while reappraisal may buffer against emotional distress, suppression is more closely associated with maladaptive outcomes and increased vulnerability to psychopathology [9, 10]. However, it is important to note that certain maladaptive cognitive strategies (e.g., self-blame, catastrophizing, or giving up) may conceptually overlap with core symptoms of depressive or anxious states. This potential redundancy raises interpretative challenges, as difficulties in emotion regulation and emotional distress can partially reflect shared psychological content rather than entirely distinct constructs [7, 8].

Notwithstanding, given the critical role of emotional regulation in psychological well-being, it is important to consider how these strategies interact with personality traits that predispose individuals to maladaptive outcomes. In particular, the Dark Tetrad of personality—which includes narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism—represents a set of aversive, socially problematic traits characterized by manipulation, egocentrism, emotional insensitivity, and a propensity to harm others. Specifically, narcissism is characterized by grandiosity, entitlement, and a strong need for admiration; Machiavellianism involves strategic manipulation, deceit, and a focus on personal gain; psychopathy is marked by impulsivity, emotional detachment, and callousness; and sadism entails deriving pleasure from the suffering of others [11, 12]. Individuals exhibiting these traits tend to experience emotional and relational difficulties, manifesting in heightened vulnerability to psychological distress and maladaptive behaviors [13, 14].

Empirical evidence indicates that the Dark Tetrad traits are differentially associated with anxious and depressive symptomatology. Traits such as Machiavellianism and psychopathy, characterized by high emotional detachment and manipulativeness, are often linked to the use of maladaptive regulation strategies (e.g., suppression, avoidance, and rumination), which increase the risk of developing anxiety and depression [15, 16]. Sadism may contribute indirectly to psychological distress through aggressive behaviors and interpersonal conflicts, which can exacerbate negative emotional states. Conversely, narcissism appears to be more nuanced: when accompanied by adaptive regulation strategies, it may buffer against emotional distress, highlighting its potential for context-dependent outcomes [17].

Recent studies suggest that not all dark traits are necessarily negative in every context. For example, Rico-Bordera et al. [17] found that narcissism, when coupled with high emotional intelligence, may be associated with adaptive behaviors—such as prosocial engagement and enhanced well-being. This finding challenges a one-dimensional view of Dark Tetrad traits and implies that the emotional regulation strategies employed by these individuals may play an important role in determining whether outcomes are adaptive or maladaptive. Conversely, individuals with traits such as Machiavellianism and psychopathy tend to favor maladaptive strategies, likely due to their inherent low empathy and high emotional insensitivity [15].

Given the inherent complexity of the behavioral tendencies associated with Dark Tetrad traits and their relevance for psychological functioning, there is a clear need to delve deeper into the mediating role played by cognitive emotional regulation strategies in emotional well-being. The aim of the present study is to analyze the mediating role of cognitive emotional regulation strategies in the relationship between Dark Tetrad personality traits and emotional distress under normative conditions. This approach has the potential to provide a more nuanced understanding of how dark traits interact with emotional regulation strategies to influence psychological well-being or distress. Specifically, the study seeks to explore whether the use of certain cognitive regulation strategies is adaptive or maladaptive in individuals with high levels of Dark Tetrad traits, and whether certain strategies increase vulnerability to developing psychopathologies such as depression and anxiety.

Method

Participants and procedure

Initially, 1,384 individuals accessed the survey; however, only 465 completed the measures of interest, and one participant was excluded for being underage. This resulted in a final sample of 464 Spanish-speaking adults aged between 18 and 68 years, with a mean age of 42.6 years (SD = 11.4). The majority of the sample were women (88.5%). Likewise, most participants were Spanish (91.6%), followed by Latin Americans (6.1%) and individuals from other European countries. Regarding education, 49.8% had university-level studies—of which 20.8% had completed postgraduate studies and 29.0% held an undergraduate degree—while 26.6% had vocational training. Concerning employment, 61.3% were employed, with the remainder comprising unemployed individuals, self-employed persons, among others.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. This research was reviewed and approved by the UCAM’s Ethics Committee (CE012517). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study. Following approval, the survey was administered via the LimeSurvey platform and distributed using snowball sampling through social networks such as WhatsApp, Telegram, Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation and did not receive any compensation.

Instruments

Short Dark Triad (SD3 [18])

The SD3 is a 27-item self-report scale designed to assess the three traits of the Dark Triad: subclinical narcissism, Machiavellianism, and subclinical psychopathy. Each trait is measured using nine items, with responses provided on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Strongly disagree”) to 4 (“Strongly agree”). For this study, the Spanish version of the instrument [19] which has demonstrated good reliability in previous research, was employed. In the present sample, internal consistency values were α = 0.63 and ω = 0.64 for narcissism, α = 0.69 and ω = 0.70 for Machiavellianism, and α = 0.62 and ω = 0.66 for psychopathy.

Assessment of Sadistic Personality (ASP [20])

The ASP is a 9-item self-report questionnaire that assesses everyday sadism, complementing the SD3 for a comprehensive evaluation of Dark Tetrad traits. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Strongly disagree”) to 4 (“Strongly agree”). The Spanish version of the ASP, previously validated [21] and demonstrating adequate reliability, was used. In this sample, the internal consistency of the ASP was α = 0.75 and ω = 0.78.

Anxiety and Depression Disorders Symptoms Scale (ESTAD-35 [22])

The ESTAD-35 is a 35-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess seven dimensions of emotional disorder symptoms. Responses are given on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“Never”) to 4 (“Often”). This instrument includes the following subscales: agoraphobia, panic disorder, generalized anxiety disorder, social phobia, illness anxiety disorder, major depressive disorder, and obsessive–compulsive disorder, as well as panic attacks. Additionally, the ESTAD-35 provides three overall scales evaluating the total score, the score of scales 1–6, and the score of the OCD scale. In the present study, the total score was used, with reliability coefficients of α = 0.97 and ω = 0.97.

Cognitive Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (CERQ [23])

The CERQ is a 36-item self-report questionnaire designed to assess cognitive coping strategies in adults when faced with negative or traumatic events. The version used was the Spanish adaptation [24]. The CERQ measures nine cognitive emotional regulation strategies that can be grouped into two categories: adaptive and maladaptive strategies. Adaptive strategies include Acceptance, Positive Focusing, Refocusing on Planning, Positive Reappraisal, and Putting into Perspective. Maladaptive strategies encompass Self-Blame, Rumination, Catastrophizing, and Other-Blame. In this study, both adaptive and maladaptive scales were used, yielding reliability coefficients of α = 0.90 and ω = 0.90 for adaptive strategies, and α = 0.82 and ω = 0.83 for maladaptive strategies.

Data analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0.0) was used for all statistical analyses. Correlations among the variables of interest were computed, and a mediation analysis was conducted to determine whether adaptive and maladaptive cognitive emotional regulation strategies mediate the relationship between each Dark Tetrad trait and anxious–depressive symptomatology. To this end, Model 4 of Hayes’ PROCESS macro (version 4.2) [25, 26] was employed to construct four separate models, one for each Dark Tetrad trait (see Fig. 1). Bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples was used to estimate the indirect effects and their 95% confidence intervals.

Results

Descriptive analysis and correlations

Table 1 presents the correlations among the study variables. The strongest inter-scale correlation was observed between the use of maladaptive strategies and the presence of anxious–depressive symptomatology (r = .48, p < .001). Additionally, the dark personality traits of Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism showed significant correlations with the use of maladaptive strategies (ranging from 0.18 to 0.282, p < .005) as well as with anxious–depressive symptomatology (ranging from 0.151 to 0.22, p < .005). In contrast, narcissism exhibited an opposite pattern by correlating with adaptive strategies (r = .15, p < .01) and showing no significant correlation with anxious–depressive symptomatology (p > .05).

Mediation analysis

The mediation analysis examined the relationships between Dark Tetrad traits and depressive–anxious symptoms, with adaptive and maladaptive emotional regulation strategies serving as mediators (see Table 2). Maladaptive emotional regulation strategies emerged as a significant mediator (p < .01) for most Dark Tetrad traits (Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism), suggesting that these traits are linked to depressive and anxious symptomatology primarily through dysfunctional regulation strategies. Conversely, narcissism was associated with symptomatology via adaptive strategies (p < .01). Specifically, both psychopathy and Machiavellianism exhibited a direct effect on symptomatology (p < .01), suggesting a partial mediation effect through maladaptive strategies. However, neither sadism nor narcissism showed a direct association with symptomatology (p > .05), indicating a full mediation effect—via adaptive strategies for one and maladaptive strategies for the other.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the mediating role of emotional regulation strategies in the relationship between Dark Tetrad traits and anxious–depressive emotional symptoms. The mediation analysis showed indirect associations through maladaptive emotional regulation strategies between Dark Tetrad traits—specifically Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism—and emotional symptomatology, whereas narcissism was associated with symptomatology primarily through adaptive strategies.

Firstly, the findings suggest that individuals with high levels of machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism are associated with greater anxious–depressive symptomatology and greater use of maladaptive emotional regulation strategies. This is consistent with existing literature indicating that Dark Tetrad traits are associated with dysfunctional regulation, which has been associated with greater psychological distress [15]. However, it is important to note that regulation processes in these individuals are complex; they may also engage in other mechanisms, such as emotional coping [27] or anger rumination [28], which can further influence their emotional outcomes. The observed associations between these traits and maladaptive strategies, coupled with the lack of association with adaptive strategies, highlight the relevance of emotional regulation in understanding the well-being of individuals scoring high on these traits.

More specifically, both Machiavellianism and psychopathy were directly associated with greater anxious–depressive symptomatology, alongside indirect associations through maladaptive regulation strategies. These associations may be interpreted in light of characteristics such as low empathy and emotional insensitivity, which have been linked to less effective stress-management patterns [29, 30]. Similarly, although sadism is also related to heightened anxious symptoms, its relationship with symptomatology was accounted for by the indirect pathway through strategies such as self-blame, other-blame, and catastrophizing. These results align with previous studies and the intrinsic nature of sadism, linking it to other maladaptive regulation strategies—such as engaging in aggression solely to improve mood [31].

It is particularly noteworthy that narcissism, in contrast to the other dark traits, was associated with lower anxious–depressive symptomatology through an indirect association involving adaptive strategies. In contrast to both the other traits and previous research on these variables [16], individuals with high levels of narcissism tend to employ more adaptive regulation strategies, such as cognitive reappraisal, which have been linked to better emotional well-being [9]. This finding supports earlier research suggesting that, in certain contexts, narcissism may not necessarily be detrimental at either the individual or social level, portraying it as the least “dark” trait of the tetrad (e.g [17]), . This phenomenon is often attributed to its associations with variables such as high self-esteem, ambition, and self-confidence, which may in some cases enhance social and professional performance [32–34]. Unlike other dark traits—such as psychopathy or Machiavellianism, which are characterized by higher emotional insensitivity and manipulative tendencies—narcissism, in the present associative models, showed a pattern involving adaptive emotional regulation and more favorable emotional symptomatology [35].

Beyond these empirical findings, the distinct pattern observed for narcissism also raises theoretical considerations regarding its place within the Dark Tetrad. Specifically, the divergence of narcissism from Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism echoes ongoing debates about whether it should be regarded as a true member of the Dark Triad/Tetrad (e.g [36]), . Furthermore, it is worth questioning whether the associations found here, based on the SD3 [18], fully represent narcissism as a broader construct or predominantly capture its grandiose dimension. Given the conceptual and empirical distinctions between grandiose and vulnerable narcissism, and their differing associations with emotional functioning (e.g [36, 37]), , future studies should examine whether these regulatory patterns extend beyond grandiose narcissism.

From a practical perspective, these findings may offer preliminary implications for the design of interventions aimed at improving emotional regulation in individuals with high levels of Dark Tetrad traits to reduce the risk of developing psychopathology. Given that maladaptive emotional regulation strategies were associated with the links between these traits and anxious–depressive symptoms, future research should examine whether interventions focused on strengthening regulation skills may help mitigate these difficulties. For instance, programs that promote adaptive strategies—such as cognitive reappraisal—could be explored as potential approaches to support more effective emotion management and reduce the tendency to resort to dysfunctional mechanisms like catastrophizing or emotional suppression. Moreover, screening for emotion regulation difficulties in patients with pronounced Machiavellian, psychopathic, or sadistic tendencies could aid in early identification of those at risk for anxiety and depression. Furthermore, considering that narcissism (or at least, as mentioned, SD3 narcissism) is associated with a greater use of adaptive strategies, future research might explore how to foster these skills in individuals with traits such as Machiavellianism and psychopathy to improve overall psychological well-being. These insights can inform the development of tailored, trait-sensitive interventions that address both emotion regulation and the specific challenges posed by dark personality traits.

Limitations

Despite the significance of these findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the present study is based on correlational data, which precludes the establishment of definitive causal relationships among Dark Tetrad traits, emotional regulation strategies, and emotional symptoms. Second, all variables were assessed through self-report measures, which may introduce common method variance and potentially inflate the observed associations. Moreover, given that both maladaptive emotion regulation strategies and anxious–depressive symptoms were assessed through self-report at the same time point, there is a possibility of conceptual overlap between these constructs. Some items of the maladaptive regulation scale (e.g., those reflecting giving up or perceiving situations as hopeless) closely resemble typical depressive cognitions, which may lead to shared variance and an overestimation of the mediation effects. Future studies employing longitudinal designs or incorporating multi-method assessments could help disentangle these overlapping psychological contents more clearly. In addition, although the ESTAD-35 total score was used to capture a broad transdiagnostic dimension of emotional symptomatology, future research should examine anxiety and depressive symptoms separately to identify potentially distinct patterns of association. Additionally, the CERQ captures relatively stable, trait-like tendencies in emotional regulation, whereas in real-life contexts, individuals often adapt their strategies depending on the situation or the type of emotional challenge they face. Furthermore, the analyses focused solely on cognitive and emotional regulation strategies. Exploring other strategies, such as behavioral ones, could provide further insight into the relationship between dark personality and emotional symptoms. Finally, the sample was predominantly composed of women and university-educated individuals, which may limit the generalizability of the results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings of this study underscore the importance of emotional regulation strategies in the associations between Dark Tetrad traits and emotional symptomatology. Narcissism appears to have a more adaptive component, associated with the use of effective emotional regulation strategies; this result likely reflects the grandiose aspect of narcissism emphasized in the SD3, rather than the vulnerable dimension. In contrast, Machiavellianism, psychopathy, and sadism are more strongly associated to dysfunctional regulation, and greater vulnerability to psychopathology. These findings support the relevance of further examining emotional regulation strategies in research aimed at understanding emotional difficulties among individuals with high levels of dark traits.
