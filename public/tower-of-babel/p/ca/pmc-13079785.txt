Study on rock energy and microscopic failure under different stress states
Authors: Lixiang Xie, Bingyang Li, Jinshan Sun, Jiawan Jin, Jiahao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
With the continuous expansion of deep underground engineering, the instability and failure of rock masses during construction have become increasingly prominent. Among these, marble has become a focal point of research due to its widespread occurrence. By integrating laboratory experiments and numerical simulations, this study systematically investigates the mechanical response, the characteristics of energy accumulation and dissipation, as well as fracture and damage evolution in marble. Experimental results demonstrate that increasing confining pressure significantly enhances both the strength and ductility of marble, while the intermediate principal stress exerts a pronounced regulatory effect on its mechanical properties and failure morphology. The stress–strain behaviour and crack evolution under uniaxial, conventional triaxial, and true triaxial loading conditions were simulated using a particle flow numerical method. Energy analysis indicates that tensile failure is the dominant failure mechanism of marble. With increasing confining pressure, the number of bonded damages during failure decreases, the proportion of tensile cracks decreases from about 75 to 61%, while the proportion of shear cracks increases from 25 to 39%. True triaxial numerical simulations further reveal a nonlinear regulatory effect of the intermediate principal stress on energy accumulation and release: a moderate increase enhances rock toughness and delays the peak of energy dissipation, whereas an excessive increase destabilizes energy release and promotes a transition in the failure mode from tensile to combined shear-dominated failure. These findings advance the understanding of failure mechanisms in deep marble and provide a theoretical basis for stability assessment and the development of energy-based criteria in underground engineering.

Introduction

Rock masses in deep underground engineering are subjected to complex geological and geomechanical environments. In addition to dynamic disturbances such as blasting vibrations and seismic waves1,2, the failure behavior of rock masses under different stress states plays an important role in the stability of underground engineering. Rock damage not only undermines structural integrity but may also trigger a series of geological disasters such as rockbursts, landslides, and hydraulic fracturing instabilities3–5. Tunnelling projects seldom meet initial budget requirements and commonly suffer from cost overruns, often driven by geological and geotechnical uncertainties that lead to design changes, scope adjustments, and project delays6–9. Therefore, quantifying the characteristics of energy storage and dissipation during rock loading and elucidating the evolution mechanisms of microcracks have become frontiers of current research10,11. Conducting research on the physicomechanical properties and energy evolution laws of deep marble, and evaluating the damage extent of surrounding rocks during underground excavation based on these findings, holds significant theoretical and practical importance for stability assessment and disaster prevention in deep rock engineering.

Substantial progress has been achieved in the study of rock mechanical properties and their energy evolution mechanisms. In terms of theoretical analysis, the mechanical behavior and energy evolution laws of rocks have been extensively explored. Li et al.12 proposed a micromechanical crack model suitable for evaluating both short-term progressive and long-term creep failure of brittle rocks under true triaxial loading, and validated the model through theoretical analysis and laboratory experiments. Zheng et al.13 investigated the mechanical properties and crack evolution mechanisms of deeply buried marble through true triaxial tests and PFC3D numerical simulations, and proposed a new three-dimensional GBM numerical modeling approach incorporating the mineral composition and particle size distribution characteristics of marble. Gong et al.14 introduced a theoretical method to characterize the damage of intact rock under uniaxial compression conditions, based on the linear energy dissipation approach and the energy dissipation coefficient. Yu et al.15 proposed a nonlinear unified strength criterion for rock materials, in which the influence of the intermediate principal stress was incorporated into the traditional Hoek–Brown criterion, thereby improving the accuracy of rock strength prediction under complex stress conditions. In addition, numerous scholars have conducted laboratory tests and numerical simulations to investigate in depth the deformation and failure characteristics of rock materials under various stress conditions. Dong et al.16 performed uniaxial compression and variable-angle shear tests under different temperature gradients to analyze variations in the mechanical parameters and acoustic emission characteristics of granite and granodiorite at elevated temperatures, revealing distinct mechanical and acoustic responses and evolutionary trends within certain temperature ranges. Zheng et al.17 examined the fracture behavior of sandstone specimens containing non-parallel prefabricated cracks under uniaxial compression and proposed a discontinuous fracture constitutive model for jointed sandstone. Liu et al.18 conducted uniaxial compression tests and acoustic emission (AE) monitoring on rock samples with various natural joint configurations to analyze the influence of joint angle, length, and distribution type on rock strength and failure characteristics. Meng et al.19 studied the energy evolution characteristics of four rock types through uniaxial compression and cyclic loading–unloading tests, finding that the energy density of all samples increased nonlinearly with axial stress. Zhu et al.20 conducted true triaxial geomechanical model tests on the deformation and failure patterns of deep soft rock tunnels, demonstrating the significant influence of support structures on the stability of surrounding rocks. Meng et al.21 investigated the strength evolution, microcrack distribution, and energy variation characteristics of layered rock masses under different initial unloading levels in triaxial unloading conditions. These studies provide valuable insights into the mechanical behavior and failure mechanisms of rocks under uniaxial, conventional triaxial, and true triaxial stress conditions, elucidate the fundamental mechanisms of crack initiation, propagation, and coalescence, and offer new approaches for predicting and controlling rock fracture.

Moreover, compared with conventional triaxial tests, true triaxial tests allow independent control of the three principal stresses, thereby providing a more realistic simulation of the in-situ stress conditions in deep geological formations22. Previous studies have demonstrated that the intermediate principal stress exerts a significant influence on both macroscopic mechanical responses and microscopic crack evolution23,24. Investigations on sandstone and diorite further revealed that the intermediate stress markedly affects the peak strength and crack orientation, confirming the necessity of three-dimensional strength criteria such as the Unified Strength Criterion (USC) and the generalized Hoek–Brown model25–27. Theoretical analyses and numerical simulations have further verified that the orientation of the intermediate principal stress has a stronger control over the cooperative crack evolution mode than the inclination of pre-existing defects28–30, while its magnitude governs the energy distribution pattern and the transformation pathway of failure modes31,32. Although substantial progress has been made in understanding the mechanical responses and failure mechanisms of rocks, the simulation of their microstructural evolution and mesoscopic energy transformation remains insufficient33–35. In particular, the mechanisms of energy dissipation and accumulation under high confining pressure have yet to be fully elucidated36,37, and the effects of intermediate principal stress-commonly present in deep and complex stress fields-have often been neglected38,39.

Most existing studies focus on one-dimensional or two-dimensional loading, or solely use peak intensity as an evaluation metric, with few quantifying crack type transitions based on energy pathways and changes in σ2 from true triaxial tests. To address the above issues, this study integrates laboratory experiments and numerical simulation to investigate the mechanical behavior of deeply buried marble, as well as the energy accumulation and dissipation characteristics during loading and failure. Conventional triaxial compression tests were performed to examine the mechanical properties of marble under various confining pressures, particularly at high confinement, while true triaxial tests were conducted to characterize its behavior under realistic three-dimensional stress states. The energy evolution and failure configurations of marble under different stress conditions were analyzed, and the complete stress–strain response curves were obtained to provide an experimental basis for theoretical and numerical analyses. Based on these results, a three-dimensional PFC numerical model of marble was established to analyze its mesoscopic energy evolution process and crack propagation mechanisms. The research findings will extend current understanding of the behavioral mechanisms of marble under real three-dimensional stress conditions, providing theoretical support for the design optimization and disaster prevention of deep underground engineering projects.

Triaxial compression test

As one of the most fundamental and widely used experimental approaches in rock mechanics and geological engineering, the conventional triaxial compression test aims to systematically obtain the strength, deformation, and failure characteristics of rocks under various stress conditions by controlling the confining pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document}=\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{3}$$\end{document}). In the laboratory, the conventional triaxial test simulates the stress environment of underground rock masses by applying axial stress while maintaining a constant confining pressure. This allows for the analysis of marble specimens’ mechanical properties and failure configurations under different confining pressures, as well as the determination of key mechanical parameters such as peak and residual strength, elastic modulus, Poisson’s ratio, internal friction angle, and cohesion.

Compared with shallow rock masses, deep-seated rocks are typically subjected to a non-uniform triaxial stress state, characterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{1}$$\end{document}>\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document}>\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{3}$$\end{document}. During deep underground excavation, the stress state of the surrounding rock transitions from a three-dimensional to a two-dimensional or one-dimensional condition. This “unloading” effect often induces local stress concentration zones within the rock mass. When stress exceeds the rock’s bearing capacity, the surrounding rock fractures. Following excavation of deep caverns, the redistribution of stresses further enhances the anisotropy of the rock mass. Although the conventional triaxial test can simulate rock deformation and failure under conditions where the intermediate principal stress equals the minimum principal stress, it cannot fully capture the true mechanical behavior of marble in deep geological environments. Therefore, true triaxial loading tests are conducted to control and vary the intermediate principal stress, enabling a comprehensive investigation of the mechanical properties and failure configurations of rocks under realistic triaxial stress conditions. Coupled with numerical simulations, these tests facilitate a detailed analysis of the damage evolution and crack propagation behavior of rock masses following excavation.

Marble, commonly found in the surrounding rock of deep underground caverns, was selected as the research material. Core drilling, cutting, and polishing were performed to prepare standard cylindrical specimens (50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height) and rectangular prismatic specimens (50 mm × 50 mm × 100 mm). The samples exhibited a compact and uniform structure, with no visible cracks or pronounced textures on their surfaces. In the natural state, the marble appeared white, with an average density of 2.7 g/cm3, as shown in Fig. 1. The deviation in end-face parallelism was controlled within 0.05 mm, and surface unevenness was limited to less than 0.02 mm. The dimensional tolerance for height and diameter was maintained within 0.3 mm. In addition, the specimen end faces were kept perpendicular to the axial direction, with a maximum angular deviation not exceeding 0.25°.Fig. 1Triaxial test specimen and numerical simulation model.

Testing equipment

To conduct the conventional triaxial compression tests, an MTS815 electro-hydraulic servo-controlled rock mechanics testing system, housed in the Deep Earth Engineering Laboratory of China University of Mining and Technology, was employed. The system is capable of applying an axial load ranging from 0 to 1700 kN and a maximum confining pressure of up to 45 MPa. The servo valve has a response frequency of 290 Hz, and the data acquisition rate can reach up to 5 kHz. The strain-controlled loading rate ranges from 10⁻7 to 10⁻2 s⁻1, with a minimum sampling interval of 50 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu s$$\end{document}, enabling high-precision and dynamic control of the loading and monitoring process.

For the true triaxial stress-path tests, a self-developed electro-hydraulic servo-controlled true triaxial loading system designed by the research team at China University of Mining and Technology was utilized. This system can independently and precisely control the loading processes along the three principal stress directions, with maximum axial, intermediate, and minimum principal stress capacities of 1600 kN, 500 kN, and 300 kN, respectively. The displacement-controlled loading rate can be as low as 0.002 mm/s, while the load-controlled rate ranges from 0.1 to 10 kN/s.

Trial protocol

(1) Conventional triaxial compression test scheme

As shown in Fig. 1, Standard cylindrical marble specimens with a diameter of 50 mm and a height of 100 mm were used for the tests. Six confining pressure levels were designed: 0 MPa, 5 MPa, 10 MPa, 20 MPa, 30 MPa, and 40 MPa. For each confining pressure, three parallel specimens were tested under displacement-controlled loading to comprehensively investigate the material response under different confining conditions. Before formal loading, a pre-contact force of 1 kN was applied to ensure full contact between the loading platens and the specimen ends. The axial displacement rate was set to 0.3 mm/min, and the confining pressure was increased at a rate of 5 MPa/min. To minimize the possible influence of hydraulic oil on the test results, each specimen was tightly wrapped with a POF heat-shrink film and reinforced with adhesive tape before loading, effectively isolating the confining medium.

(2) True triaxial loading test scheme

The true triaxial tests were also conducted on marble specimens obtained from the same rock source as those used in the conventional triaxial compression tests. The samples were machined into rectangular prisms with dimensions of 50 mm × 50 mm × 100 mm. Model development and parameter calibration

Although laboratory tests provide critical data for characterizing the macroscopic mechanical behavior of marble, they are limited in elucidating the internal fracture mechanisms and microscopic deformation features of the material. To further investigate the microscopic fracture mechanisms of marble under complex stress conditions, the DEM (Discrete Element Method) was employed, using PFC3D (Particle Flow Code in 3D) software to simulate the compressive failure process at the mesoscopic scale. The PFC3D simulations of marble under triaxial compression focused on tracking the initiation, propagation, and coalescence of microcracks, and, in conjunction with mesoscopic energy evolution analysis, revealed the microscopic fracture and damage mechanisms under compressive stress.

During model development, a sensitivity analysis of the mesoscopic parameters was conducted to identify a combination that accurately reproduces the macroscopic mechanical properties of marble. A numerical model geometrically scaled to the laboratory specimens was established according to the experimental conditions (see Fig. 1), with model dimensions identical to those of the tested samples. The particle radius was set to 1.5–2.0 mm, porosity to 0.4, and particle resolution to 0.1. The particles were distributed following a uniform random pattern to ensure isotropic characteristics in the initial model state.

Through iterative testing and adjustments, it was found that the simulated peak strength, strain, elastic modulus, and Poisson’s ratio are influenced by multiple factors, including the combination of parallel-bond cohesion and tensile strength, the ratio of normal to shear stiffness, and the inter-particle friction coefficient. Stress–strain curve analysis

Based on the calibrated mesoscopic parameters, conventional triaxial compression simulations of marble were conducted under various confining pressures of 0 MPa, 5 MPa, 10 MPa, 20 MPa, 30 MPa, and 40 MPa. The resulting stress–strain curves are presented in Fig. 7.Fig. 7Stress–strain curves under different circumferential pressures.

Stress–strain curves under different circumferential pressures.

The simulation results show that the modeled stress–strain curves successfully capture the entire deformation and failure process of marble under compression, encompassing the elastic deformation, yielding, post-peak strain softening, and residual strength stages. As servo control was applied prior to loading to ensure a relatively uniform initial compactness, the compaction stage associated with microcrack closure was not evident in the numerical curves. Under low confining pressure, the stress drops sharply after reaching the peak strength, exhibiting a typical brittle failure behavior. As the confining pressure increases to 30~40 MPa, the post-peak segment of the curve becomes progressively smoother, indicating a transition in failure mode from brittle to ductile, and a more pronounced yielding plateau appears prior to the peak. The slope of the linear elastic stage increases with confining pressure, suggesting that higher confinement enhances the overall stiffness of the rock mass. Overall, both the peak strength and the corresponding peak strain exhibit a linear increase with confining pressure, highlighting the strengthening effect of confinement on the load-bearing capacity and ductility of the marble.

The evolution patterns of bonding energy and strain energy are generally consistent with the stress–strain curves. During the stage of increasing axial stress, the growth of these two energy components reflects the accumulation of elastic strain energy and bonding deformation energy within the system. Under low confining pressure, bonding energy increases more rapidly; however, as the confining pressure or intermediate principal stress rises, the growth rate of strain energy surpasses that of bonding energy. When the model reaches its peak strength, both strain energy and bonding energy attain their maximum values and subsequently begin to decrease. Under higher confining pressures or elevated intermediate principal stresses, the strain at which the reduction begins lags further behind the peak-strength strain, indicating that the material retains a certain load-bearing capacity even after failure. These two forms of energy dominate the energy dissipation process during loading, and their post-peak decrease signifies the release of elastic and bonding energy as cracks coalesce and propagate through the material.

Frictional energy and damping energy characterize the non-elastic energy transformations associated with microcrack propagation, particle sliding, and dissipation processes. Under low confining pressure conditions, the variations of these energy components are relatively limited, indicating that the failure process is dominated by brittle fracture, with energy being rapidly released in the form of strain energy and bonding energy. As the confining pressure and intermediate principal stress increase, the growth of frictional energy becomes significantly more pronounced. Specifically, under different confining pressures, the peak friction energy increases from approximately 86 kJ/m3 at 0 MPa to about 328 kJ/m3 at 40 MPa. These results indicate that higher stress confinement promotes particle sliding, bond breakage, and energy dissipation, leading to a progressive transition of the failure mode from brittle behavior to plastic, dissipation-dominated deformation.

In the post-peak residual strength stage, the characteristics of energy distribution vary markedly under different confining pressures. Under low confining pressure, the boundary energy is almost entirely converted into frictional and damping energies, while strain and bonding energies rapidly decay to near zero, indicating that the model has undergone complete structural failure. In contrast, under high confining pressure and elevated intermediate principal stress, the strain and bonding energies are not completely released but exhibit a gradual decline. The system energy tends to reach a new equilibrium among different energy forms, where the combined frictional and damping energies progressively approach the magnitude of strain and bonding energies. This behavior indicates that, even after failure, the system retains a certain degree of energy storage and stable deformation capacity.

In summary, both confining pressure and intermediate principal stress exert a pronounced influence on the energy evolution of marble. Under low-stress conditions, rapid energy release results in brittle fracture, whereas higher stress confinement promotes energy accumulation and gradual dissipation, driving the failure mechanism toward a more ductile, energy-absorbing mode.

Under low confining pressure conditions, shear cracks are relatively sparse and primarily concentrated along the specimen edges or stress concentration zones. As the confining pressure increases, the total number of cracks decreases, but the proportion of shear cracks rises-particularly during the initiation and propagation stages, where shear fractures gradually emerge. This indicates that confining pressure not only suppresses volumetric dilation but also promotes the formation of shear slip surfaces, leading to a transition in the cracking pattern from purely tensile to a combined tensile-shear mode. At higher confining pressures, the strain-bearing capacity of the rock is significantly enhanced, allowing greater energy absorption and accumulation, which makes the crack propagation increasingly governed by shear failure. At this stage, the crack network exhibits a densely interwoven distribution, with localized regions forming distinct shear band structures-reflecting a plastic failure mechanism dominated by energy dissipation under high confining stress conditions.

Under low intermediate principal stress conditions, cracks are scarcely developed during the initial loading stage. As the intermediate principal stress increases, cracks begin to appear earlier during loading, with shear cracks progressively increasing during the middle and late stages. Overall, both experimental and numerical results demonstrate that confining pressure is the key factor controlling crack evolution and the transition of failure modes in marble. Increasing confining pressure not only delays crack initiation and coalescence but also promotes a shift in the material behavior from brittle to ductile failure.

Under unconfined conditions, the marble specimen exhibits a pronounced brittle failure characterized by typical splitting along the loading direction. The PFC simulation clearly illustrates the crack propagation trajectories, accurately reproducing the brittle failure process of marble under zero confining pressure. As the confining pressure increases to 5 MPa and 10 MPa, the specimen still exhibits predominantly brittle failure; however, the cracks become more localized, fewer in number, and oriented along preferred directions, indicating that confining pressure partially restrains the propagation of tensile cracks. The fragmented zones observed in the PFC simulations correspond closely to the failure planes in the experiments, demonstrating the capability of the PFC model to accurately capture the failure morphology under varying confining pressures. At confining pressures of 20 MPa and 30 MPa, the experimentally observed crack paths become increasingly complex, indicating a transition of the failure mode from brittle to quasi-plastic behavior. In the simulations, cracks are distributed in multiple intersecting directions, with localized shear-sliding features emerging. This suggests that under medium to high confining pressures, stress concentrations within the material become redistributed, limiting microcrack coalescence and shifting the dominant failure mechanism from tensile cracking to a combined tensile–shear mode. At a confining pressure of 40 MPa, the specimen exhibits more complex plastic failure behavior, with cracks and damage zones widely distributed while the overall structural integrity remains largely preserved. The PFC model reveals densely distributed but uniformly oriented cracks, with failure primarily governed by shear slip and localized formation of plastic shear bands. Overall, as confining pressure increases, the failure morphology of marble evolves from unconfined brittle splitting to high-pressure oriented shear-sliding failure, highlighting the transition from tensile-dominated to shear-dominated deformation mechanisms.

Discussion

During the initial loading stage, the external work is primarily stored within the rock mass as strain energy and bond energy. The rate of energy accumulation increases markedly with confining pressure and intermediate principal stress, demonstrating the energy-constraint effect in suppressing the nucleation of microcracks. When the applied stress approaches the yield point (approximately 90–95% of the peak strength), the system’s energy balance becomes unstable. The bond energy is rapidly released and transformed into surface energy of cracks, while both frictional and damping energies continue to rise-signaling the transition of the rock’s internal state from stable accumulation to non-steady dissipation. This energy leap corresponds to the transition of microcracks from isolated nucleation to connected coalescence, representing a critical energetic threshold in the failure process of rocks.

Mesoscopic analysis using the PFC parallel-bond model further elucidates the intrinsic correspondence between energy evolution and crack propagation mechanisms. Under low confining pressure (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{3}$$\end{document} ≤ 10 MPa), inter-particle bonds fail predominantly in tension, producing a typical splitting-type failure. Tensile cracks account for more than 70% of total cracks, and their formation is closely linked to the sudden surge in strain energy. As confining pressure increases (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{3}$$\end{document} ≥ 20 MPa), the crack morphology transforms from tensile to tensile-shear hybrid modes. The proportion of shear cracks rises from approximately 25% to nearly 40% (see Fig. 13), indicating that enhanced external confinement alters the energy release pathways, converting a larger portion of stored energy into frictional dissipation. This transition subsequently shifted the macroscopic failure mode from tensile splitting to shear sliding.

True triaxial loading enables independent control of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{1}$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{3}$$\end{document}, allowing a direct investigation of the effects of the intermediate principal stress on crack development and energy partitioning. The resulting observations are therefore more representative of anisotropic in situ stress conditions in deep underground environments. Under true triaxial loading, the failure mode of marble specimens evolves with increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document} following a “brittle-shear-re-brittle” sequence. At low \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document}, localized stress concentrations promote rapid microcrack initiation dominated by tensile opening. As \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document} increases, although tensile cracking remains the primary failure mechanism, the stress path is deflected and the failure pattern gradually transitions from fragmented damage to directional sliding along the maximum shear stress direction. At high \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\sigma }_{2}$$\end{document}, pre-existing shear bands partially evolve into new stress concentration zones, where stresses accumulate rapidly and brittle fracture regions re-emerge.

It should be noted that although the established PFC3D model shows good agreement with the laboratory test results in terms of peak strength, deformation characteristics, and crack distribution patterns, the rock specimen is represented as an assembly of spherical particles with homogeneous micromechanical properties, and the parallel bond model provides an idealized representation of cementation behavior. As a result, the quantitative analysis is subject to the inherent assumptions of the underlying model. Nevertheless, these limitations do not affect the main findings regarding the transition of crack patterns and the evolution of energy under different loading conditions in marble.

Conclusion

The failure mode transitions from tensile splitting at low confining pressures to shear-dominated failure at high confining pressures. The corresponding energy evolution indicates that increased confining pressure suppresses the early release of elastic energy, delays the arrival of the energy equilibrium point, and promotes the conversion of more external work into stored elastic energy during the early stages of failure.
