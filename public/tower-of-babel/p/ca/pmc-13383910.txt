Arthroscopy‐Assisted Fixation of Combined Coracoid Fracture and Acromioclavicular Dislocation: A Minimalist Approach
Authors: Yijun He, Wenxian Wen, Cheng Luo, Yiming Liao, Jiongfeng Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombined acromioclavicular dislocation and coracoid process fracture disrupts the superior shoulder suspensory complex, leading to shoulder instability that often requires surgical intervention. This rare injury, increasingly identified through advanced diagnostics, lacks consensus on the optimal treatment. We describe a minimalist arthroscopy‐assisted technique using a single cannulated screw for simultaneous fixation of both injuries. The procedure involves lateral decubitus positioning, diagnostic arthroscopy with a 70° scope for fracture visualization and debridement, ACL drill guide–assisted pin insertion from the clavicle through fracture fragments, and screw placement confirmed by fluoroscopy. Postoperative care includes 8 weeks of sling immobilization followed by implant removal and progressive rehabilitation. This approach offers small incisions, reduced tissue irritation, and applicability to common fracture types (e.g., Ogawa type I with Rockwood type III, Ye type II). Advantages include technical feasibility with standard tools, while limitations include risks of stiffness, antirotation instability, and secondary surgery. This method addresses the drawbacks of open techniques but requires careful patient selection and further comparative studies for long‐term validation.

The combination of an acromioclavicular (AC) dislocation and a coracoid fracture was initially reported as a rare, unusual, and often misdiagnosed injury. An increasing number of cases of this combined injury have been identified in recent decades due to advancements in diagnostic techniques and clinical awareness. 1 The superior shoulder suspensory complex (SSSC) is a ring of bones and ligaments that connects the arm to the body, playing a key role in shoulder stability and movement. 1 If only 1 part of the SSSC is injured, like a simple AC dislocation, stability is usually maintained. But when both an AC dislocation and a coracoid fracture occur together, it disrupts the SSSC at 2 points. This double break makes the shoulder unstable, which often requires surgical intervention. 2 Due to the ambiguity regarding the optimal surgical treatment, the choice of procedure often depends on the specific characteristics of the injury, the extent of associated tissue damage, and the surgeon's experience and preferences. Current literature describes 3 main surgical strategies, along with minimally invasive variations. The primary approaches include fixation of either the coracoid or the AC joint, dual fixation, and arthroscopy‐assisted techniques. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Arthroscopy may be used for coracoid fixation, to identify intra‐articular comorbidities, or to aid in fracture reduction before open fixation of the coracoid and AC joints. Although arthroscopic procedures are minimally invasive, they are also technically demanding. To address the limitations of open surgery, the technical challenges, and the uncertainty in choosing between coracoid or AC joint fixation, we describe a minimalist arthroscopy‐assisted technique that utilizes a single cannulated screw to treat this condition (Video 1).

Step‐by‐step narration of the arthroscopy‐assisted fixation technique for combined coracoid fracture and acromioclavicular dislocation. After completing a standard diagnostic shoulder arthroscopy, a 70° arthroscope is inserted through the posterior portal. The fracture area is then cleared via the superoanterior portal using a shaver and radiofrequency device. Once the hematoma is removed, a standard ACL drill guide is positioned between the distal and proximal fragments. The entry point is identified by projecting the distal clavicle onto the skin surface. A guide pin is drilled from the clavicle through both bone fragments, with placement verified by both arthroscopy and fluoroscopy. Next, a 4.5 mm drill bit is used, and its position is again confirmed by fluoroscopy. With manual assistance maintaining the correct alignment of the AC joint and fracture, a guide wire is placed, followed by insertion of a cannulated screw. Finally, subacromial decompression is performed, and reduction of the AC joint is confirmed. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70053.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUES

Preoperative Assessment

Routine imaging studies—including radiography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging—are utilized to diagnose AC dislocation and coracoid fracture, as well as to assist with surgical planning (Figure 1). Indications for this minimalist technique include acute combined injuries, particularly Ogawa type I coracoid fractures with Rockwood type III AC dislocations and Ye type II morphology, where the hinge effect facilitates reduction. Contraindications encompass chronic injuries (>3 weeks), open fractures, neurovascular compromise, or patients unfit for surgery due to comorbidities.

(a) Anteroposterior radiograph showing coracoid fracture and possible AC dislocation; (b‐d) computed tomography scan highlighting the coracoid fracture and AC dislocation; and (e,f) coronal and axial magnetic resonance imaging view of ruptured joint capsule and fracture line. (Red arrow: fracture line, Bracket: AC dislocation.)

Patient Positioning

After general anesthesia and regional block are achieved, the patient is positioned in the lateral decubitus position. An axillary roll is placed under the dependent axilla, and pillows are positioned between the legs to prevent pressure injuries. The torso and legs are secured with safety straps, and the head is kept in a neutral position with foam padding for support.

Arm Preparation and Traction

The operative arm is placed in a sterile stockinette and secured to a traction device. The arm is abducted 30° to 40° and forward flexed 15°, with 15 pounds of traction applied.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy

A standard diagnostic arthroscopy is then performed using a 30° arthroscope. The posterior portal is used as the viewing portal, while the anterosuperior portal is utilized for instrumentation. During arthroscopy, a horizontal fracture line traversing the superior glenoid is observed, consistent with preoperative computed tomography imaging (Figure 2).

Left shoulder, posterior view. Intraoperative arthroscopic view during diagnostic arthroscopy using a 30° arthroscope. (a) Standard diagnostic arthroscopy using the posterior and superoanterior portal, and (b) viewing from the posterior portal, fracture line is observed in the glenohumeral joint. (Black arrow: fracture line.) (G, glenoid; HH, humeral head.)

Fracture Site Visualization and Preparation

A 70° arthroscope is subsequently introduced through the posterior viewing portal. Following radiofrequency debridement of the rotator interval and further dissection, the base of the coracoid process and the fracture line are clearly visualized. The fragment is noted to be hinged on the remaining posterior glenoid and classified as type II according to previously reported literature. Fracture hematoma is debrided to optimize exposure (Figure 3).

Left shoulder, posterior view. Arthroscopic visualization and preparation of the fracture site using a 70° arthroscope after debridement. (a) A 70° arthroscope is used, and (b) viewing from the posterior portal, fracture hematoma is debrided for optimized exposure. (Black arrow: fracture line.) (C, coracoid; SSc, subscapularis tendon.)

Drill Guide Placement and Pin Insertion

An ACL drill guide (Wuyang, China) is then employed, through the anterosuperior portal, with its tip placed between the distal and proximal fracture fragments at the fracture line (Figure 4). The entry point is selected based on the skin projection of the distal clavicle. Two 24‐gauge needles are used to mark the anterior and posterior margins of the clavicle, and a 2‐cm skin incision is made in between. A guide pin is drilled from the clavicle through both the proximal and distal fragments toward the glenoid neck, with placement confirmed both arthroscopically and by intraoperative fluoroscopy (Figure 5). A 4.5‐mm drill bit is then introduced and its position confirmed by fluoroscopy (Figure 6a).

Fracture Reduction and Fixation

With assistance to reduce the AC joint and fracture fragments, a guide wire is introduced, followed by the placement of a cannulated screw (4.5 mm in diameter, 55 mm in length, Carefix, China), with fluoroscopy confirming proper positioning (Figure 6), and adequate fragment compression is verified through direct visualization (Figure 7). Subsequently, subacromial decompression is performed and verifies an appropriate reduction of the AC joint.

Left shoulder, posterior view. Verification of fracture compression after fixation. (a) External view of surgical incisions and (b) arthroscopic view showing adequate compression of the fracture fragments after screw placement. (Black arrow: fracture line.) (C, coracoid.)

Closure

After fixation, the arthroscope and instruments are removed, and the wound is irrigated to remove any debris. The portal and clavicle incisions are closed with No. 3‐0 Vicryl (Ethicon, USA) sutures in a subcuticular fashion and protected with adhesive tapes.

Postoperative Assessment

Anteroposterior and axial views of radiographs and computed tomography are obtained postoperatively to reassess both the fracture reduction and the position of the implant (Figure 8). Eight weeks after the initial surgery, the patient is readmitted for implant removal. A clavicular incision, following the original surgical site, is made to facilitate removal of the implant. Subsequent postoperative radiographs show preservation of reduction and evidence of fracture healing.

Left shoulder, postoperative imaging assessment. (a) Axial radiograph view showing fracture reduction and implant position; (b) anteroposterior radiograph view; (c,d) computed tomography scan confirming fracture compression and fragment alignment; and (e,f) 3D reconstruction confirming fracture compression, fragment alignment, and reduced acromioclavicular joint.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

The patient is advised to maintain immobilization with a sling for 8 weeks before rehospitalization for implant removal (Figure 9). Following the removal of the implant, pendulum exercises and gentle isometric exercises focusing on the periscapular muscles are recommended during the early postoperative period (the first 2 weeks after surgery). Then, the patient should progress to active movements, including lifting, forward flexion, and abduction.

Left shoulder, pre‐ and postoperative comparison. (a) Prominence of acromioclavicular joint observed preoperatively; (b) absence of prominence postoperatively; (c) anteroposterior radiograph view showing implant removed and fracture healing after implant removal; and (d) axial radiograph view showing healed coracoid fracture.

DISCUSSION

Operative management is indicated when multiple disruptions are observed in the SSSC, such as a combined AC dislocation and coracoid fracture. Due to the lack of consensus regarding the optimal surgical approach, various techniques have been reported. 3 , 7 Some authors suggest that the AC joint may achieve spontaneous reduction if the coracoid process is reduced and stabilized. If the AC joint remains dislocated, additional procedures are indicated. Therefore, one of the commonly used techniques is the open surgical procedures, typically involving screw fixation of the coracoid process, with or without the use of a distal clavicle hook plate. 8 Arthroscopy‐assisted procedures have also been described, in which the coracoid process is drilled with a K‐wire used as a joystick to facilitate fracture reduction under direct arthroscopic visualization. 4

In our current technique, we use a 70° arthroscope to assist with both fracture debridement and reduction. A single cannulated screw is then placed, traversing the distal clavicle and coracoid process. This minimally invasive approach achieves simultaneous reduction of both the fracture and the AC joint, while requiring only small incisions and causing less soft tissue irritation. Notably, the technique is technically feasible using only a standard ACL drill guide.

The commonly utilized classification systems are the Ogawa classification for coracoid fractures and the Rockwood system for AC dislocations. 1 , 9 , 10 Recently, Ye et al. developed a new classification system based on 3D CT reconstructions, which has been proposed to better reflect the fracture morphology and assist in predicting whether the coracoid fracture will indirectly reduce when the AC joint is surgically fixed. 11 It is notable that the combination of an Ogawa type I coracoid process fracture and a Rockwood type III AC dislocation accounts for more than 60% of all cases, which is consistent with the present case. 6 , 7 , 12 , 13 , 14 According to Ye's classification system, our case corresponds to type II, the most common type, where the posterior side of the fractured coracoid process remains attached to the scapula, and the fracture opens anteriorly, thus creating a hinge effect. 11 Therefore, our technique is suitable for the majority of combined cases.

Our technique has several limitations, as outlined below (Table 1), to provide surgeons with a comprehensive understanding of its drawbacks, and a concise list of pearls and pitfalls is provided to improve understanding and guide intraoperative technique (Table 2). Despite the minimally invasive nature of the procedure, strict postsurgical immobilization—typically for 8 weeks—can increase the risk of muscle atrophy, joint stiffness, and poor rehabilitation compliance. Additionally, the need for a second surgery to remove the implant adds to the treatment burden. Furthermore, using a single cannulated screw does not offer sufficient antirotation stability compared with dual‐screw or plate constructs. However, in our experience, this risk is partially mitigated in Ye's type II fractures due to the hinge effect.

Pearls and Pitfalls

In summary, while our minimalist arthroscopy‐assisted technique addresses many shortcomings of traditional open methods, these limitations highlight the need for careful patient selection, thorough preoperative planning, and close postoperative monitoring. Future studies should compare long‐term outcomes with alternative fixation strategies to better define their role in managing combined AC dislocation and coracoid fractures. Prospective, multicenter trials could help refine the technique and mitigate these drawbacks, potentially through adjunctive measures like preoperative 3D planning or bioabsorbable implants to eliminate the need for removal.
