Application of axial load-share ratio testing technology in the bone transport treatment of large segmental tibial bone defects
Authors: Zhao Liu, Ming Cai, Yu Gou, Xun Sun, Xuan Yang, Zhiming Zhao, Hongzhou Li, Ningning Zhang, Chengkuo Cai, Xiaolong Mei, Tao Zhang, Weiguo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone transport is a widely established method for treating tibial bone defects. However, safely removing the external fixator as early as possible to shorten treatment duration remains a clinical challenge. This study aimed to investigate whether axial load-share ratio (ALSR) testing can safely and effectively assess callus healing strength at the mineralization zone and docking site, and thereby guide the precise timing of external fixator removal during tibial bone transport.MethodsThis retrospective study included 42 adult patients with tibial bone defects treated by bone transport between 2019 and 2025. Patients were divided into the ALSR group (n = 20) and conventional group (n = 22) according to the external fixation removal strategy used. In the ALSR group, ALSR testing was performed to evaluate healing strength at the mineralization zone and docking site. In the ALSR group, staged removal of external fixation was considered when ALSR testing showed a value < 5%, in combination with clinical examination and radiographic evidence. In the conventional group, we based removal decisions on standard clinical and radiographic criteria. We compared the following clinical indicators between groups: operative time, intraoperative blood loss, bone transport duration, healing time of the mineralization zone, healing time of the docking site, external fixation duration, and external fixation index. At the final follow-up, we used the Association for the Study and Application of the Method of Ilizarov (ASAMI) scoring system to assess bone healing and functional recovery and performed relevant statistical analyses.ResultsThe median bone transport duration was 82.50 (IQR,63.5-109.5) days in the ALSR group and 71.50 (IQR,64.00- 120) days in the conventional group(P > 0.05). The median healing time of the mineralization zone was 182.00 days (IQR,155.25-219.75) in the ALSR group, significantly shorter than 205.50 days (IQR,177.25-285.75) in the conventional group (P < 0.05). The median healing time of the docking site was 207.50 days (IQR,189.00-262.50) in the ALSR group, significantly shorter than 259.50 days (IQR,222.5-332.50) in the conventional group(P < 0.05). The median external fixation duration, which was defined as the primary endpoint, was 219.00 days (IQR, 205.50–277.25) in the ALSR group, significantly shorter than 274.00 days (IQR, 238.25–347.00) in the conventional group (P < 0.05). The external fixation index was 33.25 ± 4.97 d/cm in the ALSR group, significantly lower than 40.05 ± 9.39 d/cm in the conventional group (P < 0.05). At the final follow-up, there was no statistically significant difference in ASAMI scores and complications between the two groups (P > 0.05).ConclusionFollowing bone transport surgery for large segmental tibial bone defects, ALSR testing provides a quantitative and reliable method for evaluating callus strength during bone transport. It enables earlier and safer removal of external fixation, reducing treatment duration without compromising clinical outcomes.Keywords: Tibial bone defect, External fixation, Bone transport, Axial load

Introduction

The global incidence of high-energy traumatic injuries from accidents and natural disasters has risen in recent years. These injuries pose a major threat to public health, ranking just behind cardiovascular disease and cancer as leading causes of death and disability. This growing burden has substantial implications for patient wellbeing and societal productivity [1–4]. Tibial bone defect can result from high-energy trauma, post-traumatic osteomyelitis, bone infection, nonunion, and other factors. Such defects often induce secondary pathological alterations in the bone, joints, and adjacent soft tissues, which may ultimately lead to limb dysfunction [5]. Depending on factors such as the etiology, length, and extent of the bone defect, different treatment methods can be applied for various types of tibial bone defects. For smaller bone defects without associated soft tissue damage, autologous or allogeneic bone grafting can be employed. For larger bone defects accompanied by soft tissue injury, treatment options may include vascularized fibular grafting, bone transport technique, and membrane induction technology. If a bone defect is accompanied by infection, the bone transport technique can be considered the preferred treatment method. External fixation provides rigid stabilization for the tibia, allowing for maximal resection of the infected bone segment and complete eradication of the infection [6, 7].

The bone transport technique is widely regarded as an effective method for treating large segmental bone defects due to its advantages, including minimally invasive surgery, broad indications, high adjustability, and strong bone regeneration capacity [8, 9]. Although the Ilizarov circular external fixator is the standard device for traditional bone transport, this technique is frequently associated with complications including deviation of the limb’s mechanical axis and displacement of the docking site, which can result in angular or rotational deformities [10, 11]. In recent years, the integration of the Taylor Spatial Frame (TSF) has been applied in bone transport procedures. The TSF stabilizes the transported bone segment and the docking site. Computer software precisely calculates the sliding trajectory of the transported segment, facilitating accurate docking. This approach shortens the overall treatment duration and alleviates the physical, psychological, and financial burden on patients [12–14]. Despite the efficacy of current bone transport techniques in treating tibial bone defects and the ability of TSF to mitigate residual deformities, the overall treatment process remains protracted. Consequently, determining the optimal timing for safe removal of external fixation continues to be a significant clinical challenge.

The current clinical approach to external fixation removal in bone transport relies mainly on imaging assessments of healing at the mineralization zone and docking site, along with clinical experience such as standard healing timelines and fracture healing criteria. This approach is subjective and provides no quantitative measure of healing, highlighting the need for a more objective evaluation method [15–17]. The relatively sparse muscle and soft tissue coverage of the lower leg permits the use of non-invasive external devices to measure callus strength, thus enabling assessment of tibial fracture healing status [15]. Aarnes et al. [18] installed three sensors on circular external fixators in 22 patients, assessed fracture healing strength using ALSR, and removed the external fixator when the ratio fell below 10%, with no refracture observed. Preliminary clinical studies conducted by our team also demonstrated the safety and feasibility of ALSR testing in guiding external fixation removal after tibial fractures [19]. In the present study, we adopted a more conservative threshold of ALSR < 5% for bone transport cases. It should be noted that this threshold was established not from ROC analysis or a formal biomechanical prediction model within the present cohort, but rather on the basis of prior ALSR-related studies and our preliminary clinical experience—specifically to further mitigate refracture risk in this more challenging clinical context. Consequently, our team elected to apply ALSR testing during tibial bone transport treatment. This requires regular and systematic testing of both the mineralization zone and the docking site. When the test criteria are met, the external fixator is removed gradually in phases.

We conducted a retrospective case series of 42 adult patients treated with tibial bone transport at Tianjin Hospital from 2019 to 2025. The primary aim was to assess the safety and effectiveness of ALSR testing in evaluating callus healing strength at the mineralization zone and docking site after bone transport for tibial bone defects.

Methods

Patients

The study was a retrospective observational cohort study conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital (approval number: 2025 Medical Ethics Review 105, 31 March 2025), which permitted the retrospective review and publication of the clinical data. A total of 42 patients who underwent tibial bone transport using the TSF for tibial bone defects between 2019 and 2025 were enrolled. General patient data were recorded, including gender, age, affected side, body mass index, cause of bone defect, length of bone defect and follow-up time.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) unilateral tibial bone defect not involving the knee or ankle joints, with an intact contralateral limb. (2) tibial bone defect greater than 5 cm. (3) treatment with bone transport surgery using the TSF and complete medical records. Exclusion criteria were: (1) systemic conditions affecting normal bone healing, such as tumors or renal insufficiency. (2) history of ipsilateral tibiofibular fracture with re-injury accompanied by bone defects. (3) poor compliance that precluded cooperation with postoperative follow-up.

Based on the postoperative management strategy used during different treatment periods, the patients were divided into two groups. Patients treated during 2019.3-2024.12 were assigned to the Conventional Group (n = 22), and patients treated during 2019.5-2025.4 were assigned to the ALSR Group (n = 20). The ALSR group underwent ALSR testing at the mineralization zone and docking site 12 weeks after docking site contact, which guided staged external fixation removal. The conventional group, by contrast, had TSF removal guided by traditional clinical and radiographic criteria. There were no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) in general data, except for follow-up time (Table 1). To minimize treatment variability, all patients were treated at the same institution by the same clinical team, following largely consistent surgical principles and postoperative management protocols. Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to surgery in accordance with institutional requirements.

Principle and device

Aarnes proposed that the total load during fracture healing with external fixation depends on the stiffness of both the external fixator and the regenerated callus [18]. ALSR was originally developed to assess fracture healing. It operates on the principle that the total external load (Ft) applied to the affected limb is distributed between the external fixation frame (Ff) and the bone (Fr), where Fr is primarily determined by callus strength. As callus mineralization progresses during healing, the external load shifts from the frame to the bone, and the ALSR ratio (Fr/Ft) declines from an initial 100%. A ratio below 10% indicates that callus strength is adequate to support the limb load and that external fixation can be removed. Therefore, ALSR serves as an indirect, objective indicator of the load-bearing capacity of the healing callus.

For clinical testing, three sensor-equipped test rods are attached to the external fixation frame, which provides sufficient stability to safely complete the procedure. The total load borne by the frame (Ff) is distributed among the three rods as forces Ff1, Ff2, and Ff3 (Fig. 1A). The ALSR tester used in this study was jointly developed by Tianjin Hospital and Tianjin Daxihuai Precision Machinery Co., Ltd. (Fig. 2). Axial load sharing by the external fixation frame (forces Ff1, Ff2, and Ff3) was measured using force sensors (1000 N maximum load, model JLBM-01, Zhongwan Jinnuo, China). The applied axial load, equal to the reaction force, was measured as Ft by an electronic mechanical platform. The ALSR ratio was calculated as (Ff1 + Ff2 + Ff3) / Ft × 100%. The integrated data tester communicated with the three sensors and the electronic platform, eliminating the need for separate software to record and analyze mechanical data by directly calculating and displaying the ALSR ratio. The sensor testing device also featured automatic calibration, reducing measurement errors while ensuring test validity. The complete testing setup is shown in Fig. 1B.

Surgical technique

The same treatment team performed the tibial bone transport procedure using the TSF in all patients, regardless of group allocation.

Preoperative comprehensive assessments were performed, including a thorough medical risk evaluation and multidisciplinary consultation to exclude surgical contraindications. In patients presenting with open fractures or osteomyelitis, microbial culture and antibiotic sensitivity testing of preoperative wound exudate were conducted to inform the selection of pathogen-sensitive antimicrobial regimens. Additionally, single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) was employed to accurately localize and delineate the extent of bone and soft tissue infections.

Intraoperative debridement was performed under direct visualization, with complete removal of inflammatory granulation tissue, infected bone, and necrotic bone segments. Non-viable soft tissue was systematically excised in a layered fashion, extending from the superficial fascial layer to the deep muscles and bone. The resection margin was determined based on preoperative lesion localization; the lesion was excised together with 2 mm of adjacent normal soft tissue and 5 mm of normal bone, until the bone ends demonstrated a uniform “paprika sign” on the cortical surface, which defines the boundary of viable bone tissue [20]. A subperiosteal minimally invasive osteotomy was performed to preserve bone end blood supply. The procedure was uneventful. Deep tissue specimens from three anatomical layers around the wound were collected aseptically for aerobic and anaerobic culture and susceptibility testing. The wound was closed with skin when possible; negative pressure wound therapy was used when closure was challenging. Threaded half-pins inserted perpendicular to the tibial shaft in different planes fixed both fracture ends, resisting shear forces and ensuring fracture stability [21].

Postoperative management

Postoperative management followed a standardized protocol. Antimicrobial therapy, guided by intraoperative tissue culture results, was administered on a sensitivity-based basis. On day 2 after surgery, patients initiated static quadriceps contractions and functional exercises of the knee and ankle joints. Bone transport commenced on day 7 according to the electronic prescription, at a rate of 1 mm per day delivered in four intervals. Non-weight-bearing walker-assisted gait training began on day 14. Following completion of bone transport, patients gradually transitioned to partial weight-bearing walking. Routine pin site care was performed throughout the treatment period. Radiographic evaluation of the affected limb was conducted every 4 weeks to monitor tibial alignment, docking status, and mineralization zone quality. Interventions were implemented in both groups at 12 weeks after successful contact at the docking site.

ALSR group

ALSR testing of the mineralization zone and docking site was performed 12 weeks after docking site contact, provided that physical examination revealed no significant abnormal movement or axial percussion pain in the affected limb.

The patient was positioned supine on the treatment bed while the operator stood beside the limb. Three test rods were connected between two rings of the TSF, aligned perpendicular to the proximal standard ring and parallel to the tibia. A specially designed universal connector (Fig. 2A) ensured alignment of the mechanical sensors with the bone’s longitudinal axis, simplifying test setup by requiring only six connection rods to be loosened—eliminating the cumbersome disassembly of previous procedures and reducing measurement time. Once the testing apparatus was connected, the six quick-release rods of the TSF were gradually loosened, linking the three sensors (Fig. 2B), the mechanical platform, and the newly developed tester (Fig. 2C). The patient was then asked to sit up, allowing the affected limb to hang freely, and the tester was calibrated. Next, the patient stood on the mechanical platform and applied successive vertical forces of 30, 40, and 50 kg, while the corresponding ALSR values were displayed on the tester (Fig. 2D); the maximum ALSR value (ALSRmax) was recorded. Using the method described above, sequential testing was performed for both the bone transport mineralization zone and the docking site.

Previous ALSR-related studies have commonly used a threshold of approximately 10% to guide external fixation removal. In the present study, we selected a more conservative threshold of ALSR < 5% for bone transport cases, aiming to further reduce the risk of refracture. This threshold was based on prior literature and our preliminary clinical experience, rather than on ROC analysis or a formal biomechanical prediction model derived from this cohort. The decision to proceed with staged frame removal was not based on ALSR alone, but was made in conjunction with physical examination findings, imaging evidence, and the planned stepwise dynamization or removal protocol. When ALSRmax < 5% was achieved and imaging evidence supported bone healing, a simulated frame removal was performed using a “full dynamization” strategy: all six connecting rods of the TSF were completely loosened to eliminate axial support from the frame, allowing the callus to receive mechanical stimulation under full weight-bearing conditions. Patients were instructed to maintain normal daily activities while wearing the frame. If no abnormalities were observed at the fracture site after two weeks, the external fixation components meeting removal criteria could be dismantled. If ALSRmax ≥ 5%, patients returned for outpatient follow-up after four weeks for repeat ALSR testing until ALSRmax < 5% was achieved. Following this protocol, the external fixation was removed in a stepwise, phased manner until completely dismantled. The affected limb was then protected with a lower leg splint for two weeks. Conventional group

At 12 weeks after docking site contact, if physical examination revealed no significant abnormal movement or axial percussion pain in the affected limb, the six connecting rods were loosened. Three threaded straight rods were then connected between the two rings using universal auxiliary connectors. A vertical external force was applied to the affected limb to simulate the testing process, and sequential testing was performed on the mineralization zone and the docking site. Subsequent X-rays were taken to assess callus growth at the fracture site. The affected limb was also evaluated against the clinical healing criteria for lower limb fractures [22]: The clinical criteria for simulated frame removal were as follows: absence of local tenderness and longitudinal percussion pain; no abnormal local movement; radiographic evidence of continuous callus at the fracture site with blurred fracture lines and clear three-cortical shadows; and the ability to walk continuously on level ground for 3 min without crutches (at least 30 steps) without pain or other abnormal reactions. After verification and approval by two senior chief physicians in the department with extensive experience in external fixation, simulated frame removal was performed. If no abnormalities were observed after two weeks, the external fixation components meeting the criteria were dismantled. This process continued until the entire external fixation was removed.

Observation indicators

The following data were recorded for both groups: operative time, intraoperative blood loss, bone transport duration, healing time of the mineralization zone, healing time of the docking site, external fixation duration, and the external fixation index. External fixation duration was defined as the primary endpoint of the study. The remaining outcomes, including ASAMI scores and postoperative complications, were treated as secondary endpoints. For both groups, anteroposterior and lateral X-rays of the affected lower leg, as well as full-length anteroposterior X-rays of both lower limbs, were obtained preoperatively and during postoperative follow-up. Deformity parameters and overall lower limb alignment were recorded. For the ALSR group, data related to ALSR testing of the mineralization zone and docking site—including the number of tests performed and test values—were also recorded.

After TSF removal, all patients received a splint for external fixation and protection of the affected limb for two weeks. They were advised to avoid unnecessary weight-bearing activities on the affected limb. At two weeks post-removal, follow-up outpatient X-rays were obtained to confirm the absence of any refracture tendency, after which regular functional exercises were initiated. A final assessment of limb function was conducted six months after external fixation removal. At the final follow-up, bone healing and limb function were evaluated for both groups according to the ASAMI scoring criteria. The occurrence of complications in both groups was also recorded. Because of the retrospective design and the visible differences in frame management, blinding was not fully feasible. To address potential detection bias, two independent orthopedic surgeons, blinded to group allocation, reviewed anonymized radiographs and clinical records. According to the ASAMI criteria, bone healing evaluation includes four indicators: degree of bone healing, presence or absence of infection, angular correction of deformities, and severity of lower limb length discrepancy. The prognosis is classified into four grades: excellent, good, fair, and poor. ASAMI lower limb function evaluation comprises six indicators: recovery of joint range of motion, degree of pain, presence or absence of claudication, local soft tissue condition, knee and ankle joint contracture angles, and the reduction in range of motion of the knee and ankle joints. Prognosis is similarly classified into four grades: excellent, good, fair, and poor [23].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 23.0. Continuous variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent-samples t-test. Skewed continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. External fixation duration was predefined as the primary endpoint, while the remaining outcomes were considered secondary endpoints. A post-hoc power analysis for the primary endpoint was performed following re-analysis of the raw data. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

All patients received regular follow-up. The median follow-up duration was 395.50 days (IQR, 381.50–437.50) in the ALSR group and 453.50 days (IQR, 416.00–523.25) in the conventional group. This variable is presented as a descriptive study characteristic rather than an independent treatment outcome. The median bone transport duration was 82.50 days (IQR, 63.5–109.5) in the ALSR group and 71.50 days (IQR, 64.0–120.0) in the conventional group (P > 0.05). The median healing time of the mineralization zone was 182.00 days (IQR,155.25-219.75) in the ALSR group, significantly shorter than 205.50 days (IQR,177.25-285.75) in the conventional group (P < 0.05). The median healing time of the docking site was 207.50 days (IQR,189.00-262.50) in the ALSR group, significantly shorter than 259.50 days (IQR,222.5-332.50) in the conventional group(P < 0.05). The median external fixation duration, which was defined as the primary endpoint, was 219.00 days (IQR, 205.50–277.25) in the ALSR group, significantly shorter than 274.00 days (IQR, 238.25–347.00) in the conventional group (P < 0.05). The external fixation index was 33.25 ± 4.97 d/cm in the ALSR group, significantly lower than 40.05 ± 9.39 d/cm in the conventional group (P < 0.05), as shown in Table 2.

In the ALSR group, the following complications were observed: pin tract infection (4 cases), deep infection (2 cases), delayed union (1 case), axial deviation (1 case), and joint stiffness (2 cases). No refracture was reported. The overall complication rate was 50%. Pin tract infections resolved following regular disinfection and standardized antibiotic therapy. Deep infections were successfully managed with debridement surgery and regular antibiotic administration. Joint stiffness demonstrated improved range of motion after specialized rehabilitation training. In the conventional group, postoperative complications included pin tract infection (5 cases), deep infection (2 cases), delayed union (2 cases), axial deviation (1 case), joint stiffness (2 cases), and refracture (1 case), yielding an overall complication rate of 59%. Pin tract infections and deep infections were managed similarly to those in the ALSR group, with resolution achieved. Joint stiffness also improved with rehabilitation. For the cases of delayed union at the docking site, the accordion technique (gradual compression and traction) was applied, achieving union without the need for surgical grafting. The single refracture case was treated with continued TSF external fixation and sustained compression at the fracture site, ultimately achieving fracture healing. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in complications, this may be due to the relatively short duration of post-operative follow-up. Discussion

In the present study, ALSR-guided staged removal for bone transport with TSF was associated with shorter healing time of the mineralization zone, shorter healing time of the docking site, reduced external fixation duration, and a lower external fixation index, without an observed increase in refracture during follow-up. These findings suggest that ALSR may provide useful quantitative support for determining the timing of staged external fixator removal.

To shorten the external fixation period, several scholars have proposed various modified techniques, such as bidirectional bone transport, shortening followed by lengthening, and bone transport combined with intramedullary nailing or plate fixation [24–29]. However, all the above methods aim to reduce external fixation time by incorporating additional surgical strategies or combining them with other fixation techniques, which increases the complexity of both the surgical procedure and postoperative rehabilitation and lack a quantitative assessment of fracture healing at the mineralization zone and docking site. As the stiffness of the callus at the mineralization zone and docking site increases, the bone will eventually restore its load-bearing capacity. Beyond this stage, the fracture site no longer requires rigid fixation, as excessive stability could otherwise impair healing at these sites [30–32]. Therefore, the timely and effective sequential removal of fixation devices from the mineralization zone and docking site remains a key clinical issue. Premature removal of the external fixator can easily lead to refracture at these sites, while prolonged use increases the risk of complications and poor mineralization or absorption of the mineralization zone. Although imaging modalities such as two-plane X-rays, DEXA, CT, MRI, and ultrasound are widely used in clinical practice and considered the gold standard for fracture healing assessment, they do not directly provide biomechanical information [33–36]. Quantitative assessment for the safe removal of external fixation has garnered significant attention as a research focus. Methods include quantitative technetium scintigraphy, X-ray pixel value analysis, mechanical vibration analysis, and ALSR testing, among others [16, 18, 37, 38]. Some researchers have developed portable external sensor devices that connect to fixator struts, enabling real-time fracture healing monitoring until fixator removal. While these devices permit quasi-real-time, autonomous, and remote operation in home care settings, their practical applicability remains limited [39].

Several methods have been developed to objectively assess fracture healing and guide external fixation removal using ALSR principles. Aarnes et al. first introduced the concept of using structural stiffness decay to determine fixation removal timing. Subsequent studies have refined this approach: Liu et al. [40] assessed callus hardness in vivo by attaching circular frame components, providing a quantitative indicator for unilateral external fixation. Seide et al. [41] developed a six-rod testing device capable of independently measuring three-dimensional forces and moments throughout the healing process. Fu et al. [42] applied dynamic load sharing ratio testing using a wearable gait analyzer to guide safe frame removal. While these techniques have advanced the field, several differences distinguish our approach. First, previous methods were validated primarily in simple tibial fractures rather than in complex bone transport patients, who have additional healing interfaces (docking site and mineralization zone). Second, our integrated testing device requires no additional computational processing and is simpler to operate than previous versions [19], potentially improving clinical adoptability. Third, we used a threshold of ALSR < 5% to initiate simulated frame removal, followed by a two-week observation period before sequential removal. This protocol differs from prior studies that typically used absolute stiffness thresholds or load-sharing ratios without a staged approach. Importantly, no refracture occurred in the ALSR group during follow-up, supporting the clinical feasibility of the 5% ALSR threshold as a conservative quantitative reference for staged removal in bone transport patients. Nevertheless, this study did not formally evaluate the diagnostic performance of the 5% ALSR threshold, including its sensitivity and specificity for predicting refracture risk. Future studies should compare alternative ALSR thresholds (5%, 10%, 15%) to determine the optimal balance between early frame removal and refracture prevention.

Dynamization during fracture healing refers to a therapeutic technique that stimulates biological responses at the fracture ends through controlled micromovement or dynamic fixation, thereby accelerating healing [43, 44]. By implementing ALSR-guided early dynamization, this study successfully alleviated stress shielding from rigid fixation and stimulated demand-driven trabecular bone remodeling [45]. Seide et al. [41] further reported that such a testing system can automatically regulate callus mechanics (“automatic dynamization”) and guide patients on safe limb loading (“smart fixator”), thereby enhancing fracture healing. In this study, ALSR testing was conducted 12 weeks after docking site alignment, after which the six TSF rods were loosened. This maneuver avoided stress shielding at the mineralization zone and docking site and completed the dynamization phase. This observation aligns with Perren’s strain theory, where controlled micromotion stimulates callus formation. The ALSR-induced dynamic loading likely increases micromotion at the docking site, accelerating healing, lowering nonunion risk, and reducing frame time [44]. This may partly explain why patients in the ALSR group had a shorter external fixation duration compared with the conventional group, and ALSR technology can also serve as a treatment strategy for precise dynamization and active intervention in fracture healing. However, because the two groups were treated during different time periods, part of the observed difference may also have been influenced by temporal factors beyond the intervention itself. Meanwhile, there were no statistically significant between-group differences in ASAMI bone healing scores, functional scores, or postoperative complications. These findings should not be interpreted as demonstrating equivalence, as the study was underpowered for these secondary outcomes due to the limited sample size. Therefore, the observed similarities should be interpreted with caution.

The mineralization zone benefits from a rich blood supply and active osteogenesis, which promote faster bone healing. By contrast, the docking site is associated with a higher risk of nonunion or refracture due to soft tissue interposition, sclerotic bone formation, and poor vascularity. Consistent with this, within-group analyses in the ALSR group showed that the mineralization zone required fewer tests and achieved shorter healing and external fixation times than the docking site. While conventional X-rays cannot reliably capture such subtle strength differences, ALSR technology enables region-specific, independent quantification, which helps avoid premature fixator removal before the docking site has healed adequately. However, because the mineralization zone and docking site differ in both biological environment and mechanical properties, whether a single ALSR threshold is appropriate for both sites remains to be validated in future studies.

This study still has the following limitations: First, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size. Although the primary endpoint reached statistical significance, the study was likely underpowered for secondary outcomes, including ASAMI scores and postoperative complications. Therefore, the non-significant findings for these secondary outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent clinical performance between the two groups. Future studies with larger sample sizes, prospective multicenter designs, and longer, more detailed follow-up are warranted to validate these findings. Second, ALSR should not be used as the sole indicator of fracture healing in bone transport. In this study, fixation removal decisions also considered fracture-site signs, imaging findings, and a staged dynamization/removal protocol. Moreover, the ALSR < 5% threshold was not derived from ROC analysis or a formal biomechanical prediction model in this cohort, and we did not formally evaluate its sensitivity or specificity for predicting refracture risk. Future studies should establish more standardized evaluation procedures and determine whether the mineralization zone and docking site require different ALSR thresholds. Thirdly, blinding of outcome assessment was not fully feasible, which may have introduced assessment bias, particularly for subjective or semi-subjective outcomes such as functional evaluation and complication adjudication.

Conclusion

In conclusion, regular ALSR testing of the mineralization zone and docking site safely and effectively guides external fixation removal after TSF bone transport for large segmental tibial defects. This approach reduces external fixation time, treatment duration, and costs, without adversely affecting clinical outcomes.
