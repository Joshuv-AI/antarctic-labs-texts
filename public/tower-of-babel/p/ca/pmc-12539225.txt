Complicated septic arthritis of the sternoclavicular joint due to Bacteroides fragilis in a healthy middle-aged woman; A case report and literature review
Authors: Aseel H Alzibdeh, Omar Aljaziri, Arun P Nair, Sreethish Sasi, Muna Almaslamani, Hamad Abdel Hadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAmongst bone and joints infections, native joints septic arthritis is uncommon, and involvement of the sternoclavicular joint (SCJ) is rare. Bacteroides fragilis is an unusual cause of septic arthritis in general and involvement of the SCJ in even more unusual. Urgent abscess drainage and washout of the SCJ was necessary to control local complications, however anaerobic cultures of the fluid were negative after 14 days of incubation. Background

Amongst bone and joints infections, native joint septic arthritis (NJSA) is an uncommon condition with an incidence rate estimated to be around 12 per 100,000 person-years [1]. Septic arthritis of the sternoclavicular joint (SCJ) is even rarer, occurring in only 0.5–1.0 % of all cases of infectious septic arthritis [2]. Predisposing factors to SCJ septic arthritis include intravenous drugs use (21 %), metastatic infection from distant primary foci (15 %), diabetes mellitus (13 %), trauma (12 %) and chronic renal disease (8 %) [3], [4]. Pathogens associated with NJSA are usually gram-positive bacteria while gram negative bacteria and anaerobes are rare; usually associated with existing medical conditions or resulting from local extension [5]. Of culture-proven septic arthritis of the SCJ, Staphylococcus aureus is the most common isolated pathogen, followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Brucella melitensis, and Escherichia coli [3], [6]. Other less commonly isolated pathogens include group B streptococcus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Streptococcus pneumoniae [6], [7]. Anaerobic causative pathogens of NJSA are extremely rare; a review of 374 cases spanning three decades reported only 2 cases [8].

Bacteroides fragilis is an obligate anaerobe and is part of the normal flora in the oral cavity and gastrointestinal tracts. Although usually commensal, the pathogen can cause a number of different infections such as skin and soft tissue infections, intrabdominal infections, primary or secondary bacteremia, infective endocarditis and less commonly osteoarticular infections [9]. The prevalence of NJSA caused by Bacteroides fragilis has not been established, but it is exceedingly rare [9]. Some studies and case series reported sporadic cases of Bacteroides fragilis septic arthritis occurring in different joints, demonstrating the ability of the organism to cause significant disease [9].

Prompt evaluation with computerized tomography (CT) scan and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is important since SCJ septic arthritis can be associated with local complications including osteomyelitis at 55 % of cases, abscesses of the chest wall at 25 %, and mediastinitis at 13 % [5], [7]. Management entails urgent surgical drainage and debridement in addition to antibiotic therapy since there is high risk of treatment failure with conservative management alone [10]. The purpose of this report is to share our experience with diagnosing and managing a healthy middle-aged woman who developed SCJ septic arthritis with Bacteroides fragilis confirmed through conventional microbiological tests, aided with genomic studies.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old Sri Lankan lady with no significant past medical history presented to the emergency department with fever, chills and progressively worsening tender swelling at the base of her neck for 10 days. She had undergone a tooth extraction two weeks prior to presentation. Upon initial assessment, she was febrile with a temperature of 38.8 °C, blood pressure of 112/79 mm Hg and tachycardiac with a heart rate of 105 beats per minute. Physical exam revealed a large tender swollen area of about 2 cm in diameter with surrounding erythema overlying the right SCJ extending into the base of the neck. Examination of her oral cavity revealed poor oral hygiene. There were no limitations of the shoulder movements. Laboratory investigations revealed elevated inflammatory markers; white blood cell count of 14.6 cells /u l, C- reactive protein level of 167.7 mg/L and procalcitonin level of 0.58 ng/mL. Screening for secondary immune dysfunction and potential underlying TB infections, revealed negative serology for HIV and QuantiFERON gold tests. A glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level was 5.6 %. Despite the probability of an underlying infection, preliminary blood cultures were negative for pathogens, and a plain chest radiograph did not reveal any abnormalities. An ultrasound (US) of the affected area, however, revealed a hypoechoic heterogenous structure measuring about 2 cm in diameter overlying the right infraclavicular region suggestive of an abscess. In view of these findings, intravenous piperacillin-tazobactam was started empirically together with IV paracetamol for pain control. Subsequently, a computerized tomography scan (CT) revealed right sternoclavicular joint septic arthritis with local extension into an abscess surrounded by myositis (Fig. 1). A magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a multiloculated abscess involving the distal insertion of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. On day 6 of management, anaerobic blood cultures collected at presentation grew sensitive Bacteroides fragilis, but the patient was kept on the same antimicrobial regimen. The source of the bacteremia was initially not identified. Following radiological evaluation, open drainage and joint washout was carried out for the right sternoclavicular joint while the surrounding areas underwent debridement. Intraoperatively, a copious amount of purulent fluid was observed, as well as destruction of the medial aspect of the sternoclavicular joint and clavicle. Anaerobic cultures were incubated for 14 days and showed no growth. Molecular testing by broad range 16S rRNA gene PCR and sequencing detected Bacteroides fragilis. The bacterium was identical to the isolate which grew from blood cultures. The patient received intravenous (IV) piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 h, later switched to IV amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 1.2 g every 8 h for 2 weeks, followed by oral amoxicillin-clavulanic acid 875/125 mg every 12 h for 4 weeks. A repeat CT scan confirmed full resolution of soft tissue swelling, collection and fat stranding along the right sternoclavicular joint.

There is also evidence of myositis in the right pectoralis major muscle.

Discussion

Septic arthritis of the SCJ can be caused by a broad range of organisms, most commonly; Staphylococcus aureus followed by Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli [3], [6]. Other less commonly isolated pathogens include group B streptococcus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and Streptococcus pneumoniae [6], [7]. In contrast, anaerobes are rarely isolated as the causative pathogen [8]. With most cases of anaerobic arthritis occurring in patients who have undergone surgical treatment for some traumatic injury or musculoskeletal surgery [10]. Bacteroides fragilis, while common as gut and oral flora, appears in only a fraction of reported cases, roughly 0.6 % in one large review of native joint infections [9]. Most of those cases involved the knee joint, often with multiple joints affected and a considerable proportion presented with concomitant bacteremia [8], [9], [11].

The pathogenesis in reported series often points toward hematogenous spread from an intra-abdominal source, although oral seeding has been described less often [12]. In our patient, the temporal link with a dental procedure makes transient bacteremia a plausible explanation. This route, though unusual, aligns with the organism’s ability to exploit disruptions in mucosal barriers.

Diagnosis is not always straightforward [9]. Blood cultures are positive in around two-thirds of published cases which makes them an important diagnostic tool when evaluating SCJ infection [13].

If there is a high degree of clinical suspicion despite negative cultures, incubating cultures for longer periods is advisable since fastidious, slow growing organisms and fungal pathogens, require prolonged incubation to increase yield of cultures [14]. Synovial fluid culture is more reliable, but culture negative cases still occur [15]. For that reason, molecular tools such as broad-range PCR and 16S rRNA sequencing have been suggested as valuable adjuncts [16].

The management of SCJ infection follows those of NJSA with an appropriate combination of medical and surgical management [17]. Medical therapy entails initiating appropriate parenteral antimicrobials based on suspected organisms [18]. When suspecting or having confirmed anaerobic isolates, anaerobic antimicrobial coverage is essential with metronidazole, quinolones such as moxifloxacin, lincomycins such as clindamycin, b-lactam b-lactamase inhibitors (BLBLIs) such as amoxicillin-clavulanic acid, or piperacillin-tazobactam [19]. Metronidazole remains the most reliable antibiotic for anaerobic pathogens in view of reported emerging resistance to other antibiotics [19]. Duration of therapy depends on the severity of infection and clinical response to therapy, ranging from 4 to 6 weeks [20]. Early switch from IV to oral therapy has been reported as safe once the patient shows improvement in clinical symptoms [20].

Surgery remains an important part of therapy, particularly when there is abscess formation, osteomyelitis, or extensive joint destruction [21]. Patients who were initially managed with antibiotics alone experienced treatment failure which later required surgery and longer duration of antibiotics [22]. Surgical interventions ranged from simple limited bone and soft tissue debridement to extensive en bloc resection of the joint [23], [24], [25]. Compared to simple debridement, en bloc resection provided a better chance of resolving the infection [26]. Hospital length of stay can be long ranging from 5 to 30 days depending on the extent of the infection, type of surgery and post operative complications [24].

Our case contributes to this small body of literature by documenting sternoclavicular involvement, a location seldom affected by anaerobic pathogens. It highlights the need to think of anaerobes when joint sepsis follows gastrointestinal or dental procedures, and to ensure that cultures are obtained under conditions that permit their growth. Prompt recognition, targeted antimicrobial therapy, and timely surgical management remain the key to preserving function and preventing serious complications.

Conclusion

Amongst NJSA, involvement of SCJ is rare and infection secondary to Bacteroides fragilis is uncommon. Hematogenous seeding and extension from locally infected sites are the most common causes. Prompt clinical, biochemical, microbiological and radiological evaluation is mandatory for safe outcomes. In difficult cases, use of genomic methods is beneficial to accurately establish diagnosis and aid management. Safe outcome for SCJ septic arthritis entails combined medical and surgical management in the form of appropriate antimicrobial therapy and surgical drainage and debridement to control local complications and disease extension. Monitoring the resolution of symptoms is important to avoid complications.
