The association between anxiety, activity performance and nomophobia in students
Authors: Ayşe Göktaş, Alev Üstündağ
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to examine the association of nomophobia on activity participation and anxiety. This cross-sectional and descriptive study was conducted between March and June of 2024 with a sample of students studying at the Faculty of Health Sciences of the University. The Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q), the Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM) and the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) were utilized for data collection. Of the 382 students who participated in the study, 93.2% were female. Female students demonstrated a mean score of 67.21 ± 16.93(min-max:20–100) on the NMP-Q scale, with a range from 20 to 100; a mean score of 6.65 ± 2.2424 (min-max:0.00–10.00) on the COPM, with a range from 0.00 to 10.00; and a mean score of 41.0 ± 10.67(min-max:20–68) on the STAI, with a range from 20 to 68. The findings of the correlation analysis revealed a significant, negative relationship between the level of activity performance among students and their levels of anxiety (rpb=-0.127, p < 0.005) and nomophobia (rpb=-0.147, p < 0.001). As the levels of nomophobia among students increased, there was a 19.7% decrease in performance levels (ß=-0.197; R2 = 0.290), a 7.9% decrease in activity satisfaction levels (ß=-0.079; R2 = 0.290), and a 6.9% increase in anxiety levels (ß=0.069; R2 = 0.290). The findings of the study indicate that nomophobia in students negatively affects activity participation and leads to elevated anxiety levels. Given the high rate of mobile device use in the young population, it can be posited that a new public health concern is emerging. Occupational therapists can design activity programs that can enhance activity performance and reduce anxiety. They can also encourage students to participate in sports or artistic activities to improve peer interactions.

Introduction

Smartphones are considered to be among the most important technological devices today. The facilitation of socialisation through online communication and social media platforms, as well as the possibility of using them without time and space constraints, renders smartphones an indispensable tool for students. The absence of these devices would therefore entail a loss of an addictive and pleasurable environment. The term ‘nomophobia’ is derived from the term ‘no mobile phobia’ and refers to the extreme fear of being disconnected from mobile communication and the constant checking of one’s mobile phone. This phenomenon is accompanied by the fear of being deprived of the mobile phone in any way1–4. This situation, which is conceptualized as a contemporary addiction, has become widespread, particularly with the increasing use of smartphones. The societal desire to be contactable and accessible at all times makes smartphones indispensable. Therefore, the absence of a mobile phone or the inability to communicate via the phone can engender a state of psychological distress in individuals, resulting in a sense of disconnection. In the absence of mobile phones, individuals may experience high levels of anxiety and discomfort1–4. In this context, the controlled use of smartphones is of great importance.

The fact that students do not want to give up their smartphones, to have them with them at all times, and frequently look at their phones in all environments, indicates that they pay a great deal of attention to this object. This passion increases anxiety levels, thereby reducing an individual’s capacity to concentrate on their daily work. In particular, it has been demonstrated that the habit of leaving the phone on at night and checking it frequently has the potential to induce feelings of anxiety. This situation has been shown to have a negative association on students’s behaviour, lifestyle, psychosocial status, communication methods, learning activities and attention5. According to the findings of one particular study, the adverse effects of nomophobia on university students include psychosocial problems such as stress, depression, avoidance, hostility, obsessive behaviour, loneliness, academic performance problems and insomnia5.

In this context, the controlled use of smartphones assumes significant importance. As students do not want to give up their smartphones, they carry them with them all the time, which has been demonstrated to give rise to performance problems in the daily activities of university students5. A body of research has shown that participants encounter challenges in many domains, including sleeping, studying, doing sports and meeting friends5. It has also been highlighted that the relationship between nomophobia and health conditions such as sleep quality, sleep disturbance, fatigue, short-term memory problems, dizziness, hypertension, obsessive-compulsive disorder, interpersonal sensitivity and depression is a worrying one6,7. However, no study on the correlation between anxiety and activity participation in university students with nomophobia was found in the literature. Although the intervention studies by Rodríguez García et al.5 and Torpil and Pekçetin (2021) emphasised that nomophobia and activity performance parameters should be studied more comprehensively, no recommendations or studies investigating the effects of nomophobia on activity participation and anxiety were presented8.

To our knowledge, there are a number of studies in the extant literature that examine the relationship between nomophobia and anxiety. However, very few have assessed the direct effects of nomophobia on activity performance, satisfaction and anxiety8although some studies have addressed issues such as smartphone addiction and internet addiction2,9,10. A review of the research indicates that the studies on nomophobia are limited. It is acknowledged that the first research on this issue in Turkey was conducted in 2015, and nomophobia and activity performance, satisfaction and anxiety were not examined together. The paucity of research in this area, particularly in relation to the influence on nomophobia and activity performance among students, suggests the idea that variables such as smartphone use, nomophobia, activity participation and anxiety may influence each other. It is therefore argued that the present study has the potential to make a significant contribution to the field4. This study sought to ascertain the correlation between anxiety, activity performance, and nomophobia in students.

Method

Research design and participants

This cross-sectional and descriptive study was conducted between March and June 2024 with students studying at the Faculty of Health Sciences of a university in Ankara, Turkey. The study was conducted using the web survey platform Google Forms. The survey link was shared via social media, including Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp. Students aged 17 and over, of both genders, who owned a smartphone or tablet, spent most of their time on electronic screens and were interested in participating in the study were included in the study. All participants were briefed on the methodology and procedures before the evaluation. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by an institutional ethics committee.The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines of University. After the purpose of the study was explained to all participants in person, those who volunteered were asked to sign an informed consent form before completing the questionnaire. Participants were informed about the purpose, duration, and procedures of the study; their right to refuse participation and to withdraw from the study at any time. In addition to the verbal explanation, they were asked to read the consent form, which included all information about the research, fill out the necessary information (I have read and I approve), and sign it.

The sample size was determined using G*POWER 3.1.9.7 statistical software. The study group consisted of 382 volunteer students who agreed to participate in the study11.

Data collection tools

The Personal Information Form, Nomophobia Questionnaire (NMP-Q), Canadian Occupational Performance Measure (COPM) and The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) were employed in the study.

Personal information form

This form was designed by the researchers to determine the demographic information of the students participating in the study.

The nomophobia questionnaire (NMP-Q)

In certain studies, the Nomophobia Questionnaire developed by Yıldırım and Correira (2015) and adapted to Turkish by Yıldırım et al. (2015) was utilised to assess the levels of smartphone addiction and internet use and nomophobia scores of students4. This scale comprises a total of 20 items and includes four sub-dimensions: Inability to access information, Loss of convenience, Loss of connection and Inability to communicate. The level of nomophobia is determined by the total score obtained from the scale. A score of 20 points or below indicates the absence of nomophobia, whereas a score of 21 points or above indicates the presence of nomophobia.

Canadian occupational performance measure (COPM)

It is a standardised tool to identify problems in activity performance, and to measure individuals’ perceptions of performance change and satisfaction. The Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Torpil in 201712. The semi-structured scale was designed to identify problems individuals encounter in the domain of activity performance and to assess their self-perceived performance and satisfaction.

In the initial phase, individuals identify problematic activities that they have difficulty doing or desire to perform in areas such as self-care, productivity and leisure in their daily lives. In the second step of the process, each of these activities is assigned an importance value between 1 and 10, where 1 is ‘not at all important’ and 10 is ‘very important’. In the third step, the individual selects a minimum of 1 and a maximum of 5 activities that they consider most important and assigns a performance and satisfaction score between 1 and 10 to each activity. The mean performance and satisfaction scores are then calculated by adding together the scores for each activity and dividing this by the number of activities that the individual deems important12.

The State-Trait anxiety inventory (STAI)

Spielberger’s The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), which was adapted into Turkish and validated by Öner and Le Comte13contains a total of 20 questions. The values assigned to each question range from 1 to 4, with − 1 representing ‘not at all’, -2 indicating ‘a little’, -3 denoting ‘quite’ and − 4 signifying ‘completely’. The questionnaire comprises 10 direct statements and 10 indirect statements, with equal weighting given to each type. Scores on the scale range from 20 to 80. Scores in the high range (approximately 80) are indicative of elevated levels of state anxiety, whereas scores in the low range (approximately 20) are indicative of reduced levels of state anxiety.

In the reliability analyses, the reliability coefficients obtained by alpha correlations, which is an improved version of the Kuder-Richardson 20 formula, were found to be between 0.83 and 0.87. The reliability correlations for the items ranged from 0.34 to 0.72. Furthermore, the test-retest reliability coefficients were found to be between 0.26 and 0.68. The response options in the State Anxiety Scale are answered by marking one of the following options: (1) not at all, (2) a little, (3) a lot, or (4) completely, according to the severity of the emotions or behaviours involved13.

Data collection and analysis

The data was collected during the students’s leisure at school, outside of school hours. The data were collected online from participants via Google Forms. Before beginning data collection, the researchers provided face-to-face briefings about the study in classrooms. Subsequently, data were acquired through Google Forms. The scale forms were completed by the pupils in groups at school. The completion of the form by each student took approximately 20 min. Descriptive analyses were conducted using percentages and means. Skewness and kurtosis tests were performed to determine whether the data were normally distributed. The normality of the data was checked and confirmed to display a normal distribution. The differences between binary variables were analysed using the independent samples t-test, and the differences between three or more variables were analysed using the one-way ANOVA test and the Tukey post hoc test. The relationship between the variables was assessed using Pearson correlation analysis and point-pair serial correlation analysis. The level of relationship between variables was assessed by multiple linear regression analysis. The statistical significance level was set at 0.05, and the SPSS (IBM SPSS for Windows, Ver.25) statistical package program was utilised for the analysis. The following cut-off points are utilised in the interpretation of the correlation coefficients obtained: 0.00-0.19 = very low; 0.20–0.39 = low; 0.40–0.69 = moderate; 0.70–0.89 = high; and 0.90-1.00 = very high14.

Results

Descriptive results

Table 1 shows that of the 382 participants in the study, 93.2% were female and 6.8% were male. The majority of participants were aged between 21 and 23 years (49.5%) and in their second year of university (38.7%). A significant proportion of these students have been active smartphone users for 6–10 years (56.3%). Almost all of them use smartphones (97.1%) and they report spending a usage duration of 4–6 h daily (56.5%). They also use their smartphones mostly in the evening hours (71.7%). The study found that a significant proportion of students (86.6%) do not switch off their mobile phones at night and that those who do not do so look at their phones both before going to sleep (93.7%) and when they wake up (87.2%). Furthermore, it is noteworthy that a significant proportion of students who primarily use the internet for social media applications (64.9%) also express a preference for using social media as a primary means of communication (42.4%).

Discussion

The present study was conducted to investigate the effect of nomophobia levels among university students on activity performance and anxiety levels. The results revealed that the level of nomophobia increased with age, as well as with the duration of smartphone use and the individual’s status as a first-year university student. The findings of the correlation analysis demonstrate a significant relationship between the performance, satisfaction, anxiety and nomophobia levels of students. As levels of nomophobia in students increased, there was a concomitant decrease in performance and activity satisfaction and an increase in anxiety levels.

This study examined the relationship between university students’ levels of nomophobia and various behaviours. The findings indicate that university students exhibit average levels of nomophobia. A systematic review of nomophobia revealed that university students exhibit diverse manifestations of nomophobia, with prevalence rates exceeding 50%5. In a study conducted by Farooqui, Pore, and Gothankar15it was found that 17.9% of 145 students exhibited mild nomophobia, 60% exhibited moderate nomophobia, and 22.1% exhibited severe nomophobia. Furthermore, the results of a subsequent study indicated that 30.9% of university students exhibited mild nomophobia, 52.5% demonstrated moderate nomophobia, and 16.6% displayed severe nomophobia16.

The results of studies conducted in Turkey and abroad using the nomophobia scale and involving different samples and participant groups were found to be mutually supportive in the present study. For instance, a study by Yıldırım et al. revealed that 42.6% (n = 206) of students were affected by nomophobia through cluster analysis with the participation of 537 university students4. Further research on nomophobia has observed its increasing prevalence among students17–19. The majority of studies on nomophobia have focused on participants in their twenties5. Several studies indicate that younger people are more prone to nomophobia and the number in this group is higher than other age groups1,5,20–23. The increase in smartphone use is widely regarded as a significant indicator of nomophobia24. However, while there are studies that support our findings, there are also studies that call this situation into question. For instance, Jilisha et al. (2019) concluded that the number of nomophobic individuals increases with age25. The present study revealed that the level of nomophobia among students aged 18–20 was significantly higher than in other age groups. As students’s communication preferences are centred on social media, it is observed that the fear of access to information and fear of not being online negatively affect activity performance and anxiety by increasing the duration of smartphone use. This assertion is further substantiated by the discovery of a substantial correlation between the anxiety levels experienced by young individuals and their degree of device deprivation26.

Excessive mobile phone use has been associated with various psychological problems, such as nomophobia, and with different consequences that individuals face in their daily lives. In the following subscales, the fear of being deprived of mobile devices is manifested: loss of access to information, loss of comfort, inability to communicate and disconnection. The relationship between psychological distress and nomophobia is a complex dynamic, revealing that the perceived lack of access to mobile devices among nursing students increases feelings of social isolation, loss of purpose, emotional emptiness, and self-alienation27. Consequently, there is a pressing need to undertake comprehensive research on the prevalence, severity, and adverse consequences of nomophobia in individuals.

A review of the extant studies provides mixed evidence regarding the effects of excessive smartphone use on sleep disturbance, anxiety, and depression28–32. However, these studies reveal a significant correlation between problematic smartphone use and anxiety28,31 and suggest that excessive smartphone use can lead to consequences such as changes in gene regulation, headaches, auditory and visual disturbances, fatigue, memory loss, difficulty concentrating, and weakening of brain tissue30,33,34. Recent research has indicated an association between problematic smartphone use and a range of psychological concerns, including repetitive thoughts35emotional dysregulation36,37behavioural problems and attention deficits38. In a recent study, Wolfers, Festl and Utz (2020) examined the association of smartphones and social media sites on stress management over four years39. For instance, the study found that individuals with elevated levels of nomophobia were also associated with higher levels of stress, and passive Facebook use was associated with heightened stress levels in younger adults after a period of six months39. Additionally, studies have shown a significant interaction between nomophobia and anxiety. It is noteworthy that individuals tend to avoid being in an environment without the Internet and are more concerned about the absence of the Internet than excessive Internet use2,40. In the case of smartphones, a strong correlation was found between the level of nomophobia and mobile Internet use among students41. A high correlation has been identified between excessive Internet use and nomophobia levels19. Yin et al. reported that smartphone use and addiction contributed to 64.9% of the variance in nomophobia in a multiple linear regression model42. In this context, there is a strong positive correlation between smartphone addiction and nomophobia42. This finding suggests that individuals are negatively affected by the social anxiety phenomenon known as nomophobia.

It is acknowledged that several studies have been conducted which examine the relationship between nomophobia and anxiety. Nevertheless, studies evaluating the direct effects on activity performance and satisfaction remain scarce. The majority of studies have focused on issues such as smartphone addiction and internet addiction2,9,10. Previous studies have demonstrated that nomophobia exerts a detrimental association on activities of daily living, leading to complications in cognitive, emotional, physical and social functioning43–46. A systematic review has identified that nomophobia negatively affects activities of daily living5,16. Another study reported that nomophobia leads to conditions such as powerlessness, meaninglessness, normlessness, social isolation and self-alienation and that these phenomena negatively affect daily life activities47. In the current study, it was found that university students with severe nomophobia experienced difficulties in a range of activities of daily living. These difficulties are evidenced by their low scores on perceived activity performance and satisfaction. Students were also found to experience problems with self-care, productivity and leisure activities. It was observed that students encountered difficulties with a range of leisure activities, including personal care (e.g., sleeping), community management (e.g. shopping), academic and recreational activities (e.g., studying, attending regular classes), socialising (e.g., meeting friends, participating in social activities), quiet recreation (e.g., travelling, going to the cinema/theatre/concert) and active recreation (e.g., playing sports). This phenomenon may be attributed to various factors such as nomophobia and inadequate stress management. It has been posited that many factors can act as triggers for nomophobia. Indeed, there is a growing body of literature that highlights the correlation between nomophobia and problems with activities of daily living (ADL) in university students. These problems in turn lead to low activity performance and high anxiety16,48. Previous research has shown that nomophobia has a negative association on activity performance5,16,49. The present study also aligns with the extant literature, which suggests a correlation between nomophobia and activity performance12,28,31,32. Furthermore, in parallel with the present study, the literature also suggests a correlation between nomophobia and anxiety7,16,29,31,32.

In the present study, the findings indicate that levels of nomophobia in students account for 29.0% of the variance in activity performance (R2 = 0.290). This conclusion is supported by the results of the multiple regression model created with nomophobia. A 19.7% decrease in performance levels and a 7.9% decrease in activity satisfaction levels (β=−0.079; R2 = 0.290) were determined as their nomophobia levels increased. Furthermore, the findings of the study indicated that nomophobia was responsible for the explanation of 29.0% of the variance in anxiety levels (R2 = 0.290). Conversely, a 6.9% rise in anxiety levels has been shown to be associated with escalating nomophobia levels (β = 0.069; R2 = 0.290).

In summary, levels of nomophobia were a strong predictor of activity performance, satisfaction and anxiety levels. The findings indicate that nomophobia exerts a substantial influence on leisure and daily living activity performance and satisfaction, as well as anxiety. It is hypothesised that the development of rehabilitation approaches aimed at preventing anxiety and enhancing activity performance in young individuals grappling with nomophobia is a crucial endeavour. A study reported that activity performance, stress and time management interventions had positive effects8. Consequently, both interventions can be utilised effectively in the treatment of nomophobia. The employment of an occupational therapy intervention has been demonstrated to engender a substantial improvement in nomophobia. This finding indicates that the intervention exerts a direct influence on meaningful activities that are significant yet problematic for the individual8. Identifying the presence of nomophobia is crucial in studies examining activity performance and anxiety problems in university students. It can be posited that the benefits gained from the leisure management skills highlighted in the literature may contribute to students’s coping with nomophobia and perhaps other types of dependency. The findings of the present study are consistent with those of analogous research in the extant literature. Within the context of higher education institutions, occupational therapy interventions should be made available to young individuals who self-identify as addicted to mobile devices. These interventions should aim to provide alternative leisure activities that can offer a comparable level of pleasure and satisfaction as that experienced in the virtual environment. This will play a preventative role in the development of nomophobia and other addictions through leisure activities.

The findings of this study indicate that the nomophobia scores of 382 participants suggest a moderate level of nomophobia. This emphasises the significance of activity performance and anxiety-related issues in university students. The study’s substantial sample size and the congruence of its findings with existing literature serve to reinforce the reliability of the research. This finding suggests that nomophobia may be a prevalent public health concern7,16,29,31,32. Therefore, it is imperative that health professionals conduct further studies on the rehabilitation of nomophobia. It is also possible that factors such as education and social communication may increase dependence on smartphones, which could have a negative association on the level of nomophobia. Consequently, it is imperative to investigate the underlying factors contributing to technology addictions, including nomophobia.

Strengths and limitations

The present study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, it is evident that the absence of students without nomophobia among the study participants, in conjunction with the omission of any comparative analysis related to this phenomenon, constitutes a conspicuous deficiency in the study’s design and execution. A further limitation is the existence of a variety of issues that have the potential to influence activity performance and anxiety levels. It is recommended that these problems be the subject of a more thorough investigation in future research. While the study is significant in demonstrating the effects of anxiety, it should be noted that the anxiety assessment scales utilised were based on self-report. Consequently, there is a necessity for further research to be conducted using more advanced methods in order to gain a more profound understanding of the issue.

Results and recommendations

The findings of the present study indicate a significant association between nomophobia and gender. The present study found that female participants exhibited higher levels of nomophobia compared to their male counterparts. Furthermore, a substantial correlation was identified between nomophobia and age, with younger demographics demonstrating higher levels of nomophobia in comparison to their older counterparts.

The levels of nomophobia exhibited by young individuals exhibited variation according to demographic characteristics. A significant disparity in nomophobia levels was observed between first-year students and students in other class levels. The former exhibited notably higher levels of nomophobia, encompassing its sub-dimensions of “Inability to Access Information,” “Loss of Convenience,” “Inability to Communicate,” and “Inability to Communicate.” Furthermore, an increase in the duration of smartphone usage was observed to be associated with an increase in both nomophobia and anxiety.

The findings of the correlation analysis demonstrate a substantial relationship between participants’ performance levels and both their anxiety levels and nomophobia levels. The phenomenon of nomophobia has been demonstrated to exert a negative influence on individuals’ daily activities. Individuals with nomophobia have been found to encounter difficulties in a range of daily life activities. These issues are also present in perceived activity performance and satisfaction. The study identified that university students experienced difficulties in a range of daily life activities related to self-care, productivity, and leisure. Specifically, they encountered difficulties with personal care (e.g. sleep), community management (e.g. shopping), school/leisure (e.g. studying, regularly attending classes), socialisation (e.g. meeting friends, participating in social activities), quiet recreation (e.g. travelling, going to the cinema/theatre/concert) and active recreation (e.g. exercising). This finding suggests that levels of nomophobia in students are a significant predictor of activity performance, satisfaction, and anxiety.

In summary, the findings of this study indicate that nomophobia should not be overlooked when aiming to increase activity participation and satisfaction and to reduce anxiety among students. In view of the presented evidence, it is imperative to refrain from the excessive and problematic use of mobile phone applications in order to prevent the development of nomophobia and its associated problems. For students with nomophobia, time management, and daily living activity (DLA) challenges, occupational therapists must implement cognitive and time management interventions customised to their specific activity-related difficulties. The identification of nomophobia may prove to be a significant factor in studies addressing activity performance problems in students. Nomophobia constitutes a significant problem in the context of smartphone use, representing a behavioural issue that necessitates a range of intervention methods. It is imperative to raise awareness about the detrimental consequences of smartphone addiction. Consequently, measures should be implemented to direct students towards alternative activities such as reading literature, participating in sporting activities, cultivating authentic social connections, and fostering enhanced life satisfaction, thereby encouraging them to refrain from excessive smartphone usage. Furthermore, measures can be implemented through the application of specific rules, such as the prohibition of smartphones in classrooms. Consequently, by reducing anxiety, which has been demonstrated to have a negative association on students’ academic lives (Smith, 2019), it is possible to increase academic motivation, success, and attention in classes.

At this stage, adopting an approach that prohibits technology or keeps students away from it is not appropriate. It is imperative that technology is not merely utilised by students as a consumption tool, but also as a production tool. This necessitates a conscious approach to its use. At this juncture, teachers, academics, and family members assume crucial responsibilities. It is imperative for families to recognise the pivotal role they play in their children’s lives, and to prioritise investing more quality time with their children. Furthermore, parents must take significant precautions to prevent their children from becoming addicted to technology at an early age. It is imperative to acknowledge that explicit guidelines concerning the utilisation of technological devices must be established within educational settings. It is equally crucial to recognise the existence of a tangible world that exists beyond the confines of the virtual realm. It is important to consider the role of real-time social interactions, physical activities, listening to calm music, and reading a good book in the psychological well-being of students.

In the field of future research, it is recommended to direct attention towards mobile applications that result in smartphone addiction or nomophobia via mobile internet and smartphones. In addition, the selection of sample groups from diverse age categories is advised. Institutions have the capacity to utilise research findings to enhance their policies concerning the utilisation of technology on campus. For instance, policies regarding the utilisation of smartphones in residential settings or during academic pursuits could be formulated with potential implications on mental health in mind.
