Rapid disease progression and hydrocephalus following tarlatamab therapy initiation: A case report
Authors: Doppo Fukui, Jun Sakakibara-Konishi, Riku Azuma, Yukiko Yoshida, Daisuke Morinaga, Shotaro Ito, Megumi Furuta, Yuta Takasihma, Hidenori Kitai, Satoshi Konno
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tarlatamab is a novel bispecific T-cell engager immunotherapy that has demonstrated efficacy against small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) in terms of noteworthy anticancer activity and survival outcomes. However, no studies have detailed the patterns of disease progression during tarlatamab treatment. Herein, we present a case of acute disease progression after tarlatamab initiation in a 71-year-old male patient with extensive-stage SCLC. Following tarlatamab administration, he developed symptoms of cognitive impairment, which raised suspicion of immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), an adverse event associated with the drug. Diagnostic evaluation revealed that the symptoms were caused by obstructive hydrocephalus due to worsening brain metastases. Following tarlatamab administration, we observed rapid progression of the previously indolent SCLC, leading to deterioration in the patient's overall condition. This case highlights the need to consider the possibility of rapid disease progression following tarlatamab administration and underscores the importance of adequate assessment to exclude ICANS. Since rapid tumor progression may lead to a decline in the patient's general condition, careful follow-up is advised.

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) is an aggressive disease associated with poor survival outcomes. Two thirds of patients are diagnosed with extensive-stage disease, and approximately 40–70 % develop brain metastases over the course of their illness [1,2]. Tarlatamab is a bispecific T-cell engager immunotherapy that redirects cytotoxic T cells to cancer cells expressing delta-like ligand 3, resulting in cancer cell lysis. In the phase 2 DeLLphi-301 trial, tarlatamab demonstrated noteworthy anticancer activity (objective response rate, 40 %) and survival outcomes (median overall survival, 14.3 months) in patients with extensive-stage SCLC previously treated with two or more lines of therapy [3]. Although adverse events are mostly tolerable, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) require management [[2], [3], [4]].

In Japan, tarlatamab has been reimbursed in 2024. Thus, the drug's real-world efficacy, adverse events, as well as the pattern of progression in cases of insufficient antitumor effect remain unclear. We present a case of rapidly progressive SCLC with subsequent hydrocephalus development after tarlatamab administration.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old Japanese man was diagnosed with cT3N3M1c-stage IVB SCLC in November of X-2. He underwent four cycles of carboplatin + etoposide + durvalumab therapy, followed by two cycles of durvalumab maintenance therapy. Due to progressive disease (PD), amrubicin (AMR) therapy was initiated in April of X-1. Partial response was observed up to the 12th AMR cycle. After 12 cycles of AMR therapy, new brain metastases were detected, indicating PD, but the rate of growth was slow (Fig. 1a). AMR therapy was continued for a total of 18 cycles as beyond PD. Prior to tarlatamab administration, the patient had a performance status score of 1 and normal cognitive function. Tarlatamab was initiated during hospitalization, with the first cycle administered on days 1, 8, and 15. Although grade 1 cytokine release syndrome was observed, no other adverse events, including ICANS, were noted during hospitalization. The patient was discharged on day 16. Approximately 1 week later, on day 23 after tarlatamab administration, his wife noticed that he had altered gait and repeatedly asked the same questions or had disjointed conversations. On day 28, due to newly observed disorientation, he was admitted for further evaluation.

There was no gross paralysis in his limbs and he did not have nausea. However, he exhibited gait instability with a wide-based gait. The Hasegawa dementia scale-revised score was 13 out of 30, suggesting cognitive decline, with particular impairment in short-term memory, but the immune effector cell‐associated encephalopathy score was 10 points, which was full. Serum Interleukin-6 was 21.6 pg/mL and C-reactive protein was 1.67, with only minimal elevation, and no fever was observed, thus CRS was not suspected. It was deemed difficult to perform a cerebrospinal fluid analysis due to the patient's clinical condition. Gadolinium-enhanced head magnetic resonance imaging revealed enlargement of the tumors in the pineal gland, left middle frontal gyrus, right occipital lobe, and bilateral cerebellar hemispheres. Furthermore, enlargement of the supratentorial ventricles was observed, particularly the temporal horns of both lateral ventricles (Fig. 2). Based on all above, ICANS was excluded and the patient was diagnosed with hydrocephalus due to a pineal region mass. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed enlargement of the primary left lung lesion and the left hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes (Fig. 2). New liver metastases were also observed. After whole-brain radiotherapy was administered at a dose of 30 Gy in 10 fractions, the patient's cognitive function improved and his gait disturbance almost completely resolved. However, hydrocephalus remains present on the image, and the Hasegawa dementia scale-revised score was 19 points and neurological abnormalities persisted. On day 50 from the initial tarlatamab administration, computed tomography revealed further progression of the truncal lesions (Fig. 3). Based on the tumor increase, the enlargement appeared rapid progression, look similar hyperprogressive disease (HPD), rather than pseudoprogression. Discussion

In the present case, the patient exhibited cognitive function decline owing to rapid disease progression after tarlatamab administration. Cognitive function began deteriorating approximately 3–4 weeks after tarlatamab therapy initiation. Thus, our initial differential diagnosis included ICANS, as the reported median time to its onset is 30 days (range, 13–47 days) from the start of tarlatamab administration [5]. ICANS can present with a wide range of nonspecific symptoms, including confusion, impaired attention, motor weakness, dysgraphia, and increased muscle tone. However, in cases of cognitive function decline, ICANS could be diagnosed only after systematic exclusion of alternative neurologic etiologies, including s epilepsy, infectious disease, progression of central nervous system metastases, adverse effects of concomitant medications, and metabolic or electrolyte disturbances, whether related to paraneoplastic syndromes or otherwise [5]. In our case, as CRS, which frequently accompanies ICANS, was absent, and examination revealed cognitive decline and gait disturbance characteristic of hydrocephalus, consistent with contrast-enhanced MRI demonstrating tumor-induced obstructive hydrocephalus, the symptoms were attributed to progression of brain metastases rather than ICANS. In our patient with extensive-stage SCLC, the disease progressed relatively slowly during AMR treatment. However, within approximately 30 days after the initiation of tarlatamab therapy, almost all lesions progressed, with a particularly notable increase in the size of brain metastases. A similar condition, known as HPD, has been reported during immunotherapy in cases of non-small-cell lung cancer. This phenomenon is clinically defined as an unexpected acceleration of the tumor kinetics that can be measured by imaging using dynamic parameters [6,7]. While the pathophysiology of hyper progression is inherently complex and remains incompletely elucidated, several reports have described proposed mechanisms. In the case of immune checkpoint inhibitors, factors such as amplification of regulatory T cells and excessive cytokine production have been implicated [8]. However, it has rarely been reported with bispecific T-cell engagers such as tarlatamab, and the underlying biological mechanisms remain poorly understood. Based on the mechansims of hyperprogression reported with immune checkpoint inhibitors, given that tarlatamab similarly functions through T cell-mediated tumor engagement and that cytokine release-related adverse events are frequently observed with this agent [5], it is biologically plausible that analogous mechanisms may contribute to hyperprogression in tarlatamab-treated patients. The PD rate was 20 % in the 10-mg tarlatamab group in the DeLLphi-301 trial [3], and 22 % among patients who received tarlatamab as second-line therapy in the DeLLphi-304 trial [2]. In real-world data, although the sample size was small (n = 22), a PD rate of 19 % was reported [9]. Thus, both clinical trials and real-world studies consistently demonstrate a PD rate of approximately 20 %. However, none of these reports have clarified what proportion of these PD cases represent hyperprogressive disease. Therefore, this case may represent a critical clinical phenomenon regarding tarlatamab associated adverse effect. Because SCLC can sometimes progress rapidly even in its natural course without treatment [1], it remains unclear whether HPD occurs with tarlatamab treatment. Nonetheless, careful monitoring is required, as suggested by the present case.

Conclusions

We present a case of rapid progression of SCLC after tarlatamab administration accompanied by obstructive hydrocephalus due to pineal gland metastasis. This case emphasizes the need to consider rapid disease progression following tarlatamab administration because it may lead to abrupt deterioration in the patients’ general condition.
