Blunt-force assembly of programmable DNA architectures using π–π stacking
Authors: Karol Woloszyn, Andrew Horvath, Mara Jaffe, Lara Perren, Joe Rueb, Samyra Mahiba, Nataša Jonoska, Yoel P Ohayon, James W Canary, Simon Vecchioni, Ruojie Sha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSticky-ended cohesion has been the driving force for DNA self-assembly and has enabled model and design programmability in DNA nanotechnology for over 40 years. Traditional units within self-assembled crystals have rationally-designed sticky ends to avoid unpredictable packing behavior, but in doing so, the surprising variety of contact flavors available to natural nucleic acids remains unexploited. Here, we employ composable DNA tiles to form complex 3D architectures using blunt-ended motifs with single duplex interfaces, thereby leveraging the geometry of the tile and the terminal nucleobase identity to control self-assembly outcomes. These crystals yielded X-ray diffraction at resolutions between 10.0 and 1.86 Å. We establish programmability and tunable packing, including translational and inversion symmetries, 5’−3’ and 5’−5’ stacking, and both positive and negative helical twist values. Finally, we establish the ability of racemic mixtures of L- and D-DNA motifs to perform programmable co-assembly driven by terminal π–π interactions, demonstrating molecular recognition and coexistence between mirror molecular systems.Subject terms: DNA nanostructures, X-ray crystallography, DNA

Introduction

DNA nanotechnology, or semantomorphic science, has thrived for forty years by exploiting the sequence programmability and geometric tunability of nucleic acids to develop a vast library of topological constructs. To join distinct subunits, or tiles, the field has long leveraged the cohesive sticky end overhang, derived from the complementary overhangs employed in molecular biology, where restriction enzymes leave cohesive cut sites to stitch disparate elements into linked plasmids1. Sticky ends were first leveraged in DNA nanotechnology when Chen and Seeman constructed the DNA cube by sticky-ended cohesion and ligation – akin to a knotted form of cloning2. Since that time, sticky ends have been a mainstay of DNA self-assembly, yielding a wide variety of extended nanostructures (Fig. 1A)3–5. Two driving forces enable sticky end bonding: (1) the programmed sequence at the overhang, which is selectively “sticky” toward its complement; and (2) the aromatic π–π stacking interactions that occur between vertically-aligned nucleobase rings inside the double helix, which have been shown to stabilize duplex formation 250-fold6. In this context, sequence dictates the selectivity, while stacking enforces assembly.

By contrast, blunted duplexes without overhangs appear, at the outset, to lack the semantic programming needed to drive complex self-assembly: the hydrogen bonding network between adjacent units is missing. There exists, however, a much greater stacking area at the helix terminus, with each nucleobase providing a distinct π-bonding interface and generating a differential energy landscape7. Indeed, aromatic stacking interactions have been studied for decades8, and have been exploited to assemble supramolecular catalysts9, metal-organic frameworks10, and nanomaterials11. Blunt ends have long been known to cohere in lower-order topological constructs. Indeed, most DNA crystals are mediated by some cohesion at the ends of the molecules, yielding close-packed, well-diffracting solids12. Complex liquid crystals have been obtained through the blunt-end stacking of duplexes, with some programmability set by sequence length and identity13,14. In higher-order assemblies, densely-packed, many-helix bundles of DNA, as well as origami tiles, have been observed to cohere by π–π stacking to generate 1D and 2D nanostructures15–17, showing that with sufficient cooperativity, the transient blunt-ended cohesion observed in the liquid phase can be retained on a surface. DNA origami crystals have been reported using blunted 14-helix bundles at the cohesive end18. These larger assemblies use both π–π stacking and the geometric guidance of many parallel helices, much like a plug-and-socket that has complementary shapes.

Rationally-designed 3D DNA crystals originated with the tensegrity triangle—a well-studied 3D DNA tile that produces diffraction-quality crystals driven by topological self-assembly (Fig. 1B)19–24. The tensegrity triangle motif has been previously modified to yield rhombohedral, trigonal, cubic, and hexagonal lattices as a result of changes to helical twist between junctions and bending at the sticky ends25–30. In contrast to dodecamer crystals, these triangles are topologically complex and possess large cavities, reducing the cooperativity typical of standard crystallization. Unlike large origami or helix-bundle assemblies, the cohesive ends are restricted to a single helix, reducing the cooperativity with respect to the lock-and-key mechanisms of previous studies.

In this work, investigate blunt-ended cohesion in higher-order assemblies, by scanning a library of triangles using only π–π stacking between purines (R) and pyrimidines (Y) at their blunt interfaces (Fig. 1C). Thus, we reduce the system to two variables: stacking identity across the cohesive end (R | R, R | Y, and Y | R), and tile geometry, dictated by the helical twist between junctions of both individual and adjacent triangles. In doing so, we generate traditional crystal contacts using a programmable design language and establish the ability to discriminate assembly outcomes using the information encoded in nucleobase-specific π systems. With this approach, we achieve: (1) a wide variety of single duplex stacking arrangements within 3D crystals; (2) several new crystal forms not observed by isomorphic motifs employing sticky ends; (3) asymmetric units that define, to our knowledge, completely new symmetries in the 3D crystal, including inversion, translation and rotation in the same lattice; (4) finally, and most interestingly, enantiomeric units that can coexist within the same 3D crystal using programmable stacking behaviors.

Results

We crystallized triangles with 7 base pairs (bp) between junctions and edge sizes of 18–24 bp (Figs. S1 and S2), designed with R | R, R | Y, and Y | R stacking patterns, and named by the tile length and stacking pair across the edge (e.g., “217-G | A” denotes a 21 bp tile with 7 inter-junction base pairs and base G at the 5′ end and base A at the 3′ end of the stacking helices; Fig. 1C). The realized stacking pair often differed from the motif design, which we show in Figs. 2C, S3–S6. We also assembled two motifs with different inter-junction lengths, including a 21 bp tile with 6 bp between junctions (216-G | A) and a 12 bp tile with 5 bp between junctions (125-G | A) (Fig. S7). Diffraction was achieved into space groups P4132, I4132, P61, P63, P32, P321, and C2221, with resolutions ranging from 1.86 Å to 10.0 Å (Tables S2 and S3, Figs. Local geometry at the stacking interface

Because the motifs are constrained neither by helical continuity at the blunt end nor by strand polarity, we observe a full spectrum of stacking arrangements. A library of base stacking is constructed (Figs. S2 and S3), and from this, six stacking geometries are identified (Fig. 2A). Depending on the geometry of the cohesion, the motifs either (1) follow the prescribed stacking arrangement (R | R, R | Y, Y | R) (Fig. S4-top, Fig. S5-top), or (2) invert to self-stack, yielding 5′ | 3′ cohesion via 180° rotation (Fig. S4-bottom) or groove-inverted 5′ | 5′/3′ | 3′ cohesion (Fig. S5-bottom). A full list of stacking angles and assembly outcomes is tabulated in Tables S3–S5.

Often, bases adopt a fully-aligned arrangement, stacking directly atop one another. In this arrangement, the nucleobases themselves are nearly perfectly parallel – not the backbones – placing the aromatic π rings directly atop one another in a way that is not possible in a sugar-locked B-form helix. This arrangement can occur with the purine on top (Aligned R | Y), most commonly yielding an effective −30° twist in the natural direction of the helical twist. This trend was so strong that 15 of the 19 stacking pairs in our library with purine-on-top geometry had negative twist values (Fig. S4). By contrast, the pyrimidine-on-top alignment (Aligned Y | R) yields a twist of approximately +20°, introducing a counter rotation at the cohesive end. Of structures with pyrimidine-on-top stacking, 11 of 15 exhibited positive twist values. In both cases, the outliers exhibited either 5′ | 5′ or Cross-stacking. Lastly, one structure exhibited the Aligned R | R class, in which A | G stacking was observed with a twist of −24.3°.

A fourth common category arose when a purine was on the bottom of an end-stacking pair. Angles exceeded standard twisting yet retained the groove geometries in the so-called Acute class with higher average angles than the aligned categories (60°> |θ| > 20°). The defining feature of this stacking pattern is the broken alignment between aromatic rings, with hydrogen bonding moieties overlapping with the π system (Fig. 2A).

Inverted 5′ | 5′ stacking induces a twist of −120° or more and was only observed when nucleobases stacked directly on their symmetry mate (G | G, C | C, T | T) (Figs. 2D, S4). This geometry was seen only in geometric edge cases, such as the 187-G | A structure in space group P321, which included a highly unique pyrimidine pivot in which the T | T stack served as the sole anchor to the cohesive end (Fig. S6-bottom). Other 5′ | 5′ stacking was observed with R | R alignment in fully-inverted, ~180° twist environments.

In the Cross-stacking category, the Watson base (leftmost in an oriented pair) stacked across the helix, atop the Crick base of the second pair (rightmost), effectively shrinking the helical diameter at the stacking interface. This was always observed with two adenines stacking from opposite sides of the interface, with the aromatic rings of thymine mostly uninvolved (Fig. 2A, D).

Global geometry of the crystal

The difference between self- and hetero-cohesion depends on both the tile geometry and stacking identity. Across lattice pairs, blunted tensegrity triangles assemble into either cubic or hexagonal/trigonal lattices. Within these types, outcomes are split into those without inversion symmetries (P4132 and P63) and those with inversion symmetries (I4132 and P321). Inversion symmetry arises from self-stacking. Tile geometry selects the lattice category, while the exact assembly outcome is dependent on the identity of the terminal stacking pair (Fig. 2B). Every space group has an inversion pair, enumerated below.

The default space group for blunt-end stacking is I4132. By contrast, all other DNA crystals employing sticky-ended cohesion in unstrained triangles follow rhombohedral space group R324,34,35. Unless a geometric parameter allows another group, all blunted tiles tend toward cubic assembly. Previous work identified two key geometric parameters: (1) “magic-number” triangles27 employ changes of 1/3 turn, enabling symmetry-protected assembly into other space groups28; and (2) adding or subtracting single base pairs from full turns of DNA causes bending, yielding hexagonal symmetry 36.

We previously observed that triangles with 24 bp edges (2+1/3 turns) and non-Watson-Crick (nWC) 2-bp sticky ends (AG:TC) bend at the sticky end and crystallize in the primitive cubic space group P4132. This structure includes two copies of the asymmetric unit (ASU) subject to translational non-crystallographic symmetry (tNCS). In the current study, the blunted 237 and 247 motifs behaved similarly (Fig. 2B), crystallizing into the P4132 space group with two triangle copies subject to tNCS (Fig. S5-top). Most other motifs crystallized into the body-centered cubic space group I4132 with a single ASU. Rather than a non-crystallographic translation, the second copy is subject to inversion – a crystallographic symmetry operation allowed by blunt-end self-cohesion. In DNA assembly, I4132 and P4132 are inversion-paired space groups.

A similar relationship occurs between hexagonal lattices with inversion-paired space groups P63 and P321. Edge lengths that are one bp from two full helical turns – 207- and 227-G | T – crystallized in hexagonal space group P63, matching previously well-characterized structures (Fig. 2B, Fig. S6-top)37. In the 187-G | A tile (5/3 total helical turns tile length) inversion yields a higher resolution trigonal lattice (3.6 Å vs. 5.1 Å) in space group pair P321, which has not been observed previously in tensegrity triangles. This lattice resembles the P63 structure but with alternating polarity due to self-stacking at the tile edges (Fig. S6-bottom). This motif in particular exhibits a deeply unusual stacking geometry on one face, with a T | T 5′−5′ self-stack which pivots exclusively on the thymine bases and aligns the six-member rings at an angle of −123°. This inversion is enabled by a combination of magic-number edge lengths (1+2/3 turns) and pyrimidine placement at the interface27. All these tile sets – 187, 207, and 227 – are able to crystallize in a hexagonal/trigonal arrangement as a result of geometry, as well as the default 14132 space group. Selection among these outcomes arises from blunt-end nucleobase identity, highlighting the role of sequence in determining global crystallographic arrangement.

Finally, we investigate tiles with internal edges that are not N±1/3 helical turns. The 216 and 125 motifs contain roughly 1/2 helical turn between junctions and consequently do not form closed triangles21,38. The 216-G | A motif crystallizes into space group P32 without inversion (Fig. S7). The one-turn 125-G | A tile also assembles in P32 but diffracts to a high resolution of 1.86 Å while retaining the packing features of its parent, two-turn tile. While individual atoms are not resolvable, this method enables a close inspection of the geometry of the blunt end, as well as global topology. We successfully soak crystals of the 125 motif in ethidium bromide and yield uniform dye penetration and unperturbed diffraction, enabling future study of guest molecules and intercalation agents at the stacking interface (Fig. S7). No inversion mate for space group P32 is identified.

Crystal gumbo: asymmetric cohesion

We next designed a tile with sequence asymmetry that would traditionally assemble in space group P1. However, we designed it to mix and match cohesion types to enable blunt-end screening of possible geometries. One crystallographic axis (or triangle arm) carried a one-bp sticky end. On the other two axes, we placed blunt ends that would self-discriminate (Fig. 3A). When crystallized, all possible stacking geometries manifest within the same crystal system. Along the sticky end axis, a translational hydrogen bonding arrangement is observed, similar to P1 and R3 tiles designed previously (yellow and orange, Fig. 3B)20,29. Along another axis (green), we observe translational hetero-cohesion by blunt stacking (Fig. 3C), still allowing for P1 symmetry. However, the third axis exhibits an inversion operation: one face engages in 5′ | 3′ self-cohesion (blue, Fig. 3D) requiring a 180° rotation at the blunt end, while the other face along that axis exhibits 5′ | 5′ stacking (red, Fig. 3E). Taken together, this set of behaviors does not yield a rhombohedral P1 lattice, but a zig-zag lattice with kinked channels in space group C2221 (Fig. 3F).

We next crystallized a motif that possessed a two-bp Watson-Crick (WC) sticky end on one axis (TG:CA), a two-bp non-Watson-Crick (nWC) mismatched sticky end on the second (AG:TC), and a blunt end (A | G) on the third axis. The default behavior of WC sticky-ended cohesion is rhombohedral assembly20; the standard for sticky end mismatches is hexagonal assembly36; and the standard for 217 blunt ends is body-centered cubic. Taken together, this motif self-assembled in a hetero-cohesive manner into space group P61. Consequently, all cohesive ends mixed: WC pairing to nWC, which then stacked on the blunt end by extruding the sticky end, and so forth (Fig. S6). Each blunt-end stacking interaction was carried out by employing the purine from the sticky end as a single nucleotide intercalator between the stacking interfaces. Of the three cohesive, paired ends, none paired with its designed partner. Two of the three resulting interactions involved blunt-end stacking, and one exhibited a nWC pair. This suggests that blunt stacking wins the energetic Rochambeau, with a hexagonal geometry providing the most favorable packing arrangement.

This is the first report of “asymmetric” tiles gaining symmetry by design of the cohesive ends. We demonstrate that it is possible to both mix and match cohesion types, enabling complex geometries from simple, composable parts.

Mirror molecules in racemic assembly

Finally, we tested whether blunt-end stacking could be used to program interactions between mirror-image tiles employing L- and D-DNA. Such molecules are known not to base pair with their enantiomorph in a canonical manner39, though L | D stacking in crystals of racemic duplexes has been observed40. Recent work has generated apprehension toward mirror molecular systems due to the lack of interaction with our predominantly homochiral biome41,42.

To assess whether blunt cohesion discriminates between nucleobase sequence via differential π–π stacking, we synthesized an L-DNA 227 R | Y (G | T) tile, which crystallizes into space group I4332 (the enantiomorph of I4132, Table S3) with a Cy3 dye appended to the 5' terminus of the triangle center strand. We crystallized this tile in tandem with D-DNA 227 tiles with three different end-stacking pairs: R | Y, Y | R and R | R (G | T, C | A, and G | A, respectively), each with an appended Cy5 dye to track assembly outcomes (Fig. 4A). Depending on the terminal pair, we observe three assembly outcomes: matching, in which the two tiles mix into a single crystal (Fig. 4B); sorting, in which the tiles segregate into different crystals (Fig. 4C); and layered epitaxy, in which the crystals mix but have distinct, microscopic grains of each tile that fold into lamellae.

Mirror triangles in racemic mixtures.

A The crystal structures of L- and D-DNA 227 tiles are shown, with the space groups by stacking identity for individual tiles notes. L-DNA tiles are dyed pink with Cy3 appended to the center strands (dye not resolved by X-ray), and D-DNA tiles are cyan with Cy5. B When the same stacking pair is used at the termini (227 G | T or “R | Y”), perfect mixing is observed by confocal fluorescence microscopy, with co-localization of both tiles shown by FRET. C When L-227 G | T (R | Y) is co-crystallized with D-227 C | A (Y | R), which crystallize in different space groups, full separation into different crystals is observed, with a faint FRET signal at the surface due to minor epitaxy. D When L-227 G | T (R | Y) and D-227 G | A (R | R) are co-crystallized, which both crystallize in cubic space groups, lamellae are formed, with grain mixing in single crystals, with co-localization observed strongest at the grain boundaries by FRET.

The matching outcome occurs when the terminal pairs are identical between the tiles (both 227 R | Y tiles), allowing for substitution in the lattice as continuous defects. The crystals assemble into space group P63 and diffract cleanly. There are two sublattices identified in the crystal structure, with the dominant lattice solving as L-DNA (Rfree of L-DNA structure: 0.28; Rfree of D-DNA refinement in the same lattice: 0.62). Furthermore, this dominant lattice diffracts to 8.6 Å, while the secondary D-DNA lattice diffracts to >17 Å, precluding determination of the D-DNA structure in the mixed crystal. Confocal fluorescence microscopy and FRET imaging show strong co-localization of the tiles (Figs. 4B, S9).

Sorting, when L-227 R | Y is co-crystallized with D-227 Y | R, produces two distinct crystals with minimal fluorescence overlay or FRET between the two tiles. The L-DNA tile diffracts into I4332 (Fig. 2B, Table S3). D-227 Y | R tile self-assembles into a different space group (P63) and does not align well with the L- tile, driving segregation of the crystals.

Finally, when mixing L-227 R | Y and D-227 R | R, epitaxial phenomena are observed. In crystallization buffers with no ammonium sulfate (a strong precipitating agent), crystal nucleation is slow. In these conditions, a D-DNA seed is always observed, often with surface epitaxy of an L-DNA grain. These composites form “epitaxial halos,” in which the L-DNA forms ring-like structures that are weakly cohesive and melt under laser heating (Fig. S10). With ammonium sulfate included, nucleation is accelerated, and competing epitaxy is observed in the crystal grains: lamellae growing atop one another to form durable, marbled crystals (Fig. 4D). A 3D reconstruction of this phenomenon is shown in Fig. S12. FRET is observed throughout but is strongest at the contacts between enantiomorphic lamellae. Nonetheless, in each case, a blue, D-DNA core is observed, suggesting faster nucleation for D-tiles.

When nucleation occurs very quickly, crystals are small and numerous. We show in Fig. 1A and Fig. S11 a series of cubic crystals displaying symmetric grain boundaries, indicating a tightly controlled process that is coherent over tens of microns. This is akin to “differentiation” seen in guided block copolymer nucleation43, albeit here the apparent guide is the stacking interface between enantiomorphs.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that mirror-image molecules can exhibit programmable, sequence-specific interaction strengths, tuning the stacking energy in an algorithmic fashion and enabling control over interactions between mirror molecules systems.

Discussion

In this study, we extensively characterize the self-assembly and self-discrimination of DNA tiles based on programmable π–π stacking. We observe a wide range of stacking geometries that are screened by both the stacking pair identity and the tile geometry, yielding translational or inversion symmetry, 5′ | 3′ and 5′ | 5′ stacking, self- and hetero-cohesion, and both positive and negative twist values. We constructed a library of stacking outcomes and further showed that we can use this information to gain crystallographic symmetry in asymmetric tiles. This in particular suggests that encoding information into the connections between tiles, rather than simply the sequence within tiles, can increase the algorithmic complexity of self-assembly.

We establish the ability to program molecules by their contact points and establish a design language using π-π stacking. These results will yield advances in diffraction experiments needing new or high-resolution lattices23,44, and enable nanomaterials design with higher information densities, such as photonic or excitonic systems that rely on geometric interactions at the lattice level45. Notably, the diffraction of a tile to better than 2.0 Å has not been previously reported in the field of structural DNA nanotechnology. We suggest that blunt stacking will provide an avenue for one-turn analogs to existing tiles that may yield high-resolution diffraction relative to the field standards in self-assembled DNA tiles (2.1–4.0 Å).

The capability of tiles whose overall geometry is identical to sort into different packing arrangements is a surprise. As shown in Fig. 2B, the space group outcomes are screened by both tile shape and stacking sequence. This is a departure from existing systems that employ DNA origami tiles with blunted multi-helix bundles, where the stacking identity does not seem to impact the results18,46. Here, the stacking interface relies on a single helix, so the registry of individual rings becomes a strong driving force for assembly. This likely leads to the dominance of the Aligned stacking geometry, which stacks through exact alignment of the nucleobase rings. Even less common cases, such as the Cross or Acute class, tend to yield perfect or rotated alignment that allows for registry of the π system. The most dramatic example is the 187 G | A motif, which exhibits a 5′−5′ inversion in order to align the pyrimidines at a 120° angle, agreeing with the hexagonal shape of the base ring.

Finally, we demonstrate that mirror molecular systems can exhibit tunable interaction strengths from the sequence identity at their stacking interface. This directly leads to the transfer of information between the mirror molecules, with assembly outcomes reporting the measurement result. One assembly outcome leads to a carefully layered heterochiral material, which exhibits micron-scale morphogenic feature discrimination. This suggests a method for our homochiral biome to interact with, and discriminate between, mirrored information at the supramolecular scale. The ability to exchange information between mirror systems, and indeed to screen the content of mirror molecules by their surface interactions, makes progress toward mitigating some of the risks raised in recent debates on the safety of enantiomeric research42,47,48. We anticipate a rich exploration of materials self-assembly based on these results.

Methods

Detailed methods can be found in the Supporting Information. Briefly:

DNA preparation

D-DNA oligomers were purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (Coralville, IA, USA) with standard desalting. L-DNA oligomers were synthesized on an Applied Biosystems 394 DNA synthesizer by standard phosphoramidite techniques using amidites purchased from Glen Research (Sterling, VA). DNA was PAGE purified, ethidium stained, eluted, and butanol extracted using standard techniques (see Supporting Information). Oligomers were quantified and resuspended at 100 μM in double-distilled water.

Crystallization

Crystallization was performed in hanging drops with 60 pmol motif (Fig. S3) containing strands at set stoichiometry (Table S1) in 10 mM 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (MOPS), 10 mM sodium hydroxide, and 125 mM magnesium sulfate at pH 8.0, equilibrated 100 mM MOPS and 1.25 M magnesium sulfate at pH 8.0 as adjusted by sodium hydroxide. Crystals were grown with a temperature ramp from 60 °C to 20 °C at a cooling rate of 0.7 °C per hour, followed by cooling from 20 °C to 4 °C at 0.4 °C per hour.

X-ray diffraction

Crystals were cryoprotected in 30% glycerol and mother liquor, immersed in liquid nitrogen, and shipped for X-ray diffraction at the National Synchrotron Light Source II at Brookhaven National Laboratory (beamlines 17-ID-1 and 17-ID-2). Indexing and integration were performed using the AutoProc software suite from Global Phasing, and scaling was performed using the STARANISO server31,32. Structure determination was carried out using molecular replacement (Table S2) in Phenix, with refinement in Phenix and COOT 33,49.

Imaging

Confocal fluorescence microscopy was performed on a Leica SP8 microscope using 532 nm excitation for Cy3 dyed crystals and 647 nm excitation for Cy5 dyed crystals. FRET experiments were performed by collecting in the Cy5 spectral window under illumination at 532 nm.
