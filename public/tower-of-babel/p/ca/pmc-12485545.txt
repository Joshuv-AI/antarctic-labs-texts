Radial Artery Avulsion During Peripheral Intervention Managed With a Nonsurgical Approach: Case Report
Authors: Matthew R Holland, Yolanda A Ofori, Michael A Sola, Stephen E Dreyer, Shea E Hogan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRadial artery avulsion is an uncommon and serious complication of transradial artery approach, which is usually managed by vascular surgery. This case report describes an 81-year-old woman with radial artery avulsion during percutaneous lower extremity revascularization for symptomatic peripheral artery disease, which was managed using an endovascular approach. While holding manual pressure on the upper forearm, transfemoral access was obtained, and coils were delivered to the proximal radial artery, resulting in hemostasis. The patient’s subsequent course was uneventful and without clinical sequelae. Surgeons who perform transradial access should be familiar with potential complications and their associated management strategies to ensure safe patient care.Keywords: case report, complication, percutaneous vascular intervention, radial artery complication

Background

Radial artery access for diagnostic coronary artery intervention and percutaneous coronary intervention has become more widely utilized and currently is the preferred route for coronary procedures globally.1 Compared with a femoral approach, radial access is associated with low bleeding risk, vascular complication rates, and short-term mortality.1 Additionally, a radial artery approach during percutaneous coronary intervention is associated with a significant decrease in all-cause mortality at 3 years.2 A radial artery approach has also been shown to be feasible and safe for peripheral angiography and percutaneous revascularization of peripheral artery disease.3 While radial artery approach is associated with lower complication rates than its femoral counterpart, complications still occur. These include, but not limited to, arterial spasm, bleeding, arterial injury, radial artery occlusion, and radial artery perforation. A rare and serious complication is radial artery avulsion, during which the artery is torn because of mechanical injury.4 The majority of published case reports and clinical series describe surgical and hybrid (combined surgical and endovascular) management of radial artery avulsion.4,5 In this case report, we describe the management of a radial artery avulsion using a completely nonsurgical approach.

Clinical case

An 81-year-old woman with dementia and peripheral artery disease presented with left-sided resting leg pain and dusky toes. During noninvasive vascular testing, the patient was unable to tolerate ankle-brachial index measurement because of pain, and toe pressure was undetectable (digit plethysmography revealed a flatline waveform). Medical history and clinical findings were consistent with Rutherford category 4 peripheral artery disease. Because of concerns about the increased risk of femoral artery access in a woman with calcified common femoral arteries, it was decided to use a radial artery approach. The patient was unable to supinate her left arm, so a right radial approach was chosen. Right radial artery access was performed under direct ultrasound guidance, which revealed a radial artery size of >2.0 mm. A 4/5F Glidesheath Slender (Terumo) was placed using modified Seldinger technique. No resistance was encountered during sheath placement. Nitroglycerin (200 μg) and verapamil (2.5 mg) were administered through the radial artery sheath to decrease radial artery spasm, and unfractionated heparin (6000 U intravenously × 1) was administered to decrease the risk of radial artery thrombosis. Peripheral angiography of the left leg was performed using a 0.035-inch crossing catheter via the 4/5F 10.0-cm Glidesheath Slender right radial artery approach, and revealed occlusion of the left common femoral artery (CFA), superficial femoral artery (SFA), popliteal artery, and tibioperoneal trunk. After discussion with vascular surgeon and the patient, it was decided to proceed with percutaneous intervention of the left CFA.

An activated clotting time (ACT) test was performed, and subsequent boluses of unfractionated heparin were given to achieve and maintain an ACT of 250 to 300 seconds. The 4/5F 10.0-cm Glidesheath was exchanged for a 5/6F 10.0-cm Glidesheath over the wire. The patient was already on aspirin therapy. Intravascular ultrasound–guided percutaneous vascular intervention was performed using balloon angioplasty throughout all occluded segments into the proximal peroneal artery. A 6.0-mm × 150.0-mm, 200.0-cm shaft self-expanding nitinol stent was deployed from the distal SFA into the midpopliteal artery. The stent was postdilated with a 6.0-mm × 150.0-mm compliant balloon, up to 18 atm proximally. Angiography revealed a focal area of extravasation within the proximal SFA. The 6.0-mm balloon was reinflated to 4 atm for 7 minutes. Angiography at this juncture revealed improvement but not resolution of the perforation. The balloon was reinflated to tamponade the perforation while evaluating available covered stents. The longest covered stent shaft length was 120.0 cm. The balloon in the SFA was deflated and removed, during which time manual compression was held on the CFA. A 0.035-inch NaviCross 150.0-cm catheter (Terumo) was advanced over the wire to the perforation. Based on the residual length of the NaviCross catheter, we estimated that a 120.0-cm shaft length would reach the perforation. The 0.035-inch NaviCross catheter was advanced into the distal SFA and the 0.035-inch wire was exchanged for a 0.018-inch Platinum Plus wire (Boston Scientific). The NaviCross catheter was removed. Since the covered stent required 7F access, the 5/6F 10.0-cm Glidesheath Slender was exchanged for a 6/7F 10.0-cm Glidesheath Slender over the 0.018-inch wire. A VIABAHN 7.0-mm × 100.0-mm, 120.0-cm shaft length stent (Gore) was advanced over the wire, but, unfortunately, the length was approximately 3.0 cm too short to reach the perforation. The undeployed stent was pulled back, but there was resistance at the level of the sheath. Consequently, the stent and sheath were removed together as a unit, with only mild resistance encountered. The 0.018-inch wire position was maintained. It was immediately apparent that a long segment of radial artery was attached to the sheath and stent and that the artery had been avulsed (Figure 1). A 5/6F 10.0-cm Glidesheath Slender was advanced minimally over the wire. Contrast injection was performed to determine the level of the radial artery avulsion, as involvement of the brachial artery would have almost certainly required open surgical repair. These data also informed about the level of manual compression on the arm. This observation revealed profound dye extravasation of the proximal radial artery approximately 10.0 mm distal to the elbow joint. The hand was perfused via the ulnar artery.

The patient was subsequently referred to vascular surgery, orthopedic surgery, and interventional radiology departments, and immediate manual compression over the entire radial side of the forearm, from wrist to elbow, was performed. ACT was checked and was allowed to drift down to 200 to 250 range without protamine administration. A 2.5-mm × 120.0-mm balloon was advanced through the 5/6F 10.0-cm Glidesheath Slender into the proximal radial artery (extending into the intact vessel) and inflated up to 5 atm. Simultaneously, using anatomic and fluoroscopic landmarks and under direct ultrasound access, right CFA access was obtained and a 5F 10.0-cm sheath was placed using modified Seldinger technique. A 5F JR4 catheter was advanced over a 0.035-inch wire and used to selectively engage the brachiocephalic artery. The wire was advanced into the right brachial artery, and the JR4 catheter was exchanged for a 0.035-inch angled NaviCross catheter. Selective angiography of the right brachial artery was performed, demonstrating the radial artery injury with ongoing extravasation during periods of manual pressure release. Both ulnar and interosseous arteries were patent. After reviewing the films and discussion with interventional radiology, it was decided that there was no clearly available approach to repair the severely traumatized radial artery. The wire and sheath through the radial artery approach were removed, and 2 separate, large compression bands were placed over the proximal forearm and inflated with 20 mL of air. Had the compression bands been too short, a manual blood pressure cuff would have been used around the proximal forearm and inflated the cuff to suprasystemic pressure. Angiography from the brachial artery revealed resolution of bleeding into the forearm. The pulse oximeter probe demonstrated a dampened waveform, which slowly improved over the remainder of the procedure. At this point, the vascular and orthopedic teams presented to the catheterization laboratory for consultation. While vascular and orthopedic teams evaluated the patient, the interventional team completed intervention of the left CFA and SFA, including delivery and placement of a covered stent via the right femoral artery approach, which successfully sealed the perforation. Final angiography revealed improved flow in the leg compared with baseline. During discussion with the surgery team, it was agreed that urgent surgical bypass would be poorly tolerated by the patient given her age and multimorbidity. Consequently, it was decided to proceed with coil embolization of the right radial artery. With the assistance of interventional radiology, a 0.018-inch Progreat catheter (Terumo) was used to deliver a 6.0-mm × 20.0-mm Concerto detachable coil (Medtronic) into the proximal right radial artery. Angiography revealed continued flow and extravasation beyond the coil, so a 30.0-mm Packing Coil (Penumbra) was placed proximally to the Concerto coil, with particular care not to extend into the brachial artery (Figure 2). Angiography in orthogonal views revealed occlusion of the radial artery, no evidence of extravasation, and continued flow in the brachial, ulnar, and interosseous arteries with flow into the palmar arch (Figure 3). One of the radial compression bands was removed, and the other was deflated to light pressure. The right CFA sheath was removed, and hemostasis was achieved with manual pressure.

The patient’s postprocedural course included severe right arm ecchymoses but was otherwise uncomplicated. She was followed up by cardiology, orthopedic surgery, and occupational therapy. No further procedures were required. She was discharged with appropriate medical therapy for peripheral artery disease (including aspirin and lipid-lowering therapy) on hospital day 4.

Discussion

Initially developed in the 1940s using surgical cutdown by Radner,6 the radial artery approach has become a preferred method for many cardiovascular interventions because of low complication rates and patient comfort and convenience. Radial artery spasm and thrombotic occlusion are the most common complications.7, 8, 9 Serious, major vascular complications are rare (0.2% of cases),10 including the most feared complication: radial artery avulsion. Most published case reports describe successful surgical and hybrid surgical-endovascular management of radial artery avulsion.4,5 While surgical intervention should always be considered in these scenarios, potential operative and postoperative complications (especially in a frail population)11 must be considered in the decision-making process. To the best of our knowledge, only 1 previously published case report has reported on successful percutaneous management of radial artery avulsion during diagnostic coronary angiography.12 As for this case, the undeployed covered stent for the peripheral perforation would enter the radial artery sheath because of sheath collapse due to compression from radial artery spasm. Vasodilators were administered throughout (total verapamil of 2.5 mg and nitroglycerin of 1600 μg), although additional vasodilators and moderate sedation could have been used at this juncture. Coiling was the only percutaneous option to achieve hemostasis; fat arterial embolization would have failed because of the radial artery size (approximately 2.0-2.5 mm diameter), and a coronary artery covered stent was not available at an optimal length (20.0 cm). In retrospect, a better technique in this case would have involved snaring the 0.018-inch wire from the femoral approach and flossing the wire (from radial access to femoral access) to aid in coil delivery and maintain wire stability.

When not quickly recognized and managed, radial artery avulsion (and likewise, perforation) can lead to significant bleeding into the forearm (particularly in the anticoagulated patient), resulting in compartment syndrome.10 In such cases, manual compression of the forearm and balloon tamponade of the intact radial artery using the maintained wire position—should be performed to achieve hemostasis.

Physicians who perform radial artery procedures should be aware of this multifaceted, nonsurgical strategy to manage radial artery avulsion, which can ensure that the patient avoids the potential surgical risks of emergent vascular surgery.

Conclusion

Radial artery access has many advantages, particularly for patients at high risk for bleeding and vascular complications.1 Most previous studies describe surgical management of radial artery avulsion.3,4 This case report demonstrates that radial artery avulsion can be managed successfully using percutaneous, nonsurgical methods. Furthermore, while peripheral intervention from a radial artery approach may be appropriate in some patients, endovascular operators must always consider alternative access approaches for complication management.
