Copper coordination polymer for multifunctional energy applications: hydrogen release, supercapacitor, and oxygen evolution reaction
Authors: Zeinab Hussein Hashem, Laila H. Abdel-Rahman, Santiago Gómez-Ruiz, Hani Nasser Abdelhamid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of multifunctional materials for integrated energy conversion and storage is a critical challenge for sustainable energy solutions. A copper-based coordination polymer (Cu–BTEB) was synthesized by a reflux method using 4,4′,4″-(benzene-1,3,5-triyl)tris(ethyne-2,1-diyl)tribenzoic acid (H3BTEB) as the organic linker. The catalytic efficiency of Cu–BTEB for hydrogen production by NaBH4 hydrolysis exhibited a marked dependence on reactant concentration and temperature. Kinetic analyses demonstrated an activation energy of 72 kJ mol−1, whereas thermodynamic factors suggested that the process is advantageous at elevated temperatures. An HGR of 6623 mL H2 g−1 min−1 was observed at 25 °C using 1 mg of catalyst. The catalyst exhibited exceptional recyclability across five cycles. Electrochemical analyses revealed outstanding supercapacitor performance, displaying a specific capacitance of 290 F g−1 at 1 A g−1. Research on charge storage demonstrated a predominant pseudocapacitive behavior, with capacitive contributions rising from 58% to 95% as the scan rate increased. The electrode exhibited remarkable durability, maintaining about 100% of its capacitance after 5000 cycles. In the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), Cu–BTEB demonstrated a low overpotential of 270 mV at 10 mA cm−2, a Tafel slope of 124 mV dec−1, and a turnover frequency (TOF) of 0.09 mol O2 s−1, indicating favorable kinetics and high intrinsic catalytic activity.

Introduction

The rapid depletion of fossil fuel reserves, rising atmospheric greenhouse gas levels linked to global warming, and surging global energy demand—propelled by population growth, industrialization, economic development, and greater dependence on energy-intensive domestic technologies—require advances in renewable energy sources and in efficient, environmentally sustainable energy storage systems.1,2 The World Energy Council predicts that global energy demand will be about twice as high by 2050 as it is today (https://www.worldenergy.org). Ongoing reliance on fossil fuel combustion for energy generation will consequently pose substantial threats to human health, environmental integrity, energy security, and global ecosystems. Confronting these difficulties necessitates a comprehensive strategy that encompasses the extensive deployment of alternative energy sources, improvements in energy efficiency, and advances in modern energy storage technology.3,4 Energy storage devices are essential for alleviating this constraint by accumulating surplus energy and enabling steady power delivery to the grid.4,5 Furthermore, these devices may recover and store kinetic energy in applications such as regenerative braking in vehicles, thereby improving overall system efficiency. Among energy storage technologies, supercapacitors have emerged as viable alternatives, especially for applications requiring high power density and rapid charge–discharge.6,7 Supercapacitors have a higher specific capacitance than traditional electrostatic capacitors and exhibit a markedly longer cycle life than batteries.8 Supercapacitors exhibit high power densities, long operating lifetimes, rapid charging, and consistent performance across a broad temperature range.9 These characteristics highlight their capability for use in mobile and stationary energy storage applications. Several materials have been reported for supercapacitor electrode preparation, including carbon nanomaterials,10–12 metal oxides,13,14 metal hydroxides,15 metal phosphides,16 MXenes,17,18 polymers,19,20 and covalent–organic frameworks (COFs).21,22

Hybrid materials consisting of metal centers and organic ligands have garnered considerable interest for advanced technological applications owing to their structural plasticity and adjustable physicochemical properties.23,24 These metal–organic hybrid systems offer significant advantages over traditional materials, including enhanced optical properties, improved catalytic performance, adjustable structural configurations, increased adsorption capacity, efficient charge transport, and superior chemical and thermal stability.25–29 These features result from the rational integration of inorganic and organic components at the molecular or supramolecular scale. A notable category of these materials comprises microporous structures, including porous coordination polymers,30–33 metal–organic polyhedra (MOPs),34–36 metal–organic cages (MOCs),37 and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs).38–41 These structures exhibit distinct porosity, high surface area, and the ability to tailor pore dimensions and functionality through controlled synthesis. The integration of metal nodes and organic linkers enables fine-tuning of electrical structure and surface chemistry, rendering these materials highly versatile across many applications. The hybrid character of these systems enables them to transcend the inherent limitations of their individual components via synergistic interactions.42–45 The metal centers can offer catalytic activity or electronic conductivity, whilst the organic moieties enhance flexibility, utility, and structural variety. This synergistic effect results in superior overall performance that exceeds that of competitors that are solely inorganic or solely organic. Consequently, metal–organic hybrid materials have been thoroughly investigated across various domains, including advanced energy storage devices such as supercapacitors, where their high surface area and efficient charge-transfer pathways enhance capacitance and cycling stability. Moreover, their tunable chemical environments and selective binding properties render them exceptionally efficient for sensing applications, enabling the detection of gases, ions, and biomolecules with remarkable sensitivity and specificity.46

Copper-based coordination polymers (CPs) have garnered significant interest among transition-metal-based CPs due to their diverse structural topologies, tunable physicochemical properties, affordability, and broad applications. The presence of Cu(ii) centers imparts these materials with distinctive redox activity, catalytic capabilities, and electrical characteristics, rendering them interesting candidates for various technological and environmental applications. Copper-based CPs exhibit significant antibacterial efficacy against a broad range of pathogenic microorganisms, underscoring their potential applications in biomedical and healthcare sectors.47 Moreover, photophysical and electrical analyses have demonstrated that copper-containing coordination polymers possess superior optical and electronic properties, including high quantum yields and improved electrical conductivity relative to comparable transition-metal systems.48 These characteristics render them appealing materials for optoelectronic and sensing applications. The synthesis of copper-based CP thin films directly on copper electrodes was published, yielding durable tribopositive layers following brief growth durations.49 These films provide superior mechanical stability and surface functioning, beneficial for advanced coating and electrochemical applications. The advantageous electronic structure and effective charge-transfer properties of copper-based CPs enhance their exceptional photocatalytic efficacy in degrading organic pollutants, providing sustainable solutions for wastewater treatment and environmental remediation.50 In addition to photocatalysis, copper-based coordination polymers exhibit considerable catalytic efficacy across several chemical transformations. They have been effectively utilized as catalysts for the selective aerobic oxidation of veratryl alcohol, a significant lignin-derived biomass model molecule, in the absence of a base.51 Furthermore, these materials exhibit exceptional efficacy in the electrocatalytic reduction of nitrate to ammonia, presenting a viable pathway for nitrogen-cycle regulation and sustainable ammonia synthesis.52 Copper-based coordination polymers have been examined as effective catalysts for the thermal breakdown of ammonium perchlorate53 and as active agents in self-driven photoinduced C–H arylation reactions, demonstrating their versatility in catalytic applications.54 Besides their catalytic and environmental uses, copper-based coordination polymers have emerged as potential materials in agriculture. Their controlled-release properties, stability, and bioactivity enable their use in effective agrochemical formulations, enhancing nutrient uptake, plant protection, and sustainable farming fields. The diverse functions highlight the growing significance of copper-based coordination polymers as multifunctional materials for environmental, catalytic, energy, medicinal, and agricultural uses.55

The development of effective, durable, and sustainable electrocatalysts for the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is crucial for advancing contemporary energy conversion and storage technologies, especially in applications such as water electrolysis.56,57 Nonetheless, the OER is a significant kinetic impediment due to its inherently slow reaction kinetics, including a complex four-electron transfer mechanism at the anode.58,59 This multistep procedure leads to high overpotentials and constrains the overall efficiency of electrochemical energy systems.60,61 Noble metal oxides are regarded as premier electrocatalysts for the OER owing to their superior catalytic activity and comparatively advantageous kinetics.62,63 Notwithstanding these benefits, their actual implementation is significantly hindered by low abundance, high cost, and limited scalability.64,65 Furthermore, despite significant efforts to improve their catalytic performance and operational stability, these materials often degrade under extended operating conditions, thereby hindering their large-scale implementation.66 Considering these constraints, contemporary research has increasingly focused on investigating earth-abundant and economically viable alternatives.67,68 Specifically, 3D transition-metal-based (oxy)hydroxides and spinel-type oxides have emerged as promising candidates for OER catalysis.69,70 These materials offer multiple benefits, including affordability, high natural prevalence, and adjustable electronic configurations, thereby enhancing their catalytic performance.71,72 Thus, they constitute a feasible route to advancing scalable, sustainable electrocatalysts that meet the requirements of next-generation energy systems.73,74

A copper-based coordination polymer was synthesized by refluxing H3BTEB (4,4′,4″-(benzene-1,3,5-triyl)tris(ethyne-2,1-diyl)tribenzoic acid) as the organic linker. The synthesized material was methodically characterized through X-ray diffraction (XRD) to determine its crystalline structure, Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy to identify functional groups and coordination interactions, diffuse reflectance spectroscopy to evaluate the optical properties, Tauc plot to determine the optical bandgap, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) to analyze surface morphology, and energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis to verify elemental composition. The synthesized coordination polymer was examined as a multifunctional material for catalytic hydrogen production via hydrolysis of sodium borohydride (NaBH4), as well as for OER electrocatalysis and supercapacitor applications. The performance was thoroughly assessed via hydrogen gas evolution measurements and various electrochemical techniques, including cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) measurements, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), Tafel slope analysis, determination of the b-value, and charge storage mechanism analysis using Dunn's method, and chronopotentiometry for stability evaluation. The material exhibited exceptional multifunctional performance, with a high hydrogen generation rate (HGR), a low OER overpotential, a high specific capacitance, and remarkable long-term stability. Moreover, it demonstrated excellent recyclability and maintained electrochemical performance throughout prolonged operation, underscoring its potential as an effective and resilient alternative for integrated energy conversion and storage applications.

Materials and methods

Copper(ii) nitrate hemi (pentahydrate) Cu(NO3)2·2.5 H2O, H3BTEB, dimethylformamide (DMF), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF), and acetylene black were supplied by Sigma-Aldrich (Germany) and utilized without further purification.

Synthesis of Cu–BTEB coordination polymer

A copper-based coordination polymer was synthesized via a reflux method. Equimolar quantities of Cu(NO3)2·2.5 H2O and H3BTEB linker were first dissolved in 50 mL of DMF and sonicated at 80 °C for 180 min to ensure complete dissolution and homogeneous mixing. The solution was subsequently placed in a reflux system and heated at 100 °C for 10 h to promote coordination-driven self-assembly and crystallization. The blue precipitate formed after cooling to room temperature was filtered, extensively washed with DMF and ethanol, and vacuum-dried at 100 °C for 12 h. The obtained material is denoted as Cu–BTEB.

The catalytic performance of Cu–BTEB for hydrogen evolution was evaluated by hydrolysing NaBH4via a water-displacement method. The generated hydrogen gas was collected and monitored with a calibrated 50 mL burette to precisely measure gas evolution. Electrode preparation and electrochemical measurements

Cu–BTEB (80 wt%, 10 mg), acetylene black (10 wt%), and PVDF (10 wt%) were mixed and dispersed in DMF (1 mL) to produce a uniform slurry for electrode fabrication. A drop (100 µL) of the slurry was coated onto nickel foam (1 × 1.5 cm2). The electrode was dried at 80 °C overnight.

Electrochemical studies were performed in a three-electrode system, where Cu–BTEB/Ni foam acted as the working electrode, Hg/HgO as the reference electrode, a platinum plate as the counter electrode, and 6 M KOH as the electrolyte. Characterization techniques

XRD measurements were performed using a Philips X'Pert diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation), while FT-IR spectra were obtained with a Nicolet 6700 spectrometer. SEM and EDX were used to investigate surface morphology and elemental composition. The optical properties of the synthesized Cu-BTEB coordination polymer were investigated by DRS using a Shimadzu UV-vis spectrophotometer (Nakagyo-ku, Japan). The optical band gap was estimated using the Tauc method by plotting (αhν)0.5 as a function of photon energy, where the photon energy was calculated according to the relation (E (eV) = 1240/λ (nm)). The extrapolation of the linear portion of the Tauc plot to the energy axis was used to determine the optical band gap.

Results and discussion

Materials synthesis and characterization

The Cu–BTEB coordination polymer was synthesized via a reflux, which enables the assembly of metal ions and organic ligands into a framework (Fig. 1). This method involves dissolving a copper salt and a tricarboxylate organic linker (H3BTEB) in DMF. Sonication facilitates complete dissolution and guarantees uniform dispersion of the reactants, which is crucial for attaining consistent nucleation and expansion of the coordination network. Heating under reflux facilitates coordination interactions between Cu2+ ions and the deprotonated carboxylate groups of the linker, thereby promoting self-assembly and yielding a coordination polymer. The increased temperature accelerates reaction kinetics, promoting bond formation and structural arrangement. Nucleation and crystal growth occur concurrently, forming a solid coordination compound, which is then isolated as a blue precipitate and washed to remove residual solvent and unreacted species (Fig. 1). The reflux method offers an efficient and direct strategy for the synthesis of metal–organic coordination polymers. Characterization was performed using XRD (Fig. 2a), FT-IR spectroscopy (Fig. 2b), SEM (Fig. 3a and b), and EDX analysis (Fig. 3c and d).

Schematic representation of Cu–BTEB synthesis; a photo camera shows the linker color change to a blue precipitate upon formation of the coordination polymer.

Structural characterization of Cu-BTEB using (a) XRD, (b) FT-IR, (c) DRS, and (d) Tauc plot.

(a–c) SEM images, and (d) EDX analysis of Cu–BTEB.

XRD analysis shows broad diffraction features with no sharp peaks, indicating a predominantly amorphous or semicrystalline structure (Fig. 2a). Such structural characteristics are common in coordination polymers with extended organic linkers. FT-IR analysis verifies the coordination between Cu2+ ions and the H3BTEB linker, while the free linker displays a broad O–H stretching band near ∼3000 cm−1, associated with carboxylic acid groups (Fig. 2b). This band disappears in the Cu–BTEB spectrum, indicating deprotonation and coordination through carboxylate oxygen atoms. Additionally, the presence of COO− asymmetric and symmetric stretching vibrations at ∼1577 and ∼1395 cm−1, respectively, along with the shift of the carbonyl band from ∼1685 cm−1 to ∼1660 cm−1, further confirms metal–ligand coordination. A new band observed at ∼475 cm−1 is attributed to Cu–O stretching vibrations, providing strong evidence of coordination bond formation.

The DRS spectrum of Cu-BTEB exhibits strong absorption in the ultraviolet and visible regions, extending from approximately 200 to 500 nm, accompanied by a weaker and broader absorption band in the 500–800 nm range (Fig. 2c). The intense absorption observed at shorter wavelengths can be attributed primarily to ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) transitions involving the organic ligand and Cu(ii) centres. In contrast, the broad low-intensity absorption in the visible region is associated with the characteristic d–d electronic transitions of the Cu(ii) ions within the coordination framework. These properties explain the observed color of the materials shown in Fig. 1.

To further evaluate the electronic structure of the material, the optical band gap was determined from the Tauc plot (Fig. 2d). The calculated band-gap energy was found to be approximately 2.2 eV, indicating the semiconducting nature of the Cu–BTEB framework. This relatively narrow band gap facilitates visible-light absorption and may enhance charge-transfer processes, making the material potentially attractive for applications in photocatalysis, oelectronics, and photoelectrochemical energy conversion.

SEM images reveal irregularly shaped particles with a porous architecture (Fig. 3a and b). The pore sizes range from approximately 10 to 35 nm, indicating a mesoporous structure. The EDX results verify the presence of C, O, and Cu, with no detectable impurities, confirming the purity of the synthesized material (Fig. 3c and d). It shows the chemical formula is Cu279O52Cu. The high carbon and oxygen content, along with relatively low copper content, suggest that the material is best described as a copper-coordinated porous organic framework rather than a simple stoichiometric compound. This composition is consistent with copper-modified porous organic polymers, in which metal centers are distributed throughout an extended organic network. Overall, the formation of the Cu–BTEB coordination polymer is confirmed by structural, spectroscopic, and morphological analyses revealing a porous architecture and well-defined metal–ligand interactions.

Hydrogen generation via NaBH4 hydrolysis

The effect of NaBH4 concentration (0.2–3.0 wt%) on hydrogen production was examined with a constant catalyst loading (1 mg) (Fig. 4a). The findings indicate that the volume of hydrogen produced increases systematically with both reaction duration and NaBH4 concentration, suggesting that greater substrate availability enhances the total hydrogen-generation potential. This behavior is ascribed to the high quantity of reactive BH4− species involved in the hydrolysis reaction.

Hydrogen production via (a) NaBH4 hydrolysis, (b) HGR, and (c) cycling stability of Cu-BTEB up to 5 runs at 298 K, and (d) the hydrogen productivity and HGR of Cu–BTEB during the reusability test.

Conversely, the impact of catalyst loading indicates that HGR, when calculated per unit catalyst mass, decreases as catalyst mass increases (Fig. 4b). This trend may correlate with the agglomeration of catalyst particles at high loadings, thereby reducing active-site accessibility and constraining mass transfer.

The recyclability and operational stability of the Cu–BTEB catalyst were assessed during five successive cycles (Fig. 4c and d). The duration required for total hydrogen release (100% conversion) decreased sequentially from 9.5 minutes in the first cycle to 7.26, 6.51, 6.28, and 5.7 minutes in the second through fifth cycles, respectively. This enhancement indicates an activation mechanism during the initial application, perhaps due to increased exposure of active sites or structural reconfiguration, illustrating the catalyst's excellent reusability and stability. The influence of temperature (30–60 °C) on hydrogen production was then analyzed (Fig. 5a). As anticipated, raising the reaction temperature considerably accelerates hydrolysis, reducing the time required to reach the final hydrogen volume. The HGR significantly increases from approx. 714.3 to 2056, 4664, and 9470 mL H2 g−1 min−1 at 30, 40, 50, and 60 °C, respectively, underscoring the significant effect of temperature on the catalytic process (Fig. 5b). Arrhenius (Fig. 5c) and Eyring (Fig. 5d) plots were generated to gain deeper insight into the reaction kinetics and thermodynamics. Both plots demonstrate exceptional linearity, with correlation coefficients (R2) nearing 0.99, thus affirming the validity of the kinetic study. The figures indicated that the activation energy (Ea) is 72 kJ mol−1, and the activation entropy (ΔS is 236 J mol−1 K−1. The Gibbs free energy of activation (ΔG‡) values were determined to be −2.29, −4.66, −7.02, and −9.39 kJ mol−1 at temperatures of 303, 313, 323, and 333 K, respectively. The declining trend in ΔG‡ with increasing temperature indicates that the reaction becomes thermodynamically more favorable at higher temperatures, thereby reinforcing the observed improvement in catalytic efficacy.

Supercapacitor application

The electrochemical performance of the material for supercapacitors is evaluated using CV (Fig. 6a), log(i) vs. log(ν) (Fig. 6b), sqrt(ν) vs. i(V)/sqrt(ν) (Fig. 6c), capacitance and diffusion contribution (Fig. 6d), GCDC (Fig. 7a), specific capacitance vs. current densities (Fig. 7b), and cycling (Fig. 7c).

(a) CV, (b) log(i) vs. log(v), (c) sqrt(v) vs. i(V)/sqrt(v), and (d) contribution of capacitance and diffusion.

(a) GCD profiles of Cu–BTEB at different current densities, (b) corresponding specific capacities, and (c) cycling performance in a three-electrode configuration.

The CV curves obtained at scan rates between 1 and 200 mV s−1 show distinct anodic and cathodic redox peaks, indicating that the charge storage mechanism is primarily governed by pseudocapacitance arising from rapid, reversible faradaic processes (Fig. 6a). With an increase in scan rate, the current response increases correspondingly, while the general morphology of the CV curves remains predominantly intact. This trend signifies swift charge-transfer kinetics and excellent electrochemical reversibility of the Cu–BTEB electrode. The link between peak current (i) and scan rate (v) was examined using a power-law model to further investigate the charge storage mechanism (Fig. 6b). The linear correlation between log(i) and log(ν) (Fig. 6b) exhibits exceptional fitting quality, with statistical parameters comprising N = 8, degrees of freedom (DoF) = 6, χ2/DoF = 0.00406, residual sum of squares (RSS) = 0.02436, coefficient of determination (R2) = 0.9944, and root mean square error (RMSE) = 0.0637. The computed b-value is approximately 1, indicating that the charge storage mechanism is mostly surface-controlled and capacitive rather than diffusion-limited. This verifies that pseudocapacitive mechanisms govern the material's electrochemical properties. Further understanding was acquired by the application of Dunn's technique. The linear correlation between i(V)/sqrt(ν) and sqrt(ν) (Fig. 6c) demonstrates strong concordance with a coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.962), a χ2/DoF of 2.35 × 10−8, a residual sum of squares (RSS) of 7.04 × 10−8, and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.53 × 10−4, so affirming the robustness of the analysis. The quantitative analysis of capacitive and diffusion-controlled contributions (Fig. 6d) indicates that the capacitive contribution markedly escalates with increasing scan rates, attaining values of 58%, 70%, 75%, 81%, 86%, 91%, 93%, and 95% at scan rates of 1, 3, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, and 200 mV s−1, respectively. The diffusion-controlled contribution diminishes from 42% to 5%. This pattern indicates that at high scan rates, charge storage is increasingly governed by surface redox reactions, which are more faster and more accessible than diffusion-controlled processes.

The GCD curves recorded at different current densities show non-linear behavior with slight voltage plateaus, confirming the pseudocapacitive nature of the electrode (Fig. 7a). The specific capacitances derived from the discharge curves are 290, 258, 252, 234, 163, 125, and 67 F g−1 at current densities ranging from 1 to 100 A g−1 (Fig. 7b). The decrease in capacitance at higher current densities is attributed to limited ion diffusion and reduced utilization of active sites at fast charge–discharge rates. Nonetheless, the electrode maintains a significant portion of its capacitance even at high current densities, demonstrating excellent rate capability. The long-term cycling stability of the Cu–BTEB electrode was evaluated over 5000 charge–discharge cycles at a current density of 10 A g−1 (Fig. 7c). The capacitance retention showed negligible degradation during cycling, indicating excellent durability and electrochemical stability. This outstanding performance can be attributed to the robust coordination framework and stable metal–ligand interactions, which avert structural collapse during repeated redox cycling.

Oxygen evolution reaction (OER)

The catalytic behavior of Cu–BTEB toward OER was examined through LSV measurements and kinetic studies (Fig. 8 and 9). The LSV curve shows a marked increase in current density beyond roughly 0.5 V vs. Hg/HgO, indicating the onset of oxygen evolution (Fig. 8a and b). Furthermore, two distinct oxidation peaks, detected at around 0.03 V and 0.3 V, indicate reversible redox transitions at the copper active sites, contributing to OER activity (Fig. 8a and b). After conversion to the RHE scale, an overpotential of 270 mV is observed at 10 mA cm−2 for the Cu–BTEB catalyst (Fig. 8c). However, the mass loading (1 mg) of the active component is low, but a significant change in the nickel (Ni) foam response was observed (Fig. 8a and b). Bare nickel foam shows low current compared to Cu–BTEB@Ni foam, with an overpotential of over 570 eV. This comparatively low overpotential indicates effective catalytic performance and is advantageous relative to other published non-noble metal-based electrocatalysts.

LSV measurements showing (a) potential reference, and (b) potential referenced to RHE.

(a) Tafel slope, (b) TOF plotted against overpotential, and (c) chronopotentiometry.

The reaction kinetics were studied using Tafel plots (Fig. 9a), which showed excellent linearity, with a correlation coefficient of approximately 0.99. The Tafel slope of 124 mV dec−1 indicates relatively rapid kinetics, suggesting that the reaction is controlled by a rate-limiting step likely involving adsorption or electron-transfer mechanisms characteristic of transition-metal-based OER catalysts.

TOF analysis (Fig. 9b) was used to evaluate the inherent catalytic activity. At an overpotential of 270 mV, the TOF is estimated to be roughly 0.09 mol O2 s−1, indicating a substantial density of active sites and effective catalytic turnover. The linear dependence of the oxidation peak current on scan rate further confirms that the process is surface-controlled, aligning with the pseudocapacitive characteristics observed in supercapacitor research. The Cu–BTEB material exhibits commendable OER performance, marked by small overpotential, advantageous kinetics, and high intrinsic activity. The interaction between the copper centers and the porous organic framework enhances charge transfer, promotes electrolyte diffusion, and provides numerous accessible active sites, thereby making this material sustainable electrocatalytic materials for water electrolysis applications.

The long-term stability of the material for the OER was assessed using chronopotentiometric measurements (Fig. 9c). The catalyst exhibited a stable potential response throughout the 12 h testing period, indicating its excellent durability and sustained electrocatalytic performance under prolonged operating conditions.

Conclusion

This study synthesized a copper-based coordination polymer (Cu–BTEB) via the reflux technique and comprehensively assessed its multifunctional energy applications. Structural and spectroscopic investigations validated the establishment of a porous coordination framework characterized by robust metal–ligand interactions. The material exhibited superior catalytic efficiency for hydrogen generation via NaBH4 hydrolysis. The catalyst demonstrated excellent recyclability and enhanced performance across multiple cycles, underscoring its durability. In supercapacitor applications, Cu–BTEB exhibited a notable specific capacitance of 290 F g−1 and exceptional rate capability, with predominant pseudocapacitive behavior, as validated by kinetic studies. The electrode also demonstrated excellent cycling stability, maintaining performance over 5000 cycles without noticeable degradation. Additionally, the material exhibited notable OER electrocatalytic activity with a low overpotential (270 mV at 10 mA cm−2), a Tafel slope of 124 mV dec−1, and a TOF of 0.09 mol O2 s−1. The results demonstrate effective charge transfer and favorable catalytic kinetics. The Cu–BTEB coordination polymer exhibits considerable promise as a multifunctional material for hydrogen production, energy storage, and electrocatalysis. The combined incorporation of copper active sites with a framework offers a potent approach for developing advanced energy materials.
