A novel small-molecule inhibitor TD6 targets prohibitin 1 (PHB1) and suppresses colorectal cancer pulmonary metastasis by destabilizing mitochondrial complex I
Authors: Kai Wang, Pengfei Xu, Yu Hu, Dengdeng Ji, Gui Shao, Mengxi Chen, Ziyan Yang, Yujing Yang, Yinshuang Chen, Xinyu Chen, Jiayi Zhou, Tingwei Ye, Jiayu Xu, Mengmeng Wang, Weitao Zhang, Jianqing Ruan, Haiyang Zhang, Huanqiu Li, Weipeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the limited efficacy of existing therapies for metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC), there is an urgent need for novel strategies. Prohibitin 1 (PHB1) is significantly upregulated in CRC, where it plays a critical role in oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) to meet the heightened energy demands of rapid tumor growth and metastasis. Here, we discover that PHB1 interacts with NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit S1 (NDUFS1), a subunit of the OXPHOS complex, thereby modulating mitochondrial respiratory function. Based on these insights, we developed TD6, a highly potent and selective small-molecule inhibitor of PHB1. In a mouse model of colorectal cancer pulmonary metastasis (CRPM), TD6 treatment significantly prolonged animal survival. Mechanistically, TD6 binds to PHB1 and induces a conformational change in the PHB complex, which reduces PHB-mediated stabilization of NDUFS1. Disruption of the PHB-NDUFS1 interaction promotes NDUFS1 degradation via the lysosomal pathway, leading to impaired activity of mitochondrial complex I (MCI) and reduced OXPHOS function. In summary, PHB1 maintains MCI stability and OXPHOS activity through its interaction with NDUFS1. By targeting PHB1, TD6 effectively disrupts this regulatory axis, demonstrating therapeutic potential against CRPM. This study presents a novel drug candidate and a theoretical foundation for PHB1-based interventions targeting cancer energy metabolism.Keywords: Metastatic colorectal cancer, NDUFS1, Oxidative phosphorylation, PHB1, Small-molecule inhibitor

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major global health concern, characterized by high incidence, aggressive progression, and substantial mortality, making it one of the leading causes of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1]. Alarmingly, both incidence and mortality rates continue to rise [[1], [2], [3]]. Current treatment primarily relies on surgical resection, systemic therapy, and radiotherapy; however, managing colorectal cancer pulmonary metastasis (CRPM) remains particularly difficult [4]. Therapeutic options are often limited to a subset of patients and are frequently compromised by the development of drug resistance [4]. Consequently, identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing effective treatments for metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) are critical priorities in oncology research.

Prohibitin (PHB) is a highly conserved protein family essential for diverse cellular processes, including mitochondrial stress signaling, cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and lifespan regulation [5]. This family comprises two subtypes, prohibitin 1 (PHB1) and prohibitin 2 (PHB2) [6,7], which are localized to various cellular compartments such as mitochondria, the nucleus, and the plasma membrane [6,8]. Data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Human Proteome Atlas (HPA) reveal that PHB1 and PHB2 are expressed at elevated mRNA and protein levels in multiple cancers, including gastric cancer, CRC, and high-grade breast cancer [9,10]. Notably, PHB1 is frequently overexpressed in human malignancies and contributes to key oncogenic processes such as cell proliferation [11,12], evasion of apoptosis [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]], and metastasis [18]. PHB1 forms a ring-like complex with PHB2 on the mitochondrial inner membrane [19], where it is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial structural integrity and functional homeostasis [8,20], particularly in the assembly and activity of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system [5]. Furthermore, studies indicate that the PHB complex can stabilize mitochondrial complex I (MCI) by directly interacting with its subunit NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit S1 (NDUFS1), thereby enhancing OXPHOS activity and promoting CRC cell proliferation and tumorigenesis [21]. Given the critical role of PHB1 in regulating mitochondrial respiration and the heightened energy demands of tumor cells, its overexpression likely fuels tumorigenesis and progression by reprogramming cellular energy metabolism.

The OXPHOS system consists of five multi-subunit complexes (I–V) embedded in the mitochondrial inner membrane. The electron transport chain (ETC), comprising complexes I-IV along with mobile electron carriers ubiquinone and cytochrome c [22,23], generates a proton gradient that drives adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthesis [24]. Accumulating evidence shows that cancer cells frequently upregulate OXPHOS to meet the bioenergetic demands of rapid growth and metastasis [25]. The PHB complex participates in regulating OXPHOS activity by physically interacting with specific subunits of the respiratory complexes to modulate their stability and translation [26]. Studies in models such as Saccharomyces cerevisiae demonstrate that PHB is involved in the assembly of the OXPHOS system [27] and in maintaining the stability of mitochondrial membrane proteins [5].

Compared with corresponding normal tissues, PHB expression is significantly altered in cancers [10]. Its involvement in critical processes such as proliferation, apoptosis, and metastasis in CRC underscores its potential as a novel therapeutic target [28]. Several small-molecule inhibitors of PHB1 have been reported, including Rocaglamide A, a natural compound that disrupts the PHB1-Raf-1 interaction to inhibit extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) signaling in leukemia cells [29,30]. Other natural products, such as Xanthohumol and Rabdosianone I, target PHB-related pathways to suppress cancer cell growth [31]. However, most of these agents are natural products with complex structures, posing challenges for synthesis and modification. Their potential toxicity and side effects also remain poorly characterized [32]. Therefore, developing novel, synthetically tractable small-molecule inhibitors of PHB1 with validated efficacy in CRC holds significant scientific and clinical promise [33].

Addressing the challenges in developing PHB1 inhibitors, we first identified potential binding pockets on PHB1 based on the PHB complex structure [34,35]. Through virtual screening, biological validation, and structural optimization, we identified TD6 as a promising PHB1-targeting inhibitor. Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) and binding affinity studies confirmed a robust and specific interaction between TD6 and PHB1. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations revealed that TD6 binding induces significant conformational changes in the PHB complex. Pharmacological evaluations demonstrated that TD6 exerts potent anti-tumor activity against CRPM both in vitro and in vivo. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) experiments verified the interaction between PHB1 and NDUFS1. Integrated proteomic and bioinformatic analyses revealed that TD6 selectively binds to PHB1, inducing a conformational change in the PHB complex that disrupts the PHB-NDUFS1 interaction. This leads to NDUFS1 degradation via the lysosomal pathway, resulting in the suppression of MCI activity and cellular OXPHOS. In summary, we have identified TD6 as a novel PHB1 inhibitor with significant anti-CRPM activity. This study elucidates its mechanism of action, providing a new therapeutic target and a promising strategy for the treatment of CRPM.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

All cell lines were acquired from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The human CRC cell line T84 was grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Procell, China), while HCT116 was maintained in McCoy's 5A medium (Procell, China), each supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). The murine CRC line CT26-luc was cultured in RPMI 1640 (Procell, China) with 10% FBS. All lines were authenticated by short tandem repeat profiling and tested negative for mycoplasma. Cells were kept at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Bioinformatics and public data analysis

To evaluate PHB1 expression in CRC, we utilized the GEPIA 2 web server (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/), which analyzes RNA sequencing expression data from TCGA (https://www.cancer.gov) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) (http://commonfund.nih.gov/GTEx) projects. This platform was used to perform differential expression analysis between colon adenocarcinoma (COAD)/rectal adenocarcinoma (READ) and matched normal tissues. Validation of PHB1 upregulation in colorectal adenoma and primary CRC tissues was performed using datasets retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The expression of PHB1 protein was collected from the HPA database (https://www.proteinatlas.org). For prognostic analysis, the Kaplan-Meier plotter database (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) was employed to assess the correlation between PHB1 mRNA expression and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in CRC patients.

Transfection of siRNA and plasmid

T84 and HCT116 cells were transfected with siRNA or plasmid DNA using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium (Hyclone, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. siRNA targeting PHB1 was designed and synthesized by Genepharma (China). All expression plasmids were constructed and verified by Azenta (China).

Quantitative PCR

T84 and HCT116 cells were transfected with 100 nM PHB1 siRNA for 48 h. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the supplied protocol and cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using random hexamer primers (TaKaRa, Japan) and a reverse transcription kit (Thermo, USA). Quantitative PCR was performed using the PHB1 forward primer (5′-CTA TGA TGA GCG TGT GCT GC-3′) and reverse primer (5′-GCT CTG TAA GGT CGT CGC TC-3′).

Western blot

Cells were harvested and lysed in lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (TaKaRa, Japan). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies used were: PHB1 (#2426, Proteintech, USA), NDUFS1 (#60153, Proteintech, USA), β-actin (#AF0003, Beyotime, China), β-tubulin (#10094-1-AP, Beyotime, China), and Flag (#M185-3L, MBL Beijing biotech, China). For the evaluation of kinase signaling pathways, primary antibodies against p-AMPKα (Thr172) (#2535, Cell signaling technology, USA), AMPKα (#2795, Cell signaling technology, USA), p-ACC (Ser80) (#3661, Cell signaling technology, USA), ACC (#3662, Cell signaling technology, USA), p-mTOR (Ser2448) (#2971, Cell signaling technology, USA), mTOR (T55306, Abmart, China), p-S6K (Thr389/Thr412) (#9205, Cell signaling technology, USA), and S6K (T55324, Abmart, China) were used. Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed and incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence system and quantified via ImageJ software.

Co-IP

Cells were transfected with PHB1-3×Flag plasmid for 48 h, harvested, and lysed in IP-compatible lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). For IP assay, cell lysates were incubated with 2 μg of anti-Flag antibody or control mouse IgG overnight at 4 °C with gentle rotation, followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads for 3 h. Beads were washed extensively with lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× laemmli sample buffer containing 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). The eluted proteins were subsequently analyzed by Western blot analysis as described above.

Methyl thiazolyl tetrazolium (MTT) for cell viability assay

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. After transfecting with 100 nM siRNA for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 0.5 mg/mL MTT reagent and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The formazan crystals formed were dissolved in DMSO (Meilunbio, China), and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

Clone formation assay

T84 and HCT116 cells were transfected with plasmids or small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) for 24 h and then were seeded into six-well plates (500 cells per well) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 weeks. Clones were stained with 0.1% crystal violet.

Cell thermal shift assay

PHB1-Flag protein was immunopurified from T84 cell lysates. The purified protein was diluted in phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and divided into two aliquots, one treated with DMSO and the other with 200 μM TD6, and incubated for 0.5 h at room temperature. Each sample was further divided and heated at different temperatures (39.0, 42.3, 46.4, 51.9, 56.1, and 59.0 °C) for 3 min, then cooled on ice. Protein stability was assessed by immunoblotting.

Transwell migration assay

To minimize the potential influence of cell proliferation on Transwell migration assays, HCT116 cells were pretreated with mitomycin C (10 μg/mL) for 1 h before seeding. After pretreatment, cells were washed with PBS, harvested, and resuspended in serum-free medium. Equal numbers of viable cells were then seeded into the upper chamber of transwell inserts. Briefly, 2 × 105 cells in 200 μL serum-free medium were placed in the upper chamber, and the lower chamber was filled with 600 μL complete medium containing 20% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 48 h of incubation, non-migrated cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed. Migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, photographed under a microscope, and counted in eight randomly selected fields at 100× magnification.

Animal studies

Male BALB/c mice (4-5 weeks old, SPF grade) were purchased from GemPharmatech (China). After one week of acclimatization, CT26-luc cells (4 × 105 cells in 100 μL PBS per mouse) were injected into the tail vein to establish a CRPM model. One week later, tumor engraftment was confirmed by in vivo bioluminescence imaging. For the in vivo CRPM experiment, mice were randomly assigned to four groups: vehicle, 20 mg/kg 5-FU, 20 mg/kg TD6, and 40 mg/kg TD6, with 6 mice in each group. D-Luciferin (15 mg/mL in saline) was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 3 mg per mouse under isoflurane anesthesia for imaging. Compounds including 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) and TD6 derivatives were dissolved in a vehicle consisting of 10% DMSO, 40% PEG300, 5% Tween 80, and 45% saline. Mice received intraperitoneal injections every two days for two weeks. Tumor growth was monitored weekly via bioluminescence imaging, and body weight and survival were recorded throughout the study.

Target pulldown

Biotin-conjugated TD6 (TD6-PEG6-biotin) was synthesized and used for pull-down assays. T84 cells expressing TD6-Flag were lysed, and the lysates were incubated with 0.2 mM TD6-PEG6-biotin for 2 h at 4 °C. Streptavidin-coupled magnetic Dynabeads (Thermo, USA) were added and incubated for an additional hour at room temperature. Beads were washed thoroughly, and bound proteins were eluted with 2× Laemmli buffer containing 100 mM DTT and detected by immunoblotting or mass spectrometry (MS).

MS analysis

Protein samples obtained from pull-down assays were prepared for MS analysis as follows. Samples were first lyophilized and resuspended in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.5) containing 8 M urea. Proteins were reduced with 20 mM DTT at 100 °C for 5 min and then alkylated with 40 mM iodoacetamide in the dark at room temperature for 40 min. The urea concentration was diluted to 1 M using 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8.5). Trypsin (sequencing grade, Promega) was added at a 1:25 enzyme-to-substrate ratio, and digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C with gentle shaking. The resulting peptides were acidified with 10% trifluoroacetic acid to pH < 3 and desalted using StagTip C18 tips (Protea Biosciences). After desalting, peptides were resuspended in 0.1% formic acid and quantified via Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific).

Liquid chromatography was performed on an Easy-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a self-packed analytical column (25 cm × 75 μm i.d., 360 μm o.d.) containing C18 resin (2.2 μm particle size, 100 Å pore size; Michrom Bioresources). The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (0.1% formic acid in 80% acetonitrile). Peptides were separated using a 60-min linear gradient: 8-10% B in 1 min, 10-32% B over 51 min, 32-95% B in 1 min, followed by 7 min at 95% B, with a constant flow rate of 300 nL/min.

MS analysis was conducted on a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) in data-dependent acquisition mode. Full MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 60,000 over the m/z range 400-1200, with an automatic gain control target of 3 × 106 and maximum injection time of 30 ms. The top 20 most intense precursor ions (charge states +2 to +7) were selected for fragmentation by higher-energy collisional dissociation with a normalized collision energy of 28%. MS/MS spectra were acquired at 30,000 resolution with an automatic gain control target of 1 × 105 and maximum injection time of 50 ms. The isolation window was set to 1.6 m/z, and dynamic exclusion was applied for 40 s to prevent repeated sequencing.

Raw MS data were processed using PEAKS Online software (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc.) against the UniProt human protein database (downloaded May 2023). Search parameters included: trypsin/P specificity with up to two missed cleavages; precursor mass tolerance of 10 ppm; fragment mass tolerance of 0.02 Da. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification, while methionine oxidation and protein N-terminal acetylation were included as variable modifications. A false discovery rate of ≤1% was applied at both peptide-spectrum match and protein levels. Proteins were identified with at least two unique peptides, and label-free quantification was based on extracted ion chromatograms of the identified peptides.

Molecular docking-based virtual screening

The crystal structure of the PHB complex (PHB:8RRH) was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org). Putative binding sites were predicted and identified using the SiteMap module in Schrödinger as well as several web servers, including DrugRep (http://cao.labshare.cn:10180/DrugRep/php/index.php), CB-DOCK (http://183.56.231.194:8001/cb-dock2/index.php), and Dogsitescorer (https://www.biosolveit.de/academic-drug-discovery/#DoGSiteScorer). For virtual screening via Glide docking, the protein structure was first prepared using the Protein Preparation Wizard module in Schrödinger 9.0. This involved removing non-essential water molecules, repairing incomplete side chains, and adding missing hydrogen atoms. The system was then parameterized with the OPLS2005 force field, assigning appropriate protonation states and atomic partial charges to each residue. A docking grid was generated using the Receptor Grid Generation module, centered on the predicted active site and sized 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å to fully encompass the potential ligand-binding region.

The compound library for screening was obtained from the ChemDiv database (TargetMol), which contains over one million small molecules with high chemical diversity. All molecules were pre-processed using LigPrep. At pH 7.0 ± 2.0, possible tautomers and stereoisomers (up to 32 per molecule, as set in Epik) were generated for each compound. After this preparation, a virtual library containing more than 2.6 million valid conformations was produced for subsequent molecular docking using Glide in standard precision (SP) or extra precision (XP) mode. This workflow ensures the structural accuracy and chemical representativeness of the screening, providing a reliable basis for identifying potential high-affinity ligands.

MD simulations

To determine a plausible transmembrane orientation, we performed membrane embedding analysis using the Orientations of Proteins in Membranes database on the conformations of the PHB complex bound to single and five TD6 domains. An all-atom system comprising solvent and a lipid bilayer was constructed using CHARMM-GUI. Each protein–ligand complex was embedded into an 80 Å × 80 Å 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) lipid bilayer. During embedding, lipid molecules within 0.6 Å of any protein atom were automatically removed to avoid steric clashes. The system was fully solvated with TIP3P water and neutralized by adding 0.154 M Na+/Cl− ions to mimic physiological salt conditions. Force field parameters for the ligand TD6 were derived from quantum chemical calculations: atomic partial charges were obtained via RESP fitting at the HF/6-31G∗ level using Gaussian 16 after geometry optimization. The protein was described with the AMBER ff14SB force field, and POPC lipids were modeled with the lipid14 force field. An energy minimization protocol was carried out in four stages, each comprising 20,000 steps: first on water molecules (with other atoms fixed), then on POPC lipids, followed by the ligand and receptor separately, and finally on the entire system. All systems were subsequently subjected to 200 ns molecular dynamics simulations under an NPT ensemble at 310 K and 1 atm. Covalent bonds involving hydrogen were constrained with the SHAKE algorithm, allowing a 2-fs integration time step. Trajectories were saved every 10 ps for analysis. Simulations were performed using the sander module in AMBER 20. Production trajectories were analyzed with the CPPTRAJ module to compute the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and free energy landscape (FEL).

Measurement of intracellular NAD+/NADH ratio

Cells were collected, washed with PBS, lysed, and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C). The supernatant was used for NAD+/NADH measurement (Beyotime, China). NADH standards were prepared by serial dilution. For each sample, untreated lysate and lysate heated at 60 °C for 30 min were added to wells, followed by ethanol dehydrogenase working solution. After incubation with chromogenic reagent at 37 °C, absorbance at 450 nm was recorded. NAD+, NADH, and the NAD+/NADH ratio were calculated according to the standard curve.

Intracellular ATP assay

ATP working solution was prepared by mixing luciferin with luciferase. After drug treatment, 100 μL of working solution was added to each well, shaken at 25 °C for 2 min, and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Luminescence was measured using a multimode plate reader.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species and mitochondrial membrane potential

To assess intracellular and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, cells were seeded in multi-well plates and treated with PHB1 siRNA or TD6 at the indicated concentrations. Following treatment, cells were washed and incubated with 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) or MitoSOX Red (Beyotime, China) at 37 °C in the dark, according to the manufacturer's instructions. DCFH-DA oxidation was used as an indicator of general intracellular oxidant production, while MitoSOX Red oxidation served as a marker for mitochondrial superoxide-related signals. To minimize artifacts inherent to these redox-sensitive probes, background fluorescence was subtracted using cell-free, dye-only blank wells. Additionally, parallel samples were pre-incubated with N-acetylcysteine as a general quencher to confirm the specificity of the oxidation signals. For the evaluation of mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were stained with JC-1 dye. After incubation, cells were washed thoroughly with PBS to remove excess probes. Representative images were immediately captured using a fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity quantification was performed using ImageJ software to determine the relative fluorescence of the oxidized DCFH-DA and MitoSOX Red products, as well as the ratio of JC-1 polymer (red fluorescence) to monomer (green fluorescence) as an indicator of mitochondrial membrane potential.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted with GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Data are expressed as mean ± s.d., unless stated otherwise. For comparisons between two groups, two-tailed unpaired student's t-test were used. Comparisons among multiple groups were carried out by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post-hoc tests. Survival curves based on the Kaplan–Meier method were compared using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. A p-value <0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

PHB1 upregulation drives CRC progression and lung metastasis

To determine the widespread clinical relevance of PHB1 and its specific role in CRC, we first analyzed its expression profile across various human malignancies using the GEPIA2 database. This pan-cancer analysis revealed that PHB1 is significantly overexpressed in COAD and READ, compared with the corresponding normal tissues (Fig. 1A), suggesting a broad oncogenic potential. Further analysis of clinical data from the TCGA database confirmed that PHB1 expression was also markedly elevated in colorectal adenoma and CRC tissues (Fig. 1B and C). To assess the clinical impact of this aberrant expression, we performed survival analysis on the TCGA cohort. Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated that patients with high PHB1 expression experienced significantly reduced RFS (HR = 1.39, p = 2.8 × 10−10; Fig. S1A), indicating that PHB1 abundance is strongly associated with disease aggressiveness and poor clinical outcomes.

Critically, to link PHB1 to the lethal metastatic process, we compared its expression between primary tumors and distant metastases using combined data from the TCGA and GEO datasets. This comparative analysis revealed a pronounced and statistically significant enrichment of PHB1 expression in metastatic lesions (Fig. 1D). Building upon these compelling clinical and prognostic correlations, we performed functional validation studies via loss-of-function approaches. SiRNAs were utilized to efficiently silence PHB1 expression in the highly metastatic T84 cell line and the primary HCT116 cell line (Fig. S1B and S1C). Cell proliferation assays confirmed that PHB1 depletion resulted in a dose-dependent and significant reduction in cell viability (Fig. 1E). Importantly, long-term clonogenic assays further revealed that PHB1 knockdown dramatically suppressed the capacity of CRC cells to form colonies, underscoring its necessity for sustained growth and proliferation (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, in assays simulating the metastatic process, PHB1 knockdown profoundly suppressed the migratory capacity of both CRC cell lines (Fig. 1G). To determine whether PHB1 regulates HCT116 cell migration independent of its effect on cell proliferation, we repeated the transwell migration assay following pretreatment with Mitomycin C. Under these proliferation-controlled conditions, PHB1 knockdown still markedly reduced the number of migrated HCT116 cells, indicating that PHB1 depletion suppresses HCT116 cell migratory capacity rather than merely reducing the number of migrated cells through impaired proliferation (Fig. S1D).

In summary, multi-database clinical evidence and rigorous functional validation studies collectively establish PHB1 as a critical oncogenic driver and a powerful prognostic marker in CRC, particularly in driving the metastatic phenotype.

A small-molecule inhibitor is discovered for targeting PHB1

Although PHB1 represents a promising therapeutic target, the development of its small-molecule inhibitors remains challenging. To advance PHB1-targeted drug discovery, we implemented a structure-based virtual screening strategy (Fig. 2A). Using the resolved crystal structure of the PHB complex, potential ligand-binding pockets were predicted with CB-DOCK, and Site 1 was selected as the target binding site. Virtual screening was then performed against over 1 million compounds from the ChemDiv library (TargetMol) using three successive Glide docking modes of increasing precision (HTVS, SP, and XP) [36]. The top 760 ranked compounds were further refined by applying REOS rules [37] and Lipinski's Rule of Five [38] to exclude molecules with undesirable toxicity, reactivity, or poor drug-likeness. Additionally, a PAINS filter was used to remove potential false positives, and molecular fingerprint-based clustering eliminated structural redundancies. Finally, based on MM/GBSA binding free-energy predictions, 40 compounds were selected from the top 200 candidates for phenotypic screening of antitumor activity. As summarized in Table S1, several compounds showed inhibitory effects against T84 and SW620 cell lines, with V019–4141 (V1) displaying the highest potency. V1 was therefore designated as the hit compound for further structural optimization.

To improve inhibitory activity, V1 was docked into the PHB complex. The docking model revealed that its core scaffold, 2-(N-(2-(dimethylamino)ethyl) benzenesulfonamide)-N-(1H-imidazole-2-yl)acetamide, occupies the PHB binding site. Structural analysis indicated that Tyr3104 (chain K, PHB1) forms a hydrogen bond with the amide nitrogen of V1, while Glu3100 interacts with the tertiary amine via another hydrogen bond. This tertiary amine also participates in a salt bridge with Glu492 (chain B, PHB2). Moreover, Lys496 (chain B) engages in a π-cation interaction with the aromatic ring of V1 (Fig. S2). Collectively, these interactions suggest that V1 binds at an interface involving both PHB1 and PHB2 subunits, resulting in limited binding selectivity.

To enhance PHB1 affinity, strengthen hydrophobic contacts, reduce unoccupied space in the binding site, and improve inhibitory potency, we designed a series of V1 analogs. These included halogen-substituted derivatives (TD1-TD3) at the R1 phenyl position (Table S2), alkyl-chain variants at different sites, a bulkier isobutyl derivative (TD4-TD8), and additional modifications of other functional groups (TD9-TD15). Among these, TD6, featuring a 3,4-dimethyl substitution on the phenyl ring, exhibited markedly increased inhibitory activity. Compared with the original 3-fluoro-4-methyl motif, the two methyl groups adjust the spatial orientation of the phenyl ring, allowing a more favorable conformation in the binding pocket and thereby stabilizing interactions with surrounding amino acid residues.

TD6 exhibits high binding affinity and selectivity for PHB1

To validate the binding mode of TD6 with PHB1, molecular docking was performed. As shown in Fig. 2B, the tertiary amine of TD6 forms salt bridges with Asp3101 and Glu3097 on chain K (PHB1). In addition, the benzene ring adjacent to the sulfonamide group engages in π–π stacking with Tyr3104, while the imidazole ring interacts favorably with Arg3108. Compared with V1, TD6 establishes enhanced hydrophobic contacts with residues lining the binding pocket, enabling deeper penetration into the hydrophobic cavity. This improved occupancy contributes to both higher binding affinity and greater selectivity for PHB1. Notably, during structural optimization, non-essential hydrogen bonds with peripheral residues were deliberately minimized in TD6. This reduction helps avoid potential binding instability associated with weak or unproductive hydrogen bonds, thereby increasing overall binding robustness, likely a key factor underlying TD6's superior inhibitory activity over V1.

The docking model indicated that the benzene ring linked to the imidazole in TD6 is solvent-exposed. Based on this insight, we synthesized a biotin-labeled affinity probe, TZ1 (Fig. 2C), by conjugating PEG-biotin via an ester bond to this benzene ring. A second probe, TZ2 (Fig. 2C), was prepared by attaching biotin through an amide bond at the tertiary amine site on the sulfonamide group. Chemical pull-down assays using T84 cell lysates incubated with 200 μM TZ1 (with free biotin as a control) followed by quantitative MS revealed that TZ1 enriched PHB1 by 113-fold relative to the control, while PHB2 enrichment was 32-fold (Fig. 2D). Other proteins showed negligible enrichment. Subsequent immunoblot and silver-staining analyses further confirmed that TZ1 selectively and robustly pulled down PHB1 over the free-biotin control, displaying higher specificity for PHB1 than for PHB2 (Fig. 2E and F). These results preliminarily identify PHB1 as a potential target of TD6.

To evaluate the binding stability of TD6 with PHB1, the CETSA was performed. Treatment with TD6 increased the denaturation temperature of PHB1 by 7.44 °C compared with the DMSO control, indicating that TD6 effectively stabilizes PHB1 and enhances its thermal resistance (Fig. 2G and H).

Multivalent TD6 binding induces conformational rigidification and locking of the PHB complex

Given the symmetrical, repeating structure of the PHB complex, it can theoretically accommodate up to six TD6 molecules, one per PHB1 subunit, though steric hindrance and potential cooperativity may limit the actual occupancy. Binding is expected to occur dynamically and in a stepwise manner, with the first molecule influencing the affinity for subsequent ones. Based on the crystal structure, we constructed initial models of the PHB complex bound to one or five TD6 molecules and performed 200 ns all-atom MD simulations under physiological conditions (TIP3P water, 0.15 M NaCl). The simulations revealed that the number of bound TD6 molecules markedly affects the structural dynamics and global conformation of the complex. When one TD6 binds to the PHB complex, although the ligand stably binds to a single PHB1 (purple), the overall ring structure does not fully maintain its initial compact state. The side view reveals significant relaxation and asymmetric fluctuations in its lower substructure, indicating that single-point binding is insufficient to maintain the overall rigidity of the complex. Conversely, when five TD6 molecules simultaneously occupy multiple PHB1 subunits, a pronounced “conformation-locking” effect is triggered. Molecular dynamics simulations reveal that the binding of multiple TD6 molecules restricts relative motion between subunits, forcing the complex to maintain a specific, rigid conformation with slight distortion. This process causes the complex to lose its inherent dynamic plasticity. (Fig. 3A).

(E) The Rgs of backbone atoms in the two systems. (F) The free energy landscape analysis of two systems. (G) Conformational cluster and RMSD heatmap of two systems.

To further assess system stability and dynamics, we computed the RMSD. The protein RMSD was lower in the five-TD6 system than in the single-TD6 system (Fig. 3B) and converged rapidly to a stable plateau in the later simulation phase, indicating that multivalent binding restricts global conformational drift and accelerates the system's transition into a confined metastable state. Interestingly, the ligand RMSD showed the opposite trend that the average RMSD of five TD6 molecules was significantly higher than that of a single TD6 (Fig. 3C). This reflects that under multivalent binding conditions, despite the protein scaffold being “locked”, each TD6 molecule retains a certain degree of local freedom within its binding pocket. This may derive from spatial repulsion between adjacent ligands or dynamic cooperative rearrangement effects. Analysis of the Rg showed that the five-TD6 system (7.0 nm) had a lower Rg than the one-TD6 system (7.4 nm), confirming global contraction and compaction of the complex (Fig. 3D). Such structural compaction may alter the surface topology of PHB complexes, thereby affecting their ability to recognize surrounding proteins. Furthermore, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis indicated that multivalent binding reduces fluctuations in the core domains, increases overall rigidity, and restricts conformational flexibility (Fig. 3E). In summary, TD6 induces the PHB complex into a unique “rigid yet locally adjustable” conformation through multivalent binding, providing the structural basis for its inhibition of protein-protein interactions.

Multivalent TD6 remodels the free energy landscape of PHB and locks into a stable conformation

Projecting the Gibbs free-energy landscape onto the RMSD and Rg dimensions reveals distinct thermodynamic behaviors between the two systems. The landscape for the 1-TD6 system (free-energy range 0–10 kcal/mol) is relatively flat, displaying multiple shallow energy valleys with similar potentials (Fig. 3F). This indicates high conformational flexibility, allowing the complex to freely sample multiple metastable states without a single dominant stable conformation. In stark contrast, the free-energy landscape of the 5-TD6 system (free-energy range 0–12.5 kcal/mol) is markedly remodeled, featuring a deeper and more pronounced global minimum surrounded by substantial energy barriers (Fig. 3F). This indicates that under the influence of multivalent ligands, the PHB complex is “locked” within a narrow and low-energy conformational basin, losing its ability to explore the functional conformational space.

To characterize this stable state structurally, we extracted the representative conformation corresponding to the global free-energy minimum from the 5-TD6 landscape. In this conformation, all five TD6 molecules are deeply embedded in the predicted binding pockets of PHB1. Their binding induces pronounced global rearrangement that chain B bends inward, reducing its Cα–Cα distance to chain C from 35.0 Å to 30.5 Å, while chain A rotates rigidly inward, increasing its interfacial distance to chain C from 63.4 Å to about 68.5 Å (Fig. S3). This asymmetric “contraction–expansion” motion leads to a clear asymmetric rearrangement of the ring-shaped complex, likely representing the functional regulatory state induced by TD6.

Cluster analysis further supports these conclusions from a dynamic perspective. Approximately 22 conformational clusters were identified throughout the simulation of the 1-TD6 system, with relatively dispersed conformational distributions consistent with the multi-stability seen in its free-energy landscape (Fig. 3G). In contrast, the 5-TD6 system underwent intense conformational exploration early in the simulation (<50 ns), followed by rapid convergence of the trajectory, as shown by the RMSD heatmap stabilizing into a few dominant clusters. This dynamic convergence directly mirrors the system's shift toward the dominant deep valley in the free-energy landscape. In summary, cooperative binding of multiple TD6 molecules reshapes the free-energy landscape of PHB, driving the complex from a flexible, polymorphic, and nonspecific state to a new equilibrium characterized by compact architecture, restricted conformations, and enhanced thermodynamic stability.

TD6 exerts potent anti-tumor activities against CRC

Subsequently, we evaluated the in vitro antitumor activity of TD6 in multiple CRC cell lines. As shown in Fig. 4A, TD6 exhibited significant inhibitory effects on the proliferation of four CRC cell lines, with median inhibition concentration (IC50) values consistently falling below 10 μM. To further investigate its mechanism of action, we detected the endogenous PHB1 protein expression levels in each cell line and performed grayscale quantitative analysis. Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative correlation between TD6 IC50 values and PHB1 protein levels (Fig. 4B and C). This suggests that PHB1 expression levels may influence sensitivity to TD6, indicating that PHB1 likely plays a key role in TD6-mediated biological effects. To further evaluate the anti-CRC activity of TD6, we performed functional assays in HCT116 and T84 cell lines. Colony formation assays revealed that TD6 treatment significantly inhibited the proliferative capacity of both cell lines (Fig. 4D), indicating its potential to suppress tumor growth in a sustained manner. In addition, the effect of TD6 on CRC cell migratory capacity was assessed using the Transwell migration assay. To avoid the potential interference caused by high-dose TD6, the Transwell migration assay was performed at concentrations below the IC50 value of TD6. The cells were pretreated with Mitomycin C (10 μg/mL for 1 h) before seeding to minimize the influence of cell proliferation. Under these proliferation-controlled conditions, the cell migration was markedly reduced by TD6 at either 5 μM or 10 μM, indicating that CRC cell migratory capacity is potently suppressed by TD6 (Fig. 4E).

To validate the antitumor efficacy of TD6 in vivo, CT26-drived CRPM mice were established to evaluate its therapeutic effect on mCRC. To validate the reliability of bioluminescent quantification in the CRPM model, we first assessed the linearity of CT26-luc signal output. Luminescence intensity increased proportionally with cell number, yielding a robust linear correlation (slope ≥200). This confirmed that the imaging system provides accurate and sensitive detection for subsequent in vivo tumor-burden measurements (Fig. S4). Consequently, tumor-bearing mice were intraperitoneally administered TD6 every two days, with 5-FU serving as a positive control. Tumor growth was monitored using bioluminescence imaging. Compared with the solvent control and 5-FU-treated groups, all TD6-treated groups exhibited varying degrees of tumor suppression. Notably, the tumor volume in the high-dose group (40 mg/kg) was significantly smaller than those in the other three groups. In addition, TD6 treatment also markedly improved mouse survival with no significant differences in body weight among groups (Fig. 4F–I).

TD6 impairs MCI stability via disruption of the PHB-NDUFS1 interaction

To investigate the antitumor mechanism of TD6 in CRPM, we first performed MS-based proteomic profiling of TD6-treated T84 cells. TD6 altered the abundance of 66 proteins (34 upregulated and 32 downregulated; Fig. 5A). A notable fraction of downregulated proteins belonged to OXPHOS pathways, particularly subunits of MCI and complex IV, including NDUFS1, NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase core subunit v1 (NDUFV1), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 7A2 (COX7A2), and cytochrome c oxidase subunit 6C (COX6C). Gene Ontology (GO) molecular function enrichment indicated that TD6-affected proteins were significantly associated with structural and enzymatic roles, such as chromatin-related activities, nucleosomal DNA binding, ribonucleoprotein interactions, and diverse enzyme-binding categories (Fig. S5A). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further highlighted enrichment in mitochondrial redox complexes, metabolic assemblies, and cellular stress responses (Fig. 5B). Consistent with these findings, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed coordinated modulation of mitochondrial protein complexes and electron-transport components (Fig. S5B), suggesting that TD6 broadly perturbs networks essential for respiratory-chain integrity.

The levels of p-AMPKα Thr172, AMPKα, p-ACC Ser80, ACC, p-mTOR Ser2448, mTOR, p-S6K Thr389/Thr412, and S6K were detected by WB.

Previous studies have linked PHB family proteins to NDUFS1, implicating PHB in the structural organization of MCI. Given that PHB1 typically forms a membrane-associated complex, we hypothesized that the PHB complex helps stabilize MCI via direct interaction with NDUFS1. To test this, we first examined whether PHB1 and NDUFS1 protein levels correlate in CRC cell lines. Indeed, a strong positive correlation was observed (Fig. 5C and D). Co-IP confirmed that PHB1-Flag directly binds endogenous NDUFS1 (Fig. 5E), and MS further validated this interaction (Fig. 5F). Structural modeling with AlphaFold3 predicted potential interaction interfaces between PHB and NDUFS1, providing a structural basis for complex formation (Fig. 5G).

We then evaluated how TD6 affects this interaction landscape. TD6 treatment resulted in a dose-dependent decrease in NDUFS1 protein levels while leaving PHB1 expression unchanged (Fig. 5H), suggesting that TD6 may interfere with PHB1-mediated maintenance of NDUFS1 rather than regulating PHB1 abundance directly. Quantification of WB analysis showed that TD6 treatment markedly reduced the protein level of NDUFS1, further supporting that TD6 destabilizes NDUFS1 through disruption of the PHB1-NDUFS1 regulatory axis. Given that PHB1 and PHB2 can form complexes in mitochondria, we further speculated the binding of TD6 to PHB1 may induce a conformational change in the PHB complex, disrupting its interaction interface with NDUFS1. This disruption likely destabilizes NDUFS1 and compromises MCI function (Fig. S5C). To determine whether these regulatory patterns persist under stronger compound exposure, we performed proteomic profiling at a higher TD6 concentration (10 μM). Similar to the low-dose results, TD6 again broadly altered protein homeostasis pathways (183 downregulated and 91 upregulated; Fig. S5D). Notably, lysosomal and endocytic vesicle-associated proteins (e.g., lysosomal-associated membrane protein 1, insulin-like growth factor 2 receptor and cathepsin D) were consistently enriched, while multiple proteasome subunits were downregulated, mirroring the pathway shifts observed at lower doses. GO, KEGG, and GSEA analyses all reinforced this pattern (Fig. S5E and S5F), indicating that TD6 promotes a lysosome-biased remodeling of protein turnover networks, which becomes more pronounced at higher concentrations. Given the recurrent enrichment of lysosomal signatures across doses, we asked whether lysosome-dependent processes contribute to MCI destabilization. Lysosomal inhibitors (CHQ, AC) effectively rescued the TD6-induced reduction in NDUFS1 levels, whereas proteasome inhibitors (MG-132, PS-341) showed no restorative effect (Fig. S5G). These functional assays corroborate the proteomic data and suggest that TD6-driven loss of NDUFS1 occurs preferentially via lysosomal degradation rather than proteasomal pathways.

To determine whether TD6 also affects PHB-associated metabolic kinase signaling, we examined the AMPK/mTORC1 pathway in T84 and HCT116 cells. TD6 increased phosphorylation of AMPKα at Thr172 and ACC at Ser80, suggesting activation of AMPK signaling. In contrast, phosphorylation of mTOR at Ser2448 and S6K at Thr389/Thr412 was reduced after TD6 treatment, indicating suppression of mTORC1 downstream signaling (Fig. 5I). These findings suggest that TD6 treatment is associated not only with disruption of the PHB1-NDUFS1 axis but also with AMPK activation and mTORC1 inhibition. Because AMPK activation and mTORC1 suppression are closely linked to autophagy-lysosomal regulation, these signaling changes may contribute to the lysosome-dependent degradation of NDUFS1 observed after TD6 treatment. However, the current data do not establish AMPK/mTORC1 signaling as the direct mediator of TD6 induced NDUFS1 degradation. Further studies using AMPK inhibition, mTORC1 reactivation, or genetic modulation of AMPK/mTORC1 components will be needed to determine whether these kinase pathways are required for TD6-induced lysosomal turnover of NDUFS1.

TD6 inhibits mitochondrial OXPHOS in CRC cells

To determine whether PHB1 contributes to mitochondrial energy metabolism, we first measured the cellular NAD+/NADH ratio, a key readout of MCI–dependent redox activity. Knockdown of PHB1 markedly reduced the NAD+/NADH ratio in both T84 and HCT116 cells (Fig. 6A). Correspondingly, TD6 treatment also produced a dose-dependent decrease in NAD+/NADH levels, supporting the notion that TD6 perturbs MCI-associated redox balance by inhibiting PHB1 function. Because total cellular NAD+/NADH ratio and ATP levels can be influenced by glycolytic metabolism, these measurements were interpreted as downstream bioenergetic readouts rather than direct indicators of MCI activity. To further evaluate the effect of TD6 on mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis, we measured the oxidation of the redox-sensitive probe DCFH-DA (as an indicator of general intracellular oxidant production) and mitochondrial membrane potential using the JC-1 dye. Interestingly, neither PHB1 knockdown nor TD6 treatment significantly altered DCFH-DA oxidation (Fig. S6A and B) or the JC-1 red/green fluorescence ratio (Fig. S6C–E), indicating no broad change in overall cellular oxidant levels or global mitochondrial membrane potential. However, when specifically assessing mitochondrial-localized oxidant production using MitoSOX Red, fluorescence microscopy revealed a significant, dose-dependent increase in MitoSOX Red oxidation in HCT116 cells after TD6 treatment (Fig. 6E).

These results suggest that while TD6 does not cause broad intracellular oxidative stress or collapse the global membrane potential, it specifically induces localized mitochondrial redox perturbation. Given the role of MCI in mitochondrial oxidant generation, this increase may represent a functional consequence of TD6-induced PHB1/NDUFS1-associated mitochondrial perturbation. This mitochondria-localized redox stress may contribute to the anti-cancer activity of TD6; however, further rescue experiments using mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants will be required to determine whether mitochondrial oxidant production directly mediates TD6-induced tumor suppression. Proteomic analysis revealed that TD6 downregulated multiple components involved in electron transport and OXPHOS. To further assess the functional consequences of these changes, we examined intracellular ATP levels, the downstream output of mitochondrial OXPHOS. TD6 significantly reduced ATP production in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 6B), indicating impaired mitochondrial bioenergetic output.

We next explored whether inhibition of ATP production is linked to compound cytotoxicity. Ten structurally related compounds with varying inhibitory activities were selected from our screening, and their effects on cell viability and mitochondrial ATP levels were evaluated. These compounds exhibited differential impacts on both ATP production (Fig. 6B) and cell viability (Fig. 6C). Regression analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the IC50 values for ATP inhibition and those for cytotoxicity (Fig. 6D), indicating that the antitumor potency of this compound series is closely associated with their ability to suppress mitochondrial energy metabolism. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that PHB1 is required to maintain MCI-dependent redox homeostasis and ATP production, and that TD6 exerts cytotoxicity in CRC cells by disrupting mitochondrial energy metabolism.

Discussion

CRPM represents a formidable clinical challenge due to its aggressive course and limited therapeutic options. While early-stage CRC can often be managed with multimodal therapy, metastatic disease remains largely incurable, underscoring the urgent need for novel targets and mechanism-based treatments. In this study, we identify PHB1 as a key driver of CRC progression and metastasis, and we develop TD6, a first-in-class synthetically tractable small-molecule inhibitor of PHB1, that effectively suppresses tumor growth and dissemination by disrupting mitochondrial energy metabolism.

Our clinical bioinformatics analyses revealed that PHB1 is consistently overexpressed in colorectal adenomas, primary tumors, and notably in metastatic lesions, and its high expression correlates with poor recurrence-free survival. Functional studies confirmed that PHB1 depletion impairs CRC cell proliferation, clonogenicity, and migration, establishing PHB1 as a bona fide oncoprotein and a promising therapeutic target in mCRC [28,39]. Unlike currently reported PHB1 inhibitors, which are predominantly natural products (such as Rocaglamide A, Xanthohumol, and Rabdosiaone I) with complex scaffolds and uncertain pharmacologic profiles [31], TD6 was rationally designed through an integrated structure-based screening and optimization pipeline. This strategy allowed us to overcome typical drawbacks associated with natural compounds and yielded a molecule with well-defined binding characteristics, excellent selectivity, and potent anti-tumor activity.

The direct interaction between PHB1 and NDUFS1 identified in this study represents a critical mechanistic link between the mitochondrial scaffold protein and the stability of MCI. While prohibitin family members have long been implicated in respiratory chain assembly, our data provide concrete evidence that PHB1 physically engages NDUFS1, a core subunit essential for the catalytic activity and structural integrity of MCI. This interaction is not merely passive; rather, PHB1 appears to act as a molecular chaperone or stabilizer that prevents NDUFS1 from entering lysosomal degradation pathways. The strong positive correlation between PHB1 and NDUFS1 protein levels across CRC cell lines, together with the co-IP and MS validations, supports a functional axis in which PHB1 safeguards NDUFS1 expression. Importantly, this regulation occurs at the post-translational level, as PHB1 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition by TD6 does not alter NDUFS1 mRNA but instead promotes its lysosome-dependent turnover. This finding diverges from the classical view of prohibitin as a membrane-bound organizer of mitochondrial morphology and instead positions PHB1 as a direct guardian of MCI stability. Recent findings on the PHB2-NDUFS1 interaction in CRC proliferation provide additional support for the direct interaction between NDUFS1 and the PHB1-PHB2 complex [40]. Moreover, our study highlights the non-redundant and metastasis-relevant role of PHB1 in this regulatory axis. The selective disruption of PHB1-NDUFS1 binding by TD6, without affecting PHB1 protein levels, underscores the therapeutic potential of targeting protein-protein interfaces within the prohibitin complex. From a broader perspective, this interaction may represent a conserved mechanism by which cancer cells addicted to OXPHOS sustain MCI integrity under metabolic stress, and its pharmacological disruption offers a rational strategy to cripple mitochondrial bioenergetics in mCRC.

The multivalent binding behavior of TD6 emerged as a critical factor governing its biological activity. Molecular dynamics simulations revealed that occupancy of multiple PHB1 subunits by TD6 molecules reshapes the free-energy landscape of the PHB complex, driving it from a flexible, polymorphic state into a compact, rigidified conformation. This induced asymmetric conformational rearrangement distorts the internal topology of the PHB complex, thereby disrupting the functional recognition interface between PHB1 and its downstream partner proteins through steric hindrance and conformational mismatch. Such a mechanism highlights the potential of multivalent targeting to achieve enhanced efficacy and selectivity, particularly for oligomeric protein complexes.

From a translational perspective, TD6 demonstrated compelling anti-tumor activity both in vitro and in vivo. It inhibited proliferation and migration of CRC cell lines in a PHB1-expression-dependent manner and significantly suppressed tumor growth and extended survival in a murine CRPM model, without observable toxicity. Moreover, the strong correlation between the ability of TD6 analogs to inhibit mitochondrial ATP production and their cytotoxicity underscores the central role of energy metabolism disruption in the compound's mechanism of action.

In addition to the direct disruption of the PHB1-NDUFS1 interaction, our study indicates that TD6 also modulates broader cellular metabolic networks. Specifically, TD6 treatment activates AMPK and suppresses mTORC1 signaling. Because AMPK activation and mTORC1 inhibition are well-established drivers of autophagy-lysosomal pathways, these kinase alterations likely provide a favorable intracellular environment for the lysosome-dependent degradation of NDUFS1 observed in our study. Furthermore, the functional consequence of this structural and signaling perturbation is characterized by a loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and a localized accumulation of mitochondrial MitoSOX Red oxidation. Rather than causing broad intracellular oxidative stress, this specific mitochondrial redox imbalance, combined with reduced downstream bioenergetic readouts, ultimately leads to metabolic collapse. These findings suggest that TD6 exerts its potent anti-tumor efficacy through a coordinated mechanism, involving both direct structural destabilization of MCI and indirect kinase-mediated metabolic reprogramming.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study not only presents TD6 as a novel and potent PHB1-targeted inhibitor but also delineates a previously unappreciated regulatory axis in which PHB1 maintains MCI stability via direct interaction with NDUFS1. By selectively disrupting this axis, TD6 cripples OXPHOS and impairs tumor growth and metastasis. These findings provide a strong rationale for targeting the PHB1-mitochondrial metabolism axis in mCRC and offer a promising therapeutic strategy worthy of further clinical development. Future work should focus on optimizing the pharmacokinetic properties of TD6, evaluating its efficacy in patient-derived models, and exploring potential synergies with existing standard-of-care regimens.
