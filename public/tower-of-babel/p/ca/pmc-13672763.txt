Oxidative protein folding and Ca2+ homeostasis under redox control
Authors: Jiro Ogura, Junichi Fujii, Hiroaki Yamaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSecretory and membrane proteins undergo oxidative folding, the process of forming disulfide bonds between cysteine side chains to construct a stable higher-order structure, primarily in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) followed by further post-translational modification in the Golgi apparatus, after which proteins proceed to pathways for secretion and membrane localization. Proteins involved in transferring oxidizing power to target proteins possess a pair of highly reactive forms of cysteine residue that mediate reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions. Oxidizing power, which is generally provided in the form of reactive oxygen/reactive nitrogen oxide species. Endoplasmic reticulum oxidoreductin 1 (ERO1) is the ancient ER oxidase that utilizes molecular oxygen to produce hydrogen peroxide, which is then utilized for thiol oxidation to form a disulfide bridge in target proteins. Excessive elevation of these species could promote aberrant oxidation of susceptible molecules, which could lead to an accumulation of misfolded proteins and consequent ER stress. The ER is also involved in cellular Ca2+ signaling in which Ca2+-pump ATPase and Ca2+ release channels coordinate to play essential roles. Ca2+ regulates the activity of some redox-reactive proteins, which are largely in the family of protein disulfide isomerase. Thus, the Ca2+ status indirectly associates with the oxidative folding of nascent proteins. Upon extensive oxidation, inactivation of the Ca2+-pump ATPase and inappropriate Ca2+ leak via the Ca2+ channels causes a depletion of Ca2+ in the ER lumen. When intracellular calcium is depleted, stromal interaction molecules (STIM) in the ER membrane sense the Ca2+ status within the ER lumen. STIM differentially regulates two types of Ca2+ channels in the plasma membrane: Ca2+-release-activated Ca2+ channel (ORAI), and voltage-operated Ca2+ channel (CaV1.2). This regulation coordinately maintains Ca2+ homeostasis within cells. Moreover, Zn2+ and H+ indirectly affect the potential of redox responses through controlling ER chaperone molecules. The redox capacity of the ER is maintained by resident proteins and small compounds, which include cations Ca2+, Zn2+ and H+, and hence the comprehensive care of them is necessary in order to maintain normal ER function.Keywords: Electron transfer, Unfolded protein response (UPR), Calcium transport, Peroxiredoxin 4, Glycosylation

Introduction

Reduction-oxidation (redox) reactions play important roles in organisms. The intracellular redox environment is generally in a significantly more reducing state than the exterior of the cell [1], and such status is maintained with the help of a variety of energy-consuming reactions. Proper maintenance of redox balance is essential for sustaining biological systems, and even transient imbalance could cause dysfunction in signaling proteins. Excessive production of oxidizing compounds, notably reactive oxygen species (ROS), could cause irreversible oxidation in redox-sensitive molecules in cells and lead to pathogenic conditions and eventual organ death [2–4]. The cysteine sulfhydryl group (Cys-SH) is the portion of proteins that is the most sensitive to oxidative modification by ROS and reactive nitrogen oxide species (RNOS). Disulfide is the dominant oxidized form that is produced nearby Cys, while Cys has three other oxidation states [5]. Cys-SH is a dominant intracellular form, but once secreted, an extracellular oxidative environment promotes disulfide bond formation, which enhances protein thermodynamic stability and resistance to proteolysis. In cases of an inappropriate oxidation reaction, the structure of the protein is destroyed, resulting in loss of function. To avoid unfavorable events, proteins undergo oxidative folding in advance through the formation of proper disulfide bridges between Cys residues within the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and partially in the Golgi apparatus.

Oxidative protein folding of secretory/membrane proteins in the ER involves multiple redox-reactive proteins that include ER oxidoreductin 1 (ERO1), peroxiredoxin 4 (PRDX4), and a family of protein disulfide isomerases (PDI) [6, 7]. These enzymes work in concert to maintain the normal structure and function of secretory/membrane proteins in cells (Fig. 1). The numbers of such enzymes increase during evolution, which enables precise control of multiple intercellular message molecules in higher animals. Besides the production and post-translational modification of secretory/membrane proteins, the ER also plays a primary role in maintaining cellular Ca2+ homeostasis that influences a variety of mechanochemical responses [8, 9]. Although Ca2+ does not directly play a role in redox reactions, it controls molecules associated with redox reactions through modulation of the protein structure, and it functions notably in the mitochondria-associated ER membrane (MAM) [10, 11]. Additionally, enzymes, transporters, and channels involved in Ca2+ homeostasis in the ER membrane are also under redox regulation, and exert much influence over ER function [12–14]. Accumulation of misfolded proteins due to either excessive production of proteins, dysregulation of responsible redox proteins, aberrant protein glycosylation, Ca2+ deficiency, or oxidative stress leads to ER stress that is associated with a variety of diseases such as diabetes, cancer, and neurodegenerative diseases [15–20].

Here, we provide a brief overview on these issues and discuss how the two major functions of the ER, production and post-translational modification of secretory/membrane proteins and regulation of cellular Ca2+, collaboratively maintain the dynamics of the ER redox state and functions.

Redox relay in the oxidative folding of proteins for secretion and membrane localization

During maturation, secretory proteins and the extracellular domain(s) of nascent membrane proteins undergo oxidative folding in the ER lumen to counteract the dramatic changes that occur upon exposure to the oxidative environment outside the cell (Fig. 2). This is accomplished by co-translational oxidation of Cys-SH to disulfides by means of a series of redox-reactive proteins [16, 21]. ERO1 is the primary enzyme that employs molecular oxygen to form disulfide bonds in PDI family proteins, which results in the oxidative folding of nascent proteins [6, 7]. The ER in higher animals additionally contains other types of thiol-oxidizing enzymes that include PRDX4, glutathione peroxidase 7 (GPX7) and 8 (GPX8), and vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) [22–25].

Electrons that originate from nascent proteins are oxidized by several mechanisms in the ER. ERO1 is the only thiol oxidase that utilizes molecular oxygen to oxidize electrons that originate from nascent proteins through PDI and produce H2O2 in the ER. While PRDX4 appears to dominantly reduce H2O2 by employing the electrons from donor proteins such as PDIs, enzymes such as thioredoxin, GPX7/GPX8, and VKOR also catalyze the reaction. Ascorbate (vitamin C in primates) is abundantly present in the ER, so that the oxidized form of DHA may also be involved in the non-enzymatic oxidation of electrons

Mature proteins that have undergone oxidative folding could also experience redox remodeling, which leads to functional alteration through rearrangement of disulfides in the extracellular milieu [26]. Proteins involved in platelet aggregation and processes of virus infection are examples of such proteins. While there remains ambiguity in the significance of the redox remodeling of disulfides from the functional aspects of most membrane proteins, the relationship between the redox state and the function of Ca2+-transport ATPase and Ca2+ releasing channels is finally becoming understood in terms of ER Ca2+ homeostasis [8, 9].

Redox machineries responsible for oxidative protein folding

Major proteins and factors that reportedly play roles in oxidative protein folding in the ER are given below. ERO1 is highly evolutionarily conserved and is therefore thought to be the ancestral form of ER-resident sulfoxidase [7, 27]. Mammals have two genes encoding ERO1 isoforms, ERO1α and ERO1β, which are differentially expressed in organs. It contains FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide) as a redox cofactor and utilizes molecular oxygen. ERO1 primarily utilizes molecular oxygen to oxidize Cys-SH in target proteins, notably PDI [28, 29]. Thus, ERO1 and PDI constitute a conventional electron-transferring partnership for oxidative protein folding in the ER in a rapid and effective manner (Fig. 2) [30]. Structural studies indicate that ERO1 directly produces hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) instead of superoxide, the latter of which is sometimes produced in reactions involving oxygen consumption, such as respiratory chain in mitochondria, and becomes a trigger of oxidative stress [31]. The redox state of cells is determined by the quantitative balance between antioxidants, such as glutathione, and oxidants. Glutathione is abundant among anti-oxidants in most cells, but the ratio of reduced glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione dimer (GSSG) is low in the ER compared to the cytoplasm and other organelles, thus making the ER more oxidized state [32]. H₂O₂ is stable and abundant compared to other oxidants, thus its production can serve as an indicator that the redox balance is shifting toward oxidation. It has been reported that the ER is the organelle with the highest H₂O₂ content, and manipulating ERO1, either overexpression or knockdown of the encoding mRNA, affects H₂O₂ levels in the ER [33]. H2O2 could also be derived from other source(s) [34, 35], which include ER- NADPH-oxidase 4 (NOX4) [36, 37] and the mitochondrial respiratory chain through MAM [38]. Many proteins in both the ER and mitochondria at the contact site undergo redox-reactive modifications and synergistically exert primary roles in cellular homeostasis. PERK and IRE1 that act as signaling in the unfolded protein response (UPR) are abundantly present at MAM. From aspect of crosstalk between calcium and redox regulation, aberrant UPR signaling initiated by dysfunctional PERK causes excessive calcium import into mitochondria and elevation in ROS, which results in activation of apoptotic pathways [39].

Peroxiredoxin (PRDX) is a family of redox proteins that utilize electrons from thioredoxin (TRX) and other donor compounds to reduce H2O2 to water via peroxidase activity [40]. PRDX4 is the fourth member of this family and possesses a hydrophobic signal peptide at the amino terminus that is required for secretion [41]. However, PRDX4 primarily resides within the ER lumen and uniquely utilizes the oxidative power of H2O2 to introduce disulfide bonds into proteins via PDI family proteins [42, 43]. PRDX4 first oxidizes PDI family proteins, preferably P5 and ERp46, which then introduces disulfide bonds into nascent proteins that are the ultimate targets [44]. From another perspective, PRDX4 may also simultaneously alleviate oxidative damage to the ER caused by H2O2. While individual deficiencies in ERO1 [45] and PRDX4 [41] exhibit subtle phenotypes in mice, a double deficiency of ERO1 and PRDX4 could result in an atypical type of scurvy in mice [46]. Details of roles in oxidative protein folding in the ER and other aspects of PDRX4, such as an alternatively spliced form and presumptive functions in spermatogenesis, have been reported in review articles [47, 48].

Glutathione is present in quantities of as much as 15 mM in the ER, and reportedly fuel electrons to reduce PDI family members [49, 50]. However, the elimination of GSH by forced expression of ChaC1, which is the GSH-specific enzyme that exhibits γ-glutamylcyclotransferase activity to cleave GSH to 5-oxo-L-proline and L-cysteinyl-glycine, does not affect the redox state of proteins in the ER. The results suggest that direct involvement of GSH in oxidative protein folding in the ER lumen is unlikely [51]. The antioxidant action of GSH is mediated mainly by glutathione peroxidases (GPX). Among the eight members of this family found in mammals, GPX1through GPX4 contain selenocysteine at the catalytic center, which endows them with a high level of catalytic activity [50]. Two ER-resident forms, GPX7 and GPX8, contain Cys instead of selenocysteine and exhibit lower GSH-dependent peroxidase activity in vitro but reportedly exhibit thiol-oxidizing activity in the ER [52, 53]. GPX7, also called NPGPx, reportedly enhances GRP78 chaperone activity, and thereby suppresses ER stress [54]. GPX8 forms a complex with ERO1α and exerts peroxidase activity that effectively prevents the toxic effects of H2O2 produced by the ERO1α reaction [55]. The expression levels of these GPX genes differ depending on the cell type [56], and their cellular contexts appear to determine which redox couple will be used for oxidative protein folding.

An established function of VKOR is reduction of vitamin K 2,3-epoxide and vitamin K to vitamin K hydroquinone, which thereby supports the γ-glutamyl carboxylation of coagulation factors [57]. VKOR also acts as a thiol-oxidizing enzyme [25]. VKOR is an ER membrane protein and couples an electron-transfer pathway with membrane-bound thioredoxin-like redox partners [58]. RNA interference experiments in human liver cancer cells have shown that VKOR is involved in oxidative protein folding [25]. Further examination reveals that VKOR is a bona fide thiol-oxidizing system in the ER [22, 59]. Cys43 and Cys51 in the human VKOR embedded in the ER lumen are responsible for electron-transfer reactions. VKOR can oxidize electrons from a reduced form of PDI as well as in association with γ-glutamyl carboxylase that produces γ-carboxyglutamate in a vitamin K-dependent manner [60, 61]. While cells with VKOR deficiency are still capable of supporting viability and protein secretion, the triple depletion of ERO1, PRDX4, and VKOR causes cell death [25]. Thus, the function of VKOR in oxidative protein folding may be particularly important under conditions in which other thiol-oxidizing enzymes are deficient.

Ascorbate (vitamin C in primates) is abundantly present in the ER, and it is considered that the oxidized form dihydroascorbic acid (DHA) may also be involved in the non-enzymatic oxidation of proteins [62]. Ascorbate is a strong electron-donating compound and acts as a cofactor for a variety of enzymatic reactions and antioxidation [62, 63]. Absence of both ERO1 and PRDX4 causes scurvy due to ascorbate deficiency [46], which supports the significant role of ascorbate in collagen synthesis through donations of electrons to prolyl hydroxylase. DHA acts as an oxidant for several sulfhydryl-containing compounds in proteins [64] and could support the oxidative folding of proteins, although the data on this point are based largely on in vitro studies [65, 66]. Several reductase systems in fact reduce DHA back to ascorbate in an NAD(P)H-dependent manner in vivo [67]. Although the levels of these reductases, as well as that of NAD(P)H, are low in the ER lumen, an abundant presence of ascorbate could enable non-enzymatic redox cycling. A major concern with this issue could be that the second-order reaction rate constant for the oxidation of reduced PDI by DHA is small at 12.5 M−1s− 1, indicating a slow reaction compared with a maximum of 186 M−1s− 1 for other systems such as those involving dithiols in the unfolding or partial folding of proteins [68]. Thus, the extent to which ascorbate/DHA contributes to oxidative protein folding in the ER lumen in the abundant presence of other thiol-oxidizing enzymes remains questionable [69].

PDIs use a redox-dependent mechanism to strictly regulate the structure and function of proteins

PDI exerts dual enzymatic roles, introduction of disulfide bond (Fig. 3A) and reduction/isomerization of disulfides in targeted proteins (Fig. 3B) [70, 71]. PDI family proteins play essential roles in oxidative folding of nascent proteins through formation of disulfide in nascent proteins within the ER, where oxidizing power is recruited from molecular oxygen via ERO1 or H2O2 via PRDX4 and GPX7/8 [6, 30, 68], as mentioned above. Additionally, PDI in its reduced form catalyzes reduction of disulfides in oxidized proteins and isomerization of incorrect disulfide bridge in misfolded proteins without additional cofactors, although an electron donor is required for consecutive PDI-catalyzed disulfide reductions [70, 71].

There are more than 20 members in the PDI family that regulate the structure and function of target proteins via redox reactions. Although ERO1 and PRDX4 can quickly oxidize the final target proteins, proper PDIs are required to correctly fold them [46, 72]. Almost all PDI family proteins have an evolutionarily conserved thioredoxin-like domain (CysXaaXaaCys). PDIA1 (also known as PDI or P4HB) is the first PDI family protein to be discovered and remains the most studied. PDIA1 has a U-shape structure composed of four structural domains, a, b, b’, and a’, an x linker region between the b’ and a’ domains, and a C-terminal acidic extension [73]. The a and a’ domains are located at either end of the U-shape and include CGHC (CysGlyHisCys) sequences in the thioredoxin-like domain. N-terminal cysteine residue that resides in the thioredoxin-like domain of PDIA1 has a low pKa value, and the C-terminal cysteine residue in the thioredoxin-like domain has a high pKa value under physiological pH conditions [74, 75]. The pKa values of the active-site cysteine residue play an important role in the function of thioredoxin-like domains. The b and b’ domains are located at the base of the U-shape. The b’ domain is known as the principal protein binding domain and contains a hydrophobic pocket [76, 77]. Although the function of the b domain remains unclear, it is believed to act as a structural spacer and is responsible for substrate recruitment. PDI exists as oxidized (Fig. 3C) and reduced (Fig. 3D) forms depending on the redox state of the active motif. PDI exerts the isomerase activity in its reduced form and promotes disulfide bond rearrangement in oxidized substrate proteins that are often dysfunctional. On the other hand, oxidized PDI introduces disulfide bonds in nascent proteins within the ER, resulting in reduction of PDI itself. Oxidizing power, which is mainly from molecular oxygen via ERO1 (as mentioned in the ERO1 section), converts reduced PDI back to its oxidized form. These continuous redox reactions ensure the ongoing oxidative folding of proteins within the ER lumen. The open side of U-shape protein in oxidized human PDI is significantly broader than that in reduced human PDI [73]. This broader open side of U-shape favors binding to large substrate proteins [73].

Since association of ERO1 and GPX7 increases oxygen consumption, the ERO1-GPX7-PDI redox relay may also contribute to effective oxidative protein folding [23]. This consecutive reaction can reduce H2O2 to water, as the case of PRDX4, which would also help suppress oxidative stress. In vitro, oxidized PDI can be rapidly reduced by reduced form glutathione, suggesting a potential role of glutathione in oxidative protein folding [78]. However, in insulin-secreting INS-1E cells, overexpression of GPX7/GPX8 neither increases insulin content nor facilitates disulfide bond formation [56]. Moreover, a study using cells depleted of glutathione in the ER does not support the hypothesized role of glutathione in oxidative protein folding [51], as mentioned above.

Superoxide, nitric oxide (NO), and secondary products ROS/RNOS are produced in large amounts, particularly during inflammation, and may cause oxidative/nitrosative stress. Reaction of RNOS with sulfhydryls in sensitive proteins produces S-nitrosylation, which may affect redox-reactive responses [2–4]. Reduced PDI can react quickly with S-nitrosocompounds, leading to S-nitrosylation of specific two cysteines in PDI [79]. While S-nitrosylation in the active site cysteine is reversible by GSH, another cysteine remains S-nitrosylated. It is therefore proposed that S-nitrosylation in PDI may associate with some inflammatory diseases by impairing PDI function. In addition, reduced PDI functions as a chaperone that facilitates the refolding of misfolded proteins [80, 81], which is performed by a redox-regulated manner [70, 82]. The other PDI family proteins also have varied domain compositions that define their substrate specificity and cellular function [83, 84]. Domain-a and domain-a’ are highly conserved among the PDI family proteins and have catalytic functions similar to those of thioredoxin. On the other hand, the sequence identities of the b and b’ domain are low among the PDI family proteins, which results in diverse substrate recognition and differences in intracellular functions.

Factors that cause ER stress

Approximately 30% of nascent proteins produced by ribosomes reportedly undergo misfolding [85], and hence sustained or excessive production of secretory proteins can lead to accumulation of misfolded proteins, leading to a potential cause for ER stress if not dealt with appropriately. In addition to the aberrant production of secretory proteins, inhibition of the N-glycosylation of proteins and Ca2+ depletion also causes ER stress [17, 21]. Under activated metabolism and oxidative stress, excessive oxidation or inappropriate disulfide linkage could occur and also result in an accumulation of unfolded proteins. The regulation of Ca2+ homeostasis in the ER has been associated with its signaling function in a variety of cellular processes, but Ca2+ deficiency could have an indirect association with ER stress. Therefore, this issue will be discussed separately in a later section.

Stimulated production of secretory/membrane proteins as a potential cause of ER stress

Insulin signaling is involved in important metabolic reactions that include the utilization of blood glucose and lipogenesis. Since pancreatic β cells are the only cells that produce insulin during hyperglycemia, ER stress has attracted much attention as a potential cause for diabetes. Insulin production is stimulated at the initial phase of insulin-resistant conditions, and prolonging the overproduction of insulin inevitably increases the incidences of unfolded proinsulin, which likely causes ER stress responses in pancreatic β-cells [86, 87]. Almost all types of cells are involved in protein secretion, and, hence, conditions requiring sustained secretion may result in similar outcomes to them. To overcome these adverse situations, cells use several defense mechanisms to resolve stress conditions, which is referred to as UPR. The details surrounding UPR are beyond the scope of this article, so readers are referred to pertinent articles in the literature for more information [17, 18, 21, 40].

Glycosylation as a major post-translational modification of secretory/membrane proteins

For proteins that are normally glycosylated, the lack of glycosylation disrupts their proper folding, which results in an accumulation of misfolded proteins and eventual ER stress. Since the conjugation of N-glycans increases the hydrophilicity of unstructured nascent polypeptides, aggregation appears to be promoted via hydrophobic interactions under defected N-glycosylation. This results in suppression of the translocation of nascent glycoproteins to the Golgi apparatus [88]. Some antibiotics, like tunicamycin, inhibit the formation of N-linked glycosylation by creating a complex with N-acetylglucosamine-1-phosphate transferase that catalyzes N-glycosylation in its nascent process [89, 90]. While UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc) is the substrate for this enzyme, tunicamycin is a substrate mimetic that binds the enzyme and inhibits its activity in a competitive manner.

Members of the quiescin-sulfhydryl oxidase (QSOX) family that consists of QSOX1 and its paralog QSOX2 are Golgi apparatus-resident flavoenzymes that are reported to be responsible for oxidative protein folding. In overexpressed cells, QSOX initially showed properties similar to those of ERO1 [91, 92], but subsequent studies partly failed to support QSOX function in the ER [22, 59]. Instead, QSOX was recently identified as a Golgi apparatus-resident enzyme that catalyzes the disulfide bond formation of proteins — preferentially that in sialyltransferase (Fig. 4) [93, 94]. Terminal sialylation and O-linked glycans are characteristically inhibited in a hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-independent manner, which accompanies a lowering of the oxidative potential in the Golgi apparatus and a loss of two disulfide bonds in the catalytic domain of the α−2,6-sialyltransferase (ST6Gal-I) [95]. Sialylations appear to affect the function of glycoproteins other than their secretory process. It remains elusive if other enzymes responsible for glycan formation are also regulated by QSOX.

Zn2+ supports redox reactions in the ER and Golgi apparatus from the ground up

Zn2+ is the second most abundant trace metal after iron. Iron and copper are redox-reactive and involved in multiple metabolic processes, whereas the direct action of Zn2+ in redox reactions is unknown in the physiological state. Zn2+ is taken up via specific Zn2+ transporters on plasma membrane and regulates the functions of as much as 10% of human genome-coded proteins [96–98]. However, the function of bound Zn2+ is not as well understood as those of other metal ions. Recently, employment of targetable fluorescent sensors specific to Zn2+ has revealed the unique distribution of Zn2+in cellular compartments [99, 100]. Based on these findings, Zn2+ is considered to be abundant in some organelles and appears to indirectly regulate redox reactions of responsible proteins, which means it regulates functions of the ER-Golgi system [101]. Several Zn2+ transporters share roles in carrying out the important functions of Zn2+ in the Golgi apparatus [102, 103].

The ER-resident chaperone ERp44 plays a primary role in binding client proteins such as ERO1 and PRDX4 in the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC)−53 in downstream compartment Golgi apparatus and returning them to upstream compartment the ER [104, 105]. In this process, Zn2+ and pH gradients exert a direct influence, which means that Zn2+ transporters are responsible for fundamental roles of the gradients in the ER-Golgi. Upon Zn2+ binding in an acidic environment, ERp44 undergoes a conformational change, which recruits cargo proteins to KDEL (LysAspGluLeu) receptors [106]. Zn2+ is involved in the normalization of cellular functions in a number of pathological conditions, such as neurodegenerative diseases [107] and the immune system [108]. Therefore, further elucidation of the relationship between the distribution and function of intracellular Zn2+ could provide clues to better explain the mechanisms involved.

Oxidative stress associated with the metabolic process could aggravate ER stress

The association of oxidative stress with the induction of ER stress has been precisely overviewed [109], so this topic is only briefly covered here. It is true that the ER requires oxidative power for the oxidative folding of proteins and is dependent on molecular oxygen and external oxidants. Superoxide is a primary ROS that is produced in a variety of oxygen-metabolizing reactions such as respiration, detoxification, and non-enzymatic glycooxidation [31]. Because radical electrons coupled with free iron could promote lipid peroxidation and induce non-apoptotic cell death ferroptosis, in order to prevent cell damage, it is necessary to avoid the presence of oxygen radicals as much as possible. ERO1 produces H2O2 directly through a two-electron reduction of molecular oxygen [28, 29], and the resultant H2O2 can then be utilized for oxidative folding by other thiol-oxidizing enzymes as described above. Since many oxygen-utilizing reactions produce superoxide as a byproduct, ERO1-catalyzed H2O2 production appears to avoid oxidative damage in the ER by avoiding superoxide production as a by-product. However, radical species could still arise from electron transport reactions in nearby mitochondria, from detoxification reactions by P450 coupled with a reductase (POR), and from eicosanoid production initiated by cyclooxygenase on the cytosolic surface of the ER membrane [31, 110]. It is generally assumed that superoxide is less toxic than other ROS/RNOS partly because superoxide dismutase (SOD)1-deficient mice represent a subtle phenotypic abnormality. However, a depletion of ascorbate due to the knockout of the responsible enzyme for its production under SOD1 deficiency is fatal in mice due to the induction of acute pneumonia coupled with metabolic remodeling, which implies ascorbate scavenges superoxide or other radical species in a compensatory manner [111, 112]. Although superoxide is spontaneously dismutated to H2O2, catalysis by SOD accelerates the reaction approximately 3,000-fold, which thereby attenuates radical chain reactions through elimination of the primary radical electrons [31, 110].

Association of Ca2+ homeostasis with oxidative protein folding in the ER

Concentrations of Ca2+ are approximately 3- to 4-orders less in the cytoplasm compared with that in extracellular spaces and in the ER lumen, which enables an increase in trace levels of Ca2+ to act as intracellular signaling. The essential role of the ER in Ca2+ signaling has initially been demonstrated in the process of excitation/contraction coupling in muscle, which was subsequently extended to other non-muscle cells. The sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) is a unique type of ER that is highly organized in muscular cells and is rich in differentiated proteins for Ca2+ storage and transport. Stored Ca2+ is released from the SR in response to electrical stimuli from motor neurons, and this initiates muscle contraction [113]. When Ca2+ is not properly recruited, muscle tissue cannot exert sufficient contractile force. Furthermore, Ca2+ also modulates ER function by binding to redox-reactive proteins, which is why the depletion of Ca2+ leads to ER stress [12–14].

In order to form a large concentration gradient of Ca2+ between extracellular/ER lumen and cytosol, lowering the level of cytosolic Ca2+ requires active transport supported by ATP hydrolysis. The active Ca2+ transport is supported by two classes of ATP-hydrolyzing enzymes. The Ca2+-transport ATPase present in plasma membrane is designated as plasma membrane Ca2+-ATPase (PMCA), and that present in the SR/ER membrane is designated as the SR/ER Ca2+-ATPase (SERCA) [113, 114]. Thapsigargin, a plant-derived sesquiterpenoid, specifically inhibits members of SERCA family but not PMCA [115], which decreases ER luminal Ca2+ concentrations. While excessive cytosolic Ca2+ is cytotoxic [116, 117], induction of ER stress caused by treatment with thapsigargin appears to be due mainly to decreased ER Ca2+ concentrations but not to an increase in cytosolic Ca2+ [118]. Accordingly, thapsigargin is commonly used to investigate ER stress caused by decreasing Ca2+ concentrations inside the ER [14].

Calcium ions, in trace amounts, mediate cell proliferation as well as muscle contraction mainly through the activation of protein kinases, such as protein kinase C (PKC) and calmodulin-dependent protein kinases (CaMPK) [119, 120]. On the other hand, excessive calcium ions may cause cellular dysfunction and, in severe cases, cell death through, for example, abnormal activation of calcium-dependent protease calpains in cytoplasm [121]. Thapsigargin specifically inhibits SERCA, causing ER stress by depleting luminal calcium ion [122]. Therefore, in this case, it is thought that the calcium requirement mechanism in the ER does not properly function, leading to ER stress. The association of ER Ca2+ deficiency with defected oxidative protein folding was first observed in the asialoglycoprotein receptor [123]. H1 subunits of the asialoglycoprotein receptor undergo misfolding via the depletion of Ca2+ in the ER by treatment with either a calcium ionophore (A23187) or thapsigargin. Thapsigargin decreases Ca2+ in the ER by inhibiting SERCA, which leads SR/ER more reduced state [124, 125]. Under Ca2+ depletion, calreticulin, an ER chaperone for glycosylation, interacts with PDI. This interaction leads to preventing formation of disulfide bonds and leaves target proteins in reduced state [125]. In addition to PDI family proteins, relatively high levels of Ca2+ are required to maintain functions of the glucose-regulated proteins GRP78/BiP and GRP94, which act as ER chaperones [12]. Since these Ca2+-sensitive factors play primary roles in forming proper disulfide bridges in many target proteins, their dysregulation by calcium depletion is likely involved in the shift to the reductive state of the ER lumen. To cope with such aberrant situation under lower levels of ER Ca2+, ERdj5 activates the SERCA2b through reduction of the key disulfide bonds [126], which results in replenishment of ER Ca2+ and then recovers PDI-mediated oxidative folding of proteins. In fact, many ER-resident proteins, including the PDI family proteins and proteins involved in glycosylation such as calnexin and calreticulin, require Ca2+ binding for proper function [127]. Hence, the lowering of ER Ca2+ leads to impaired ER proteostasis and eventually causes ER stress.

Players in controlling cellular Ca2+ dynamics

Excitation-contraction coupling is a unique function of muscular cells, and Ca2+ increases in the cytoplasm in response to membrane depolarization promotes rapid activation of the contractile machinery. For smooth muscle and non-muscle cells, the corresponding stimuli are rather divergent, with humoral factors dominating neural stimulation. Ca2+ is provided via Ca2+ channels in both the plasma and SR/ER membranes [12–14, 113]. Different types of Ca2+ channels are present in these membranes, and their opening causes a transient increase in cytoplasmic Ca2+ that promotes a variety of physiological processes. Ca2+ channels in the plasma membrane are further categorized into one of two groups; one is a voltage-dependent Ca2+ channel and the other is a receptor type of Ca2+ channel.

In the case of the SR/ER membrane, there are two types of Ca2+ channels that show a similar overall structure but differ in size and regulation between the SR and ER. One class is dominantly expressed in the SR membrane, which is referred to as a ryanodine receptor (RYR) because it is artificially activated by the plant alkaloid ryanodine [113]. Skeletal, cardiac, and smooth muscle dominantly express RYR1, RYR2, and RYR3 genes, respectively, but non-muscle cells also express them, albeit to a lesser extent. Conformational change in the voltage-dependent Ca2+ channel in plasma membranes is a direct cause for activation of skeletal muscle RYR1, but Ca2+ modulates both cardiac RYR2 and smooth-muscle RYR3. In contrast, the other class of channels opens in response to the binding of inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and is referred to as the IP3 receptor (IP3R) [12, 128]. Three types of genes, IP3R1, IP3R2, and IP3R3, are expressed in many types of cells. The expression of RyRs is much lower than that of IP3Rs in many non-muscle types of cells, but the opening of RyRs releases approximately 20-times more Ca2+ compared with that of IP3Rs, and their contribution to the ER Ca2+ pool is considered to vary depending on the type of cell and stimulus. Ca2+ homeostasis in the SR/ER is balanced via uptake by SERCA and release via RyR/IP3R (Fig. 5). Both systems are involved in Ca2+ homeostasis and regulated in response to redox conditions.

(B) Oxidative modification of essential Cys-SH in the individual proteins stimulates the Ca2+ release but suppresses Ca2+ uptake, which results in a decrease in ER Ca2+ concentrations. (C) Excessive ROS/RNOS could sustain Ca2+ deficiency and lead to ER stress

Redox regulation of proteins involved in active Ca2+ transport

In cardiac myocytes, H2O2 treatment decreases SERCA activity but activates the Na+- Ca2+ exchanger, which is localized in plasma membranes and exports Ca2+ in combination with Na+ uptake outside cells. These coordinated actions deplete ER Ca2+ [129]. NO physiologically stimulates SERCA to increase ER Ca2+ concentrations. NO reacts with superoxide producing peroxynitrite (ONOO–) and promotes the S-glutathionylation of Cys674 in SERCA, which enhances Ca2+ uptake activity [130, 131]. Since accelerated Ca2+ uptake is associated with stimulated relaxation of muscular cells, ONOO– is proposed to be an intracellular mediator for NO-dependent relaxation. To the contrary, irreversible oxidative modifications consisting of sulfonylation at cysteine 674 (Cys674) and nitration at tyrosine 294/295 in overexpressed Gαq protein, one of the α subunits making up G proteins, is known to decrease SERCA activity of mouse myocardium [132]. This results in a depletion of SR Ca2+, which leads to a progressive decrease in muscular cell contraction followed by diastolic arrest. Moreover, oxidation of Cys674 in SERCA in cardiac myocytes regulates SR Ca2+ content, which results in a modulation of mitochondrial Ca2+ content and apoptosis [133].

The heart-specific isoform of SERCA, SERCA2a, is suppressively by direct binding of phospholamban in unphosphorylated state, which is a small protein (51-amino acids in size) present in cardiac SR membrane [134]. Upon β-agonist stimuli, phospholamban is phosphorylated by protein kinase A, which enables it to release the suppression of SERCA2a. As a result, β-agonist contributes to these positive inotropic effects by enhancing SERCA2a activity. Phospholamban has three Cys residues at positions 36, 41 and 46 within a phospholipid bilayer of the SR membrane. Phospholamban binds and suppresses SERCA2a in pentameric form, and all three Cys residues are responsible for the formation of the functional pentameric structure [135]. Nitroxyl is a one-electron reduced form of NO and reportedly enhances the activity of SERCA2a via redox modulation of Cys41 and Cys46 in phospholamban, and increases the inotropic effects that affect the force of heart contractions [136, 137].

DJ-1 is one of the causative genes for familial Parkinson’s disease and exhibits antioxidant activity [138]. Aberrant function of DJ-1 causes familial Parkinson’s disease, and defects in Drosophila ortholog of DJ-1 are associated with carbonylation and inactivation of SERCA [126]. Disulfide reductase ERdj5 reduces disulfide bonds in the misfolded proteins inside the ER lumen to promote their passage through the retro-translocation channel, which leads to degradation by proteasomes in cytosol [139, 140]. While ERp57 (also known as PDIA3) promotes the formation of disulfide bonds in loop 4 of SERCA2b in a Ca2+-dependent manner and suppresses its activity [141], ERdj5 can activate the SERCA2b via reduction of the disulfide bonds under lower levels of ER Ca2+ [142]. SEPN1 is a selenoprotein that specifically localizes to the luminal side of the ER membrane and promotes redox regulation of its partners, including SERCA [143, 144]. The EF-hand domain in SEPN1 binds Ca2+ with high affinity, which allows for regulation by Ca2+ in the ER lumen [145]. Luminal Ca2+ depletion causes conformational changes in SEPN1 via the EF-hand domain, which increases reductase activity catalyzed by the thioredoxin-like domain of SEPN1. While SERCA with oxidized Cys represents low activity, the SEPN1-mediated reduction increases the capacity of SERCA to uptake cytosolic Ca2+ [145]. Thus, oxidizing conditions enhance ER stress by decreasing luminal Ca2+ and impairing the thiol oxidation responsible for oxidative protein folding.

Redox regulation of the RyR Ca2+ channel

RyR has many Cys residues with different redox sensitivities, which are intricately related to channel activity, and therefore results vary depending on the experimental conditions. Aberrant activation of variant RyR1 increases cytosolic Ca2+, which causes contraction of muscular tissues and could damage cells due to excessive heat generation, which is observed in malignant hyperthermia [146]. In many cases, an increase in intracellular Ca2+ is observed under oxidative stress, so it is reasonable to assume that a significant level of oxidation will promote Ca2+ release from RyR and could cause cytotoxicity in a manner similar to malignant hyperthermia.

Cardiac RyR2 activity is regulated by multiple mechanisms such as the redox reaction and phosphorylation [147]. ROS/RNOS induce redox-reactive modifications such as S-glutathionylation, S-nitrosylation, and disulfide bond formation on RyR2 [148]. The tetrameric form of RyR2 contains 89 Cys residues, and S-nitrosylation occurs in as many as 12 sites in all protein channels, which leads to its activation. While denitrosylation reverses this function, the oxidation of additional thiols irreversibly activates it [149]. Thus, RyR2 has multiple redox-sensitive mechanisms that respond to oxidative conditions to strictly regulate Ca2+ channel function — in excess, however, such regulation could associate with muscular dysfunction.

Redox regulation of IP3 receptor Ca2+ channels

When ER Ca2+ concentrations become low, ERp44 binds to IP3R1 and decreases its sensitivity to IP3 [150]. Under ER stress, ERO1 is upregulated in a C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP)-dependent manner and causes hypersensitization of IP3R1 through its oxidation and displacement of ERp44 [151]. Excessive cytosolic Ca2+ then triggers apoptosis in cells that are under ER stress. By contrast, ERp46 mediates the oxidizations of Cys2505 and Cys2513 in IP3R1 to form disulfide bonds, which activates the channels. At low luminal Ca2+ levels, the ER-resident reductase ERdj5 reduces the same disulfide bonding in IP3R1, which results in inactivation of the channel activity [152]. When all redox regulation of SERCA2b is considered, ERdj5 appears to play reciprocal regulatory roles for IP3Rs and SERCA2b in response to the ER luminal Ca2+ concentrations [152].

Stromal interaction molecule 1 (STIM1) is a sensor for ER Ca2+ and mobilizes extracellular Ca2+

Channels in the ER and plasma membranes are sensitive to redox reactions, and their activities change in response to ROS/RNOS [153]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that ROS increase cytosolic Ca2+, which could cause aberrant activation of signaling and damage cells and ultimately lead to death [116]. The ER is the main organelle that is actively involved in regulating cytosolic Ca2+, and hence its dysfunction could increase cytosolic Ca2+. On the contrary, a decline in intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, notably within the ER, induces Ca2+ influx into the cytoplasm across the plasma membrane, which is accomplished by store-operated Ca2+ channels. Two-types of specific Ca2+ channels present in the plasma membrane are reportedly responsible for fueling Ca2+ (Fig. 6).

Cellular Ca2+ concentrations are differentially regulated by STIM in association with plasma membrane channels. (A) When the ER contains a sufficient amount of Ca2+, the STIM that binds Ca2+ via the EF hand domain is not associated with other proteins. (B) Upon Ca2+ depletion in the ER lumen, Ca2+-free STIM binds Ca2+ channels for either ORAI or CaV1.2 and causes differential responses to them. ORAI assembled with Ca2+-free STIM becomes an active Ca2+ channel, which increases cytosolic Ca2+ and as a consequence fuels Ca2+ to the ER lumen via SERCA. On the contrary, CaV1.2 assembled with Ca2+-free STIM becomes an inactive form, which prohibits Ca2+ entry from extracellular sources

STIM is known to sense Ca2+ in the ER membrane and promote function of Ca2+ channels present in plasma membranes [154, 155]. When intracellular Ca2+ is depleted, it must be refueled from extracellular sources. The corresponding system primarily involves ORAI and STIM1, and more details of them can be found in the review article [156, 157]. STIM has a single transmembrane region and a Ca2+ binding EF hand structure at its C-terminal domain facing the ER lumen. On the other hand, ORAI1 is a plasma membrane protein with four transmembrane segments, with amino- and carboxy-termini facing the cytoplasm, and functions as a Ca2+-release activated Ca2+ (CRAC) channel. Two STIM isoforms (STIM1 and STIM2) and three ORAI isoforms (ORAI1, ORAI2, ORAI3) are present in humans. Redox-mediated regulation of ORAI and STIM1 has now been precisely described [158, 159]. STIM1 is associated with both ORAI1 and transient receptor potential canonical channel 1 (TRPC1), and is responsible for store-operated Ca2+ entry (SOCE) in mouse smooth muscle cells [160]. A new splice variant of STIM1, TIM1L, is expressed in various tissues of rodent and in human skeletal muscle and binds to ORAI1, TRPC3, and TRPC6 [161]. STIM has several redox-sensitive Cys-SH, and the two Cys in the STIM1 luminal domain (Cys49 and Cys56) are the most sensitive [158]. S-nitrosylation of Cys-SH increases Ca2+ affinity of the EF-hand structure, but S-nitrosylated STIM1 is unable to bind ORAI1 [162]. On the other hand, among several reactive forms of Cys, Cys195 in ORAI1 is the most redox sensitive [163].

STIM1 also binds to the C terminus of voltage-operated Ca2+ channel (CaV1.2) in the plasma membrane through the activation domain and triggers an influx of extracellular Ca2+ via the channels, which suppresses channel function [164, 165]. Thus, STIM exerts reciprocal regulation of these two types of Ca2+ channels in cells [166]. When assembled with Ca2+-free STIM, ORAI becomes an active Ca2+ channel, whereas CaV1.2 becomes an inactive form. The T-type and high-voltage-activated subtypes of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels are also redox regulated, although many unknowns remain regarding their structure-function relationship [167]. Some TRP channels are redox-sensitive, and upon oxidation by ROS/RNOS, they are reported to mediate biological functions [168]. In response to the IP3R channel, TRP channels could also be involved in this process [169, 170], although there is not full consensus on this issue.

Concluding remark

The ER-Golgi-secretory vesicle network plays a central role in intercellular communications through releasing the messenger compounds in both unicellular and multicellular organisms. Nascent proteins co-translationally inserted within the ER experience oxidative folding by thiol-oxidizing enzymes, which is an essential process for conformational assembly of functional proteins. Since these processes are accomplished by proteins that are highly sensitive to a redox microenvironment, ROS/RNOS exert a marked influence over the corresponding proteins and their cellular functions. Fine tuning of the redox process is performed not only by redox reactive compounds, but also by other molecules indirectly, as represented by Ca2+ and Zn2+ [13, 14, 105].

Research from the aspect of redox homeostasis has focused mainly on intracellular machineries, as overviewed in this communication. However, it will be necessary to broaden our perspective in order to understand the redox states of extracellular/neighboring cells as well. Interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) are representative proteins that are secreted without amino-terminal signal peptide and collectively termed leaderless secretory proteins. Several pathways that include membrane-associated machineries such as exosome and vesiculation and cell death such as pyroptosis are responsible for the leaderless secretion and are controlled in redox-dependent fashion [171]. Some extracellular proteins appear to be regulated in either disulfide exchange or redox-dependent mechanism [172–174]. Proteins involved in redox reactions in the ER and Golgi apparatus can also be secreted outside the cell particularly under conditions of inflammation [175]. In case of ER-resident proteins ERO1 and PRDX4 that lack the KDEL ER-retention signal, ERp44 mediates the retrograde transport of them from the Golgi apparatus to the ER via the KDEL receptor [24, 176]. Dysfunction of ERp44 tends to occur under stress conditions of the ER, which may cause secretion of such KDEL-less proteins. Secretion of redox proteins is not merely accidental but rather appears to be related to extracellular functions of them. PDI is involved in the regulation of blood coagulation and cell functions induced by external signals through integrin remodeling [177, 178]. Another example is the redox remodeling of human immune deficiency virus (HIV) envelop glycoprotein gp120 by PDI, which is required for HIV binding to the cell surface receptor CD4 in T cells [179, 180]. Pre-existing cargoes, which are vesicle-encapsulated forms, are responsible for the secretion of large components in response to the redox environments surrounding cells [171, 181]. Since animal health is associated with both intracellular and extracellular status, it is necessary to understand the relationship between molecular structure and function, keeping in mind that it changes depending on surrounding environmental factors.
