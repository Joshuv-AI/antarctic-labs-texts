Effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions for lower limb motor impairment in post-stroke hemiplegia: a systematic review and Bayesian network meta-analysis
Authors: Di Zhang, Yating Yang, Guixing Xu, Lu Wang, Guilin Zhang, Wanyi Song, Miaoyu Ye, Jian Luo, Dehua Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke-related hemiplegia often results in significant lower limb dysfunction, severely affecting walking ability, balance, and daily activities. Although various non-pharmacological interventions have shown potential benefits, the optimal rehabilitation strategy remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy and comparative ranking of non-pharmacological interventions in improving lower limb motor function, balance, walking ability, and activities of daily living in individuals with post-stroke hemiplegia.MethodsWe conducted a search of PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published from January 2010 to August 2025. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool and Review Manager 5.4 were used to assess study quality, and evidence was graded with GRADEPro. Using R Studio software, a NMA was carried out to evaluate the clinical efficacy of various treatments in improving lower limb motor function in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia, ranked by the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). The study was officially registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD420251169037.ResultsA total of 82 RCTs involving 3514 participants and 16 non-pharmacological interventions were included. The results indicated that repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) showed favorable effects on lower limb motor function measured by FMA-LE (MD = 3.7, 95% CI 2.5 to 4.9; SUCRA = 88.13%). rTMS also demonstrated positive effects on balance (MD = 8.5, 95% CrI: 5.1 to 11; SUCRA = 98.41%) and activities of daily living (MD = 14, 95% CrI: 11 to 16; SUCRA = 94.68%). For walking independence assessed by FAC, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) showed considerable effects (MD = 1.5, 95% CrI: 0.41 to 2.5; SUCRA = 87.60%). Furthermore, virtual reality combined with robotic rehabilitation showed a relatively marked effect in reducing TUG time (MD = − 6.6, 95% CrI: − 8.9 to − 4.3; SUCRA = 95.27%).ConclusionDifferent non-pharmacological interventions may provide distinct benefits for lower limb rehabilitation after stroke. rTMS appears favorable for improving motor function, balance, and daily living ability; tDCS may help enhance walking independence; and virtual reality combined with robotic rehabilitation may be beneficial for functional mobility. Further large-scale, multicenter, standardized RCTs with longer follow-up are needed to confirm these findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12984-026-02081-9.Keywords: Network meta-analysis, Stroke, Hemiplegia, Non-pharmacological interventions, Randomized controlled trials

Introduction

Stroke is a sudden neurological disorder caused by disrupted cerebral circulation, leading to localized or widespread neurological impairment [1]. According to the latest Global Burden of Disease report, stroke is the second leading cause of death worldwide and the third leading cause of disability [2]. The period from 1990 to 2019 witnessed a significant surge in the annual rate of stroke cases and deaths, with stroke incidence climbing by 70% and stroke-related mortality rising by 43% [3]. Projections indicate that stroke-related deaths are expected to increase by 47% by 2050 [4]. Hemiplegia is a common complication after stroke and is mainly caused by damage to the corticospinal tract, resulting in unilateral motor impairment [5]. Among these impairments, lower limb motor dysfunction is particularly common, often presenting as increased muscle tone, abnormal gait control, and reduced coordination and balance. These deficits not only impair patients’ functional recovery and quality of life, but also place substantial emotional, economic, and caregiving burdens on families and society [6]. Research shows that 50–65% of stroke patients struggle with walking independently shortly after onset, and despite rehabilitation, 20–30% still cannot walk independently by the end of treatment [7]. Therefore, improving lower limb motor recovery and walking independence in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia remains a major goal of stroke rehabilitation.

Stroke management mainly consists of two stages: acute-phase pharmacological treatment and subsequent rehabilitation intervention [8]. Although acute treatment can stabilize the condition, many patients still present with motor dysfunction; therefore, rehabilitation remains essential for functional recovery [9]. Among these, lower limb motor dysfunction is common and can affect standing, walking, balance, and activities of daily living. While conventional rehabilitation shows some benefits, recovery is often limited and some patients may reach a rehabilitation plateau [10, 11]. Therefore, exploring more effective non-pharmacological interventions is important for improving post-stroke rehabilitation outcomes. Non-pharmacological interventions refer to rehabilitation approaches that do not rely on drugs or surgery and typically include physical therapy, mechanical-assisted therapy, and neuromodulation. These interventions may improve post-stroke functional recovery by promoting neuroplasticity, functional reorganization, and motor learning [12]. According to the American Stroke Association guidelines [13], non-pharmacological interventions currently used for lower limb motor recovery after stroke mainly involve neuromodulation techniques [14–16], rehabilitation engineering, device-assisted training [17, 18], motor relearning [19], task-oriented training [20], physical modalities [21] and some traditional Chinese rehabilitation therapies [22]. Although a variety of non-pharmacological interventions have shown potential benefits for improving lower limb motor function after stroke, the wide range of available approaches and their differing mechanisms of action mean that clear and sufficient evidence to guide clinical selection among these options remains relatively limited. Previous pairwise meta-analyses have mainly focused on comparisons between a single intervention and conventional rehabilitation or control conditions. While these studies have provided important evidence regarding the effectiveness of specific interventions, they still have certain limitations in informing optimal clinical decision-making. First, conventional pairwise meta-analyses are generally restricted to comparisons between two interventions and therefore have limited ability to simultaneously evaluate multiple active treatment options, particularly when direct head-to-head evidence between different non-pharmacological interventions is lacking. Second, this approach does not usually allow a unified quantitative assessment or ranking of the relative efficacy of multiple interventions, and therefore remains limited in addressing the clinically relevant question of which intervention may be more effective. In contrast, network meta-analysis (NMA) can integrate both direct and indirect evidence within a single analytical framework, thereby enabling comprehensive comparisons and probabilistic ranking of multiple interventions and providing a more systematic evidence base for identifying potentially more effective rehabilitation strategies [23].

Against this background, the present study further extends the existing literature. First, we constructed a relatively broader intervention comparison network by incorporating different categories of non-pharmacological interventions into a single evaluation framework, including neuromodulation techniques, rehabilitation engineering and device-assisted training, motor relearning and task-oriented training, physical modalities, and some traditional and complementary therapies. This approach allowed us to attempt cross-category comparisons of efficacy rather than limiting the analysis to comparisons within a single intervention category. Second, we updated the evidence base by including randomized controlled trials published up to 2025, with the aim of improving the completeness, timeliness, and robustness of the study conclusions. Accordingly, this study employed a Bayesian network meta-analysis to systematically evaluate the effectiveness and relative ranking of different non-pharmacological interventions for lower limb motor recovery in stroke survivors with hemiplegia, thereby providing more comprehensive evidence to inform the development and optimization of clinical rehabilitation strategies.

The study complies with the PRISMA extension guidelines for systematic reviews and meta-analyses and has been registered in PROSPERO with the registration number CRD420251169037 [24].

Search strategy

Two researchers (DZ and YTY) independently conducted literature searches across four English-language databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Embase. The search encompassed publications from January 2010 to August 2025, with no restrictions on language. Key search terms included stroke, hemiplegia, lower limbs, non-pharmacological interventions, randomized controlled trial, among others. For each database, detailed search strategies are available in the supplementary materials. To ensure comprehensive coverage, references from eligible studies and relevant prior research were manually reviewed. For studies with incomplete data, we reached out to the corresponding authors to obtain the required information and conducted a thorough online search for all relevant studies.

Data extraction

Two researchers (DZ, YTY) imported the retrieved studies into EndNote reference management software, and after removing duplicates, conducted an initial screening by reviewing the titles and abstracts. The studies were subsequently chosen independently according to pre-established eligibility criteria. Full-text articles from potentially eligible studies were obtained for additional evaluation. The two researchers (DZ, YTY) then independently extracted the relevant study details using a standardized data collection form and cross-verified the data. Conflicts were addressed through consultation with a third researcher (DHL). For every included study, the following information was extracted: lead author, year of publication, country of origin; participant characteristics (sample size, gender distribution, average age, type of stroke, affected side, disease stage); details of interventions, stimulation sites, and treatment duration for both the experimental and control groups; and outcome measures.

Risk-of-bias assessment and evidence quality assessment

Two researchers (DZ, YTY) evaluated the risk of bias in the selected studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool, and the analysis was conducted using Review Manager 5.4 software [27]. The assessment included seven areas: method of randomization, concealment of allocation, blinding of both participants and intervention providers, blinding of those assessing outcomes, completeness of data, selective reporting, and other possible biases. Each domain was classified as “low risk,” “unclear risk,” or “high risk.” Any discrepancies were addressed by a third researcher (DHL) who made the final inclusion decision. The evidence quality was evaluated with GRADEPro, and in the event of substantial differences or concerns regarding study inclusion, a third-party expert was consulted for resolution.

Statistical analysis

This study conducted a Bayesian random-effects NMA using the gemtc package in R version 4.0. Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulation was performed with four chains, each running for 50,000 iterations, with the first 20,000 iterations discarded as burn-in. For continuous outcomes, treatment effects were expressed as mean differences (MDs) with corresponding 95% credible intervals (CrI). For FMA-LE, BBS, MBI, and FAC, a positive MD favored the intervention, whereas for TUG, a negative MD favored the intervention. To support the validity of indirect comparisons, the transitivity assumption was assessed by comparing potential effect modifiers across studies. In addition, direct comparisons were analyzed using a random-effects model to account for potential clinical and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic. Local and global consistency were assessed using the node-splitting method and the design-by-treatment interaction model, respectively. Model convergence was examined using density plots, trace plots, and convergence diagnostic plots. Network plots were used to present the evidence structure. The relative ranking of interventions was estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). SUCRA values range from 0 to 100%, with higher values indicating a greater probability that an intervention ranks among the more favorable options. However, SUCRA provides only a relative ranking of treatments and should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in the presence of heterogeneity or inconsistency within the network. Therefore, SUCRA values were not interpreted in isolation but in conjunction with the magnitude and direction of treatment effects, the corresponding 95% CrI, and their clinical relevance. To further explore robustness and potential effect modifiers, network meta-regression was performed using mean age, proportion of ischemic stroke, proportion of male participants, and proportion of right-sided hemiplegia as study-level covariates. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to stroke stage, lower limb motor impairment severity, and treatment duration. Stroke stage was classified as subacute (8 days to 6 months) or chronic (> 6 months) [28]. Motor impairment severity was defined by baseline FMA-LE score as higher (≥ 21) or lower (< 21) [29]. and treatment duration was categorized as ≥ 4 weeks or < 4 weeks. Results were presented using forest plots and cumulative ranking probability plots. Sensitivity analyses excluded studies involving acute-phase patients (0 days to 7 days) and those with sample sizes < 20. Comparison-adjusted funnel plots and Egger’s test were performed using Stata 17.0 to assess small-study effects and potential publication bias.

Results

Literature screening

Four electronic databases provided a collection of 3,071 articles. After excluding 1,002 studies published before 2010 and 886 duplicate studies, 1,183 articles remained. The titles and abstracts of the studies were assessed, resulting in the exclusion of 970 articles. A total of 213 studies were read in full. After further examination, 132 additional studies were excluded. Ultimately, 82 studies [30–111] with 3514 participants were included in the NMA. Quality assessment of the included studies

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (Review Manager 5.4) was used to evaluate the risk of bias in 82 studies. Of these, 69 studies specified the methods used for random sequence generation and were categorized as ‘low risk,’ while the remaining 13 studies referenced randomization without providing specific details, and were labeled as ‘unclear risk of bias’. Allocation concealment was achieved using opaque sealed envelopes in 28 studies, which were classified as ‘low risk’; the other 54 studies did not specify their methods for allocation concealment and were categorized as having an ‘unclear risk of bias’. Owing to the inherent characteristics of certain non-pharmacological interventions, researchers encountered challenges in implementing blinding during the intervention process. Only 10 studies reported blinding of both participants and researchers, whereas 61 studies reported blinding of outcome assessments. 62 studies were rated as ‘low risk’ due to having complete data. All 82 studies reported pre-specified outcome measures, rated as ‘low risk’. None detailed other sources of bias, thus rated as ‘risk of bias unclear’. Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence for the outcomes was assessed using the GRADEpro tool. The findings showed that the certainty of evidence for changes in FMA-LE was rated as high, whereas the certainty of evidence for the four secondary outcomes was rated as moderate. The main reasons for downgrading were indirectness and imprecision in part of the evidence. For details, see Appendix 5.

Assessment of transitivity, consistency, and heterogeneity

The transitivity and consistency assumptions of the NMA were comprehensively assessed in this study. The results indicated that the included comparisons generally satisfied the transitivity assumption, thereby supporting the validity of the indirect comparisons (Appendix 6). Overall, good agreement was observed between the direct and indirect evidence, and no obvious inconsistency was detected. The results of the global inconsistency test showed that no statistically significant global inconsistency was identified for most outcome measures (Appendix 7). In the local inconsistency assessment, the node-splitting analysis further confirmed the consistency of the network evidence (Appendix 7). In addition, the density plots, trace plots, and convergence diagnostic plots also supported the robustness of the data (Appendix 8 and Appendix 9). The heterogeneity assessment showed that most comparisons had low to moderate levels of heterogeneity (Appendix 7).

Primary outcome: FMA-LE

Figure 3a shows a network diagram of 47 RCTs [30, 31, 33, 43, 44, 46–50, 53, 54, 56–58, 61, 63–65, 69, 72, 74–76, 78–81, 83–95, 98, 101] evaluating the efficacy of FMA-LE under different individual interventions, involving 2,134 participants and 13 non-pharmacological treatments. The network primarily focuses on conventional treatment. Interventions are represented by points, with lines connecting them to show direct comparisons. As shown in Fig. 4, compared with conventional therapy, 7 interventions showed statistically significant advantages in improving FMA-LE scores. Among them, rTMS demonstrated the most favorable efficacy profile (MD = 3.7, 95% CrI: 2.5 to 4.9; SUCRA = 88.13%), followed by tDCS (MD = 3.4, 95% CrI: 2.3 to 4.5; SUCRA = 82.91%), MT (MD = 3.4, 95% CrI: 0.9 to 6.0; SUCRA = 78.68%), RAR combined with FES (MD = 3.0, 95% CrI: 1.5 to 4.5; SUCRA = 72.33%), RAR combined with VR (MD = 3.0, 95% CrI: 2.0 to 4.0; SUCRA = 71.83%), acupuncture (MD = 2.6, 95% CrI: 1.3 to 3.8; SUCRA = 61.58%), and RAR (MD = 2.5, 95% CrI: 1.4 to 3.6; SUCRA = 59.97%). Overall, SUCRA results suggested that rTMS and tDCS may rank among the better-performing interventions. However, these rankings should be interpreted together with the magnitude and precision of the treatment effects, rather than being viewed as direct evidence of clinical superiority. NMA

The NMA showed that, compared with sham stimulation, rTMS (MD = 4.38, 95% CI 2.97 to 5.79), tDCS (MD = 4.12, 95% CI 3.28 to 4.95), MT (MD = 4.11, 95% CI 1.35 to 6.87), RAR combined with FES (MD = 3.69, 95% CI 1.82 to 5.56), RAR combined with VR (MD = 3.66, 95% CI 2.16 to 5.14), RAR (MD = 3.18, 95% CI 1.63 to 4.73), acupuncture (MD = 3.23, 95% CI 1.57 to 4.92), CIMT (MD = 1.84, 95% CI 0.13 to 3.58), and FES (MD = 1.09, 95% CI 0.17 to 2.02) were all associated with improvements in FMA-LE scores, suggesting that these interventions may offer some benefit in promoting lower limb motor recovery in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia. Further comparisons indicated that, relative to CIMT, rTMS (MD = 2.52, 95% CI 0.76 to 4.30), tDCS (MD = 2.27, 95% CI 0.56 to 3.97), and RAR combined with VR (MD = 1.81, 95% CI 0.16 to 3.46) may be associated with greater improvements in FMA-LE scores. Compared with FES, rTMS (MD = 2.28, 95% CI 1.98 to 4.57), tDCS (MD = 3.02, 95% CI 2.17 to 3.88), RAR combined with FES (MD = 2.59, 95% CI 0.94 to 4.24), RAR combined with VR (MD = 2.56, 95% CI 1.32 to 3.80), MT (MD = 3.02, 95% CI 0.38 to 5.64), acupuncture (MD = 2.14, 95% CI 0.71 to 3.58), and RAR (MD = 2.08, 95% CI 0.78 to 3.38) may also show a relatively more favorable trend. In addition, compared with VR, rTMS (MD = 3.66, 95% CI 1.12 to 6.17), MT (MD = 3.40, 95% CI 0.04 to 6.73), tDCS (MD = 3.39, 95% CI 0.93 to 5.85), RAR combined with FES (MD = 2.98, 95% CI 0.30 to 5.63), and RAR combined with VR (MD = 2.93, 95% CI 0.51 to 5.35) may also demonstrate a tendency toward greater improvement in FMA-LE scores. NMA

A total of 210 pairwise comparisons were included in the NMA . Compared with sham stimulation, rTMS (MD = 7.51, 95% CI 4.86 to 10.17), MT (MD = 5.69, 95% CI 1.89 to 9.49), VR combined with RAR (MD = 3.48, 95% CI 0.92 to 6.08), tDCS (MD = 3.36, 95% CI 0.51 to 6.20), RAR combined with FES (MD = 3.14, 95% CI 0.46 to 5.83), and iTBS (MD = 3.12, 95% CI 0.57 to 5.63) were all associated with improvements in balance function in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia.Further comparisons suggested that rTMS and MT tended to show relatively more favorable effects in several comparisons. Relative to CIMT, rTMS (MD = 5.59, 95% CI 2.25 to 8.91) and MT (MD = 3.76, 95% CI 0.32 to 7.20) were associated with greater improvements in balance outcomes. Compared with FES and iTBS, rTMS also showed a relatively larger improvement in balance function. In addition, compared with MT combined with FES, RAR, VR, WT, and VT, rTMS and, in some comparisons, MT or VR combined with RAR, also appeared to show more favorable trends in balance recovery. The findings suggest that rTMS and MT may be associated with relatively more favorable improvements in balance function, although the comparative effects across interventions should be interpreted with caution. Meta-regression, subgroups analysis, and sensitivity analysis

Meta-regression analysis was further performed for the primary outcome, FMA-LE, to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The covariates included mean age, proportion of patients with ischemic stroke, proportion of male participants, and proportion of patients with right-sided hemiplegia. The results showed that the regression coefficients for all these covariates were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), suggesting that these study-level baseline characteristics were unlikely to be the main sources of heterogeneity in the present study (Appendix 12). Taken together, the observed heterogeneity is more likely attributable to differences in the intervention characteristics themselves.

Given that the capacity for neurological recovery may vary substantially according to stroke stage and the severity of lower limb motor impairment, subgroup analyses were further performed based on stroke phase, severity of lower limb dysfunction, and intervention duration. Stratified by stroke phase, the results showed that, in patients with subacute stroke, rTMS, tDCS, acupuncture, VR combined with RAR, and RAR were all superior to conventional therapy in improving FMA-LE scores. Among these, rTMS ranked first in efficacy, suggesting that it may be the most favorable intervention for improving lower limb motor function during the subacute stage. In patients with chronic stroke, VR combined with RAR, RAR combined with FES, acupuncture, and RAR all demonstrated favorable therapeutic effects, among which RAR combined with FES appeared to be the optimal intervention. When stratified by the severity of lower limb motor impairment, patients with FMA-LE scores < 21 were classified as the low-function group. In this subgroup, rTMS, tDCS, acupuncture, VR combined with RAR, and RAR all showed beneficial effects, with rTMS ranking highest, indicating that it may be the most effective treatment option for patients with lower functional status. Patients with FMA-LE scores ≥ 21 were classified as the high-function group. In this subgroup, VR combined with RAR, RAR combined with FES, and RAR all demonstrated favorable effects, with RAR combined with FES ranking highest, suggesting that it may be the optimal treatment choice for patients with better baseline lower limb function. Subgroup analysis based on treatment duration showed that, in the subgroup with treatment duration < 4 weeks, rTMS, MT, RAR, and acupuncture were all more effective than the control group, with rTMS ranking first. In the subgroup with treatment duration ≥ 4 weeks, rTMS, tDCS, acupuncture, VR combined with RAR, RAR combined with FES, and RAR also demonstrated superior efficacy compared with the control group, and rTMS remained the top-ranked intervention.

To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding 2 studies [33, 87] involving patients in the acute stage of stroke and studies with a sample size of < 20. The sensitivity analysis yielded results that were generally consistent with those of the primary analysis, indicating that the findings of this study were relatively robust. Details are provided in the appendix 14.

Assessment of publication bias

Utilizing a random-effects model, a corrected contrast funnel plot was developed. Overall, the distribution of study points appears relatively symmetric, with no significant signs of small-study effects or publication bias observed. It should be noted that for certain interventions within the network, the number of available studies is limited, which may affect the interpretability of the funnel plot. Hence, the associated results should be viewed with caution. Please refer to the appendix 15.

Discussion

Key findings

This study is a systematic NMA aimed at evaluating the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions in the rehabilitation of lower limb motor function in patients with hemiplegia after stroke. A total of 82 studies involving 3,514 patients were included, comprehensively assessing the effects of various non-pharmacological interventions on lower limb motor function, balance, and activities of daily living. Subgroup analyses further explored whether stroke stage, severity of lower limb motor impairment and treatment duration may influence the relative effects of different non-pharmacological interventions on lower limb motor function outcomes in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia. The results of this study suggest that several non-pharmacological interventions may be associated with favorable outcomes in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia. In the NMA of lower limb motor function, rTMS showed a relatively high ranking probability (SUCRA = 88.13%), followed by tDCS (SUCRA = 82.91%). For balance function assessed by the Berg Balance Scale, rTMS also ranked highest among the included interventions (SUCRA = 98.41%). In terms of activities of daily living, rTMS showed a relatively high SUCRA value on the Modified Barthel Index (SUCRA = 94.68%). For functional gait assessed by the Functional Ambulation Category, tDCS ranked relatively high (SUCRA = 87.60%). In addition, virtual reality combined with robot-assisted therapy showed a favorable ranking for reducing Timed Up and Go time (SUCRA = 95.27%). However, these findings should be interpreted in the context of the underlying risk of bias and certainty of evidence. According to the GRADE assessment, the overall quality of evidence for the main comparisons was moderate, mainly because of study indirectness and imprecision. For many non-pharmacological interventions, blinding of participants and treatment providers was difficult to implement, and several comparisons were based on relatively small sample sizes with wide credible intervals. Therefore, confidence in the estimated treatment effects remains limited, and the apparent relative advantages of some interventions should be interpreted cautiously.

Compared with previous studies

Compared with previous systematic reviews and NMA, this study has notable differences. Earlier NMAs mostly focused on a specific category of non-pharmacological interventions, including comparative studies of different repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation protocols, overall assessments of non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, and efficacy analyses of acupuncture for post-stroke lower limb motor dysfunction [112–114]. These studies have provided important evidence for the effectiveness of specific interventions, but no NMA has yet comprehensively compared different categories of non-pharmacological therapies within the same framework. This study is the first to incorporate multiple non-pharmacological interventions into a single network, systematically comparing their efficacy in improving lower limb motor function in post-stroke hemiplegic patients, thus filling the gap in cross-category efficacy comparisons. The conclusion of this study is consistent with the findings of several previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses. Fan's research findings indicate that rTMS can improve lower limb motor function and balance function at different stages of stroke, which is consistent with the conclusions of this study [14]. Ni's study confirmed that rTMS is superior to tDCS in improving FMA-LE, which is consistent with our findings [115]. Xie's study also confirmed that rTMS can improve motor function, balance, and activities of daily living in stroke patients [116]. Liu's study suggests that in the rehabilitation of lower limb motor function after stroke, different electrical stimulation therapies have varying effects on FAC, with tDCS ranking first [117]. This finding aligns with the conclusions of our study. Ke's systematic review and meta-analysis similarly revealed that tDCS may be an effective option for restoring walking independence and functional walking in stroke patients [118]. Compared with previous studies, this study has several advantages. First, the literature search covered a broad time range (from January 2010 to August 2025), encompassing nearly fifteen years of publications, which facilitated the inclusion of recently emerging interventions and long-term evidence. Second, this study included a wider range of non-pharmacological interventions, including neuromodulation techniques, rehabilitation engineering, traditional therapies, motor relearning, among others, for a total of 16 intervention types, allowing for comparative efficacy assessments across different intervention categories. Third, the Bayesian network meta-analysis method was adopted to indirectly compare the efficacy of different interventions and to provide probabilistic ranking results based on SUCRA values, thereby helping to identify relatively more optimal treatment options. Fourth, subgroup analyses were conducted based on stroke stage, severity of lower limb motor impairment, and treatment duration, to further explore the influence of different clinical characteristics on treatment outcomes.

Mechanistic explanations

This study suggests that rTMS demonstrates relatively favorable effects in improving lower limb motor function, balance function, and activities of daily living in post-stroke hemiplegic patients compared to other interventions. rTMS is a non-invasive technique for brain stimulation that modulates neuronal membrane potential through pulsed magnetic fields, enabling targeted regulation of neural excitability in particular brain regions [119]. rTMS has been widely used in managing neurological and psychiatric conditions, such as cognitive deficits, depression, psychosis, and Alzheimer's disease [120–122]. After a stroke, there is often an imbalance in interhemispheric inhibition, where the inhibitory influence of the healthy hemisphere on the affected hemisphere is enhanced, while the inhibitory influence of the affected hemisphere on the healthy hemisphere is reduced. This imbalance may exacerbate neurological deficits and hinder motor function recovery through the corpus callosum pathway [123]. Systematic reviews show that low-frequency rTMS, by inhibiting the healthy hemisphere's M1, can reduce excessive excitability in the healthy hemisphere and indirectly increase excitability in the affected hemisphere's M1. On the other hand, high-frequency rTMS can directly enhance excitability in the affected hemisphere's M1, thereby promoting the rebalancing of bilateral cortical excitability [124, 125]. Bai 's study further suggest that low-frequency rTMS may reduce excitability in the stimulated hemisphere (usually the healthy side) and increase excitability in the non-stimulated hemisphere (typically the affected side), while high-frequency rTMS primarily enhances excitability in the affected hemisphere's M1. These findings provide supportive neurophysiological evidence for the potential of rTMS in improving post-stroke motor function. Neuroimaging studies indicate that after rTMS treatment, there is a significant increase in cerebral blood flow in regions such as the affected hemisphere's supplementary motor area, precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, pons, medulla, as well as the contralateral midbrain, superior cerebellar peduncle, and middle cerebellar peduncle. These fiber bundles form the cortico-ponto-cerebellar-cortical circuit, and changes in cerebral blood flow in the precentral gyrus, postcentral gyrus, and pons are positively correlated with improvements in Fugl-Meyer motor function scores [126]. In addition, rTMS can enhance the anisotropy score values in the affected hemisphere's postcentral gyrus, precentral gyrus, contralateral supplementary motor area, and bilateral cerebellum. These fiber bundles form the corticospinal tract, and improvements in their structural integrity are closely associated with motor function recovery [126]. At the spinal level, transcranial magnetic stimulation can directly affect the lower limb motor neurons through the corticospinal tract, inducing spike-timing-dependent plasticity. This results in an increased amplitude of tibialis anterior muscle motor evoked potentials and enhances ankle dorsiflexor electromyographic activity and strength [127]. rTMS can influence neuronal activity and synaptic plasticity by regulating the levels of neurotransmitters such as glutamate, GABA, and dopamine [128–130]. At the same time, it activates neurotrophic factor receptors and promotes the expression of neurotrophic substances including brain-derived neurotrophic factor, thereby providing a favorable microenvironment for synaptic remodeling and neural regeneration [131, 132]. In addition, rTMS plays an important role in neuroimmunomodulation: it promotes the transformation of astrocytes from the pro-inflammatory A1 subtype to the neuroprotective A2 subtype, induces microglial polarization from the M1 to the M2 phenotype, downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, and upregulates anti-inflammatory cytokines including IL-10 and TGF-β [133, 134]. These mechanisms help alleviate neuroinflammation, inhibit neuronal apoptosis, and maintain blood–brain barrier integrity, thereby providing sustained biological support for functional remodeling.

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a subthreshold neuromodulation technique that applies weak direct current to specific brain areas through electrodes placed on the scalp. This modulates the resting membrane potential of cortical neurons, thereby altering their spontaneous firing rate [135]. From a mechanistic perspective, tDCS and rTMS share similarities but also have important differences. At the system level, tDCS is also based on the theory of interhemispheric inhibition imbalance following stroke. It increases excitability in the affected hemisphere's M1 region through anodal stimulation, or it reduces excessive activity in the healthy hemisphere's M1 region through cathodal stimulation, thereby restoring the excitatory-inhibitory balance between the two hemispheres [136]. A notable feature of tDCS compared to rTMS is that it does not directly induce action potentials in neurons. Instead, it modulates the resting membrane potential of cortical neurons through weak, constant direct current, which can alter their excitability [137]. Specifically, anodal stimulation causes depolarization of the neuronal membrane potential, increasing cortical excitability, while cathodal stimulation induces hyperpolarization, reducing cortical excitability [138]. Animal experiments suggest that tDCS can also reduce the activation level of microglial cells in the ischemic penumbra, decrease the release of pro-inflammatory factors, and thus alleviate secondary neural damage [139]. Additionally, tDCS devices are relatively portable, cost-effective, and operate quietly during use. The above mechanisms may act synergistically to form the intrinsic basis of the therapeutic effects of tDCS. It is worth noting that although this study has explored the relevant mechanisms from the perspectives of neuroelectrophysiological mechanisms, synaptic plasticity, and neuroimmunomodulatory mechanisms, these mechanistic understandings are mainly derived from small-sample human studies or animal experiments, and there is still a lack of direct evidence from large-scale clinical neuroimaging studies and biomarker-based investigations. Therefore, the current interpretation of the mechanisms should be regarded as a theoretical framework for understanding clinical efficacy rather than definitive conclusions. Future research should integrate multimodal techniques such as neuroimaging, neuroelectrophysiology, and molecular biology to systematically elucidate the neurobiological mechanisms by which neuromodulation techniques improve lower limb motor function after stroke.

Interpretation of subgroup analyses

The subgroup analysis conducted in this study based on stroke phase shows that, in patients during the subacute phase, rTMS ranks highest in improving lower limb motor function. This result suggests that rTMS may be one of the more effective interventions for promoting functional recovery during the subacute phase. The subacute phase is often considered a critical window for neural plasticity after stroke, during which the brain's responsiveness to neuromodulation interventions is relatively high [140]. Existing studies have pointed out that early endogenous repair mechanisms are more active after a stroke, and the potential for neural network reorganization is stronger. Therefore, implementing targeted neuromodulation interventions during this period may be more beneficial for promoting functional recovery [141]. rTMS can help utilize this plasticity window to create more favorable neurophysiological conditions for lower limb motor function recovery by modulating interhemispheric inhibition imbalance, enhancing excitability in the affected hemisphere's cortex, and promoting reorganization of motor-related cortical networks [142]. In chronic phase patients, robot-assisted rehabilitation combined with functional electrical stimulation and robot-assisted rehabilitation combined with virtual reality, as comprehensive intervention strategies, show a relatively favorable trend in efficacy. These combined interventions, by integrating central regulation with peripheral training, task-oriented training with multisensory feedback, may better meet the rehabilitation needs of chronic phase patients, focusing on motor pattern reconstruction and functional compensation. Robot-assisted therapy can be divided into two main categories based on structural form and mode of action: wearable exoskeleton robots and end-effector robots [143]. The former closely fits the patient's limbs and provides direct gait trajectory guidance through joint actuation, whereas the latter indirectly drives lower limb movements via a foot platform or pedal. Robot-assisted therapy can create a high-intensity, high-repetition, task-specific training environment and achieve a level of standardization that is difficult to attain with conventional manual therapy, by precisely adjusting parameters such as movement trajectory, gait phase, walking speed, and resistance [144, 145]. Functional electrical stimulation can provide synchronized peripheral stimulation during movement, thereby facilitating muscle activation and sensory input associated with gait control [146]. In combination with robot-assisted therapy, FES may create a dual-facilitation training paradigm consisting of robot-guided repetitive task practice and electrically induced sensorimotor stimulation, which may be beneficial for gait recovery in patients with chronic stroke [68]. Virtual reality technology, by virtue of its immersive and interactive features, can not only improve patients’ adherence to training but also further optimize the rehabilitation process by promoting active engagement [147]. On this basis, the combination of virtual reality therapy and robotic technology may constitute a comprehensive rehabilitation strategy with complementary advantages. Virtual reality training can provide real-time feedback, adjustable task difficulty, and diverse simulated scenarios, thereby enhancing the enjoyment, adaptability, and engagement of training, whereas robotic therapy can offer stable mechanical assistance to support highly repetitive, goal-directed movement practice [148, 149]. For patients with limited voluntary movement ability, this combined approach may improve training participation and increase the intensity of task-specific practice, while being more conducive to the recovery of balance, gait, and overall function. The results stratified by the severity of lower limb dysfunction further support the above pattern. In the low-function group (FMA-LE < 21), rTMS ranked first, suggesting that for patients with severely impaired lower limb function, restoring interhemispheric balance and increasing cortical excitability of the affected hemisphere may be more critical than simply increasing the amount of training, as these patients often present with more pronounced corticospinal tract damage and interhemispheric inhibition imbalance. In the high-function group (FMA-LE ≥ 21), RAR combined with FES emerged as the optimal intervention, indicating that for patients who already have some active motor ability and basic walking capacity, the focus of rehabilitation should shift toward translating neuroplasticity into actual motor control improvements through repetitive, task-specific, and multisensory integration training. RAR combined with FES provides precise movement guidance, rhythmic gait practice, and proprioceptive feedback, which are more conducive to gait optimization and motor learning consolidation. The higher ranking of combined interventions in the long-term treatment subgroup may be related to their cumulative effects and multi-target characteristics. Short-term interventions typically lead to immediate changes in cortical excitability or short-term facilitation effects, with single central interventions like rTMS potentially showing significant effects during this phase. However, combined interventions involve more complex processes such as sensory-motor integration, motor skill learning, and neural remodeling, which may require a longer intervention period to support the transition from "structural repair" to "functional compensation. Gait and balance impairments in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia are not solely attributable to insufficient motor output, but may also be associated with impaired proprioceptive function, abnormal postural control, and reduced cognitive–motor integration under dual-task conditions [150–152]. Therefore, incorporating proprioceptive training and dual-task practice into a comprehensive rehabilitation framework may have important clinical implications. Recent systematic reviews have shown that proprioceptive training combined with dual-task practice can improve several key gait parameters in patients with chronic stroke, including gait speed, cadence, stride time, stride length, and step length [153]. Previous randomized controlled trial evidence suggests that dual-task specific training may be more effective than conventional physical therapy in improving spatiotemporal gait parameters in patients with chronic post-stroke hemiplegia, such as cadence, step length, stride length, and gait cycle time [154]. Given that gait and balance impairments in patients with post-stroke hemiplegia often arise in real-life situations where motor control and cognitive processing occur simultaneously, task-specific integrative training strategies may offer greater advantages than single-component interventions. Combining proprioceptive training with dual-task practice can simultaneously target two key domains, namely sensorimotor integration and cognitive resource allocation, and therefore may yield greater rehabilitation benefits while, to some extent, improving postural stability and functional walking performance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although pooled analyses were conducted according to intervention categories, substantial clinical heterogeneity remained within each category with respect to specific treatment protocols, treatment intensity, and treatment duration. In particular, interventions classified under the same treatment node were not always identical in terms of procedures, stimulation or training parameters, session frequency, session length, and total treatment course. Such variations may have influenced the estimated treatment effects and may partly explain the variability in treatment efficacy and ranking results observed in this network meta-analysis. Therefore, the pooled effect sizes in this study should be regarded as summary estimates under heterogeneous clinical conditions rather than evidence for an optimal treatment protocol or parameter combination. Future studies should perform more standardized and stratified analyses to clarify the potential moderating effects of different protocols, intensities, and durations on treatment outcomes. Second, most included studies had relatively small sample sizes, which may have reduced statistical power. Larger, multicenter randomized controlled trials are therefore needed. Third, according to the GRADE assessment, the overall quality of evidence for the main network comparisons was moderate. The main reasons for downgrading were risk of bias and imprecision. For non-pharmacological interventions such as physical therapy, neuromodulation, and acupuncture, strict blinding of participants and treatment providers is often difficult to implement, which may increase the risk of bias. In addition, some comparisons were based on limited sample sizes and were associated with wide credible intervals, which may have reduced the precision of the effect estimates. Moderate-quality evidence suggests that the true effect may be close to the current estimate, but some uncertainty remains. Therefore, the findings of this study, including the relative advantages of certain interventions, should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for comparisons supported by limited or less precise evidence. Finally, although a comprehensive literature search was conducted and the funnel plots did not indicate substantial publication bias, the possibility of unpublished studies or selective reporting cannot be completely excluded. As a result, the intervention effects reported in this study may still have been overestimated to some extent, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

This study suggests that rTMS tended to rank favorably for lower limb motor function, balance, and activities of daily living; tDCS showed potential benefits for improving walking independence; and virtual reality combined with robot-assisted training ranked relatively high for functional mobility. Subgroup analyses further indicated that stroke stage, severity of lower limb impairment, and treatment duration may influence the relative effects of these interventions. Based on the current evidence, these findings provide preliminary guidance rather than definitive clinical recommendations. Clinicians should consider individual patient characteristics and intervention accessibility when interpreting these results, rather than directly equating ranking order with clinical superiority. Future research should prioritize large-scale, high-quality randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols, head-to-head comparisons between interventions, and long-term follow-up to better establish comparative effectiveness and clinical applicability.
