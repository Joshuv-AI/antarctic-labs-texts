Combined curettage, fibular and iliac bone grafting, and dynamic hip screw fixation for a femoral neck simple bone cyst in a child with excellent functional outcome: a case report
Authors: Mohammad Alaa Aldakak, Omar Al Ayoubi, Grace Tannous, Shaza Saba, Mahmoud Ghazi, Alaa Alddin Awad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSimple bone cysts are benign, fluid-filled lesions commonly affecting the metaphyseal regions of long bones in children and adolescents. While often asymptomatic, they may present clinically when complicated by pathological fractures. Diagnosis is primarily radiographic, and management ranges from observation to surgery. This report highlights a rare femoral neck simple bone cyst with fracture, focusing on the technical nuances of definitive surgical treatment in a weight-bearing location.Case presentationA 14-year-old Arab female with no prior medical history presented with acute left hip pain after a minor fall. Radiographs revealed a displaced sub-capital femoral neck fracture overlying a well-defined cystic lesion. Surgical management included dynamic hip screw-guided cortical windowing, meticulous curettage, alcohol irrigation, tightly packed cancellous grafting from the iliac crest, and structural fibular strut grafts placed above and below the screw path. Internal fixation was achieved with a femoral head screw and dynamic hip screw plate. Histopathology confirmed a simple bone cyst.ConclusionThis case underscores the value of a tailored, multi-component surgical technique in managing simple bone cyst-associated pathological fractures in pediatric weight-bearing bones. The approach resulted in excellent structural stability and functional recovery, with the patient regaining independent, unrestricted ambulation by 10 weeks postoperatively and no complications observed.

Introduction

Simple bone cysts (SBCs), also termed unicameral or solitary bone cysts [1], are benign fluid-filled cavities, first defined by Virchow in 1876 [2]. They are frequently located in the metaphysis of long bones in children and adolescents, predominantly in the proximal humerus (55–65%) and femur (25–30%) [2] with an average onset age ranging from 8 to 14 years [3]. They account for approximately 3% of all bone lesions in this age group [2]. SBCs are typically asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on imaging performed for unrelated reasons [4]; however, in symptomatic cases, pain due to a concurrent pathological fracture is often the initial presenting symptom, with 63–87% of cases involving such fractures before surgical intervention [5, 6]. On plain radiographs, SBCs appear as well-defined, mildly expansile lesions with cortical thinning [7]. Various treatment options exist for unicameral bone cysts, including intralesional injections, curettage with bone grafting, and local adjuvant therapies [8, 9]. Despite these advances, recurrence rates remain notable, reaching up to 25% [10].

This report presents the case of a 14-year-old female who sustained a pathological sub-capital femoral neck fracture due to a simple bone cyst, managed with surgical curettage, bone grafting, and internal fixation.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old Arab female, with no history of smoking, alcohol consumption, or chronic medical conditions, and no prior surgical history, presented to the orthopedic surgery department following a fall from standing height. The patient complained of acute pain over the left hip immediately after the incident. The pain was described as sharp, localized to the groin and anterior aspect of the hip, exacerbated by limb movement.

Prior to the event, the patient was in good general health with no history of similar complaints or systemic symptoms such as fever, weight loss, night sweats, and no known family history of bone disorders. She also had no previous fractures or injuries at the affected site, and there was no history of joint swelling, stiffness, or neurological symptoms such as numbness or tingling.

On examination, the patient was unable to bear weight on the left lower limb. Inspection of the left hip revealed no visible deformity, skin changes, or open wounds. Palpation revealed tenderness over the anterior joint line with associated swelling without local rise in temperature. Passive range of motion was restricted due to pain. The distal neurovascular status of the affected limb was intact, with preserved motor strength, normal sensation, symmetrical pulses, and normal capillary refill time.

Plain radiography of the pelvis revealed a displaced sub-capital fracture of the left femoral neck overlying a well-defined, radiolucent lesion in the proximal femur (Fig. 1). Further assessment with computed tomography (CT) scan—including coronal, axial, and 3D reconstruction views—confirmed an expansile, osteolytic lesion involving the femoral neck, with thinning of the surrounding cortex, absence of periosteal reaction, and no evidence of soft tissue extension (Figs. 2, 3, and 4). The surgery was performed under general anesthesia using a posterolateral approach to the left hip. Layered dissection was carried out to expose the fracture site. A cortical window was created in the femoral neck, aligned with the intended trajectory of the dynamic hip screw (DHS). Through this window, the cystic cavity—filled with hemorrhagic fluid—was fully curetted, evacuated, and irrigated with absolute alcohol. Histopathological samples were collected from the debrided tissue. The length of the planned DHS screw was measured, and a fibular cortical graft of equal length was harvested and cut into two wedge-shaped struts (Fig. 5). These were positioned above and below the screw path to provide reinforcement. In addition, cancellous bone harvested from the iliac crest was tightly impacted into the cavity before placing the cortical struts, ensuring both biological activity and structural support. The postoperative period was clinically uneventful. The patient commenced supervised physiotherapy. Wound inspection revealed appropriate healing, and sutures were removed on postoperative day 12. At discharge, the patient was hemodynamically stable, ambulating with assistance, and demonstrated preserved distal neurovascular status. A follow-up radiograph at 3 months postoperatively showed proper implant positioning and early radiographic signs of healing and cyst consolidation (Fig. 9). Discussion

Unicameral bone cysts (UBCs), also referred to as simple bone cysts or solitary bone cysts, are common benign, tumor-like lesions of the bone [11]. Simple bone cysts typically present between the ages of 8 and 14 years and are more commonly observed in males, with a reported male-to-female ratio ranging from 2:1–4:1 [1, 3, 5, 6, 12, 13]. Although simple bone cysts are benign lesions, they frequently develop in the long bones of the extremities and are prone to pathological fractures, which can result in impaired limb function and restricted mobility [5]. According to the Neer evaluation criteria, bone cysts are classified into latent and active stages, with the active stage comprising approximately 37.9–57.9% of cases. Cysts in the active stage typically have a poorer prognosis compared with those in the latent stage [14–16]. On plain radiographs, simple bone cysts appear as well-defined lesions, often exhibiting mild expansile remodeling and cortical thinning. Characteristic signs such as the fallen fragment and rising bubble help distinguish them from other cystic lesions such as aneurysmal bone cysts, fibrous dysplasia, or intraosseous lipomas. However, diagnostic accuracy may decrease following pathological fractures. In uncertain cases, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can aid diagnosis by showing low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and bright, homogeneous signal on T2-weighted or fluid-sensitive sequences, reflecting the cyst’s fluid-filled nature [7].

Unicameral bone cysts are typically lined by a thin fibrous membrane and contain clear or serosanguinous fluid. Histologically, they may show fibrous tissue with hemosiderin, giant cells, reactive bone, and occasionally cementum-like material; blood products or fracture callus may also appear in cases of pathological fracture. The differential diagnosis includes aneurysmal bone cysts—usually eccentric with prominent cortical expansion—fibrous dysplasia, which presents with a characteristic ground-glass appearance, and enchondromas, which often show a chondroid matrix and lack sclerotic margins [11]. In our case, the lesion was centrally located with mild cortical expansion, contained serosanguinous fluid, and histology revealed benign fibrous lining without atypia, all consistent with a typical unicameral bone cyst associated with a pathological fracture. These findings, along with the absence of features typical of the aforementioned differential diagnoses, support the definitive diagnosis of a unicameral bone cyst.

Currently, the treatment of simple bone cysts primarily involves invasive methods such as intracavitary corticosteroid or bone marrow injections, as well as surgical options including cyst wall curettage, bone grafting, drilling decompression, and intramedullary fixation. These can be applied individually or in combination, with curettage and bone grafting being widely used; however, outcomes may be limited by the lack of continuous cyst drainage postoperatively [3]. Despite the variety of approaches, there is no consensus on an optimal treatment, and asymptomatic cases often require no intervention. Treatment failures—mainly due to fracture or recurrence—have been reported across all modalities [11]. In a retrospective study of 129 pediatric patients, curettage with bone grafting resulted in faster healing and fewer complications than steroid injection or demineralized bone matrix (DBM), the latter showing the shortest healing time but highest complication rate (25%). These findings support curettage with grafting as a balanced and effective option, particularly in cases with pathological fractures [4]. Our case aligns with these outcomes, showing successful recovery and no postoperative complications following curettage and autologous bone grafting. Complications such as varus deformity, premature physeal closure, and hardware-related issues were prominent—particularly in cases where less rigid fixation or delayed diagnosis occurred. Our patient experienced an uneventful postoperative course, achieved early mobilization, and showed no signs of recurrence or deformity, supporting the efficacy of aggressive curettage and anatomical reconstruction in pediatric weight-bearing bone lesions [17, 18].

Conclusion

This case highlights the need to consider simple bone cysts in pediatric pathological fractures. A customized surgical approach—combining DHS-guided curettage, alcohol irrigation, iliac cancellous impaction, and fibular strut grafting—led to full recovery without complications. By 10 weeks, the patient regained independent, unrestricted ambulation, demonstrating the technique’s efficacy in managing fragile, weight-bearing bone in children.
