Enhancing nuclear medicine residency standardization training: a comprehensive exploration of integrating a flipped classroom and case-based learning for effective instruction
Authors: Yifeng Ji, Xin Zhang, Xikui Han, Gang Xiong, Xinxin Chen, Yuanyuan Lu, Qian Zhang, Shi Sui, Zhiguang Yang, Shupeng Yu, Weina Xu, Guoliang Zheng, Wenjie Zhang, Ying Liu, Jun Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ContextIn universities outside China, particularly in Western countries, flipped classrooms and case-based learning have been used to address drawbacks of traditional teaching. Yet, in China, these models are still under exploration.AimTo investigate the preliminary outcomes and implementation challenges of implementing the flipped classroom combined with CBL teaching model.MethodsThe study was conducted at the Department of Nuclear Medicine, Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University, and included pre-course study of clinical cases, in-class group presentations, and pre- and post-course teaching assessments.ResultsResults revealed a notable improvement in post-course knowledge quiz scores, suggesting promising preliminary outcomes of this integrated teaching approach in this exploratory setting.Trainees praised the participatory and enjoyable nature, fostering a relaxed learning atmosphere. However, some found the pre-course phase time-consuming.ConclusionThis exploratory implementation suggests that the integrated model may deepen trainees’ understanding, stimulate independent learning, and improve clinical thinking. Positive feedback highlights its potential value in improving the quality and efficiency of standardized training in nuclear medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09808-1.

Background

Nuclear medicine, being a profoundly interdisciplinary clinical field, encompasses the domains of nuclear physics, chemistry, pharmacy, electronics, computer science, and medicine [1, 2]. Consequently, many trainee residents frequently experience apprehension and a lack of enthusiasm in actively participating in this field, impeding their motivation to learn. Standardized residency training for medical practitioners represents a pivotal stage in postgraduate medical education, an essential pathway for the professional development of graduates into competent clinical physicians.Its objective is to equip residents with the skills to proficiently undertake practical clinical responsibilities, thereby significantly contributing to the cultivation of highly skilled clinical professionals and the enhancement of overall medical service quality in our country [3,4]. Implementing traditional teaching methods using passive instructional models, wherein teachers primarily lecture in classrooms while students passively receive information, may lead to low teaching efficiency, limited knowledge retention, and inadequate capacity for knowledge innovation and application.Hence, innovating the training model in nuclear medicine is crucial for enhancing the quality of talent development. The flipped classroom, distinct from conventional teaching methods, shifts the instructional focus from teacher-led to student-led learning. Before class, students engage in self-directed learning using online resources, course materials, etc. During class, they share their pre-study content, which teachers enhance and refine. After class, students perform thorough self-assessment based on classroom feedback, fostering innovation and expansion to a certain extent. Integrating pre-class self-study, in-class feedback, and post-class self-assessment aims to foster active learning and augment students’ thinking and innovation capabilities [5–9]. The CBL teaching model entails crafting open-ended problems derived from real cases, demanding students to apply their acquired knowledge to resolve practical issues. This method not only stimulates divergent thinking but also improves students’ abilities in clinical practice and application [10–14]. Internationally, flipped classroom and CBL have been successfully applied in various medical education settings, including undergraduate pathology [15], endodontics residency [16], and nephrology clerkships [17]. These studies report improved student engagement and learning outcomes. More recently, Li et al. demonstrated the effectiveness of flipped classroom combined with CBL in environmental health science education [18]; Kolahdouzan et al. found that this approach significantly improved learning and satisfaction among surgical internship students compared to traditional lectures [19]; and Hu et al. reported that integrating flipped classroom and CBL in medical morphology postgraduate training promoted the integration of theory and practice [20].However, challenges remain, including increased student workload and the need for adequate pre-class preparation. Few studies have explored these methods in nuclear medicine residency training, particularly in the Chinese context. At our institution, residents come from multiple departments with heterogeneous backgrounds, and traditional teaching has proven inadequate.Therefore, this exploratory study aims to investigate the preliminary outcomes and implementation challenges of integrating a flipped classroom with CBL in the standardized training of nuclear medicine residents, using hyperthyroidism as a teaching example.

Methods

Study participants

This study adopted a retrospective design and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (approval number: 2024PS757K). We retrieved and statistically analyzed questionnaire data accumulated from previous resident teaching evaluations, without implementing additional interventions or prospective data collection for the present research. The questionnaire data collected from the residents during their routine standardized residency training activities at the department of Nuclear Medicine, Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University, in 2023. The participants were from the nuclear medicine unit, radiology unit, and ultrasound unit in the hospital. All residents who rotated through the department during the study period were consecutively enrolled. No formal sample size calculation was performed, as this was an exploratory study of the entire eligible cohort.

Analysis of pre-class and post-class test results

The knowledge test was newly developed by three senior nuclear medicine educators based on course learning objectives. Content validity was assessed by a panel of five nuclear medicine experts. A pilot test was conducted with 10 residents who were not included in the study sample, and minor wording adjustments were made based on their feedback.

Notably, this knowledge test was developed as a study-specific assessment tool for the present exploratory investigation. No formal psychometric evaluation of reliability (e.g., internal consistency) was conducted in this initial implementation, and the instrument should be interpreted as a non-validated, context-specific assessment scale.

Concept-based questions covered various aspects, including the classification of hyperthyroidism, nuclear medicine diagnostic methods related to hyperthyroidism diagnosis, clinical manifestations of hyperthyroidism, indications for iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism, repeated treatment with iodine-131 for hyperthyroidism, contraindications for iodine-131 treatment, properties of iodine-131 and technetium-99m, thyroid imaging process, and iodine uptake rate process.

Application-based questions delved into areas such as laboratory indicators of hyperthyroidism, treatment options for hyperthyroidism, preparation before iodine-131 treatment, determination of iodine-131 treatment dosage, process and precautions of iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism, post-treatment precautions for iodine-131 treatment, and follow-up after iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism. Each correct answer earned 1 point, with a maximum achievable score of 20.

To assess the instructional outcomes of the flipped classroom in conjunction with CBL, the researcher calculated the percentage of correct responses on both pre- and post-class tests.

Evaluation of post-course perceived outcomes

Post-class feedback assessment through questionnaire (Form B)

After the class, participants completed a questionnaire (Form B) to assess feedback on the CBL teaching model in the flipped classroom. Likert scales were employed to quantitatively rate the feedback, with 1 indicating "strongly disagree," 2 for "disagree," 3 for "not necessarily," 4 for "agree," and 5 for "strongly agree."

Post-class feedback assessment through questionnaire (Form C)

Another questionnaire (Form C) was administered following the session to further gauge the perceived outcomes of the teaching model.

Both Form B and Form C are study-specific feedback questionnaires designed exclusively for this exploratory evaluation. No prior reliability validation (e.g., internal consistency or test-retest reliability) was performed for these instruments, which were developed to collect formative feedback on this single educational intervention.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was executed utilizing GraphPad Prism 9 software. Descriptive statistics were employed to portray continuous data, including knowledge test scores and Likert scale ratings, represented as mean±standard deviation. Inter-group comparisons were conducted using paired t-tests.

For pre- and post-class correct response rates to 20 questions, which exhibited a non-normal distribution, median values along with quartiles were employed. Group comparisons were executed through the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as frequencies (percentages), and inter-group comparisons were undertaken using either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

A cohort of 40 trainees participated in this study, with a gender distribution of 15 males and 25 females. The distribution across trainee categories was as follows: 9 individuals from the Nuclear Medicine unit, including 4 from the 2020 cohort, 2 from the 2021 cohort, and 3 from the 2022 cohort. Additionally, there were 25 trainees from the department of radiology, and 6 trainees from the department of ultrasound.

Evaluation of knowledge test outcomes

Before the class, all 40 participants (100%, 40/40) completed the knowledge test. The average score was 9.95 ± 3.236 points, with a median correct response rate of 45.00% for the 20 questions.

After the class, 36 participants (90%, 36/40) took the knowledge test, resulting in an average score of 14.5 ± 3.989 points. The median correct response rate rose to 69.45%. A significant difference in correct response rates between pre- and post-test was observed (P = 0.0265), as depicted in Figs. 2 and 3.

Discussion

The significance of standardized training for resident physicians lies in its role as a crucial avenue for enhancing their multifaceted capabilities. To improve the quality of this training, there has been a continuous evolution in the modes of instruction and mentorship provided by training educators.

Currently, the prevalent teaching methodologies adopt a predominantly didactic approach, characterized by teacher-centric instruction where students passively absorb information. This approach, however, has limitations as it restricts students’ opportunities for active critical thinking and analysis during class. Consequently, there is a risk of a constrained grasp of essential and challenging concepts.

In response to these limitations, the flipped classroom instructional model has emerged as a transformative alternative. This model challenges the traditional paradigm by shifting the learning and exploration phases of relevant knowledge to occur before the class. In-class activities then become focused on application, analysis, evaluation, and high-order cognitive training. Consequently, this model significantly enhances the understanding and mastery of pivotal and challenging knowledge areas.

The CBL approach involves instructing students through the examination of clinical cases, merging theoretical concepts with practical applications to enhance trainees’ comprehension, application, and mastery of medical knowledge.

The integration of the flipped classroom and CBL instructional models not only addresses the limitations of CBL, such as an excessive reliance on student initiative and imprecise teaching methods but also capitalizes on the advantages of pre-class learning and reflective efforts during the flipped classroom sessions. This combined instructional methodology has demonstrated success in various fields, including undergraduate pathology education [15], endodontic residency training [16], and nephrology internship education [17].

The training staff at the standardized training base of nuclear medicine consists of undergraduates with five years of medical professional experience. The student body comprises individuals from the nuclear medicine base (specialized master and social standardized training students) and standardized training rotation students from radiology and ultrasound bases. Standardized training trainees exhibit a multidisciplinary educational background, have limited time for learning clinical medicine-related knowledge, and face high expectations for cultivation objectives. The outcomes of teaching this diverse group have garnered significant attention.

This study provides pioneering exploratory evidence regarding the application outcomes of the flipped classroom combined with the CBL small-group instructional model in the education of medical trainees undergoing standardized training.

The study findings revealed that the median correct response rate for all questions in the pre-course knowledge test was 45.00%. Notably, the correct response rates varied significantly across different questions, ranging from 15% to 100%. Conceptual questions, such as those pertaining to the classification of hyperthyroidism and the nuclear medicine examination methods for its diagnosis, demonstrated a commendable correct response rate exceeding 90%. However, questions related to practical application, such as the correct response rate of the questions on the choice of treatment modality for hyperthyroidism, the determination of the dose of iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism, the process of iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism, and precautions was lower than 65%, indicating that the ability of the trainees to apply the theoretical knowledge in practice is still insufficient through the static learning of courseware materials and other static learning (the traditional mode of teaching). This pattern—high performance on factual recall versus lower performance on application—is consistent with the well-documented phenomenon that passive learning often fails to develop clinical reasoning skills [10,13]. It underscores the need for active learning strategies that bridge the gap between knowing and doing.

After implementing flipped classroom combined with CBL in medical education, the median correct response rate for all questions among medical trainees was 69.45%. Moreover, there was a significant improvement in the correct response rates for both conceptual questions (such as repeated treatment of hyperthyroidism with iodine-131, contraindications of iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism, properties of iodine-131, and the process of thyroid scintigraphy) and application questions (determining the dosage of iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism-1, post-treatment precautions for iodine-131 treatment for hyperthyroidism-2) (P < 0.001). The observed improvements in test scores suggest that the combination of flipped classroom and CBL group teaching may support trainees’ understanding and mastery of knowledge while enhancing their application abilities. This improvement in both question types aligns with the theoretical advantages of flipped classroom and CBL: pre-class self-study builds foundational knowledge, while in-class case discussions require students to actively apply that knowledge, thereby strengthening both retention and transfer [9,13]. Similar gains have been reported in other disciplines, such as nephrology [17] and pathology [15].

In comparison to pre-class knowledge test results, residents who underwent flipped classroom combined with CBL group teaching experienced a significant and noteworthy overall increase in knowledge test scores. This improvement extended to all students and those whose pre-class knowledge test scores were less than or equal to the median score of all participants (9.5 points). The average knowledge test scores exhibited a substantial increase for all students, progressing from 9.95 before the class to 14.5 after the class. Notably, for students with pre-class knowledge test scores less than or equal to the median, the average knowledge test scores rose from 7.6 to 14.2. These findings are consistent with anticipated conclusions, suggesting that this teaching method has the potential to enhance overall learning outcomes for medical students, with a more pronounced impact on students exhibiting relatively weaker self-directed learning abilities. The particularly large gain in the lower-baseline subgroup (an increase of 6.6 points, compared to 4.55 points for the whole cohort) suggests that the structured pre-class materials and collaborative in-class activities may provide effective scaffolding for learners who initially struggle with self-directed study, a finding that echoes previous research on flipped classroom benefits for lower-performing students [6].

In evaluating teaching feedback, the study gathered responses from 40 trainees, indicating a notable satisfaction with the “flipped classroom combined with CBL teaching model.” Students articulated that this approach fosters higher engagement and contributes to the development of critical thinking, collaboration, mutual assistance, and innovation skills. Nevertheless, the study identified practical implementation challenges, including insufficient pre-class research by students and inappropriate difficulty levels in the assigned problems.The lower scores on the item “the duration of pre-class learning is appropriate” (4.38 ± 0.31) and the fact that only 72.22% agreed that the model provided broader knowledge acquisition reflect challenges commonly reported in flipped classroom literature, such as increased student workload and the need for better guidance on pre-class activities [5,6].

To optimize teaching outcomes, the study recommends providing trainees with sufficient preparation time before class to conduct case analyses and literature review in a relaxed manner, and promptly adjusting teaching methods and content based on ongoing trainee feedback.

This study has several limitations. First, this study did not employ qualitative research methods (such as interviews or focus groups); therefore, our understanding of trainees’ in-depth experiences, perceptions, and the reasons behind their feedback is limited, which is a shortcoming of this study. Second, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size (N = 40), which limits the generalizability of the findings. Third, no control group (e.g., traditional lecture-based teaching) was included, so we cannot rule out maturation effects or the possibility that the observed improvements were due to repeated testing rather than the intervention itself. Accordingly, the observed improvements in knowledge scores cannot be definitively attributed to the teaching intervention, and all findings should be interpreted as preliminary and exploratory.Fourth, the knowledge test was developed by the teaching team and may have been biased toward the specific content emphasized in the course. Fifth, long-term knowledge retention was not assessed; therefore, we do not know whether the learning gains persist over time. Future studies should include randomized controlled designs, larger multicenter samples, qualitative interviews to capture learner perspectives, and follow-up assessments to evaluate the durability of learning outcomes.

Conclusion

To realize the foundational objective of education—cultivating individuals of exemplary moral character—it is imperative to focus on nurturing adept and innovative medical professionals harmonized with the evolving landscape of the national healthcare sector. A pressing necessity exists to proactively introduce avant-garde concepts and methodologies in medical education, spanning teaching philosophies, methods, objectives, and content. Given the limitations of conventional teaching paradigms in addressing the evolving requisites of contemporary and prospective medical talent, this research pioneers the application of a blended teaching methodology. Specifically, it integrates the flipped classroom and CBL approaches for the training of nuclear medicine residents.

This innovative pedagogical strategy enhances interactive discourse between supervising teachers and residents, empowering the latter to autonomously acquire knowledge, formulate queries, dissect and resolve challenges, and invigorate their passion for the discipline. Furthermore, it expedites residents’ transition from theoretical foundations to pragmatic competencies, thereby augmenting the caliber of their training. This teaching model is well-suited to the characteristics and patterns of adult learning, contributing to the cultivation of medical professionals aligned with the requirements of the modern medical education system. Given its commendable student satisfaction metrics, this methodology exhibits potential to emerge as a premier pedagogical model in nuclear medicine clinical education.
