Development of the FGFR4-ADC bearing the ferroptosis inducer sulfasalazine for hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Ying Zhang, Xiaohong Cui, Jing Du, Ge Cui, Yuhang Ling, Chengwu Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the most prevalent liver cancer, accounts for ∼90% of all cases. Despite antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) showing significant efficacy in treating various hematologic malignancies and solid tumors, their application in HCC treatment is unimpressive. In this study, we identified that fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 (FGFR4) is highly expressed in HCC patient tumors and its expression correlates with overall survival rates, suggesting that FGFR4 could be an excellent therapeutic target for HCC. We subsequently developed an innovative anti-FGFR4 antibody exhibiting robust binding and endocytosis activities within HCC cells. Utilizing a GGFG linker coupled with the ferroptosis inducer sulfasalazine, we produced an FGFR4-ADC compound. This ADC specifically induced ferroptosis in FGFR4-expressing HCC cells and demonstrated FGFR4 expression-dependent anti-tumor activity in xenograft models. The preclinical pharmacokinetics and safety profile of our developed FGFR4-ADC suggest it may represent a pioneering ADC molecule offering a strategy for treating patients with tumor-associated FGFR4 expression.

Introduction

Primary liver cancer ranks among the most prevalent forms of cancer globally, standing as the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths in men and the seventh in women.1 HCC, which constitutes approximately 90% of primary liver cancers, presents a significant global health challenge.2 Predictions suggest that HCC could result in up to one million deaths annually by 2030. The difficulty associated with early diagnosis often results in patients presenting at advanced stages of HCC, where effective treatment options are limited.3 Small-molecule inhibitors such as sorafenib, regorafenib, and lenvatinib have been utilized to target multiple tyrosine kinase receptors; however, these therapies have only demonstrated marginal survival benefits.4 Immunotherapy combinations involving PD-L1 and CTLA-4 monoclonal antibodies have shown promise by improving progression-free survival rates and overall patient outcomes.5 Despite these advancements, there remains an urgent need for more efficacious treatments for individuals diagnosed with HCC. The development of therapeutic strategies is crucial to address this unmet clinical need and improve the prognosis for those affected by this aggressive form of liver cancer.

The fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) signaling pathway is pivotal in regulating cell survival, proliferation, and migration.6,7 Its dysregulation has been frequently observed across various types of cancer.8,9 While most of the past research has focused on FGFR1, FGFR2, and FGFR3, recent studies have highlighted the significant role that FGFR4 plays in tumorigenesis, disease progression, and resistance to anti-tumor therapies.10,11,12,13 Structurally, FGFR4 shares a canonical RTK architecture, including a large extracellular ligand-binding domain composed of three immunoglobulin-like subunits followed by a single transmembrane helix and an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain.14 The aberrant expression of fibroblast growth factor 19 (FGF19)-FGFR4 has been identified as an oncogenic driver pathway for patients with HCC, which makes FGFR4 an appealing target for therapeutic intervention in HCC treatment strategies.15 Beyond targeting small molecules within the intracellular region, there is growing interest in drug discovery efforts focusing on monoclonal antibodies or ADCs that target the extracellular domain of FGFR4. These approaches could potentially offer more effective treatments for those affected by this aggressive form of liver cancer.

Ferroptosis, a unique form of cell death reliant on the transition metal iron, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and phospholipids with polyunsaturated fatty acid chains (PUFA-PLs), has garnered significant attention in recent years due to its distinct morphological and mechanistic differences from other types of programmed cell death.16,17,18 Its association extends beyond degenerative and ischemic diseases to include potential applications in anti-tumor therapy. Cancer cells resistant to traditional treatments are particularly susceptible to ferroptosis.19,20,21 The Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11 (SLC7A11) is the catalytic subunit of the amino acid transporter system Xc−, facilitating extracellular cystine absorption. This absorbed cystine is subsequently converted into cytosolic cysteine, a critical precursor for glutathione (GSH) biosynthesis.22 GSH is an effective scavenger of lipid peroxides and an essential cofactor for the selenoenzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4).22,23,24 Thus, the SLC7A11-GSH-GPX4 axis is a primary cellular mechanism that confers protection against ferroptosis.24 Sulfasalazine was initially approved by the FDA for treating rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and certain dermatological conditions.25,26,27 Recently, it has been identified as an effective inducer of ferroptosis through the potent inhibition of SLC7A11 and exhibits anti-tumor activity across various preclinical models.28,29 Despite the effectiveness of sulfasalazine, it still suffers from a lack of selectivity.

ADCs represent an optimal drug modality for cancer treatment, merging the specificity of antibodies with potent cytotoxic drugs that selectively target antigen-expressing tumor cells.30 Since the approval of the first ADC drug, Mylotarg, in 2000, a total of 16 ADC drugs have been approved. However, it is essential to note that current cytotoxic warheads in clinical trials rely only on limited types of drugs. This dependence highlights the significant challenge of selecting a suitable and effective ADC payload.31 In addition, identifying and validating tumor-associated antigens (TAA) for monoclonal antibodies is also a primary focus in developing ADCs.32 To minimize off-target toxicity and achieve a satisfactory therapeutic index for the ADCs, it is crucial that target antigens exhibit high expression levels within tumors while maintaining negligible or no expression in normal tissues. Despite these advancements in targeted therapies, no specific ADC has yet been approved for HCC. At present, Glypican-3 (GPC3) is the only TAA of clinical ADC drugs for treating HCC. The lack of specific TAA presents a considerable challenge to advancing HCC-specific treatments.33

This study identified FGFR4 as a potential therapeutic target for treating HCC. We generated an innovative antibody and conjugated it with the ferroptosis inducer sulfasalazine to create an innovative ADC molecule. This developed FGFR4-ADC demonstrated promising anti-tumor activity alongside favorable pharmacokinetic properties and toxicological profiles. Our findings suggest that by specifically inducing ferroptosis, FGFR4-ADC could serve as an innovative therapeutic agent in HCC treatment.

Results

Fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 is highly expressed in hepatocellular carcinoma and associated with overall survival

An analysis was conducted on the publicly accessible mRNA database, the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (www.proteinatlas.org), to identify antigens that could potentially serve as suitable targets for the development of antibody-based therapeutics. Among a wide array of proteins, FGFR4 is highly expressed in various types of cancers, especially liver cancer (Figure 1A). High FGFR4 expression was also detected in sarcoma, breast, gastric, and colorectal cancers (Figure 1A). Further analysis revealed that FGFR4 also showed high expression of mRNA in many HCC tumor cells (Figure 1B). By querying the Gene Expression Profiling and Interactive Analyses 2 (GEPIA2) (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/)34 and the University of Alabama at Birmingham CANcer data analysis (UALCAN) (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) databases,35 the overexpression of FGFR4 was confirmed in primary HCC, as indicated by significantly higher mean mRNA expression in tumors versus their corresponding normal tissues (p < 0.0001; Figure 1D). However, the expression of homologous family members FGFR1 and FGFR2 in HCC tumor tissues was lower than that in normal tissues (Figures S1A and S1B). Although the expression of FGFR3 in HCC tissues was slightly higher than that in normal tissues, the difference was not as significant as that of FGFR4 (Figure S1C). To further investigate FGFR4 expression in patients with HCC, immunohistochemistry (IHC) studies were performed in 5 pairs of matched HCC tissues and corresponding normal tissues. The results also demonstrated increased expression of FGFR4 in tumors versus normal tissues (Figure S1D).

To further determine whether the high expression level of FGFR4 in patients with HCC was associated with poor prognosis, we performed analysis using the Kaplan-Meier plotter (KM plotter) (https://kmplot.com/analysis/),36 OncoLnc (http://www.oncolnc.org/)37 and GEPIA2 databases. Our results revealed that higher FGFR4 expression in HCC is associated with significantly decreased Overall Survival (OS) in the KM plotter database (Figure 1E; hazard ratio = 1.48; log rank p = 0.032) and OncoLnc database (Figure 1F; hazard ratio = 0.6751; log rank p = 0.0393). Similar analyses also showed that high FGFR4 expression was associated with low Progression-Free Survival (PFS) in the KM plotter database (Figure 1G; hazard ratio = 1.38; log rank p = 0.05) and low Disease-Free Survival (DFS) in the GEPIA2 database (Figure 1H; hazard ratio = 1.4; log rank p = 0.03). Different from FGFR4, the elevated expression of other FGFR family members was not associated with decreased OS or DFS in patients with HCC. In PFS analysis, only high FGFR3 expression was associated with decreased PFS (Figures S1E–S1G). Together, these findings support that FGFR4 may play an important role in the development of HCC tumors.

Development of an anti-fibroblast growth factor receptor 4 monoclonal antibody

Anti-FGFR4 monoclonal antibodies were generated by immunizing FGFR4 knockout mice with human FGFR4 protein. These antibodies were subsequently screened to identify clones suitable for ADC generation (Figure 2A). We established HEK293T cell lines overexpressing human, cynomolgus monkey, or mouse FGFR4 and conducted screening procedures to isolate antibodies that bind specifically to both types of FGFR4. Given that endocytosis is a crucial property for antibodies used in ADCs, we further investigated these antibodies' endocytic capabilities within FGFR4-expressing cells. Considering the role of FGF19-FGFR4 in promoting HCC cell proliferation, our screening process also focused on identifying antibodies capable of blocking the FGF19-FGFR4 signaling pathway. Our screening resulted in an effective monoclonal antibody, clonal 3E5 (Figure 2A). As shown in Figure 2B, 3E5 binds effectively to HEK293T cell lines overexpressing human, cynomolgus monkey or mouse FGFR4 but does not interact with wild-type HEK293T cells. Importantly, it showed no binding affinity toward other members of the FGFR family (Figure S2A), indicating its specificity toward recognizing FGFR4. We further analyzed the binding capacity and internalization of 3E5 to human HCC cells. As shown in Figure 2C, 3E5 exhibited strong binding affinity to FGFR4 high-expressing Huh-7 cells (EC50 = 1.825 ng/mL) and Hep 3B cells (EC50 = 5.469 ng/mL). However, when interacting with SK-HEP-1 cells with low FGFR4 expression, its binding strength was significantly reduced (EC50 = 36.54 ng/mL). As expected, 3E5 had a higher antibody internalization rate in Huh-7 and Hep 3B cells with high FGFR4 expression. In SK-Hep-1 cells with low FGFR4 expression, the antibody internalization rate of 3E5 was significantly reduced (Figure 2D). Our ELISA analysis further revealed that 3E5 possesses an effective blocking mechanism against FGF19’s ability to bind to FGFR4, as illustrated in Figure 2E. To substantiate this finding, we engineered a luciferase reporter cell line and found that 3E5 can indeed effectively inhibit FGF19-induced signaling via FGFR4, as indicated by Figure 2F. In addition to these findings, we also observed that 3E5 exhibits a robust Antibody-Dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity (ADCC) activity (Figure 2G). This potent ADCC activity of 3E5 not only enhances its ability to recruit immune cells and induce targeted cell death but also makes it an ideal candidate for the development of ADCs. These experimental results strongly suggest that the unique properties of 3E5 make it well-suited for the development of ADCs specifically targeting FGFR4-expressing cancer cells.

Fibroblast growth factor receptor 4-ADC inhibits the growth of fibroblast growth factor receptor 4-expressing hepatocellular carcinoma cells

The FGFR4-targeted ADC, named RZ-11, was generated by bio-conjugating the ferroptosis inducer sulfasalazine to endogenous cysteine residues on 3E5 via a Glycine-Glycine-Phenylalanine-Glycine (GGFG) linker (Figure 3A). Hydrophobic interaction chromatography showed a homogeneous drug distribution, and the DAR of the synthesized ADC was approximately four (Figure 3B). As expected, RZ-11 showed a similar ability to bind to FGFR4 as the uncoupled monoclonal antibody (mAb) 3E5, which was confirmed by flow cytometry of Huh-7 cells (Figure 3C). In addition, RZ-11 also showed the same endocytotic activity as 3E5 in Huh-7 cells, further suggesting that neither the payload nor the linker affected the biological activity of the antibody (Figure 3D).Figure 3Structure and in vitro efficacy of anti-FGFR4 ADC(A) The chemical structure of FGFR4-ADC RZ-11, composed of anti-FGFR4 mAb 3E5, a GGFG linker, and the ferroptosis inducer payload sulfasalazine. To evaluate the cell cytotoxicity of RZ-11 against FGFR4-positive HCC cells, we treated Huh-7, Hep 3B, and SK-HEP-1 cells with different concentrations of RZ-11. A concentration-dependent in vitro antiproliferative response to RZ-11 was observed in all three HCC cell lines. Moreover, Huh-7 and Hep 3B cells with high FGFR4 expression were almost completely inhibited, while SK-HEP-1 cells with low FGFR4 expression were only partially inhibited, with the IC50 values of 1.654, 4.091, and 8.930 ng/mL, respectively (Figure 3E). Although the unconjugated mAb 3E5 showed cell growth inhibitory activity against all three HCC cells, these activities were rather weaker than that of the RZ-11, with the IC50 values of 5.600 ng/mL in Huh-7 cells, 7.184 ng/mL in Hep-3B cells, and 22.00 ng/mL in SK-HEP-1 cells. A control ADC, designated as hIgG1-ADC, which is composed of a non-binding mAb and the same linker and payload as RZ-11, did not exhibit any inhibitory activity on cell growth across all tested cell lines. Taken together, these results indicate that the cell growth inhibitory activity of RZ-11 was remarkably enhanced by drug conjugation to the anti-FGFR4 mAb, and also that RZ-11 shows target-specific growth inhibition against FGFR4-positive cell lines.

Fibroblast growth factor receptor 4-antibody-drug conjugates induced ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma tumor cells

Next, we set out to confirm whether RZ-11 induced cell death through ferroptosis. Huh-7 and SK-HEP-1 cells were treated with hIgG1-ADC, 3E5, and RZ-11 for 48 h, followed by the assessment of relevant ferroptosis markers. Given that sulfasalazine can inhibit the function of SLC7A11 and suppress intracellular GSH synthesis, we first measured the concentration of intracellular GSH. As shown in Figure 4A, RZ-11 treatment significantly reduced the amount of intracellular GSH compared to hIgG1-ADC and 3E5 treatment groups. Ferroptosis is often accompanied by an accumulation of lipid peroxides and iron ions within cells. Therefore, we further examined these parameters in our cell samples. We found that cells treated with RZ-11 exhibited a markedly higher level of both lipid peroxides and iron ions than those treated with hIgG1-ADC or 3E5 (Figures 4B and 4C). Additionally, using BODIPY-C11 staining to visualize mitochondrial changes in our cell samples allowed us to further investigate alterations in lipid peroxidation levels. As shown in Figure 4D, the lipid peroxidation products in HCC cells increased significantly after RZ-11 treatment, but there was no change in the cells treated with hIgG1-ADC or 3E5. Antibody-drug conjugates are highly effective in fibroblast growth factor receptor 4-expressing xenograft models

The anti-tumor efficacy of RZ-11 was further investigated in three subcutaneous tumor models. Specifically, nude mice were implanted with 1 x 106 tumor cells. Once the tumors reached an average volume of 100–200 mm3, the mice were randomized into groups consisting of five animals each and treated with indicated dose levels of RZ-11, 3E5, or a vehicle control. For mice bearing Huh-7 tumors, RZ-11 was administered at doses of either 1 or 3 mg/kg. Notably, we observed a dose-dependent inhibition of tumor growth and even regression when the dosage reached 3 mg/kg (Figure 5A). Furthermore, this potent anti-cancer effect persisted for three weeks following the final administration of RZ-11. Six doses of 3E5 were administered at 3 or 10 mg/kg, and dose-dependent tumor growth inhibition was also observed. The anti-tumor activity of 3E5 may be attributed to its ADCC activity and ligand-blocking activity. Most importantly, it was found that the anti-tumor efficacy displayed by RZ-11 significantly outperformed that shown by 3E5. In addition, tumor samples were collected and analyzed to evaluate major indicators of ferroptosis, including reduced intracellular glutathione levels and accumulation of lipid peroxides. These changes were only significantly observed in tumor samples treated with RZ-11. In contrast, none of these markers changed significantly in tumors treated with 3E5 (Figure 5B). These further highlight the unique efficacy of RZ-11 in inducing ferroptotic cell death within tumor cells. Similarly, RZ-11 showed a strong tumor suppressor effect in FGFR4 high-expressing Hep 3B xenograft models (Figure 5C). Although the anti-tumor efficacy of RZ-11 in FGFR4 low-expressing SK-HEP-1 cell models was not as obvious as against Huh-7 and Hep 3B cells, it still showed a good tumor inhibition effect (Figure 5D). These findings suggest that RZ-11 may provide significant therapeutic benefits in patients with high FGFR4 expression, and moderate therapeutic effects in patients with low FGFR4 expression. These results further suggest the potential applicability and generality of FGFR4-ADC in different FGFR4-positive patients.

Fibroblast growth factor receptor 4-antibody-drug conjugates has good PK and safety profiles in the mice model

Sandwich ELISA was performed to determine concentrations of total mAb (both conjugated and unconjugated) and intact ADC (conjugated only) in the blood. The half-lives of the total and conjugated antibodies were found to be approximately 9.9 days and 10.4 days, respectively (Figure 6A). This observation further indicates the excellent stability these antibodies exhibit within an in vivo environment, thereby highlighting their potential for therapeutic applications. Next, we evaluated the nonclinical safety profile of the RZ-11. BALB/cJ mice were administered with RZ-11 and 3E5 once weekly over three cycles at doses reaching up to 20 mg/kg. Throughout the duration of this study, no significant toxicity was observed in either treatment group as determined by monitoring factors such as body weight loss and daily behavioral observations (Figure 6B). To further assess potential liver toxicity, blood chemistry tests were carried out fifteen days post-injection of single-dose both RZ-11 and 3E5. We quantified the enzymes aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) which indicate liver function, and found that these enzymes remained within the normal range (Figure 6C). These results suggest that the risk of RZ-11 and 3E5 inducing hepatotoxicity is almost negligible. In addition, Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) Staining showed no target-dependent toxicity in normal tissues including the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys (Figure 6D).

RZ-11 exhibits anti-tumor activity in other fibroblast growth factor receptor 4-expressing cancers

Consistent with this, FGFR4 showed high mRNA expression levels in many gastric cancer and colorectal tumor cells in the HPA database (Figures S3A and S3B). By testing four cancer cell lines, we found that FGFR4 was highly expressed on NUGC-4 and SW620 cells, and was also moderately expressed on HT-29 and SNU-5 cells, further confirming FGFR4 expression on multiple cancers (Figure 7C). We further examined the binding capacity and internalization of 3E5 in NUGC-4 and HT-29 cells. As expected, 3E5 had a stronger binding affinity (IC50 = 12.88 ng/mL) and internalization with NUGC-4 cells with higher FGFR4 expression, while HT-29 cells had lower binding affinity (IC50 = 17.64 ng/mL) and internationalization. To verify whether RZ-11 has a cytotoxic effect on these tumor cells as well, we treated the NUGC-4 and HT-29 cells with RZ-11 and found that RZ-11 could effectively inhibit the proliferation of these tumor cells, and the IC50 was 2.834 and 5.919 ng/mL, respectively (Figure 7D). We also found that although 3E5 has anti-tumor activity against NUGC-4 and HT-29 cells both in vitro and in vivo, its activity is relatively weaker than RZ-11. Comparative analysis showed that RZ-11 not only inhibited tumors through the ligand blocking and ADCC activity of the antibody, but also induced ferroptosis by transporting payload sulfasalazine into cells. Importantly, this mechanism is not limited to HCC but also applies to other tumor types expressing FGFR4. In conclusion, our study suggests that RZ-11 has the potential to be an effective therapeutic agent for patients with various types of FGFR4-positive cancers.

Discussion

ADC is a specialized form of targeted chemotherapy.38 The evolution of ADC payloads has progressed from traditional chemotherapy drugs to tubulin inhibitors and further to DNA-modifying agents. This ongoing discovery of more balanced payloads in terms of safety, cellular activity, and solubility effectively propels the advancement of ADC products.31 Numerous small molecules capable of inducing ferroptosis have been identified.39,40 Specific delivery of certain ferroptosis inducers to cancer cells via ADC could potentially improve therapeutic efficacy while improving safety. Previous research has successfully conjugated the ferroptosis-inducing compound RSL3 with the HER2 antibody to create an ADC, which demonstrated efficacy against cancer cells in vitro.41 In our study, we chose sulfasalazine as a payload for the FGFR4 antibody due to its proven anti-tumor activity both in vitro and in vivo, along with its favorable safety profile approved by the FDA for treating rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease.25,27,42 Given that tumor cells exhibit heightened sensitivity toward ferroptosis compared to normal cells, using sulfasalazine as a payload can selectively eliminate tumor cells while minimizing damage to healthy tissues.19,43,44 In the future, it may be worthwhile exploring other small molecules that induce ferroptosis as potential payloads for developing more effective ADC therapies.

HCC often presents insidiously, with approximately 55% of patients diagnosed at intermediate or advanced stages. At these stages, early surgical resection and local treatments are no longer viable options, leaving systemic treatment as the only potentially beneficial therapeutic approach. Historically, chemotherapy was the primary systemic treatment for advanced HCC; however, its efficacy was limited and accompanied by significant side effects. The FDA’s approval in 2007 of Sorafenib, an orally administered multi-kinase inhibitor, marked a turning point in targeted therapy for advanced HCC.2,44 Despite this breakthrough and subsequent approval of Lenvatinib, solidifying multi-kinase inhibitors' role in first-line treatment for advanced HCC, their therapeutic impact remains unsatisfactory.45 The introduction of immune checkpoint blockade into first-line treatments has shown promise through the combination of Durvalumab (an anti-PD-L1 mAb) and Tremelimumab (an anti-CTLA-4 antibody).46 However, current systemic therapies still exhibit low response rates and highly variable efficacy among patients with advanced HCC. Given these challenges, there is an urgent need to identify appropriate TAA to benefit patients with advanced HCC.

FGFR4, as an important target in the field of cancer treatment, has received extensive attention in recent years. A number of studies have confirmed that FGFR4 is highly expressed in a variety of tumor types, including liver cancer, breast cancer, colorectal cancer, rhabdomyosarcoma, and nasopharyngeal carcinoma.10,47,48,49,50 Compared with traditional FGFR small molecule inhibitors, FGFR4-targeted antibodies demonstrate significant specificity advantages. They can precisely bind to FGFR4 and avoid non-specific inhibition of other FGFR family members, thereby significantly reducing potential side effects. At present, the research and development of antibody drugs targeting FGFR4 is progressing rapidly. For instance, the monoclonal anti-FGFR4 antibody LD1 developed by Genentech has been proven to specifically block the binding of FGF19 to FGFR4 and inhibit downstream signal transduction. And in FGF19 transgenic mouse models, effectively block the liver tumor formation.51 Furthermore, chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) technology has also shown significant potential in FGFR4-targeted therapy. Studies have shown that bispecific CAR-T cells (BiCisCAR) targeting both FGFR4 and CD276 significantly enhance the anti-tumor effect in the rhabdomyosarcoma model.52 Furthermore, FGFR4-targeted CAR-T cells based on highly specific and highly affinity nanobodies also exhibited significant anti-tumor activity in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) models.53 These research results further confirm the potential of FGFR4-targeted therapy. Our study shows that FGFR4 expression is significantly increased in patients with HCC and is strongly correlated with survival, making FGFR4 a promising TAA for the treatment of HCC. FGFR2, a member of the same family, has already been utilized as a TAA in cholangiocarcinoma treatment, with antibodies showing encouraging clinical results.46 Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that FGFR4-based liver cancer therapies, including ADC, CD3 bispecific antibody products, and so forth., may yield better therapeutic outcomes in the future.

Fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling pathways are instrumental in various cellular processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, wound healing, and angiogenesis.6,54,55 FGFR4 is the predominant subtype of FGFRs found in human hepatocytes. Both FGF19 and FGFR4 exhibit high expression levels in primary liver cancer. The FGF19/FGFR4 signaling pathway has been shown to promote survival, proliferation, and metastasis of HCC through a variety of mechanisms.15,56,57 This suggests that targeting this specific pathway could provide a promising approach for treating HCC. In our research efforts, we have developed an anti-FGFR4 antibody capable of blocking the FGF19/FGFR4 signaling pathway. Our experimental results indicate that this antibody can effectively inhibit growth in HCC cells with high FGFR4 expression but shows less significant effects on those with low FGFR4 expression. This demonstrated that its blocking activity indeed inhibits cell growth in FGFR4-expressing cancer cell lines. We subsequently synthesized an ADC using this FGF19/FGFR4 signaling-blocking antibody conjugated with ferroptosis inducer sulfasalazine. Experimental results revealed significantly superior anti-tumor effects of ADC both in vitro and in vivo. Initial safety evaluations in mice showed that neither monoclonal nor ADC treatment alone produced significant side effects, suggesting their relative safety at this testing stage. If these encouraging results regarding efficacy and safety are successful in primate models and future clinical trial phases, drugs targeting FGFR4 may offer hope for patients battling HCC.

Limitations of the study

This study has certain limitations and requires further investigation. The efficacy and safety profile of FGFR4-ADC in patient-derived models, such as patient-derived xenografts and organoids, remains unvalidated, and as ex vivo and in vivo systems, more accurately summarizes the genetic heterogeneity and complex tumor microenvironment of primary human HCC. In addition, the potential synergistic anti-tumor effect of this FGFR4-ADC in combination with standard of care HCC therapies such as sorafenib is not clear, and clarifies whether such combination regimens can enhance treatment efficacy, overcome intrinsic or acquired resistance, or optimize therapeutic indices will yield valuable insights to inform its clinical transformation, and these two research areas will be prioritized in subsequent studies to further strengthen the evidence supporting FGFR4-ADC transformation potential.

Quantification and statistical analysis

To minimize estimation error and ensure sufficient power, we employed large sample sizes and multiple independent repetitions by separate researchers. We also conducted various experimental approaches including different quantification techniques for consistent and corroborative results. The sample size was determined based on well-documented guidelines in the literature and our extensive prior research experience. Data are shown as mean ± SD. Unpaired student t-tests were utilized to compare two group means. One-way ANOVA was used for comparisons among different groups, with post hoc testing using the LSD test when ANOVA showed significance. All data were processed through GraphPad Prism 6.0. A p value below 0.05 indicated statistical significance.
