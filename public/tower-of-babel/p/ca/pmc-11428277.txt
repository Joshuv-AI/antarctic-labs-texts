Assessing the mechanism of facilitated proton transport across GUVs trapped in a microfluidic device
Authors: Dominik Ruppelt, Elena LM Ackermann, Tom Robinson, Claudia Steinem
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProton transport across lipid membranes is one of the most fundamental reactions that make up living organisms. In vitro, however, the study of proton transport reactions can be very challenging due to limitations imposed by proton concentrations, compartment size, and unstirred layers as well as buffer exchange and buffer capacity. In this study, we have developed a proton permeation assay based on the microfluidic trapping of giant vesicles enclosing the pH-sensitive dye pyranine to address some of these challenges. Time-resolved fluorescence imaging upon a rapid pH shift enabled us to investigate the facilitated H+ permeation mediated by either a channel or a carrier. Specifically, we compared the proton transport rates as a function of different proton gradients of the channel gramicidin D and the proton carrier carbonyl cyanide-m-chlorophenyl hydrazone. Our results demonstrate the efficacy of the assay in monitoring proton transport reactions and distinguishing between a channel-like and a carrier-like mechanism. This groundbreaking result enabled us to elucidate the enigmatic mode of the proton permeation mechanism of the recently discovered natural fibupeptide lugdunin.

Significance

Proton permeation processes mediated by small molecules, peptides, or proteins are challenging to measure and usually rely on fluorescent pH indicators. In this study, we have developed a fluorescence microscopy-based proton permeation assay using giant vesicles trapped in a microfluidic device that enables rapid pH changes. This assay allows us to distinguish between carrier-mediated and channel-mediated transport processes and thus paves the way to understanding the mode of action of new compounds, as exemplified by the recently discovered naturally occurring fibupeptide lugdunin.

Introduction

Proton translocation across lipid membranes is one of the most prevalent reactions constituting life and plays pivotal roles in, e.g., mitochondrial ATP synthesis (1), photo reactions in chloroplasts (2), or secondary transport processes (3). Consequently, there is a great interest in quantitatively analyzing these transport processes and the involved biomolecules. In this context, in vitro experimental setups have become invaluable tools, as the biomolecules of interest, often peptides or proteins, can be investigated without interference from other, possibly active, cellular components. Owing to the low proton concentration at neutral pH, investigating proton transport processes is, however, challenging or even impossible using electrical recordings (4). Hence, fluorescent pH indicators are generally employed to measure changes in proton concentrations. For in vitro vesicle-based measurements, water-soluble pH-sensitive dyes were entrapped (5,6), membrane permeable dyes like acridine derivatives were used (7,8), or lipids that are fluorescently labeled at their headgroup were employed (9,10). For a comprehensive overview of these different pH-sensitive fluorophores, the reader is referred to a recent review (11). Bulk vesicle assays based on fluorescent pH indicators are generally accompanied by several experimental drawbacks: first, vesicle bulk assays do not allow observing proton permeation on a single-vesicle level, thus neglecting possible differences between individual vesicles (12). Second, a rapid pH change to establish the required pH gradient for passive proton permeation processes is not readily achieved, and third, an unstirred layer (USL) adjacent to the membrane can act as a barrier (13,14). USLs are regions of varying thicknesses that are constituted by a stagnant layer of water molecules with only limited convectional mass transport, even when the surrounding solution is stirred. The transport of solutes across USLs is realized solely by diffusion and imposes an additional barrier to transmembrane transport processes (14). These drawbacks call for alternative approaches. To observe proton translocation on a single-vesicle level, either large or giant vesicles have been employed. Large unilamellar vesicles (LUVs) were immobilized on support to image them individually as fluorescent spots and measure the proton pump activity of ATPases (15,16). Giant unilamellar vesicles (GUVs) have the advantage that the inner volume can be readily visualized under a light microscope while sedimented at the bottom of an observation chamber (17,18) or attached to a support (19). Another advantage of GUVs is the size of their inner volume. As the proton concentration at pH 7 is only 0.1 μm, an LUV with a diameter of 120 nm only contains 0.05 protons per vesicle on average. Thus, on the scale of LUVs, pH has to be viewed as the temporal average of proton concentration (14). As this is not the case for GUVs, a detailed analysis of the proton translocation can be gathered, however at the expense of easily accessible good statistics. Frese et al. (20) and Watanabe and co-workers (21,22) developed arrayed lipid bilayer systems to achieve a parallel readout scheme providing a better statistical analysis. By spreading lipids in an organic solvent, they obtained membranes that covered atto- or femtoliter-sized cavities in which pH-sensitive dyes were enclosed. With this device, Watanabe et al. (21) could measure the proton pump activity of an FOF1-ATPase. It is, however, conceivable that the function of the reconstituted protein is affected by the residual solvent in the lipid bilayer, which can alter, e.g., the membrane’s mechanical properties (23). Microfluidic devices are capable of trapping solvent-free GUVs (12,14,24,25,26). Of note, depending on the formation procedure, GUVs are also accompanied by certain drawbacks such as limitations concerning lipid and buffer composition (27,28,29). Yandrapalli et al. (24) reported on a microfluidic device allowing the collection of several tens of GUVs in individual micrometer-sized traps that were accessible by fluorescence microscopy. The GUVs were positioned close to the microscope to image them while simultaneously collecting the fluorescence information to obtain statistically significant data. Moreover, the microfluidic channels allowed for a constant and fast buffer exchange with a dead time of 10–20 s (24).

In this study, we exploited the potential of this microfluidic device. Based on the possibility of rapidly exchanging the buffer, we established a defined proton gradient while simultaneously recording fluorescence micrographs allowing the readout of transport rates. By using the channel peptide gramicidin D (30,31) as well as the protonophore carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (32), both textbook examples of biomolecules that facilitate proton permeation across membranes, we demonstrated that the assay enables one to distinguish between a channel-like transport and a carrier-like transport. This result paves the way to unravel the transport mechanism of newly discovered proton-translocating compounds, whose mechanisms are still unknown. Here, we showed the potential of the device by investigating the proton translocation mechanism of the recently discovered naturally occurring antimicrobial fibupeptide lugdunin (33,34). This cyclic heptapeptide is the first drug-like molecule found in bacteria of the human nose and has opened a new class of cyclopeptides harboring a unique thiazolidine moiety within the macrocycle, a feature that is invaluable for its antimicrobial activity. Recently, we proposed that lugdunin assembles into peptide nanotubes, facilitating the permeation of protons across lipid membranes following a channel-like mechanism (35). With the established microfluidic-based assay, we tested this hypothesis by evaluating the transport rates of lugdunin-mediated proton translocation.

Materials and Methods

Materials

1-Palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (POPC) was purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, USA). The fluorescently labeled lipid Texas Red 1,2-dihexadecanoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (TxR) and pyranine (8-hydroxypyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, USA). Glass slides coated with indium tin oxide (ITO) were from Präzisions Glas & Optik (Iserlohn, Germany). HEPES, KCl, and casein were obtained from Carl Roth (Karlsruhe, Germany). 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorododecyltrichlorsilane and gramicidin D were obtained from Merck (Taufkirchen, Germany). Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS, Sylgard 184) was obtained from Dow Chemicals (Midland, USA). Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) was from Fluka Chemie (Seelze, Germany). Lugdunin was synthesized and gratefully provided by the group of S. Grond (Eberhard-Karls Universität, Tübingen, Germany). All aqueous solutions were prepared in ultrapure water (MilliQ, Millipore, Burlington, USA).

Preparation of giant unilamellar vesicles

GUVs were prepared by electroformation in a salt-containing buffer following a procedure described by Lefrançois et al. (36). 0.04 mg lipid (POPC/TxR (99.6/0.4, n/n)) was dissolved in 70 μL chloroform and placed onto the conducting side of two ITO-coated glass slides. For measurements using gramicidin D, the peptide was added to the lipid solution in a peptide/lipid ratio of 1:750 (n/n) from an ethanol stock solution. The solvent was removed in vacuo for 2 h. The ITO slides were sealed with a Teflon spacer and fixed with paper clips. Vesicle buffer (100 mm KCl and 10 mm HEPES (pH 7.4)), supplemented with 0.5 mm pyranine, was filled into the formed chamber. The chamber was connected to an AC generator, and GUVs were produced using the settings given in Table 1. After electroformation, the GUV suspension was collected from the chamber.

Fabrication of microfluidic devices

A silicon wafer master mold was produced via soft photolithography as described previously (24). Briefly, silicon wafers with master structures with a height of 40 μm were obtained by UV exposure of the photoresist SU8-3050 through a film mask with a design comprising 10 individual devices for trapping GUVs (24). After fabrication, the wafer surface was incubated in a desiccator containing 100 mg 1H,1H,2H,2H-perfluorododecyltrichlorsilane overnight at 80°C to prevent unwanted polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) adhesion. To produce the microfluidic devices, 70 g PDMS base was mixed with 7 g curing agent and added on top of the silicon wafer. The mixture was degassed for 60 min until no air bubbles remained on the surface and then cured for 45 min at 80°C. The PDMS was separated from the wafer and cut into individual pieces. Inlets and outlets were added using a puncher with a 1.5 mm diameter (Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, USA). A cut from a 200-μL pipette tip served as a reservoir and was glued on top of the inlet with PDMS cured at 80°C for 30 min. One PDMS piece and a glass coverslip (24× 40 mm) were each cleaned with ultrapure water and ethanol. After treating both with oxygen plasma (2% relative power, 5 s, Zepto, Diener electronic, Ebhausen, Germany), the PDMS was fixed on the coverslip. Incubation of the device for 10 min at 80°C annealed the PDMS piece onto the coverslip surface.

Proton translocation assay

Before each measurement, the inside of the microfluidic device was passivated with a fresh casein solution (3 mg mL−1) via centrifugation for 10 min and subsequent incubation for 20 min. The use of gentle centrifugation was needed at this step to ensure that all the microfluidic channels were completely filled with aqueous solution and that no air remained in the system. The microfluidic device was then mounted on an inverted microscope (IXplore TIRF system, cellTIRF-4Line operated in epifluorescence mode (Olympus Deutschland, Hamburg, Germany) or FluoView1200 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and connected to a syringe pump (AL1000, World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, USA) in withdraw mode set to 8 μL min−1 to exchange the casein solution inside the device with vesicle buffer without pyranine. The channel of the microfluidic device was rinsed for 10 min. Afterward, the GUV suspension was filled into the reservoir, and GUVs were sucked through the channel. To remove extravesicular pyranine, the reservoir was washed three times with vesicle buffer before starting the flow again. The proton-transporting compounds were added to the lipids as follows.

(1)Experiments with gramicidin: gramicidin D was added to the lipid solution in a peptide/lipid ratio of 1:750 (n/n) from an ethanol stock solution to minimize the possibility of forming inactive structures due to the presence of inappropriate solvents (37) and deposited on the ITO slides before GUV formation (Fig. 1, left).

(2)Experiments with CCCP: electroformed GUVs were incubated in vesicle buffer with 1 μm CCCP and 10 μm valinomycin for 15 min (Fig. 1, middle).

Gramicidin-doped GUVs were directly exposed to a pH gradient, and proton influx (pHout<pHin) led to pyranine protonation and fluorescence quenching (left). For experiments with CCCP/valinomycin or lugdunin, the acidic vesicle buffer contained 1 μm CCCP and 10 μm valinomycin or 0.25 μm lugdunin, respectively, to prevent the formation of a concentration gradient between the membrane-inserted and noninserted compound. Proton transport was monitored in a time-dependent manner for 5 min recording a fluorescence micrograph every 0.5 s.

Data analysis

The mean fluorescence intensity of one GUV trap was extracted from the time series using ImageJ. The intensities were normalized to the maximum fluorescence intensity and then converted into pH values using a pyranine calibration curve. A linear regression was fit to the linear part of the time-dependent change in pH, and the rate of pH change ΔpH/Δt was extracted from the slope.

Results and Discussion

Chip design and GUV trapping

The microfluidic device used in this study (Fig. 2 A) consists of 204 individual traps that are distributed along 12 lines, allowing an efficient trapping of vesicles (24). These traps enabled us to exchange the buffer solution within a few seconds by applying an external suction (Fig. S1), which is a prerequisite to assess the kinetics of a facilitated proton translocation across the GUV membranes upon a defined pH shift. Furthermore, the fast perfusion of 8 mL min−1 reduces the size of the USL adjacent to the GUV membranes, minimizing their impeding influence on the transport process (14). For comparison, Li et al. determined the size of the USL of GUVs in a similar microfluidic T-junction system to be less than 1 μm internally and 5 μm externally (38). Fluorescence micrographs revealed that GUVs loaded with the pH-sensitive fluorescence dye pyranine (39) can be prepared and trapped successfully (Fig. 2 B) (26). Depending on the yield of GUVs during the electroformation process, it took around 30–60 min until the first trap in a row was half-filled with GUVs, which was sufficient for the following experiments. To analyze the success of GUV trapping, fluorescence micrographs were recorded after exchanging the extravesicular pyranine-containing solution with a pyranine-free vesicle buffer. In most cases, the first trap in each line was filled with a sufficient number of GUVs for the proton transport assay (Fig. 2 B1). Owing to the inclusion of side posts, also, the preceding traps down the line were filled with a sufficiently large number of GUVs, as shown in Fig. 2 B2. The mean number of GUVs in each trap that was used for the proton translocation experiments was determined to be 43±15 obtained from 81 individual traps. However, the occupancy can be increased by using a larger volume of GUV suspension and a longer trapping period.

Proton translocation facilitated by gramicidin and CCCP

The GUVs trapped in the microfluidic device with the enclosed pH-sensitive dye pyranine allow a rapid buffer exchange. To quantify how fast the solution exchange can be achieved, we determined the system’s dead time to be around 17 s, which is in line with previously reported dead times and which is sufficiently small to not interfere with the kinetics of the proton transport processes (24). To explore the potential of this setup, we chose two different proton-translocating compounds: 1) the channel gramicidin known to transport protons and small monovalent cations (31) and 2) carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP), known as a proton carrier (32). A pH gradient between the inner lumen of the GUVs (pH 7.4) and the more acidic outer solution was adjusted by the buffer exchange (Fig. 1). To ensure that the GUVs do not become proton permeable without a proton-translocating compound, we adjusted different pH gradients and took fluorescence micrographs of an individual GUV-filled trap in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 4 A, − gramicidin). In the absence of a proton transporter, no change in fluorescence intensity was observed up to a proton concentration gradient of Δc(H+)= 0.584 μmol L−1, demonstrating that the GUV membrane is proton impermeable and that no dye release occurs under these experimental conditions (Fig. 4 B, Video S1).

From the pH-time traces, we extracted the rate of pH change ΔpH/Δt from the slope as a measure for proton translocation activity. By varying the pH gradient between the inner lumen and outer solution, different proton concentration gradients were established, resulting in a linear dependency of the rate of pH change on Δc(H+) (Fig. 4 C). We interpret the rate of pH change as a proton transport rate according to Li et al. (42). They have shown in their study on the proton pump activity of Heme-copper oxidases that ΔpH/Δt is almost indistinguishable from Δn(H+)/Δt in the observed pH ranges and can therefore be used as a measure of proton translocation activity (42).

We pursued the same procedure for CCCP acting as a well-characterized proton carrier. As CCCP can only transport protons and no other monovalent ions, an antiport of cations needs to be induced to ensure charge equilibration. Thus, GUVs were incubated with CCCP and a high concentration of valinomycin, a carrier that shuttles potassium cations across membranes (43). The difference in the transport rate of CCCP and gramicidin as a function of increasing proton gradients is in good agreement with our expectations. Gramicidin acts as a cation-transporting channel. The channel is filled with a single-file water chain (30), and the transport of protons following the Grotthuss-type mechanism (45,46) is only limited by diffusion to the channel mouth. This is shown in measurements of the proton conductance at different pH values (47) and is confirmed by the observed linear dependence of the proton transport vs. the applied proton gradient. Compared to gramicidin, CCCP acts as a pure proton carrier. It needs to uptake a proton on one side of the membrane, diffuse through the membrane, and release the proton on the opposite side (32). This transport mechanism is limited by the number of accessible carriers in the membrane at large proton gradients. When all carriers are occupied, the proton transport rate cannot be further increased (44). A limitation of the transport rate given by the presence of the carrier valinomycin, which dissipates the charge imbalance by transporting potassium cations in the opposite direction, can be ruled out as an increase in valinomycin concentration does not increase the proton transport rates (Table S1). Although we observe a significant difference in the dependence of the proton transport rates on the applied proton gradient, the absolute values obtained for the peptide channel and the protonophore cannot be compared directly as the number of proton-transporting molecules in the membranes is different. However, we can safely conclude that the established proton translocation assay is capable of distinguishing between a channel-like behavior (gramicidin, linear dependency) and a carrier-like behavior (CCCP, saturation curve).

Proton translocation facilitated by lugdunin

Our results show that the microfluidic approach allows us to distinguish between a channel-like and a carrier-like proton translocation mechanism. This assay thus puts us in the position to gain valuable insights into the mode of action of novel proton transporters. In 2019, we reported on a cyclo-heptapeptide, called lugdunin, which is composed of alternating d,l-amino acids, and a unique thiazolidine heterocycle (Fig. 5 A) and which has been shown to transport protons in an in vitro assay (33,34). Lugdunin is produced by the nasal and skin commensal Staphylococcus lugdunensis and displays potent antimicrobial activity against a broad spectrum of Gram-positive bacteria, including challenging-to-treat methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (33). Lugdunin specifically inhibits target bacteria by dissipating their membrane potential (34). The enantiomeric compound of lugdunin is as potent as wild-type lugdunin, suggesting that a specific protein interaction at the bacterial membrane is not required for the peptide’s activity. As also no large pores induced by the peptide are formed in the membrane (34), which is often found for other antimicrobial peptides (48), it is assumed that the dissipation of the membrane potential is mainly a result of proton permeation induced by the peptide. Recently, we proposed a model of lugdunin’s mode of action based on the spontaneous formation of peptide nanotubes, spanning through a lipid membrane and facilitating the translocation of ions according to a channel-like mechanism (Fig. 5 B) (35).

Based on the established microfluidic approach, we were able to analyze the lugdunin-mediated proton translocation kinetics and to prove this hypothesis. We trapped the pyranine-filled GUVs in the microfluidic device and incubated them with a lugdunin solution for 5 min, allowing the peptide to partition into the membrane phase (35). No change in pyranine fluorescence during this time was observed, indicating no dye release and supporting the notion that lugdunin does not form large pores (34). Upon changing the outer solution pH to a more acidic one, a change in fluorescence intensity was recorded, indicating the transport of protons along the proton gradient. The proton transport rates as a function of Δc(H+) clearly follow a linear behavior (Fig. 5 C). This linear dependence was also observed for the channel gramicidin indicating that lugdunin acts as a proton channel rather than a carrier (Fig. 5 B). This finding provides a pivotal piece of information for our understanding of how the newly discovered fibupeptide acts on membranes and provides paramount evidence for our current model of lugdunin activity (35). Moreover, the results demonstrate the general applicability and the potential of the established microfluidic assay to unravel unknown proton transport mechanisms.

Conclusion

Over the last decades, vesicle-based transport studies have provided a wealth of information about the passive transport of ions. Among these ions, protons are particularly challenging to measure as they pass through a lipid bilayer with exceptionally high rates, whereas their bulk concentration is very low. To investigate ion permeation processes across membranes, GUVs appear to be particularly suited. Individual GUVs can be visualized under a light microscope, enabling one to measure the ion permeation with high precision. By combining GUVs trapped in a microfluidic device with fluorescence microscopy, we have been able to study the mode of proton permeation of different membrane-active compounds. This experimental setup enables one to investigate unresolved proton permeation processes across membranes mediated by membrane-active compounds such as the naturally occurring fibupeptide lugdunin as shown in this study or other synthetic cyclic peptides. It is, however, not only limited to proton permeation but has also the potential to screen synthetic ion transporters that are generally derived by a combinatorial synthesis approach. Ion transporters, e.g., for chloride or calcium ions, can be readily investigated based on Cl−- and Ca2+-sensitive fluorophores that are commercially available.
