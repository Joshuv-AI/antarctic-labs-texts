An erythrocyte membrane-fused plant-derived nanoparticles as a gene therapy vehicle for the treatment of CI/R injury
Authors: Shiyi Li, Anni Wang, Ru Zhang, Miaomiao Zhang, Pengcheng Guo, Bixue Chen, Yangke Yuan, He Wang, Jianxin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIschemic stroke is currently the second leading cause of death worldwide, and insufficient endogenous neurogenesis is the greatest cause of post-stroke disability. MicroRNAs have been proven to hold therapeutic potential, unfortunately, they have a low stability that hinders their clinical usage. Our earlier work revealed that Panax notoginseng derived exosome like nanoparticles, namely PDNs have potential to bypass BBB and reduce the cerebral ischemia/reperfusion (CI/R) damage. In this study, we employed microRNA-124 as a model therapeutic gene, utilizing its engineered variant Agomir-124 (Ago124) to optimize loading efficiency. The therapeutic effects of Ago124@R-PDN were further assessed in several sets of experiments. Pharmacokinetic study showed that erythrocyte membrane extended the half-life of PDNs from 7 min to 11.3 h, and the loading efficiency of Ago124 reached 40 %. In an in vitro oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) model, Ago124@R-PDN enhanced IL-10 production in microglia by 67 % (vs 11.7 % with free Ago124), and promoted Tuj1+ neuronal differentiation by 2.23-fold compared with vehicle. Also, Ago124@R-PDN brought gene cargo into the brain, alleviated infarct volume, and improved functional behaviors in model mice. At last, we demonstrated that surface glycosyl of PDN facilitated its brain-entering ability by being recognized by sodium-glucose linked transporter-1 protein. In conclusion, our erythrocyte fused PDNs offer a promising strategy for delivering biomacromolecule to treat brain diseases.Keywords: Microrna-124, Ischemic stroke, Plant-derived exosome like nanoparticles, Gene therapy, Erythrocyte membrane

Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally, with approximately 12 million new cases annually [1]. Ischemic stroke accounts for about 80 % of the stroke incidents, features with multiple pathological processes which involve activation of microglia and loss of neural cells. Remarkably, loss of neural cells can lead to long-term disability, which significantly lowers the patients’ quality of living. After stroke onset, endogenous neural stem cells go through neurogenesis to offset the damage, but is insufficient and results in irreversible loss of neurons. Thus, enhancing this intrinsic regenerative mechanism represents a potentially transformative therapeutic strategy for stroke recovery.

Numerous therapeutic candidates, including cytokines, small molecules, and nucleic acids, have been explored for stroke treatment. Among these, microRNAs (miRNAs) are particularly promising due to their ability to regulate multiple genes within a pathway simultaneously, accelerating rapid therapeutic development [2]. MicroRNA-124 (miR-124), the most abundant miRNA in the central nervous system (CNS), has been shown to play crucial roles in both neuroprotection and neurogenesis through its regulation of key targets including Sox9, REST, and PU.1 [3,4].

Experimental evidence highlights miR-124′s multifaceted neurogenic effects: Li et al. revealed that miR-124-mediated suppression of Sox9 is critical for neuronal differentiation of subventricular zone (SVZ) stem cells [3]. Thorsten et al. further established that sustained miR-124 administration over eight weeks promoted post-stroke neurogenesis by facilitating USP14-dependent degradation of the RE1-silencing transcription factor (REST) [4]. Additionally, miR-124 overexpression has been shown to modulate microglial activity by directly targeting CCAAT/enhancer binding protein alpha (C/EBP-α), and downregulated the critical myeloid transcription factor PU.1 [5]. Together, these findings established miR-124 as an effective regulator of multiple pathophysiological processes in cerebral ischemia.

Despite their therapeutic potential, miRNAs face significant pharmacokinetic challenges. For example, miRNAs are notorious for rapid enzymatic degradation and systemic clearance before reaching target cell cytoplasm. For stroke therapy, miRNAs are also compounded by the hurdle of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). These dual challenges underscore the critical need for developing sophisticated drug delivery systems (DDS) capable of both protecting miRNA integrity and facilitating brain penetration. In the past decade, several carriers were developed for miRNA delivery, i.e., lipid nanoparticles (LNP), lentiviral, cationic liposomes and polymeric delivery systems [6]. Although artificial nanoparticles can be easily modified according to therapy needs and produced in large-scale [6], they usually require further modification with ligands for brain delivery, which often leads to high cost. And some nanoparticles such as LNP and polymeric delivery system often have low gene delivery efficiency in vivo [6]. More importantly, artificial nanoparticles often have safety concerns.

Exosomes have emerged as a novel class of biological nanocarriers for miRNA delivery. These naturally occurring, heterogeneous membrane vesicles (30–150 nm in diameter) play a pivotal role in intercellular communication by facilitating the transport of various biomolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and miRNAs [7]. However, mammalian cell derived exosomes are reported with some disadvantages, such as low yield and toxicity concern [6]. In contrast, plant-derived exosome-like nanoparticles (PDEN) offer distinct advantages as alternative delivery vehicles. These nanovesicles can be isolated from abundant plant sources with significantly higher yields. And their edible nature ensures superior biocompatibility and minimal toxicity [[8], [9], [10]]. In our earlier work, we have demonstrated that Panax ginseng-derived nanoparticles (PDNs) could simultaneously enter the brain parenchyma and reduce infarct volume by altering microglia polarization [11]. These characteristics establish PDNs as an optimal delivery platform that not only enables brain-targeted miRNA transport but also exhibits synergistic therapeutic effects with miR-124 for ischemic stroke treatment.

A major challenge in exosome-based therapeutics, however, is their rapid clearance from systemic circulation, with a plasma half-life typically <10 min following intravenous administration [12]. For this concern, red blood cell membrane (RBCM) can be fused with PDNs to prolong its half-life while maintaining its brain targeting property. Previous studies have validated this approach, showing that it significantly prolongs nanoparticle circulation time (extending half-life to >12 h) while maintaining targeting specificity, through mechanisms including CD47-mediated “self” recognition and reduced macrophage uptake [[13], [14], [15]].

An effective loading method is crucial for achieving high encapsulation and transfection efficiency of miRNA. Current techniques for loading nucleic acids into extracellular vesicles primarily involve electroporation, donor cell modification [16] and modification of exosome with lipophilic agents and cationic polymers [17]. Electroporation requires high voltage pulses and may harm the intactness and bioactivity of exosomes, and in our context, modification of origin cells is not applicable since we directly isolate PDNs from fresh root of Panax notoginseng. Hence, we selected Agomir, a cholesterol-modified miRNA mimic [18], as our cargo. The 3′-end cholesterol modification enables spontaneous membrane penetration [19], allowing efficient loading via mild sonication that preserves vesicle structure while achieving high loading efficiency.

In this study, we first engineered hybrid vesicles by fusing PDNs with erythrocyte membranes through extrusion. Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) analysis confirmed successful membrane fusion. Pharmacokinetic evaluation revealed that PDNs, like mammalian cell-derived exosomes, exhibit a short plasma half-life (7 min), which RBCM fusion extended to 11.3 h. Next, we loaded Ago124 into the hybrid vesicle by sonicating, and loading efficiency reached 40 %, demonstrating that Agomir structure was ideal for loading miRNA into natural vesicles. The therapeutic efficacy of Ago124@R-PDN was systematically evaluated across multiple experimental systems: (1) In an oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) in vitro model, the nanoparticle enhanced IL-10 production in microglia by 67.0 % (versus 11.7 % with free Ago124) and promoted a 2.23-fold increase in Tuj1+ neuronal differentiation; (2) Pharmacokinetic study confirmed the extended circulation time (from 7 min to 11.3 h); (3) In a transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO) mouse model, intravenous administration of Ago124@R-PDN reduced infarct volume to 15 % compared to 28 % with PDN alone and improved cognitive function. Mechanistic studies revealed PDN's glycosyl groups mediate BBB penetration via SGLT-1 binding, confirmed by both surface plasmon resonance assays and in vivo biodistribution studies.

Material and methods

Materials

C57 mice (20–25g) were purchased from Shanghai Sipper BK Laboratory Animals Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All mice were raised in the animal holding unit in Fudan University and were provided with free access to food and water under controlled conditions (12 h/12 h light/dark cycle with humidity of 60 % ± 5 %, and a temperature of 22 ± 3 °C). All animals were treated according to the Guide for the Care and Laboratory Animals and all experiments were approved and performed according to the guidelines of the Ethics Committee of Fudan University (Ethical approval number: 2020-05-WJX-01). Reagents including 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindodicarbocyanine perchlorate (DiD), 1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′-tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide (DiR) were purchased from Dalian Meilun biotechnology Co. LTD. (Dalian, China). Agomir and Trizol® were purchased from GenePharma Co. Antibodies for Nestin, Discorticoid (Dcx), GFAP, Tuj1 and Brdu were purchased from Abclonal Co. (Wuhan, China). Fresh Panax notoginseng root wassourced from local market in Yunnan Province, China.

Isolation of PDNs

PDNs were isolated from Panax notoginseng roots using an optimized sucrose density gradient ultracentrifugation protocol, as previously established [11]. The extraction process was performed as follows: fresh roots were thoroughly washed with distilled water and homogenized in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4) using a pre-chilled blender. The resulting slurry was sequentially centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 20 min (4 °C) to remove plant debris. The supernatant was then centrifuged at a speed of 10,000 × g for 1 h (4 °C) to eliminate larger organelles. The clarified supernatant was then subjected to differential ultracentrifugation for vesicle isolation. First, the supernatant was layered onto a discontinuous sucrose gradient (27 %/68 %, w/v in PBS) and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1.5 h (4 °C) using a swing-bucket rotor. The band at the 27 %/68 % interface, containing crude PDNs, was carefully collected using a sterile syringe. For further purification, the crude PDN fraction was loaded onto a refined discontinuous sucrose gradient (8 %/30 %/45 %/60 %, w/v) and centrifuged at 200,000 × g for 1.5 h (4 °C). The purified PDNs, concentrated at the 30 %/45 % sucrose interface, were collected and lyophilized for further use. All centrifugation steps were performed using polycarbonate tubes to minimize vesicle loss.

Isolation of mouse erythrocyte membrane

Mouse erythrocyte membrane was isolated as previous described with modifications [20]. Whole blood was collected from male C57 mice (8w) eyes and washed with disodium EDTA (1M), and then placed at −80 °C overnight. At the second day, the sample was lysed with 0.2 mM ETDA in PBS at 4 °C, and then centrifuged at 14,000 g for 8 min. The supernatant was discarded and the hemolysis process was repeated thrice. The resulting RBCM was collected and dissolved in PBS.

Preparation and characterization of PDN and RBCM hybrid nanovesicles

Hybrid nanovesicles were prepared by extruding a mixture of PDNs and RBCMs through a series of polycarbonate membranes (800, 400, 200 and 100 nm pore sizes) using a mini-extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids). To optimize the formulation, PDN-to-RBCM ratios (based on protein weight) were tested at 1:4, 1:2, 1:1, 2:1 and 4:1. The 1:1 ratio demonstrated minimal macrophage uptake in RAW264.7 cells after 2 h incubation with DiD-labeled vesicles and was selected for subsequent studies. The resulting R-PDNs were characterized by dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano, Malvern Instruments) to determine hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential.

Protein profiles of PDNs, RBCMs, and R-PDNs were analyzed by SDS-PAGE. Briefly, vesicles were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime) and protein concentrations quantified using the Bradford assay (Yeasen Biotechnology). Equal protein amounts (20 µg/lane) were separated on 12.5 % polyacrylamide gels and visualized with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 staining.

Ago124 was loaded into R-PDNs using a probe sonicator (100 W, 1 s pulse on/off cycles for 80 times total) at 4 °C to maintain vesicle integrity. Unencapsulated Ago124 was removed by centrifugal filtration (8,000 ×g, 10min) through 50 kDa MWCO filters (Amicon Ultra, Millipore). The final formulation, designated Ago124@R-PDN, was stored in PBS at −20 °C. A negative control (NC@R-PDN) was prepared similarly using scrambled agomir.

Proteomic analysis

The proteomic data analysis of the hybrid vesicle was performed by Shanghai Luming biological technology co., LTD (Shanghai, China). Each sample was separated by an Ultimate 3000 RSLCnano (ThermoFisher Scientific) and absorbed to a trap column of 100 µm × 20 mm (RP-C18, Agilent). Then, the sample went through an analysis column of 75 µm × 150 mm (RP-C18, New Objective, USA) at a flow of 300 nl/min. After separation, the sample was analyzed by a Q-Exactive plus mass spectrometry (MS) (Thermo Scientific).

The scanning range of parent ions was corrected by standard correction fluid at m/z, and the scanning mode of MS was Data Dependent Acquisition (DDA). The 20 strongest fragment profiles (MS2 Scan) were collected after each scan. Fragmentation was performed using high-energy collision dissociation (HCD, high energy) with an NCE energy of 28 and dynamic removal time of 25 s. The resolution of MS1 was 70,000 at m/z 200, the AGC target was 3e6, and the maximum injection was 100 ms. The resolution of MS2 was 15,000. The AGC target was Ie5, and the maximum injection time was 50 ms. MS/MS spectra were searched using the ProteomeDiscover 2.5 software against the Uniprot-Rattus norvegicus_UP000002494 database. Search database specific parameters were set as follows: Fixed modifications: Carbamidomethyl (C); Dynamic modification: Deamidated (NQ), Oxidation (M) and Acetyl (Protein N-term); MS1 tolerance: 10 ppm; MS2 tolerance: 0.05 Da; Missed cleavage:2.

Cell culture

Primary NSCs were isolated from fetal C57 mice at embryonic d 14. Briefly, fetal mice were extracted from deeply anesthetized mothers and immediately placed in ice-cold PBS. The brains were carefully dissected, followed by removal of the cerebellum and olfactory bulb. The remaining brain tissue was mechanically dissociated into single cells and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 2 % B27 (17504044, Gibco), 20 ng/ml FGF2 (450–33, PeproTech, USA), 20 ng/ml EGF (315–09, PeproTech), and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5 % CO₂ atmosphere and passaged every 3–5 days For expansion, NSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/cm² in T75 flasks.

Primary microglia isolation followed established protocols [21]. Briefly, the cerebral cortex was dissected from neonatal mice (<24 h old) and minced into small fragments (∼1 mm²). Tissue digestion was performed using 8 U/ml papain (Sangon, China) and 125 U/ml DNase (Meilunbio, China) for 20 min at 37 °C to obtain a single-cell suspension. The suspension was filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 5 min. The pelleted cells were resuspended and plated onto poly-L-lysine (PLL, 0.1 mg/ml)-coated T75 flasks. Upon reaching 100 % confluency (typically 5 d), microglia were harvested by gently shaking the flasks at 200 rpm for 4 h at 37 °C.

Oxygen/glucose-deprived cell model

For proliferation and differentiation assay, primary NSCs were seeded onto the coverslip coated with poly-D-lysine (25 ug/ml) and cultured with DMEM/F12 supplemented with 2 % FBS and 1 % penicillin-strptomycin, which is referred as differentiation media, and allowed to attach overnight. Then the cells were pre-incubated with PDN, Ago124, Ago124@PDN, Negative control (NC@R-PDN), Ago124@R-PDN (0.36 µg/ml Ago124) for 2 h. Later, the medium was changed to Earle’s balanced salt solution (EBSS) and cells were incubated in a hypoxia chamber at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2, 1 % O2 and 94 % N2 gas environment for 4 h. Afterwards, the cells were transferred to normal condition and the media was changed into differentiation media supplemented with drugs. The cells were analyzed by immunofluorescence staining for proliferation and differentiation 2 or 7 d later, respectively. The media was changed every other day.

For primary microglia, cells were seeded on PLL coated plate and allowed to adhere for 24 h and then pre-incubated with PDN, Ago124, Ago124@PDN, Negative control (NC@R-PDN), Ago124@R-PDN (0.36 µg/ml Ago124) for 2 h. After pre-treatment, the medium was changed into glucose-free DMEM and transferred to a hypoxia chamber at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2, 1 % O2 and 94 % N2 gas environment for 2 h. After normoxic recovery in high-glucose DMEM/10 % FBS, supernatants were collected at 72 h for ELISA analysis of IL-6 and IL-10 (Boster Biological Technology, China) per manufacturer's protocol.

ELISA measurement

The concentration of cytokine IL-6 and IL-10 in ischemic brain tissue or cell culture supernatant was measured with ELISA kit (Boster Biological Technology, China) according to manufacturer’s instruction.

BrdU incorporation cell proliferation measurement

NSCs were pulse-labeled with 5‑bromo‑2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) (10 µM, MCE) for 24 h prior to assay termination (48h post-reperfusion). Cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by three washes with tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 5 min each. Non-specific binding was blocked with 3 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min at 37 °C. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C using anti-BrdU (1:100 dilution, Abclonal, China). The next day, samples were washed three times with TBS and incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (Abclonal, China) for 1 h at 37 °C. After final TBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) using Fluoroshield Mounting Medium. BrdU-positive cells were visualized and imaged using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (Olympus, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

Seven days post-reperfusion, NSC differentiation was assessed by immunocytochemistry. Cells were sequentially washed with TBST (once) and TBS (twice), 5 min per wash, followed by fixation with 4 % PFA for 10 min at 4 °C. After three additional TBS washes (5 min each), nonspecific binding was blocked with 3 % BSA for 30 min at 37 °C. Primary antibody incubation was performed overnight at 4 °C using anti-Tuj1 and anti-GFAP antibodies (Abclonal, China).

The following day, cells were washed three times with TBS and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C with species-matched secondary antibodies: Cy3-conjugated goat anti-mouse (1:200 dilution, Abclonal, China) and FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:200 dilution, Yeasen, China). After final TBS washes, nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, China) using Fluoroshield Mounting Medium. Fluorescence images were acquired using a spinning-disk confocal microscope (Olympus, Germany).

Surface plasmon resonance

To characterize the molecular interaction between PDNs and SGLT-1, SPR experiments were conducted using a Biacore system (Cytiva, USA). The Series S CM5 sensor chip (carboxylated dextran matrix, Cytiva, USA) was prepared by immobilizing recombinant SGLT-1 protein (5 µg/ml in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 4.0) via standard amine-coupling chemistry. The chip surface was first activated for 7 min with a 1:1 mixture of 0.4 M EDC and 0.1 M NHS, followed by SGLT-1 protein injection (5 µg/ml in 10mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 4.0; 120 s contact time) and subsequent deactivation with 1 M ethanolamine-HCl (pH 8.5).

Binding kinetics were analyzed by injecting serially diluted PDNs (0.125–4 mg/ml in HBS-EP buffer: 10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.05 % [v/v] surfactant P20, pH 7.4) were injected over the functionalized surface at 30 µl/min (association: 180 s; dissociation: 300 s). Between runs, the chip was regenerated with 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.0). All experiments were performed at 25 °C with reference subtraction from blank flow cells.

Establishment of mice tMCAO model

C57BL/6 mice were selected for this study due to their well-characterized response to transient middle cerebral artery occlusion (tMCAO), despite potential differences in blood-brain barrier properties compared to humans. Mice tMCAO model was established according to previously reported [11]. Briefly, anesthesia was induced with 4 % isoflurane (70 % N₂O/30 % O₂) and maintained at 1.5 %−2 %, with core temperature stabilized at 37 ± 0.5 °C. Following midline neck incision and carotid bifurcation exposure, the external carotid artery (ECA) was ligated and cauterized. A filament was introduced into the internal cerebral artery (ICA) to occlude the blood flow for an hour before removal for reperfusion. The sham group was subjected to the same procedure except for the insertion and withdrawal of the filament.

Seven experimental groups (n = 6–8/group) were randomly assigned: sham+PBS; tMCAO+PBS; tMCAO+PDN (8 µg/mouse); tMCAO+free Ago124 (8 µg/mouse); tMCAO+Ago124@PDN (8 µg Ago124 equivalent); tMCAO+NC@R-PDN (8 µg scrambled miRNA equivalent); and tMCAO+Ago124@R-PDN. Different preparation was administrated via tail vein at a dose of 8 µg Ago124 per mouse 1 d post stroke onset and administrated for consecutive 7 d.

Mice brain RT-qPCR

Brains were collected from model mice established in 2.1.12 1 d post administration. Total RNA was isolated from 100 mg brain tissue using the TRIeasy™ (Yeason, China) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Then, the stem-loop method was used to synthesize the first-strand cDNA of miR-124 using miRNA First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Sangon, China) according to its instruction. Later, the relative expression levels of miR-124 of each group were detected via RT-qPCR according to the instructions of MicroRNAs qPCR Kit (Sangon, China). The reaction conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 3 min, 94 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 40 s for a total of 45 cycles. As for the expression of target mRNAs of Ago124, namely, E2F1, Stat3 and TCF12, first-strand cDNAs were synthesized using the cDNA Sythesis Kit (Yeason, China) according to its instruction. Finally, the relative expression of E2F1, Stat3 and TCF12 were detected via RT-qPCR according to the instructions of RT-qPCR Kit (Yeason, China). In all RT-qPCR experiments, β-actin was selected as a control, the relative expression of each mRNA was calculated by the 2-△△Ct method. All primers used in this section were listed in Table S2.

Magnetic resonance imaging

In vivo magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed using an 11.7 T scanner with mice under isoflurane anesthesia (5 % induction, 1.5 % maintenance). T2-weighted sequences were acquired with TE=26 ms, TR=3 s, and 0.3 mm slice thickness.

Modified neurological severity score

The modified neurological severity score (mNSS) was used to evaluate the functional impairments, as previously described [22]. The mNSS contains motor, sensory and reflex tests and was graded on a scale of 0–18. The higher score represents worse functional impairments. Tests were carried out by two observers blinded to the experiments on Day 2, 3 and 8 post stroke onset.

Morris water maze

The Morris water maze task was carried out to evaluate spatial memory performance as previously described [23]. The mice were trained to find a hidden, transparent platform (10 cm diameter) placed in a water maze 100 cm in diameter. The training was carried out 21–25 d post ischemia, and each mouse was trained 4 times per day by placing them in different quadrant of the water maze. During each trial, the mice were allowed to look for the platform for 1 min. If the mice were able to find it within 1 min, the time it used was recorded as escape latency. If not, the mice were guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 15 s and escape latency was recorded as 60 s. On Day 26, the platform was removed for a 120-s probe trial, during which platform crossings were quantified.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of Ago124@R-PDN

To optimize the R-PDN formulation, hybrid nanovesicles were prepared by extruding PDNs with RBCMs at varying protein concentration ratios. Following DiD labeling and 12 h incubation with RAW cells, flow cytometry (FACS) and fluorescence imaging (Fig. 1B&1C) revealed an inverse correlation between RBCM content and macrophage uptake, which plateaued at a 1:1 PDN:RBCM ratio. FRET analysis with DiI (donor)-labeled PDNs and DiD (acceptor)-labeled RBCMs confirmed successful membrane fusion, evidenced by a 35.6 % reduction in emission at 670 nm (Fig. 1D). SDS-PAGE and proteomic profiling demonstrated that R-PDNs retained 81.25 % of PDN proteins and 48.87 % of RBCM proteins, including the immune-evasive marker CD47 (Fig. 1E–1G). The cholesterol-modified Ago124 exhibited ∼40 % loading efficiency, facilitated by its enhanced membrane integration capacity (Fig. 1H).

Ago124 inhibited OGD-induced microglia inflammation

As the resident immune cells of the CNS, microglia demonstrate heightened sensitivity to pathological changes during ischemic stroke. Their excessive activation worsens neuronal damage through the sustained release of inflammatory mediators and cytotoxic substances [24,25], making microglial polarization a validated therapeutic target for cerebral ischemia. Primary microglia isolated from neonatal mice showed >95 % purity (Fig. 2A). Cellular uptake studies revealed efficient internalization of Ago124@R-PDN within 12 h (Fig. 2B), with 55 % of vesicles escaping lysosomal degradation by 24 h. This enhanced endosomal escape likely results from the cholesterol modification, which disrupts lysosomal membrane integrity [26].

(scale bar: 50 µm); (B) Fluorescence images of microglia internalizing DiD-labeled Ago124@R-PDN after 12 h (scale bar: 5 µm); (C) FACs images and (D)quantitative analysis of microglia after OGD interruption (mean ± SD, n = 3); (E) IL-10 and (F) IL-6 concentration in the microglia supernatant in the OGD model (mean ± SD, n = 3). *P < 0.05, ⁎⁎P < 0.01, ⁎⁎⁎P < 0.001.

Flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 2C and 2D) demonstrated that oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) promoted microglial polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Both Ago124 and PDN treatment increased CD206+ anti-inflammatory M2 microglia populations. Cytokine profiling (Fig. 2E and 2F) showed that OGD elevated IL-6 while suppressing IL-10 production. Notably, the combined Ago124@R-PDN system showed markedly greater IL-10 induction (67.0 % ± 5.6 %) versus other groups (Ago124: 11.7 % ± 1.3 %, PDN: 9.8 % ± 1.5 %; P < 0.01 for both comparisons), confirming their cooperative anti-inflammatory action. The confidence intervals for other groups were 12.8 % ± 0.6 % (control), 8.6 % ± 1.2 % (OGD/R), 12.1 % ± 0.9 % (Ago124@PDN) and 9.3 % ± 3.0 % (NC@R-PDN), respectively.

These findings demonstrated that Ago124@R-PDN effectively modulated microglial polarization and suppressed neuroinflammation in the OGD/R model, highlighting its therapeutic potential for ischemic stroke.

Ago124@R-PDN induced neurogenesis of neural stem cells post OGD

While endogenous NSCs initiate neurogenesis after ischemic stroke, this repair mechanism remains insufficient for functional recovery. To evaluate the therapeutic potential of Ago124@R-PDN, we conducted systematic in vitro neurogenesis assays. Primary NSCs isolated from fetal mice demonstrated >95 % purity (Fig. S2). Cellular uptake studies revealed that Ago124@R-PDN was efficiently internalized and escaped lysosomes within 48 h in NSCs (Fig. 3A), mirroring our observations in microglia. According to the BrdU staining, proliferation of NSC was inhibited in the OGD/R model (Fig 3B), and BrdU+ NSCs increased by 1.89-fold with Ago124@R-PDN vs. vehicle. By d7 post OGD interruption, Tuj1+ neurons increased by 2.23-fold with Ago124@R-PDN compared to control (Fig. 3D&3E). These results conclusively demonstrate that Ago124@R-PDN significantly enhances both NSC proliferation and neuronal differentiation following OGD/R disruption in vitro.

Pharmacokinetic and biodistribution in vivo

The pharmacokinetic properties and biodistribution of RBCM-modified PDNs were systematically evaluated following intravenous administration of DiD-labeled nanoparticles in mice. RBCM fusion remarkably extended the circulation half-life of PDNs from 0.1 ± 0.007 h to 11.0 ± 3.8 h by reducing RES-mediated clearance, as shown in Fig. 4A and 4B. This enhanced pharmacokinetic profile translated to improved brain delivery in tMCAO model mice, with R-PDNs demonstrating 3.1-fold higher brain accumulation and reduced lung sequestration compared to unmodified PDNs (Fig. 4E&4F). Quantitative analysis revealed that Ago124@R-PDN achieved 3.2-fold greater delivery of Ago124 to brain tissue compared to Ago124@PDN (Fig. S3A), accompanied by significant downregulation of miR-124 target mRNAs including E2F1, Stat3 and TCF12 (Fig. S3B-S3D).

Biodistribution of R-PDN in vivo. In order to elucidate the mechanism of how PDN and R-PDN cross the BBB, an in vitro BBB model was established using b.END3 cells. PDN and R-PDN was labeled with DiD, and added to the upper side of the Transwell and incubated for 1 h. As showed in Fig. 4J, around 3 % PDN and R-PDN crossed the cell monolayer in 1 h. According to previous report, glycosyl side chain on the surface of exosome determines its biodistribution [27], and hexose transporters are reported to distribute on the surface of BBB [28,29]. Hence, we hypothesized the transportation of PDN and R-PDN across BBB were related to hexose transporters. As a proof-of-concept, phloridzin, a sodium-glucose linked transporter (SGLT) non-selective inhibitor, could inhibit the transportation. However, quercetin didn’t show similar effect (Fig. 4I). We further evaluated the role of SGLT-1 and SGLT-2 using selective inhibitors, and found that SGLT-1 was responsible for the transportation of PDN and R-PDN (Fig. 4J). The essential role of glycosyl moieties was further demonstrated by PNGase F treatment, which completely abolished BBB penetration capability (Fig. 4K). SPR analysis provided direct evidence of concentration-dependent PDN-SGLT1 binding, with this interaction being significantly downregulated following PNGase F pretreatment (Fig. 4L&4M). In vivo validation studies confirmed that glycan-depleted nanoparticles showed markedly reduced brain accumulation (Fig. S4), collectively establishing that PDNs exploit SGLT-1-mediated transcytosis through their surface glycosyl groups for efficient BBB penetration. It is important to note the absence of the concentration of glycosyl side chains in this study, hence the Kd value for the binding between PDN and SGLT1 could not be determined.

Therapeutic benefits of Ago124@R-PDN in tMCAO mice

We systematically evaluated the therapeutic potential of intravenously administered Ago124@R-PDN in the tMCAO mouse model. According to the MRI results, PDN and Ago124 exerted synergetic effect in decreasing infarct volume. When fabricated into Ago124@R-PDN, infarct volume was ameliorated to 15 % (Fig. 5A). Behavioral assessments demonstrated significant functional recovery, as evidenced by mNSS scores decreasing from 12 to 3 by 8-d post-treatment (Fig. 5B). ELISA measurement demonstrated that PDN could inhibit the microglia-induced inflammation, the Ago124@R-PDN combination yielded significantly enhanced anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 5C), underscoring their therapeutic complementarity. The long-term neurological benefits were further evaluated using the Morris water maze test. During the 5d training period, Ago124@R-PDN-treated mice exhibited consistently shorter latencies to locate the hidden platform (Fig. 5F). Probe testing on day 6 revealed a 70 % reduction in escape latency and increased platform crossings (Fig. 5E&5G), indicating preserved spatial memory.

Immunohistochemical analysis revealed the treatment's impact on post-stroke neurogenesis. At 7 d post-tMCAO, we observed increased Nestin+ neural stem cells in the SVZ of Ago124@R-PDN-treated mice (Fig. 5H and 5I). By 28 d, more ki67+/MAP2+ newborn neurons were detected in peri‑infarct regions (Fig. 5J&5K), confirming enhanced neurogenesis. These multimodal analyses confirmed that R-PDN served as an effective delivery platform for Ago124 while exhibiting superior pharmacokinetic properties relative to unmodified PDN.

Biosafety of Ago124@R-PDN in vivo

Biosafety of Ago124@R-PDN was evaluated. Mice were intravenously injected with Ago124@R-PDN and major organs were collected. According to the HE staining, no changes in liver, kidneys, spleen, heart and lungs histology were observed by 8 µg/mouse dose (Fig. S5). Hence, Ago124@R-PDN has the potential to be translated from bench to bedside.

Conclusion

In summary, for the first time, we developed an erythrocyte-fused PDEN for targeted therapy of cerebral ischemic stroke. Erythrocyte membrane prolonged the circulation of PDN and enhanced its accumulation in the brain. Meanwhile, the results demonstrated that glycan side chains on PDNs’ surface were essential for its brain targeting ability, which can be recognized by SGLT-1 on BBB and the binding ability between PDN and SGLT-1. We chose miR-124 as a model gene drug for CI/R therapy. In this study, Ago124 was chosen for the cholesterol tail allowed its effective loading into the vesicle. The hybrid vesicle, R-PDN, could efficiently carry Ago124 and deliver the gene to the lesion area, thus significantly inhibit microglia-induced inflammation, induce neurogenesis and improve learning and memory skill of CI/R mice. In conclusion, the present study provides not only a safe and effective platform for the treatment of CI/R, but also a targeting delivery system of genes into the brain, which can be utilized for the prevention and therapy of various brain disorders.

Compared with other gene delivery systems, our platform features with easy preparation, high targeting efficiency and good safety profile. For example, LNP is a kind of most commonly used gene drug vehicle, which has good endosomal escape ability, high encapsulation efficiency (70 %−80 %) and low toxicity [30]. However, only <1 % LNP of injected dose can achieve the brain and mostly accumulated in the liver (>80 %). In addition, targeting ligands such as transferrin [31], lactoferrin [32] and etc. were conjugated to the surface of LNPs to achieve brain penetration. The conjugation process was costly and involves usage of organic reagents, which were difficult to remove and may affect the biomacromolecules. In contrast, our platform utilized the inherent brain-targeting ability of PDN and no organic reagents was involved in the preparation process. Viral vector, particularly adeno-associated viruses (AAVs), are another type of gene delivery system to brain and known for its efficient gene transfer, long-term transgene expression and scalable manufacture [33]. However, AAVs can only carry small transgenes (<4.7 kb) [34] and high doses can cause liver toxicity or immune response, as seen in non-human primate studies [35]. Comparatively, no safety issue was observed in our research, and further studies should be done to clarify its safety profile under high dose therapy.
