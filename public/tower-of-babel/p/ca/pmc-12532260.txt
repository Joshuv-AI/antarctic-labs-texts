Multiorgan Assessment with Transesophageal Ultrasound in Prone Ventilation Patients with Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome: An Observational Study in Argentina
Authors: Martín Isa, Fernando A Sosa, Lucía Bertorello-Andrade, Javier E Roberti, Juana Fernández, Barbara Tort, Federico Daldoss, Pablo Merlo, Hatem Soliman-Aboumarie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsIn critically ill patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, prolonged prone positioning complicated conventional monitoring. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) was often unfeasible, highlighting the need for alternative methods. Transesophageal ultrasound (TEUS) allows bedside, multiorgan assessment but has been underutilized in prone patients. To evaluate the feasibility and clinical utility of TEUS for multisystem monitoring in patients with moderate to severe ARDS in the prone position.Patients and methodsThis retrospective observational study was conducted in two ICUs in Argentina from October 2020 to October 2022. Adult patients with COVID-19-related ARDS who underwent TEUS while ventilated in the prone position were included. Certified intensivists performed standardized TEUS protocols to assess cardiac function, pulmonary status, and venous congestion using the venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) score.ResultsTransesophageal ultrasound was performed in 109 patients. Assessment of left and right ventricular function, diastolic parameters, and lung patterns was feasible in nearly all cases. Right ventricular dysfunction was present in 36.7%, and VExUS-related congestion markers (portal pulsatility, hepatic/intrarenal vein changes) were frequent. Lung ultrasound via TEUS identified interstitial-alveolar patterns in 98.2%. Transesophageal ultrasound findings guided fluid and ventilatory management.ConclusionTransesophageal ultrasound is a feasible, safe, and clinically valuable tool for comprehensive, real-time multisystem monitoring in prone, mechanically ventilated ARDS patients. It enables informed decision-making without patient repositioning, especially when other imaging is limited. Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is characterized by severe and sustained impairment in gas exchange. It may be associated with hemodynamic instability, multiorgan dysfunction, and the need for extended prone position ventilation.1 Although prone positioning improves oxygenation, it poses significant challenges for patient monitoring due to the dynamic progression of the disease.2 During the COVID-19 pandemic, a large number of patients with ARDS required multiple cycles of prone ventilation, often exceeding the recommended 12 hours.3 This situation revealed the limitations of conventional monitoring methods, such as transthoracic and transabdominal ultrasound, which are often impractical because of the limited access to ventral regions and patient instability. In this context, TEUS emerged as a key tool for assessing prone patients.4

This minimally invasive modality allows comprehensive imaging of the cardiovascular, respiratory, and gastrointestinal systems through a single access route, with adequate patient tolerance and a low rate of complications. Transesophageal ultrasound is especially valuable in critically ill patients, enabling real-time hemodynamic monitoring, even in the prone position.4,5 In a cohort of patients with severe COVID-19-related ARDS, TEUS was successfully performed without the need to modify ventilator settings or hemodynamic support.4 The procedure was well tolerated, with a mean duration of 20 minutes and minimal complications, primarily nasogastric tube displacement. Importantly, TEUS enabled the identification of right ventricular dysfunction and acute cor pulmonale in many patients, directly informing therapeutic decisions related to fluid management, vasopressor use, and ventilator adjustments.4 This underscores TEUS's role not only in cardiac assessment but as a comprehensive tool that contributes to individualized, multisystem care in high-risk ICU patients.4

Although several studies have explored the use of TEUS in ARDS patients in the prone position, most have focused primarily on cardiac function, with limited attention to its broader diagnostic utility. One study demonstrated the feasibility of prone TEUS and its value in assessing left and right ventricular function during severe ARDS. Still, it was restricted to cardiac imaging and hemodynamic evaluation without addressing extrapulmonary applications.5 Similarly, a review emphasized the role of TEUS in evaluating acute cor pulmonale and volume status in prone patients, noting technical advantages over transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), but again limited its scope to cardiovascular parameters.6 During the COVID-19 pandemic, Teran et al.7 supported the use of TEUS in prone ventilated patients as a safe and effective modality for real-time cardiac monitoring; however, their multicenter report focused solely on hemodynamic instability and cardiac complications, without integrating TEUS into broader multisystem strategies. While such studies demonstrate the safety and feasibility of prone TEUS for cardiovascular assessment, they do not explore its potential for evaluating respiratory, renal, and hepatic systems, highlighting a gap that this study aims to address. Here, we describe our clinical experience using TEUS for multiorgan and hemodynamic assessment in critically ill patients with moderate to severe ARDS in the prone position, exploring its utility as an integrated monitoring tool in this complex scenario. The primary objective was to assess the feasibility of TEUS for multiorgan evaluation in prone ventilated ARDS patients; the secondary objective was to describe the frequency of abnormal findings.

Patients and Methods

We conducted a retrospective observational study in two multidisciplinary ICUs in Argentina: Clínica Regional del Sud in Río Cuarto, Córdoba, and Hospital Alemán in Buenos Aires. Together, these units included 44 medical-surgical ICU beds. The study period was October 2020 to October 2022. Transesophageal ultrasound was conducted as part of routine diagnostic workup in critically ill patients and was not performed exclusively for research. The study remained observational, and no protocol-driven interventions were introduced.

We included adult patients (≥18 years) consecutively admitted to the ICU with a diagnosis of ARDS according to the Berlin definition,1 secondary to COVID-19 infection, who underwent TEUS while receiving invasive mechanical ventilation in the prone position. Patients in the upright position were excluded to ensure consistency in evaluating the feasibility of TEUS in the prone position. We also excluded patients in the prone position with contraindications for TEUS, such as upper digestive bleeding and esophageal diseases like tumors, stenosis, or perforations.

Ultrasound assessments were performed using a Mindray M9 system with a P7-3Ts transesophageal probe and a Philips CX50 (Koninklijke Philips N.V., The Netherlands) with a 5 MHz multiplanar transducer.

All TEUS studies were performed by intensivists certified in critical care echocardiography (MI and FAS). Echocardiographic views and parameters followed the 2016 American Society of Echocardiography guidelines (https://www.asecho.org/guidelines). Patients were in the swimmer's position during the examination. The ultrasound system was positioned on the left side of the patient's bed. A bite block was used to protect the patient's teeth and the probe. The probe was inserted via the oral cavity after lubrication with xylocaine, with slight neck hyperextension to facilitate insertion.

For cardiac assessment, the probe was advanced to a deep transgastric position (~50 cm from the dental arch), fully anteflexed to obtain a five-chamber apical view. The left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral (LVOT VTI) was measured to estimate stroke volume. The probe was then rotated clockwise to assess right heart structures, including tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) and systolic tissue Doppler velocity (S’ wave). The probe was then withdrawn approximately 5 cm to obtain a short-axis view at the level of the papillary muscles and qualitatively assess left ventricular systolic function.

Using an apical four-chamber view, diastolic function was assessed using E/A and E/e’ ratios at the mid-esophageal level. In a bicaval view, the collapsibility index of the superior vena cava was measured to evaluate preload status. From this position, clockwise probe rotation with the multiplane angle set to 0° allowed hepatic visualization and identification of vascular structures, including the inferior vena cava, where the distensibility index was measured. The portal and hepatic veins were visualized with the multiplane angle adjusted to 30°–40°.

The left renal vein was assessed from the transgastric short-axis view at the level of the papillary muscles with counterclockwise probe rotation. The right renal vein was assessed by advancing the probe 7–10 cm from the deep transgastric view and rotating it clockwise. Color and pulsed Doppler were used to evaluate morphology and flow patterns. Renal venous flow was assessed using a transgastric short-axis view, as previously described.8 These measurements were used to calculate the venous excess ultrasound (VExUS) score. Significantly, VExUS scoring was not affected by patient positioning, as transesophageal imaging provided optimal alignment with hepatic, portal, and renal veins in both prone and supine positions. There is a technical description of the proper way of doing these measurements.9 Regarding your question, to our knowledge, there are no studies on performing VExUS in the prone position; however, there are physiological studies that demonstrate no hemodynamic changes in this position.10

Lung ultrasound was performed with TEUS at three esophageal levels (upper, middle, and lower), using anatomical landmarks: the left subclavian artery, the left superior pulmonary vein, and the origin of the inferior vena cava, respectively. Once identified, clockwise and counterclockwise probe rotation allowed evaluation of pulmonary parenchyma in both hemithoraces, characterizing lung patterns (A-lines, B-lines, consolidation, and pleural sliding).8,11–14 Appropriate infection control precautions were taken before all examinations, following institutional protocols to minimize healthcare worker exposure to SARS-CoV-2. Transesophageal ultrasound was repeated in 40 patients to assess the progression of previously diagnosed acute cor pulmonale.

The following variables were collected: age, sex, comorbidities, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation (APACHE) II score, sequential organ failure assessment (SOFA) score at admission, admission diagnosis, PaO2/FiO2 ratio, mortality, use of vasopressors and inotropes, mechanical ventilation parameters, left and right ventricular systolic function, LVOT VTI, E/e’ ratio, superior vena cava collapsibility index, lung ultrasound pattern, and flow patterns of the hepatic, portal, and intrarenal veins.

Data were analyzed using MedCalc version 20.011. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on data distribution. Normality was evaluated through visual methods using histograms and analytically via the Shapiro–Wilk test. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA version 15 (College Station, TX, USA).

The Instituto Médico de Río Cuarto, Córdoba Ethics Committee reviewed and approved the study protocol, No. 2025-0010, dated June 9, 2025. The committee waived the requirement for informed consent, as all data were retrospectively collected, anonymized, and derived from routine clinical practice without additional interventions beyond standard care.

Results

Between October 2020 and October 2022, 109 patients receiving invasive mechanical ventilation in the prone position were evaluated using TEUS. The total of 149 TEUS was performed. Table 1 shows demographic and clinical characteristics. The mean age was 59.4 years, and most patients (80.7%) were male. The most frequent comorbidities were hypertension (51.4%), obesity (40.4%), diabetes (20.2%), and smoking history (9.2%). The predominant admission diagnosis was COVID-19 pneumonia (98.2%), and the median SOFA at admission was 5 (IQR 4–7). The median PaO2/FiO2 ratio was 140, with a median PEEP of 12 cm H2O. Sixty-six patients (60.6%) required vasopressors, and 3 (2.8%) required inotropes. The median APACHE II score was 17.5 (IQR 14–20), and the overall in-hospital mortality was 42.2%.

Left ventricular (LV) systolic function was preserved in 56% of patients (Table 3). These measurements provided insight into the degree of pulmonary involvement and venous congestion, particularly in patients with right ventricular (RV) overload, and enabled calculation of the VExUS score. Lung ultrasound revealed interstitial-alveolar syndrome in 98.2% of patients, consolidation in 38.5%, and pleural effusion in 2.8%. Regarding the VExUS protocol, mildly abnormal hepatic vein flow (D wave > S wave) was observed in 18.3% of patients, and S-wave inversion was observed in 1.8%. Pulsatility of the portal vein flow >30% was found in 32.1% of patients, and impaired intrarenal venous flow in 25.7%. These findings guided individualized fluid management and PEEP adjustments to unload the right ventricle.

Discussion

This observational study demonstrates the feasibility and clinical utility of TEUS for multiorgan assessment in patients with moderate to severe ARDS due to COVID-19 while in the prone position. We show that TEUS enabled systematic hemodynamic, pulmonary, and venous congestion evaluations, which supported bedside clinical decision-making when standard imaging modalities were often unfeasible.

Echocardiography is a fundamental tool in managing patients with severe respiratory distress, routinely used to assess cardiac function, volume status, and signs of acute cor pulmonale.6 In patients with severe ARDS, prone positioning is frequently used to improve oxygenation through better ventilation-perfusion matching.3 However, this position poses technical limitations for TTE due to restricted acoustic windows and patient instability. As a result, TEUS has been recommended in this setting, although few studies have systematically explored its use during prone ventilation.4,5,7

In our cohort, prone TEUS allowed for real-time, bedside evaluation of multiple organ systems under pandemic conditions that restricted the use of conventional diagnostic imaging. Through TEUS, we identified a high prevalence of right ventricular dysfunction (36.7%) and acute cor pulmonale (RV/LV ratio >0.6 in 27.5% of cases), findings consistent with the pathophysiology of COVID-19-related ARDS and its impact on the pulmonary circulation. Lung ultrasound patterns such as interstitial-alveolar syndrome (98.2%) and consolidation (38.5%) were also detected through esophageal views, reinforcing the value of TEUS for pulmonary evaluation in this setting.

Similar studies showed comparable results.14 In a cohort of 140 patients with moderate to severe COVID-19 ARDS, 9% showed left ventricular systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction <45%; 39% exhibited right ventricular dysfunction, of whom 14% had acute cor pulmonale.14 Their global ICU mortality was 29%.14 Importantly, TEUS enabled assessment of systemic venous congestion using the VExUS protocol. Portal vein pulsatility, hepatic and intrarenal venous Doppler findings contributed to fluid management and ventilatory adjustments, particularly in patients with right ventricular overload, as Koratala and Hatem Soliman described.15 These insights would not have been possible through TTE or clinical examination alone in the prone position. Although surface ultrasound can be used to assess specific organ systems, TEUS enabled a comprehensive multiorgan evaluation within a single procedure, without the need to reposition the patient or change transducers, an advantage particularly relevant in unstable or severely hypoxemic patients.16

The use of TEUS in closed units has traditionally focused on cardiac assessment, but its potential for multisystem evaluation has also been recognised. Transesophageal ultrasound allows the assessment of other organs, such as the liver and kidneys, without the need to change probes or reposition the patient, overcoming many limitations of transthoracic imaging. Early studies demonstrated its utility in evaluating hepatic venous flow to assess right ventricular function in cardiac surgery settings.17 More recently, VExUS scoring using TEUS has been reported, and renal resistive indices obtained via TEUS have shown good correlation with translumbar ultrasound.18 A technique for direct renal flow visualization using TEUS has also been described.19 Furthermore, TEUS has proven useful in evaluating hypoxic patients.9

The COVID-19 pandemic saw an unprecedented increase in the use of prone ventilation in critically ill patients. Global mortality from COVID-related ARDS was significant,13,19,20 with a large proportion of patients admitted to the ICU and who required mechanical ventilation. The inability to perform adequate transthoracic imaging in prone patients, because of positioning, lack of acoustic windows, or clinical instability, underscored the need for alternative, multimodal monitoring approaches. Transesophageal ultrasound met this need, offering a safe and effective solution for serial assessment without requiring patient transport.

Teran et al.7 reported that TEUS could be safely performed during the COVID-19 pandemic with appropriate infection control measures, without increasing operator risk. Arntfield et al.21 validated the use of TEUS by intensivists, showing excellent agreement with cardiologist-performed studies. Mayo et al.22 showed that intensivists could easily adopt TEUS, given the technique's similarity to TTE and the utility of mastering probe manipulation.18 Cavayas et al.8 introduced the transesophageal lung ultrasound (TELU) concept, emphasizing its potential to identify the etiology of acute hypoxemia and supporting our approach to pulmonary evaluation. An observational study showed that the frequent indications for TEUS studies were hemodynamic instability (45.2%), evaluation of infective endocarditis (22.2%), and cardiac arrest (20.1%).11 Observational data supported intensivist-performed TEUS as safe and therapeutically impactful across diverse indications in ICUs.21 However, more studies exploring its utility are still needed, especially in the Latin American population. Studies conducted in India showed the widespread use of prone positioning for COVID-19 ARDS, indicating its role in improving oxygenation and reducing intubation rates.23,24 However, challenges such as resource limitations and the need for specialized prone teams underline the importance of safe, efficient monitoring.25

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective and observational design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between TEUS findings and clinical outcomes. Second, the study was conducted in two tertiary centers with intensivists trained in critical care ultrasound, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings with less TEUS expertise or access. Third, although image acquisition was successful in nearly all patients, interoperator variability in image interpretation and measurement was not assessed, which could impact reproducibility. Additionally, while we report that TEUS contributed to clinical decision-making, we did not systematically measure how TEUS-guided interventions altered patient outcomes such as length of stay, ventilator-free days, survival, or how they led to specific clinical interventions, which limits our ability to quantify its direct impact on management decisions. Lastly, the study focused on patients with COVID-19-related ARDS. While the findings may be relevant to other causes of ARDS requiring prone positioning, further studies are needed to confirm their applicability. To our knowledge, this is one of the first studies to describe the comprehensive use of TEUS for multiorgan monitoring in prone, critically ill patients with COVID-19-associated ARDS.

Clinical Significance

This study shows the clinical relevance of TEUS as an effective and practical bedside tool for evaluating critically ill patients with moderate to severe ARDS in the prone position. Transesophageal ultrasound allows for real-time assessment without requiring patient repositioning, where traditional imaging techniques are often impractical because of positioning constraints or hemodynamic instability. The ability to evaluate cardiac function, pulmonary pathology, and venous congestion in a single session facilitated adjustments to fluid and ventilatory management. The frequent detection of right ventricular dysfunction and venous congestion demonstrates the pathophysiological burden in these patients and supports TEUS-guided therapeutic strategies. These findings are pertinent in resource-constrained or pandemic settings where rapid, integrated, and non-transport-based monitoring is essential.

Conclusion

Multisystem assessment using transesophageal echocardiography in prone-positioned patients is feasible, safe, and reproducible. In critically ill patients with ARDS, TEUS enables real-time evaluation of cardiac, pulmonary, and hemodynamic status at the bedside, supporting appropriate interventions. Its integration into routine ICU practice may improve the management and prognosis of patients requiring prone ventilation. Transesophageal ultrasound has the potential to help diagnose and change decision-making, improving critical care.
