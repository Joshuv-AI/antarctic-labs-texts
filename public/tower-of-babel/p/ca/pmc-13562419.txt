Monitoring the 2023 Omicron waves in Morocco: Trends in incidence, variants, and testing disparities
Authors: El Kamouni Youssef, Almaggoussi Rim, Arsalane Lamiae, Merabet Moad, El Youbi Mohammed, Zouhair Said
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Regional disparities in testing and the need for continued surveillance.•Insight into the national impact of Omicron variants within a global context.•The effectiveness of vaccination in reducing coronavirus disease 2019 severity and mortality in Morocco during 2023.•Strengthening surveillance and health care systems is crucial for early detection and rapid response to future variants.Keywords: Omicron, COVID-19, Epidemiology, Morocco, Vaccination

Introduction

In Morocco, the first case of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) was recorded on March 2, 2020. A total of 1,279,447 confirmed cases were registered by November 20, 2024, with 16,310 reported deaths across the country [1]. Since the first documented cases of COVID-19 in China, the pandemic has gone through several phases marked by successive waves of transmission that overwhelmed health care systems worldwide, resulting in high mortality rates. Prompt global actions were taken to control the spread and reduce mortality, and the development of vaccines significantly lowered both transmission and death rates [2]. Over 4 billion vaccine doses were administered in 2023 [3], though access remained unequal in many regions. In Morocco, numerous vaccination campaigns were conducted; however, the emergence of new variants such as Omicron posed new challenges requiring adaptations to vaccination and public health strategies.

This study aims to examine the evolution of the COVID-19 pandemic in Morocco during 2023, a year marked by active vaccination efforts and the emergence of new variants, by providing an in-depth understanding of its epidemiological and evolutionary aspects.

Methods

Ethical considerations

Professional confidentiality was strictly maintained in accordance with the applicable regulatory provisions, particularly Law No. 28-13 on the protection of individuals involved in biomedical research [4]. The study was conducted without any direct interaction with individuals, making informed consent unnecessary. Anonymity and confidentiality were ensured for all personal data collected.

Study population and setting

This was a retrospective, descriptive study conducted at the national level. The study period ran from January 1 to December 31, 2023, covering a full year.

All patients with confirmed severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection during this period were included.

The inclusion criteria included all individuals with a positive COVID-19 diagnosis confirmed by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or rapid antigen test and detected by the national disease surveillance system, encompassing various demographic groups and regions. Patients without evidence of SARS-CoV-2 infection were excluded.

Case definition

Symptomatic case: all cases presenting with fever and one or more symptoms related to COVID-19 infection (shortness of breath, fatigue, muscle aches, headache, sore throat, congestion, or loss of taste or smell) and with a COVID-19 diagnosis confirmed by RT-PCR or rapid antigen test through the national disease surveillance system

Asymptomatic case: all cases with no evident symptoms of COVID-19 infection that had a positive routine COVID-19 test (RT-PCR or rapid antigen test) and were detected by the national disease surveillance system.

Severe case: all confirmed COVID-19 cases requiring hospitalization in the intensive care unit and detected by the national disease surveillance system.

Data collection

Data were collected from the national database of the National Public Health Emergency Operations Center of the Ministry of Health and Social Protection (Centre National d'Opérations d'Urgence de Santé Publique du Ministère de la Santé et de la Protection Sociale).

Collected data included age, gender, geographic location, number of COVID-19 cases per week, positivity rate, incidence, distribution of cases by region, number of severe cases, mortality rate, vaccination status, RT-PCR and rapid antigen test results, and the type of COVID-19 variant detected.

Study analysis

Data analysis involved calculating measures of central tendency and dispersion for quantitative variables and proportions for qualitative variables. The collected data were entered and analyzed using Microsoft Word and Excel 2019.

Results

Demographic characteristics of COVID-19 cases in Morocco

The median age of COVID-19 patients in Morocco in 2023 was 48 years. The male-to-female sex ratio was 1, indicating an almost equal distribution, with men accounting for 49.6% of cases and women for 50.4%.

Vaccination status was analyzed only for patients older than 18 years of age: 42% had received three doses, 30% were unvaccinated, 2% had received a fourth dose, and 1% received a single dose.

Geographical distribution

Distribution of cases by region

Of the 6674 cases recorded in Morocco in 2023, the Rabat-Salé-Kénitra region reported the highest number, with 2573 cases, whereas the Dakhla-Oued Ed-dahab region recorded the lowest, with 35 cases.

Distribution of tests by region

The distribution of COVID-19 testing varied significantly across regions. Out of the 218,745 tests conducted nationwide in 2023, the Rabat-Salé-Kénitra region performed the highest number, with 84,911 tests, whereas the Dakhla-Oued Ed-dahab region conducted the fewest, with 1441 tests (Table 1).

Furthermore, the positivity rate showed relative uniformity across regions, with a national average of 3%.

Weekly distribution and predominant variant

Morocco recorded a total of 6674 confirmed COVID-19 cases in 2023, with two peaks during the year (Figure 2). The first peak occurred from April 8 to June 2, lasting 8 weeks and accounting for 2168 cases. The second peak spanned from August 12 to October 13, lasting 9 weeks, with approximately 1762 cases.

These two waves corresponded to the circulation of Omicron subvariants—XBB.1.5 predominated during the first wave, while XBB.1.9 and EG.5.1 were dominant during the second.

The annual incidence was approximately 18 cases per 100,000 inhabitants. The overall positivity rate was 14.2% during the first peak and 6.1% during the second.

Evolutionary aspects

Demographic characteristics and weekly distribution of severe COVID-19 cases

In 2023, a total of 53 severe cases of COVID-19 were recorded among the 6674 confirmed cases, representing 0.8% of all cases.

The median age of patients with severe COVID-19 was 67.7 years, with ages ranging from 30 to 88 years. The majority of patients were men, accounting for 62% of cases, whereas women represented 38%.

Regarding vaccination status, 46% had received three doses, 23% had received two doses, 16% had received a fourth dose, and 15% were completely unvaccinated.

Mortality and lethality

A total of six deaths were recorded in Morocco in 2023. The country reported a mortality rate of 0.02 per 100,000 inhabitants and a case-fatality rate of 0.09% among confirmed COVID-19 cases.

Discussion

Nationwide

Our study revealed a high concentration of COVID-19 cases in specific regions. Rabat-Salé-Kénitra (38.55%) and Greater Casablanca-Settat (24.41%) recorded the highest numbers of cases, indicating a significant clustering of the epidemic in these areas. In contrast, Dakhla-Oued Ed-dahab (0.52%) and Guelmim-Oued Noun (0.76%) were among the least affected regions.

This disparity could be attributed to higher population density and a greater volume of testing in the more impacted areas, which facilitated increased case detection. The number of tests conducted and the level of screening coverage varied considerably between regions, highlighting inequalities in Morocco’s COVID-19 detection efforts.

The Rabat-Salé-Kénitra and Greater Casablanca-Settat regions exhibited the highest screening coverage and number of tests performed, representing the likelihood that these two regions have better health care systems and resources. Similarly, while Tangier-Tetouan-Al Hoceima had a larger population, they had lower screening coverage and fewer tests. These findings reflect limited testing capacity and show the need to increase testing capacity to improve detection in disadvantaged populations.

The differences in test coverage certainly had a clear impact on case detection. Regions with the most testing identified more cases, while regions with limited testing may not have captured the real number of cases. Overall, the difference in the positivity rate did not vary widely throughout the country, with the national average positivity rate being 3.1%. This suggests a steady nationwide prevalence rate regardless of differences in testing coverage.

Internationally

Our study offers an overview of the epidemiological and evolutionary aspects of the COVID-19 wave that impacted Morocco in 2023. A total of 218,745 PCR tests were performed during that year, with 6674 positive cases, resulting in a positivity rate of 3.1%. This positivity rate is comparable to that reported in studies conducted during the same period in France [5]. The similarities between Morocco and France could be due to historical relations between the two countries, such as human mobility, trade, and similar public health policies.

In other international studies, varying positivity rates have been reported. The United States [6] experienced a positivity rates of 5.4%, which is relatively close to that of Morocco, while Taiwan [7] and Canada [8] had higher positivity rates of 9% and 14%, respectively. The reason for this disparity across countries could be explained by several contributing factors, primarily differences in screening policies, pandemic management approaches, and geographical differences.

Regarding the national testing coverage, Morocco performed 218,745 tests in 2023, yielding a national coverage of 591 screening tests per 100,000 inhabitants. This remained considerably lower than the national coverage in other countries, such as France [9], which reported 82,406 tests per 100,000, and the United States [10], which reported 1512 tests per 100,000. The aforementioned differences may be explained by the fact that the United States and France likely have more public health resources, allowing greater testing capacity. Other factors include population density, the availability and accessibility of other health care infrastructure, and national policies regarding the emphasis placed on testing and the location of testing sites.

SARS-CoV-2 variants have evolved through a series of mutations, each are associated with different waves of the epidemic. The principal variants include Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta. Omicron rapidly became the dominant variant after its detection because of its extremely high transmissibility. The varied appearances of the Omicron subvariants are largely due to genetic recombination [11,12]. This mechanism allows Omicron to incorporate mutations from various viral lineages, enhancing both its transmissibility and its ability to evade immune responses.

The spike protein plays a central role in this process, particularly the receptor-binding domain, which enables viral attachment to the angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 receptor on human cells. Mutations in the receptor-binding domain, including N501Y, Q498R, and T478K, increase its affinity for angiotensin-converting enzyme 2, facilitating infection and rapid spread. More than 30 mutations have been identified in the spike protein, many of which reduce the effectiveness of neutralizing antibodies and contribute to immune escape.

Our study found that among the 6,674 confirmed COVID-19 cases in Morocco, there was a predominance of Omicron subvariants, with Omicron 4 accounting for 32.48% of cases and Omicron 5 for 26.40%. Differences in the distribution of Omicron subvariants can be attributed to local screening policies, region-specific epidemiological dynamics, and international mobility. The similarity between Morocco and France—both showing a relatively balanced distribution of Omicron 4 and Omicron 5—supports the hypothesis that trade and close international ties influence variant circulation patterns.

The mean age of 48 years observed in our study falls within the intermediate range when compared with other studies: 41 years in Ivory Coast [17], 38 years in India [18], 56 years in the United States, and 75 years in France. These differences in average age can be explained by variations in population age structure, socioeconomic conditions, and national strategies for screening and health surveillance. In particular, the older average age observed in France may be linked to a longer life expectancy, resulting in a higher proportion of elderly individuals.

The distribution of cases between men and women in Morocco was equal, with a sex ratio of 1. This ratio varied internationally: countries such as France and Ivory Coast reported similar gender distributions, while others, such as South Korea and the United States, had a higher male-to-female ratio (1.27), and India reported a lower ratio (0.92).

Greater exposure of men to the virus may be related to occupational or social factors, while better health care access for women may influence testing rates. The observed gender balance in Morocco, France, and Ivory Coast may reflect equitable screening practices or balanced gender roles in public life.

In terms of vaccination status, the majority of COVID-19 cases in Morocco occurred in vaccinated individuals: 1.39% had received one dose, 24.79% had received two doses, 44% had received three or more doses, and 29.82% remained unvaccinated. This distribution indicates a relatively high level of vaccination coverage among confirmed cases.

Differences in vaccination distribution are due to varying levels of vaccination coverage and vaccination policies: In South Korea, the high proportion of people who had received three or more doses, together with a low rate of unvaccinated people, indicates high vaccination coverage and good adherence to booster doses. In the United States and France, higher rates of unvaccinated individuals indicate less homogeneous coverage and less adherence to additional doses. In Morocco, on the other hand, the proportion of unvaccinated people is higher (29.82%), and the proportions of individuals who had received three or more doses are lower than those observed in the previously cited countries. These variations highlight the impact of local vaccination campaigns, public health policies, and cultural differences on vaccine acceptance.

Two major epidemic waves were recorded. The first, corresponding to the sixth wave, lasted 8 weeks, from April 8 to June 2, and totaled 2168 cases, with a positivity rate of 14.2%. The second, the seventh wave, lasted 9 weeks, from August 12 to October 13, and totaled 1762 cases, with a positivity rate of 6.1%. The annual incidence in Morocco was 18 cases per 100,000 inhabitants.

In other studies, mainly those conducted in France [19] and the United States, the first peak ran from January to March/February, with incidences of 1278 case per 100,000 inhabitants and 133 case per 100,000 inhabitants, respectively. In New Zealand, the first peak occurred in January. As for the second peak, the United States experienced a second peak from August to September, New Zealand experienced during June, and France did not report a second peak in 2023.

The distribution of COVID-19 peaks between Morocco and other countries shows both similarities and differences influenced by the spread of variants: The presence of two peaks in Morocco, as in the USA and New Zealand, could be explained by the global circulation of variants. The relatively low incidence in Morocco (18/100,000) could be influenced by population density, limiting transmission compared with countries such as New Zealand. Peak variations could also result from local public health strategies, such as screening or booster vaccination programs. The reported incidence could also have been underestimated because of limited testing capacity and underreporting in low-testing regions.

Our study revealed a total of 53 severe cases of COVID-19 among the 6674 confirmed cases, representing a severity rate of 0.8%, which is relatively low compared with other countries, such as India [20] (20.33%), Peru [21] (50.00%), and China [22] (64.97%).

These differences in the rates of severe forms of COVID-19 could be explained by differences in the capacity of health care systems to deal with the disease, which varies from one region to another and affects the management and outcomes of severe cases.

Epidemiological differences, such as herd immunity rates, virus variants present, and vaccination rates, may also play an important role in these variations in severity rates.

We analyzed the personal characteristics of severe COVID-19 cases. Data from our study indicated that severe cases of COVID-19 shared two main characteristics: advanced age and the presence of comorbidities. Morocco recorded a total of six deaths linked to COVID-19, with weekly variation marked by no deaths in most weeks and a slight increase at the end of the year. The mortality rate was calculated at 0.02 per 100,000 inhabitants, while the case-fatality rate was 0.09%, which is a low rate compared with those reported in other studies, as shown by the World Health Organization data.

These differences can be attributed to factors such as (1) differences in the population at risk: variations may be influenced by the proportion of vulnerable populations, such as the elderly or those with comorbidities; (2) hospital management capabilities: the quality of health care infrastructures and the availability of resources influence the management of severe cases, and thus have an impact on mortality rates; (3) epidemiological particularities of each region: transmission levels, variant prevalence and population density directly influence the intensity and severity of cases.

The lower rates in Morocco suggest effective management of the pandemic and lower severity of cases in 2023.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, its retrospective and descriptive design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between epidemiological variables and the observed outcomes. Second, the reliance on data from the national surveillance database may be subject to underreporting and reporting delays, particularly in regions with limited testing capacity, which could lead to an underestimation of the true incidence of COVID-19. In addition, disparities in testing coverage across regions may have introduced detection bias, thereby affecting the comparability of regional data. The use of aggregated data also prevented more detailed analyses of individual risk factors, such as comorbidities or socioeconomic status. Finally, the study did not account for potential variations in diagnostic practices or changes in testing policies over time, which may have influenced the observed trends in incidence and positivity rates.

Recommendations

In light of the results and analyses of our study, several recommendations can be made to strengthen epidemiological management and improve the response to future waves of infection: (1) strengthening testing coverage in regions with low-testing rates to detect cases early and limit spread; (2) increasing access to vaccines and booster doses for at-risk populations, particularly the elderly and those with comorbidities, in order to maintain good vaccination coverage; (3) improving surveillance of variants through regular genetic analysis; (4) maintaining awareness campaigns; (5) establishing an early warning system based on key epidemiological indicators, such as monitoring trends in hospitalizations and consultations for respiratory symptoms, to enable a rapid response in the event of a resurgence of cases; (6) strengthening collaboration with international surveillance systems to better anticipate threats from new variants and adapting local strategies to global trends.

Conclusions

Our study of the epidemiological evolution of COVID-19 in Morocco in 2023 highlights a mixed picture, marked by a significant reduction in severity and mortality but also by persistent challenges in certain regions. Morocco, like other countries, experienced two waves of infection associated with Omicron subvariants, but with relatively low incidence and mortality rates compared with the early years of the pandemic. These results may indicate the possible positive effect of vaccination campaigns and health measures on controlling the spread of the virus and reducing severe cases.

The geographical disparities observed in testing and case distribution underscore the importance of strengthening health infrastructure. In addition, variations in vaccination coverage and positivity rates between regions highlight the need to adjust local response strategies according to regional dynamics. The continued emergence of new mutations underscores the need for rigorous epidemiological surveillance to anticipate and respond quickly to variants of concern.
