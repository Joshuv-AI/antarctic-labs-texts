Ontogeny of digestive enzymes for weaning and role of dietary taurine supplementation in growth, survival and physiology of Channa striata juveniles
Authors: Shrinath Vaijanath Gavhane, C Judith Betsy, I Kaviyarasi, A Anix Vivek Santhiya, P Sivasankar, E Prabu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAquaculture plays a vital role in meeting the growing global demand for high-quality protein, particularly in the context of a rapidly increasing human population. Channa striata is an important aquaculture species due to its high nutritional value, rapid growth and adaptability to diverse environmental conditions. However, the demand for C. striata cannot be met due to the limited availability of its seed, as low larval survival remains a significant challenge in hatchery production. Therefore, the present study aimed at studying the influence of dietary taurine supplementation on the growth and survival of C. striata larvae by understanding the ontogeny of digestive enzyme activity. Hence, the study was conducted as two experiments. In the first experiment, the ontogeny of digestive enzyme activity in C. striata larvae was examined to assess the optimal weaning stage. To facilitate this, larvae were sampled at 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 25 and 30 days post-hatch (dph). The activities of digestive enzymes such as trypsin, chymotrypsin, leucine aminopeptidase and lipase peaked at 18 dph, while alkaline phosphatase activity peaked at 21 dph. Based on these findings, 18 dph was identified as the optimal time to initiate the gradual weaning of larvae to pellet feed. In the second experiment, a 60-day feeding trial was conducted using 27-day-old weaned larvae (0.23 ± 0.03 g and 2.42 ± 0.12 cm) to evaluate the effects of dietary taurine supplementation. Five experimental diets were formulated with graded levels of taurine: 0 g/kg (control), 5 g/kg (T1), 10 g/kg (T2), 15 g/kg (T3) and 20 g/kg (T4). Growth and survival significantly (P < 0.05) improved up to T3, with 90% survival in T2 and T3. Antioxidant enzyme activity was highest at T4. Taurine supplementation significantly (P < 0.05) increased haemoglobin, haematocrit and WBCs in blood and elevated glucose, cholesterol, HDL and LDL in serum, while reducing serum triglycerides and VLDL; no significant changes were observed in serum total protein, albumin, globulin, or RBC indices. These findings indicate that 18 dph is the optimal time to initiate weaning to a formulated diet in C. striata larvae, and the post-weaning dietary taurine requirement for C. striata fry was estimated at 16.31–16.40 g/kg feed based on broken-line regression analysis.Keywords: Channa striata, Digestive ontology, Taurine, Growth, SurvivalSubject terms: Animal physiology, Ichthyology

Introduction

The problem of world hunger has stayed largely unchanged over the past three years; the affected global population grew to 9.1% in 2023, compared to 7.5% in 20191. FAO2 asserts that aquaculture can satisfy the rising worldwide demand for aquatic foods. The rising consumer demand for fish, largely attributed to its health benefits, highlights the significant potential and necessity for expanding fish production through aquaculture to satisfy future consumption needs3. Global aquaculture production increased by 8.1 million tonnes from 122.8 million tonnes in 2020 to a record high of 130.9 million tonnes in 20222.

Captive breeding and larval rearing play a pivotal role in accelerating aquaculture development, with hatchery production serving as a critical foundation for the successful cultivation of species4,5. Larval nutrition is a critical determinant of survival, growth and developmental success in aquaculture species, particularly during early life stages when the digestive and immune systems are still immature6. In the larval development of fish, feeding is one of the crucial stages, and it needs great care7. A thorough understanding of larval nutritional requirements is crucial for the development of optimised diets and feeding strategies, which in turn can significantly improve the growth performance and quality of fish larvae and juveniles8. The water quality of the rearing environment and the nutritional content of external feed significantly impact larval development and survival3. Although notable advancements have been made in the field of larval fish nutrition, numerous fundamental questions remain unresolved, highlighting the need for continued investigation6.

The striped murrel (Channa striata Bloch, 1793) is a highly regarded freshwater carnivorous fish species that shows great promise for intensive aquaculture due to its adaptability and high market demand8. It is a significant food fish with high economic importance, primarily because of its preferred flesh quality, therapeutic properties and antinociceptive effects. C. striata is widely distributed across South and Southeast Asia9,18 and it is widely consumed for its potential to aid in healing and pain management, particularly following clinical procedures10.

In India, the cultivation of striped murrel primarily relies on natural production via capture fisheries. This reliance is due to the difficulties related to the production of hatchery-reared juveniles and captive breeding, which are currently insufficient to satisfy the demands of commercial striped murrel farming11,12. Along with that, even with advancements in breeding technology, larval rearing of striped murrel remains challenging due to the species’ cannibalistic behaviours in early developmental stages, the absence of a standardized weaning protocol, insufficient understanding of digestive ontogeny, and poor feed management practices13.

In striped murrel, intraspecific predation has been reported in the initial stages, lowering their survival rate14. The shift from endogenous to endoexogenous, and eventually to exogenous feeding, is an important phase in fish larval growth7. Weaning from live to formulated feed is crucial for growing larval and juvenile fish in aquaculture. Its success depends on the initial size of fish, diet palatability, food, feeding approach and cannibalism14. Elevated and early expression of digestive enzyme activities was linked to improved survival rates following the transition to formulated diets15. It has been reported that certain dietary supplements, such as polyunsaturated fatty acids, vitamin16, phospholipid17 and amino acid18 during larval rearing, can increase the survival rate and improve the overall physiological metabolism of the fish.

Taurine is one such amino acid that is used as a dietary supplement in many fish species. It has been identified as a necessary amino acid, particularly in the juvenile and larvae of a variety of fish species19. It is the last byproduct of the metabolism of amino acids that contain sulfur. It is referred to as 2-aminoethanesulfonic acid and is an organic acid26,28. Taurine cannot be metabolized for energy or utilized in protein synthesis; instead, it is typically found as a free amino acid or a simple peptide29,30. It is a neutral β-amino acid that can ionize both the amine and sulfuric groups. Fish species vary greatly in their taurine synthesis, and the importance of taurine for fish performance could be influenced by factors such as the species, their inherent feeding habits and their previous dietary experiences and the enzymatic activity of cysteinesulphinate decarboxylase (CSD)25,31. In fish nutrition, taurine is an amino acid that is conditionally necessary for several carnivorous fish33. It holds significance as a vital nutrient in the nutritional requirements of broodstock, as well as in the diets of larval and juvenile fish19. Taurine deficiency in the diet is thought to be especially risky for fish larvae27.

The inclusion of taurine in the diets of juvenile grass carp, (Ctenopharyngodon idella) and Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus) larvae fed plant protein-based diets resulted in improved growth rates, feed digestibility, feed efficiency and enhanced activities of hepatopancreatic and intestinal proteases, lipases, and amylases26. Furthermore, common dentex (Dentex dentex) juveniles receiving a diet based on soy protein concentrate (SPC) demonstrated enhanced growth rates, feed efficiency, and lipid metabolism when given an extra 0.2% taurine28. Elevated dietary taurine supplementation enhanced feeding activity and growth rates in the larvae and juveniles of European sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax)26. Supplementing the diet of grass carp with taurine led to a decrease in the hemolysis rate, which was linked to improved tolerance to acute hypoxia23.

During larval and juvenile phases, many fish species possess underdeveloped taurine biosynthesis pathways, making dietary taurine essential for optimal physiological functioning. For example, Solea senegalensis larvae benefitted from taurine‑supplemented diets in terms of growth and metamorphosis, accompanied by increased expression of taurine transporter at critical developmental stages35. Similarly, rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss) show ontogenetic variation in taurine‑biosynthesis enzymes, with CSD expression rising only after larval development29. Juvenile fish of yellowtail (Seriola quinqueradiata)30 and black carp (Mylopharyngodon piceus)31 exhibit markedly enhanced growth, digestive enzyme activities, and lipid metabolism when supplemented with taurine.

Against this backdrop, the present study initially identified the digestive ontogeny of C. striata. Subsequently, based on the identified digestive ontogeny, the fish were weaned onto pellet feed, and the influence of dietary taurine supplementation on the growth and survival of C. striata larvae was investigated. The results of this study will assist in identifying the optimal weaning period and developing acceptable feed alternatives to live food, and it will enhance the understanding of the impact of dietary taurine incorporation in feeding regimes on the growth performance and survival of C. striata larvae.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Committee constituted by Tamil Nadu Dr. J. Jayalalithaa Fisheries University, Tamil Nadu, India. All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the relevant institutional guidelines and regulations, and the study complied with the ARRIVE guidelines for responsible research practices.

Breeding of fish

The experiment was conducted at Freshwater Fish Farm Complex, Department of Aquaculture, Fisheries College and Research Institute, Thoothukudi, Tamil Nadu. C. striata brooders with an average body weight of 450 ± 50 g were procured from a private farm. Immediately after procurement, the brooders were stocked in a large rectangular cement tank for acclimatization. Brooders were fed with weed fish, boiled chicken intestine, black soldier fly larvae, trash fish and earthworms at 2–3% of their body weight3,10,32 twice a day for 8 weeks. After acclimatization, 5 pairs of brooders were selected for breeding. The selected brooders were induced to breed through delivering WOVA-FH™ (BIOSTADT INDIA LIMITED, Mumbai) to females (640 ± 30 g) at a dosage of 1 ml/kg and to males (500 ± 20 g) at a dosage of 0.6 ml/kg, divided into two doses over 6 h. Following hormone injection, male and female brooders were relocated to a fibre-reinforced plastic (FRP) tank in a 1:1 ratio. Following a latency period of 19 to 20 h, the brooders spawned, while the temperature was kept steady at 29 ± 2 °C with gentle aeration (Fig. 1a).

Rearing of larvae for digestive ontology

The hatchlings were placed in three FRP tanks, each with 300 L capacity and were raised until the juvenile stage, which occurred 30 days post-hatching (dph). The larvae were fed with Artemia nauplii at a rate of 200 to 300 individuals per larvae in the first week from 3 dph onwards, and at a rate of 300 individuals per larvae in the second week7. From 15 dph, the larvae were daily provided frozen artemia at a feeding rate of 6–8% of their body weight3. The diet was provided 4 times a day at 6:00, 9:00, 12:00, and 16:00 h. Siphoning and water exchange were done on alternate days.

Sample collection for the digestive enzyme

Larvae were collected on 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, 25 and 30 dph to assess the progression of the digestive enzymes. The samples were collected early in the morning, before feeding, to mitigate the possible impacts of exogenous enzymes derived from live food. The number of larvae and juveniles within each sample fluctuated from 220 on 3 dph to 5 at 30 dph, contingent upon their size. The weight of the larvae was measured using an advanced balance (SHIMADZU, ATY224).

Tissue collection and homogenate preparation

Each larva was rinsed with distilled water (DW), and any excess moisture was absorbed with tissue paper. The entire fish larvae were dissected, and the anterior and posterior sections were excised according to standardized procedure33. Using a mechanical tissue homogenizer, a 5% tissue homogenate was produced in a cooled sucrose solution (0.25 M). The tissue homogenate was centrifuged (SPAN, SAC-R7) at 5000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, after which the supernatant was collected into Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. The entire operation, beginning with tissue collection, was executed meticulously to maintain the cold chain and retain enzyme activity. The total protein concentration in the tissue homogenate was assessed using the Lowry et al.42 method.

Digestive enzymes assay

Trypsin specific activity

Trypsin activity was assessed employing the procedure detailed by Gorrill and Thomas43. A 1% casein solution was prepared in a buffer with a pH of 7.6. A 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution was prepared using DW. Then 2 ml of casein was taken and 0.2 ml of tissue homogenate was added to it, followed by incubation for 20 min. After incubation, 2 ml of 5% TCA was added and the solution was kept for 10 min. The mixture was then filtered. Absorbance measurements of the filtrate were taken at 280 nm using a spectrophotometer. The whole process was carried out at room temperature. Trypsin activity was denoted in U/mg protein.

Chymotrypsin specific activity

The method outlined by Gorrill and Thomas43 was used to estimate the activity of chymotrypsin. 1% of casein (a pinch of CaCl2 added to 1% casein) was prepared in buffer with a pH of 8.0 and 5% TCA was prepared in DW. Then, 2 ml of casein was taken, and 0.2 ml of the tissue homogenate was added to it. The mixture was incubated for 20 min. After incubation, 2 ml of 5% TCA was added and the solution was left for 10 min for incubation at room temperature. The mixture was subsequently filtered using filter paper. The absorbance of the filtrate was assessed using a spectrophotometer at 280 nm. The entire process was completed at room temperature. The activity of chymotrypsin was expressed in U/mg protein.

Lipase specific activity

The lipase activity was assessed using the Cherry and Crandell method44. Two labelled test tubes designated “T” for test and “C” for control, were prepared. In each of the two tubes, 3 ml of DW and 1 ml of tissue homogenate were added. The tube labelled as “C” was immersed in boiling water for 5 min to deactivate the lipase enzyme, serving as the control. Next, 0.5 ml of phosphate buffer with a pH of 7.0 was introduced. Then, both the tubes received 2 ml of olive oil emulsion and were thoroughly shaken and incubated at 37o C for 24 h. Upon completion of incubation, 3 ml of 95% alcohol and 2 drops of phenolphthalein solution were introduced to the tubes, which were subsequently titrated with 0.05 M NaOH until a stable pink hue was observed. The difference between the volumes (in ml) of NaOH solution needed for 100 mg of tissue in the experimental tube and the control tube represented the units of intestinal lipase activity per gram of tissue.

Alkaline phosphatase specific activity

The activity of alkaline phosphatase was analyzed with a commercially available kit (Jeev Diagnostics Pvt. Ltd.) following the guidelines set forth by the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry (IFCC).

Leucine amionopeptidase specific activity

The activities of aminopeptidase were measured in 96-well microtiter plates with leucine-p-nitroanilide (Leu-p-Nan) serving as the specific substrate. In performing the assay, 30 µL of the sample was combined with 140 µL of 0.1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) and 30 µL of 8 mM Leu-p-Nan, then incubated for 10 min at 25 °C. Using a microplate reader, the release of p-nitroaniline (the product) was measured at a wavelength of 405 nm (Thermoscientific, Multiskan FC). To prepare a blank control, the sample was replaced with 0.1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). A unit (U) of enzyme activity was established as the amount of enzyme required to hydrolyze 1 µmol of Leu-p-Nan per minute under the defined conditions35.

Experimental fish and feeding management

The fish were bred following the protocol described in Section Breeding of fishes. The larvae obtained through induced breeding were weaned onto the experimental diets based on the findings of digestive ontogeny. Beginning on 18 dph, 10% of the diet was gradually replaced with the experimental pellet feed each day, leading to complete transfer (100% pellet diet) by 27 dph. A total of 3000 fry (including triplicates), with an initial weight of 0.23 ± 0.03 g and length of 2.42 ± 0.12 cm, were randomly distributed into 15 tanks (80 cm length × 50 cm width × 35 cm height) having a water-holding capacity of 140 L, and each tank was stocked with 200 fry at a density of 2 fry/L10. The fry was initially fed at a rate of 6–8% of body weight each day, gradually decreasing to 4–5% split into four times per day3. The feeding frequency was maintained at 4 times a day (6:00, 9:00, 12:00 and 16:00) for 60 days (Fig. 1b). Faeces and uneaten feed were removed daily, and a 40% water exchange was done on alternate days. The water quality was monitored under the following conditions: temperature, 32 ± 0.3 °C; dissolved oxygen, 5.3 ± 0.4 mg/L; pH, 7.27 ± 0.1; total ammonia, 0.02 ± 0.005 mg/L; nitrite-N, 0.024 ± 0.01 mg/L; total alkalinity, 160.79 ± 7.4 mg/L; and total hardness 196.19 ± 19.3 mg/L (Table 3).

Tissue collection and homogenate preparation for antioxidant enzyme analysis

The procedure for tissue homogenate preparation was the same as described for digestive ontology tissue homogenate preparation. At the end of the feeding trial, five fish were randomly sampled from each tank and anesthetized using clove oil at a concentration of 50 µL L⁻¹. Following euthanasia, the fish were dissected, and gill tissues were carefully excised and immediately processed for tissue homogenate preparation as per the method described by Bhusare et al.46. The total protein concentration in the tissue homogenate was assessed using the Lowry et al.42 method.

Catalase activity (CAT)

CAT activity was recorded using the protocol described by Takahara et al.47. To a 50 µl gill tissue homogenate, 2.45 ml of phosphate buffer (50mM, pH 7.0) were added and the reaction was initiated by adding 1 ml of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). The reduction in absorbance was recorded at 240 nm wavelength, at 15-second break, spanning duration of 3 min. The enzyme blank was measured concurrently with the phosphate buffer. CAT activity was quantified as moles of H2O2 decomposed/protein/minute. One unit of catalase is measured as the amount of enzyme used to breakdown 1 µmol of H2O2/minute.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD)

The procedure outlined by Kono et al.48 was used to estimate the SOD activity. The sample volume of 70 µl was mixed with the following reagents. 50 mM sodium carbonate was prepared by taking 0.529 g of sodium carbonate in 100 ml DW. Nitro blue tetrazolium (NBT) (0.096 M) was prepared by taking 0.004 g of NBT in 50 ml DDW. Triton X-100 was prepared by taking 0.06 g in 100 ml DW. Hydroxylamine hydrochloride (20 mM) was prepared by taking 0.138 g in 100 ml DW and the pH was regulated to 6.0. OD value was recorded for 5 min at 540 nm at room temperature. The enzyme activity was indicated as SOD units/min/mg protein. One unit of SOD activity represents the amount of enzyme required to decrease the reaction of O2 with NBT by 50%.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx)

GPx activity was measured by following Paglia and Valentine49. 0.2 ml of gill tissue homogenate was mixed in to a 0.2 ml of PO4 buffer, 0.2 ml of EDTA, 0.1 ml of sodium azide, 0.1 ml of reduced glutathione and well blended. To this mixer, 0.2 ml glutathione reduced (GSH), 0.1 ml of H2O2 and 0.1 ml NADPH were added and kept in a water bath at 37 ℃ for incubation for 10 min. Upon completion of the incubation period, 0.5 ml of 10% TCA was introduced and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. In a test tube, 1 ml of the supernatant was taken, and 2.0 ml of the Tris buffer and 50 µl of DTNB (5, 5-dithio-bis-2-nitrobenzoic acid) were added. The OD value was recorded at 412 nm for 3 min. The enzyme activity was quantified as unit/min/mg protein. A single unit of activity is characterized as the quantity of enzyme needed to reduce 1 µmol/L of GSH in one minute.

Blood parameters analysis

Blood samples were collected from 3 to 5 fish per treatment via the caudal vein using a 1 mL sterile syringe (DISPO VAN) fitted with a 26-gauge needle containing 5% EDTA (Ethylenediaminetetraacetic Acid) as an anticoagulant. The collected blood samples were then pooled to obtain a sufficient volume for analysis. Each pooled sample was equally divided, with one portion used for haematological examinations and the other for immunological assays39. For serum analysis, after being left for two hours to allow for proper clotting, the tubes were centrifuged at 7,000 rpm for 10 min. The serum obtained was subsequently stored at −20 °C in a deep freezer. Later, it was used for serum biochemical parameter analysis36.

Haematological parameters

After the completion of the experiment, the haematological parameters such as Red Blood Cell (RBC), White Blood Cell (WBC), Haematocrit (HCT), Haemoglobin (Hb), Mean Corpuscular Volume (MCV), Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin Concentration (MCHC) and Mean Corpuscular Haemoglobin (MCH) concentration in the collected blood samples, were assessed using an automated haematological analyser (Laboholic, China).

Serum biochemical parameters

The blood biochemical parameters, such as serum protein and lipid profile, were analysed at the end of the experiment. Statistical analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA in SPSS version 22.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) after testing for homogeneity of variances. For digestive enzyme activity, Duncan’s Multiple Range Test was used for post-hoc comparisons, while Tukey’s test was applied for taurine supplementation experiments. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A broken-line regression analysis based on weight gain and average daily weight gain was used to estimate the minimum dietary taurine requirement.

Growth performance and feed utilization

The impact of taurine on the growth rate and feed efficiency of C. striata is given in Table 4. The control group, devoid of taurine in the diet, shown markedly reduced growth. The growth rate, measured by MWG (8.27 ± 0.08 g), MLG (7.91 ± 0.13 cm), ADWG (137.87 ± 1.31 mg), SGR (3.52 ± 0.02%), WG% (3479.89 ± 223.86) and LG% (325.00 ± 30.43), elevated significantly (P < 0.001) with taurine supplementation, reaching its peak at T3 as compared to control. Nonetheless, at the highest taurine supplementation level (T4), growth performance declined, as reflected by lower ADWG (131.45 ± 1.1 mg), MWG (7.89 ± 0.06 g), WG% (3261.82 ± 176.97), MLG (7.45 ± 0.14 cm), LG% (300.47 ± 18.41) and SGR (3.44 ± 0.01). The FCR (1.97 ± 0.05) and FER (0.49 ± 0.01) have shown significantly (P < 0.001) higher values with the T3, whereas the values of FCR and FER are, 2.59 ± 0.07 and 0.37 ± 0.01, respectively in control group. The broken-line regression analysis indicated that the optimal dietary taurine inclusion for C. striata is ranging between 16.31 and 16.40 g/kg, as determined by the relationship between dietary taurine levels and weight gain, ADWG (Figs. 7 and 8). The T2 and T3 treatment groups demonstrated the highest survival rates among all groups, with a survival rate of 90%, whereas the survival in control group was 72.7%.

Discussion

Fish larvae require an extremely active enzymatic digestive system capable of effectively processing a large number of nutrients they consume to support their rapid growth and development41. Comprehending the development of digestive physiology is essential for choosing the appropriate feed type, as larvae need to efficiently capture, digest and absorb nutrients from their food13. The functional development of the digestive system is essential for the survival and growth of fish larvae, since a proficient digestive system allows the fish to catch, eat, digest, and absorb nutrients from their diet efficiently42.

This study demonstrated a significant (P < 0.05)increase in the activity of chymotrypsin and trypsin from 3 dph, which reached its peak at 18 dph. Furthermore, comparable expression profiles or patterns have been detected in Asian seabass43, Indian walking catfish42 and milkfish33. From 3 dph onwards, the larvae were fed with Artemia nauplii, which might have produced these digestive enzymes. This was in line with Munilla-Moran et al.55, who mentioned that the live feed in turbot stimulates the secretion of endogenous digestive enzymes, including amylase, trypsin and chymotrypsin, thereby improving the larvae’s nutritional absorption efficiency.

In addition to digestive proteases, the activity of lipolytic enzymes has a crucial role in providing sufficient nutrition for fish larvae during their initial stages of life33. This study assessed that the activity of the lipase enzyme increased from 3 dph and reached its peak at 18 dph. This agrees with the results obtained by Comabella et al.56, who found that lipase activity peaked between 14 and 18 days after hatching in Cuban gar, Atractosteus tristoechus. In Sciaenops ocellatus larvae, lipase activity reached its highest point between 17 and 22 days after hatching when fed live prey46. The increase in lipase activity could be associated with the maturation of the exocrine pancreas, the utilization of accumulated lipids and the increased capacity to break down dietary lipids45.

In the current study, the activity of alkaline phosphatase reached its highest level at 21 dph, while leucine aminopeptidase activity peaked at 18 dph. Farhoudi et al.58 discovered a significant rise in the specific activity of alkaline phosphatase and aminopeptidase N during the third week in common carp larvae.

The current investigation indicates that 18 dph is the optimal time to begin transitioning C. striata larvae to formulated diets, as most enzyme activities peaked at this stage. This is congruent with Kumari et al.8 who proposed that the larvae of C. striata should be optimally weaned from 18 dph to 25 dph to align with the functional maturation of the exocrine pancreas and stomach. Similarly, Hien et al.14 found that, for the domesticated Vietnamese strain of C. striata, the ideal feeding transition protocol begins at 17 dph, substituting 10% of live feed with formulated feed daily.

Melianawati and Astuti59 mentioned that in various fish species, the increased activity of digestive enzymes is closely associated with advancing larval age, the progressive development of digestive organ structures, and the transition from the larval to juvenile stage. As these organs develop, their capacity to secrete digestive enzymes also increases, supporting enhanced digestive functionality during ontogeny. The expression of digestive enzyme genes is tightly regulated by intrinsic developmental genetic programs. Notably, certain enzymes, such as amylase, are expressed even in the absence of exogenous feeding49, indicating the presence of genetically pre-programmed triggers. As larval development progresses, there is a marked upregulation in the mRNA expression of digestive enzymes and key regulatory hormones, which collectively enhance digestive capacity. This molecular upregulation is typically reflected in increased enzymatic activity, often reaching a peak around 18 dph, corresponding with critical stages of digestive system maturation50,51.

Taurine is typically regarded as an acid derivative that exhibits key features of a feeding attractant for fish, particularly in terms of its nitrogen content, molecular weight, water solubility and acidic properties22. The impact of dietary taurine administration on enhancing growth performance and optimizing feed consumption in diverse aquatic species has been thoroughly assessed52. The growth-stimulating properties of taurine have been documented in yellowtail juveniles Seriola quinqueradiata30, rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss)53, Nile tilapia54, channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus)55, yellowfin seabream (Acanthopagrus latus)56 and European sea bass57.

In this study, C. striata showed a considerable increase (P < 0.05) in growth performance when taurine was supplemented in their diet, up to T3. The increase in growth may be attributed to an increase in feed uptake by the fish. Increased feed consumption is generally noticed when the palatability of the feed is enhanced. Adeshina and Abdel-Tawwab31 suggested that in African catfish, Clarias gariepinus, taurine enhanced the flavour quality of feed, leading to increased feed consumption by fish, which resulted in improved growth performance. Thus, the enhanced feed palatability must have increased the feed intake, leading to higher growth performance as reported in the pangasius species58. The provision of taurine increased both the consumption and efficiency of feed in turbot (Scophthalmus maximus)22. Gaylord et al.32 proposed that better growth rate reported in rainbow trout can possibly be due to better protein utilisation and retention. Nakamura et al.68 demonstrated that Japanese flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus) possesses the ability to synthesize taurine from cysteic acid. They also indicated that dietary supplementation with up to 0.5% cysteic acid not only enhances growth performance but also modulates the growth hormone–insulin-like growth factor (GH–IGF) axis in this species.

Taurine enrichment is necessary to sustain the physiological functions and promote the growth of rainbow trout29 and S. quinqueradiata69. It plays a critical role in bile salt conjugation, thereby facilitating the emulsification, digestion and absorption of dietary lipids. By promoting efficient lipid metabolism, taurine contributes significantly to the energy supply required for optimal growth and development in fish larvae. It also influences growth by modulating molecular pathways involved in metabolism and cellular homeostasis, including the regulation of mitochondrial function and calcium signalling, both essential for muscle development and overall physiological integrity. Taurine functions as a key osmoregulatory molecule, contributing to the maintenance of intracellular volume and ionic homeostasis. This role is particularly crucial in aquatic environments, where fish larvae are continually exposed to osmotic fluctuations60. However, Salze and Davis28 mentioned that, the maximum inclusion level of taurine in the diet has to be determined for each species. In the current research, the maximum weight gain of C. striata was noticed at T3 treatment, suggesting that this concentration is sufficient for achieving optimal growth performance.

Over-supplementation of taurine (1.5%) may hinder growth by curtailing feed consumption, as reported in pangasius, Pangasianodon hypophthalmus58 and rainbow trout53. It is assumed that the diminished feed uptake at higher concentrations of taurine was attributed to the decreased palatability of the diet, which may have resulted from the elevated acidic properties associated with excessive taurine accumulation in the feed22. Taurine has been shown to modulate the growth hormone (GH)–insulin-like growth factor (IGF) axis. While moderate levels of dietary taurine can stimulate this pathway and support growth, excessive supplementation, such as 1.0% in Japanese flounder, may lead to hormonal imbalances or desensitization of GH receptors, ultimately resulting in diminished somatic growth59. Excessive taurine supplementation may exert negative feedback on endogenous biosynthetic pathways by downregulating key genes involved in taurine synthesis and transport, including cdo (cysteine dioxygenase), csad (cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase), and taut (taurine transporter). Such gene suppression in response to high exogenous taurine levels can disrupt metabolic homeostasis and contribute to physiological imbalances, ultimately leading to reduced growth performance61. Zheng et al.72 discovered that an excessive concentration of taurine at 2% adversely affected the survival and growth of tongue sole, Cynoglossus semilaevis larvae.

The current investigation revealed a positive correlation between elevated taurine concentrations and enhanced larval survival. Salze et al.63 observed that dietary taurine inclusion may have increased the availability of highly unsaturated fatty acids (HUFA) to the larvae, leading to enhanced growth and survival rates in Cobia (Rachycentron canadum). Additionally, they have mentioned that taurine reduces the production and longevity of osteoclasts, which are cells responsible for breaking down bone tissue, while simultaneously enhancing bone integrity. In red seabream (Pagrus major), dietary taurine supplementation has been shown to stimulate the secretion of connective tissue growth factor (CTGF), a cysteine-rich protein that plays a critical role in the development and maintenance of skeletal tissues64. Additionally, it contributes to bone health through its involvement in calcium signalling pathways23 and by inhibiting bone resorption mediated by osteoclasts65, thereby supporting both bone development and maintenance. In hybrid snakehead (C. maculatus × C. argus), taurine supplementation enhanced immunity by elevating plasma total protein levels and alkaline phosphatase activity, improved antioxidant capacity through the upregulation of CAT and GR (glutathione reductase) gene expression while diminishing reactive oxygen species production, and reduced blood cell apoptosis by augmenting HSP 70 (Heat Shock Protein) expression. These impacts may have enhanced the survival rate66. Sampath et al.19 documented that taurine improved the survival rates of various fish species, including California yellowtail, S. lalandi; Gilthead seabream, Sparus aurata; Korean rockfish, Sebastes schlegeli and Yellow drum, Nibea albiflora.

Digestive enzyme ontogeny provides a physiological framework for interpreting the taurine feeding trial, as increasing enzyme activities during early development reflect progressive digestive maturation and enhanced nutrient assimilation. The improved growth and survival of taurine-fed larvae, therefore, likely result from a synergistic interaction between stage-specific digestive capacity and taurine-mediated metabolic support rather than from dietary supplementation alone.

Research on antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT and GPx in fish, can offer important biomarkers for evaluating fish physiology in response to stress or environmental fluctuations67. These enzymes have crucial functions in safeguarding cells contrary to unregulated oxidative reactions, hence reducing the harm caused by superoxide radicals and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)68. Taurine, acting as a passive antioxidant, may enhance the body’s defence system against oxidative stress by safeguarding mitochondria from a surplus of superoxide52.

Zhang et al.31 found that taurine may enhance the movement of nuclear factor E2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) into the nucleus and its capability to bind to the antioxidant response element. This, in turn, leads to an increase in the expression of antioxidant enzymes at the mRNA level. Findings from this study reveal that dietary taurine supplementation in the T4 group showed the highest antioxidant capabilities. In common carp, enhanced activities of SOD, CAT and GPx were noted in individuals fed taurine-enriched diets, indicating an improved antioxidant ability26. Taurine enhanced antioxidant capability and mitigated lipid peroxidation in the liver of Persian sturgeon69.

Haematology and biochemical characteristics of the blood are important indicators for monitoring the health of fish during environmental and nutritional changes70. The current research revealed a significant difference (P < 0.05) in the values of WBC, RBC, Hb, and haematocrit. However, no notable disparity was found in the MCV, MCH and MCHC of the fish blood samples. Li et al.71 reported that RBC, Hb and haematocrit values were elevated with enhanced dietary taurine intake, although no significant variations were noted among the diets. Wassef et al.72 observed comparable outcomes in D. labrax when the fish were provided with a diet enriched with taurine, likely due to taurine’s role as an immunostimulant.

The current investigation found no significant variations in albumin, globulin, and total protein levels across the treatment groups (P > 0.05). Nonetheless, a significant (P < 0.05) difference was seen in glucose levels. Taurine augments glycolytic enzyme activity in T. macdonaldi via amplifying insulin effects and promoting glucose transport through the cellular membrane73. López et al.74 observed that when soy protein concentrate was supplemented with taurine, it resulted in increased glucose levels in the juveniles of T. macdonaldi. Tong et al.75 discovered that the inclusion of taurine in all-plant protein diets led to improvements in total protein and albumin levels. This suggests that taurine supplementation may have a beneficial effect on repairing liver cell damage and promoting protein anabolism.

The impact of taurine on the lipid profile of fish seems to vary depending on the species. Liu et al.76 identified that adding taurine to the diet can reduce the levels of total cholesterol and triglycerides in the blood of turbot. Contrary to this, Satriyo et al.77 noted that the inclusion of taurine in the diet of T. macdonaldi resulted in elevated concentrations of plasma cholesterol and triglycerides. In the present study, the levels of cholesterol, HDL and LDL were influenced by the progressive increase in taurine concentration. Unlike other serum lipid measures, triglycerides exhibit a decrease in values as the concentration of taurine increases. This may be due to enhanced lipid metabolism caused by taurine, which stimulates bile salt-dependent lipase activity in the liver, as observed in common dentex28. Also, this enzyme plays a critical role in breaking down dietary triglycerides. In addition, taurine conjugates with primary bile acids (BAs) to form taurocholic acid and other conjugated BAs, which possess both hydrophilic and lipophilic properties, making them more efficient emulsifiers than unconjugated BAs78. Bavi et al.79 also noticed comparable results, showing that the fish receiving high dietary taurine supplementation in the experiment had the highest content of cholesterol, HDL and LDL. White seabream fed with low-fishmeal diets had comparable findings, wherein the augmented taurine supplementation resulted in elevated levels of bile salts. This, in turn, led to decreased plasma triglyceride levels, but cholesterol levels remained unaffected80.

While the present findings clearly demonstrate the benefits of taurine supplementation, the potential influence of broodstock nutritional status on larval digestive development cannot be fully excluded and warrants further investigation under controlled broodstock feeding regimes.

Conclusion

In the present study, the digestive ontology of C. striata larvae was first examined by assessing the specific activity of various digestive enzymes such as trypsin, chymotrypsin, lipase, alkaline phosphatase and leucine aminopeptidase. The higher level of specific activity of all the enzymes except alkaline phosphatase was found at 18 dph, while for alkaline phosphatase it was found at 21 dph. Hence, it can be suggested that the digestive system of C. striata larvae was fully developed on 18 dph, which can be considered as the optimum time for weaning. In the second set of experiments, larvae were weaned to a taurine-supplemented diet from 18 dph onwards. The results reported that taurine can significantly elevate the growth rate, antioxidant status, and biochemical properties of C. striata fry. The results indicated that the optimum post-weaning dietary taurine requirement for C. striata fry was achieved at 15 g/kg (T3), with broken-line regression analysis further estimating the requirement to be 16.31–16.40 g/kg feed. This study enhanced the understanding of digestive system development in C. striata larvae and evaluated the impact of dietary taurine supplementation on their growth and survival during the transition to formulated diets.
