A study on predicting malnutrition risk in Parkinson’s disease patients using a nomogram model
Authors: Qiuxiang Huang, Honghao Xu, Yong Luo, Jie Zhou, Mengjia Li, Yujia Li, Qingping Xue, Zichen Wang, Zhuochi Zou, Haroona Bashir, Xianwei Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Developed and validated a PD malnutrition risk nomogram.•Primary malnutrition risk factors in PD patients identified.•Nomogram shows strong survival prediction (AUC 0.85)Keywords: Parkinson’s Disease, Nomogram, Malnutrition, Risk factors, Prediction model

Introduction

Patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) face a heightened risk of nutritional deficiencies and weight loss, which can be attributed to both the disease’s inherent symptoms[1]. Epidemiological studies reveal that the prevalence of malnutrition among PD patients ranges from 5.3 % to 12.2 %, with those at risk of malnutrition varying between 29.0 % and 41.7 %[2]. The interplay of clinical symptoms, including tremors, rigidity, dyskinesia, and non-motor symptoms such as hyposmia, cognitive impairment, depression, constipation, and dysphagia, significantly affects energy expen- diture and intake[3,4,5,6]. While motor symptoms increase energy output, non-motor symptoms often lead to decreased appetite and food intake, exacerbating the risk of malnutrition[7,8].

Furthermore, the side effects of anti-Parkinson’s medications and the patients’ dependence on assistance for daily activities further complicate their nutritional status[3,5]. Despite the critical role that nutrition plays in influencing clinical symptoms and treatment outcomes, the nutritional status of PD patients is frequently overlooked. Current research has primarily focused on only a limited range of factors affecting nutritional status, leading to inconsistent findings and a lack of effective tools for assessing nutritional risk.

Addressing malnutrition is vital, particularly in clinical settings where it can lead to severe com- plications, such as in major surgical procedures[9]. Therefore, implementing effective malnutrition screening tools is critical not only for pre-operative assessments but also for ongoing management in PD patients. The present study aims to bridge the existing gaps by developing a predictive model to identify risk factors associated with malnutrition in individuals with PD. Utilizing the Mini Nu- tritional Assessment (MNA) as a screening tool, this research will employ a cross-sectional design to evaluate the nutritional status of patients with PD. By integrating univariate and multivariate lo- gistic regression analyses, the study seeks to identify key risk factors that contribute to malnutrition, ultimately leading to the development of a Nomogram-based predictive model. By identifying and quantifying the relevant risk factors, this study aspires to provide clinicians with a practical tool that can enhance the efficiency of nutritional assessments and facilitate the formulation of individ- ualized care plans. Ultimately, the goal is to improve the overall quality of life for PD patients by addressing their nutritional needs and mitigating the adverse effects of malnutrition on disease progression.

Methods

Participants

From February 2022 to December 2023, a total of 163 PD patients (87 males and 76 females) were enrolled from the inpatient ward and outpatient clinic of the Department of Neurology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College. These participants met the diagnostic crite- ria of the 2015 Movement Disorder Society (MDS) for Parkinson’s disease and signed their written informed consent[10]. The exclusion criteria included severe cardiac, hepatic, or renal diseases; dementia; malignant tumors; gastrointestinal disorders; hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism; and other chronic conditions potentially affecting nutritional status. Patients with incomplete clinical or laboratory data were also excluded from the study. Additionally, we assessed and omitted indi- viduals with significant frailty, dysphagia, esophageal motility disorders, or hiatal hernia that could impact the ability to feed. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Medical Ethics Committee (2023CYFYIRB-BA-Jun06) and was consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

The demographic data included gender, age, occupation, educational level, and history of hy- pertension, diabetes, peptic ulcers, stroke, and other comorbidities. It also included history of smoking and alcohol consumption, and levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD). Additionally, in the morning, fasting venous blood samples were obtained to measure total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), triglycerides, albumin, creatinine, uric acid lev- els, hemoglobin concentration, and lymphocyte counts.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were conducted in the morning, with patients dressed in stan- dardized uniforms and without footwear. Calibrated scales and stadiometers were used to measure weight and height, respectively. Body Mass Index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). BMI was then categorized into underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to 23.9 kg/m2), and overweight (≥24 kg/m2). For patients with mobility impairments, weight was measured while seated in their wheelchairs, and height was measured by their caregivers. All anthropometric measurements were performed by a single trained investigator.

Nutritional screening

All participants underwent the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), a validated screening in- strument specifically designed for the elderly population[11]. MNA scores are typically classified into three categories: normal nutritional status (≥24 points), risk of malnutrition (17 to 23.5 points), and malnourished (<17 points). However, for this study, we have streamlined these classifications into two categories: normal nutrition (≥24 points) and abnormal nutrition (<24 points)[12].

Assessment of Parkinson’s disease

(1) Motor Symptoms: The newly developed Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale II and III were employed to assess motor symptoms. Elevated scores signify more severe motor mani- festations. (2) Non-Motor Symptoms: Overall non-motor symptoms were evaluated using Part I of the MDS-UPDRS and the Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS), with elevated scores reflecting more severe non-motor symptoms. Mental status was assessed through the Hamilton Anxiety Scale and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale, where higher scores correspond to more pronounced symp- toms. Cognitive function was appraised utilizing the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) and MOCA, with increased scores indicating superior cognitive performance. The dysphagia ques- tionnaire, Wexner Constipation Scale, and GCSI were employed to assess swallowing function, gastroparesis-related symptoms, and defecation respectively, with higher scores denoting more se- vere gastrointestinal manifestations. (3) Disease Severity: The Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) staging scale was employed to evaluate disease severity, classifying the stages into early (0 to 2.5), middle (3), and late (4 to 5) categories. (4) Assessment of Motor Complications: The MDS-UPDRS Part IV and the end-dose self-rating questionnaire were employed to evaluate the presence and sever- ity of motor complications. Daily living ability and quality of life were assessed using the Barthel Index (BI), with elevated scores reflecting improved activities of daily living. Additionally, the 39- item Parkinson’s Disease Quality of Life Questionnaire (PDQ-39) was utilized to assess patients’ quality of life, where higher scores indicate a poorer quality of life.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables that conform to a normal distribution, the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Quantitative data not meeting the criteria for normality are described using median and interquartile range (IQR). Qualitative data are expressed in terms of proportions or ratios. Quantitative data exhibiting normal distribution and homogeneity of variance were an- alyzed using independent samples t-tests; if variances were not homogeneous, Welch’s t-test was employed. For quantitative data failing to meet the assumptions of normality, nonparametric tests were utilized. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests were conducted to compare qualitative data. Pearson correlation analysis (for continuous variables with normal distributions) and Spearman correlation analysis (for continuous variables with skewed distributions) were applied to assess the relationships between variables. Subsequently, joint Lasso regression was employed to construct a multivariate regression model. Variables were initially screened using univariate analysis to iden- tify candidates for inclusion, and Lasso regression was then applied to mitigate multicollinearity and overfitting. Selected variables were subsequently analyzed using logistic regression, and a pre- dictive model was established through stepwise backward regression, yielding coefficients, odds ratios (OR), 95 % confidence intervals (CI), and p-values for each variable. Model discrimination was evaluated by generating the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test along with calibration curves were uti- lized to assess model calibration. Internal validation of the model was conducted using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations, with ROC curves generated for each iteration to ultimately com- pute the mean AUC, thereby evaluating the stability of the model.

Feature selection

In the univariate analysis, 24 features were found to be significantly associated with abnormal nutritional status. Additionally, age, disease duration, comorbidities, MDS-UPDRS IV score, and lymphocyte count—each with P-values greater than 0.05—were included in the analysis. These 29 features were considered potential predictors. We employed a LASSO regression algorithm for feature selection based on each variable. The optimal tuning parameter λ for LASSO regression was determined to be 0.032 when the partial likelihood binomial deviance reached its minimum value (Fig. 1A). Eight variables with non-zero coefficients were retained in the LASSO analysis: gender, MoCA score, PDQ-39 score, GCSI score, Barthel Index (BI), body mass index (BMI), high- density lipoprotein (HDL), and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) score (Fig. 1B).

Risk factors for malnutrition in patients with Parkinson’s disease

The eight retained variables were utilized for multivariate logistic regression analysis, which identified the following independent risk factors for malnutrition in patients with Parkinson’s dis- ease (Table 2): female sex (OR 3.34, 95 %CI 1.28–8.73), lower BMI (OR 0.62, 95 %CI 0.88–0.96), higher GCSI score (OR 1.37, 95 %CI 1.15–1.63), lower Barthel Index (BI) score (OR 0.92, 95 %CI 0.89–0.96), and lower Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score (OR 0.87, 95 %CI 0.50– 0.76).

Development and validation of nomogram

A nomogram model was developed based on multivariate logistic regression analysis, incor- porating five variables as illustrated in Fig. 2. The score for each variable at every level can be determined by drawing a vertical line upward to the points axis. The total score is calculated by summing the scores of each variable. Subsequently, the total score obtained is located on the total points axis, and a vertical line is drawn downward to indicate the patient’s probability of malnutri- tion. The AUC of the nomogram was 0.92 (95 % CI: 0.89–0.96), with an optimal cutoff value of 0.39 The sensitivity and specificity of the model were 88.00 % and 77.50 %, respectively (Fig. 3A). Calibration plots (Fig. 3B) demonstrated optimal agreement between the probabilities of malnutri- tion predicted by the nomogram and the actual observed outcomes. Furthermore, the bootstrap resampling method was employed for internal validation, which yielded a C-index of 0.92, and the standard deviation was 0.019 (Fig. 3C). In summary, the nomogram model has demonstrated good predictive performance for malnutrition among patients with Parkinson’s disease.

Discussion

Malnutrition represents a significant yet frequently overlooked challenge in PD management, associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes including accelerated motor decline and reduced quality of life[13,14]. This study sought to address critical gaps in nutritional risk assessment by identifying key predictors of malnutrition and developing an accessible predictive tool for early clinical intervention. Our findings reveal that female gender, lower BMI, elevated gastrointesti- nal symptom burden (measured by GCSI), cognitive impairment (reflected by MoCA scores), and reduced functional independence (Barthel Index) collectively contribute to malnutrition vulnera- bility in PD patients. The constructed nomogram model demonstrates substantial predictive power (AUC = 0.92) and offers a practical solution to current limitations in nutritional screening protocols.

Gender differences are particularly pronounced, with female patients facing 3.339 times the risk of malnutrition compared to male patients—a finding consistent with some Parkinson’s disease literature[15,16]. This vulnerability may stem from neuroendocrine interactions between estro- gen and dopamine pathways, exacerbating levodopa-induced dyskinesias that increase metabolic demand while concurrently suppressing appetite[17,18]. Furthermore, women demonstrate height- ened susceptibility to neuropsychiatric comorbidities like depression, which independently com- promises nutritional behaviors[19]. Consequently, female PD patients warrant prioritized screening through validated tools like the MNA during regular neurology consultations, with consideration for gender-adjusted levodopa dosing when appropriate.

Beyond gender, cognitive status significantly influences nutritional outcomes. Lower MoCA scores correlated strongly with malnutrition risk, likely mediated through disrupted executive func- tions that impair meal planning and preparation[20]. Dopamine depletion in prefrontal regions may diminish food-seeking motivation and hedonic responses to eating[21]. This creates a pernicious cycle where malnutrition further exacerbates cognitive decline, suggesting that nutritional inter- ventions could yield dual therapeutic benefits. Similarly, gastrointestinal dysfunction captured by elevated GCSI scores directly impacts nutrient absorption and triggers aversive symptoms like nausea and early satiety. Gastroparesis also disrupts levodopa pharmacokinetics, creating motor fluctuations that demand increased caloric expenditure. Recognition of these interconnected mech- anisms highlights the necessity for multidimensional risk assessment rather than isolated symptom management[22,23].

The Barthel Index and BMI provided complementary functional and anthropometric insights. Reduced Barthel scores indicate impaired activities of daily living that compromise independent eating—a factor compounded when caregiver support is inconsistent. While BMI served as a useful indicator, its limitations deserve emphasis. Osteoporosis-related height loss common in PD may inflate BMI values, masking significant muscle wasting[24,25]. Additionally, 51 patients in our malnourished cohort paradoxically presented with normal BMI, illustrating its inadequacy as a standalone screening metric and reinforcing the need for comprehensive evaluation through our multimodal nomogram.

From a statistical perspective, our predictive model achieved robust discrimination between high- and low-risk individuals (AUC 0.92; sensitivity 77.5 %; specificity 88 %) through rigorous development and internal validation. The bootstrap-corrected C-index of 0.92 and well-calibrated prediction curves confirm model stability within our cohort. Such performance surpasses con– ventional tools like MNA-SF alone and offers clinically meaningful risk stratification at the 0.39 probability threshold. This level of precision enables clinicians to reliably identify candidates for nutritional interventions before irreversible decline occurs. Unlike complex machine learning algo- rithms requiring specialized infrastructure, this nomogram functions as a”bedside calculator” that integrates readily available clinical variables—enhancing practical implementation across diverse healthcare settings.

Clinically, this model empowers proactive nutritional management across the PD continuum. Its deployment could reduce hospitalizations by enabling preemptive interventions like gastroparesis- directed therapies or cognitive support for meal routines. Economically, preventing malnutrition- related complications represents significant cost savings relative to treating advanced sequelae[3]. Future iterations could incorporate biomarkers like serum prealbumin or microbiota profiles to enhance sensitivity. Expansion of this framework to conditions like Alzheimer’s disease appears promising given overlapping nutrition-related pathophysiology[4,26].

Several limitations temper the generalizability of our conclusions. The cross-sectional design captures associations but cannot establish causation between identified factors and malnutrition outcomes. Our single-center recruitment (in CHINA) and modest sample size (n = 163) warrant caution regarding broader ethnic and geographic applicability. The predominance of early-stage patients may underestimate malnutrition drivers in advanced PD where dysphagia and profound motor impairment emerge. Future longitudinal studies tracking malnutrition evolution across dis- ease stages would be invaluable, alongside external validation in multi-ethnic cohorts to establish cultural adaptability.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed and validated a predictive model to assess the risk of malnutrition in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD). Our findings indicate that female gender, lower body mass index (BMI), higher Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale (GCSI) score, lower Montreal Cogni- tive Assessment (MoCA) score, and reduced Barthel Index are significant predictors of malnutrition in this population. Internal validation results demonstrated commendable predictive performance for the model. Consequently, our nomogram serves as a convenient and reliable tool for predicting malnutrition in PD patients.
