Associations of anthropometric-related phenotypes with functional and clinical outcomes in community-dwelling older adults: a cross-sectional analysis of ELSI-Brazil
Authors: Raquel de Arruda Campos Benjamim, Jarson P Costa-Pereira, Clécia Alves da Silva, Anna Karla de Oliveira Tito Borba, Hugo Moura de Albuquerque Melo, Ana Paula de Oliveira Marques
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity remains a major public health concern, particularly among older adults. These concerns are further amplified by the challenges associated with its diagnosis. In this context, anthropometry remains the most practical and widely used method to “identify” obesity. In population-based studies, body mass index (BMI) is commonly accepted; however, its use remains debatable, especially in older populations. In contrast, waist circumference (WC) is more strongly associated with visceral adiposity and, consequently, with greater health repercussions than BMI alone. As this field continues to evolve, studies combining these parameters (i.e., different BMI classifications alongside WC) remain scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the associations of different anthropometric parameters with functional and clinical outcomes in a population-based sample of Brazilian older adults.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from 5,380 community-dwelling older adults (aged ≥ 60 years) from ELSI-Brazil (2019–2021). Anthropometric phenotypes were classified using BMI and WC. Functional outcomes included basic and instrumental activities of daily living, while clinical outcomes consisted of frailty, falls, fractures, and hospitalizations.ResultsThe underweight phenotype was associated with higher odds of frailty (adjusted OR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.2–3.3) and prolonged hospitalization (adjusted OR = 4.1; 95% CI: 1.2–13.4). The combination of underweight by BMI with abdominal obesity (by WC) showed a borderline significant association with functional disability (adjusted OR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.0–10.2). Overweight by BMI was inversely associated with frailty (adjusted OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.5–1.0). Obesity, combining BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 and high WC, was associated with a higher risk of falls (OR = 1.3; 95% CI: 1.1–1.6) and functional impairment in the unadjusted models.ConclusionOur study suggests the clinical relevance of combining anthropometric markers of obesity in community-dwelling older adults. We provide initial evidence that the coexistence of low BMI and high WC, although rare, is suggestive of potential clinical implications due to its association with higher odds of functional impairment. Additionally, both underweight and obesity may offer added clinical value beyond single measures.Keywords: Aging, Body composition, Obesity, Frailty

Introduction

Population aging has intensified worldwide, and advancing age, despite a natural process, is accompanied by significant changes in body composition, including weight loss, reductions in skeletal muscle mass, and increases in adiposity, particularly in visceral and intramuscular depots [1, 2]. These age-related alterations challenge traditional assessments of “nutritional status” in older adults, as body weight alone may not accurately reflect metabolic risk [3].

Different body mass phenotypes (i.e., based on body mass index – BMI), ranging from underweight to obesity, have been associated with impaired immune response, a higher burden of chronic conditions, and adverse effects on functional capacity and quality of life among older adults [2, 3]. However, most population-based studies rely on isolated anthropometric indicators, particularly BMI, which does not distinguish between fat and fat-free mass and may misclassify underlying body composition in older adults [3]. As a result, individuals with “normal” BMI may still present with excess visceral adiposity, a condition often referred to as normal-weight abdominal obesity. This phenotype has been associated with increased cardiometabolic risk [4].

Additional anthropometric measures have been proposed to overcome these limitations. Waist circumference (WC), for instance, has emerged as a complementary marker more closely associated with visceral adiposity and cardiometabolic risk in older adults [5]. However, evidence integrating BMI and WC to characterize anthropometric phenotypes in older populations remains limited. In particular, phenotypes combining different BMI classifications with high WC are rarely explored, and their potential functional and clinical implications are poorly understood. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the associations of combined anthropometric phenotypes with functional and clinical outcomes in a population-based sample of community-dwelling older adults. We hypothesized that integrating BMI and WC would provide additional clinical insight beyond using either measure alone.

Methods

Study design and sample

This study is a cross-sectional analysis of a population-based household survey, from the second wave (2019–2021) of the Brazilian Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSI-Brazil). ELSI is a nationally representative sample of the Brazilian population encompassing 70 municipalities across all five regions of Brazil. To ensure representation of urban and rural areas and municipalities of varying sizes, participants were selected through a multistage probability sampling design that included stratification of primary sampling units (municipalities), census sectors, and households. Detailed information on the study design, recruitment procedures, and data collection has been previously published (Lima-Costa et al.) [6]. For this analysis, individuals aged ≥ 60 years were eligible, while those with incomplete anthropometric data were excluded.

Anthropometric procedures and phenotypes

WC and BMI were measured. WC was measured using a non-elastic tape at the midpoint between the 10th rib and the iliac crest. All measurements were taken at the end of expiration, and the region was free of clothing [7]. WC was classified using cutoff points of 102 cm for males and 88 cm for females [8]. BMI (body weight in kg divided by height m2) was classified according to the World Health Organization criteria [9]. Based on the combination of established BMI and WC cutoff points [8, 9], eight anthropometric phenotypes were defined for comparisons:

Underweight: BMI < 18.5 kg/m² with normal WC.

Underweight: BMI < 18.5 kg/m² with normal WC.

Underweight with abdominal obesity: BMI < 18.5 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Underweight with abdominal obesity: BMI < 18.5 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Eutrophy: BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m² with normal WC.

Eutrophy: BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m² with normal WC.

Normal-weight abdominal obesity: BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Normal-weight abdominal obesity: BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Overweight: BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m² with normal WC.

Overweight: BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m² with normal WC.

Overweight with abdominal obesity: BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Overweight with abdominal obesity: BMI 25.0–29.9 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Obesity: BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m² with normal WC.

Obesity: BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m² with normal WC.

Global and abdominal obesity: BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Global and abdominal obesity: BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m² with elevated WC.

Functional and clinical outcomes

Functionality was assessed based on independence in basic activities of daily living (BADL) using the adapted Katz Index [10, 11] and in instrumental activities of daily living (IADL) using the adapted Lawton and Brody Scale [12, 13]. Functionality was originally classified into three categories:

Functional disability: dependence in both BADL and IADL.

Functional disability: dependence in both BADL and IADL.

Moderate functional capacity: dependence in either BADL or IADL.

Moderate functional capacity: dependence in either BADL or IADL.

Full functional capacity: independence in both. However, for the main analyses, this variable was dichotomized into “functional impairment” (moderate capacity and functional disability) versus “full capacity”. This approach was adopted due to sample size constraints across multiple anthropometric phenotypes and to ensure adequate statistical power and model stability.

Full functional capacity: independence in both. However, for the main analyses, this variable was dichotomized into “functional impairment” (moderate capacity and functional disability) versus “full capacity”. This approach was adopted due to sample size constraints across multiple anthropometric phenotypes and to ensure adequate statistical power and model stability.

Frailty syndrome was assessed using the phenotype proposed by Fried et al. [14], based on the presence of five components: unintentional weight loss, self-reported exhaustion, low physical activity, slow gait speed, and low muscle strength. Individuals presenting three or more components were classified as frail.

Weight loss was identified through self-report of unintentional weight loss in the previous 12 months, exhaustion through self-reported symptoms of fatigue/exhaustion, and low physical activity was assessed using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [6]. Participants reporting < 150 min/week of moderate- and/or vigorous-intensity physical activity were classified as having low physical activity [15]. Low muscle strength was assessed by handgrip strength, and population-specific cutoff points proposed for the Brazilian population were applied (< 30 kg for males and < 16 kg for females) [16], as endorsed by the Brazilian Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition [17]. The walking speed test was used to assess physical performance. However, considering that the ELSI-Brazil study adopted a 3-meter course, in contrast to the traditional 4 meters [18], we adopted the lowest tertile (< 0.23 m/s) to classify our sample.

The number of falls, fractures, and hospitalizations occurring in the 12 months preceding the interview conducted by the ELSI-Brazil research team was also recorded and included in the analyses. Prolonged hospitalization was defined as at least one self-reported hospital stay lasting ≥ 7 consecutive days.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

The following parameters were considered: region of residence (North, Northeast, Midwest, South, and Southeast); age (in complete years); sex (male or female); education (years of schooling completed); household income (in minimum wages); and self-reported previous medical diagnosis of the following conditions: systemic arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, arthritis or rheumatism, chronic kidney disease, heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease or other dementias, osteoporosis, depression, psychiatric disorders, and cancer.

Statistics

Data were analyzed using Stata version 15 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA) with the survey module to account for the complex sampling design and sampling weights. Descriptive sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were presented as weighted prevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Associations of anthropometric phenotypes (exposure) with functional and clinical outcomes (outcomes) were assessed using weighted binary logistic regression with the svy command. The phenotype characterized by normal BMI and WC (i.e., eutrophy) was used as the reference group. Crude and adjusted models were fitted to account for potential confounding. Variable selection/Confounders were identified using a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) constructed in DAGitty (https://www.dagitty.net/dags.html ) and included age, sex, income, and number of comorbidities (Fig. 1). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Population representativeness was ensured using sampling weights that accounted for unequal selection probabilities, non-response, and oversampling of specific subgroups. All analyses incorporated these weights and the complex survey design to ensure statistical validity. No additional sample size calculation was performed. Analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach after excluding participants with missing anthropometric or hand grip strength data. Collinearity was not an issue (all variance inflation factors < 3).

Ethics

The ELSI-Brazil study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the René Rachou Institute, Oswaldo Cruz Foundation, Minas Gerais (CAAE 34649814.3.0000.5091). All participants provided written informed consent before interviews and physical assessments.

Results

A total of 5,380 individuals were included in the analysis, representing an estimated 22,644,439 Brazilian older adults (Fig. 2). Sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. The prevalence of anthropometric phenotypes varied across regions (Fig. 3; Table 2). The Northeast showed the highest prevalence of underweight, whereas the Central-West presented the highest prevalence of underweight with abdominal obesity. Eutrophy (normal BMI and WC) was most prevalent in the Northeast and least prevalent in the South. For overweight without abdominal obesity, the highest prevalence was observed in the North, while the lowest was found in the Central-West.

For the overweight with abdominal obesity phenotype, the highest prevalences were observed in the South and Southeast. Normal BMI with abdominal obesity was most prevalent in the Central-West, whereas lower prevalences were observed in the remaining regions. The highest prevalence of obesity by BMI occurred in the North, while the lowest was found in the Central-West. The global and abdominal obesity phenotype was most prevalent in the South, followed by the Southeast. Overall, the findings showed marked regional heterogeneity in anthropometric phenotypes among Brazilian older adults, with differences in the distribution of nutritional and cardiometabolic risk across regions.

Table 3 shows the associations between phenotypes and functional outcomes. Underweight was associated with higher odds of frailty (adjusted OR = 2.0; 95% CI: 1.2–3.3; p = 0.013). Underweight with abdominal obesity was not associated with frailty in the adjusted analysis (adjusted OR: 2.3; 95% CI: 0.7–7.9; p = 0.159), although a significant association was observed in the unadjusted model (OR: 3.1; 95% CI: 1.1–9.6; p = 0.042). Overweight by BMI showed a borderline inverse association with frailty (adjusted OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.5–1.0; p = 0.056), indicating a possible but limited protective effect. Underweight with abdominal obesity showed a borderline independent association with functional impairment (adjusted OR = 3.1; 95% CI: 1.0–10.2; p = 0.052). Global + abdominal obesity was associated with higher odds of functional impairment in the unadjusted model (OR = 1.5; 95% CI: 1.1–2.1; p = 0.008), but this association was attenuated after adjustment (adjusted OR: 1.3; 95% CI: 0.9–1.9; p = 0.103). The same phenotype was also associated with higher odds of falls in the unadjusted analysis (OR = 1.3; 95% CI: 1.1–1.6; p = 0.022), whereas no other significant associations were observed (adjusted OR: 1.1; 95% CI: 0.8–1.4; p = 0.386), suggesting a limited contribution of this phenotype to fall risk.

Table 4 presents the associations with clinical outcomes. In the unadjusted analysis, underweight by BMI was associated with higher odds of prolonged hospitalization (OR = 4.0; 95% CI: 1.2–12.8; p = 0.016), while the global + abdominal obesity phenotype was associated with lower odds of hip or femur fracture (OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.6–0.9; p = 0.010), which may reflect a potential inverse relationship between higher body mass and bone-related outcomes, although this finding should be interpreted with caution. For wrist or forearm fractures, in the unadjusted model, being overweight by BMI alone was associated with higher odds (OR = 1.3; 95% CI: 1.1–1.9; p = 0.030), whereas global and abdominal obesity showed lower odds (OR = 0.7; 95% CI: 0.6–0.9; p = 0.044). After adjustment, most associations were attenuated, except for underweight, which remained independently associated with prolonged hospitalization (adjusted OR = 4.1; 95% CI: 1.2–13.4; p = 0.021), highlighting a clinically meaningful association with adverse outcomes.

Discussion

Our findings suggest that distinct anthropometric phenotypes are differentially associated with functional or clinical outcomes in older adults, reinforcing the relevance of anthropometric assessment beyond BMI alone. Phenotypes reflecting nutritional depletion (i.e., underweight) and abnormal body composition (i.e., high abdominal obesity) appear to confer increased vulnerability among community-dwelling older Brazilians.

Our main results indicate that underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m²) was independently associated with frailty, supporting previous evidence that low body weight in later life reflects reduced physiological reserves and increased vulnerability [13, 14]. Similar findings have been reported in Brazilian populations, including the FIBRA study, in which frail and pre-frail individuals showed a higher prevalence of underweight [19], and in systematic reviews demonstrating a consistent association between underweight and frailty among community-dwelling older adults [20].

The association between underweight and frailty may reflect a bidirectional process, as older adults with underweight often present malnutrition, which is linked to functional decline and adverse health outcomes [21, 22], while frailty itself may contribute to inadequate intake through appetite loss and reduced functional capacity [20, 23]. These shared features help explain the robustness of this association across different populations.

A relevant additional finding of this study was the identification and prevalence of older adults presenting with underweight and abdominal obesity, a phenotype that remains poorly explored in population-based studies. Although relatively uncommon, this phenotype was associated with frailty in the unadjusted analysis and with functional impairment after adjustment (borderline significance). These results suggest potential clinical relevance, particularly given that this phenotype reflects a discordant body composition characterized by low body mass and increased central adiposity [24]. These observed associations are consistent with previous evidence highlighting the role of abdominal adiposity as a marker of metabolic risk and functional decline, supporting the use of WC as a complementary anthropometric indicator in geriatric assessment [25–29].

Collectively, our findings reinforce that reliance on BMI alone may obscure relevant body composition patterns associated with adverse outcomes. However, it may also present clinical relevance on a population level. For instance, overweight (BMI 25–29.9 kg/m²) showed a borderline inverse association with frailty, in line with previous studies reporting lower frailty prevalence among individuals in this BMI range [26–28]. This range may reflect greater muscle and energy reserves in older adults [30–32]; however, excess adiposity may still adversely affect physical performance and metabolic health [33], which helps explain why optimal BMI classification in older adults remains an ongoing debate.

Our additional findings demonstrated that the phenotype of global and abdominal obesity was associated with functional impairment and falls in unadjusted analyses, but these associations were attenuated after adjustment, suggesting that intermediary factors may partly explain these relationships [34, 35]. Overall, our results support a U-shaped relationship between body mass and functional outcomes, in which both underweight and obesity are potentially associated with increased functional limitations [36–38].

Additional results reflect regional differences in the distribution of anthropometric phenotypes, highlighting the potential influence of social and economic determinants. The higher prevalence of underweight in the Northeast may reflect persistent social inequalities and food insecurity [39, 40], whereas the predominance of global and abdominal obesity in the South and Southeast suggests a higher burden of metabolic risk in these regions [41–43].

Strengths of this study include the national representativeness of ELSI-Brazil and the use of combined, novel anthropometric phenotypes. However, some limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Reduced precision was observed for less prevalent phenotypes, and the relatively large number of individuals excluded due to missing HGS data may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, outcomes such as falls, fractures, and hospitalizations were self-reported and therefore subject to recall bias. Potential residual overadjustment in the analytical models should also be considered, as the inclusion of comorbidities may have partially accounted for pathways linking anthropometric phenotypes to the outcomes, despite no evidence of numerical collinearity.

Furthermore, some exposure–outcome combinations resulted in sparse data, leading to unstable estimates and wide confidence intervals. In certain cases, model convergence was not achieved, and corresponding estimates could not be reported. These issues likely reflect the limited number of events within specific phenotype groups and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The absence of formal correction for multiple testing is another limitation, as multiple comparisons may increase the risk of type I error, particularly for associations with borderline statistical significance. In addition, the use of WHO BMI cut-off points, rather than those proposed by the Pan American Health Organization [44] for older adults, may have influenced the classification of overweight and, consequently, the observed associations. Despite these limitations, the identification and prevalence of underweight with abdominal obesity represent an important contribution, highlighting a rarely explored profile and supporting more comprehensive approaches to anthropometric assessment in older adults.

Future studies should explore different potential anthropometric combinations, including indices based on body proportions such as waist-to-height ratio or waist-to-hip ratio, to better understand their complementary value in risk stratification among older adults.

Conclusion

Distinct anthropometric phenotypes were associated with different functional and clinical outcomes in Brazilian older adults. Underweight showed consistent associations with adverse outcomes, while the identification of underweight with abdominal obesity (i.e., using high WC) highlights a rarely reported phenotype with potential clinical relevance. Although some associations were attenuated after statistical adjustment and require confirmation in longitudinal studies, these findings support the use of combined anthropometric measures to improve risk assessment in older populations.
