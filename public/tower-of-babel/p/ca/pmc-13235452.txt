Phase 1 study of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide for patients with relapsed/refractory diffuse large B-cell lymphoma
Authors: Zheng Song, Ying Cheng, Haiyan Yang, Liling Zhang, Liqun Zou, Ye Guo, Junning Cao, Huiqiang Huang, Zhao Wang, Sha Huang, Yiqian Fang, Jiaoyan Lyu, Yiting Zhang, Yang Liu, Keshu Zhou, Huilai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide combination therapy demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity with durable responses across DLBCL subtypes.•This all-oral combination has a favorable risk-benefit profile and may be a potential option for patients ineligible for other therapies.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) is the most common form of non-Hodgkin lymphoma, comprising 30% to 50% of all cases globally.1 Standard first-line chemoimmunotherapy for DLBCL includes R-CHOP (rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone); Pola-R-CHP (polatuzumab vedotin plus rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin and prednisone) has been approved recently as first-line treatment in countries including the United States, United Kingdom, and China.2, 3, 4, 5, 6 Although these regimens can improve survival in treatment-naive patients,7 those with relapsed or refractory (R/R) disease generally have poor prognosis, with decreasing response rates, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS) with each successive line of treatment.8 For patients with R/R disease, high-dose chemotherapy (HDT) and an autologous stem cell transplant (SCT) may be used as second-line treatment, but this treatment is limited to eligible patients with few comorbidities.7 Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy is another second-line treatment option with potential to induce long-term survival, but these therapies may not be accessible to many patients.9 Pola-BR (polatuzumab vedotin plus bendamustine plus rituximab); glofitamab plus gemcitabine plus oxaliplatin; epcoritamab, tafasitamab plus lenalidomide; and other regimens in development are potential options for patients ineligible for autologous SCT; however, due to the risk of toxicity and potential side effects, they are not suitable for all patients and may not be effective for some patients due to tumor heterogeneity.7 The pursuit of effective chemotherapy-free treatment options for R/R DLBCL in patients ineligible for HDT/SCT is longstanding. Despite recent treatment advances, a need remains for novel, safe and effective treatments with different mechanisms of action.

In recent years, the identification of DLBCL subtypes with distinct molecular and genetic profiles has provided additional rationale for the development of novel targeted treatment strategies for DLBCL. Approximately 80% of DLBCLs can be categorized as 1 of 2 cell-of-origin subtypes, germinal center B-cell like (GCB) or activated B-cell like (ABC),10 the latter of which is associated with markedly poorer outcomes with first-line R-CHOP.11,12 The greater chemoimmunotherapy resistance observed in ABC DLBCL may be explained by its selective dependence on constitutive B-cell receptor and NF-κB signaling for survival13; blocking those pathways with targeted agents may be a more effective therapeutic approach than R-CHOP for this DLBCL subset. Although GCB DLBCL does not strictly rely on B-cell receptor– and NF-κB–mediated signaling for malignant transformation in the same way,14 dysregulation of these oncogenic pathways does occur in this disease subset, suggesting that it may also be sensitive to targeted therapy.

Zanubrutinib is a next-generation Bruton tyrosine kinase (BTK) inhibitor that was designed with greater potency and specificity than ibrutinib for the purpose of limiting off-target effects and for efficacy across B-cell malignancies.15 In a phase 2 trial, zanubrutinib monotherapy was generally well tolerated and demonstrated an overall response rate (ORR) of 29.3%, with a median duration of response (DOR) of 4.5 months in patients with R/R non-GCB DLBCL.16 Treatment with lenalidomide, an immunomodulatory drug with antitumor activity in multiple hematologic malignancies, is associated with ORRs of 27.5% to 34.2% when administered as monotherapy for R/R DLBCL, with median DOR ranging from 4.6 to 17.0 months.17, 18, 19 In preclinical studies, the combination of lenalidomide and a BTK inhibitor had synergistic activity against DLBCL cells,20,21 suggesting that the combination of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide might have a greater antitumor effect than zanubrutinib alone.

Based on the acceptable safety profiles and antitumor activity observed in previous trials of each treatment as monotherapy, a phase 1 study was conducted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of combination treatment with zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide in patients with R/R DLBCL.

Methods

Study design and participants

BGB-3111-110 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04436107) was an open-label, multicenter, phase 1, dose-escalation (part 1) and dose-expansion (part 2) study conducted in China to assess zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide in patients with R/R DLBCL. Eligible patients were aged ≥18 years with histologically confirmed DLBCL that was characterized as stable or progressive disease after completion of the most recent treatment before study entry or that had progressed after remission with the most recent treatment. Patients must have received ≥1 line of adequate systemic anti-DLBCL therapy, defined as an anti-CD20 antibody and an anthracycline-based combination therapy for at least 2 cycles; been ineligible for a HDT/SCT if not received previously; had no previous exposure to a BTK inhibitor, lenalidomide, or thalidomide; had no history of or current central nervous system lymphoma; and did not have histologically transformed lymphoma.

In the part 1 dose-escalation study, zanubrutinib was administered orally at the fixed dose of 160 mg twice daily, and lenalidomide was administered orally at doses of 15, 20, or 25 mg on days 1 to 21 of each 28-day cycle. Lenalidomide dose escalation proceeded according to 3+3 rules to assess the maximum tolerated dose. In addition to the number of enrolled patients required by 3+3 rules, additional patients were enrolled in part 1 according to safety monitoring committee recommendations to allow for comprehensive evaluation of the safety and efficacy of each dose level before the recommended phase 2 dose (RP2D) was determined. Dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were assessed for 28 days after the first dose; the definitions of hematologic and nonhematologic DLTs are included in the supplemental Methods. Treatment with zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide continued until disease progression or intolerability. In the part 2 dose-expansion study, zanubrutinib 160 mg twice daily was administered in combination with lenalidomide at the RP2D determined in part 1. Study treatment continued until disease progression or intolerability.

Study assessments and end points

The primary end points for part 1 were the safety of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide as assessed by National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.022 and identification of the RP2D for lenalidomide. The primary end point for part 2 was the ORR, defined as the proportion of patients with a best overall response of partial response or complete response (CR) based on the Lugano classification.23 For response assessments, computed tomography (CT) with contrast was performed at screening, every 12 weeks for the first 48 weeks, every 16 weeks for the next 48 weeks, and every 24 weeks thereafter. A positron emission tomography (PET)/CT scan or PET scan was performed at screening. For patients with PET-avid disease at screening, PET and CT scans were performed on the same visits. Assessments of CR were confirmed by PET scan.

DLBCL subtyping

Fresh tumor biopsies were collected at screening from all patients unless archival tumor tissue was available. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) DLBCL subtyping data were collected from local laboratories when available. For patients without local IHC data, unstained formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue specimens were prepared by a central laboratory (KingMyLab, Shanghai, China). After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, unstained FFPE slides were separately stained with antibodies to CD10, B-cell lymphoma 6 (BCL6), and multiple myeloma oncogene 1 using the Ventana HE 600 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Expression of CD10, BCL6, and multiple myeloma oncogene 1 was evaluated microscopically by a pathologist. Each sample was classified as GCB DLBCL or non-GCB DLBCL using the Hans algorithm.24

Cell-of-origin gene expression profiling (GEP) was conducted at a central laboratory by loading tumor cell lysates from unstained FFPE slides in an HTG EdgeSeq processor (HTG Molecular Diagnostics, Tucson, AZ). Sequence-specific probes were used to bind and protect select RNA sequences in a quantitative nuclease protection sequencing reaction. The protected probes then underwent polymerase chain reaction amplification with barcoded-adapter addition, library bead cleanup, and quantitative polymerase chain reaction. The cleaned libraries were normalized, pooled, and sequenced (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The resulting FASTQ files were parsed using EdgeSeq host software to align reads to panel genes and count their abundance; read counts or expression levels were generated for the genes of interest. Each sample was categorized as ABC DLBCL, GCB DLBCL, or unclassified.

For DLBCL genetic subtyping assays, genomic DNA (gDNA) from FFPE tumor tissue was extracted using FirePure FFPE gDNA extraction kit (FireGen Biomedicals, Beijing, China). gDNA was sheared to 200– to 250–base pair fragments; then 200 ng of gDNA was captured with 413 gene capture probes for library construction and sequenced on Gene+seq2000 or DNBSEQ-T7 (PE100) (GenePlus, Beijing, China). The clean reads were aligned to the reference human genome (hg19) with the Burrows-Wheeler aligner tool.25 Variants were called with realDCaller2 (GenePlus) and TNscope. CNVkit was used to detect copy-number variants,26 and NCsv (in-house software) was used to detect structural variants. The final candidate variants were all manually verified using the Integrative Genomics Viewer.27 Samples from patients treated at the RP2D were assigned to DLBCL subtypes that included TP53MUT, MCD-like (hallmark mutations: MYD88 and CD79B mutations), BN2-like (hallmark mutations: BCL6 fusion and NOTCH2 mutations), N1-like (hallmark mutations: NOTCH1 mutations), EZB-like (hallmark mutations: EZH2 mutations and BCL2 fusion), and ST2-like (hallmark mutations: SGK1 and TET2 mutations) using the LymphPlex algorithm.28,29

PKs

Blood samples were collected to characterize the pharmacokinetic (PK) profiles of zanubrutinib and lenalidomide after single doses and at steady state in all patients in part 1 and the first 12 patients enrolled in stage 1 of part 2. Intensive PK profiling was conducted in patients on days 1 and 21 of cycle 1. Plasma samples were analyzed using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry method. PK parameters were estimated based on noncompartmental analysis methods and were computed using Phoenix WinNonlin version 8.0 or higher (Certara, Radnor, PA) with actual dose and blood draw times. PK parameters were summarized by treatment using descriptive statistics. Possible drug-drug interactions between zanubrutinib and lenalidomide were evaluated in an exploratory analysis by comparing the PK data generated in this study with historical data.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and baseline characteristics and safety data were summarized descriptively. For response rates, 2-sided Clopper-Pearson 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were generated. For part 2, a Simon 2-stage design was used.30 The null hypothesis (H0: ORR of 25%) was tested against a 1-sided alternative hypothesis (Ha: ORR of >25%). A binomial exact test was used for hypothesis testing. Time-to-event analyses, including DOR, PFS, and OS, used the Kaplan-Meier method and the Brookmeyer and Crowley method for generating 2-sided 95% CIs. Landmark rates for DOR, PFS, and OS were estimated at selected time points using the Kaplan-Meier method, and their corresponding 95% CIs were calculated using the Greenwood formula.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Guidelines for good clinical practice. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before participation in any study procedures, and institutional review board approval was obtained at each study site.

Results

A total of 66 patients from 10 centers across China were enrolled in the dose-escalation (part 1; n = 27) and dose-expansion (part 2; n = 39) groups, and all received treatment. In part 1, lenalidomide was administered once daily at dose levels of 15 mg (n = 6), 20 mg (n = 10), and 25 mg (n = 11) in combination with zanubrutinib. In all patients, the median study follow-up was 16.5 months (range, 0.5-41.6). Study patients had a median age of 59 years, 53.0% were male, and the median number of previous lines of therapy was 2 (Table 1). Of the total study population, 83.3% had stage III or IV disease, 42.4% had refractory disease, and 54.6% had extranodal lesions.

DLBCL subtyping by IHC classified 65.2% of patients as having non-GCB DLBCL and 34.8% as having GCB disease. GEP subtyping identified ABC DLBCL in 66.7% of patients and GCB DLBCL in 21.2%. Of 50 patients treated at the RP2D, genetic subtyping was successfully performed in 45 patients. Of 45 tested patients, 66.7% could be assigned a genetic subtype based on the LymphPlex algorithm, most commonly TP53MUT, present in 28.9% of patients, followed by MCD-like (15.6%) and EZB-like (15.4%) (supplemental Figure 1).

Safety

In all 66 patients treated in parts 1 and 2, the median duration of exposure to zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide was 4.9 months. In the part 1 dose escalation, no DLTs occurred at any lenalidomide dose level tested up to 25 mg once daily; thus, the maximum tolerated dose was not reached. Based on a comprehensive evaluation of part 1 safety and efficacy data by the safety monitoring committee, lenalidomide 25 mg once daily was determined to be the RP2D in combination with zanubrutinib.

Across dose cohorts, all patients had ≥1 treatment-emergent adverse event (TEAE) of any grade. Overall, patients treated with the lenalidomide R2PD vs lenalidomide 20 mg once daily experienced similar frequencies of grade ≥3 TEAEs (76.0% vs 70.0%, respectively), serious TEAEs (36.0% vs 30.0%), and TEAEs leading to treatment discontinuation (10.0% vs 20.0%) or interruption (68.0% vs 60.0%; Table 2). Two grade 5 TEAEs occurred (3.0%); cardiopulmonary failure in 1 patient (20-mg dose level; considered unrelated to study treatment by the investigator and likely due to disease progression; primary cause of death was DLBCL) and pneumonia in 1 patient (25-mg dose level; considered unrelated to study treatment by the investigator; the patient had an ongoing medical history of pneumoconiosis and a recurrent cough; primary cause of death was DLBCL). Of 50 patients treated at the lenalidomide RP2D, 7 (14.0%) experienced a TEAE leading to lenalidomide dose reduction, but no patient required zanubrutinib dose reduction. Of all patients, 5 (7.6%) discontinued study treatment due to TEAEs that were considered treatment related: decreased platelet count (n = 2), pulmonary embolism (n = 1), incomplete intestinal obstruction (n = 1), and rash (n = 1).

The most common TEAEs in all patients were largely hematologic; decreased neutrophil count was the most common all-grade and grade ≥3 TEAE, occurring in 77.3% and 57.6% of patients, respectively; neutropenia occurred in 4.5% of patients (all grade ≥3; Table 3). One patient experienced dose reduction (lenalidomide) due to grade 4 platelet count decrease without hemorrhage. Infections occurred in 28 patients (all-grade, 42.4%; grade ≥3, 13.6%). The most common type of infection was pneumonia (all-grade, 21.2%; grade ≥3, 10.6%); however, 22.7% of patients had a medical history of pneumonia before study enrollment, including ongoing low-grade pneumonia that did not require IV anti-infective therapy at the time of enrollment. All-grade liver function test elevations were seen in 20% to 30% of patients; however, grade ≥3 events remained very low at all doses tested. Serious TEAEs that occurred in ≥4% of patients were pneumonia (12.1%), and decreased neutrophil count and decreased platelet count (both 6.1%). Of the TEAEs of interest for the BTK inhibitor class, grade 1 or 2 hemorrhage occurred in 4 patients (6.1%), and no grade ≥3 TEAEs of hemorrhage occurred. Hypertension occurred in 4 patients (6.1%; grade 2, n = 3; grade 3, n = 1). No cases of atrial fibrillation or second primary malignancies occurred.

PKs

Single-dose and steady-state zanubrutinib plasma exposures with zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide were comparable with those observed for zanubrutinib monotherapy and indicated minimal impact of lenalidomide on zanubrutinib exposure. Zanubrutinib was rapidly absorbed after oral administration. With single dose and at steady state of zanubrutinib 160 mg twice daily, the median time to maximum drug concentration (Tmax) was 2.0 and 2.5 hours on days 1 and 21 of cycle 1, respectively; geometric mean maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) with geometric coefficient of variation (GCV) was 281.2 ng/mL (GCV, 85%) and 208.7 ng/mL (GCV, 61%); and estimated area under the curve to last measurable concentration was 798.4 ng·h/mL (GCV, 94%) and 638.4 ng·h/mL (GCV, 57%; supplemental Table 1).

Lenalidomide was rapidly absorbed after oral administration, with a median Tmax of 1.5 to 2.1 hours after a single dose and multiple doses of 15 to 25 mg once daily. Cmax was 332.0 ng/mL (GCV, 123%) and 372.1 ng/mL (GCV, 67%) after a single dose and multiple doses of lenalidomide 25 mg, respectively. The area under the curve extrapolated to infinity was 1752.4 ng·h/mL (GCV, 28%) after single-dose lenalidomide 25 mg. These data are consistent with published data for lenalidomide monotherapy and indicate that zanubrutinib had minimal impact on lenalidomide exposure.

Mean concentration-time plots for plasma zanubrutinib and lenalidomide are presented in supplemental Figure 2.

Response rates

Overall, ORR was 50.0% with a CR rate of 34.8% in all patients. In 50 patients who received lenalidomide at the R2PD, the ORR was 58.0% (29/50), with a CR rate of 42.0% (21/50; Figure 1A). In part 1 dose escalation, ORRs for 15-mg, 20-mg, and 25-mg dose cohorts were 16.7% (1/6), 30.0% (3/10), and 90.9% (10/11), respectively, with CR rates of 16.7% (1/6), 10.0% (1/10), and 72.7% (8/11), respectively. In part 2, ORR was 48.7% (19/39), with a CR rate of 33.3% (13/39). At the lenalidomide RP2D, ORRs were generally consistent in all assessed subgroups based on baseline patient and disease characteristics (Figure 2).

For IHC-derived DLBCL subtypes, ORRs at the lenalidomide RP2D were numerically higher in patients with non-GCB DLBCL (61.8%) compared with GCB DLBCL (50.0%), although these differences were not statistically validated (Figure 1B). CR rates were numerically higher in patients with GCB DLBCL (50.0%) vs non-GCB DLBCL (38.2%). Similarly, GEP-derived subtyping demonstrated that patients with ABC DLBCL had a numerically higher ORR (68.6%) than those with GCB DLBCL (45.5%), although CR rates were consistent across both subtypes (ABC, 45.7%; GCB, 45.5%). At the lenalidomide RP2D, most patients with non-TP53MUT DLBCL subtypes had numerically higher ORRs and CR rates than those with TP53MUT subtypes (Figure 1C).

Time-to-event outcomes

Time-to-event analyses in patients who received lenalidomide at the RP2D are shown in Figure 3. The median DOR was 14.9 months (95% CI, 5.5 to not estimable [NE]; Figure 3A), and the 12-month DOR event-free rate was 53.3%. Patients had a median time to response of 2.8 months (range, 2.5-5.6). The median PFS was 5.5 months (95% CI, 2.9-11.1; Figure 3B). The 12-month PFS event-free rate was 34.4%. Median OS was NE; the estimated 12-month OS event-free rate was 73.8% (Figure 3C).

Discussion

The pursuit of effective, easily administered chemotherapy-free treatment options for patients with R/R DLBCL who are ineligible for HDT/SCT is longstanding. The aim of this phase 1 study in patients with R/R DLBCL was to evaluate combination therapy with zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide, both of which are orally administered treatments that have demonstrated acceptable tolerability and antitumor activity as monotherapy in previous clinical studies.16, 17, 18, 19

At baseline, the disease characteristics of the study population were consistent with what would be expected in the R/R DLBCL setting.31 Both methods of performing DLBCL subtyping indicated that approximately two-thirds of patients had non-GCB or ABC DLBCL. This is consistent with the higher proportion of patients with ABC DLBCL observed in the Asia-Pacific region relative to most other regions of the world, in which GCB DLBCL is more prominent.32

In this study, the combination of zanubrutinib 160 mg twice daily plus lenalidomide 25 mg once daily (RP2D) was generally well tolerated, with safety profiles consistent with those observed for zanubrutinib and lenalidomide in previous studies. The dose-escalation portion of the study showed that increasing the lenalidomide dose did not result in significant increases in the frequency of any-grade TEAEs, serious TEAEs, or TEAEs leading to discontinuation. In this study, the frequency of grade ≥3 TEAEs at the RP2D (76.0%) was numerically higher than that seen in a previous phase 2 zanubrutinib study in patients with R/R DLBCL (48.8%)16 but was similar to data from a phase 2/3 lenalidomide study in patients with R/R DLBCL (79.6%).19 The grade ≥3 TEAEs observed in this study were most often hematologic and generally manageable with concomitant medication and/or dose modification. Treatment discontinuations due to TEAEs occurred in 7 patients (10.6%); the only event observed in ≥1 patient was decreased platelet count, which occurred in 2 patients. It is notable that the frequency of TEAEs leading to discontinuation in this continuous treatment regimen is similar to that in the phase 1b/2 study in which patients with R/R DLBCL received fixed-duration ibrutinib and lenalidomide with dose-adjusted EPOCH-R (etoposide, prednisone, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and rituximab; TEAEs led to ibrutinib and lenalidomide discontinuation in 12% of patients each), whereas serious TEAEs were more frequent (having occurred in 73%-93% of patients) with chemotherapy-containing therapy.33 Another phase 1b/2 study of fixed-duration ViPOR (venetoclax, ibrutinib, prednisone, obinutuzumab, and lenalidomide) in patients with R/R DLBCL showed a slightly lower rate of TEAEs leading to discontinuation (8.3%); however, the frequency of serious TEAEs with ViPOR (42%) was higher than that of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide in this study.34

At the lenalidomide RP2D, the combination of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide demonstrated encouraging antitumor activity. The observed ORR and CR rates of 58.0% and 42.0%, respectively, compared favorably with results of clinical studies of zanubrutinib monotherapy (ORR, 29%; CR, 17%)16 and lenalidomide monotherapy (ORR, 27%-34%; CR, 7%-13%).17, 18, 19 Additionally, the observed responses were durable, with a median DOR of 14.9 months. The response rates and DOR observed with zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide in this phase 1 study were comparable with those seen with some other treatments in development for R/R DLBCL, including tisagenlecleucel (phase 2: ORR, 52%; CR, 40%; median DOR, not reached),35 odronextamab (pooled phase 1 and 2: ORR, 51%; CR, 32%; median DOR, 10.5 months),36 and loncastuximab tesirine (phase 2: ORR, 48%; CR, 24%; median DOR, 10.3 months).37 Regimens that include chemotherapy have demonstrated similar or higher ORRs than zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide, including polatuzumab vedotin plus bendamustine and rituximab (phase 1b/2: ORR, 56.6%; CR, 52.8%; median DOR, 9.5 months),31 glofitamab plus gemcitabine and oxaliplatin (phase 3: ORR, 68.3%; CR, 58.5%; median DOR, not reached),38 and ibrutinib and lenalidomide with dose-adjusted EPOCH-R (phase 1b/2: ORR, 62%; CR, 19%; median DOR in responders, 3.9 months).33 However, the oral chemotherapy-free combination of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide, with comparable or slightly lower response rates and durable DOR, is still a promising treatment option for patients ineligible for HDT/SCT.

The ORR benefits seen with zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide were generally consistent across subgroups based on baseline patient and disease characteristics, as well as DLBCL subtypes. It is particularly encouraging that benefits were observed in both GCB and non-GCB or ABC subtypes, because ABC DLBCL is more resistant to chemoimmunotherapy than GCB DLBCL. Moreover, response rates with the 2-drug combination of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide were similar to results with the 3-drug combination of ibrutinib, lenalidomide, and rituximab in DLBCL in a phase 2 study (ORR, 44%; CR, 28%),39 and similar to 5-drug combination of ViPOR (ORR, 54%; CR, 38%).34 Although these findings must be validated in further studies, these data suggest that it may be possible to reduce the patient burden traditionally associated with infusion therapy by using newer treatment regimens that consist solely of orally administered drugs.

In summary, in this phase 1 dose-escalation/expansion study of zanubrutinib plus lenalidomide treatment in patients with R/R DLBCL, combination treatment at the RP2D had an acceptable safety profile and encouraging antitumor activity. ORR benefits were observed in patients with a range of disease characteristics and DLBCL cell-of-origin subtypes. These findings highlight the great potential for this orally administered combination treatment to be a more convenient option for patients compared with standard chemoimmunotherapy or other regimens with infusion drugs. Future studies will further investigate resistance biomarkers and mechanisms of disease.
