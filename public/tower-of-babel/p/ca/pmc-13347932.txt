When network biology meets human genetics
Authors: Sai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rare variant association analyses are typically performed at the single-gene level, overlooking the molecular interactions that organize cellular systems. In this issue, Nazeen et al. introduce NERINE, a probabilistic rare variant burden test that integrates gene and protein networks to improve statistical power and biological interpretability.

Main text

Rare variant association studies (RVASs), complementing genome-wide association studies (GWASs), examine associations between rare genetic variants (e.g., variants with minor allele frequency [MAF] below 1% in a given population) and human quantitative traits and diseases. Unlike GWASs, RVASs are constrained by the low frequency of the variants they interrogate and, consequently, by limited statistical power. A common strategy is to aggregate variants across functional units such as genes or proteins, giving rise to a basket of tools including CMC,1 SKAT,2 SKAT-O,3 and STAAR.4 Yet genes and proteins do not act in isolation. They cooperate through interactions that form biological hierarchies, from complexes and pathways to broader biological processes. Indeed, genetic studies formulated on biological networks have revealed the convergence of disease risk variants onto gene modules.5,6 This systems-level complexity, long central to systems biology but largely overlooked by conventional RVASs, is brought into rare variant genetics by Nazeen et al. to increase both discovery power and interpretability.

In this issue, Nazeen et al. develop NERINE,7 a statistical framework that uses the topology of gene and protein networks to refine rare variant association testing (Figure 1). NERINE models gene-level effects with a multivariate skew-normal distribution, incorporating gene and protein interactions into the scale matrix. Rare variant counts for each gene are modeled as Poisson random variables controlled by the corresponding gene effect. The key parameter is the network effect, which is tested through maximum-likelihood estimation and a log-likelihood ratio statistic. For significant networks, NERINE estimates the most likely gene-level effects. Schematic of NERINE

NERINE is a rare variant association test that evaluates whether rare variants are enriched across a biologically connected gene or protein network. It incorporates network topology to borrow information across related genes, improving statistical power and helping prioritize disease-relevant pathways and modules.

NERINE outperforms traditional rare variant tests. In simulations, NERINE consistently yielded higher empirical power than conventional methods and than NERINE runs with randomized networks, supporting the value of explicitly modeling biological topology in RVASs. This biologically informed modeling also facilitates downstream interpretation. Pathways, protein interactions, and gene co-expression networks encode different layers of molecular and cellular organization; by mapping NERINE-derived network effects and gene effects back onto these structures, the model can highlight disease-relevant biology. Nazeen et al. demonstrate this by linking liver co-expression networks to HDL cholesterol, DNA repair, cell-cycle regulation, and hormone signaling pathways to breast cancer, and lipid metabolism, immune response, blood coagulation, and apoptosis to cardiovascular diseases.

Beyond recovering known associations, NERINE also reveals new disease biology. In breast cancer, it identifies a significant rare variant burden in the estrogen receptor regulation pathway that is nominally independent of BRCA1. In coronary artery disease (CAD) and myocardial infarction (MI), it prioritizes the alternative complement pathway through significantly elevated rare variant burden. Although these discoveries have partial or indirect support from prior work, they nominate targets for future validation and potential therapeutic development.

In addition to observational data, perturbation experiments focused on disease-specific pathological hallmarks provide another resource for disentangling causal biology from genetic association. Loss-of-function (LoF) variants can be viewed as natural gene perturbations, albeit in a setting complicated by genetic background, cellular context, and environment. Wet-lab screening assays offer a simplified yet specified readout of gene perturbation in a dish. Combining these two sources of evidence is a promising strategy for causal mapping in disease biology.8 Nazeen et al. demonstrate this principle in Parkinson’s disease (PD), where NERINE tests network modules derived from experimental screens for dopaminergic (DA) neuron survival and α-synuclein (αS) proteotoxicity. Although gene-level RVASs remain underpowered for PD, NERINE identifies high-burden networks linked to autophagy regulation, vesicle trafficking, and protein homeostasis. The NERINE-prioritized association between damaging missense variants at PRL and PD risk is supported by an independent functional screen in a CRISPRi-induced synucleinopathy cortical neuron (CiS-CN) model and by subsequent validation in a chronic fibrillar αS mouse model. Together, these results suggest an unexpected intraneuronal role for PRL in the αS stress response in PD.

Causal inference promises a principled way to search for needles in the haystack of biological associations. Yet causal inference in biology remains challenging because biological datasets are heterogeneous and complex, and because generating data that are sufficiently informative for causal models is often demanding. Data integration through machine learning or statistical modeling offers an alternative strategy: narrowing the search space so that putative causal signals can be more efficiently located and tested. NERINE provides a compelling example of this strategy by integrating human genetics, systems biology, and experimental biology to identify disease-causing genes and pathways.

The story does not stop here. NERINE opens several avenues for future genetic studies. First, common and rare variants are typically studied separately in disease genetics, and whether they converge or diverge across pathways, cell types, and other biological contexts remains underexplored. Together with network-based GWASs, NERINE could help determine whether common and rare variants converge on shared biological programs or implicate distinct disease mechanisms. Second, NERINE primarily analyzes LoF and missense variants; how to formulate noncoding variants within the NERINE framework remains an open question. Because noncoding variants act in highly context-dependent ways, such analyses could generate finer dissections of disease genetic architecture. Finally, traditional genetic risk models such as polygenic risk scores (PRSs) generally assume a linear, additive architecture. Could integrating biological networks into rare variant risk models improve prediction? We have shown that this strategy can be fruitful for common variants,9,10 but its utility for rare variants remains to be established. NERINE’s improvement in empirical power suggests an affirmative answer, but additional evidence will be needed.
