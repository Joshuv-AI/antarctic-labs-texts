The incidence, causes, risk factors, and predictive nomogram of 30-day unplanned reoperation after spinal surgery: a multi-center retrospective study
Authors: Jiao jiao Dai, Wei chen Dong, Shuo Wang, Yu xin Tian, Da ming Liu, Pan Li, Zi xiang Wu, Wei Lei, Yang Zhang, Hai yang Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to elucidate the demographic characteristics, incidence, causes, and risk factors of unplanned reoperations post-spinal surgery (URPS) within 30 days, and to develop and validate a nomogram-based prediction model.MethodsWe retrospectively analysed data from patients who underwent spinal surgery at three tertiary hospitals between January 2013 and January 2023, with a particular focus on those requiring unplanned reoperation within 30 days postoperatively. Demographic characteristics, incidence rates, and temporal patterns of URPS were assessed. A detailed review of causes and presenting symptoms across different postoperative intervals was also performed. Furthermore, we investigated risk factors for URPS and constructed a predictive nomogram.ResultsAmong 77,026 individuals included, 476 cases of URPS within 30 days were identified, comprising 258 males (54.2%) and 218 females (45.8%). Patient ages ranged from 4 to 84 years, with a mean of 48.98 ± 14.75 years. The overall incidence of URPS was 0.62%, showing a gradual annual increase. The leading causes were surgical site infection (SSI) and symptomatic epidural haematoma (SEH), which predominantly manifested as signs of surgical infection, radicular pain, and motor paralysis. The acute phase (postoperative days 2–10) represented the most critical period for symptom presentation, whereas SEH occurred mainly during the hyper-acute phase (days 0–1). Independent risk factors for URPS included drinking (p = 0.027, OR: 1.36, 95% CI: 1.040–1.790), diabetes mellitus (DM) (p < 0.001, OR: 2.056, 95% CI: 1.580–2.676), length of hospital stay (LOS) ≥ 7 days (p < 0.001, OR: 2.269, 95% CI: 1.566–3.288), first operation time (FOT) ≥ 3 h (p = 0.008, OR: 1.396, 95% CI: 1.093–1.783), drainage volume (DV) ≥ 300 ml (p < 0.001, OR: 5.156, 95% CI: 3.854–6.899), and disease type (DT) (p < 0.001, OR: 1.571, 95% CI: 1.382–1.787). The predictive nomogram demonstrated good discriminative ability, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.752 (95% CI: 0.726–0.778). Calibration curves showed excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Hosmer-Lemeshow test, p = 0.892, Brier score = 0.185, R2 = 0.232), and decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities. Patients who experienced URPS had significantly higher nomogram scores than those who did not (p < 0.001), validating the model’s risk-stratification capacity.ConclusionsThis study delineates the incidence, causes, and independent risk factors for URPS within 30 days. The newly developed nomogram exhibits good discrimination, favourable calibration, and superior net clinical benefit, enabling accurate individualized prediction of short-term unplanned reoperation. This predictive tool can assist clinicians in preoperative risk stratification, identification of high-risk patients, and design of tailored intervention strategies, thereby potentially reducing the incidence of unplanned reoperations and improving postoperative outcomes.

Introduction

The increasing prevalence of spinal disorders, including cervical spondylosis and lumbar disc herniation, is largely attributable to rising occupational workloads and prolonged sedentary behaviour [1]. The complexity of spinal pathologies, combined with the technical intricacy of surgical interventions, can give rise to various complications-such as surgical site infection (SSI), symptomatic epidural haematoma (SEH), and cerebrospinal fluid leak (CSFL)-which may necessitate unplanned reoperation in severe cases [2, 3]. Complications arising from the index procedure account for approximately 25% of medical disputes and frequently lead to unplanned reinterventions [4–6]. Such reoperations carry a mortality rate of 10.3%, three times higher than that observed in patients undergoing elective surgery [4, 6, 7]. In orthopaedic surgery overall, the incidence of unplanned reoperations is 2.2%, exceeding the rate of planned revisions (1.7%) [8]. Notably, the incidence of unplanned reoperation post-spinal surgery (URPS) has reached 7.9%, substantially higher than in other orthopaedic subspecialties [9]. The most common causes of unplanned reoperation include bleeding (15–41%), infection (8.3–11%), wound dehiscence (4.6–7.5%), and implant displacement (5.1–10.1%) [10, 11].

Compared with complications emerging later in recovery, those occurring in the immediate postoperative period exert a considerably more detrimental effect on patient outcomes [11]. Although some studies define the early postoperative period as the first seven days, the length of hospital stay after spinal surgery can extend to 23 days, depending on the surgical procedure and insurance policies; consequently, reoperations may take place during the same hospitalisation [12–14]. The present study therefore focuses specifically on the 30-day postoperative window, which we consider a critical phase for preventing complications and improving patient outcomes throughout the perioperative period.

Postoperative mortality has traditionally served as a key metric of healthcare quality. In this context, our study aims to reduce surgical complications and lower postoperative mortality rates [15]. Nevertheless, the literature remains sparse regarding the causes and risk factors for early reoperation. Accordingly, the objectives of this study are threefold: (1) to characterise the demographic characteristics, incidence, and epidemiological trends of URPS; (2) to evaluate the temporal distribution of causes and symptoms across defined postoperative intervals; (3) to identify risk factors for URPS, and (4) to develop and validate a nomogram-based prediction model.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This multi-center, retrospective case-control study was conducted in the orthopedic departments of three medical centers, each with established surgical expertise and a consistent annual volume of spinal procedures. Data were systematically retrieved from the electronic medical record systems (EMRS) of the spinal surgery units within the orthopaedics departments of the participating hospitals. The study retrospectively analysed all patients admitted to these spinal units between 2013 and 2023. The cohort included only individuals who underwent spinal surgery. Patients receiving non-surgical spinal interventions (e.g., bedside debridement and drainage, traction, biopsy, rehabilitation), those with indeterminate aetiologies who did not undergo surgery, and those who underwent non-spinal procedures were excluded. All age groups were included, with data covering primary and revision surgeries as well as a range of comorbidities.

Patients who experienced URPS within 30 days were identified using predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study employed a 1:2 propensity score-matched case-control design, with URPS as the outcome event. For each URPS case, two non-URPS patients were matched as controls based on the following factors: same hospital, same surgeon, surgical date within one month, and same type of spinal surgery. Demographic information, perioperative indicators and laboratory parameters were systematically compiled to identify risk factors associated with URPS.

Ethical approval

The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of AFMU (approval NO. KY20232136-C-1). Owing to the retrospective design of the study, the institutional ethics review committees of the three participating medical centers waived the requirement for prior informed consent. Postoperative period

The 30-day postoperative period was stratified into hyper-acute (0–1 day), acute (2–10 days) and sub-acute (11–30 days) phases, allowing analysis of causes and symptom patterns within each interval.

Study variables

The primary outcome of this study was URPS. Informed by previous research, we incorporated a comprehensive set of variables, including Demographic characteristics: gender, age, hospital number (HN), smoking, drinking, hypertension and diabetes mellitus (DM); Perioperative indicators: disease type (DT), surgical site (SS), surgical path (SP), length of stay (LOS), first operation time (FOT), first bleeding (FB), implant, maximal blood pressure (MBP), postoperative anticoagulation (PTAG), preoperative antibiotics (PTAB), blood transfusion (BT), gelatin sponge (GS) application and drainage volume (DV); Panel of laboratory parameters: thrombin time (TT), haemoglobin (HGB), albumin (ALB), Ca2⁺, coagulation function (CF), liver and kidney function (LKF), D-dimer (DD) and K⁺.

Statistical analysis

All potential risk factors were treated as categorical variables, and differences among them were assessed using the chi-square test. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed, and variables showing statistical significance in the univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for URPS. Multicollinearity was examined using the variance inflation factor (VIF); only variables with VIF values below 10 were retained. Results of the regression model are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05. Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver (AUC) operating characteristic curve, and model calibration was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) test. Clinical utility and net benefit were determined through decision curve analysis (DCA). The distribution difference between patient groups was evaluated using the nomogram score. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0, GraphPad Prism 9.0, and R software (version 4.4.2).

Results

Incidence and demographic characteristics of URPS

The study cohort comprised 77,026 individuals, of whom 476 (0.62%) underwent URPS. Among these patients, 258 were male (54.2%) and 218 were female (45.8%), with ages ranging from 4 to 84 years (mean, 48.98 ± 14.75 years). By contrast, the 76,550 patients with non-URPS included 41,339 males (54.0%) and 35,211 females (46.0%), with a mean age of 40.36 ± 14.08 years. The overall incidence of URPS was 0.62%. Hospital-specific incidences were 0.46% (241 cases) at Hospital 1, 0.92% (122 cases) at Hospital 2, and 0.97% (113 cases) at Hospital 3. Over the observation period, annual incidence rates steadily increased across all three hospitals, rising from 0.23% to 1.88% (Fig. 2). Cause and symptom

Based on medical records and clinical assessments by attending physicians, the 476 URPS cases were attributed to the following causes: neurological deficits (ND), surgical site infection (SSI), symptomatic epidural haematoma (SEH), poor screw position (PSP), inadequate decompression (ID), drainage tube fracture (DTF), incision fat liquefaction (IFL), cerebrospinal fluid leakage (CSFL), nerve root entrapment (NRE), sacral cyst (SC), wound dehiscence (WD), titanium mesh dislocation (TMD), and fusion cage dislodgement (FCD). SSI and SEH were the predominant causes. Presenting symptoms included motor paralysis, radicular pain, signs of surgical site infection, combined motor paralysis and radicular pain, and an absence of symptoms (asymptomatic patients). Table 2 delineates the causes of URPS alongside their corresponding symptoms. The findings reveal that SSI predominantly presented with signs of infection, whereas SEH primarily manifested as neurological compression due to haematoma. More than half of the PSP cases exhibited radicular pain. CSFL and IFL mainly produced symptoms resembling those of SSI. Notably, most patients with NRE presented with motor paralysis, with only a minority experiencing radicular pain.

Risk factors for URPS

Table 4 presents the distribution of variables between the URPS group and the matched non-URPS group. Univariate analysis identified eight variables with statistically significant differences between the two groups: drinking, DM, DT, SP, FOT, DV, PTAB, and LOS. These variables were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. The results confirmed that drinking, DM, DT, FOT ≥ 3 h, DV ≥ 300 ml, and LOS ≥ 7 days are independent risk factors for URPS (Table 5). The forest plot of the multivariate regression is shown in Fig. 4A. All significant variables exhibited statistical differences with P < 0.05, and their corresponding odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals indicated positive correlations with the occurrence of URPS. No protective factor was observed in the final regression model. These screened independent predictors constituted the core clinical variables closely associated with short-term postoperative adverse events in spinal surgery patients, providing a reliable basis for the subsequent construction of the prediction model.

Development of the predictive nomogram

To achieve individualized and visualized risk prediction, a quantitative nomogram prediction model was established based on all verified independent risk factors (Fig. 4B). Each predictive variable was assigned a corresponding weighted score according to its regression coefficient. By summing the total score of all indicators and converting it through the scale of the nomogram, clinicians can directly obtain the precise individualized probability of 30-day unplanned reoperation for each patient. Compared with traditional regression equations, the nomogram simplifies complex statistical calculations into intuitive graphical scoring, which is more suitable for rapid clinical preoperative risk assessment and bedside decision-making.

Verification of the logistic regression model

The regression model was validated using R software. The AUC was 0.752 (95% CI: 0.726–0.778) (Fig. 5A). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded a p-value of 0.892, indicating good calibration (Fig. 5B). DCA curve showed that as the risk threshold increased from 0 to 0.8, the number of patients identified as high-risk by the model and the number of true positive cases both gradually decreased. At a threshold probability of 0.4, approximately 300 out of every 1,000 patients were classified as high-risk, of whom about 180 actually underwent unplanned reoperation (Fig. 5C). The nomogram score distribution (box plot) revealed that patients with URPS (group 1) had significantly higher scores than those without unplanned reoperation (group 0; p < 0.001), indicating that the model effectively distinguishes between high-risk and low-risk populations for URPS and possesses good clinical risk-stratification ability (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

We performed a focused analysis on patients with URPS. Global differences in healthcare policies lead to substantial variation in postoperative hospital stay. For example, the average hospital stay after spinal surgery in the United States is approximately 3.2 days, which underscores the importance of research into postoperative readmissions [9]. By contrast, Japan’s comprehensive welfare benefits and health insurance coverage tend to encourage physicians to delay discharge until full recovery is confirmed, resulting in an average hospital stay of around one month [14]. Previous studies have reported a reoperation rate of 1.6% following spinal surgery, which is higher than the rate observed in our study. However, those studies did not distinguish between scheduled and unplanned reoperations, whereas our study specifically focuses on the latter. We argue that studying unplanned reoperations has substantial clinical implications: it facilitates a more detailed informed consent process, supports the development of more comprehensive surgical protocols, and enables the implementation of targeted postoperative health management strategies.

Our study revealed a progressive annual increase in the incidence of URPS, which may be related to the growing use of internal implants. Indeed, the reoperation rate was significantly higher among patients with internal implants than among those without (0.78% vs. 0.20%). Additionally, over the ten-year study period, improvements in EMRS led to the inclusion of a greater number of cases, and enhanced hospital monitoring of complications may also have contributed to the observed increase. Future studies should incorporate longer-term follow-up data to further investigate the incidence of URPS.

SSI and SEH were the leading causes of URPS, together accounting for more than half of cases (64.3%). Given that symptoms of SSI are primarily infection-related, we hypothesised an association with hypoalbuminaemia; indeed, 32.7% of these patients had albumin levels below the normal range. Previous studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between hypoalbuminaemia and post-discharge complications [16]. Albumin possesses anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties that can regulate inflammatory responses and promote wound healing [17, 18]. However, hypoalbuminaemia did not emerge as a risk factor for URPS in this study, possibly owing to the size of the control cohort. DTF and SC were often asymptomatic. CSFL was frequently associated with a high infection rate (66.7%). Furthermore, SSI was identified as the predominant pathogenic factor in nearly all patient subgroups. This may be related to the need for prolonged bed rest after spinal surgery, which compresses the surgical site and delays wound healing. Given that the absolute number of URPS cases is small relative to the total patient population, future studies should include more positive cases to further investigate the basis for SSI as the main pathogenic factor.

Regarding the temporal distribution of URPS, most reoperations occurred within the first two postoperative weeks, peaking around day 7. This finding underscores the critical need for close monitoring of symptoms and signs during this interval, as well as the urgency of preemptive interventions. Within specific postoperative periods, 79.6% (379/476) of URPS cases were confined to the acute phase (days 2–10), with infection being the predominant presentation. In the hyper-acute phase (days 0–1), SEH-related URPS were observed in 85.1% of patients, consistent with symptoms of motor paralysis or radicular pain. The acute phase marked the peak of overall symptom manifestation. Therefore, in clinical practice, intensified vigilance is warranted during both the hyper-acute and acute phases, particularly for symptoms of motor paralysis and radicular pain. These symptoms are critical for spinal surgeons to assess patient status and recovery, thereby guiding the development of targeted clinical prevention and treatment strategies.

In this study, the risk factors for URPS included drinking, DM, DT, FOT ≥ 3 h, DV ≥ 300 ml, LOS≥7days. Previous research has shown that alcohol consumption increases the risk of postoperative infections [19]. Adequate vascular function after surgery is essential for wound healing, and evidence indicates that drinking promotes arteriosclerosis and slows the healing process, thereby prolonging wound healing and hospital stay [20]. Nevertheless, some studies have reported that compared with abstainers and heavy drinkers, moderate or light alcohol consumption is associated with a lower risk of heart failure [21], type 2 diabetes [22], hypertension [23], cardiovascular events and mortality [24]. Our study found that drinking is a significant risk factor for URPS, with an odds ratio of 1.36. This finding suggests that in the perioperative management of spinal surgery, reasonable preoperative guidance-such as advising patients to abstain from alcohol-is extremely necessary.

Among diabetic individuals, the global rise in the incidence of delayed wound healing is largely attributable to a lack of effective preventive and control measures. Studies have shown that insufficient chemokine secretion in diabetic patients impairs muscle fibre healing, thereby adversely affecting postoperative wound repair [25]. In this study, DM was identified as a risk factor for URPS, with an odds ratio of 2.056. The postoperative application of glucose oxidase (GOx), for example, can accelerate wound healing by regulating the hyperglycaemic microenvironment while also providing antibacterial and oxygen-releasing properties [26]. Similarly, the use of growth factors can enhance the healing rate of diabetic wounds, positively influencing the recovery process [27].

Emerging evidence indicates a strong association between prolonged operative time and a range of complications, including SSI, venous thromboembolism (VTE), haemorrhage, haematoma formation and necrosis [28, 29]. FOT has been identified as an independent and potentially modifiable risk factor for these complications [29, 30]. In the present analysis, the threshold for prolonged FOT was set at three hours, based on input from spinal surgery specialists. Our findings show that an FOT exceeding three hours is an independent risk factor for URPS (odds ratio, 1.396), consistent with previous reports. Prolonged FOT is associated with increased blood loss, and extended wound exposure leads to greater exudate production, which complicates the wound healing process and elevates the risk of subsequent surgical intervention.

The complexity of spinal anatomy often necessitates the use of surgical drainage after spinal procedures. Some studies have suggested that transient exposure of the drainage system to the external environment during bag exchange may increase the risk of retrograde bacterial infection, potentially discouraging drain use [2]. However, research by Adogwa and colleagues demonstrated that negative-pressure drainage in extensive thoracolumbar fusion and corrective surgery reduced wound dehiscence by 50% and significantly lowered the incidence of postoperative SSI [31]. Furthermore, drain lavage and high-negative-pressure drainage are more effective than simple closed drainage in managing URPS [32, 33]. Nevertheless, these studies did not evaluate the impact of DV on postoperative recovery. In our cohort, 94% (447/476) of patients who experienced URPS had drains placed. Accordingly, examining the influence of postoperative DV, rather than only the use of negative-pressure drainage, may offer deeper clinical insight. Our findings show that DV ≥ 300 ml is an independent and significant risk factor for URPS, with an odds ratio of 5.156. Increased DV suggests heightened wound exudate or minor vascular bleeding. Therefore, a thorough preoperative assessment of the patient’s surgical status is essential. Intraoperatively, surgeons should optimise haemostasis, strive to shorten operative time, and reduce overall blood loss, thereby decreasing drainage volume. Such measures may be crucial for preventing postoperative URPS.

According to published data, 75% of patients with spinal tuberculosis also present with paravertebral and/or extradural abscesses [34]. Excessive accumulation of a paravertebral abscess can rupture the periosteum and track down into the muscle space, thereby increasing surgical difficulty and impeding complete lesion removal. Patients with spinal tuberculosis often have poor nutritional status and low immunity, rendering them susceptible to bacterial infections; moreover, co-infection with non-tuberculous bacteria in the setting of Mycobacterium tuberculosis is not uncommon [35]. In the present study, the incidence of URPS in patients with spinal infection was 1.571 times that in patients with spinal degeneration. This finding suggests that for surgeries performed on patients with spinal infection, postoperative care should be further intensified to reduce the occurrence of complications and reoperations.

As of 2021, the “Chinese Clinical Practice Guidelines for Enhanced Recovery After Surgery” (ERAS) emphasize the importance of minimising hospital stay length and reducing postoperative complications. Empirical evidence suggests that shortened hospital stays may negatively affect the postoperative rehabilitation of critically ill patients, primarily owing to inadequate access to rehabilitative care. Conversely, the present study found that prolonged LOS was an independent risk factor for URPS, with an odds ratio of 2.269. This association may be explained by the increased likelihood of wound infection associated with longer hospital stays. Previous research has also indicated that a hospital stay exceeding nine days is a risk factor for reoperation [36]. Arora and colleagues defined a postoperative hospital stay exceeding seven days as prolonged [37]. The present study similarly found that a length of stay more than seven days after spinal surgery is a risk factor for URPS (odds ratio, 2.27). Abdulmajeed et al. also reported that prolonged hospital stay is a risk factor for reoperation within 90 days [38]. Taylor observed that in anterior cervical spine surgery, each additional day of hospital stay increases the rate of readmission within 30 or 90 days postoperatively [39]. This finding is consistent with the results of Goyal [40], Rumalla [41] and Elsamadicy [42], all of whom identified prolonged hospital stay as a risk factor for readmission and reoperation. In contrast, the present study found that the surgical segment was not a risk factor for URPS, in agreement with the findings of Sheha [43], Samuel [44] and Khalid [45].

Accurate identification of high-risk populations and early prevention of unplanned reoperation are critical to improving the safety and prognosis of spinal surgery. In the present multi-center retrospective study, we systematically screened the risk factors and constructed a visualized nomogram prediction model. Consistent with previous clinical evidence, all enrolled independent variables were closely related to postoperative surgical site complications, neurological function recovery, and surgical trauma severity, which reasonably explained the occurrence of short-term unplanned reoperation from clinical perspectives.

Traditional risk factor analysis can only qualitatively judge the correlation between clinical indicators and adverse events, but it cannot achieve quantitative individualized risk evaluation. In contrast, the newly constructed nomogram model fully integrates multiple key predictive factors and assigns scientific weight to each variable. It transforms abstract statistical results into intuitive and operable graphical tools, making up for the deficiencies of traditional single-factor and multi-factor analysis. This model enables precise stratification of reoperation risk for individual patients, which is conducive to implementing targeted preoperative optimization, intraoperative precise manipulation, and intensified postoperative monitoring for high-risk patients.

Notably, the variables included in the nomogram are routine and easily accessible clinical indicators, without the need for additional expensive examinations or complex detection techniques. Therefore, this prediction model possesses excellent clinical practicability and popularization value. It can serve as a convenient auxiliary tool for spinal surgeons to evaluate postoperative reoperation risk before surgery, standardize perioperative risk management, and ultimately reduce the incidence of short-term unplanned reoperation and improve patients’ postoperative clinical outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the inclusion of three centers provides a substantial dataset, the generalisability of our findings requires validation through future studies involving broader and more diverse cohorts. Second, owing to the constraints of the retrospective study design, our analysis did not include all possible variables from patient demographics, perioperative indicators and laboratory parameters; variables that were not associated with reoperation rates in previous studies were omitted. Future research should therefore endeavour to incorporate an expanded set of predictive factors. Finally, the determination of reoperation causes relied on the subjective assessments of surgical staff, which may introduce variability in the criteria and decision-making processes for reoperation, potentially affecting the study outcomes. Despite these limitations, the present findings offer significant value for preoperative risk stratification, patient counselling, and the reduction of perioperative risks and complications.

Conclusion

This multi-center retrospective study systematically analysed the incidence, causes and independent risk factors of URPS within 30 days, and developed an effective nomogram-based prediction model for individualised risk assessment. The results identified SSI and SEH as the main causes of URPS. Multi-dimensional validation confirmed that the constructed nomogram exhibited good discriminative ability and excellent calibration, with high concordance between predicted probabilities and actual clinical outcomes. Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the model provided stable net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities. Furthermore, an increase in the nomogram score was positively correlated with a significantly elevated risk of reoperation, further verifying the model’s reliable risk-stratification performance. This tool can be used for preoperative risk assessment and screening of high-risk patients undergoing spinal surgery, facilitating the implementation of personalised clinical interventions, reducing the incidence of short-term unplanned reoperations, and improving overall postoperative outcomes.
