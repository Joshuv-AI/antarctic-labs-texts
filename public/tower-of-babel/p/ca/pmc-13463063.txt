An experimental study on the effects of different strength training methods on smash performance and scoring in adolescent badminton players
Authors: Liu Mingyang, Xiang Hexuan, Leng Bo, Wei Songwei, Liao Huiling, Feng Weiping, Sun Lei, Sukwon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study examined the effects of ballistic training (BT), blood flow restriction training (BFRT), and traditional resistance training (TRT) on the smash performance of adolescent badminton players. Thirty athletes were randomly assigned to BT, BFRT, or TRT groups and completed 12 training sessions over four weeks. Heart rate (HR) was continuously monitored to adjust intensity and rest intervals. After the intervention, data were analyzed using two-way mixed-design ANOVA. Smash speed significantly improved across all groups (F(1,27) = 22.34, p < 0.05, η²ₚ = 0.66), with a significant time by group interaction (F(2,27) = 6.71, p = 0.004, η²ₚ = 0.33). In the BFRT group, smash speed increased from 166.8 ± 9.7 to 202.0 ± 11.3 km·h⁻¹. Smash accuracy improved slightly without significant group differences (F(2,27) = 4.23, p = 0.026, η²ₚ = 0.24). Exercise heart rate and recovery showed significant time and time by group effects (Ex HR: F(2,27) = 7.08, p = 0.003; HRR: F(2,27) = 5.42, p = 0.010), while resting HR remained stable. In conclusion, both BT and BFRT increase smash speed in adolescent badminton players, suggesting that combining these methods can improve performance and training efficiency.Keywords: Ballistic training, Blood flow restriction training, Smash performance, Badminton, AdolescentSubject terms: Physiology, Health care, Engineering

Introduction

Ballistic training (BT) and blood flow restriction training (BFRT) have recently attracted attention in sports science as approaches for enhancing athletic performance. BFRT, in particular, is suitable for individuals with weaker physical condition and lower tolerance to training, because it produces muscle strength gains even at low loads, making it useful for both strength and rehabilitation purposes1. By partially restricting blood flow, BFRT increases metabolic stress and anaerobic muscle responses, producing high-intensity training effects at relatively low exercise intensities2. This process improves anaerobic metabolic capacity, which can directly influence speed performance in sports—especially in disciplines such as badminton3–5. Although BFRT has shown clear effects in adult athletes, evidence in adolescents is sparse, particularly for sports-performance outcomes rather than rehabilitation. Because adolescents are still growing, the safe and effective use of BFRT in this population is an important topic for further research6.

Ballistic training (BT) refers to exercises in which the load or body weight is released at the end of the movement, such as bench press release or squat jumps. This method involves full-range acceleration and simple movements, and matches the demands of certain sports7, especially those requiring rapid reactions and precise control, such as badminton, tennis, and volleyball. By improving neuromuscular activation, BT can increase athletes’ racket swing speed and explosive power8. Increased strength both reduces injury risk and increases ball-striking speed. However, the suitability of BT for adolescents remains debated, especially given concerns about declines in movement quality and accumulated fatigue under high-intensity training9. In contrast, TRT primarily focuses on muscle strength gains, typically using higher loads (high %1RM) to improve muscle power and endurance. The training movements are generally stable and controlled, emphasizing muscle strength rather than explosive power or speed. Although TRT is simpler than some newer methods, it remains the main training approach in most training camps10.

Research on BFRT and BT in adolescent athletes is scarce, particularly in sports such as badminton that demand high explosive power and speed. Because badminton requires rapid racket swings and agile footwork, both training methods may enhance athletic performance. Because adolescents remain in a critical growth stage, excessive training can cause irreversible injuries, so appropriate load management is essential. Heart rate is an important indicator of fatigue and recovery capacity, and reflects cardiorespiratory endurance. Real-time HR monitoring during training interventions enables timely adjustments to training loads and recovery status, thereby providing a higher level of safety assurance for athletes11. We used a randomized, assessor-blinded design to minimize bias and applied standardized testing procedures to ensure reliability.

We hypothesised that BFRT and BT, even at low loads, would improve smash speed and scoring more than TRT. We further expected BFRT to produce greater gains in upper-limb strength and speed. By testing these hypotheses, the study informs the use of BT and BFRT in adolescent training and offers a reference model for specialised badminton training, addressing current research gaps.

Materials and methods

The study employed a 3-by-2 mixed factorial design, with time points of badminton smash testing (pre- and post-intervention) as the within-subject factor and training methods (TRT, BT, BFRT) as the between-subjects factor. Participant characteristics including height, age, and training experience were controlled as covariates, while environmental variables such as wind speed (< 2 m/s), lighting, temperature (29 °C ± 1 °C), and ambient noise were strictly regulated. All participants used the same racket with identical string tension across both testing phases. The dependent variables were two smash performance metrics: shuttle velocity (km·h⁻¹), recorded with a calibrated radar gun, and accuracy, quantified as a cumulative placement score (0–24 points) across six smash trials.

Subjects

The study was conducted within a high school badminton team with approval from the team management. The intervention period lasted four weeks, with three training sessions per week, for a total of 12 training sessions12. All experimental procedures were conducted in strict accordance with relevant ethical regulations and institutional guidelines. Prior to participation, all subjects and their legal guardians provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Jeonbuk National University (No. JBNU2024-09-015-002).

Participants were recruited from adolescent badminton athletes aged 16–18 years with 6–10 years of badminton training experience. Inclusion criteria were as follows13: healthy non-smoking individuals; abstention from caffeine supplementation during the intervention period; avoidance of additional high-intensity physical activities during the study; and regular badminton training experience. Exclusion criteria included diagnosed hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular diseases, and the use of vasoactive medications, antioxidants, or hormone therapies.

A total of 45 adolescent badminton players were initially assessed for eligibility. Of these, 15 participants were excluded: 9 did not meet the inclusion criteria, 2 declined to participate, and 4 reported a previous musculoskeletal injury that could potentially affect training participation. The remaining 30 eligible participants were randomly assigned in equal numbers to the ballistic training (BT), blood flow restriction training (BFRT), and traditional resistance training (TRT) groups (n = 10 per group) using a computer-generated random allocation sequence. Allocation concealment was ensured through sealed opaque envelopes until the commencement of the formal trial. Sample size estimation was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.6 software. The following parameters were applied: two-factor repeated-measures design (training method × time), F-test (within–between interaction), effect size f = 0.4 based on previous resistance-training studies involving adolescent athletes14,15, α = 0.05, statistical power = 0.80, three intervention groups (BT, BFRT, TRT), and two measurement time points (pre- and post-intervention). The minimum required sample size was calculated to be 21 participants. Considering a potential dropout rate of 20%16, the final sample size was deemed sufficient for statistical analysis. All participants completed the 4-week intervention and post-intervention assessments, with no loss to follow-up or exclusion from the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Experimental arrangement

Baseline measurements were taken 48 h before the experimental intervention began. All participants underwent tests to assess the following parameters and exclude any abnormalities: (1) one-repetition maximum (1RM); (2) blood pressure; (3) heart rate; (4) smash velocity.

The one-repetition maximum (1RM) test measured maximal strength in the primary training muscles, including the barbell deadlift, bench press, dumbbell curl, and band-resisted deadlift. Testing procedures followed the guidelines of the National Strength and Conditioning Association (NSCA)8. Participants completed a 10-min warm-up consisting of light jogging and dynamic stretching. Thereafter, they performed 8–10 repetitions at approximately 50% of the estimated 1RM and 3–5 repetitions at 70%, with 3–5 min rest intervals between attempts. The load was increased progressively by 2.5–10% until the participant could complete only one correctly executed repetition. The heaviest successful lift was recorded as the 1RM.

Test–retest reliability was established in a pilot subsample (n = 10). Participants repeated identical testing sessions 72 h apart under the same conditions. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, model 3,1) and coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated according to Koo and Li (2016)17. Reliability was interpreted as poor (< 0.5), moderate (0.5–0.75), good (0.75–0.9), or excellent (> 0.9). All primary variables showed excellent reliability (ICC = 0.91–0.96; CV < 5%), consistent with previous findings18, indicating high reproducibility of the 1RM testing procedure.

Before each intervention session, participants performed a standardized warm-up of 4 min of general exercises followed by 3 min of stretching. Following the warm-up, participants proceeded to their assigned group training protocols. The detailed experimental procedure is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Experimental process.

The experimental interventions for BT, BFRT, and TRT were implemented concurrently using identical exercise programs, with training intensity (%1RM) adjusted to enforce progressive overload. Real-time heart rate monitoring was maintained throughout the intervention period to ensure appropriate training intensity and track physiological recovery capacity19. Rest intervals were dynamically adjusted based on heart rate levels (typically maintained at 40–60% of maximum heart rate), a strategy that mitigated excessive fatigue and supported cardiopulmonary function and metabolic efficiency20. All three groups (BFRT, BT, and TRT) followed a standardized four-phase protocol: preparatory activities (jogging and stretching), a four-week experimental training intervention, and post-intervention smash testing.

In the smash performance test, the primary outcome variables were shuttle speed and placement score. At the start of each test, participants stood approximately 1 m behind the mid-point of the baseline. Each athlete performed six jump smashes in total (three forehand and three backhand smashes) targeting the area near the opponent’s backcourt baseline. The scoring zones were arranged from the sideline toward the court center, with higher scores assigned to shuttle landings closer to the sideline. A recording station was positioned lateral to the landing area, where trained personnel filmed each trial with a high-speed video camera. The placement evaluation is an indirect measure of smash accuracy rather than an assumption that shots closer to the sidelines always yield higher scores in real matches; actual scoring advantage depends on tactical context. Shuttle speed was measured using a radar gun (max mode, detection range: 80–332 km·h⁻¹) positioned 2 m behind and 2.5 m above the player, directed toward the target area. The radar operator reported each smash velocity, and the same evaluator recorded all data. Smash accuracy used a cumulative score across six attempts (Fig. 3). Missed shots scored 0; successful smashes scored 1 to 4 points by shuttle landing position relative to the court boundaries. The total smash accuracy score therefore ranged from 0 to 24 points. The same scoring and calculation procedures were applied in both the pre- and post-intervention tests to keep procedures consistent.

BFRT training group

Pressure was regulated with a KAATSU Master pressure control device (Japan) during blood flow restriction training. The 30 mm wide pressure cuff for upper-limb application allowed independent control of binding pressure and inflation pressure. During equipment fitting, participants were instructed to maintain a relaxed arm position with natural downward hang. The cuff was secured around the proximal third of the upper arm (near the axillary region of the biceps brachii), perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the limb. The cuff was fitted closely to the skin without wrinkles or slippage, to avoid discomfort or training interference from uneven pressure. For badminton-specific training, the optimal binding pressure was set at 25–35 mmHg, with inflation pressure ranging from 80 to 120 mmHg, aligning with recommendations for the target population and sport21,22.

The blood flow restriction training protocol was structured based on the optimal load ranges proposed by Scott et al. Each exercise consisted of 4 sets at 20–30% of 1RM. The first set involved 30 repetitions, followed by 15 repetitions for each subsequent set, with 60-second inter-set rest periods. Rest intervals were adjusted based on heart rate responses, with cuff pressure maintained or slightly reduced to resting levels during these periods, based on individual comfort. Total training duration was limited to 20–30 min to limit the risk of adverse effects of prolonged ischemia on muscle and nerve function22. Throughout the sessions, subjective fatigue was monitored with the Borg RPE scale, together with observations of limb color, skin temperature, and blood flow status. Post-exercise, cuff deflation was performed gradually to prevent blood pressure fluctuations or dizziness associated with rapid blood flow restoration. Heart rate recovery was recorded, and participants were asked about any discomfort. The detailed training schedule is presented in Table 2.

BT group and TRT group

Ballistic Training (BT) emphasizes high-velocity, explosive movements executed with maximal effort and minimal deceleration at the end range of motion. This method uses relatively light loads (lower %1RM) to maximize movement velocity, aiming to enhance neuromuscular activation and muscle contraction speed23. The BT protocol included four exercises: (1) Medicine Ball Slams to develop upper body power and core strength; (2) Side-to-Side Push Presses to improve shoulder and upper limb dynamic strength, improving racquet stability and power transfer; (3) Supine Medicine Ball Throws to simulate the smashing motion and increase rapid force production in the forearms and shoulders; and (4) Band-Resisted Deadlifts to strengthen lower limb and core stability, crucial for body control and power generation during shots23,24. Training intensity ranged from 40% to 70% of 1RM, progressed weekly as detailed in Table 3, with movement velocity prioritized over load magnitude.

Resistance Band Training

In contrast, Traditional Resistance Training (TRT) focused on conventional strength-building exercises: (1) Dumbbell Training targeting upper body and core musculature to build overall strength; (2) Barbell Deadlifts for lower body and core power, improving overall stability; (3) Traditional Bench Presses to strengthen the pectorals, deltoids, and triceps, important for racquet control; and (4) Resistance Band Training to develop shoulder and arm muscle endurance and stability25. TRT employed higher loads (70–90% 1RM) to stimulate maximal strength gains26. Both protocols consisted of 4 sets per exercise, with 15 repetitions in the first set followed by 10-12-8 repetitions in subsequent sets, using higher loads but fewer repetitions than BT. Inter-set rest periods were adjusted by heart rate recovery to maintain training quality. The comprehensive training schedule for both modalities is detailed in Table 3. Total workload and rate of perceived exertion (RPE) were continuously monitored to maintain equivalent training stimulus among groups. This design limits confounding from training load, so performance differences can be attributed to each training modality. Similar load-equated designs are common to compare neuromuscular adaptations between explosive and traditional resistance protocols27,28.

Data acquisition and processing

Because of the nature of exercise training, participants and coaches were not blinded. However, outcome assessors and data analysts were blinded to group allocation. The statistician received data coded as Group A, Group B, and Group C to minimize analysis bias. During the training period, heart rate was monitored and recorded, mainly focusing on the maximum heart rate during training (HRpeak), the heart rate at the end of training (HR1min), and the rate of heart rate decline during the recovery period (HRR = HRpeak − HR1min)29. Through the heart rate monitoring devices, data were collected at regular intervals and the changes in heart rate at each stage were recorded. After data collection, the data were checked for obvious outliers in the data (such as data deviations caused by instrument malfunctions), and these data points were eliminated according to predefined criteria; The heart rate and racket speed of each subject were standardized. Heart rate and racket speed were standardized to relative values by subject age, gender, and training years to ensure comparability across subjects. Training data from the three groups were time-synchronized so that training time points were consistent across groups, avoiding time-period effects on the results.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. Prior to hypothesis testing, normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variance with Levene’s test. When both assumptions were satisfied, a one-way ANOVA was conducted to examine between-group differences among the three interventions (BT, BFRT, and TRT) in smash speed, placement score, and heart rate responses. Within-group effects (pre- vs. post-intervention) were analyzed using paired-samples t-tests, while between-group differences in the magnitude of change were evaluated using two-way mixed-design ANOVA (time by group). Potential differences in training workload and RPE were controlled for. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η²ₚ). Statistical significance was defined a priori as α = 0.05, with results interpreted as significant (p < 0.05) or not (p ≥ 0.05). All tests were two-tailed.

Results

Smash speed

Effect sizes are reported as partial eta squared (η²ₚ), with values of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 denoting small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

A two-way mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of time on smash speed (F(1,27) = 22.34, p < 0.05, η²ₚ = 0.66), indicating that smash speed increased from pre- to post-intervention across all participants. The main effect of group was not significant (F(2, 27) = 1.45, p = 0.25, η²ₚ = 0.10), indicating no overall difference among training groups when averaged across time. A significant time by group interaction was observed (F(2,27) = 6.71, p = 0.004, η²ₚ = 0.33), indicating that changes in smash speed over time differed between groups (Table 4). Post hoc analyses showed that smash speed increased significantly from pre- to post-intervention in the BFRT group (166.8 ± 9.7 to 202.0 ± 11.3 km·h⁻¹, p < 0.05), the BT group (169.4 ± 8.3 to 199.6 ± 10.5 km·h⁻¹, p < 0.05), and the TRT group (167.6 ± 9.5 to 186.0 ± 10.1 km·h⁻¹, p = 0.02). Between-group comparisons at post-intervention showed that smash speed was significantly higher in the BFRT group than in the TRT group (p = 0.01), while no significant difference was observed between the BFRT and BT groups (p = 0.12) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This study examined the effects of BFRT, BT, and TRT on smash velocity and accuracy in adolescent badminton players. The hypothesis that BFRT and BT could improve strength and, in turn, smash velocity and scoring under low-intensity loads was partially supported. Post-intervention, both the BFRT and BT groups showed increased smash velocities. However, shuttle-placement accuracy did not improve significantly, and smash scoring did not change. Real-time heart rate monitoring did not significantly improve heart rate indicators after the short-term intervention but was useful for guiding training intensity and recovery intervals.

Both BFRT and BT enhance the coordination and sequencing of the kinetic chain, allowing energy generated by the lower limbs to be transmitted more effectively through the trunk to the upper limbs and finally to the racket30. In badminton, the smash relies heavily on the stretch–shortening cycle of the lower extremities and the rotational acceleration of the trunk31. Improved intersegmental coordination enables faster energy transfer and higher shuttle speed even without large increases in maximal muscle strength. BFRT also stimulates the release of muscle growth-related hormones, promotes muscle protein synthesis, and increases muscle strength and volume32. This metabolic adaptation allows athletes to generate greater force during high-intensity shots, thus increasing smash velocity. Supporting evidence comes from a 5-week, 15-session BFRT intervention study on tennis players reported significant increases in muscle cross-sectional area33. These findings indicate that low-intensity BFRT can improve muscle strength and athletic performance. In fast-paced sports such as badminton, smash velocity depends on coordination, movement control, and technical execution as well as strength. Previous research has suggested that BFRT may improve flexibility, which can help maintain posture and movement efficiency during rapid actions34. Although flexibility was not measured in this study, such adaptations may help support smoother and more coordinated smash execution, providing a plausible explanation for the observed performance changes. In our experimental design, the load intensity for BFRT was significantly lower than that of TRT and BT, allowing all participants to complete the training regimen. The comparable effects of low-intensity BFRT and high-intensity TRT, together with the difficulty of completing equivalent volumes under high loads, may explain why BFRT outperformed TRT and BT. These findings suggest that BFRT is an effective low-load alternative for improving smash velocity in adolescent badminton players. These findings can help coaches and trainers balance performance gains with appropriate load management and recovery for young athletes.

BT improved smash velocity in badminton players, likely through several mechanisms. Based on the experimental design and prior literature, BT emphasizes muscle extensibility and elasticity during rapid explosive movements, both of which are central to the badminton smash. Lopes et al. (2019) highlighted that greater muscle elasticity improves athletes’ responsiveness during explosive actions. By including specific dynamic stretching and movement patterns, BT supports muscle performance across a wider range of motion, thereby increasing power efficiency during smashes35. Ballistic training has also been reported to be associated with improvements in joint range of motion in athletic populations. Previous studies indicate that flexibility-related adaptations may support athletic performance, particularly in sports involving rapid directional changes and precise movement patterns36. In this short-term intervention, such adaptations should be interpreted as contributing factors rather than direct causal mechanisms. For badminton players, greater flexibility allows force generation from favourable postures during shots, improving both smash velocity and accuracy. Ballistic movements in the experimental design improved participants’ body control during smashes. BT also offers advantages for muscle recruitment and neuromuscular adaptation. Stanković et al. (2023) reported that combining strength training with ballistic movements improves neuromuscular adaptation, enabling athletes to more effectively recruit muscle fibers during complex actions37. This heightened neuromuscular control is useful for high-intensity shots, as it allows athletes to generate greater force and improve smash velocity23,24. In summary, our results align with previous research and indicate that BT can improve flexibility, neuromuscular control, and power efficiency in badminton players. These findings show how BT can be integrated into training programs to improve smash performance.

Smash scoring did not differ significantly between pre- and post-intervention, reflecting the technical complexity of the badminton smash. This finding indicates that successful smashing requires both explosive power and precise hitting technique under high-intensity conditions38. Strength training can directly translate to performance improvements in some sports, in skill-dominated activities like badminton and tennis, the synergy between strength and technique is crucial27. In explosive movements, peak force output depends on precise timing, which in turn depends on technical proficiency. The badminton smash demands complex neuromuscular coordination to deliver power. Without adequate technical control, increases in strength alone may not translate into better scoring. Research indicates that successful smashing depends on power output and on technical details such as the impact point, racket face angle, and timing of contact39. Although physical interventions such as BFRT or BT may increase power output or endurance, their direct impact on smash scoring was not pronounced. This outcome suggests that athletes may need more technical practice and competitive experience to improve scoring. The results highlight the multifaceted nature of badminton skill development, particularly for the smash. Although strength and conditioning enhance physical capacity, the transfer of these gains to scoring appears to be mediated by technical proficiency. Therefore, technical training should be combined with physical conditioning to improve overall badminton performance.

Research Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the intervention did not use individualized arterial occlusion pressure (AOP) in the BFRT protocol. A fixed range of 25–35 mmHg for the binding pressure and 80–120 mmHg for the inflation pressure was applied uniformly to all subjects. However, the appropriate occlusion pressure can vary with limb circumference, arterial blood pressure, and cuff width. Second, the outcome variables were limited to smash speed, placement accuracy, and heart rate response. Future research should add badminton-specific performance indicators (such as movement time, return accuracy, shuttle trajectory, and match-simulated performance) to better capture the sport-specific transfer effects of training interventions. Finally, the intervention period lasted only four weeks, which may have been too short to capture long-term neuromuscular or technical adaptations. This duration was constrained by the training schedule and athlete availability during the competitive season. Future studies should use longer training durations, personalised BFRT prescriptions, and a wider set of performance indicators to improve ecological validity and generalisability.

Conclusions

This study shows that low-load BFRT can increase upper-limb strength in adolescents without raising injury risk. Compared with high-intensity traditional resistance training, BFRT uses low loads with blood flow restriction to improve muscle strength without the muscle soreness and injury often associated with high-intensity loads. Ballistic Training (BT) improves explosive power and neuromuscular coordination, making it suitable for sports requiring high reaction speeds such as badminton and tennis. BT increases smash velocity without high training loads, supporting its use in adolescent training programs. Both BFRT and BT are promising for adolescent athletes, particularly those who tolerate high loads poorly or recover slowly. Low-load BFRT is a more suitable option for these athletes. Proper integration of these methods can improve athletic performance in adolescents and reduce injury risk during training, increasing training effectiveness and sustainability. These findings indicate that BFRT and BT can serve as alternatives in youth sports training, balancing performance gains with safety. Using these methods, coaches and trainers can design training regimens that are better tailored to young athletes and may improve development while reducing injury rates. This study adds to the evidence supporting innovative training techniques in youth sports and points toward more individualized strength and conditioning for adolescent athletes.

Practical applications

Because BFRT uses low intensities, it can improve strength while protecting adolescents’ skeletal and joint health. In adolescents, high training intensity can increase injury risk, whereas low-load BFRT provides a safer route to strength gains. This approach is particularly suitable for adolescent athletes because it permits strength development without compromising growth or health.

Research on BT and BFRT in adolescents, particularly on athletic performance, is limited. The study provides theoretical support for badminton training and for diversification in similar sports (such as tennis, table tennis, and volleyball, which rely heavily on upper-limb explosive power) and school physical education. By examining these methods in young athletes, the study addresses a gap in the sports-science literature and offers practical guidance for coaches and educators across disciplines.

Using BT and BFRT in routine physical education and training can improve smash velocity and upper-limb strength in adolescent badminton players. This has direct practical value for competitive badminton, increasing shot speed and improving tactical execution. These methods give coaches more evidence-based tools that can help athletes improve specific abilities within a shorter timeframe. Integrating these techniques into training programs can produce more efficient performance gains and may accelerate skill development in young athletes.
