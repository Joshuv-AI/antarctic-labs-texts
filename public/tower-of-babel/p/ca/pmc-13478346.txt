Heat-inactivated Bacillus spores attenuate poly I:C-induced lung inflammation and microbiome alterations through modulation of host immune responses
Authors: Fatemeh Baghoveh, Maryam M Matin, Masoud Fereidoni, Ali Makhdoumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUncontrolled cytokine responses are a major driver of acute lung injury and poor outcomes in respiratory viral infections such as COVID-19. Strategies that can safely restrain hyperinflammation while preserving host defense remain limited. Here, we show that pretreatment with heat-inactivated Bacillus spores markedly protects against the viral mimetic polyinosinic˗polycytidylic acid (poly I:C)-induced pulmonary inflammation in BALB/c mice. Structural and in vivo imaging system analyses confirmed that heat treatment was successful in eliminating the viability of spores without rupturing or changing their penetration into the airway via instillation. The results indicated that in a poly I:C-induced model of acute lung injury, spore parabiotics reduced pulmonary edema, histopathological damage, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Moreover, parabiotic spores suppressed Ace2 and Tlr3 expression in murine macrophages, suggesting regulation of receptors implicated in downstream inflammatory signaling. Parabiotic treatment attenuated lung microbial alteration triggered by viral mimic challenge. Together, these findings identify spore-based parabiotics as a potentially safe immunomodulatory approach capable of limiting excessive inflammatory responses and supporting respiratory function in the context of viral-like lung inflammation.Keywords: Bacillus, Covid-19, Inflammation, Lung, Parabiotic, Spore, Poly I:CSubject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Inflammation is a crucial part of the immune system response to detrimental stimuli, participating in defense and the healing process. Nonetheless, the uncontrolled and overwhelming release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, known as a cytokine storm, results in systemic inflammatory symptoms along with substantial secondary organ damage1. The term “cytokine storm” (CS) was initially introduced in 1993 to describe the impacts of graft-versus-host disease (GvHD) and later used in the early 2000s to elucidate the consequences of various infectious and non-infectious diseases2,3. However, the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic highlighted its serious consequences4,5.

The SARS-CoV-2 virus starts infection via binding its spike protein to the ACE2 (angiotensin converting enzyme-2) receptor, mainly found on the surface of lung alveolar epithelial cells6. Upon cellular entry, viral genomes can be detected by corresponding pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs) such as TLR3, which initiates an inflammatory cascade of signaling events resulting in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, like interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α)7. These inflammatory cytokines subsequently recruit more innate immune cells from peripheral tissues, resulting in the continuous production of inflammatory cytokines that stimulate myelopoiesis and emergency granulopoiesis, thereby exacerbating lung epithelial damage8,9. Therefore, the mitigation CS is a primary strategy in the treatment of cytokine storm syndrome (CSS), including COVID-191,10,11.

The prevailing treatment for CS relies mainly on conventional anti-inflammatory drugs, including corticosteroids, IL-1 blockers (canakinumab, anakinra), IL-6 blockers (tocilizumab, sarilumab), and recombinant monoclonal antibodies such as itolizumab12,13. While systemic anti-inflammatory medications effectively dampen pro-inflammatory cytokines, they possess some drawbacks, such as diverse side effects, limited local efficacy, variable clinical outcomes, and an increased risk of subsequent infections14–16. Therefore, there is a critical necessity for developing local anti-inflammatory medicines for managing the inflammatory response in the lung following infection with the COVID-19 virus and other inflammatory stimuli.

Probiotics, live microorganisms that confer health benefits to their host when administered in adequate amounts, have attracted significant interest for their therapeutic effects in inflammatory disorders like inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), arthritis, skin ailments, and asthma17,18. However, concerns have been increasing regarding the safe use of these live microorganisms19. Others highlight altered long-term immune responses and the potential for the development of antibiotic resistance following the consumption of live cells17,20. Parabiotics, also referred to as “ghost probiotics,” are inactivated microbial cells that provide almost the same health benefits as probiotics (such as immunomodulation), but without the drawbacks of living cultures21. While notable probiotic strains belong to the Lactobacillus genus, spore-based probiotics, derived mainly from Bacillus species, have garnered interest due to their exceptional resilience to environmental variables such as temperature, acidity, and desiccation22. Recent publications have documented their anti-inflammatory benefits in gastrointestinal applications via coat proteins, as well as their antiviral capabilities23,24.

Polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly I:C) is a synthetic analog of viral double-stranded RNA and a widely used viral mimic that activates TLR3. Although poly I:C does not involve active viral replication, it reproducibly recapitulates key host-driven inflammatory features observed in severe viral pneumonia, while avoiding biosafety and confounding pathogen-specific variables. Therefore, in this study, we used poly (I:C) to establish an inflammatory murine model that mimics the pathologic changes of SARS-CoV-2.

Here, we demonstrate that heat-inactivated Bacillus spores act as parabiotics to mitigate poly I:C-induced inflammatory responses and lung injury. We show that these parabiotic spores reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine release, suppress expression of receptors for viral entry and innate sensing pathways, and counteract poly I:C-associated microbial alterations. Our findings identify spore-based parabiotics as a novel, safe, and effective approach to modulate immune responses and protect against respiratory hyperinflammation.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and cell line

Bacillus sp. 1630 F was previously isolated in our group from a fermented pickle25. The bacterium was cultured on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA, Merck Millipore, Germany) at 30 °C. RAW 264.7 cells (a murine macrophage cell line) were obtained from Ferdowsi University of Mashhad (Mashhad, Iran). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Spore parabiotic preparation and characterization

The Bacillus sp. 1630 F was cultivated in sporulation medium26 at 30 °C and 150 rpm for 5 days. The cell pellets were collected through centrifuging, suspended in sterile deionized water, and treated with lysozyme (0.1 mg/mL) for 10 min at 37 °C to lyse any remaining vegetative cells. The bacterial spores were washed with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the ghost cells were prepared by heat inactivation at 121 °C for 15 min. The desired cell concentrations were determined by direct microscopic counting using a hemocytometer. The sterility of the inactivated spores was verified via spread plating on TSA plates.

To evaluate the potential functional groups in the surfaces of live and dead spore cells, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were analyzed using the KBr pellet method within the range of 400–4000 cm− 1 at a resolution of 2 cm− 1 by a Thermo Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Nicolet, Madison, WI, USA).

The surface topography of the live and heat˗inactivated spores was studied using a multimode full plus model atomic force microscope (AFM) 0101/A (Ara Research, Iran). An aliquot of each cell was spread evenly onto a freshly cleaved mica sheet and then dried at ambient temperature. AFM images were obtained in a tapping mode using a silicon nanoprobe cantilever with a tip height of 10 nm and the resonance frequency of 218 kHz.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; LEO 435 VP) was used to examine the morphological characteristics of spores before and after undergoing heat treatment. The cell pellets were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, serially dehydrated with ethanol, and then coated with gold. The samples were observed under a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) (Mira 3-FEG TESCAN˗Czech Republic). The FE-SEM operated at an approximate accelerating voltage of 30 kV.

In vivo experiments

Male BALB/c mice were purchased from the Razi Institute (Mashhad, Iran). All mice were housed under a 12 h light/dark cycle, with temperature controlled at 20 ± 3 °C, relative humidity at 50% ± 10%, and provided a conventional balanced diet ad libitum. The animal experiment protocol was approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of Ferdowsi University of Mashhad (IR.UM.REC.1400.345). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Mice aged 6 to 8 weeks and weighing 22.7 ± 1.6 g were used; the experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1. The animals were anesthetized using 2% isoflurane in oxygen, delivered via an anesthetic vaporizer, until the righting reflex was lost. On days 1 to 5 heat˗inactivated spores (108 cells/mL in 20 µL PBS) were administered intranasally once a day. From days 6 to 8, 20 µL of poly I:C (1 mg/kg, Invivogen, USA) was administered daily, dropwise into their nostrils (parabiotic treatment in this study, Para). Regarding the inflammatory group (Inf in this study), 20 µL of sterile PBS buffer was administered on days 1 to 5, followed by three doses of poly I:C administration on days 6–8. Mice were euthanized on the ninth day of an experiment (24 h after the final poly I:C administration) to allow for the collection of samples and study the animals’ responses to the treatment conditions. Animals were euthanized by isoflurane overdose (2–5% in oxygen) followed by cervical dislocation to confirm death.

Experimental design. Mice were divided into an inflammation group (Inf) and a parabiotic-treated group (Para). Following induction of inflammation, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and tissue responses were assessed on day 9. Figure created using BioRender.com.

Bronchoalveolar lavage

Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed immediately following the collection of cardiac blood. A catheter was used to intubate the trachea, and 1 ml of PBS buffer was lavaged three times in the lung. The recovered fluid was centrifuged at 4 °C, 9000 g, for 10 min, and the supernatant was stored at − 80 °C until use.

Measurement of cytokines and proteins in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF)

Cytokines in BALF, including IL-1, IL-6, IL-10, TNF˗α, and TGF˗β, were quantified using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kit (ELISA, Karmania Pars gene, Iran).

Total protein levels in the acellular BAL fluids were measured via the Bradford test to assess overall cytotoxicity and heightened permeability of the bronchoalveolar-capillarity barrier. The lungs of mice were weighed and desiccated for seven days at 55 °C. The ratio was subsequently estimated without adjustment for blood content as an index of intrapulmonary fluid accumulation27.

Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)

The cell pellets from BAL (2˗4 × 104) were deposited on a slide. The slides were dried, and cells were subsequently stained using Giemsa. A microscopic examination was conducted to determine the respective amounts of neutrophils and lymphocytes.

Lung wet-to-dry weight ratio

Lung edema was quantified using the wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio. A defined portion of lung tissue from each mouse (approximately 50–100 mg wet weight) was excised immediately after sacrifice and weighed to obtain the wet weight. Samples were then dried in an oven at 60 °C for 48 h until a constant weight was reached and reweighed to determine dry weight. Measurements were performed using an analytical balance with 0.1 mg precision. The W/D ratio was calculated as wet weight divided by dry weight.

Bacterial cell counts in lung homogenate and BALF

The lungs were excised, weighed, and homogenized in 5 mL of sterile peptone water. The desired dilutions of the lung tissue homogenate and the BALF were inoculated on TSA and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C. The results were expressed as log10 colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of lung tissue or milliliter of BALF.

Histopathology

The excised lungs were rinsed with PBS buffer at pH 7.2 and fixed in a 10% (v/v) formalin solution. Subsequently, the lungs were cut into 4-mµ serial sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and toluidine blue for histopathological evaluation using light microscopy. To assess the severity of lung injury, a scoring system ranging from 0 to 2 was employed based on the following criteria: (i) presence of neutrophils in the alveolar space, (ii) presence of neutrophils in the interstitial space, (iii) number of hyaline membranes, (iv) amount of proteinaceous debris, and (v) extent of alveolar septal thickening. The final injury score was calculated using the following formula28:

Score = [20 × (i) + 14 × (ii) + 7 × (iii) + 7 × (iv) + 2 × (v)] / (number of fields × 100).

Imaging of parabiotics in vivo

An in vivo imaging system (IVIS) was employed to assess the presence of parabiotics in the lung. Rhodamine B (Ex:571; Em:591 nm) fluorescent dye was added to the 108 cells/mL spore pellets (live or heat-inactivated) (50 mg/mL) and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h with constant agitation. The bacterial spores were rinsed with PBS and administered intranasally to the mice as previously described. Animals were anesthetized 3 h after the intranasal administration, and the whole-body fluorescence imaging was performed using an IVIS Lumina LT series III imaging system (Caliper, MA, USA). Quantitative fluorescence intensity was calculated from the same images by defining a thoracic region of interest (ROI) and measuring total radiant efficiency (photons/sec/cm²/sr) using Living Image software. Control mice receiving Rhodamine solution without spores were included to determine the background fluorescence.

Quantitative real-time PCR

The murine macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 was cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% (v/v) FBS. RAW 264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells /well) were seeded in 6-well plates containing fresh medium and incubated overnight. The cells were subsequently exposed to spore parabiotic (1 × 106 cells/mL) for 3 h, after which poly I:C (10 µg/mL) was added for a further 3 h. A distinct group of cells only treated with poly I:C was used as an inflammatory control. Unstimulated cells served as baseline controls. Total RNA was extracted from flash-frozen cells using the kit prepared from Riz Molecule Dana (Mashhad, Iran) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The DNase-treated RNA was subsequently transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) with a cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. qRT-PCR was performed for Tlr3 and Ace2 genes. PCR reactions were conducted using SYBR Green I (Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan), in the ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems Co, California, USA). The primer sequences for each gene and amplification conditions were described elsewhere29,30. The expression levels of the target genes were normalized to the degree of β-Actin endogenous control expression present in each sample, and relative gene expressions to the untreated control group were calculated using the formula 2−ΔΔCT, while all primer pairs displayed similar amplification efficiency.

Microbiome analysis

Lungs from parabiotic and inflammatory mice groups were collected and stored at − 80 °C. Genomic DNA was extracted from lung samples using the FavorPrep™ Stool DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Taiwan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16 S rDNA gene were amplified using the multiplex barcoded primers 341 F (CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG) and 806R (GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT). The sequencing reaction was conducted using the DNBSEQ sequencing platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China), and 300-bp paired-end reads were generated. Raw data were processed to generate high-quality, clean reads using iTools Fqtools fqcheck (v.0.25), cutadapt (v.2.6), and readfq (v1.0). Paired-end reads were merged via FLASH (v1.2.11) with a minimum overlap of 15 bases and a maximum mismatch ratio of 0.1. Chimeric sequences were detected and removed with UCHIME (v4.2.40). Sequences were clustered to OTU (Operational Taxonomic Unit) with USEARCH (v7.0.1090) with a 97% threshold. Rarefaction curve analysis was used to evaluate whether sequenced data are sufficient to cover all potential species. OTU representative sequences were aligned against the database for taxonomic annotation by the RDP classifier (v2.2) software (sequence identity is set to be 0.6). The common/unique OTUs in each treatment were displayed with a Venn diagram generated by the R software (v3.4.1). Taxon abundance barplot was created with R Software (v3.4.1). The taxa will be classified into ‘others’ if their relative abundance is less than 0.5%. Alpha diversity is quantified using Shannon’s, and Simpson’s indices, as computed by Mothur (v.1.31.2).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure accuracy, and the resulting means and standard deviations (SD) were calculated. To compare the means at a 95% confidence level, ANOVA analysis, Dunnett’s multiple comparison tests, and Tukey’s test were implemented. GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1 software was utilized for statistical analysis.

Results

The experimental design and study timeline are summarized in Fig. 1. Mice were intranasally pretreated with heat-inactivated Bacillus spores before poly I:C challenge, followed by collection of lung tissues and BALF. Subsequent immunological, histological, and microbiome analyses performed in this study are outlined in the schematic.

Heat inactivation did not significantly alter the surface properties and lung distribution of the spores

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy of the bacterial spore surface exhibited characteristic absorption bands corresponding to various functional groups and chemical constituents integral to the spore’s complex structure. No significant shifts in peak positions or changes in band intensities were observed between probiotic and heat-inactivated parabiotic spores. Figure 2A, illustrates that the functional groups associated with proteins (amide bands at 1657 and 1544 cm-1 and N–H stretching around 3301 cm-1), lipids (C–H stretching at 2960 and 2872 cm-1), polysaccharides (C–O and P = O stretching at 1075 cm-1 and bands at 933 cm-1), and aromatic compounds (band at 3080) on the spore surface remained unchanged after heat exposure. AFM analysis revealed a measurable increase in surface roughness of bacterial spores following heat treatment. The average roughness (Ra) increased from 81.3 nm in untreated spores to 127.9 nm in heat-treated spores, while the root mean square roughness (Rq) rose from 94.2 nm to 148.6 nm. These results indicate enhanced nanoscale surface irregularities and heterogeneity due to thermal exposure (Fig. 2B). SEM images provided qualitative visualization of the spore surface morphology. Untreated spores exhibited smooth and intact surfaces with well-preserved structural features. In contrast, heat-treated spores showed a slightly rougher and more wrinkled surface texture. Although the surface of heat-treated spores appeared more irregular and folded, these changes were subtle and did not indicate significant physical damage or loss of spore integrity (Fig. 2C).

Quantitative analysis of IVIS fluorescence within the thoracic region of interest showed no statistically significant difference between groups (p > 0.05), consistent with comparable initial lung deposition observed in representative fluorescence images (Fig. 2D).

Intranasal administration of spore parabiotic modulates the inflammatory effects of poly I:C in BALB/c mice

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effects of heat-inactivated bacterial spores on lung inflammation, key cytokines were quantified in the BALF collected on day 9 from both experimental groups (Fig. 3A). In the inflammatory group (buffer administration on days 1–5, followed by poly I:C on days 6–8), pro-inflammatory cytokines were significantly elevated, with TNF-α reaching 32 ± 6 pg/mL, IL-1β at 165 ± 12 pg/mL, and IL-6 levels at 80 ± 7 pg/mL. Anti-inflammatory cytokines were comparatively lower, with TGF-β at 7 ± 2 pg/mL and IL-10 at 58 ± 6 pg/mL. In the parabiotic group (spores administered on days 1–5, followed by poly I:C), a significant reduction in pro-inflammatory cytokines was observed: TNF-α decreased to 10 ± 3 pg/mL (p < 0.01), IL-1β to 90 ± 8 pg/mL (p < 0.01), and IL-6 to 25 ± 4 pg/mL (p < 0.05). In contrast, anti-inflammatory cytokines showed a notable increase, with TGF-β rising to 20 ± 4 pg/mL (p < 0.05) and IL-10 to 124 ± 11 pg/mL (p < 0.001).

In the poly I:C-induced inflammation group, the BALF NLR (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio) was markedly elevated at 0.28 ± 0.03, indicating a neutrophil-dominant inflammatory milieu. In contrast, mice treated with intranasal parabiotic spores along with poly I:C showed a significantly lower NLR of 0.08 ± 0.02 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B).

Total protein concentration in BALF was measured on samples collected on day 9 from both experimental groups (Fig. 3C). Elevated BALF protein levels are indicative of increased epithelial permeability. They are commonly used as a marker of lung inflammation. Mice treated with Poly I:C alone (inflammatory group) exhibited significantly elevated BALF protein concentrations (0.060 ± 0.005 mg/mL) compared to those that received intranasal parabiotic spores in addition to poly I:C (0.050 ± 0.004 mg/mL) (p < 0.001).

To assess pulmonary edema and tissue inflammation, the lung wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio was measured (Fig. 3D). In the Poly I:C-induced inflammation group, the W/D ratio was significantly elevated at 11.0 ± 1.2, indicating substantial pulmonary edema. In contrast, mice treated with intranasal parabiotic spores exhibited a markedly reduced W/D ratio of 4.7 ± 0.8, demonstrating a significant attenuation of edema formation (p < 0.001).

Spore parabiotics suppress the receptor for viral entry and innate sensing pathways

We evaluated the in vitro expression levels of Tlr3 and Ace2 in cultured RAW 264.7 murine macrophage cells. Cells were treated with either poly I:C alone or in combination with heat-inactivated spores, and mRNA expression levels were quantified using real-time PCR. Poly I:C stimulation led to robust upregulation of Tlr3, with a 213-fold increase compared to untreated controls. However, co-treatment with bacterial spores dramatically reduced Tlr3 expression to 8-fold relative to the same control (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3E). Similarly, the expression of Ace2 was significantly induced by poly I:C, reaching a 2.8-fold increase relative to baseline (Fig. 3F). Notably, this induction was markedly attenuated in the presence of bacterial spores, with Ace2 expression reduced to 0.3-fold compared to controls (p < 0.001).

Spore parabiotic attenuates Poly I:C-induced tissue injury

Lung histology revealed extensive peribronchial and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates, alveolar wall thickening, and disruption of alveolar architecture in mice challenged with poly I:C (Fig. 4A1, B1). In contrast, pretreatment with heat-inactivated Bacillus spores markedly attenuated these changes, with only mild focal infiltrates and preservation of alveolar spaces and epithelial integrity (Fig. 4A2, B2) that were comparable to the normal lung architecture (Fig. 4A3, B3). The degree of inflammation was significantly less in the parabiotic group than in the inflammation group.

Spore parabiotic mitigates poly I:C-induced alteration in the lung microbiome

The microbial populations in lung tissue BALF were quantified by culturing on TSA. Within the poly I:C-induced inflammatory model, parabiotic pretreatment was associated with a relative reduction in culturable microbial burden in lung and BALF samples compared with poly I:C-challenged animals without spores (Fig. 5A). Specifically, the logarithmic count of culturable microbes in lung tissue was 4.75 ± 0.2 in the inflammatory group, which decreased markedly to 1.93 ± 0.2 in the parabiotic group (p < 0.05). Similarly, in BALF samples, the microbial population in the inflammatory group was 4.86 ± 0.1, whereas the parabiotic treatment reduced it significantly to 1.67 ± 0.1 (p < 0.05).

(B) Rarefaction curves showing sequencing depth and observed species richness across inflammation (Inf) and parabiotic (Para) groups. (G, H) Heatmaps of relative phylum and genus level abundances (log10-transformed) demonstrating distinct clustering of Inf and Para microbial communities. To investigate the impact of intranasal administration of heat-inactivated Bacillus spores on the pulmonary microbiome under poly I:C–induced inflammation, 16 S rRNA sequencing was performed on lung samples obtained on day 9 from BALB/c mice. A total of 67,888 paired-end clean reads were obtained from the inflammatory group (Inf), and 68,555 paired-end reads from the parabiotic group (Par), ensuring sufficient sequencing depth for downstream analyses (Fig. 5B). Alpha-diversity analysis revealed that microbial richness was comparable between groups (chao1 and ace indexes). For inflammation and parabiotic cohorts, a total of 121 and 124 OTUs were identified (sobs index). Venn diagram analysis identified 80 taxa shared between both groups, constituting the core lung microbiome. Nevertheless, the inflammatory group harbored 40 unique strains, while the parabiotic group contained 43 unique strains. The evenness and overall diversity differed markedly, while the Shannon index increased from 1.56 to 2.07, and the Simpson index decreased from 0.36 to 0.26 in the inflammation and parabiotic groups, respectively (Fig. 5C). These findings indicate that spore pretreatment alleviated the loss of microbial diversity typically associated with poly I:C-induced inflammation. Taxonomic profiling further supported these trends (Fig. 5D-F). At the phylum level, both groups were dominated by Proteobacteria, yet the parabiotic group displayed higher proportions of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria. Low abundance of Cyanobacteria and Candidatus Saccharibacteria was identified among the lung sample of the parabiotic group. At lower taxonomical classification, the microbial composition between the inflammation and parabiotic mouse cohorts became more apparent. A closer inspection of the lung microbial composition at the Family level shows that Lactobacillaceae, Pasteurellaceae, Burkholderiaceae, Bradyrhizobiaceae, and Propionibacteriaceae were more abundant in parabiotic mice. In contrast Moraxellaceae and Erythrobacteraceae were more abundant in inflammatory mice. At the genus level, Enhydrobacter, Psychrobacter, Pasteurella, and Porphyrobacter were more abundant in the inflammatory group, whereas parabiotic samples exhibited a more balanced composition with enrichment of commensal genera such as Lactobacillus, Propionibacterium, and Burkholderia. The higher abundance of unclassified and uncultured bacteria in the parabiotic samples reflects further differences in the microbial community driven by the ghost spore. Heatmap analysis (log10-transformed abundances) demonstrated clear segregation between groups (Fig. 5G-H). The inflammatory group clustered with a higher representation of Proteobacteria and reduced abundance of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria. In contrast, the parabiotic group exhibited increased contributions of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria, along with the emergence of low-abundance phyla such as Candidatus Saccharibacteria, Cyanobacteria, and Chloroflexi, consistent with enhanced microbial heterogeneity. Heat map analysis at the genus level shows the inflammatory group clustered with higher representation of Enhydrobacter, Psychrobacter and reduced abundance of Skermanella, Saccharibacteria, and Burkholderia. In contrast, the parabiotic group exhibited increased contributions of Lactobacillus and Burkholderia and reduced abundance of Brucella and Serratia.

Discussion

This study was designed as a preventive proof-of-concept investigation to assess whether heat-inactivated Bacillus spores can prime the pulmonary immune environment and mitigate subsequent inflammatory responses to a viral mimic challenge. Heat-inactivated spores retain functional surface components essential for lung deposition and immunomodulation while eliminating risks associated with live probiotic administration. The absence of significant changes in the FTIR spectra of bacterial spores before and after heat treatment highlights the remarkable thermal stability of their surface molecular structures. The preservation of key functional groups, including amide bonds in proteins, aliphatic chains in lipids, and carbohydrate moieties in polysaccharides, suggests that the biochemical content of the spore remains intact even after exposure to elevated temperatures. This resilience can be attributed to the unique architecture of bacterial spores, which includes highly cross-linked proteins and durable polysaccharide layers that form a protective barrier against environmental challenges, like heat, desiccation, and other extreme conditions31,32. These findings reinforce the understanding of bacterial spores as highly resistant biological entities and provide significant insights for developing strategies targeting inactivated probiotics (parabiotics). The in vivo imaging data in this study demonstrated that both heat-treated and untreated bacterial spores were capable of retention in the lungs following intranasal administration. These findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that intranasal administration was an effective route for delivering biotherapeutics to the lungs24,33.

The cytokine profile observed in BALF reveals a marked suppression of key pro-inflammatory mediators accompanied by a substantial increase in anti-inflammatory cytokines. These results are parallel with previous findings where probiotics modulated inflammatory responses by promoting the activation of T regulatory cells (Treg)34, while suppressing Th17 activity35 or converting macrophages to M2 phenotype36–38. However, as cytokine levels were measured at a single post-challenge time point, the observed concentrations likely reflect subacute inflammatory activity rather than the early peak cytokine surge typically associated with an acute cytokine storm. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as modulation of ongoing inflammatory responses and tissue injury, rather than prevention of an immediate hyper-acute cytokine storm. The total protein concentration in BALF serves as a well-established biomarker for assessing pulmonary inflammation and alveolar-capillary barrier integrity39. Elevated BALF protein levels indicate increased vascular permeability and leakage of plasma proteins into the alveolar space, which are hallmark features of lung injury and inflammation. Our findings indicate that poly I:C administration significantly increased BALF protein concentration in the inflammatory group. Conversely, pretreatment with heat-treated bacterial spores resulted in a statistically significant reduction in BALF protein levels (p < 0.001), suggesting a protective effect on the alveolar-capillary barrier. This protective effect is likely mediated by the spores’ immunomodulatory properties, including downregulation of key pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α), which are known to exacerbate endothelial and epithelial permeability in the lung40,41. The upregulated anti-inflammatory cytokines IL-10 and TGF-β in the parabiotic group likely contribute to restoring and maintaining lung tissue homeostasis by promoting resolution of inflammation and tissue repair42,43. An elevated neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in BALF reflects the pro-inflammatory milieu44, while the observed reduction of BALF NLR with parabiotic intervention represents a marked shift in the lung inflammatory environment.

ACE2 serves as the primary receptor for the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into host cells45,46. Similar downregulatory effects on ACE2 expression have been reported with other immunomodulatory agents, such as corticosteroids47. Although the bacterial spores slightly suppress the poly I:C-induced upregulation of Ace2 gene expression in RAW 264.7 macrophages, since this protein dose not mediate viral entry in the murine cell line, further studies are required in human cells to determine whether parabiotics can influence viral entry. TLR3 is a key pattern recognition receptor that senses viral double-stranded RNA and activates downstream pro-inflammatory signaling cascades48. While TLR3 activation is crucial for antiviral defense, its overactivation contributes to cytokine storm and immunopathology in viral infections, including SARS-CoV and influenza49. Our observation of a dramatic reduction in Tlr3 expression suggests a potent anti-inflammatory effect, potentially curtailing excessive immune activation. This finding aligns with prior studies reporting that the immunomodulatory properties of probiotics can attenuate TLR-mediated signaling and cytokine production50. Beyond viral infections, the control of TLR3 signaling also holds therapeutic potential in chronic inflammatory diseases where aberrant activation of TLR pathways contributes to pathology51.

While the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed downregulation of Tlr3 and Ace2 expression were not directly investigated in the present study, based on prior reports, spore surface components such as coat proteins, peptidoglycan, and polysaccharides may interact with innate immune receptors, leading to modulation of TLR signaling and immune training effects52. Intranasal administration of poly I:C induced a significant increase in culturable microbial load in both lung tissue and BALF. This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that inflammation disrupts pulmonary homeostasis, leading to microbial dysbiosis and increased bacterial colonization53. The elevated microbial burden in inflamed lungs likely exacerbates the inflammatory response, creating a vicious cycle that contributes to disease progression. Pretreatment with bacterial spores markedly reduced the microbial load in both lung and BALF samples, highlighting the potent microbiota-modulating effects of spores. Given that the spores used in this study were heat-inactivated, the observed microbiome-associated effects are unlikely to result from direct colonization. Rather, the data support an indirect, host-mediated mechanism in which parabiotic structural components modulate immune balance and inflammatory tone, thereby reshaping the local microbial ecosystem. Metagenomic results underscore the significant role of the inflammatory response modulation in shaping the lung microbiome, thereby contributing to further attenuation of inflammation. Poly I:C-induced inflammation disrupted the pulmonary microbiome, reducing diversity indices and enriching the Proteobacteria/ Firmicutes ratio, commonly observed in inflammatory disorders54. Parabiotics may alter this unfavorable ratio while increasing the overall population of Firmicutes. Members of this phylum can mitigate inflammation by producing chemicals like short-chain fatty acids55. Inflammatory condition was also associated with a reduction in Lactobacillaceae, consistent with prior observation that negatively correlated low abundance of this family with lung inflammation and IL-6 production56. We found that Psychrobacter was highly abundant in the lungs of inflammatory mice, while its prevalence markedly diminished in the parabiotic-treated cohort. Psychrobacter may serve as a potential marker for pulmonary inflammation. Prior research has demonstrated that lipopolysaccharides (LPS) from various Psychrobacter species, notably P. cryohalorolentis and P. arcticus, can activate macrophages and elicit inflammatory responses57. Because an untreated healthy baseline microbiome was not included, the observed microbial changes should be interpreted as relative modulation within the inflammatory model rather than complete restoration to physiological lung homeostasis. Future studies incorporating baseline-controlled and longitudinal microbiome analyses will be required to define the full restorative potential of spore-based parabiotics.

In summary, heat-inactivated Bacillus spores act as safe and effective parabiotics, retaining key structural and functional features while eliminating the risks associated with live bacteria. Intranasal administration provides a localized immunomodulatory effect, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines, preserving alveolar integrity, and modulating pulmonary microbial balance. While this approach demonstrates the capacity of heat-inactivated spores to prime the pulmonary immune environment and restrain subsequent inflammation, it does not address therapeutic efficacy when administered after inflammation is established. In addition, the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed downregulation of Tlr3 and Ace2 expression were not directly interrogated. Future studies should evaluate post-injury administration, downstream signaling pathways, and dose dependency, and validate these findings in live respiratory viral infection models. Translational efforts will also require assessment in additional animal models (both sexes) and human trials to establish safety and long-term impact on the pulmonary microbiome.
