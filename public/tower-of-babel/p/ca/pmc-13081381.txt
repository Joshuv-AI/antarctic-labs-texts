Clinical profile of diabetic ketoacidosis and its severity in adults: evidence from ALERT Comprehensive Specialized Hospital
Authors: Minychel Wale Aynalem, Biniyam Nigusse, Yabetse Legesse, Dawit Girma Abebe, Teshale Ayele, Hailemichael Getachew, Asteway Mulat Haile, Kibret Enyew Belay

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is one of the most common acute complications that necessitates immediate medical intervention [1]. DKA results from absolute or relative insulin deficiency and elevated counterregulatory hormones, leading to accelerated catabolism, hyperglycemia, lipolysis, ketonemia, and metabolic acidosis [2]. DKA is associated with high mortality rates, and its mortality rate ranges from 2 to 5% in industrialized and 6 to 24% in developing nations [3]. DKA has a significant economic burden on individuals, health care systems, and payers, which imposes severe financial strain on individuals and families [4, 5]. In Ethiopia, mortality from DKA was found to be > 10% [6].

The accepted diagnostic criteria for DKA proposed by recent consensus report is glucose level ≥ 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or prior history of diabetes, the presence of beta-hydroxybutyrate concentration ≥ 3 mmol/l or ketones in the urine (+ 2) or greater, and serum bicarbonate < 18 mmol/l or PH < 7.3 [7]. In low-resource settings like Ethiopia, availing of the accepted diagnostic criteria for DKA is challenging, due to the ill supply of laboratory reagents and non-functioning laboratory machines. Thus, clinicians are managing patients and grouping the severity of DKA based on limited diagnostic criteria, such as clinical signs and symptoms (such as nausea, vomiting, Kussmaul breathing, acetone breath with a sweet fruity odor, altered mentation etc.), supported by serum glucose level, urine ketone, and sometimes PH or bicarbonate. The JBDS aims to improve DKA treatment by offering evidence-based guidelines and improving inpatient care through a standardized classification of DKA severity [8].

Globally, only a few studies examined the clinical features and severity of DKA among patients with diabetes implying a dearth of evidence on the clinical features, severity, and factors associated with DKA severity in Ethiopia [9, 10]. This hinders efforts by researchers, physicians, and policymakers to improve DKA care services and lower DKA-related mortality. Thus, this study sought to close the gaps by evaluating the clinical characteristics, severity of DKA, and factors associated with its severity in adult DKA patients admitted to ALERT Comprehensive Specialized Hospital.

Methods

Study design and population

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was employed on adult DKA patients presented to ALERT Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The medical records of all DKA patients who were admitted to the emergency unit from September 1st, 2020 to September 1st, 2023, were included in the study. Age above 16 years old patients with diabetes and DKA who received treatment in ALERT hospital’s medical emergency unit were included. Patients under 16 years of age, pregnant women, breastfeeding women, and incomplete medical charts were excluded from the study. The sample size was determined based on the single population proportion formula: n= [(Zα/2)2 p (1-p)]/d2 with the assumption of a 95% confidence interval (Zα/2= 1.96), marginal error (d) of 5% and P = 50%. With a 10% non-response rate, the required total sample size was 422. Patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were selected using a convenience sampling technique. The medical records of patients with DKA admitted to the hospital from September 1, 2020, to September 1, 2023, were traced from the patients’ logbook and drawn from the card room. Finally, patients were enrolled in the study based on the previously defined eligibility criteria. The questionnaire was developed and pretested after reviewing relevant literature (see supplementary file) [11]. The ethical approval for this study was submitted to and approved by the ALERT comprehensive specialized hospital research and academic department institutional review board (IRB), under a reference number AR/02/2016.

Definitions

Diabetic ketoacidosis was defined by clinical signs and symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, Kussmaul respiration, acetone breath with a sweet fruity odor, and altered mental status, supported by hyperglycemia (blood glucose levels ≥ 200 mg/dL), the presence of ketones in the urine, and/or a pH < 7.3. Measurements of pH (for most participants) and serum bicarbonate levels were unavailable due to the low-resource setting [12]. Polyuria was defined as excretion of a urinary volume of > 50 ml/Kg/24 hours [13]. Polydipsia was defined as the intake of excessive fluid of more than three liters daily [14]. Comorbidities mean the occurrences of one or more medical problems (hypertension, overweight/obesity, dyslipidemia, or other chronic non-communicable diseases) with diabetes at the time of DKA presentation [15]. The severity of DKA was determined based on the modified version of JBDS guidelines [16].

JBDS criteria for DKA severity measurement

Severe DKA is characterized by the presence of one or more of the following criteria: blood ketones over 6 mmol/L; bicarbonate level below 5 mmol/L; venous/arterial pH below 7.0; hypokalemia on admission (under 3.5 mmol/L); Glasgow coma score less than 12 or abnormal alert, verbal, pain, unresponsive (AVPU) scale; peripheral capillary oxygen saturation below 92% on room air; systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg; pulse over 100 or below 60 bpm; and anion gap above 16 [9, 16]. In this study, we applied a modified version of the JBDS severity criteria that excluded blood ketone, anion gap, and bicarbonate measurements due to their unavailability.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS software, version 27.0, based in Chicago, IL, USA. Variables were assessed for a normal distribution. Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was done to check model fitness. Continuous variables were reported as means (± standard deviation), while categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Factors associated with DKA severity were identified by multivariate stepwise forward logistic regression analysis. The results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of DKA patient

A total of 422 charts were reviewed. Most 256 (60.7%) of the study participants were males. The age range was from 16 to 89 years with a mean (± standard deviation) age of 38.9 ± 16.6 years. The majority 102(24.2%) of respondents were within the age range of 25–34 years. About 389(92.2%) and 261(61.8%) participants reside in urban areas and came from public health centers through referral linkage, respectively (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics of study participants

Of the total 422 reviewed charts, 151 (35.8%) of DKA patients were known type 2 DM patients. Among the participants, 298 cases (70.6%) had diabetes for less than 5 years. Most of study participants (410 cases, 97.2%) had a normal GCS score of 15/15. Besides, more than half (226 cases, 53.6%) of the study participants were diagnosed with a certain form of comorbidity. The frequency of DKA events in most of patients was reported only once (87%) per year. Most of the study participants presented with polyuria (in 319 cases, 75.6%), followed by dryness of mouth 298(70.6%). The most common precipitating factor was infection 141(33.4%) (Table 2).

Factors associated with severe DKA among study participants

Logistic regression was employed to identify the determinants of severe DKA. In the bivariate analysis, three variables, including sex, duration of DM, and comorbidities were associated with severe DKA (p < 0.25). In multivariable logistic regression model, only two variables, namely sex and comorbidity were significantly associated with severe DKA (p ​​< 0.05). Female patients had 2.1 times higher odds of severe DKA [AOR = 2.1, 95% CI: 1.40–3.19, P < 0.001], while those with comorbidities had 50% lower odds of severe DKA [AOR = 0.50, 95% CI: 0.34–0.75] (Table 5).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the clinical pattern, severity of DKA, and factors associated with severe DKA among patients above 16 years old admitted with DKA at ALERT Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. The study findings revealed that DKA was prevalent among males aged 25 to 34 years. The finding was in line with studies conducted in Hawassa and Addis Ababa in Ethiopia [3, 17], India [18], and Brazil [19]. This is further supported by evidence showing that DKA commonly occurs in adults under 45 years, unlike hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state, which is more frequent in older individuals aged 45–64 years [7]. In this study, we found that the DKA occurred frequently in patients with known T2DM, similar to previous studies in China, Thailand, north Sweden, and Qatar [11, 20–22]. This may be due to the very high prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus in Ethiopia and the recent rise in the use of Sodium-Glucose Cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors. This fact aligns with other studies’ findings which emphasize that T2DM is more common cause of DKA than T1DM in sub-Saharan Africa [23, 24].

In this study, 31.9% of the DKA presented among newly diagnosed DM, which is in line with a study done in Qatar, where 35% of the newly diagnosed DM patients presented with DKA [25]. However, this figure is more than what was observed in Hawassa University comprehensive specialized hospital (53.85%) [3] and England (22.5 to 23.9%) [26]. The cause of these variations could probably be multifactorial, including differences in socioeconomic status, and race/ethnicity.

Comorbidities significantly contribute to the development of DKA in patients with T2DM, and increase the risk of DKA severity [27]. In this study, 53.6% of the DKA patients had baseline comorbidities, similar to other studies finding from Northern Ethiopia (52.9%), Benghazi Libya (50%), and Dubai (46.3%) [28–30]. On the other hand, our finding was higher than a study done in Debre Tabor General Hospital (8.3%) [6], and lower than a study done in Johannesburg (71.5%) [31]. The existing difference in patient clinical characteristics might explain the discrepancy. Based on the modified version of JBDS criteria, 55% of the patients encountered severe DKA. This finding was higher than the studies done in Pakistan (12.4%), Gondar-Ethiopia (5.4%), USA (38%), and Libya (22%) [6, 32–34]. On the other hand, the current finding is lower than the studies from Ghana (71%) [9] and Brazil (71.1%) [19]. The reason for this variation might be due to differences in DKA severity measurement tool, and sample size; since the current study enrolled more participants. Only 20.9% of patients in our study had pH data available, and we acknowledge that this limitation may have affected the severity classification.

In this study, the frequency of DKA events in most patients was reported only once (87%) per year. This showed a lower rate of recurrence in our context compared with a study conducted at Prince Sultan Military Medical City (45.6%), King Abdulaziz Medical City Emergency Department and Medical Wards (60.56%), Adama University Hospital (65%), and public hospital in Nekemte (88.1%) in which the recurrence was reported ≥ 2 episodes per year [35–38]. Our study depicted that the most frequent clinical presentations were polyuria (75.6%%), followed by dryness of mouth (70.6%). In this regard, our finding was similar with a study done at Saint Paul’s Hospital Millennium Medical College and Dilla University Referral Hospital, respectively [17, 39]. Surprisingly, a few patients had polyphagia (11.6%). It varied from a study conducted in Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital in Ethiopia and Morocco, where polyphagia was reported in 50.3% and 58% of DKA patients, respectively [3, 40]. The reason for this variation might be due to dietary habits and medical conditions.

In the current study, infection was the first most common precipitating factor (33.4%), consistent with other reports from developing nations [41, 42]. However, some studies revealed that inappropriate insulin therapy followed by infections are the most important precipitating factor [35, 43]. This variation could be attributed to differences in patient demographic and clinical characteristics such as the patient’s admission features and precipitating condition for DKA. Urine ketone levels interpreted as + 1 (small), + 2 (moderate), and + 3 (large) indicate increasing concentrations of ketonuria [44]. In this study, 32.9% of DKA patients had urine ketone level + 2; a consistent finding with studies from St. Paul’s Millennium Medical College (39.78%), and Adama Hospital Medical College (37.25%) [36, 45]. However, the finding was higher than the study conducted in Japan (23.6%) and Kenya (20.5%) [46, 47]. Though difficult to explain this discrepancy, differences in etiology of DKA, and ethnic variations in insulin secretion and action might be responsible.

In our study, the average baseline potassium was 4.42 ± 0.8 mmol/L. The present study found two factors that were associated with severe DKA. Females were more likely to experience severe DKA, consistent with findings from a study conducted in Ghana [9]. This may be explained by hormonal influences on insulin sensitivity, an increase in blood sugar fluctuations, higher rates of stress and depression affecting adherence, and delayed hospital presentation due to social or caregiving roles [48]. In addition, insulin omission and disordered eating behaviors, which are more common in females, may also contribute. However, a study done in Tunisia revealed that male sex was significantly associated with the severity of DKA [49]. The presence of comorbidities is less likely to have severe DKA. This may be explained by patients with chronic conditions often have earlier detection and treatment through more frequent medical check-ups, and maintain established connections with healthcare professionals, incidental identification of hyperglycemia or early ketosis, greater awareness of symptoms, and use of medications such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or statins that provide secondary metabolic benefits [50].

Indeed, our research has certain limitations. First, this study was retrospective and cross-sectional, which limits the ability to infer causality or observe changes over time. Second, this study was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings or populations. Thus, future multicenter and follow-up studies are needed to re-assess our findings in a larger cohort of patients. Lastly, we used a modified version of the JBDS clinical criteria to define and categorize DKA severity due to the unavailability of key investigations such as blood ketones, serum bicarbonate, and anion gap. This limitation may have introduced misclassification bias and affected the accurate estimation of DKA prevalence and severity.

Conclusion

In this study, more than half of the study participants had severe diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Early identification and management of infections in patients with diabetes are crucial to prevent severe DKA. Female gender was found to be an associated factor for severity. According to the JBDS criteria for severe DKA, the most frequently observed abnormalities were tachycardia and pH below 7.0. In resource-limited settings, using JBDS clinical features can help categorize DKA patients and guide management. However, responsible stakeholders should ensure access to basic laboratory tests for more accurate diagnosis and follow-up.
