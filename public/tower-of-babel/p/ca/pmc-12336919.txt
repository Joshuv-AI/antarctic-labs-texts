The role of VISTA engagement in limiting neutrophil-mediated inflammation
Authors: Elizabeth C Nowak, Jiannan Li, Zachary T Peters, Lindsay K Mendyka, Mohamed A ElTanbouly, Wilson L Davis, Petra Sergent, J Louise Lines, Nicole C Smits, Rodwell Mabaera, Shibani Rajanna, Catherine Carriere, Brent Koehn, Jeremy Allred, Bruce R Blazar, Christopher M Burns, Randolph J Noelle, Sladjana Skopelja-Gardner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA growing body of evidence suggests that the immune checkpoint inhibitory receptor VISTA plays a central role in the regulation of innate immunity in the settings of inflammatory diseases and cancer. Neutrophils are among the cells that have the highest membrane density of surface VISTA. In this study, targeting VISTA on neutrophils with a monoclonal antibody resulted in a striking reduction in their lipopolysaccharide (LPS)- and chemokine-induced peripheral accumulation but did not reduce neutrophil levels at steady state. Fc receptor engagement and macrophages were required for the effects of anti-VISTA antibody on neutrophils. Concomitant with reduced peripheral neutrophil numbers, targeting VISTA increased neutrophil clearance by macrophages in the liver. In a murine model of neutrophil-mediated arthritis, anti-VISTA antibody treatment ameliorated disease severity, which was associated with reduced myeloperoxidase activity in the joints. These studies identify a novel therapeutic opportunity for targeting VISTA to control neutrophil-mediated inflammation and tissue injury.

Introduction

Numerous studies have underscored the critical role of neutrophils as effectors of innate immunity.1–4 More recently, their roles as important regulators of chronic inflammation and adaptive immunity have also been elucidated.5–7 While the cascade of events regulating neutrophil recruitment and initiation of the inflammatory response has been explored at the cellular and molecular levels, the role negative checkpoint regulators play in neutrophil activation and chemotaxis remains largely unknown.

Our group has extensively studied V-domain Ig suppressor of T cell activation (VISTA,8 also known as PD-1H9 and DD1-α10 and encoded by the gene Vsir), a negative checkpoint regulator highly expressed on myeloid cells and to a lesser degree on T lymphocytes.8,11,12 Multiple observations suggest that VISTA may play a cell-intrinsic role in the function of innate immune cells, including neutrophils. First, Vsir deficient (Vsir−/−) mice develop markedly attenuated collagen antibody-induced arthritis, a disease model mainly driven by neutrophils.13 Similarly, VISTA deficiency ameliorates nephrotoxic nephritis, an effect associated with reduced neutrophil activation.14 VISTA deficiency significantly reduced the presence of granulocytic myeloid-derived suppressor cells (G-MDSCs) in tumors.12 In macrophages, its loss alters chemokine receptor recycling and subsequent migration.15 Finally, several reports demonstrated an important role for VISTA in MDSC-mediated suppression of T cell responses in both human and murine systems.16–18 Therefore, while accumulating evidence suggests that VISTA plays an important role in regulating innate immune cells and inflammation, if and how antibody targeting of VISTA on neutrophils shapes their response to different inflammatory stimuli has not been addressed.

In this study, we provide the first insights into the profound and primary role of VISTA in regulating neutrophil accumulation in response to systemic inflammatory stimuli. We show that in vivo antibody targeting of VISTA decreases peripheral neutrophil levels in both lipopolysaccharide (LPS)- and chemokine (CXCL2)- stimulated inflammation. The suppressive effects of engaging VISTA in vivo require VISTA expression on neutrophils and the expression of Fc receptor γ (FcR-γ) on phagocytic macrophages. Anti-VISTA antibody increases neutrophil cell death in the context of inflammation but not at steady state, particularly in the liver. Finally, we show that antibody targeting of VISTA attenuates K/BxN serum transfer arthritis, a model dependent upon neutrophil migration to the affected joints.19 These results have broad implications for both the fundamental understanding of neutrophil persistence in peripheral tissues and the therapeutic targeting of VISTA in neutrophil-mediated inflammatory diseases.

Materials and methods

Mice

Vsir−/− mice were obtained from the Mutant Mouse Resource & Research Center and bred onto a C57BL/6N (B6) background. Controls for them were C57BL/6 (Strain Code: 027) mice from Charles River Laboratories (Wilmington, Massachusetts, USA). Fcer1g−/− (Strain: 583), Fcgr2b−/− (Strain: 580), and control C57BL/6 (Strain: B6) were purchased from Taconic Biosciences (Germantown, New York, USA). Vsirfl/fl mice were previously described and provided by Sam Lee.10 These were intercrossed with S100a8cre (JAX Stock no: 021614) mice to generate S100a8cre VISTAfl/fl mice and cre-negative littermates. Balb/cJ (Stock no: 000651) were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine, USA). Human VISTA knock-in (hVISTAKI) mice were previously described.20 KRN T cell receptor transgenic mice were provided by Diane Mathis and Christopher Benoist (Harvard Medical School). They were interbred with NOD/ShiLtJ (JAX Stock no: 001976) to generate K/BxN mice. All mice were at least 8 wk old at the start of the experiment. Controls were age and sex-matched across groups. Studies were approved by Dartmouth’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. All animals were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility at Geisel School of Medicine at Dartmouth.

In vivo neutrophil activation and migration

Mice were injected i.p. with 10 µg LPS purified from E. coli O55: B5 (Millipore Sigma, St Louis, Missouri, USA) and treated i.p. with 200 µg hamster Ig (Control Ig; BioXcell, Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA), anti-mVISTA (8G8 or 13F3), human IgG2 (Control Ig, CB2, Crown Bioscience, Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA), or anti-hVISTA (803), respectively. Antibodies against mouse and human VISTA were generated as previously described.8,11 For NK cell depletion, mice received 250 µg of anti-NK1.1 (clone PK136) or mIgG2a isotype controls (Bioxcell) i.p. one day prior to LPS treatment. For phagocytic macrophage depletion, mice received 1 mg of clodronate liposomes (Liposoma BV, Netherlands) or 200 µl of control liposomes i.p. daily for 4 consecutive days with the last shot occurring the day before LPS treatment. To assess the effects of anti-Ly6G antibody, mice received 200 µg of clone 1A8 or rIgG2a isotype control (Bioxcell) i.p. In adoptive transfer experiments, bone marrow was harvested from donor mice and enriched for neutrophils using a Neutrophil Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Isolated cells were labeled with Cell Trace CFSE or Violet Proliferation Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s directions and injected at 5 × 105 to 1 × 106 cells/mouse i.v. before LPS and antibody treatment. Tissues were harvested at 12 or 20 h after LPS treatment. To study neutrophil migration, mice were injected with 200 µg antibody i.v. 1 hour before receiving an i.p. injection of 1 µg CXCL2 (Peprotech, Rocky Hill, New Jersey, USA). Readout was 1 h after the CXCL2 injection.

Cell isolation and flow cytometry

Harvested tissues were initially placed in Hank’s balanced salt solution. Spleens were manually dissociated, filtered, and red blood cells lysed. Bone marrow was isolated using centrifugation as described by Amend et al.21 and underwent red blood cell lysis and filtering. The liver was processed as described by Daemen et al.22 except the perfusion was done manually. The cell numbers were normalized to the total weight of the liver in experiments where a portion of the tissue was taken for histology and the remaining was processed to obtain cells for flow cytometry.

The following antibodies were purchased conjugated to FITC, PE, PerCP, PE/Dazzle594, PE/Cyanine5, PE/Cyanine7, APC, Alexa Fluor 647, APC-Cy7, APC/Fire810 Brilliant Violet 421, eFluor 450, Brilliant Violet 510, Brilliant Violet 605, and/or Brilliant Violet 711: CD45 (30-F11), CD11b (M1/70), Ly6G (1A8), Ly6C (HK1.4), mVISTA (MIH63), hVISTA (MIH65), CD19 (6D5), NKp46 (29A1.4), F4/80 (BM8), CD3 (17A2), CD4 (RM4-5), CD8a (53-6.7), CD44 (IM7), FoxP3 (FJK-16S), CD47 (miap301) from Biolegend (San Diego, California, USA), ThermoFisher Scientific (Waltham, MA), or BD Biosciences (Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA).

Except for the experiments using SYTOX, cells were stained using LIVE/DEAD Fixable Blue, Near-IR, or Yellow Dead Cell Stain Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s directions. Samples were incubated with TruStain FcX PLUS (S170011E; Biolegend) and stained with an antibody cocktail. For nuclear staining, cells were fixed and stained using a Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Kit (Tonbo Biosciences, San Diego, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s directions. Flow cytometry was performed on MACSQuant, Accuri, or ZE5 instruments. Data analysis was done using FlowJo software (Treestar Incorporated, Ashland, Oregon, USA). In experiments using SYTOX Blue (ThermoFisher Scientific), cells were stained for surface markers as above, ApoTracker Green (Biolegend) according to the manufacturer’s directions, and SYTOX Blue was added shortly before performing flow cytometry.

Histology

Liver tissue was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and paraffin embedded into cassettes that were used to make slides with 4-micron sections. Staining was done following the guidelines in the Opal 7-color Manual IHC Kit (Akoya Biosciences, Catalog: NEL811001KT). After the last round of multiplexing, slides are incubated with Spectral DAPI, washed, and mounted with Prolong Diamond (Thermo Fisher, Catalog: P36961). Antibodies used include anti-myeloperoxidase (MPO, ThermoFisher Scientific, Catalog: PA5-16672), anti-F4/80 (Cell Signaling Technologies, Catalog: 70076), and goat-anti rabbit HRP (ThermoFisher Scientific, Catalog: 31462). A Vectra 3.0 microscope was used for image acquisition. Briefly, whole slide scans were done, Phenochart was used to select MSI regions, and images were acquired at 40× magnification. Spectral libraries were generated using an unstained control slide, single color control slides for each Opal stain, and a slide for DAPI. Images were unmixed using InForm 2.60 software. ImageJ software was used to add scale bars and adjust brightness and contrast. Analysis was performed using HALO (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, New Mexico, USA) modules Area Quantification FL v2.3.3 and CytoNuclear FL v2.0.12.

K/BxN serum transfer arthritis

Serum from donor K/BxN mice was isolated after mice had developed spontaneous arthritis. To induce arthritis in Balb/cJ mice, pooled K/BxN serum was injected into mice at 200 µl/mouse i.p. on day 0. Each paw was scored every day over 8 d as follows: 1—swelling in 1 joint type (considering ankle, foot, and toes), 2—swelling in 2 joints, and 3—swelling in all 3 joints. Total score for a mouse was the sum of score of all 4 paws. For these experiments, mice were treated with 300 µg/mouse with indicated antibody. In some experiments, mice were given luminol at 100 mg/kg and imaged on a Xenogen IVIS machine to assess flux (photons/second).

Results

Treatment with anti-VISTA antibody suppresses LPS-induced neutrophil accumulation in the periphery

VISTA is a negative checkpoint regulator that is expressed on both myeloid cells and T cells, with a higher expression on granulocytes and monocytes in the periphery.8,9,11 To first assess if VISTA was expressed on neutrophils in the bone marrow, the site of their generation, or was upregulated upon their entry into the periphery, we evaluated VISTA expression on bone marrow and spleen cells. Robust VISTA expression was observed in neutrophils from both sites (Fig. 1A), suggesting anti-VISTA antibodies could directly target neutrophils.

To test if targeting VISTA impacts neutrophil responsiveness to an inflammatory challenge, we treated B6 mice with anti-VISTA monoclonal antibody (mAb) immediately following systemic administration of LPS (Fig. 1B). In vivo stimulation with the Toll-like receptor 4 ligand LPS results in the release of chemokines and cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor (TNF-α), interleukin 6, CXCL2, and CXCL5, that cause activation and mobilization of neutrophils into the periphery.23–25 Mice treated with anti-VISTA mAb had significantly decreased neutrophil levels in the spleen and blood 1 d after the LPS challenge compared to the control group (Fig. 1B). The same effect was seen with two different clones of anti-VISTA mAb, 8G8 and 13F3 (Fig. S1A). Like with 8G8, a previous study showed that anti-VISTA 13F3 did not decrease neutrophil levels at steady state.20 Targeting VISTA had a similar effect on neutrophils in human VISTA knock-in (hVISTAKI) mice, which express human VISTA in the mouse locus of this gene.20 In comparison to the control antibody, anti-human VISTA (hVISTA) antibody significantly decreased neutrophil levels in the spleen and blood following LPS challenge (Fig. S1B). Administering anti-VISTA mAb at the time of peak neutrophil infiltration (6 hours after LPS) effectively reduced peripheral neutrophil accumulation (Fig. 1C). In contrast, when anti-VISTA mAb was administered 6 h prior to LPS exposure, its effect on neutrophils was lost, although a trend in decreased spleen neutrophils was noted. This diminished may be due to the short half-life of the antibody.

LPS challenge decreased neutrophil numbers in the bone marrow of both anti-VISTA and control antibody-treated mice to a similar extent at 1 d post-treatment, suggesting egress from the bone marrow (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the effect of anti-VISTA mAb on neutrophils was not associated with an increase in neutrophil VISTA expression after LPS treatment, as cell surface VISTA levels on neutrophils either remained constant or decreased 1, 3, and 6 h after LPS challenge (Fig. S2A, B). Despite also expressing VISTA, the numbers of different myeloid or T cell subsets did not decrease in response to anti-VISTA treatment (Fig. S2A, C). Like with 8G8, other VISTA-expressing myeloid cells were not reduced with 13F3 treatment at steady state and LPS treatment (not shown). These data suggest that anti-VISTA mAb primarily suppresses neutrophil response to inflammation without affecting other VISTA-expressing cell populations.

VISTA deficiency or anti-VISTA antibody do not impact neutrophil numbers or distribution in the absence of inflammation

To understand if VISTA deficiency and antibody targeting of VISTA affect neutrophils at steady-state, we quantified neutrophil numbers, trafficking, and distribution in the absence of an inflammatory stimulus. No differences in neutrophil numbers (bone marrow and spleen) or frequency (blood) were observed between wild-type (B6) and Vsir−/− mice at steady state (not shown). This was consistent with a previous report showing a minor increase in granulocyte colony-forming potential from VISTA-deficient bone marrow in comparison to wild-type controls.15 In addition, treatment of B6 mice with anti-VISTA mAb did not affect neutrophil levels at steady state in the bone marrow, spleen, or blood one day following treatment, relative to mice treated with the control antibody (Fig. 1B). Similar findings were observed in hVISTAKI mice treated with anti-hVISTA mAb (Fig. S1B). To further confirm that engaging VISTA on neutrophils does not lead to their depletion at steady state, we compared the effects of anti-VISTA mAb to that of anti-Ly6G mAb (1A8), a canonical neutrophil-depleting antibody. While treatment with the equivalent dose of anti-Ly6G immunoglobulin G (IgG) almost completely depleted neutrophils across tissues, anti-VISTA mAb did not affect neutrophil numbers at steady state (Fig. S3A). Moreover, the levels of neutrophils undergoing both early (ApoTracker+ SYTOX-) and late (SYTOX+) apoptosis were significantly increased with anti-Ly6G treatment, while we detected no evidence of neutrophil death with anti-VISTA mAb at steady state (Fig. S3B, C).

VISTA expression on neutrophils is required for anti-VISTA antibody to suppress neutrophil response to LPS

To determine if the effects of the anti-VISTA antibody observed in vivo require neutrophil expression of VISTA, we generated mice carrying neutrophil-specific VISTA deletion (S100a8creVsirfl/fl). S100a8-cre expression is restricted to neutrophils and a proportion of their progenitors.26 Flow cytometry analysis confirmed the predicted loss of VISTA expression on neutrophils in the bone marrow and spleen of S100a8cre Vsirfl/fl mice, compared to cre negative (Vsirfl/fl) controls (Fig. 2A). As expected, anti-VISTA antibody treatment following LPS challenge resulted in reduced neutrophil numbers in the spleen and blood of cre negative (Vsirfl/fl) mice (Fig. 2B), comparable to that seen in wild-type B6 mice (Fig. 1). In contrast, anti-VISTA mAb treatment of S100a8cre Vsirfl/fl mice following LPS challenge did not decrease neutrophil levels in the spleen and blood (Fig. 2B). Therefore, VISTA expression by neutrophils is critical for the in vivo effects of anti-VISTA antibody treatment.

FcRγ engagement is required for anti-VISTA antibody to suppress LPS-induced neutrophil accumulation in the periphery

One critical consideration of antibody treatment is that the binding of the Fc portion of the antibody to the Fc receptor (FcR) can influence its mechanism of action and the downstream effects on the targeting cell. This could work through a variety of mechanisms, including but not limited to crosslinking of the target antigen, leading to apoptosis and/or clearance of antibody-bound cells via phagocytosis or cytotoxicity.27 To initially test if FcR binding impacts the ability of anti-VISTA mAb to suppress neutrophil accumulation in response to LPS, an engineered Fc silent anti-hVISTA antibody was used in hVISTAKI mice. Fc silent anti-hVISTA antibody did not reduce neutrophil levels in the spleen following LPS challenge, while mice that received the cytophilic version of anti-VISTA mAb showed a significant reduction in splenic neutrophils, compared to the isotype control (Fig. 3A). To define the contribution of specific FcRs, we evaluated the impact of anti-VISTA mAb treatment on neutrophil levels in response to LPS in Fcer1g−/− mice, which lack FcR-γ and have impaired Fc-γRI and Fc-γRIII signaling,28,29 and Fcgr2b−/− mice, which are FcγRII-deficient. We found that Fcer1g−/− mice treated with anti-VISTA mAb showed no decrease in neutrophil numbers in the spleen after LPS challenge (Fig. 3B). In contrast, anti-VISTA mAb potently suppressed splenic neutrophil accumulation in the Fcgr2b−/− mice (Fig. 3B). These data suggest that Fc-γRI and/or Fc-γRIII are required for anti-VISTA mAb to prevent peripheral neutrophil accumulation in response to LPS. As neutrophils express these FcRs, we wanted to determine if FcR expression on the neutrophils themselves was necessary to cause their loss with anti-VISTA mAb treatment. Therefore, wild-type and Fcer1g−/− neutrophils were isolated, labeled with different dyes, and transferred into wild-type recipient mice that were challenged with LPS and subsequently treated with anti-VISTA mAb (Fig. 3C). We found that anti-VISTA mAb effectively suppressed splenic accumulation of both WT and Fcer1g−/− neutrophils upon transfer into wild-type hosts (Fig. 3C), demonstrating that the lack of FcR-γ expression on neutrophils does not impair their response to anti-VISTA mAb.

NK cells and phagocytic macrophages are two candidate cell populations expressing FcR-γ that may be eliminating neutrophils. To test if NK cells are required, mice were treated with anti-NK1.1 (clone PK136) prior to LPS immunization and mAb treatment. A decrease in NK cell numbers, confirmed by staining with NKp46, did not alter the effect of anti-VISTA mAb, which still caused a reduction in circulating neutrophils following LPS challenge (Fig. S4A, B). To test if phagocytic macrophages were needed for the effect of anti-VISTA mAb on neutrophils, mice were treated with clodronate or control liposomes prior to LPS and mAb challenge. Macrophage depletion, confirmed by a decrease in F4/80+ cells, prevented the anti-VISTA-mediated decrease in LPS-recruited circulating neutrophils (Fig. 3D and Fig. S4C, D).

Treatment with anti-VISTA antibody increases neutrophil death and macrophage-mediated neutrophil elimination in the liver

To test if the FcR-dependent effects of anti-VISTA antibody led to neutrophil death, bone marrow, spleen, and liver were examined 12 h after LPS challenge and antibody treatment. The liver was included as it is a known site of neutrophil recruitment and clearance during LPS-induced inflammation.30,31 Both the spleen and liver had decreased live neutrophil numbers with anti-VISTA mAb treatment by flow cytometry (Fig. 4A). Neutrophil expression of SYTOX, which directly binds DNA, was increased the most in the liver, ∼2-fold, with anti-VISTA mAb, suggesting their preferential death in this tissue (Fig. 4A). Since phagocytic macrophages were required for anti-VISTA-mediated decrease in LPS-recruited neutrophils, we asked if VISTA targeting led to neutrophil removal by the liver macrophages. Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated an overall increase in the frequency of F4/80+ cells containing MPO in the livers of anti-VISTA treated mice (Fig. 4B). As MPO is primarily made by neutrophils in the liver,32 this suggests their engulfment by macrophages. Anti-VISTA mAb decreased surface expression of CD47, a “don’t eat me” signal, on liver neutrophils (Fig. 4C), potentially further lowering the threshold for macrophage-mediated engulfment. This is consistent with the finding that depletion of macrophages by chlodronate prevented neutrophil elimination in the liver by anti-VISTA mAb (Fig. 4D and Fig. S4C, D).

Anti-VISTA antibody suppresses peripheral neutrophil accumulation in response to CXCL2

To ask if the effects of anti-VISTA mAb are restricted to neutrophils recruited in response to an infectious stimulus, we tested if antibody targeting of VISTA can impact neutrophil accumulation in response to CXCL2, a critical regulator of neutrophil recruitment in response to inflammation1,33 that is sufficient to induce neutrophil mobilization from the bone marrow.34 Mice were pre-treated with anti-VISTA or control mAb and challenged with CXCL2 (Fig. 5C). Splenic neutrophil numbers were reduced in the anti-VISTA mAb treated group one hour after CXCL2 treatment, which was paralleled by an increase in the percentage of SYTOX+ neutrophils (Fig. 5). Even though neutrophil numbers did not differ between treatment groups in the liver, likely due to the timing of examination, anti-VISTA mAb increased neutrophil cell death, as demonstrated by a significant increase in SYTOX+ neutrophils (Fig. 5). These results demonstrate that targeting VISTA mediates cell death of neutrophils in the periphery, irrespective of the inflammatory stimulus that recruits them from the bone marrow.

Treatment with anti-VISTA antibody reduces the severity of K/BxN serum transfer arthritis

To examine the potential therapeutic implications of targeting VISTA to suppress neutrophil accumulation, we utilized K/BxN serum transfer arthritis model. The serum donor K/BxN mice express a KRN transgenic TCR recognizing glucose-6-phosphate isomerase in the context of I-Ag7 and develop spontaneous chronic arthritis. Transfer of serum from arthritic K/BxN mice results in transient disease in susceptible strains.35,36 In this model, as well as in human arthritis, neutrophils play a crucial role in the induction and maintenance of disease.19,37 We found that anti-VISTA mAb treatment markedly reduced disease severity (Fig. 6A). To visualize neutrophil recruitment to the joints, mice were injected with luminol, a myeloperoxidase substrate.38 We found significant reductions in myeloperoxidase flux into the front and back paws of mice treated with the anti-VISTA mAb in comparison to control-treated mice (Fig. 6B). The flux observed in the group treated with the anti-VISTA mAb was comparable to that seen in the unimmunized (naïve) controls, indicative of the potent neutrophil elimination in the joints (Fig. 6B). These data suggest that anti-VISTA mAb reduces disease severity by inhibiting neutrophil accumulation in the joint.

Discussion

Here we demonstrate that anti-VISTA mAb inhibits neutrophil inflammatory response in vivo by predisposing these cells to death and elimination by phagocytic macrophages. In the context of LPS stimulation, increased neutrophil cell death is observed in the liver at just twelve hours post-challenge and treatment with anti-VISTA mAb. By 1 d post-challenge, the splenic neutrophil numbers are substantially reduced compared to those of the controls. After in vivo treatment with CXCL2, a chemokine that mediates neutrophil egress from the bone marrow in the absence of other inflammatory stimuli,34 mice given anti-VISTA mAb had decreased splenic neutrophil levels as well as increased neutrophil death in the liver and spleen. These findings establish that targeting VISTA on neutrophils selectively enhances their susceptibility to clearance, thus modulating neutrophil response in inflammation.

At steady-state, mature neutrophils enter the periphery due to increased CXCR2 signaling, and as they age, their CXCR4 expression will increase to promote homing back to the bone marrow to undergo clearance by macrophages.39 Anti-VISTA antibody did not reduce neutrophil levels at homeostasis but only following an inflammatory stimulus (LPS or CXCL2). This differential susceptibility of neutrophils to anti-VISTA mAb is intriguing, particularly since VISTA levels on neutrophils did not increase following the inflammatory challenge. We can speculate that neutrophil activation by an inflammatory stimulus and engagement of surface VISTA may have synergistic effects that render neutrophils more susceptible to macrophage-mediated phagocytosis. Supporting this possibility, we observed that anti-VISTA mAb downregulates surface expression of CD47 (a “don’t eat me” signal that engages SIRP-α on macrophages) on neutrophils, which may further lower the threshold for macrophage-mediated engulfment of these cells. However, it is also possible that LPS impacts apoptotic pathways in neutrophils,40 rendering them more susceptible to VISTA targeting. Anti-VISTA mAb, contrary to anti-Ly6G IgG, led to increased SYTOX+ but not ApoTracker+ neutrophils, suggesting that LPS may sensitize neutrophils to non-apoptotic cell death pathways that are triggered by VISTA engagement. On the other hand, LPS could have effects on macrophages that enhance macrophage phagocytic potential, such as increased expression of FcR-γ that could potentiate crosslinking of anti-VISTA mAb. The synergistic effects of LPS and anti-VISTA mAb have also been reported by our group in macrophage studies of LPS tolerance, where anti-VISTA mAb conferred protection against LPS-induced lethality only when administered with low-dose LPS.41 Nevertheless, the effect of anti-VISTA mAb on neutrophil reduction in our studies was cell specific. Despite VISTA expression on macrophages and other immune cells, anti-VISTA mAb did not decrease levels of other VISTA-expressing immune cells. Future studies will quantify changes in FcR-γ levels on macrophages and surface proteins associated with neutrophil activation, such as CD62L, CD177, CD11b, CD63, and CXCR2.

During inflammation, bone marrow neutrophils will enter the periphery in higher numbers, and these cells are recruited to the liver due to P-selectin-dependent homing and eliminated via apoptosis or phagocytosis of phosphatidylserine positive neutrophils by Kupffer cells.30,31 Under inflammatory conditions, targeting neutrophils with anti-VISTA mAb may supply additional signals to call for their recruitment to and elimination in the liver. This strategy could be particularly relevant to infections such as coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), to suppress the exaggerated tissue-damaging neutrophil responses in the lung.42 Macrophage depletion with clodronate liposomes prevented anti-VISTA mAb-mediated neutrophil elimination in the liver, suggesting that Kupffer cells may be important for the effects of anti-VISTA mAb. A similar dependence on phagocytic macrophages was observed in a murine model of acute GVHD, where anti-VISTA mAb-mediated deletion of alloreactive CD4+ T cells required macrophages.43 Together, our studies suggest that antibody binding of VISTA on neutrophils allows FcR-expressing macrophages to mediate VISTA crosslinking, which leads to pro-apoptotic signaling cascades and/or targeting them for phagocytosis. As previously reviewed, this is observed with some antibodies used in cancer immunotherapy.27,44 It is also possible that engaging VISTA on neutrophils can modulate their effector functions directly, as antibody crosslinking has previously been shown to induce neutrophil extracellular traps.45 Future studies will address whether VISTA targeting potentiates specific neutrophil functions and probe the pertinent mechanisms.

The studies presented here are confined to modulating VISTA function with an antibody and are influenced by the FcR-mediated effects on VISTA. It is possible that VISTA cross-linking on the neutrophils also affects its interactions with binding partners on macrophages. Prior studies using a multimeric recombinant form of the extracellular domain of murine VISTA (VISTA.COMP) demonstrated that there is an LPS-induced VISTA binding partner (though unknown) on macrophages and neutrophils and that binding of VISTA.COMP to LPS-treated macrophages and neutrophils suppressed their expression of inflammatory genes.46 Curiously, treating macrophages with anti-VISTA mAb also suppressed their inflammatory transcriptional profile.41 This could suggest VISTA may be capable of bi-directional cross-talk and leaves open the option that antibody cross-linking in vivo may also be modulating the binding of neutrophil VISTA to its ligand/receptor on macrophages and vice versa.

Beyond the potential implications of targeting VISTA to modulate neutrophil-mediated responses to pathogens, targeting VISTA on myeloid cells with therapeutic antibodies is of great interest and promise in both cancer and inflammatory diseases.12,13,47 In terms of the latter conditions, treatment with anti-VISTA antibodies may be an effective strategy to abolish the acute influx of neutrophils into inflammatory microenvironments. Here, we reported that anti-VISTA antibody suppressed neutrophil recruitment to the joints and suppressed disease severity in the murine model of arthritis. This approach could potentially have profound clinical benefits on diseases such as inflammatory arthritis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, reperfusion injury, and myocardial infarction, where exuberant neutrophil responses may result in organ damage, morbidity, and in some cases mortality. In the context of cancer, eliminating neutrophils by targeting VISTA may be beneficial in suppressing neutrophil-mediated angiogenesis in the tumor environment.48 Most murine studies have used anti-Ly6G IgG to deplete neutrophils in different disease models, which has proven highly effective in reducing neutrophils and suppressing K/BxN arthritis severity.49 Though Ly6G is not a feasible target on human neutrophils and may cause severe neutropenia even at steady-state, prior studies have shown that low doses of this antibody may block neutrophil recruitment and attenuate pathology.49 The effects of targeting VISTA on neutrophil chemotaxis and trans endothelial migration are not addressed here and future studies should elucidate dose-dependent effects of anti-VISTA on neutrophil recruitment, depletion, and neutrophil-mediated pathology. Interestingly, 2 distinct anti-VISTA mAb clones had the same effect on neutrophils, despite exhibiting divergent effects on T cell tolerance.20 While the underlying reason remains unclear, it is possible that “agonist” versus “antagonist” activity of anti-VISTA antibodies is cell-specific and influenced by factors such as antigen availability—given the markedly higher VISTA expression on neutrophils compared to T cells—or the extent of antibody cross-linking, which may be enhanced in the context of LPS-induced neutrophilia and macrophage activation.
