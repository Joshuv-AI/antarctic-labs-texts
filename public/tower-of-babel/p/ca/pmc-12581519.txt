Adherence to the MIND diet and its association with the risk of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances in physically active adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Zahra Arabpour, Alireza Milajerdi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysically active persons frequently change their dietary intakes to get the best results in their exercises. These changes in dietary intakes, which are not healthy in some cases, might influence the risk of psychological disorders in them. In the present study, we investigated the association between adherence to the MIND diet and the odds of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances among physically active adults.MethodsA total of 684 participants were included in this cross-sectional study. Dietary intakes of study participants were assessed using a validated 168-item semi-quantitative Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ). The MIND diet score was calculated based on participants’ dietary intakes obtained from FFQ. To assess depression, anxiety, and sleep quality, the Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II), the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) were used, respectively. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (SPSS Inc., version 18).ResultsAfter adjusting for the potential confounding factors, participants at the top tertile of MIND diet score were 51% less likely to have depression compared with those at the bottom tertile (OR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.18, 0.90). We found no significant association between the MIND diet score and odds of anxiety (OR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.71, 1.95) and sleep disturbances (OR: 1.25; 95% CI: 0.86, 1.81).ConclusionOur findings showed that more adherence to the MIND diet might be negatively associated with the odds of depression in physically active adults. No such significant association was found for the odds of anxiety or sleep disturbances. More studies are warranted.Keywords: Anxiety, Depression, MIND diet, Physical activity, Sleep

Introduction

Mental disorders cause negative consequences for human health and could lead to certain medical conditions, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, kidney failure, dementia, cancer, and eating disorders [1–5]. More than 1 billion people are living with mental disorders such as anxiety and depression in 2025 [6]. The prevalence of sleep disturbances is almost 45% [7]. In Iran, the prevalence of psychiatric disorders is about 31% [8].

Regular physical activity in most persons is related to a lowered risk of depression and anxiety [9–11] through the release of endorphins [12]. Exercise stimulates serotonin and melatonin production, thereby supporting circadian rhythm regulation and improving sleep quality [13]. Physical activity can influence food intake through alteration of appetite-related hormones [14, 15]. In addition, some physically active persons might change their regular dietary intakes to reach higher lean body mass as soon as possible [16]. These changes in dietary intake might cause an increase in the risk of different diseases, including mental diseases, in some people. In contrast, adherence to a healthy dietary pattern rich in fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, whole grains, and fish and avoiding processed foods, carbonated drinks, and sweets could protect against psychological and sleep disorders [17–20]. Adherence to the Mediterranean diet was associated with a decreased risk of depression [21]. Furthermore, adherence to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet was inversely associated with depression [22], difficulty with sleep onset [23], and daytime sleepiness score [24]. Recently, a neuro-protective dietary pattern described as the Mediterranean-DASH diet Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay (MIND) has been introduced as a healthy dietary pattern [25]. Earlier studies reported that consumption of the MIND diet reduced the odds of depression [26, 27]. But some other studies have failed to find such an association [28–30]. Furthermore, several studies did not find a significant association between adherence to the MIND diet and odds of anxiety [27, 28, 30]. This contradictory finding has also been reached for the sleep quality [28, 30]. It might be suggested that differences in the physical activity of participants are one reason for these controversial findings.

Due to the controversial findings of earlier studies about the association of MIND diet adherence with the risk of psychological disorders and the need for investigation of this association in persons with different physical activity, new original and systematic review studies are required. Changes in dietary intake, psychological health patterns, and sleep patterns in physically active adults underscore the importance of investigating the relationship between diet and psychological health in these individuals. Therefore, we decided to examine the association between adherence to the MIND diet and the risk of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances in a group of Iranian physically active adults.

Methods

The present cross-sectional study was carried out among athletes engaged in bodybuilding clubs in Kashan, Iran. A total of 684 participants were chosen from two clubs, within each of the five regions of Kashan. One club was selected for women and one for men in each region. In our study, participants were selected using a simple sampling method based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age between 18 and 50 years, (2) Healthy adults without a history of chronic diseases, (3) BMI between 20 kg/m2 and 30 kg/m2, (4) Participants who trained for at least 1 h on at least 3 days a week. Exclusion criteria: (1) Suffering from other mental disorders or brain damage, (2) Consumption of antidepressants, (3) Not being a physically active person as we defined, (4) Following specific diets (e.g., high protein, ketogenic, or vegetarian diets), (5) Pregnancy or breastfeeding.

All subjects filled out a written consent form. Our study protocol was ethically approved by the Ethics Committee of Kashan University of Medical Sciences, Kashan, Iran (IR.KAUMS.MEDNT.REC.1402.082).

Sample size

The sample size was calculated with the following formula. n = z2 p (1-p) / d2, z = 1.96 is the level of confidence of 95%, p = 0.26 is the estimated proportion of the population that presents the characteristic [27], and d = 0.033 is the tolerance margin of error.

Assessments

In the first phase, we collected information about demographic factors, anthropometric characteristics, physical activity, history of chronic diseases, and drugs by a questionnaire that was completed by an interview with the participants. Furthermore, Beck Depression Inventory II (BDI-II) [31], Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) [32], and Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) [33] were also completed for psychological assessment. The Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) [34] was filled out for dietary assessments. The evaluation of physical activity was done by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) [35].

Assessment of dietary intakes and MIND score calculation

A validated 168-item semi-quantitative FFQ questionnaire was used to assess dietary intakes [34]. This questionnaire was completed through a face-to-face interview. Its food items included: bread and cereals 14 items, legumes 7 items, meat 17 items, dairy products 18 items, vegetables 26 items, fruit 40 items, fats and nuts 13 items, beverages 5 items and snacks and sweets 28 items. For every food item, a standard serving size was defined. Subjects reported the amount and frequency (daily, weekly, or monthly) of their food consumption over the previous year. The serving sizes of each food item are converted to grams by the household measures book [36]. Daily energy and nutrient intake of subjects was calculated by Nutritionist-IV software (First Databank; Hearst, San Bruno, CA, USA).

These data were used to calculate the MIND diet score. The MIND diet includes 15 dietary components: 10 healthy food groups for the brain (green leafy vegetables, other vegetables, nuts, berries, legumes, whole grains, fish, white meat, olive oil and wine) and 5 unhealthy food groups for the brain (red/processed meat, butter and margarine, cheese, pastries, and fast/fried foods) [25]. Study participants were classified into tertiles based on their consumption of those food groups. Data were available for 14 components of this diet (in the present study, we did not have enough data about wine consumption). For brain-healthy food components, those at the highest tertile of consumption were given a score of 3, those in the middle tertile were given a score of 2, and those at the lowest tertile were assigned a score of 1. For unhealthy components, these scores are reversed, and the lowest to highest tertile of the intake of these food items were given a score of 3, 2, and 1, respectively. Then, the overall scores were summed up. The total score ranged from 14 to 42. Greater adherence to this diet was indicated by a higher MIND score.

Assessment of main outcomes

The depression score was calculated by the Beck depression questionnaire. This questionnaire consists of 21 questions [31]. Participants responded to each item using a four-choice scale with scores from 0 to 3; 2 questions related to emotional issues, 11 questions related to cognition, 2 questions related to behavior, 5 questions related to physical symptoms, and 1 question related to interpersonal behaviors. The total score of each person was calculated, which was between 0 and 63.

The anxiety score of the participants was evaluated by the Beck anxiety questionnaire. It consists of 21 questions [32]. Participants responded to each item using a four-choice scale with scores from 0 to 3. Every question in this test represents a prevalent symptom of anxiety, including mental, physical, and panic symptoms. The overall score for this questionnaire is between 0 and 63.

We examined sleep disturbances by PSQI [33]. The PSQI assesses seven primary factors within the last month, including: subjective sleep quality, sleep duration, sleep latency, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, sleeping medication usage, and daytime discomforts. Each question is scored on a scale of 0 to 3. The total score of this questionnaire is between 0 and 21.

Anthropometric measures

Weight was measured using a Seca scale while individuals were wearing light clothing without shoes, with an accuracy of 0.1 kg. Standing height was measured using a Seca stadiometer without shoes and with an accuracy of 0.5 cm. The Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height squared (m2).

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into tertiles based on scores of the MIND diet. The general information of the participants was expressed as mean ± standard deviation for quantitative variables and percentage for qualitative variables across those tertiles. To compare categorical variables and continuous variables across tertiles of MIND score, the Chi-square test and the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used, respectively. Dietary intake of participants among tertiles of the MIND diet score was compared by analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), adjusted for energy intake. Logistic regression test was used to estimate ORs and 95% CIs for the association between tertiles of the MIND diet score and risk of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances, in crude and multivariable-adjusted models. Age was adjusted in the first model. Age, gender, and marital status were controlled in the second model, and additional adjustment for BMI was applied in the third model. P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (SPSS Inc., version 18).

Crude and multivariable-adjusted ORs and 95% CIs for depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances across tertiles of MIND diet score are provided in Table 3. No significant associations were observed between adherence to the MIND diet and odds of depression (OR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.21, 1.02, P = 0.071), anxiety (OR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.71, 1.94, P = 0.545), and sleep disturbances (OR: 1.27; 95% CI: 0.88, 1.83, P = 0.195) in the crude model. After controlling for potential confounders at the final model, individuals at the top category of the MIND diet score had lower odds of depression (OR: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.18, 0.90, P = 0.04) compared with those at the bottom category. However, the associations between the MIND diet score and odds of anxiety (OR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.71, 1.95, P = 0.53) and with the odds of sleep disturbances (OR: 1.25; 95% CI: 0.86, 1.81, P = 0.22) remained non-significant.

Discussion

In our cross-sectional study, we examined the association between adherence to the MIND diet and odds of some psychological and sleep disturbances in physically active adults. We found that adherence to the MIND diet was significantly associated with lower odds of depression, but not with odds of anxiety and sleep disturbances in those participants.

Our results align with some previous studies. Salari-Moghaddam et al. [27] observed that higher adherence to the MIND diet was linked to lower odds of depression, while no significant relationship was found between MIND diet consumption and odds of anxiety. Cherian et al. [37] found such an association for depressive symptoms in 709 older adults for 6.53 years of follow-up. However, a cohort study with 10.4 years of follow-up on 15,980 adults showed no significant relationship between adherence to the MIND diet and reduction in depression risk [29]. Similar to our findings, some studies on the relationships between the MIND diet and psychological disorders in overweight women and healthy adults found no significant association between this diet and odds of anxiety [27, 28, 30]. According to a cross-sectional study investigating the association between the MIND diet and psychological disorders among 7165 Iranian adults, more adherence to this diet was significantly linked to lower odds of anxiety and depression [26]. The results of a case–control study performed on 85 patients with anxiety disorders also showed that adherence to the MIND diet is associated with lower odds and severity of anxiety disorders in adults [38]. In contrast, another cross-sectional study conducted on 400 healthy men, showed no relationship between greater adherence to the MIND diet and odds of anxiety, depression, and stress; however, a higher MIND diet score was negatively associated with odds of low sleep quality [30]. Similar to the results of our study, Seifollahi et al. did not find a relationship between adherence to the MIND diet and odds of anxiety and sleep disorder [28]. These controversies between earlier studies are partially explained by differences between their participants (their health condition and age) and also their heterogeneous physical activity levels. We focused on healthy adults with determined physical activity and found that only depressive disorders were less common among those who followed a healthy diet called as MIND dietary pattern. Further studies among different populations should be done to investigate the association of this dietary pattern with various mental diseases.

The mechanisms by which adherence to the MIND diet might influence brain health have not been precisely clarified. Diets high in processed meat, refined grains, sweeteners, and saturated fats have been associated with an increased serum level of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-alpha, IL-1, IL-6, and hs-CRP [39]. Systemic inflammation can enter the brain to accelerate direct and indirect neurotoxic responses, especially in prefrontal and limbic systems, which are reported to be affected by neuroinflammatory cascades. In addition, changes in the immune system have been associated with anxiety disorders [40]. In contrast, adherence to a healthy diet rich in fruits, vegetables, lean protein sources, and whole grains has been associated with a reduction in levels of inflammatory markers [41]. Furthermore, diet influences gut microbiota. Consumption of a healthy diet rich in fiber and polyphenols has been found to have beneficial effects on gut microbiota [42, 43]. However, a significant relationship has been found between dysbiosis of the gut microbiota and psychological disorders [44]. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by the microbiota through the anaerobic fermentation of dietary fiber attach to receptors on enteroendocrine cells, which in turn regulate the release of gut peptides. These peptides then bind to receptors in the amygdala, affecting the anxiety and stress responses [45, 46]. However, considering the physical activity when interpreting the relationship between adherence to the MIND diet and psychological outcomes is so important. Physical activity can cause the brain to produce more endogenous opioid peptides like endorphins, which may reduce depressive and anxiety symptoms and boost mood [12]. Exercise may stimulate the release of serotonin, which is the precursor of the circadian neurohormone melatonin. Thus, physical activity may indirectly support sleep quality [13]. In addition, physical activity could affect appetite and dietary intake by restoring the balance of leptin and ghrelin and enhancing leptin sensitivity [14, 15].

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study investigating the association between adherence to the MIND diet and risk of depression, anxiety, and sleep disturbances in a group of physically active adults. The limitations of our study should also be noted. The cross-sectional design of the study makes it impossible to find a causal association between the MIND diet and psychological and sleep disorders. The probability of the participants’ misclassification due to the use of questionnaires for exposure and outcome assessment should be kept in mind. We selected subjects from bodybuilding clubs to reduce heterogeneity between participants in terms of their exercise type; however, some differences might remain. Due to the lack of data about wine consumption, wine was not considered in the scoring. Further studies, in particular prospective cohorts, among participants with different levels of physical activity, investigating the association of this dietary pattern with various mental diseases, are warranted for the future.

Conclusion

Our cross-sectional study showed that more adherence to the MIND diet might be negatively associated with the odds of depression, but not with anxiety or sleep disturbances. More prospective cohort studies will expand knowledge in this topic.
