Unveiling the culprits: A retrospective case-control study on risk factors of mucositis in autologous stem cell transplantation
Authors: Guixia Wang, Feng Wei, Nana Ping
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The risk factors for mucositis in patients undergoing autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (AHSCT) are complex and encompass conditioning regimens, patient demographics, and microbiological alterations. A comprehensive understanding of these factors is essential for the development of targeted interventions aimed at reducing the severity of mucositis and enhancing patient outcomes. This study sought to identify the risk factors associated with mucositis in patients undergoing AHSCT in the blood transplantation purification ward of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University and to propose strategies for its prevention. A retrospective analysis was conducted on hematological patients (N = 104) who developed mucositis following AHSCT in our transplantation purification ward between June 2021 and March 2024. Patients were categorized based on the severity of mucositis using grading systems from the World Health Organization or the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events. Potential risk factors, including conditioning regimens, post-transplant infections, and other clinical variables, were evaluated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Univariate analysis identified the BEAM conditioning regimen, comprising carmustine, etoposide, cytarabine, and melphalan (P = .03), along with post-transplant infections (P = .02), as significant risk factors for the onset of mucositis. Subsequent multivariate analysis corroborated the carmustine, etoposide, cyarabine, and melphalan (BEAM regimen) as an independent risk factor (P = .01). Other variables, such as age, type of underlying disease, and duration of neutropenia, did not exhibit a significant association with the development of mucositis. In conclusion, the BEAM regimen and post-transplant infections markedly elevate the risk of mucositis in patients undergoing AHSCT. Early identification of individuals at high risk, optimization of conditioning regimens, rigorous infection control protocols, and proactive oral care interventions may reduce the incidence and severity of mucositis, thereby improving patient outcomes. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and refine preventive strategies.

1. Introduction

Autologous hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (AHSCT) has several clinical advantages, including the absence of graft rejection, reduced transplantation-related complications, and rapid hematopoietic recovery, all of which significantly improve patient prognoses. Consequently, the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy has endorsed this approach as an effective treatment modality for patients with hematological malignancies.[1] However, chemotherapeutic agents employed in pre-transplant conditioning regimens lack selectivity and indiscriminately target both malignant and normal cells, with a pronounced impact on rapidly proliferating cells such as those in the gastrointestinal tract.[2] Clinically, these agents can cause localized or diffuse mucosal ulcers and inflammation of the digestive system, leading to varying degrees of pain. Such complications typically lead to a marked reduction in quality of life as they extend hospital stays, impair nutritional status, elevate the risk of infections, and increase the need for opioid prescriptions and healthcare expenditures.[3]

Mucositis is an important complication of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) that potentially causes treatment interruptions and adversely affects therapeutic outcomes in severe cases. Effective prevention of mucositis relies fundamentally on precise risk identification and early intervention.[4] One systematic review by Eichhorn et al[5] highlighted the paucity of independent studies investigating the risk factors for mucositis among autologous HSCT recipients, thereby revealing a gap in the current understanding of the factors that may increase mucositis risk in this patient population.

We evaluated patients who underwent autologous HSCT and subsequently developed mucositis in the transplantation purification ward of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University. This study aimed to analyze the factors influencing the occurrence of mucositis and provide a reference and guiding framework for clinical practice, thereby facilitating a smoother transplantation period for patients.

2. Patients and methods

2.1. Ethical approval

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (IRB No. 2025454). All patients provided informed consent for the use of their medical records for scientific research.

2.2. Patients

The study population comprised 104 patients with hematologic conditions who underwent AHSCT in the transplant isolation ward between June 2021 and March 2024. Inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged 16 to 70 years who developed mucositis following AHSCT and had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 3 or lower. Exclusion criteria included a history of recurrent oral ulceration, the presence of uncontrolled severe medical conditions during transplantation, such as cardiac, renal, or respiratory diseases and concurrent progressive malignancies.

All patients underwent a comprehensive pre-transplantation physical examination and evaluation to exclude transplantation-related contraindications. Routine procedures included intestinal decontamination preparation and chlorhexidine-medicated baths, after which patients were admitted to a 100-level laminar flow ward. The pretreatment regimen was as follows: high-dose melphalan (200 mg/m2) was administered via intravenous infusion on days −3 and −2. The carmustine, etoposide, cyarabine, and melphalan (BEAM regimen) included oral semustine at 250 mg/m2/day on day −8, etoposide at 200 mg/m2/day via intravenous infusion on days −7 to −4, cytarabine at 400 mg/m2/day via intravenous infusion on days −7 to −4, and melphalan at 140 mg/m2/day via intravenous infusion on day −3. The simustine and thiotepa regimen involved the administration of semustine at 200 mg/m2/day orally on day −6 and thiotepa at 5 mg/kg every 12 hours via intravenous infusion from days −5 to −4. The modified leucovorin and cyclophosphamide included leucovorin 0.8 mg/kg every 6 hours via intravenous infusion from days −7 to −5 and cyclophosphamide at 1800 mg/m2/day via intravenous infusion from days −4 to −3. Frozen autologous hematopoietic stem cells were re-infused 24 or 48 hours after pretreatment. Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor at a dose of 5 to 10 μg/kg/day was administered to stimulate hematopoiesis 24 hours after stem cell transfusion. Upon admission to the facility, the patients were administered a sterile diet and subjected to hydration, alkalinization, diuresis, and standard prophylaxis for fungal and viral infections, which continued until discharge. Patients with gastrointestinal reactions received symptomatic treatment with gastric and antiemetic medications. Throughout the transplantation process, nutritional support was provided via oral or parenteral nutrition.

Patients were assessed and recorded according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Mucositis Grading Scale: Grade 0: normal; Grade 1: erythema, mildly painful; Grade 2: erythema, edema, ulceration, feedable; Grade3: painful erythema, edema, ulceration, non-feedable; and Grade 4: complete need for parenteral or enteral nutritional support.[6]

2.3. Statistical analysis

The collected data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 25.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago). Missing data were analyzed using multiple imputation techniques. Further statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, May 24, 2024). The Brant package was used to perform parallelism tests, and the MASS package facilitated the ordered logistic regression analyses. Baseline data and inter-group differences were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables with normal distribution, Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, χ2 test for categorical variables, and Kruskal–Wallis test for ordered categorical variables. The statistical significance of various risk factors was assessed using ordered logistic regression with the occurrence of mucositis as the endpoint. Two-sided tests were used, and the results were considered statistically significant at a P-value of <.05.

3. Results

A total of 104 patients (46 males, 58 females) developed mucositis. Of these, 71 patients were pretreated with the melphalan regimen, 31 with the BEAM regimen, one with the simustine and thiotepa, and one with the leucovorin and cyclophosphamide. Mucositis was classified as grades 1, 2, or 3 in 62, 36 and 6 cases, respectively. Body mass index at the time of hospitalization and discharge was used to evaluate the nutritional status before and after transplantation, respectively. The clinical data of 104 patients are presented in Table 1. A parallel test indicated no significant differences (P > .05). However, one-way regression analysis identified the use of the BEAM regimen (P = .03, OR = 2.47, 95% CI [1.06–5.75]) and the presence of an infection post-transplantation (P = .02, OR = 2.54, 95% CI [1.14–5.66]) as significant risk factors for the development of mucositis. Furthermore, multifactorial regression analysis revealed that use of the BEAM regimen (P = .01, OR = 3.48, 95% CI [1.27–9.56]) was an independent risk factor for mucositis development. 4. Discussion

The primary chemotherapeutic agents implicated in the induction of oral mucositis include melphalan, cytarabine, etoposide, and methotrexate. The incidence of oral mucositis is associated with the use of total body irradiation in HSCT regimens, melphalan dosage, and specific combinations of chemotherapeutic agents. High-dose melphalan (≥140 mg/m2) in pretreatment regimens significantly contributes to the development of oral mucositis, with its occurrence and severity are predominantly influenced by the melphalan dose. Additionally, combination therapy with other chemotherapeutic agents may exacerbate this condition. One study demonstrated that the incidence of oral mucositis was significantly higher in patients receiving the BEAM regimen than in those receiving the 200 mg/m2 regimen,[7] which is consistent with the results of the current study.

Several studies have identified post-infusion infection as a risk factor for the development and severity of mucositis in patients undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation.[5] This association has been attributed to the complex pathogenesis of mucositis, which extends beyond simple epithelial injury. Chemotherapy-induced cellular damage results in the death of basal epithelial cells. Reactive oxygen species (free radicals), which are generated as byproducts of oxygen metabolism during chemotherapy, contribute to cell death. These free radicals also activate secondary messengers that transmit signals from cell surface receptors to the cell interior, leading to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This upregulation results in tissue damage and cell death. Notably, the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, primarily produced by macrophages, exacerbates mucosal cellular damage and activates molecular pathways that amplify mucosal injury. Mucosal ulcers exhibit pronounced inflammatory cellular infiltrates, which are partially attributable to metabolic byproducts of the oral microbiota. This secondary infection further exacerbates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby intensifying mucosal damage.[8]

Previous studies have identified female sex as a risk factor for severe oral mucositis[5]; however, the underlying mechanisms are not fully understood, and several biologically plausible explanations have been proposed. Sex differences in pharmacokinetics and drug metabolism may result in increased mucosal toxicity in women, leading to increased systemic exposure to chemotherapeutic agents. Furthermore, hormonal influences, particularly the pro-inflammatory effects of estrogen, may enhance mucosal sensitivity and impede epithelial recovery after cytotoxic injury.[9] However, these effects were not observed in the current study, possibly because we used single-center data or due to racial differences.

Furthermore, one meta-analysis examined the factors influencing mucositis in the context of HSCT and found that individuals under the age of 40 years and with renal insufficiency (e.g., creatinine clearance below 60 mL/min), particularly those with multiple myeloma, elevated ferritin levels (above the 75th percentile), the presence of specific bacteria such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and fungi in the oral microbiota, and HSV-1, have an increased risk of developing oral mucositis.[5]

The prevalence of pretreatment-related mucositis before HSCT can reach 80%.[10] This condition typically manifests 5 to 7 days after high-dose pretreatment, persists for approximately 6 days, and resolves by day 14 post-AHSCT.[11] The symptoms vary according to the affected site. Oral mucositis is characterized by dryness of the oral and labial mucous membranes, as well as impaired absorptive and secretory functions of the oral mucosa, with increased salivation, pain, erythema, and ulceration, which collectively contribute to difficulties in eating and swallowing. In contrast, gastrointestinal mucositis is associated with symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, bloating, intestinal cramping, and anal pain. Damage to the gastrointestinal mucosa is not directly observable but can be assessed using the sugar osmosis test. Lactulose, a disaccharide absorbed via paracellular transport, serves as an indicator of intestinal mucosal integrity, with increased urinary excretion indicating compromised mucosal integrity. In contrast, levulose, methylglucose, and d-xylose are monosaccharides, and reduced urinary excretion of these sugars suggests a diminished intestinal surface area and a reduction in the function or number of intestinal epithelial cells, leading to decreased absorption. The urinary lactulose/levulose ratio is a valuable metric for evaluating intestinal permeability or integrity, with a ratio of 0.02 or lower considered indicative of normal intestinal mucosal function.[12] Unlike gastrointestinal mucosal damage, oral mucositis can be directly observed; thus, we employed oral mucositis as an observational index.

Preventing and repairing gastrointestinal mucosal damage is crucial to effectively reduce the incidence of post-transplant bacteremia and enhance transplantation success rates.[12] The Multinational Association of Supportive Care in Cancer/International Society of Oral Oncology clinical practice guidelines advocate various strategies to prevent oral mucositis.[13] One such strategy is cryotherapy during melphalan administration, which mitigates mucosal exposure to chemotherapeutic agents by constricting the oral vasculature and reducing blood flow to the mucosa. However, this technique demands significant patient cooperation, and some individuals may find cold stimulation intolerable, leading to poor compliance or gastrointestinal disturbances, such as diarrhea resulting from ice cube ingestion. To address these challenges, our transplantation center uses sterilized water maintained in a continuous refrigeration cup to ensure a consistently low-temperature liquid state. Patients were instructed to hold the liquid in their mouths to allow thorough contact with the oral mucosa before swallowing once the temperature normalized, thereby facilitating effective cooperation with cryotherapy. Photobiomodulation, a relatively novel intervention, is predominantly used in developed countries because of the substantial equipment cost. Valeh et al[14] studied the combined use of a nystatin suspension (100,000 U/mL, 20 drops), chlorhexidine (0.2%, 10 mL), diluted povidone-iodine (10 mL povidone-iodine + 1000 mL saline), and diluted amphotericin B (10 mg amphotericin B + 1000 mL saline) as mouth rinsing solutions, and demonstrated favorable outcomes. Our results suggest a viable preventive strategy in settings with limited resources.

Understanding the risk factors for oral mucositis will facilitate the development of tailored prevention programs for patients. Essential oral care practices should be implemented, including brushing twice daily with a soft-bristled toothbrush and mild toothpaste, as well as rinsing the mouth with antimicrobial solutions such as 0.12% chlorhexidine to decrease microbial colonization in the oral cavity. Patients should avoid spicy, acidic, or coarse foods and beverages, maintain oral hydration, and use oral moisturizers to alleviate symptoms of dry mouth, such as oil-based lecithin chews. For patients undergoing the BEAM regimen, intensive oral care interventions should commence with the administration of keratinocyte growth factor-1 3 days prior to pretreatment.[15] The Oral Mucositis Daily Questionnaire can be employed to enhance assessment of the oral mucosa, facilitating early detection of oral mucosal damage.[16] The questionnaire is self-administered by patients, thereby offering advantages such as earlier symptom detection, more accurate assessment of severity and pain, minimal subjective judgment by medical staff, and comprehensive collection of information compared to the WHO Mucositis Grading Scale. However, this approach may increase the workload of medical staff. Patients experiencing post-transplant infections were monitored for cytokine inflammatory markers, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or corticosteroids were administered to mitigate the inflammatory response and alleviate pain, thereby preventing the exacerbation of mucositis. At our transplant center, patients presenting with pharyngeal obstruction in the early stages of the disease received throat drops containing 80,000 U gentamicin and 5 mg dexamethasone. A lidocaine gargle was used to relieve the pain. Malnutrition in patients undergoing HSCT is associated with increased mortality owing to, the side effects of high-dose pretreatment regimens and mucositis, which can result in inadequate nutritional intake.[17] The European Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition and the American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition have formulated clinical practice guidelines to inform decision-making regarding the use of nutritional support for patients undergoing HSCT. These guidelines recommend providing oral nutritional supplements, enteral nutrition, or parenteral nutrition to patients with cancer who are malnourished or who experience prolonged inadequate oral intake.[18,19] Although these guidelines offer evidence-based recommendations, the selection of specific treatments should be tailored by the physician according to the individual patient’s circumstances, considering a comprehensive treatment plan that should initially focus on oral hygiene, nutritional support, and pharmacological prophylaxis.

This study was conducted at a single center, and certain pretreatment protocols did not yield significant results because of the limited sample size. Additionally, this retrospective study faced constraints in terms of human and material resources, which hindered the objective assessment of mucosal damage in the lower gastrointestinal tract. Studies on the effects of combining drugs with different mechanisms of action on mucositis are limited, and cost-effective clinical outcomes have not yet been achieved. Nonetheless, by identifying risk factors and developing predictive models alongside the test results for inflammatory markers, assessment scales can be created to evaluate risk and identify patients who would benefit from intensive oral care interventions. Further comprehensive studies are needed to develop effective predictive models for early identification of oral risk, thereby enabling the provision of more cost-effective treatments for patients at increased risk.

5. Conclusions

Prevention and management of mucositis are essential for patients undergoing HSCT. Further research in this field can enhance patient outcomes by facilitating the development of personalized treatment strategies, thereby reducing the incidence of oral mucositis and minimizing the need for unwarranted chemotherapy dose reductions or treatment interruptions attributable to mucositis.
