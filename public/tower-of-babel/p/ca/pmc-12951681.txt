Serum vitamin D and lean non-alcoholic fatty liver disease: a nonlinear ‘L-shaped’ association in a national cross-sectional study
Authors: Gang Ma, Chongxiao Li, Erzhuo Xia, Jingbo Wang, Ying Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the association between vitamin D and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is well-established in obese populations, its role in lean NAFLD remains controversial.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the potential nonlinear dose-response relationship between serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] concentrations and lean NAFLD by leveraging large-scale data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES).MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of 4,201 adults with BMI < 25 kg/m2. Lean NAFLD was defined using the US Fatty Liver Index (USFLI). The association between serum vitamin D and lean NAFLD was assessed employing multivariable logistic regression models, alongside more advanced techniques including restricted cubic splines (RCS) and generalized additive models (GAM) to capture potential nonlinearity.ResultsInitial linear models revealed no significant association. However, subsequent nonlinear analyses uncovered a pronounced ‘L-shaped’ curve relationship (P-nonlinear < 0.05). A segmented regression approach identified an inflection point at 60.3 nmol/L. Notably, at serum vitamin D levels below this threshold, concentrations were significantly inversely associated with lean NAFLD risk (Odds Ratio [OR] = 0.71, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 0.57–0.89). Conversely, above this inflection point, the inverse association attenuated and plateaued. The robustness of this nonlinear association was corroborated by the GAM analysis.ConclusionThis large-scale study is the first to identify a significant ‘L-shaped’ nonlinear association between serum vitamin D and lean NAFLD, wherein the association is most potent at concentrations below approximately 60 nmol/L. This finding highlights an epidemiological inflection point and underscores the need for nonlinear approaches in future research.

Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), a chronic hepatic condition affecting approximately one-quarter of the global population, is pathologically characterized by excessive triglyceride accumulation within hepatocytes and is intimately linked to obesity and insulin resistance [1]. It is noteworthy, however, that NAFLD is not exclusive to individuals with obesity; its presence in those of normal weight—categorized as lean NAFLD—has garnered increasing clinical and research interest. Epidemiological data indicate that lean NAFLD may constitute up to 19.2% of the overall NAFLD burden [2]. The pathogenesis of this particular subtype appears to extend beyond conventional metabolic drivers, implicating a more complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and metabolic heterogeneity [3,4]. Although the overall metabolic dysregulation in these patients is often less pronounced, a significant subset can nonetheless develop substantial histologic liver injury and face a tangible risk of disease progression. This reality challenges the traditional metabolic-centric paradigm of NAFLD and underscores the considerable uncertainties that persist regarding its optimal clinical management and preventive strategies.

The association of vitamin D with NAFLD has attracted considerable scientific scrutiny, owing to its pleiotropic physiological functions, including modulation of inflammation, mitigation of oxidative stress, and enhancement of insulin sensitivity [5]. A substantial body of evidence suggests an inverse association between serum levels of 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], the predominant circulating metabolite, and both the prevalence and severity of NAFLD [6]. Yet, the nature of this association within the lean NAFLD cohort remains a subject of significant controversy. While the link between hypovitaminosis D and NAFLD risk is consistently observed and recognized as an independent risk factor in the context of obesity [7–9], findings in lean individuals are markedly discordant. For instance, a cross-sectional study in Chinese adults reported no significant association between serum vitamin D and lean NAFLD [7]. In stark contrast, a Korean investigation demonstrated that low vitamin D levels independently increased NAFLD risk in non-obese and lean individuals, irrespective of their insulin resistance status [10]. Adding further complexity, foundational research implies a context-dependent role for vitamin D; in certain experimental models, vitamin D supplementation has been shown to paradoxically exacerbate hepatic steatosis in lean subjects [11], whereas it typically confers protective effects in models of obesity-driven NAFLD [12]. These contradictory findings not only highlight the elusive connection between vitamin D and lean NAFLD but also strongly suggest that their relationship may not be adequately captured by simplistic linear models.

Given this ongoing debate and the limitations inherent in prior analytical approaches, which have potentially masked true underlying complex relationships by relying on linear assumptions, the present study seeks to utilize large-scale, nationally representative data. Specifically, we aim to: (1) investigate the association in the understudied lean NAFLD population; and (2) systematically evaluate the potential nonlinear dose-response relationship between serum 25(OH)D and lean NAFLD by employing advanced statistical modelling techniques (RCS and GAM), which have not been previously applied in this specific context. Elucidating this relationship is important, as it may refine our etiological understanding of lean NAFLD and provide a basis for future investigations into vitamin D-related nutritional approaches for this distinct patient population.

Methods

This study utilized data from nine consecutive cycles (2001–2018) of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative cross-sectional program directed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. population [13]. From an initial pool of 91,351 participants, a rigorous, pre-defined screening protocol was applied. We excluded individuals who were under 18 years of age, lacked key variables for calculating the US Fatty Liver Index (USFLI) [14], were heavy alcohol consumers (>21 drinks/week for men and >14 drinks/week for women), tested positive for hepatitis B surface antigen or hepatitis C antibodies, reported use of known steatogenic medications (e.g. amiodarone, valproate, methotrexate, tamoxifen, corticosteroids) [15], had a body mass index (BMI) ≥25 kg/m2 or missing BMI data, or had incomplete information on crucial covariates including serum vitamin D, demographics, metabolism, and lifestyle factors. This process yielded a final analytical cohort of 4,201 participants, with the detailed selection flowchart presented in Figure 1.

Flowchart of participant selection from the NHANES 2001–2018 cycles.

NAFLD was defined using the USFLI, calculated according to the established formula [14], with a score ≥30 indicating the presence of NAFLD. In alignment with the 2022 American Gastroenterological Association clinical practice guidelines, we defined Lean NAFLD as NAFLD occurring in individuals with a BMI < 25 kg/m2 [16].

The core exposure variable was the serum concentration of total 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D], measured from baseline samples obtained from NHANES participants. These samples, processed and stored at −30 °C, were analyzed centrally at the NHANES laboratory division using rigorous methodologies, including high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry; detailed laboratory procedures are accessible via the NHANES website. Our analysis incorporated a comprehensive set of potential confounders. Demographic covariates included sex, age (categorized as <40, 40–60, >60 years), race/ethnicity, educational attainment (less than high school, high school or equivalent, above high school), and the poverty-income ratio (PIR). Metabolic covariates encompassed BMI, hypertension, diabetes, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Lifestyle factors included smoking and drinking status (both classified as never, former, or current), physical activity level, and daily energy intake. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported history, an average systolic blood pressure ≥130 mmHg, an average diastolic blood pressure ≥80 mmHg, or current use of antihypertensive medication [17]. Diabetes was diagnosed based on a self-reported history, a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥6.5%, a fasting plasma glucose level ≥125 mg/dL, or current use of insulin or hypoglycaemic agents [17]. Physical activity levels were quantified using metabolic equivalent minutes per week (MET-min/week) and categorized, per U.S. guidelines [18], as inactive (<300), insufficient (300–599), sufficient (600–2999), or highly active (≥3000). For participants with two valid days of dietary recall data, daily energy intake was calculated as the average of the two days.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1), incorporating the ‘survey’ package to account for the complex, multi-stage sampling design of NHANES and to produce nationally representative estimates. Baseline characteristics of the study participants, stratified by Lean NAFLD status, were summarized and compared using the ‘gtsummary’ package in R. Categorical variables are presented as n (%), and continuous variables are presented as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). Between-group differences were assessed using Fisher’s exact test with Monte Carlo simulation (2,000 replicates) for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. To evaluate the association between serum vitamin D and Lean NAFLD, we first constructed multivariable logistic regression models, treating vitamin D both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable based on quartiles. Four progressively adjusted models were specified: Model 0 (unadjusted); Model 1 (adjusted for demographic factors); Model 2 (adjusted for demographic and lifestyle factors); Model 3 (adjusted for demographic and metabolic factors); and Model 4 (additionally adjusted for lifestyle factors). In our multivariable-adjusted models, covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with NAFLD and/or vitamin D status in the literature, with the goal of minimizing confounding [19–21]. The models were specified progressively to examine the influence of different covariate sets. Model 2, which adjusts for demographic and lifestyle factors but deliberately excludes all metabolic variables, was designed to address a specific methodological concern. Metabolic factors could be mediators on the causal pathway between vitamin D and NAFLD. Including them in adjustment models (Models 3 and 4) might introduce over-adjustment bias, potentially underestimating the total association. Therefore, Model 2 serves as a strategic model to estimate the association free from this specific bias, providing an estimate less likely to be confounded by the consequences of the disease process itself. The consistency of findings across models, especially between Model 2 and the more fully adjusted Model 4, would support the robustness of the observed association. Results were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Potential multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and all retained variables had a VIF < 5. Notably, to avoid collinearity with components of the USFLI formula, gamma-glutamyl transferase, glucose, and insulin were not included as covariates, and age was treated as a categorical variable in the models. To investigate the potential nonlinear dose-response relationship between serum vitamin D and Lean NAFLD, we employed restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression models within the logistic regression framework. The RCS model was specified with 3 knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the serum vitamin D distribution, a common choice for moderate sample sizes that balances model flexibility with the risk of overfitting [22]. As a complementary sensitivity analysis to verify the robustness of the nonlinear pattern, we also fitted a generalized additive model (GAM) with a thin-plate regression spline for vitamin D, using the default basis dimension (k = 10) as implemented in the mgcv package in R [23]. The consistency between the RCS and GAM results was assessed to confirm the stability of the identified association. Furthermore, stratified analyses were conducted across subgroups defined by sex, age, race, education level, and the presence of diabetes or hypertension. Interaction terms were introduced to evaluate effect modification, with the significance of interaction assessed via P-interaction. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant for all tests.

Results

The final analytical cohort comprised 4,201 eligible participants, of whom 211 (5.02%) were identified as having Lean NAFLD and 3,990 (94.98%) served as non-NAFLD controls. The baseline characteristics of the study population, stratified by Lean NAFLD status, are detailed in Table 1. Compared to the control group, participants with Lean NAFLD exhibited a distinct demographic profile, characterized by a higher proportion of males, an older age distribution, a greater representation of Mexican Americans, and a higher percentage of individuals with an educational attainment below high school level. Anthropometrically, this group demonstrated significantly higher BMI and waist circumference measurements. Perturbations in metabolic parameters were pronounced in the Lean NAFLD group, which displayed a more adverse glycaemic and lipid profile, marked by elevated levels of fasting blood glucose, insulin, total cholesterol, triglycerides, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, coupled with reduced levels of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Liver function indices were also notably aberrant, with significant elevations in alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), concurrent with decreased serum albumin and increased globulin levels. Furthermore, the prevalence of both diabetes and hypertension was significantly higher among those with Lean NAFLD. Lifestyle characteristics also diverged; individuals with Lean NAFLD included a larger proportion of former or current smokers and drinkers, demonstrated a higher prevalence of physical inactivity, and reported a lower mean daily energy intake. The magnitude of between-group differences was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMD). Notably, the Lean NAFLD group exhibited large differences (SMD > 0.5) across key metabolic features, including insulin (SMD = 1.16), triglycerides (0.99), waist circumference (1.04), and HDL cholesterol (−0.63), alongside a markedly higher prevalence of diabetes (0.95) and hypertension (0.69). Although the overall mean serum vitamin D levels did not differ statistically between the two groups, a notably higher proportion of individuals in the Lean NAFLD group fell within the lowest quartile of vitamin D concentration. Other metrics, such as the PIR and total bilirubin, showed some numerical differences between groups, though these failed to reach statistical significance.

In the analysis of linear associations, no significant relationship emerged between serum vitamin D, whether treated as a continuous or an ordinal categorical variable, and the risk of Lean NAFLD (Table 2). In the unadjusted crude model, the OR for Lean NAFLD per 1 nmol/L increase in vitamin D was 0.999 (95% CI: 0.992–1.006, p = 0.79). This null finding persisted across all subsequent multivariable models, which incrementally adjusted for demographic, metabolic, and lifestyle confounders (all p > 0.05). Similarly, when vitamin D was analyzed by quartiles, the tests for linear trend across these categories were non-significant in all models (all p > 0.05). The lack of a significant linear association was consistent across all models, including Model 2 which excluded metabolic covariates. Intriguingly, however, the quadratic trend term achieved statistical significance across several models, providing an initial hint that the underlying relationship might be nonlinear.

To rigorously investigate this potential nonlinearity, we employed RCS analysis (Table 3). The initial unadjusted model already suggested a nonlinear trend (P-nonlinear = 0.0547). As covariates were sequentially incorporated, this nonlinear association became more pronounced and statistically robust. The fact that the association was significant in Model 2 indicates that the observed nonlinear relationship between vitamin D and Lean NAFLD is not an artifact of over-adjustment for potential metabolic mediators. The effect estimates across Models 2–4 were quantitatively similar (ORs from RCS analysis around 0.71–0.74), suggesting that the inclusion of metabolic covariates did not substantially alter the strength or shape of the association. Finally, the fully adjusted model (Model 4) was chosen and a significant nonlinear, inverse association was firmly established between vitamin D levels and Lean NAFLD risk (P-nonlinear = 0.0175). Specifically, an increase in vitamin D from 49.5 nmol/L to 83.6 nmol/L was associated with a substantial 29.4% reduction in the odds of Lean NAFLD (OR = 0.706, 95% CI: 0.563–0.886, p = 0.0064). To precisely quantify this relationship, a segmented regression model was fitted, identifying an inflection point at 60.3 nmol/L (95% CI: 36.2 − 84.5). Below this threshold, vitamin D levels exhibited a strong, significant inverse correlation with Lean NAFLD risk. To verify the plateau phase, a bootstrap analysis (1,000 replicates) tested the difference in adjusted predicted probabilities between the 25th and 75th vitamin D percentiles above the inflection point (>60.3 nmol/L). The difference was not significant (95% CI: −0.009 to 0.031; p = 0.92), confirming a flat ‘L-shaped’ curve beyond the threshold without a significant U-shaped rebound. Above this point, however, the association plateaued, delineating a characteristic ‘L-shaped’ curve and indicating a clear saturation effect for vitamin D’s protective influence (Figure 2).

The robustness of these findings was confirmed through a sensitivity analysis using a GAM. The results from the GAM were highly consistent with the RCS analysis, demonstrating a statistically significant nonlinear term for vitamin D (edf = 2.306, p = 0.049). The model exhibited a good overall fit (adjusted R2 = 0.191, deviance explained = 29.4%), thereby providing strong corroborative evidence for a stable and robust nonlinear association between vitamin D and Lean NAFLD (Supplementary Figure 1).

Subgroup analyses revealed that the inverse association between vitamin D and Lean NAFLD was generally consistent across most pre-specified subgroups (Supplementary Figure 2). The inverse relationship was particularly pronounced and statistically significant among males (p = 0.013), individuals aged ≥60 years (OR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.48–0.96), and those with an education level below high school (OR = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.31–0.75, p = 0.003). Furthermore, vitamin D showed a significant association irrespective of the presence or absence of either diabetes or hypertension (all p < 0.05). It is important to note that some subgroup analyses (e.g. age < 40) included a relatively small number of cases, which limits statistical power and necessitates cautious interpretation of these specific estimates. Critically, tests for interaction across all these subgroups were non-significant (all P-interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

Leveraging large-scale, nationally representative data from NHANES (2001–2018), this study systematically investigated the relationship between serum vitamin D levels and Lean NAFLD by employing logistic regression alongside more sophisticated techniques, namely RCS and GAM. After comprehensive adjustment for demographic, metabolic, and lifestyle confounders, the RCS analysis revealed a significant nonlinear association, characterized by a distinct ‘L-shaped’ dose-response curve. A pivotal inflection point was identified at 60.3 nmol/L via segmented regression. The robustness of this nonlinear relationship was further corroborated by the GAM, which yielded a statistically significant smooth term and demonstrated a satisfactory model fit. These findings extend prior research in two key aspects: first, by focusing specifically on the lean NAFLD subtype, and second, by being the first large-scale study to formally identify and characterize this nonlinear relationship, thereby providing a plausible explanation for inconsistent results from previous linear analyses in this population.

The role of vitamin D in NAFLD has garnered considerable interest. Observational studies consistently report a high prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in both NAFLD and its progressive form, NASH [24,25]. Lower serum 25(OH)D levels are associated with an increased risk of developing NAFLD [10,21,26], an association that appears more pronounced in individuals with co-existing type 2 diabetes [27]. Further supporting this link, cross-sectional [28] and histologic [29] studies have established a correlation between vitamin D deficiency and NAFLD severity. Moreover, evidence from animal models and clinical interventions suggests that vitamin D supplementation can ameliorate serum lipid metabolites, inflammatory markers, and insulin resistance in NAFLD [30–34]. However, consensus is lacking regarding this association in the specific subtype of Lean NAFLD. For instance, a study by Wang et al. in a Chinese population found no significant association between serum vitamin D and Lean NAFLD [7], whereas Kim et al. reported a significant association between vitamin D deficiency and NAFLD risk in non-obese and lean Asian individuals [10]. Our study, through nonlinear modelling, elucidates a potential reason for this discrepancy: the inverse association of vitamin D exhibits a saturation effect. Its relationship with Lean NAFLD is not a simple linear one but rather follows an ‘L-shaped’ curve, where the effect is pronounced below a specific threshold and plateaus thereafter. Our findings are robust to concerns regarding over-adjustment for metabolic mediators. The significant ‘L-shaped’ association was already present in Model 2, which excluded all metabolic covariates. This indicates that the observed link between vitamin D and Lean NAFLD is not an artefact of adjusting for variables potentially on the causal pathway (e.g. lipids, glucose). Instead, it suggests an association that is independent of the typical metabolic disturbances often adjusted for in NAFLD studies, highlighting a potentially distinct pathway relevant to the lean phenotype.

The inflection point identified in our study was 60.3 nmol/L. This value sits slightly above the commonly used clinical cutoff for vitamin D insufficiency (typically 50 nmol/L) but aligns closely with the risk reduction threshold observed in the study by Kim et al. (approximately 50 nmol/L) [10]. This finding suggests that in the context of Lean NAFLD, vitamin D levels ≥60 nmol/L are associated with a lower risk, with the inverse association appearing strongest below this range. The existence of such a specific threshold may reflect an underlying biological mechanism. Vitamin D is involved in biological processes through binding to the VDR, which regulates downstream genes related to hepatic lipid metabolism, inflammatory responses, and insulin sensitivity. Research suggests that the activation of the VDR-PPARα signalling pathway may be subject to a saturation effect, whereby the biological response stabilizes once serum 25(OH)D reaches a certain concentration [35–37]. This provides a plausible mechanistic explanation for the observed ‘L-shaped’ curve. In lean individuals, whose metabolic abnormalities are often more subtle (e.g. mitochondrial dysfunction), a higher vitamin D level might be requisite to effectively activate these protective pathways [36].

The confirmation of a plateau effect, rather than a U-shaped rebound, at higher vitamin D levels is a critical finding of this study. This ‘L-shaped’ association suggests that vitamin D levels above ∼60 nmol/L are associated with a stable, lower risk of Lean NAFLD, without evidence of harm within the physiological range studied. This finding helps reconcile the discrepant results from experimental models, such as the study by Yang [11], which reported exacerbated steatosis following high-dose vitamin D intervention in a specific knockout mouse model of Lean NAFLD. The apparent contradiction may be attributed to fundamental differences in vitamin D metabolism or the supraphysiological doses employed in animal studies compared to the physiological variations observed in our human cohort. Nonetheless, the lack of a significant upward trend in our study should be interpreted with caution, as it might be influenced by the relatively smaller number of individuals at the very high end of the vitamin D spectrum, limiting our statistical power to detect a subtle ‘U-turn’. Future studies with a specific focus on populations with high vitamin D levels are warranted to conclusively rule out any potential adverse effects.

The findings of our study carry significant clinical and public health implications. Primarily, the identified inflection point of 60.3 nmol/L offers a potential, quantifiable target for managing vitamin D status in individuals at high risk for Lean NAFLD. For individuals with levels below this threshold, especially those with Lean NAFLD and metabolic anomalies, vitamin D supplementation could be explored in future interventions, though causality remains to be established. Secondly, our research underscores the critical importance of employing nonlinear modelling approaches in future nutritional epidemiological studies, especially for factors like vitamin D with potentially complex biological effect profiles, to avoid biased conclusions resulting from model misspecification.

It is important to consider the possibility of reverse causality in interpreting our findings. The observed ‘L-shaped’ association does not establish the direction of the relationship. It is plausible that underlying liver dysfunction in Lean NAFLD could influence vitamin D homeostasis [25]. The liver is the primary site for the hydroxylation of vitamin D to 25(OH)D, its major circulating form. Hepatic steatosis or inflammation may impair this enzymatic activity, leading to lower serum 25(OH)D levels [24]. Furthermore, NAFLD is often associated with alterations in bile acid metabolism and intestinal absorption, which could affect vitamin D uptake [38]. Therefore, the inverse association we observed might partly reflect a consequence of liver disease rather than a causative role of vitamin D deficiency. While our cross-sectional design cannot disentangle this temporal relationship, future longitudinal studies measuring changes in both vitamin D status and liver fat over time are needed to clarify the directionality of this association.

Nevertheless, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. The cross-sectional nature of NHANES data limits causal inference. While we identified a significant nonlinear association, the direction of the relationship remains uncertain. As discussed, reverse causality is a plausible alternative explanation, whereby hepatic steatosis or related metabolic disturbances in Lean NAFLD could lead to reduced synthesis, release, or altered metabolism of vitamin D [39,40]. Although we utilized the USFLI, a validated non-invasive index, its diagnostic accuracy remains inferior to that of liver biopsy. Furthermore, serum vitamin D was measured only once, which may not perfectly reflect an individual’s long-term vitamin D status or account for seasonal variations. Although NHANES data collection spans the entire year, intra-individual variability and the potential for regression dilution bias remain important considerations. While we observed a consistent association across most subgroups, the statistical power in certain strata may have been limited by smaller sample sizes. Besides, although observational studies are susceptible to biases such as over-adjustment, we addressed this directly by including a model (Model 2) that excluded metabolic covariates. The persistence of our core finding in this model mitigates the concern that it is driven by such bias. Nevertheless, the possibility of residual confounding or collider bias cannot be fully excluded.

It is important to contextualize our finding that vitamin D exhibits a nonlinear association with lean NAFLD, a distinct subtype which, while constituting a minority of NAFLD cases, involves etiologies (e.g. genetic predisposition, gut microbiota alterations) that may differ from classic metabolic-driven NAFLD. The association observed here suggests vitamin D status may be a relevant, modifiable component within this multifactorial framework, potentially interacting with inflammation or insulin sensitivity pathways. To advance this observation, future research should prioritize longitudinal cohorts to verify this nonlinear relationship and elucidate its causal nature. Ultimately, randomized controlled trials targeting lean NAFLD patients with vitamin D deficiency are essential to determine whether supplementation can ameliorate hepatic steatosis. Concurrently, research should explore effect heterogeneity across subgroups defined by genetic background, gut microbiota, or lifestyle, thereby assessing the potential for personalized strategies based on vitamin D status.

In summary, leveraging a large, nationally representative sample, this study is the first to demonstrate a significant ‘L-shaped’ nonlinear dose-response relationship between serum vitamin D levels and the risk of Lean NAFLD, identifying an effect inflection point at approximately 60.3 nmol/L. This discovery deepens the understanding of the association between vitamin D and Lean NAFLD, clarifies previous inconsistencies, and identifies a potential threshold that may inform future research into nutritional approaches for this population.
