Minimally invasive adrenalectomy for Cushing’s syndrome and mild autonomous cortisol secretion: a 20-year experience from a tertiary endocrine surgery center
Authors: Irem Karatas, Eray Koc, Yalin Iscan, Ummu Mutlu, Hulya Hacisahinogullari, Sidar Bagbudar, Ismail Cem Sormaz, Nihat Aksakal, Arzu Poyanli, Fatih Tunca, Yasemin Senyurek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCushing’s syndrome is a systemic disorder caused by prolonged exposure to excessive glucocorticoids and is associated with significant morbidity and increased mortality. Minimally invasive adrenalectomy has become the preferred surgical approach for adrenal tumors due to favorable perioperative outcomes. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, surgical outcomes, and histopathological findings of patients who underwent minimally invasive adrenalectomy for overt Cushing’s syndrome and mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS).MethodsThis retrospective study included 114 consecutive patients who underwent minimally invasive adrenalectomy between January 2006 and December 2025 at our institution. The diagnosis of cortisol excess was established through multidisciplinary evaluation. Hormonal assessment consisted of late-night salivary cortisol measurements, 24-hour urinary free cortisol levels, adrenocorticotrophic hormone (ACTH), and a 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test. Patients with typical clinical features of hypercortisolism and abnormal hormonal findings were classified as having overt Cushing’s syndrome, whereas patients without overt Cushingoid features but with a post-dexamethasone suppression test cortisol level > 1.8 µg/dL were classified as having MACS. Demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, radiological findings, surgical approach, postoperative complications, histopathological and clinical outcomes were analyzed.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 47.8 ± 12.4 years, and the female-to-male ratio was 100:14. Adrenalectomy was performed on the right adrenal gland in 43 patients (37.7%), on the left adrenal gland in 62 patients (54.4%), and bilaterally in 9 patients (7.9%). Transabdominal laparoscopic adrenalectomy (TLA) was performed in 111 patients (97.4%), and posterior retroperitoneoscopic adrenalectomy (PRA) was performed in 3 patients (2.6%), with no conversion to open surgery. The mean hospital stay was 4 days. Postoperative complications were rare and mostly minor, with no mortality or major complications. Histopathology revealed adrenocortical adenoma in 76 cases (61.8%), hyperplasia in 39 (31.7%), carcinoma in 3 (2.4%), and other benign lesions in 5 (4.1%). Among patients with available postoperative hormonal follow-up, biochemical remission was achieved in 88.8% of patients, while profound cortisol suppression (< 2 µg/dL) was observed in 75.0% of patients. Adrenalectomy was also associated with significant postoperative improvements in metabolic parameters, particularly LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels.ConclusionsMinimally invasive adrenalectomy is a safe and effective treatment option for patients with overt Cushing’s syndrome and MACS. The low complication rate and absence of perioperative mortality observed in this study support the role of minimally invasive surgery in appropriately selected patients and highlight the importance of management in experienced, high-volume endocrine surgery centers.

Introduction

Cushing’s syndrome is a chronic systemic disorder resulting from prolonged exposure to endogenous or exogenous glucocorticoid excess, which may lead to multiple comorbidities and is associated with increased mortality. ACTH-independent Cushing’s syndrome is most commonly caused by benign adrenal tumors, particularly unilateral cortisol-producing adenomas, whereas malignant lesions such as adrenocortical carcinoma are rare [1, 2]. Less frequent causes of this condition, which affect both adrenal glands, include bilateral macronodular adrenocortical disease and bilateral micronodular adrenal hyperplasia. Additionally, McCune-Albright syndrome and bilateral adenomas or carcinomas are uncommon sources of ACTH-independent Cushing’s syndrome [3, 4]. Until the early 1990s, open adrenalectomy was considered the standard surgical approach for adrenal tumors. However, after the introduction of laparoscopic transperitoneal adrenalectomy (LTA) by Gagner et al. in 1992, minimally invasive techniques have been increasingly adopted for the surgical treatment of adrenal tumors [5]. Currently, LTA and posterior retroperitoneoscopic adrenalectomy (PRA) represent the two main minimally invasive surgical approaches for adrenalectomy. Several studies have demonstrated that minimally invasive techniques are associated with lower postoperative morbidity and mortality, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery compared with open adrenalectomy [6]. In this study, we evaluated the outcomes of 114 consecutive patients who underwent minimally invasive adrenalectomy for overt Cushing’s syndrome or mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) at a tertiary endocrine surgery center over a 20-year period.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

Between January 1, 2006, and December 31, 2025, 114 patients who underwent minimally invasive adrenalectomy for Cushing’s syndrome or MACS at the Division of Endocrine Surgery, Istanbul University Faculty of Medicine, were included in this study. The exclusion criteria were open surgery, age < 18 years, inconclusive diagnosis of Cushing’s syndrome, and incomplete data records. The diagnosis of cortisol excess was established by a multidisciplinary team including endocrinologists, radiologists, and endocrine surgeons. The diagnostic evaluation included late-night salivary cortisol measurements, 24-hour urinary free cortisol levels, and a 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test (DST). Plasma ACTH levels were measured to differentiate ACTH-dependent from ACTH-independent Cushing’s syndrome. Suppressed ACTH levels were considered consistent with adrenal cortisol excess. When indicated, second-line tests such as the CRH stimulation test and high-dose dexamethasone suppression test (HDDST) were performed [7]. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were used as preoperative imaging modalities.

Patients with typical clinical features of hypercortisolism and abnormal hormonal tests were classified as having overt Cushing’s syndrome. In accordance with the guidelines of the European Society of Endocrinology, patients without overt Cushingoid features but with a post-1 mg dexamethasone suppression test cortisol level > 1.8 µg/dL were classified as having MACS.

Early postoperative morning serum cortisol levels were evaluated to assess postoperative remission. Biochemical remission was defined as a postoperative serum cortisol level of < 5 µg/dL [8]. In patients with postoperative cortisol levels > 5 µg/dL, additional endocrinological evaluation, including DST and/or urinary and salivary cortisol measurements, was performed when clinically indicated to differentiate persistent hypercortisolism from physiological cortisol levels within the normal range.

Metabolic parameters, including fasting plasma glucose, triglyceride, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels, were evaluated preoperatively and postoperatively. Postoperative biochemical follow-up data for remission assessment and metabolic evaluation were available for 80 of the 114 patients included in the study.

Demographic characteristics, preoperative symptoms, radiological findings, length of hospital stay, surgical approach, final histopathological diagnoses, and early and late postoperative complications were retrospectively analyzed.

Surgical technique and postoperative follow-up

All procedures were performed by five surgeons who specialized in endocrine surgery. Patients were admitted to the hospital at least one day before surgery for preoperative preparation. A standardized perioperative management protocol, including thromboprophylaxis and glucocorticoid replacement, was applied to all patients. Low-molecular-weight heparin was initiated one day before surgery and continued postoperatively for thromboprophylaxis purposes.

Patients with comorbidities, such as cardiac disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or obesity, were monitored in the postoperative intensive care unit when necessary. Early oral intake and mobilization were encouraged during the postoperative period.

Perioperative steroid coverage was provided with intravenous glucocorticoids (equivalent to approximately 200 mg hydrocortisone), which was initiated during the induction of anesthesia and continued during surgery. Postoperatively, intravenous glucocorticoid therapy was gradually tapered over the first 2–3 days, followed by a transition to oral glucocorticoid therapy, which was further tapered over the subsequent days. All patients underwent either transabdominal laparoscopic adrenalectomy or PRA. Total adrenalectomy was performed as the standard approach for all cases. Patients with malignant pathology were referred to the oncology department for further management, whereas patients with benign pathology were followed up in the endocrinology and surgery outpatient clinics at six-month intervals. During follow-up, recurrence was evaluated annually using biochemical tests and imaging studies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 15.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual histograms. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. According to the data distribution, preoperative and postoperative continuous variables were compared using either the paired-samples t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The mean age of the patient cohort (n = 114) was 47.8 ± 12.4 years, with a marked female predominance (female-to-male ratio, 100:14). Of the patients, 45 (39.5%) presented with overt Cushing’s stigmata, whereas 69 (60.5%) had mild clinical signs and symptoms potentially suggestive of hypercortisolism and were classified as having MACS. The most common clinical manifestations included central obesity or weight gain in 62 patients (54.4%), fatigue or proximal muscle weakness in 53 patients (46.5%), abdominal or back pain in 25 patients (21.9%), hypertension in 71 patients (62.3%), diabetes mellitus (DM) in 49 patients (43.0%), bone fractures in 5 patients (4.4%), hirsutism in 19 patients (16.7%), and Cushing’s stigmata, such as buffalo hump, moon face, or striae, in 41 patients (36.0%).

Among the 69 patients with MACS, the most common indications for surgery were hypertension, DM, or both in 50 patients (72.5%), muscle weakness, menstrual irregularities, or other cortisol-related symptoms in 28 patients (40.6%), and central obesity or weight gain in 20 patients (29.0%). Ten patients (14.5%) had no overt clinical manifestations attributable to cortisol excess and underwent surgery because of tumor growth during follow-up and/or concerning radiological features.

Preoperative radiological assessment revealed imaging findings suggestive of adenoma in 102 patients (89.5%), while hyperplasia was suspected in 12 patients (10.5%). The median lesion size was 35 mm (range, 9–85 mm). Adrenalectomy was performed on the right adrenal gland in 43 patients (37.7%), on the left in 62 patients (54.4%), and bilaterally in 9 patients (7.9%). Among the patients who underwent bilateral adrenalectomy (n = 9), all were classified as having overt Cushing’s syndrome. Preoperative imaging demonstrated bilateral adrenal enlargement with radiological features suggestive of adrenal hyperplasia and/or bilateral adenomas in all cases, and the final histopathological examination confirmed adrenal hyperplasia. Six patients had a history of pituitary surgery for ACTH-dependent Cushing’s disease and underwent bilateral adrenalectomy because of persistent or recurrent hypercortisolism despite previous pituitary-directed treatment. In one patient, despite comprehensive endocrinological and radiological evaluations, no central source of hypercortisolism could be identified, and bilateral adrenalectomy was performed due to severe and uncontrolled clinical hypercortisolism. The remaining two patients demonstrated progressive bilateral adrenal lesions on follow-up imaging studies without radiological findings suspicious for malignancy, and bilateral adrenalectomy was performed based on clinical progression and multidisciplinary endocrine council decision.

The surgical approach consisted of transabdominal laparoscopic adrenalectomy in 111 patients (97.4%) and PRA in 3 patients (2.6%). No conversion from minimally invasive to open surgery was observed. Additionally, no patient required intraoperative or postoperative blood transfusions. Histopathological examination included 123 adrenalectomy specimens from 114 patients, including nine bilateral adrenalectomy specimens. Adrenocortical adenoma was the most common diagnosis in both groups, observed in 26 patients (49.1%) with Cushing’s syndrome and 50 patients (71.5%) with MACS. Adrenocortical hyperplasia or nodular disease was the second most frequent finding (39.6% vs. 25.7%). Adrenocortical carcinoma was rare (3.8% vs. 1.4%), and other benign adrenal lesions were observed in a small proportion of patients (7.5% vs. 1.4%), including adrenal cysts, myelolipomatous metaplasia, and focal lymphocytic infiltration of the adrenal medulla (Table 3).

Postoperative serum cortisol data were available for 80 (70.2%) of the 114 patients included in the study. Biochemical remission was defined as a postoperative morning serum cortisol level of < 5 µg/dL. Overall, 71 patients (88.8%) achieved biochemical remission, whereas 60 (75.0%) demonstrated profound cortisol suppression with postoperative cortisol levels < 2 µg/dL. Seven patients (8.8%) had postoperative cortisol levels > 5 µg/dL but were within the normal reference range. Persistent hypercortisolism was observed in two patients (2.5%).

One patient in the MACS group demonstrated persistently elevated postoperative serum cortisol levels; however, further endocrine evaluation revealed adequate suppression on high-dose dexamethasone suppression testing, with both urinary free cortisol and late-night salivary cortisol levels within the normal limits. Based on these findings, the patient was considered to be in a state of biochemical remission. The second patient belonged to the overt Cushing syndrome group and had a complex clinical history of pituitary-dependent Cushing disease, including two previous transsphenoidal pituitary surgeries and a bilateral adrenalectomy. Follow-up imaging revealed recurrent disease in the right adrenal surgical bed. Although repeat surgical intervention was recommended, the patient declined further surgery and was subsequently managed with medical therapy alone.

Metabolic follow-up data were available for 80 patients in this study. In the overall cohort, postoperative LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels were significantly lower than preoperative values (p = 0.001 and p = 0.02, respectively), whereas no significant differences were observed in fasting plasma glucose or HDL cholesterol levels. In the subgroup analysis, patients with overt Cushing syndrome demonstrated significant postoperative reductions in LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels (p = 0.006 and p = 0.025, respectively). In the MACS group, a significant decrease was observed only in LDL cholesterol levels following adrenalectomy (p = 0.05), whereas fasting glucose, triglyceride, and HDL cholesterol levels did not change significantly (Table 4).

Discussion

Cushing’s syndrome is a serious endocrine disorder that leads to cardiovascular, metabolic, musculoskeletal, psychological, and immunological complications due to chronic exposure to excess cortisol [9]. Hypertension, insulin resistance, DM, and dyslipidemia markedly increase the risk of cardiovascular disease [10, 11]. Musculoskeletal complications, such as osteoporosis and muscle atrophy, reduce patients’ functional capacity, while psychological effects, such as depression and cognitive impairment, negatively affect the quality of life [12, 13]. Furthermore, high cortisol levels suppress the immune system, increasing the risk of infection [14].

This study evaluated the clinical characteristics, surgical outcomes, and histopathological findings of patients who underwent minimally invasive adrenalectomy for overt Cushing’s syndrome or MACS. These results are consistent with previous reports demonstrating low morbidity and favorable outcomes associated with minimally invasive adrenalectomy in patients with hypercortisolism [15–18]. Importantly, our findings further support the use of minimally invasive approaches as a standard surgical strategy, even in patients with MACS, in whom the indication for surgery remains controversial. From a clinical perspective, the absence of major complications in our cohort reinforces the feasibility of surgical management in experienced centers and highlights its potential role in improving disease-related comorbidities.

Surgical intervention, particularly adrenalectomy, has been shown to improve metabolic abnormalities in many patients; however, the extent of improvement varies among individuals. Miyazato et al. evaluated patients who underwent laparoscopic adrenalectomy for overt Cushing’s syndrome and subclinical Cushing’s syndrome (SCS) and reported long-term improvement or normalization of hypertension and diabetes in 66.7% and 47.1% of patients, respectively [19].

Recent studies suggest that MACS is present in approximately 5–30% of patients with adrenal incidentaloma. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that MACS is associated with an increased prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors and metabolic abnormalities, including hypertension and impaired glucose metabolism, highlighting the clinical importance of identifying cortisol excess even in the absence of overt Cushingoid features [19–21].

Several studies have compared surgical and conservative treatment approaches in patients with MACS. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated that adrenalectomy provides significant improvement in metabolic parameters and improves cardiometabolic risk, including glucose metabolism, lipid profile, blood pressure, and body weight, compared with conservative management. These findings suggest that surgery may be an important treatment option for appropriately selected patients [21]. In the present study, which included 114 patients, 45 patients (39.5%) underwent surgery for overt Cushing’s syndrome, whereas 69 patients (60.5%) underwent surgery for MACS. Patients in the MACS group did not exhibit the typical Cushingoid stigmata. The relatively high proportion of MACS in our cohort may be explained by the tertiary referral nature of our center, where adrenal incidentalomas are frequently detected and comprehensively evaluated. At our center, the decision for adrenalectomy in patients with MACS was individualized and made through a multidisciplinary endocrine council in accordance with current guidelines. Surgery was not routinely performed in all patients with MACS and a single adenoma but was primarily considered in those with comorbidities potentially attributable to cortisol excess, such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, or other metabolic disturbances, particularly when these comorbidities were progressive or difficult to control. Tumor-related factors, including interval growth and suspicious imaging features, were also considered.

A recent meta-analysis by Salvio et al. reported significant reductions in LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels following treatment of Cushing’s syndrome, whereas HDL cholesterol levels generally remained unchanged [22]. Similarly, recent evidence suggests that adrenalectomy in patients with MACS may improve cardiometabolic comorbidities, particularly glucose metabolism and cardiovascular risk parameters, compared with conservative management [21, 23]. In our cohort, postoperative LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels were significantly lower than preoperative values, whereas no significant differences were observed in fasting plasma glucose or HDL cholesterol levels. Subgroup analysis demonstrated significant postoperative reductions in LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels in patients with overt Cushing’s syndrome. In the MACS group, adrenalectomy was associated with a significant decrease only in LDL cholesterol levels, whereas fasting plasma glucose, triglyceride, and HDL cholesterol levels remained unchanged.

The management of Cushing’s syndrome involves several surgical modalities, including laparoscopic, retroperitoneoscopic, and open adrenalectomy. A thorough understanding of the distinctions among these techniques is essential to optimize patient outcomes in terms of perioperative safety, therapeutic efficacy, and postoperative recovery. Since it was first introduced by Gagner in 1992, laparoscopic adrenalectomy has increasingly become the preferred surgical approach due to its minimally invasive nature. Compared with open adrenalectomy, this technique is associated with lower morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery, while achieving comparable success rates in symptom resolution [24, 25]. Because laparoscopic adrenalectomy causes minimal tissue damage, it is associated with a lower rate of postoperative complications and allows for a quicker return to daily activities [26]. Retroperitoneoscopic adrenalectomy is an alternative surgical approach for the treatment of adrenal masses, offering specific advantages over open and transperitoneal laparoscopic adrenalectomy. Studies have shown that the retroperitoneoscopic approach is associated with less postoperative pain and enables a faster return to daily activity [6, 27]. Moreover, the posterior approach allows direct access to the adrenal gland through the retroperitoneal space without traversing intra-abdominal organs, thereby reducing the risk of injury to intra-abdominal structures [28]. In our study, the majority of patients underwent transabdominal laparoscopic adrenalectomy, which has been the standard surgical approach for adrenal masses at our institution for many years. Although PRA was performed in selected cases, the number of patients treated with this approach was limited, precluding a meaningful comparison between the two techniques.

Elfenbein et al. analyzed the data of 3100 patients who underwent open or laparoscopic adrenalectomy using the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Program data. Although there was no difference in the 30-day mortality rates between the two techniques, postoperative morbidity was significantly higher in the open surgery group (18.8% vs. 6.4%, P < 0.01). Similarly, the length of postoperative hospital stay was significantly longer in patients who underwent open adrenalectomy (5 vs. 2 days, P < 0.01). The incidence of specific complications was also significantly higher in the open surgery group, including superficial surgical site infection (3% vs. 0.8%), deep incisional surgical site infection (1.1% vs. 0.1%), pneumonia (3.3% vs. 1%), unplanned reintubation (2.5% vs. 0.7%), ventilator support > 48 h (2.3% vs. 0.6%), urinary tract infection (3.4% vs. 1.2%), postoperative bleeding within 72 h (4.8% vs. 0.5%), and systemic sepsis (3% vs. 0.7%). All differences were statistically significant, further supporting the perioperative safety advantages of the laparoscopic approach over open adrenalectomy [29].

Kazaure et al. reported that adrenalectomy performed by high-volume surgeons is associated with lower complication rates than that performed by low-volume surgeons [30]. Similarly, Al-Qurayshi et al. evaluated the impact of surgeon experience on surgical outcomes and healthcare costs following adrenalectomy and found that patients operated on by low-volume surgeons had significantly higher rates of postoperative complications. The study also demonstrated that referring patients to high-volume surgeons could lead to a 7.7% reduction in overall healthcare costs [31].

Studies examining the relationship between hospital adrenalectomy volume and postoperative outcomes are fewer in number compared to those focusing on surgeon volume, and their results are heterogeneous. In the study by Bergamini et al., which analyzed 833 adrenalectomy cases recorded in the Italian Registry of Endoscopic Surgery–Adrenalectomy database between 2000 and 2009, surgical centers were stratified into “referral centers” (> 30 adrenalectomies performed) and “non-referral centers” (< 30 adrenalectomies), based on a predefined threshold. The findings demonstrated that patients operated on in referral centers experienced significantly lower rates of conversion to open surgery (1.6% vs. 6.0%, p = 0.003) and postoperative complications (4.8% vs. 22.0%, p < 0.001), highlighting the potential benefits of higher institutional surgical volume [32].

Consistent with these findings, postoperative complications in our study were rare and predominantly minor, with no major complications (Clavien–Dindo grade ≥ III) or mortality observed. These outcomes likely reflect the experiences of a high-volume tertiary endocrine surgery center.

This study had several limitations. First, it had a retrospective design, which may have introduced selection and information biases. Second, the study was conducted at a single tertiary referral center, which may have limited the generalizability of the findings to other institutions. In addition, although the sample size represented a relatively large single-center experience, the number of patients who underwent PRA was limited, precluding a robust comparison between surgical techniques. Finally, long-term functional and metabolic outcomes after adrenalectomy were not systematically evaluated in all patients. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable data on the surgical outcomes of minimally invasive adrenalectomy in patients with overt Cushing’s syndrome and mild autonomous cortisol secretion.

Conclusion

This study evaluated the outcomes of patients who underwent minimally invasive adrenalectomy for overt Cushing’s syndrome and MACS at a tertiary endocrine surgery center over a 20-year period. Our findings demonstrate that minimally invasive adrenalectomy is a safe and effective surgical treatment strategy with no perioperative mortality or major complications and a very low rate of long-term morbidity. The predominance of MACS cases in our cohort highlights the increasing detection of adrenal incidentalomas using modern imaging techniques and supports the potential role of surgery in selected patients with MACS. Overall, our results support the use of minimally invasive procedures in patients with overt Cushing’s syndrome and MACS and emphasize the importance of management in experienced, high-volume endocrine surgery centers.
