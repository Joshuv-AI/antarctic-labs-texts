Polarization of tumor-infiltrating macrophages predicts complete response to neoadjuvant treatment in patients with rectal cancer
Authors: Aylin Alkan, Fabian Byvald, Rode Grönkvist, Gustaf Danielsson, Joshua Cumming, Peter Falk, Eva Angenete, Ulf Yrlid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCharacterizing the composition of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) has shown promise in understanding variable responses to neoadjuvant treatment in rectal cancer. Despite this, no method has been established that can confidently predict the response to pre-operative chemoradiotherapy. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) in the TME can be polarized by the biochemical milieu to adopt pro-inflammatory (M1-like) and anti-inflammatory (M2-like) phenotypes. Utilizing the spectrum of TAM polarization as a biomarker to predict patient response to neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer patients has not been fully explored. We address this using 7-plex immunofluorescence staining to quantify 16 subsets of TAMs in rectal adenocarcinoma pretreatment biopsies. Pretreatment biopsies were obtained from 128 patients with known tumor regression grades (TRG). Through quantifying the prevalence of TAM subsets, we identified a higher density of the completely M1-polarized subset and a lower density of the completely M2-polarized subset in patients with a complete pathological response, the absence of detectable tumor cells following treatment. Predictive modeling using TAM subsets showed that only the densities of two completely polarized subsets were predictors of therapeutic response in patients with rectal cancer. Significantly, we demonstrate that the full panel of markers we employed were required to define predictive populations, as total TAMs or TAM subsets defined by fewer markers were incapable of predicting treatment response. Taken together, we validate the utility of a multiplex approach to define the spectrum of TAM polarization, which can be leveraged to identify patients that will have a complete pathological response to neoadjuvant treatment.

Introduction

Rectal cancer is one of the most common malignant tumors, with an estimated 700,000 new cases and 300,000 deaths globally each year.1 Surgery is the primary treatment, although preoperative (neoadjuvant) treatment reduces local recurrence rates.2 Patients show variable levels of response to neoadjuvant treatment. Whilst some patients show very little reduction in tumor burden, almost 20% of patients with advanced rectal cancer achieve a pathological complete response (pCR) in which no viable tumor cells can be detected following treatment.2-4 Such patients may stand to benefit from avoiding surgery, with the associated risk of significant postoperative complications, and are instead treated according to “watch and wait”, where they are monitored for disease recurrence. It would be beneficial in a clinical setting to identify patients who are likely to achieve a CR. Despite this there are currently no effective methods employed to identify complete responders.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) has recently emerged as a promising candidate in understanding treatment response.5 Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are one of the most abundant immune cells present in the TME in several human cancers, including colorectal cancer.6 TAMs exhibit plasticity in response to environmental stimuli and polarization of macrophages can occur between TAM phenotypes.7,8 TAMs can be polarized towards a pro-inflammatory M1-like state and an anti-inflammatory M2-like state, though their phenotypes frequently span between these extremes and exist in a spectrum of activation states directed by the local environment.7-11 Several studies have investigated the prognostic role of TAMs in colorectal cancer, and some have shown an association between higher macrophage density and improved survival.12-14 However, there is contrasting evidence of the prognostic role of TAMs in colorectal cancer with studies also showing an association with worse survival or no significant association with survival.15-17 In addition, evaluation of TAMs is often performed using a single polarization marker,12-15,17 which provides a limited assessment of the diversity of macrophage phenotypes. Studies have shown extensive heterogeneity among macrophages through profiling tissues with single cell RNA sequencing, particularly in inflammatory conditions like cancer.11,18 However, such studies are unable to define the spatial distribution of phenotypically distinct TAM subpopulations. Therefore, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) has been used to study TAMs in patients with colorectal cancer to determine their M1/M2 polarization state and quantify the prevalence of subsets in stromal and epithelial tumor regions.19

The possible predictive role of the M1/M2 polarization spectrum of TAMs in relation to the neoadjuvant treatment response in rectal cancer has not yet been addressed. We therefore utilized two markers (CD86 and interferon regulatory factor 5 (IRF5)) associated with the M1 phenotype and two markers (transcription factor cMaf (MAF) and mannose receptor C-type 1 (CD206)) associated with the M2 phenotype, which were previously used in relation to long-term survival in colorectal cancer.19 Among these markers, CD86 has been associated with the M1 phenotype and plays an important role as a co-stimulatory signal for antigen presentation to T cells.20-22 IRF5 is a transcription factor that promotes M1 macrophage polarization through the induction of IL-12A and IL-12B and the repression of IL-10 in macrophages.23 cMaf is a transcription factor that is associated with the M2 phenotype and is downregulated in IFN-γ driven M1 macrophages.24 CD206 is a scavenger receptor that promotes several cellular functions, including the endocytosis of mannosylated inflammatory glycoproteins25 and is often highly expressed in M2 macrophages.26

We demonstrate that a higher density of the completely M1 polarized TAM subset (expressing CD86 and IRF5 but not cMaf or CD206) and a lower density of the completely M2 polarized TAM subset (expressing cMaf and CD206 but not CD86 or IRF5) are present in patients with a complete pathological response to neoadjuvant treatment. Our findings illustrate that unveiling the polarization spectrum of TAM phenotypes can be exploited to predict patient response to neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

In total, 1774 patients with rectal adenocarcinoma were diagnosed between 2007 and 2019 at Sahlgrenska University Hospital (Gothenburg, Sweden) and were identified in the Swedish Colorectal Cancer Registry. Patients who had an interval after neoadjuvant treatment sufficient to expect tumor regression were included. The median value (range) from the start of treatment until surgery was 104 d (28–594 d) (4 patients missing details). In total, 128 patients fulfilled the inclusion criteria and had available diagnostic biopsies (Figure 1). Pseudonymized biopsy samples were obtained from the Pathology Department at Sahlgrenska University Hospital. Informed consent was waived by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority (registration number 2019-04748), as the samples were pseudonymized and could not be traced to individual patients. Clinical data for the study population were collected from the Swedish ColoRectal Cancer Registry as well as patient charts. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

H&E staining of the specimens was performed as described elsewhere.27 Briefly, deparaffinized slides were stained with hematoxylin solution (Histolab) for 5 min, followed by a 3-min wash in water and then stained with Eosin (Histolab) for 30 s. The slides were then placed in an ethanol gradient series [70%, 95%, 99.5% (twice)] for ten quick dips, followed by two 5-min incubations in xylene before mounting with Pertex (Histolab).

Tumor response evaluation

H&E stained surgical specimens were retrieved for all patients, and the response to treatment according to the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) tumor regression grade (TRG)28 was determined by a pathologist (G.D.). TRG is graded from 0 to 3, where 0 indicates a pathological complete response, and 3 indicates no response/no regression to the neoadjuvant treatment.

Uncertainties regarding tumor regression were resolved by a consultant gastrointestinal pathologist. Investigators involved in preparing and analyzing the preoperative tissue slides were blinded to the TRG, until all multiplex immunofluorescence staining and analyses were completed and the statistical analyses were initiated.

Multiplex immunofluorescence staining

A multiplex immunofluorescence panel to characterize macrophage polarization using the tyramide signal amplification technique to maximize sensitivity was designed.29 Based on previous data,19 two M1 (CD86 and IRF5) and two M2 phenotype markers (CD206 and cMaf) were used together with the pan-macrophage marker CD68 and the pan-epithelial/tumor cell marker cytokeratin (KRT), as listed in Table S2. 4 μm formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of the pre-operative biopsies were prepared and fixed on Superfrost Plus microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The slides were kept at 37 °C for 1 h and then incubated at 60 °C for 1 h. Deparaffinization of the slides prior to staining was performed in xylene (twice) for 5 min, followed by an ethanol gradient series [99.5% (twice), 95%, and 70%], and distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed on deparaffinized slides submerged in 1× citrate buffer (pH 6) using a 2100 Retriever pressure cooker (Aptum Biologics). Two rounds of heating were applied, and the slides were kept in a pressure cooker to cool off. Thereafter, the slides were rinsed with 1× PBS twice. The moisture around the sections was wiped off with tissues and the sections were encircled with a hydrophobic pen (Histolab) for the staining process.

Before the first staining, the slides were incubated with ice-cold 3% hydrogen peroxide solution (Honeywell, Fluka) for 10 min, followed by two washes with 1× PBS for 3 min each. The rest of the staining protocol for each marker was started with 10 min blocking with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (Sigma Aldrich) in 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The slides were sequentially stained with antibodies and tyramide signal amplification (TSA) reagents as listed in Table S2. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h and washed twice with 1× PBS for 3 min. The slides were then incubated with secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated reagent (Dako, EnVision FLEX/HRP) for 10 min and washed twice with 1× PBS for 3 min. After washing, the slides were incubated with TSA reagents for 10 min as per manufacturer's protocol. To remove the primary and secondary antibodies for each upcoming staining, the slides were submerged in 1× citrate buffer, followed by heating in a microwave oven at maximum until the buffer started to boil and at minimum for 10 min. The slides were placed in the dark to cool down and then rinsed with 1× PBS prior to the next marker staining procedure. This process was repeated until the last marker staining. For the last marker staining (CD206), the Opal 780 fluorochrome was used as a fluorophore so the manufacturer's protocol was followed. Briefly, after washing of secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated reagent, the slides were incubated with TSA-DIG (1:100 dilution) in TSA buffer for 10 min. Then the slides were submerged in 1× citrate buffer, followed by heating in a microwave oven at max until the buffer started to boil and at minimum, for 10 min. The slides were placed in the dark to cool down and then rinsed with 1× PBS. For detection, the slides were incubated with Opal 780 reagent (anti-DIG conjugated with Opal 780 fluorophore; 1:25 dilution in 0.1% BSA; Akoya Biosciences) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The slides were washed once with 1× PBS for 3 min, and the cell nuclei of each section were labeled with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI; Sigma Aldrich) for 10 min. Finally, the slides were rinsed twice with 1× PBS and once with MilliQ water and mounted with ProLong™ Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen).

Image analysis

All diagnostic H&E-stained specimens were examined by a pathologist (GD) who also marked the invasive tumor areas. The corresponding areas were localized on the immunofluorescence-stained sections (Figure S1). Immunofluorescence-stained slides were scanned with TissueFAXS (TissueGnostics) equipped with 7-channel SpectraSplit7 filter sets (Kromnigon) and a Hamamatsu C13440-20CU digital camera with 20× magnification (Hamamatsu Photonics). The scanned slides were analyzed with StrataQuest software (v. 7.0.1.189 and v. 7.1.1.129; TissueGnostics). The tissue segmentation algorithm was manually processed based on cytokeratin expression to define the epithelium, stroma, and background (Figure 2A). Manual correction for tissue segmentation was performed if cytokeratin staining was weak. The areas of high erythrocyte, autofluorescence, tissue folds, and necrotic regions were manually marked and excluded from image analysis. DAPI was used to define the cell nuclei. The staining intensity of the markers varied slightly between sections. Therefore, the marker-specific threshold was defined by the positive and negative cell intensity levels of each marker. This was established as (1) the lowest intensity value of marker-positive cells and the highest intensity value of marker-negative cells and, if the intensities of the positive and negative cells did not overlap, or (2) as the intensity value that minimized the false positive cells counts error based on the density distributions, if there was overlap. After establishing the intensity threshold of each marker separately for the region of interest intensity thresholds range groups categorized as low, low to intermediate, intermediate, intermediate to high, and high intensity levels were defined (Table S3). The intensity threshold value for each marker was then set as the mean value of the threshold intensities of the marker values included in each of the five groups of ranges. Finally, the whole cohort was analyzed by the set intensity threshold value based on the marker-specific thresholds range groups (Table S3). The performance of the cell subset quantification by the StrataQuest software using the set intensity threshold for each marker within the range group was verified by random selection of stained sections in the range group followed by manual counting by a researcher (A.A.).

Evaluation of macrophage phenotyping and polarization

CD68+ cells were used to define TAMs. The density of TAM populations was determined in the TME of each patient by dividing the number of TAMs detected in the stroma by the area of stromal tissue (in mm2) of the individual region of interest (ROI). The TAMs were then assigned to 16 specific phenotypes using CD86, IRF5, cMaf, and CD206. A complete M1-polarized phenotype was defined as CD68+IRF5+CD86+cMaf−CD206− while a complete M2-polarized phenotype was defined as CD68+IRF5−CD86−cMaf+CD206+. A mixed phenotype was defined as TAMs with both M1 and M2 markers (CD68+IRF5+CD86+cMaf+CD206+), and M0 TAMs were defined as TAMs without M1 and M2 markers (CD68+IRF5−CD86−cMaf−CD206−).

Statistical analyses

TAM subsets comparisons which were defined a priori were conducted by comparing each TRG category against the union of the remaining three categories using Welch's unequal-variance two-sample t-test (two-sided). To control the family-wise error rate within each population, p-values were adjusted using Holm's step-down procedure (α = 0.05). To perform an unbiased screen across the 16 full phenotypes, we first tested for any TRG effect using Welch's one-way ANOVA. The resulting p-values were controlled across phenotypes using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR; q = 0.05). Only the phenotypes passing this screen underwent post-hoc testing as follows: four pre-specified one-vs-rest (for each TRG score category) Welch's unequal-variance two-sample t-test with Holm adjustment within the phenotype (α = 0.05). A post hoc contrast was considered significant only if its phenotype passed the FDR-adjusted screen and its Holm-adjusted p-value was ≤0.05.

Bootstrapped LASSO (Bolasso)30 logistic regression models were used (500 bootstrap runs) to investigate the associations between the 16 macrophage phenotypes and the TRG stage. Optimism-corrected Brier scores31 were used to compare the performance of different models, with optimism-corrected AUC values used for further illustration. Optimism correction is a resampling-based method to reduce bias in predictive performance statistics due to overfitting.31

Heatmap-based immunoscores were generated as previously described27 and compared using one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. ROC analysis was performed in R (pROC), and we evaluated TRG binary discrimination for the CD3+CD8, CD8+MxA, M1+M2, and composite CD8+MxA+M1+M2 predictors, with AUCs computed in the direction = “<”. Pairwise AUC differences versus M1/M2 were tested using DeLong's test (two-sided).

Results

Multiplex immunofluorescence analysis of M1/M2 markers reveals a spectrum of macrophage polarization in rectal cancer

To evaluate TAM density and polarization, multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF) staining with a 7-color panel (Table S2) was performed in the tumor tissue from pre-treatment biopsies of 128 patients with rectal adenocarcinoma (Figure 2A). The expression of CD68 was used as a pan TAM marker. Each CD68+ TAM was then assigned a specific phenotype according to two M1 (CD86 and IRF5) and two M2 (cMaf and CD206) markers (Figure 2B). Completely M1-polarized (CD68+IRF5+CD86+cMaf−CD206−), completely M2-polarized (CD68+IRF5−CD86−cMaf+CD206+), and mixed phenotypes were defined to evaluate TAM polarization individually (Figure 2C). Taken together, this method allowed for a detailed phenotypic characterization of the macrophage microenvironment in rectal cancer by revealing 16 distinct subsets of CD68+ TAMs.

The density of completely polarized M1 and M2 subsets are altered in patients with pathological complete response to neoadjuvant treatment

We next quantified the densities of specific TAM subsets in the TME of pre-treatment biopsies from patients with variable responses to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. The response to neoadjuvant therapy is classified according to tumor regression grade (TRG) using the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) classification system, where TRG 0 corresponds to a pathologic complete response (pCR) and TRG 3 indicates no response (no regression) to neoadjuvant treatment. We found that total macrophage density was not significantly associated with the TRG score (Figure 3A). Furthermore, when using either only M1 markers (CD68+IRF5+CD86+) or only M2 markers (CD68+cMaf+CD206+) to define TAM polarization subsets, no significant association with the TRG score was observed (Figure 3B and C). These findings indicate that these broad TAM phenotypes do not significantly differ in patients with variable responses to neoadjuvant treatment.

Therefore, the analyses extended to include expression, or no expression, of the four markers (IRF5, CD86, cMaf, or CD206), generating 16 different subsets of CD68+ macrophages. The phenotype variable names were abbreviated using the expression of the four markers. Phenotypes are abbreviated as M1±±M2±±, where the two signs after M1 correspond to CD86, IRF5 (left-to-right), and those after M2 correspond to cMaf, CD206, for example, CD86− IRF5+ cMaf− CD206+ is written as M1−⁺M2−⁺. We found that the proportion of macrophage subsets among all the macrophages (CD68+) varied, with M0 macrophages (M1−M2−) being the most prevalent subset (24.3%), whilst the M1−+M2+− subset was the least abundant (0.9%) (Figure S2A).

An unbiased screen of the densities of all 16 TAM phenotypes identified five macrophage subsets which showed a significant difference across TRG scores (Figure S2A). This revealed that higher densities of completely M1-polarized macrophages (M1++M2−−) were observed in patients with a complete pathological response to treatment (pCR = TRG0) (p-value < 0.011; Figure 4A) and that these patients also had lower densities of completely M2-polarized TAMs (M1−−M2++) compared to patients with TRG1, 2, or 3 (p-value < 0.0001; Figure 4B). In addition, three macrophage subsets that were partially M2 polarized (expression of more M2 than M1 markers) had significantly lower densities in TRG 0 patients (Figure S2B–D). There was a weak yet significant inverse correlation between the densities of completely polarized M1 and M2 TAMs (Figure 4C and Table S4).

The densities of completely polarized TAM subsets are both significantly altered in patients with pCR to neoadjuvant treatment (TRG0) compared to the other response groups (TRG1, 2, and 3). Patients with pCR have elevated densities of completely polarized M1 TAMs and lower densities of completely polarized M2 TAMs and partially polarized M2 TAMs. Taken together, this evidences an association between the TAM polarization spectrum and response to neoadjuvant therapy.

The densities of completely polarized M1 and M2 subsets strongly predict a complete pathological response to neoadjuvant treatment

To investigate the predictive performance of different TAM phenotypes on TRG stage, the densities of all 16 phenotypes were used as predictors. The TRG stage was grouped into the clinically most relevant groupings: 0 vs 1–3 (complete responders vs non-complete responders) and 0–2 vs 3 (responders/partial responders vs non-responders). We aimed to determine whether predicting patients who would achieve a pCR (complete responders) or patients who would not benefit from neoadjuvant treatment (non-responders) was possible using TAM subset density. Bootstrapped LASSO (Bolasso)30 logistic regression models were used (500 bootstrap runs) to investigate the associations between TAM phenotypes and TRG stage. A selection threshold of 0.9 was used, meaning that variables with nonzero regression coefficients in at least 90% of the bootstrap samples were included (Figure 5 and Table 1). Models were compared using likelihood ratio tests and optimism-corrected Brier scores,31 and performance was illustrated using optimism-corrected AUC (Table 2).

The Bolasso procedure using TRG 0 vs 1–3 as the outcome selected the densities of M1++M2−− (completely M1-polarized) and M1−−M2++ (completely M2-polarized) TAM densities (Figure 5) as predictors. We found that the apparent Brier score of this model was 0.063, and the optimism-corrected Brier score was 0.07 (Table 2). The apparent AUC was 0.905, supporting excellent discrimination between TRG 0 vs TRG 1–3. The optimism-corrected AUC was only slightly lower at 0.903, suggesting only slight overfitting. When the TRG stage was grouped as 0–2 vs 3, using the threshold of 0.9, there were three selected phenotypes (Figure S3). A logistic regression model using the three most selected variables as predictors had an optimism-corrected AUC of just 0.681, indicating low discriminative ability in distinguishing between the TRG 0–2 vs 3 groups (Table 2).

We have previously shown, in the same patient cohort, that a footprint of type I interferon, identified by MxA expressing cells in the stroma, is increased in TRG0.27 Furthermore, we showed that adding MxA as an independent parameter to the previously published Immunoscore (ISB) assessment of CD8 T cells32 improved prediction of pCR.27 However, there was no correlation between the quantity of either the completely polarized M1 or M2 TAMs and the density of MxA or CD8 T cells (Table S4).

We used densities of completely polarized M1 and M2 TAMs to generate a macrophage-only ISB including only completely polarized macrophages. In addition, we generated ISB by combining completely polarized macrophages with CD8 T cells and MxA. The classical ISB based on CD3 and CD8 T cell densities showed a borderline significant increase in TRG0 compared to TRG1–3 (p = 0.0505, Figure S4A). Our previously published two-parameter ISB based on MxA and CD8 T cells was significantly higher in TRG0 patients (p = 0.006, Figure S4B). Both the macrophage-only ISB using completely polarized macrophage subsets alone and the ISB including completely polarized macrophages in combination with MxA and CD8 were significantly higher in TRG0 (p = <0.0001, Figure S4C and D).

We evaluated the discriminative performance of distinct Immunoscores for classifying TRG0 vs TRG1-3 by comparing ROC AUCs (Figure S4E). The macrophage-only ISB (AUC = 0.87) performed significantly better than the classical ISB (AUC = 0.65, p = 0.0015) and our previously published two-parameter ISB (AUC = 0.71, p = 0.027). There was no significant difference between the macrophage-only ISB and the four-parameter ISB, which was based on completely polarized macrophage subsets, stromal MxA+ cells and CD8 T cells (AUC = 0.89, p = 0.62). In accordance, including the density of MxA and/or CD8 T cells together with completely polarized M1 and M2 TAM densities did not improve the predictive performance of the Bolasso model (Table S5).

These results demonstrate that the density of two discrete TAM subsets, completely M1 and completely M2 polarized, identified by a multiplex approach in pretreatment biopsies, are strongly predictive of which patients will have a pCR to neoadjuvant treatment.

Discussion

Rectal cancer tumors respond variably to neoadjuvant treatment, but there are currently no established methods to predict this and thereby tailor treatment. The immunological composition of the tumor microenvironment has been identified as a potential candidate for predicting the response to neoadjuvant treatment. Given that myeloid populations have not been extensively examined in this context, we hypothesized that characterizing macrophage polarization in pre-treatment rectal cancer biopsies could be utilized to identify novel predictive components of the immune landscape.

Using 7-plex mIF, we define a spectrum of macrophage polarization states in the stroma of rectal cancer. We defined discrete subsets of TAMs based on the presence or absence of two M1 markers (CD86 and IRF5) and two M2 markers (cMaf and CD206), yielding 16 distinct phenotypes. Our approach reflects the consensus that the M1/M2 binarization of macrophage polarization is an oversimplification and that TAMs instead exist across a spectrum of polarization states.7-11 In further support of the greater heterogeneity of macrophage subsets, the results emerging from single-cell RNA studies of TAMs in CRC describes at least seven different TAM subsets.33 However, how TAM subsets described in single-cell RNA sequencing studies correspond to those defined by the expression of M1 and M2 markers, as in our study, has yet to be clarified and would be an important addition to the field. What has already been shown is that both CD86 and CD206 are broadly expressed across macrophages at the RNA level in single-cell studies34 suggesting that directly mapping the protein-defined “M1/M2” states used in this study onto transcriptomic phenotypes will be challenging and could require additional protein markers and therefore even higher tissue multiplexing.

The importance of using multiple markers to define TAM polarization is underlined by our findings, where neither the total number of macrophages nor macrophage subsets defined by co-expression of only M1 or M2 markers independently could be used to determine response to neoadjuvant treatment, i.e. the density of CD68+IRF5+CD86+ or CD68+cMaf+CD206+ macrophages in the stroma did not have a significant association with TRG scores. However, when characterizing the expression of M1/M2 markers simultaneously to define the fully polarized M1 and M2 subsets, we identified an association between TAM subsets and the response to neoadjuvant treatment. We demonstrated that increased densities of completely M1-polarized macrophages and reduced densities of completely M2-polarized macrophages are present in patients who will achieve pCR to neoadjuvant treatment. Completely polarized TAM subset densities were also able to strongly predict pCR with optimism-corrected AUC values of 0.903.

In support of our findings, profiling pre-treatment biopsies in rectal cancer with bulk RNA sequencing identified reduced expression of the M2 marker CD163 in patients with a good response to neoadjuvant treatment.4 Furthermore, immunohistochemistry staining of CD163 showed reduced expression in treatment naïve biopsies of patients with a good response to neoadjuvant treatment compared to those with a poor response, though this was only assessed in a limited number of biopsies.4 Liu et al also described reduced expression of CD163, in addition to the M2 macrophage-associated factor MCSF, in pre-treatment rectal cancer biopsies of patients with pCR.35 However, using CD163 alone or in combination with MCSF to predict pCR yielded AUC values of 0.77 and 0.79, respectively, demonstrating worse performance than our more multiplexed approach. Another study using the M2 marker CD206 in isolation detected no difference in expression between responders and non-responders, further highlighting the importance of applying a multi-marker approach.36 Taken together, we demonstrate that a multi-marker approach to defining the spectrum of TAM polarization can identify completely polarized macrophage subsets that are strongly predictive of pCR to neoadjuvant treatment.

Evidence of the predictive capacity of M1/M2 polarization prior to chemoradiotherapy treatment has been reported in other solid tumors. In a study of cervical cancer, patients with a high M1:M2 ratio, defined by pSTAT1 and c-MAF expression in TAMs, experienced more frequently complete pCR compared to cases with low a M1:M2 ratio.37 Furthermore, a low M1:M2 ratio was an independent predictor of poor response to the chemoradiotherapy.37 A significant association of high infiltration of M2 macrophages, defined by CD163 and CD206 expression, and a poor response to neoadjuvant treatment has also been reported in esophageal cancer.38 In breast cancer, it was found that the absence of a beneficial response to neoadjuvant treatment was associated with the presence of an M2 macrophage phenotype.39 Finally, a higher density of intraepithelial M1-polarized TAMs was found in good responders to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in pancreatic cancer.40 In addition, higher densities and proximities of M1-polarized TAMs to tumor cells in neoadjuvant-treated tumors were associated with improved tumor histologic response.40 Hence, prior to chemotherapy the M1:M2 balance present in the microenvironment of tumors, other than rectal, can be correlated to the ensuing response to neoadjuvant treatment.

In the tumor microenvironment, there are several potential mechanisms underlying the different roles of M1 and M2 TAMs.41,42 Briefly, anti-tumorigenic M1-like TAMs will phagocytose tumor cells and efficiently present antigens to lymphocytes, thereby increasing the antitumor immune response, whereas M2-like TAMs are implicated in pro-tumorigenic effects through secretion of anti-inflammatory mediators.42 In the context of response to neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, the role of polarized macrophages can be linked directly or indirectly. Macrophage polarization is modulated by the cellular and biochemical milieu of the tumor microenvironment. IFNγ is classically associated with M1 polarization, and type I IFN signaling has also been linked with an inflammatory phenotype in macrophages.43 Elevated levels of type I and II IFN signaling, in addition to the IFN-induced cytokine CXCL10, have been defined in complete responders to neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer.4,44,45 In contrast, increased TGFβ signaling in the TME can drive M2 polarization.46 Enhanced TGFβ signaling has been linked directly to impaired response to neoadjuvant therapy.47 In addition, a consensus molecular subtype of rectal cancer partially defined by elevated TGFβ signaling (CMS4) was found to be associated with a lack of pCR.48 Taken together, fully polarized M1 and M2 macrophages can reflect tumor microenvironments enriched in IFN and TGFβ signaling, respectively. As these signaling programs are closely tied to neoadjuvant treatment outcomes, macrophage polarization may serve as an indirect indicator of the immune and stromal signaling programs that shape therapeutic responsiveness.

There is also evidence that polarized macrophages can directly modulate responses to neoadjuvant therapy. CXCL7 secreted by M2-polarized TAMs has been shown to confer chemoresistance through metabolically reprogrammed tumor cells.49 Furthermore, M2 macrophage-derived CCL22 has been shown to prevent chemotherapy-induced apoptosis in tumor cells.50 In contrast, it has been demonstrated that low-dose irradiation can stimulate M1 macrophage polarization, which in turn promotes an anti-tumor immune response.51 Notably, nanoparticles that enhance reactive oxygen species within the tumor microenvironment have been shown to promote macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype, concomitant with improved radiotherapy efficacy. By augmenting oxidative stress during irradiation, this approach supports a role for M1 macrophages as contributors to enhanced responses to neoadjuvant radiotherapy rather than solely correlates with treatment sensitivity.52 Taken together, these findings suggest that macrophage polarization may both directly influence responses to neoadjuvant therapy and indirectly serve as a surrogate of microenvironmental signaling states associated with differential treatment outcomes. This evidence demonstrates how elevated infiltration of completely polarized M1 macrophages and reduced densities of completely polarized M2 macrophages can define a beneficial response to neoadjuvant treatment, as observed in our data.

Whilst we have identified completely polarized macrophage subsets as components of the tumor microenvironment capable of predicting the response to neoadjuvant therapy, previous studies have identified additional immune subpopulations with a predictive capacity. The density of CD8+ T cells in rectal cancer has been determined to be a predictor of a beneficial response to neoadjuvant treatment in several studies, although contradicting results have been reported.32,53-56 Two meta-analyses have concluded that tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells are biomarkers of the response to treatment57 as well as the long-term survival of rectal cancer patients.58 A biopsy-adapted immune score (ISB) that determines the density of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells in the entire tumor area has also been developed. Patients defined as having a high ISB compared to intermediate or low are significantly enriched in patients with pCR (TRG0).32 We have previously shown that a footprint of type I interferon, defined by the expression of MxA, is an independent biomarker that, in combination with CD8+ T cell quantification, improves the prediction of pCR to neoadjuvant treatment of rectal adenocarcinoma.27 However, the densities of CD8 or MxA were not significantly correlated with the density of the completely polarized M1 or M2 subsets. In addition, including the CD8 and MxA densities did not improve the performance of the predictive models based on the M1 and M2 densities. Taken together, this demonstrates that although adding stromal MxA cell density to CD8+ T-cell quantification can improve the performance of the classical ISB,32 including these two parameters does not significantly enhance the already strong capacity to predict pCR by completely polarized TAMs.

We have demonstrated the utility of characterizing macrophage polarization to build predictive models for classifying pCR to neoadjuvant therapy in rectal cancer. However, further validation is necessary to confirm the broad applicability of the model with larger cohorts. Another limitation in this study was the size of the study population and that few tumors defined as TRG0 (n = 13) were available in the cohort. Moreover, other patient characteristics, such as radiological features from MRI, microsatellite instability status, and mutational data, were not available for this cohort, restricting the interpretation of the results. In addition, implementing multicolor IHC would be challenging in a clinical setting owing to increased turnaround time and cost as well as requiring access to specialized equipment and analysis. However, our findings demonstrate that multiple markers are required to unveil predictive populations, suggesting that this may become a necessity to accurately predict the response to treatment.

In conclusion, we employed quantitative, multiplex immunofluorescence to assess M1 and M2 polarization in TAMs in the rectal cancer tumor microenvironment. Through characterizing macrophage polarization with a defined panel of four markers, we demonstrated that the density of completely polarized M1 and M2 TAM subsets can strongly predict pCR in patients receiving neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. Overall, these findings underscore the potential of using this as the basis for developing a model used to guide neoadjuvant treatment decisions in a clinical setting and potentially improve patient outcomes by identifying those who will respond best to neoadjuvant treatment and may stand to benefit from avoiding surgery.
