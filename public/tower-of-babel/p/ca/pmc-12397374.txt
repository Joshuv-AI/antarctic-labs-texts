Acoustic generation of orbital currents
Authors: Mari Taniguchi, Satoshi Haku, Hyun-Woo Lee, Kazuya Ando
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In solids, the crystal field couples the electronic orbital degree of freedom to the lattice. This coupling suggests that an excitation of lattice dynamics could trigger the dynamics of orbital angular momentum of electrons, thereby generating orbital currents—a flow of electronic orbital angular momentum. However, the interplay between orbital currents and lattice dynamics has been elusive. Here, we report the observation of the acoustic orbital Hall effect, demonstrating the generation of orbital currents by lattice dynamics. By investigating the acoustoelectric properties of Ti/Ni bilayers induced by surface acoustic waves (SAWs), we demonstrate the generation of an orbital current polarized transverse to the SAW propagation direction. This phenomenon is reminiscent of the electric orbital Hall effect, which generates an orbital current polarized transverse to an applied electric field. We also show that acoustically driven ferromagnetic resonance generates an orbital current in the Ti/Ni bilayer, demonstrating acoustic orbital pumping. These findings highlight the potential of lattice dynamics in generating orbital currents, paving the way for exploring acoustic orbitronics.

Introduction

Understanding the dynamics of angular momentum in solids is fundamental to condensed matter physics. In magnetic heterostructures, various spintronic phenomena are driven by the transfer of angular momentum between different degrees of freedom, including the electron orbital and spin, the crystal lattice, and the magnetic order parameter1,2. One prominent example is the spin Hall effect, where an applied electric field generates a spin current3,4. In the spin Hall effect, the lattice serves as the fundamental source of angular momentum; the applied electric field triggers the transfer of angular momentum from the lattice to the electron orbital through the crystal field, which is then transferred to the electron spin through spin-orbit coupling2. The crucial role of the orbital degree of freedom in this process suggests that the spin Hall effect is a secondary phenomenon arising from the orbital Hall effect5, which generates an orbital current in response to an electric field, combined with spin-orbit coupling5–13. In fact, although the orbital angular momentum has often been assumed to be frozen in solids, recent experiments have demonstrated the significance of the orbital Hall effect in the dynamics of angular momentum in various systems14–26.

Lattice dynamics can play a primary role in generating spin currents. In particular, a surface acoustic wave (SAW) is promising lattice dynamics that carries lattice angular momentum. In metals with strong spin-orbit coupling, a SAW generates a spin current that flows transverse to the propagation direction of the SAW27,28. This phenomenon is called the acoustic spin Hall effect, as it is reminiscent of the spin Hall effect, where a spin current flows transverse to an applied electric field. It is noteworthy that the spin degree of freedom is coupled to the lattice only indirectly, whereas the electronic orbital degree of freedom is directly coupled to the lattice2, as illustrated by the process of angular momentum transfer in the spin Hall effect. This suggests the possibility that the acoustic spin Hall effect is driven by the generation of orbital currents from lattice dynamics, as the orbital Hall effect underlies the spin Hall effect. However, such acoustic generation of orbital currents—the acoustic orbital Hall effect—remains unexplored.

In this work, we report the observation of the acoustic orbital Hall effect—a phenomenon where a SAW generates an orbital current that flows transverse to the propagation direction of the SAW (see Fig. 1). We show that a magnetic-field-dependent voltage is induced under the excitation of a SAW in Ti/Ni bilayers, where the sizable orbital Hall effect has been observed recently17,18,29,30. The acoustic voltage changes systematically with variations in the excitation power, frequency, and propagation direction of the SAW, as well as the magnetic field. The acoustic voltage is found to depend on the thickness of the Ti layer, indicating that the voltage originates from the bulk of the Ti layer. We find that the observed characteristics of the acoustic voltage are consistent with the generation of an orbital current in the Ti layer polarized transverse to the propagation direction of the SAW. In the Ti/Ni bilayer, we also observe acoustic orbital pumping, where ferromagnetic resonance (FMR) driven by a SAW pumps an orbital current from the Ni layer to the Ti layer. Acoustic orbital pumping

As the first mechanism for the acoustic generation of orbital currents, we introduce the concept of SAW-induced orbital pumping, which we term acoustic orbital pumping. When a SAW propagates through a ferromagnetic film, the periodic elastic deformation of the film can induce magnetization precession via magnetoelastic coupling, provided the SAW frequency satisfies the FMR condition33,34. This phenomenon, known as acoustic FMR, has been shown to drive spin pumping, demonstrating that the coupled magnon-phonon dynamics can serve as a mechanism for spin current generation, referred to as acoustic spin pumping35. In the Ti/Ni bilayer, acoustic FMR is expected to drive acoustic orbital pumping, where the magnon-phonon coupled dynamics generates an orbital current. This orbital current can be detected electrically via the inverse orbital Hall effect in the Ti layer.

To investigate the acoustic orbital pumping using the inverse orbital Hall effect, the Ti/Ni bilayer was grown on a piezoelectric Y+128°-cut LiNbO3 substrate, positioned between two interdigital transducers (IDTs), IDT1 and IDT2 (see Fig. 3a, b). The bilayer was patterned into Hall bar structures using the standard photolithography followed by the lift-off technique. The IDTs were connected to a vector network analyzer (VNA) to excite and detect a Rayleigh-type SAW36. The SAW is excited by the inverse piezoelectric effect, where elastic deformation is produced by applying RF voltage. The SAW frequency is defined as fSAW = vs/λSAW, where vs is the sound velocity of the piezoelectric material. The wavelength of the SAW, λSAW, is defined by the periodicity of the IDTs. Acoustic orbital Hall effect

To quantify the DC voltage induced by lattice dynamics without triggering acoustic FMR, we measured Vxx while exciting the SAW at its fundamental frequency, f ~ fSAW, which is significantly below the FMR frequency. The observed characteristics of the acoustic voltage are the same with those of the voltage arising from the acoustic spin Hall effect. The mechanism underlying the voltage generation induced by the acoustic spin Hall effect in nonmagnetic/ferromagnetic (NM/FM) bilayers is similar to the spin Hall magnetoresistance. In the scenario of the acoustic spin Hall effect27, the lattice dynamics is assumed to generate an alternating current (AC) spin current that varies in both time and space in sync with the SAW in the NM layer. The spin current induces spin accumulation at the NM/FM interface. The magnitude of the spin accumulation at the interface depends on the direction of the magnetization in the FM layer as a result of the spin torque. This spin accumulation triggers a back flow spin current in the NM layer, which is then converted into a charge current through the inverse spin Hall effect, giving rise to the acoustic voltage. In the FM layer, the SAW-induced straining and magnetoelastic coupling lead to changes in the magnetization direction relative to the magnetic field, causing the acoustic voltage to acquire an extra factor of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(1/H)\sin 2{\phi }_{H}$$\end{document}(1/H)sin2ϕH compared to the resistance change originating from the spin Hall magnetoresistance. Although the above results align with the scenario of the acoustic spin Hall effect, the magnitude of ΔV in the Ti/Ni bilayer is orders of magnitude larger than that expected from the acoustic spin Hall effect in the Ti layer. It has been shown that the acoustic voltage ΔV scales with the spin Hall magnetoresistance ratio27, which is consistent with the above scenario where the electric inverse spin Hall effect mediates the voltage generation. The electric spin Hall angle of Ti is three orders of magnitude smaller than that of Pt, suggesting that ΔV originating from the acoustic spin Hall effect in the Ti/Ni bilayer is orders of magnitude smaller than that of the Pt/Ni bilayer. Nevertheless, ΔV of the Ti/Ni bilayers is only several times smaller than that of a Pt(3 nm)/Ni(3 nm) bilayer, as shown in Fig. 6c, d. This result indicates that the acoustic spin Hall effect is not the primarily source of the observed ΔV signals in the Ti/Ni bilayer. Here, the acoustic spin Hall signal in Pt-based structures has been shown to be maximized at approximately the Pt thickness used in this study27.

The acoustic voltage observed in the Ti/Ni bilayer suggests the presence of the acoustic orbital Hall effect, in which lattice dynamics generates an orbital current that varies in both time and space in sync with the SAW in the Ti layer (see Fig. 1). It has been shown that, in contrast to the electric spin Hall effect, which originates from spin-orbit coupling, the electric orbital Hall effect arises without relying on spin-orbit coupling9. In fact, recent experiments have demonstrated a large orbital Hall effect in Ti, despite its weak spin-orbit coupling17,18,29,30. Notably, the orbital Hall magnetoresistance, which provides a mechanism to explain the emergence of the acoustic voltage via the acoustic orbital Hall effect, has been observed for Ti/Ni bilayers29. The presence of the orbital Hall magnetoresistance suggests that a sizable ΔV can be produced if an orbital current is generated by the acoustic orbital Hall effect in the Ti/Ni bilayer.

The orbital current arising from the acoustic orbital Hall effect can produce the acoustic voltage ΔV through its interaction with the magnetization. Among conventional 3d transition ferromagnetic metals, Ni exhibits the strongest interaction between orbital currents and magnetization due to its strong spin-orbit correlation2,14,15,17,30. Consequently, the reflection of the orbital current, associated with orbital torque, arising from the acoustic orbital Hall effect depends on the magnetization direction in the Ti/Ni bilayer. For instance, when the magnetization is perpendicular to the polarization direction of the orbital current in the Ti layer, the orbital current exerts an orbital torque and is effectively absorbed by the Ni layer17. In contrast, when the magnetization is parallel to the orbital current polarization, the absence of orbital torque suppresses orbital current absorption. As a result, the orbital current produced by the acoustic orbital Hall effect is maximally reflected at the Ti/Ni interface when the polarization is parallel to the magnetization and minimally reflected when it is perpendicular. The reflected orbital current is subsequently converted into a charge current via the inverse electric orbital Hall effect in the Ti layer (see Fig. 1). Due to the magnetization-dependent reflection of the orbital current, this charge current depends on the magnetization direction, which serves as the underlying mechanism of orbital Hall magnetoresistance29. This mechanism, combined with the rectification associated with magnetization oscillations induced by lattice dynamics and magnetoelastic coupling, results in the generation of a DC voltage from the AC orbital currents produced by the acoustic orbital Hall effect. Here, the acoustic voltage observed in the Ti/Ni bilayer is approximately one-fourth of that in the Pt/Ni bilayer (see Fig. 6d). This difference aligns with the difference in the magnetoresistance observed in these structures: the spin Hall magnetoresistance ratio in the Pt/Ni structure is approximately40 0.05%, while the orbital Hall magnetoresistance ratio in the Ti/Ni structure is around29 0.01%. This consistency supports that the origin of the acoustic voltage in the Ti/Ni bilayer is closely related to the mechanism of the orbital Hall magnetoresistance.

A key difference between the acoustic voltage generation mechanisms of the acoustic spin Hall effect and the acoustic orbital Hall effect lies in the role of spin-orbit coupling in the FM layer. In voltage generation by the acoustic spin Hall effect, spin-orbit coupling in the FM layer plays a minor role, whereas it plays a central role in generating acoustic voltage through the acoustic orbital Hall effect. This difference arises from the fact that the interaction between orbital currents and magnetization is mediated by spin-orbit coupling in the FM layer41, while spin currents interact with magnetization through exchange coupling. In the case of the acoustic spin Hall effect, the absorption and reflection of spin currents at the NM/FM interface, associated with spin torque, depend on the magnetization direction even in the absence of spin-orbit coupling in the FM layer. Consequently, the charge current generated via the inverse electric spin Hall effect, arising from the reflected spin current, depends on the magnetization direction regardless of spin-orbit coupling in the FM layer. In contrast, for the acoustic orbital Hall effect, the charge current generated via the inverse electric orbital Hall effect, arising from the reflected orbital current, depends on the magnetization direction only when spin-orbit coupling in the FM layer is sufficiently strong to couple the orbital current and magnetization. Thus, the acoustic voltage induced by the acoustic orbital Hall effect is expected to be highly sensitive to the choice of FM layer, unlike that induced by the acoustic spin Hall effect.

To verify this interpretation, we investigate the ϕH dependence of Vxx for a Ti/CoFeB bilayer. CoFeB is known to exhibit strong magnetoelastic coupling, which is sufficient to induce an acoustic voltage through the acoustic spin Hall effect, as reported previously27. In fact, we observe a sizable acoustic voltage ΔV in a Pt/CoFeB bilayer, as shown in Fig. 6e. However, as shown in Fig. 6f, the acoustic voltage ΔV is vanishingly small in the Ti/CoFeB bilayer (see also Fig. 6g). These results contrast sharply with those observed in the Pt/Ni and Ti/Ni bilayers, where sizable acoustic voltages are present in both systems. A key difference between the Ni- and CoFeB-based structures lies in the role of orbital currents. Since the orbital response of CoFeB is negligible compared to that of Ni15, the acoustic voltage in the CoFeB-based structures is dominated by the acoustic spin Hall effect, whereas the acoustic orbital Hall effect plays a significant role in the Ni-based structures. In systems with weak orbital-magnetization interactions, such as the CoFeB-based structures, the magnetization-dependent absorption and reflection of the orbital current are ineffective, and the charge current arising from the inverse orbital Hall effect barely depends on the magnetization direction. This mechanism leads to the suppressed ΔV in the Ti/CoFeB bilayer compared to that observed in the Ti/Ni bilayer. These findings demonstrate that generating an acoustic voltage in the Ti/FM structure requires not only strong magnetoelastic coupling but also a robust orbital response. These results further support that the acoustic voltage observed in the Ti/Ni bilayer originates from the acoustic orbital Hall effect in the Ti layer, while the contribution from the acoustic spin Hall effect is negligible.

This distinction is reminiscent of the difference between the electric spin Hall and orbital Hall effects: the spin Hall conductivity can be substantial only in the presence of strong spin-orbit coupling, whereas the orbital Hall conductivity can be significant even in the absence of spin-orbit coupling8.

Two possible mechanisms may explain the acoustic orbital Hall effect: (1) the mechanical motion of the lattice directly generates an orbital current in the Ti layer, or (2) the lattice dynamics indirectly generates an orbital current in the Ti layer by inducing an electric field first that in turn drives the electric orbital Hall effect. A recent study has suggested that the acoustic spin Hall effect can be attributed to an in-plane electric field associated with a SAW-induced acousto-electric evanescent wave, which drives the electric spin Hall effect43. This implies that the SAW-induced acousto-electric evanescent wave and the electric orbital Hall effect may also play significant roles in the acoustic orbital Hall effect.

From the Ti-thickness dependence of the acoustic voltage shown in Fig. 5f, we obtain a characteristic length of λ = 2.5 nm for the acoustic voltage generation. This length scale is significantly shorter than that observed in orbital torque measurements17,18, but comparable to values from orbital pumping and orbital Hall magnetoresistance measurements in Ti/Ni bilayers29,30. A hint into the origin of the difference can be obtained from the problem of the transverse spin relaxation in a ferromagnet. When a spin current is injected into a ferromagnet, the spin component transverse to the magnetization precesses incoherently around the magnetization due to exchange splitting, leading to rapid decay over a short length scale, typically less than 1 nm, which is known as spin dephasing44. In contrast, spin currents intrinsically generated by the spin Hall effect in ferromagnets are not subject to spin dephasing (even though the spin polarization of the spin currents is transverse to the magnetization) and exhibit much longer transport lengths44–46. We suspect that a similar situation may be realized for the orbital relaxation in a nonmagnet: orbital angular momentum injected into a nonmagnet, where crystal field splitting plays a role similar to the exchange splitting in a ferromagnet, undergoes rapid decay over a short length scale, whereas orbital currents intrinsically generated by the orbital Hall effect in a nonmagnet are not subject to orbital dephasing and exhibit long transport lengths. According to this reasoning, orbital pumping and orbital Hall magnetoresistance measurements are characterized by the short length scale of orbital dephasing whereas orbital torque measurements are characterized by the long length scale of the intrinsically generated orbital current. This may account for the different length scales observed across the various phenomena, though further study is needed to verify our reasoning, which goes beyond the scope of our manuscript. Understanding the microscopic mechanisms behind the acoustic orbital Hall effect and its associated voltage generation remains a challenge for future studies.

In conclusion, we have demonstrated the acoustic generation of DC electric voltage in the Ni/Ti bilayers. The acoustic voltage varies systematically by changing the magnetic field angle and strength, as well as the SAW frequency and power. The characteristics of the observed voltage suggest that the lattice dynamics along the x direction generates a y-polarized orbital current \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${j}\,_{{{{\rm{L,0}}}}}^{y}$$\end{document}jL,0y flowing along the z direction: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${j}_{{{{\rm{L,0}}}}}^{y}=A\partial {u}_{x}/\partial t$$\end{document}jL,0y=A∂ux/∂t. This form of orbital current is reminiscent of the orbital Hall effect and the orbital Nernst effect, where an electric field and temperature gradient along the x direction generates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${j}_{{{{\rm{L,0}}}}}^{y}$$\end{document}jL,0y flowing along the z direction, respectively. While quantifying the efficiency of orbital current generation via the acoustic orbital Hall effect remains challenging at this stage—primarily due to the limited understanding of the orbital mixing conductance, a key parameter governing interfacial reflection (see Supplementary Note 2)—a systematic comparison of orbital current generation efficiencies driven by different mechanisms, such as lattice dynamics and electric fields, along with the optimization of device architecture, represents an important direction for future research.

The orthogonality among the flow direction, the orbital polarization direction, and the perturbation direction of the orbital current in the acoustic orbital Hall effect is distinct from the phenomenology of the orbital pumping driven by lattice dynamics, which was predicted recently47. In the latter, the generated orbital current jL,0 is proportional to u × ∂u/∂t, and the orbital polarization direction, defined by its vector direction, remains the same regardless of the flow direction of the orbital current. Therefore, our results on the acoustic orbital Hall effect provide important information for a deeper understanding of the interplay between orbital currents and lattice dynamics. This is anticipated to stimulate further experimental and theoretical studies, thereby paving the way for exploring acoustic orbitronics, where lattice dynamics are harnessed to drive orbital-current physics and develop orbital-based devices.

Methods

Device fabrication

The films used in this study are substrate/NM(tNM)/FM(tFM)/SiN(3 nm) structures (NM = Ti or Pt, FM = Ni or CoFeB), where the values in parentheses represent the thickness of each layer. For the orbital torque and pumping measurements, the Ti/Ni bilayers were deposited on SiO2 substrates by RF magnetron sputtering. For the ST-FMR measurement, the Ti/Ni bilayer was patterned into a rectangular stripe with a width of 10 μm and a length of 150 μm using photolithography and Ar milling. On the edges of the strip, Ti(3 nm)/Au(200 nm) electrodes were deposited. For the pumping measurement, the Ti/Ni film was patterned into a Hall cross structure with a width of 8 μm using photolithography and Ar milling. The Ti/Ni device was positioned beneath a CPW composed of Ti(3 nm)/Au(400 nm), with a SiO2(170 nm) insulating layer separating the device and the CPW.

For the acoustic voltage measurements, the NM/FM films were deposited on piezoelectric Y+128°-cut LiNbO3 substrates by RF magnetron sputtering. For the acoustic orbital pumping measurement, the films were patterned into Hall bar structures, whose length and width are 280 μm and 50 μm, respectively, and electrodes were fabricated by photolithography and lift-off techniques. Subsequently, IDTs consisting of Ti(3 nm)/Al(50 nm)/Ti(3 nm) were fabricated using photolithography and a lift-off method. The distance between the two IDTs is 300 μm, with each IDT featuring 20 pairs of single-type fingers. The dimensions of the fingers are characterized by the parameter a, and the corresponding SAW wavelength is around 4a. The overlap of the fingers, referred to as the SAW aperture La, is set to ~80 μm. The Hall bar made from the film is positioned at the midpoint between the two IDTs. For the measurement of the acoustic orbital Hall effect, the films were patterned into Hall bar structures with 450 μm in length and 400 μm in width. IDTs consisting of Ti(3 nm)/Au(25 nm) were fabricated by electron beam lithography or photolithography and a lift-off process. The distance between the two IDTs is 600 μm, with each IDT featuring 20 pairs of single-type fingers. La is set to 450 μm. The SAW resonance frequency fSAW was controlled by changing a. The input RF power P applied to the IDT was calibrated using a VNA.
