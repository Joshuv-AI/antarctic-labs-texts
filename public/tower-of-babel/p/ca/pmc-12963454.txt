Associations Between Pepsinogen Levels and Various Health Indicators in a Healthy Guangzhou Population
Authors: Shao‐Qiong Chang, Yu‐Qi Zheng, Wei‐Li Liu, Rui‐Fang Chen, Zheng‐Kun Hou, Pei‐Wu Li, Xing‐Hua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study investigates the determinants of pepsinogen (PG) levels in a healthy population from Guangzhou, China, aiming to refine its reference interval for better clinical assessment.MethodsWe analyzed data from 1341 healthy participants to explore the relationship between PG levels and potential influencing factors, including gender, Helicobacter pylori (HP), body weight (BW), body mass index (BMI), body fat rate (BFR), cholesterol (Total cholesterol [TC], triglycerides, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL‐C]), and glucose (GLU), using both correlation analysis and multivariate linear regression models.ResultsHP‐positive individuals exhibited higher TC and LDL‐C compared to those without infection (p < 0.05). A negative correlation was found between the PG ratio and BFR in HP‐positive participants (p < 0.01). Age positively correlated with PG I levels regardless of HP infection status (p < 0.05), although this correlation did not hold in postmenopausal women. In males, there was a negative correlation between BW, BMI, and BFR (p < 0.01); such correlations were absent in females. The PG ratio appeared largely independent of these variables. After multivariate adjustment, age, sex, obesity‐related indices, and HP infection remained independently associated with PG levels. The incidence of precancerous lesions was higher in males (13.73%) compared to females (6.54%; p < 0.05).ConclusionThe reference intervals for PG I and II in Guangzhou should account for the interactive effects of age, gender, obesity, and HP infection.Keywords: body fat rate, glycolipidmetabolism, Helicobacter pylori, obesity, pepsinogen

Introduction

The need for non‐invasive testing methods to enhance early gastric health risk assessment and public acceptance is increasingly recognized, particularly in populations at high risk for gastric cancer. Compared with invasive endoscopic screening, serum‐based biomarkers provide a practical and cost‐effective approach for large‐scale population screening. Pepsinogen (PG) assays represent a promising approach for the non‐invasive early screening of gastric cancer.

PG I and PG II not only indicate the density of glands and cells in the gastric mucosa but also serve as indirect markers of the secretory functions of various mucosal sections. Changes in these markers, such as increased PG II and decreased PG I/PG II ratio (PG‐Ratio), are indicative of gastric mucosal damage, including atrophic gastritis and intestinal metaplasia. Importantly, a lower PG‐Ratio is associated with an elevated risk of gastric cancer, with significant correlations noted at PG I levels below 70 ng/mL and PG‐Ratio below 3 [1, 2, 3]. Previous studies have demonstrated close associations between abnormal PG profiles and gastric precancerous lesions, supporting the clinical value of PG testing in gastric cancer risk stratification. However, serum PG levels are influenced by multiple host‐related factors. A large nationwide study involving 40,383 Chinese participants demonstrated that Helicobacter pylori (HP) infection, age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) significantly affect PG I levels [4]. In addition, regional variations in PG levels have been reported, potentially related to differences in ethnicity, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and smoking prevalence [5]. These findings suggest that uniform reference intervals for PG may not be universally applicable across different populations.

Despite extensive research, data focusing on healthy populations with strict exclusion of gastric diseases remain limited, particularly regarding the combined effects of age, sex, obesity‐related indices, metabolic factors, and HP infection on PG levels. Moreover, the extent to which these factors independently influence PG I, PG II, and PG‐Ratio has not been fully clarified. Therefore, the present study aims to comprehensively evaluate the associations between PG levels and various health indicators in a healthy population from Guangzhou, China, using both correlation and multivariate analyses, with the goal of providing a more scientifically grounded reference interval for clinical application.

Materials and Methods

Data Collection

Data for this study were retrieved from the physical examination database of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, following approval from the hospital's Ethics Committee (Approval No. K[2020]093).

Study Participants

The cohort comprised 1341 individuals who underwent routine health assessments at the mentioned facility between 2018 and 2019. Participants were selected based on specific inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Exclusion criteria:

History of drug or alcohol addiction.

History of drug or alcohol addiction.

Measured Indices

Demographic information: Age and gender.

Gastric cancer risk factors: Pepsinogen I (PG I), Pepsinogen II (PG II), and PG‐Ratio.

Obesity indicators: Body weight (BW), BMI, and body fat rate (BFR). Obesity was defined according to the Chinese criteria for adults, with a BMI ≥ 28.0 kg/m2. BFR was analyzed as an obesity‐related indicator reflecting body fat distribution and adiposity, rather than as a criterion for the definition of obesity.

Glycolipid metabolism markers: Total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C), and glucose (GLU). Body composition was assessed using a TBF 418B human body fat measuring instrument, which operates based on bioelectrical impedance technology.

Serum PG I and PG II levels were measured using a latex‐enhanced immunoturbidimetric assay with commercially available kits (specify manufacturer, city, country), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The PG‐Ratio was calculated as the ratio of PG I to PG II. Helicobacter pylori infection status was determined using the 13C‐urea breath test, and the delta over baseline (DOB) value was recorded. A DOB value above the manufacturer‐recommended cutoff was considered positive for H. pylori infection.

Follow‐Up and Definition of Precancerous Lesions

One year after the initial physical examination, participants who reported gastrointestinal symptoms underwent electronic gastroscopy. Gastric precancerous lesions were defined based on endoscopic and histopathological findings, including chronic atrophic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia, and dysplasia, according to established diagnostic criteria. All diagnoses were confirmed by histopathological examination following electronic gastroscopy.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0). The independent sample t‐test was employed to compare mean values between two distinct groups. Bivariate correlation analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationships between indices. Pearson's r correlation was used for normally distributed variables, while Spearman's correlation was applied for variables not following a normal distribution. A two‐tailed significance test was employed with significance levels set at p < 0.05 and p < 0.01. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as follows: 0 < r < 1 indicated a positive correlation, −1 < r < 0 indicated a negative correlation, and r = 0 indicated no correlation.

In addition, multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate independent associations between PG levels (PG I, PG II, and PG‐Ratio) and potential influencing factors. Separate models were constructed for the overall population and for participants with available Helicobacter pylori status. Age, sex, obesity‐related indices, and glycolipid metabolism parameters were simultaneously adjusted in the models. To avoid multicollinearity, closely related variables (BMI and BFR; TC and LDL‐C) were not entered into the same model simultaneously.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Group Differences

Gender Differences

Based on the independent sample t‐test of 1341 participants, significant differences were observed between males and females. Males exhibited higher values in BW, BMI, TC, TG, LDL‐C, GLU, PG I, and the PG‐Ratio, all with p < 0.05. Conversely, BFR and HDL‐C were lower in males compared to females, with significant differences (p < 0.05). Regardless of gender, their average BFR is higher than the normal standard. The male showed higher levels of PGI and PG‐Ratio compared to the female when their BFR was lower than female (Table 1). A similar trend was observed in the subgroup of 269 Helicobacter pylori ‐infected individuals (Table 2).

Premenopausal Versus Postmenopausal Differences

The independent sample t‐test showed that postmenopausal women had significantly higher BMI, BFR, TC, TG, LDL‐C, GLU, PG I, and PG II compared to premenopausal women (p < 0.05). Additionally, the PG‐Ratio was significantly lower in postmenopausal women (p < 0.05) (Table 1).

HP‐Positive Versus HP‐Negative Differences

In 561 participants tested with the 13C‐urea breath test, those positive for Helicobacter pylori showed higher levels of TC, LDL‐C, PG I, and PG II, and a lower PG‐Ratio than the negative group, with significant differences (p < 0.05). The positive male body fat percentage is lower than female, while PG Ratio is higher than female (p < 0.05) (Table 2).

Correlation Analysis of PG Levels

Correlation With Age

PG I was significantly positively correlated with age across genders and Helicobacter pylori infection status (0 < r < 1, p < 0.05). This correlation vanished in postmenopausal women. The relationship between PG II and age varied, showing a significant positive correlation in males (0 < r < 1, p < 0.05) but was indeterminate in females. PG‐Ratio was unaffected by age (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6).

Correlation With Obesity‐Related Indices

In males, PG I negatively correlated with BW, BMI, and BFR (−1 < r < 0, p < 0.01), while no correlation was found with PG‐Ratio. The relationship between PG II and obesity factors was uncertain (Tables 3, 6). In postmenopausal women, only PG I showed a negative correlation with BFR (−1 < r < 0, p < 0.05). No correlations were observed between PG levels and BW, BMI, or BFR in premenopausal and postmenopausal women otherwise (Tables 3, 4, 6).

Correlation With Glycolipid Metabolism

The correlations between PG levels (I, II, and Ratio) and glycolipid metabolism indicators (TC, TG, HDL‐C, LDL‐C, and GLU) were inconsistent across different groups defined by gender, menopausal status, and Helicobacter pylori infection (Tables 3, 4, 6).

Correlation With Helicobacter pylori Infection

There was no age difference between Helicobacter pylori ‐positive and ‐negative groups, but PG I and PG II levels were higher, and the PG‐Ratio was lower in the Helicobacter pylori ‐positive group (p < 0.05). The DOB value from the 13C‐urea breath test significantly negatively correlated with PG‐Ratio (−1 < r < 0, p < 0.05) (Table 5).

Multivariate Analysis of Factors Associated With PG Levels

Multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors independently associated with PG levels after adjustment for potential confounders (Tables 7 and 8). In the overall population, age was independently and positively associated with both PG I and PG II levels, whereas BMI was negatively associated with PG I and PG II. Male sex remained a strong independent predictor of higher PG I levels and a higher PG‐Ratio. In contrast, the PG‐Ratio showed no consistent independent association with age or obesity‐related indices after multivariable adjustment.

Follow‐Up and Precancerous Lesions

One year following the initial examination, 209 participants (102 males and 107 females) who reported gastrointestinal discomfort underwent electronic gastroscopy. Among them, 14 males (13.73%) and seven females (6.54%) were diagnosed with precancerous lesions, indicating a higher incidence in males (p < 0.01). Furthermore, five males (4.90%) and 13 females (12.15%) presented with ulcerative lesions, with a higher prevalence among females.

Discussion

Impact of Helicobacter pylori on PG Levels

Helicobacter pylori infection, a known carcinogenic factor for gastric cancer, affects levels of PG I, PG II, and the PG‐Ratio in normal individuals, similar to those with chronic gastric diseases [6]. Studies, including our own, have consistently shown that PG I and PG II are elevated while the PG‐Ratio is diminished during Helicobacter pylori infection in normal populations. Someone found that PG‐Ratio < 3 might be better for diagnosing the presence of gastritis [3]. This pattern has been confirmed by larger sample size studies [4]. Research indicates that the initial attachment of H. pylori to cells heavily relies on host cholesterol, suggesting that individuals with high blood lipids might be more susceptible to H. pylori infection, which in turn affects PG levels [7]. A multicenter randomized controlled trial revealed that post‐eradication of H. pylori led to recovery in gut microbiota diversity, increases in BW and BMI, reductions in TG and LDL‐C, and increases in HDL‐C, further supporting the interaction between blood lipids and PG levels in H. pylori infection [8].

PG and Age: Influence of Gender

Our findings demonstrate that PG I and PG II levels correlate positively with age in males, but this association becomes uncertain in females, particularly postmenopausal women, where PG I shows no correlation. This suggests potential hormonal influences, possibly estrogen, affecting PG levels differently across genders. Literature supports that individuals over 60 have a heightened risk of gastric intestinal metaplasia, with certain PG I levels suggesting a threshold for further diagnostic procedures like gastroscopy [9]. Detection of gastric intestinal metaplasia was higher in individuals with PG I levels > 127.20 ng/mL, which could be used as a threshold indicating the need to perform gastroscopy and histopathologic biopsy. In addition, PG C, which has been associated with gastric carcinogenesis, may also be influenced by estrogen‐related mechanisms. Other literature also found that there were differences in PG between male and female, suggesting that estrogen might be one of the hidden influencing factors [10, 11, 12, 13].

Although PG I and PG II levels increased with age in our cohort, the PG‐Ratio remained largely unaffected by aging, particularly after adjustment for Helicobacter pylori infection and metabolic factors. This finding differs from previous reports showing an age‐related decline in the PG‐Ratio, which were often based on older populations or individuals with a higher prevalence of gastric mucosal atrophy or Helicobacter pylori infection, conditions known to disproportionately elevate PG II levels [4, 9, 14]. In contrast, our study focused on a rigorously defined healthy population with strict exclusion of gastric diseases, which may attenuate age‐related alterations in the PG‐Ratio. These results suggest that the impact of aging on the PG‐Ratio may depend on population health status rather than age alone.

Notably, our finding that males exhibited higher PG I levels and PG‐Ratio than females differs from the large nationwide Chinese study by Yu et al., which reported a lower PG I/II ratio in males [4]. This discrepancy may reflect differences in population characteristics and analytical approaches. Yu et al. analyzed a multicenter asymptomatic population, whereas our study focused on a rigorously defined healthy cohort from Guangzhou with strict exclusion criteria and stratification by BFR and menopausal status. Given the significant negative association between PG I and body fat–related indices observed specifically in males, sex‐related differences in body fat distribution may partly account for the inconsistent results. Regional variations and Helicobacter pylori infection patterns may also contribute to these differences, underscoring the importance of considering population heterogeneity when interpreting sex differences in PG levels.

Obesity's Interaction With PG and Gender Differences

The relationship between obesity and PG levels is complex. In males, there is a clear negative correlation between PG I and obesity markers (BW, BMI, BFR), which is consistent with findings from Japanese studies showing a J‐shaped relationship between BMI and PG I [15]. However, in females, whether premenopausal or postmenopausal, no significant correlations were found. The presence of Helicobacter pylori infection accentuates the reduction in PG‐Ratio with increasing obesity, suggesting different metabolic or inflammatory responses based on gender and infection status. There was a positive correlation between obesity and gastric cancer of the cardia [16]. PGI and PGII reduced after bariatric surgery, and significantly influenced the ABC classification. The application of ABC classification for gastric cancer screening was limited after bariatric surgery [17].

Obesity and Pathways to Gastric Cancer

Obesity may influence gastric cancer development through various mechanisms. Increased intra‐abdominal pressure from obesity can lead to gastric acid reflux, which aggravates the gastric mucosa and potentially triggers carcinogenic pathways [18]. Additionally, obesity may facilitate H. pylori infection, known to be involved in gastric cancer. Further studies suggest obesity could promote gastric cancer metastasis through mechanisms involving lipid droplet accumulation and changes in metabolic hormone levels, necessitating further research into these pathways [19].

Uncertain Relationship Between PG and Glycolipid Metabolism

While the positive group for Helicobacter pylori showed higher cholesterol and LDL levels, no significant correlation was established between these factors and PG levels. Although some studies suggested a potential link between blood GLU levels and PG, inconsistencies across gender, menopausal status, and infection status leave this relationship uncertain. Consistently, multivariate linear regression analyses did not identify lipid parameters or GLU as independent determinants of PG levels after adjustment for potential confounders, further supporting the instability and context‐dependent nature of these associations.

Racial Variations in PG Levels

Studies highlight racial differences in PG levels, with significant disparities among Chinese, Malaysians, Indians in Singapore, and Caucasian populations in Western Siberia [20, 21]. And PG ratio was relatively stable. Scholars had once defined the reference range for Chinese people's PG but only considered gender and age factors, without taking into account factors such as blood lipids, BFR, and HP infection [14]. These findings suggest that the reference ranges for PG might need adjustment based on race, alongside other factors such as gender, age, HP infection, and blood lipid levels to enhance the predictive accuracy for gastric cancer.

Conclusion

In the Guangzhou, the reference interval for PG I and PG II needed to consider factors such as age, gender, and Helicobacter pylori infection. BW, BMI, and BFR had an impact on the PG I reference range of the male population. Except for Helicobacter pylori infection, the PG‐Ratio was largely independent of age and obesity‐related factors, whereas gender remained an independent influencing factor. Abnormal body fat might increase the risk of gastric cancer in males, especially middle‐aged and elderly males. This association was less evident among females.
