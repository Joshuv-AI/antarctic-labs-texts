A comprehensive landscape of mechanical stress–mediated lipid metabolism in human cavernous fibroblasts
Authors: Biao Liu, Yuzhuo Chen, Jiarong Xu, Zhitao Han, Zitaiyu Li, Junliang Qiu, Hongji Hu, Yanghua Xu, Yinghao Yin, Liangyu Zhao, Yingbo Dai, Ningjing Ou, Yuxin Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fibrotic diseases involve increased mechanical stress due to extracellular matrix deposition, significantly altering cellular metabolism. However, a systematic understanding of how mechanical force regulates lipid metabolism across different molecular layers remains limited. We employed an integrated multi-omics approach, including transcriptomics, Ribo-seq, proteomics, and lipidomics, to comprehensively assess the effects of mechanical stretch on lipid metabolism in human cavernous fibroblasts. Our analysis revealed that while mechanical force elicits complex multi-layered responses, post-translational regulation predominantly drives this lipid metabolic reprogramming. Lipidomics identified a significant reduction in fatty acids and an upregulation of ganglioside. Key genes central to this mechanosensitive metabolic shift were pinpointed. This study demonstrates that mechanical force hierarchically reprograms lipid metabolism. The shift from fatty acids to ganglioside underscores a key metabolic adaptation in fibroblasts. Targeting the identified critical genes may offer a promising therapeutic strategy for fibrosis-related diseases.

Introduction

Fibrotic diseases are characterized by excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, tissue stiffening, and structural remodeling, which collectively impair organ function1,2. A hallmark of fibrosis is the abnormal accumulation of collagen and other ECM components, resulting in a progressive increase in tissue mechanical stiffness3,4. Many studies have reported that increased mechanical stress can drive fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition5–7. In the context of diabetes mellitus-induced erectile dysfunction (DMED), fibrosis of the corpus cavernosum is a central pathological feature8. The condition is marked by ECM hardening, loss of elastic fibers, and a significant increase in the mechanical tension experienced by resident cells, which collectively contribute to impaired erectile function9.

The corpus cavernosum presents a unique and clinically relevant model for studying mechanobiology. It undergoes dynamic mechanical changes during daily physiological cycles of tumescence and detumescence10. This repetitive mechanical loading makes it an ideal system to investigate how alterations in the mechanical microenvironment influence cellular behavior. Within this tissue, corpus cavernosum fibroblasts are the most abundant cell type11. They play a pivotal role in maintaining tissue homeostasis by regulating the synthesis and turnover of the ECM. Under pathological conditions, such as those present in DMED, fibroblasts can undergo phenotypic transition into myofibroblasts, which exhibit enhanced contractility and secrete large amounts of ECM proteins. This fibroblast-to-myofibroblast conversion is a key driver of fibrosis and highlights the importance of understanding how fibroblasts respond to biomechanical cues12,13. Consequently, understanding the mechanisms governing fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition is critical for developing anti-fibrotic strategies.

Despite growing recognition that fibrosis drastically alters the mechanical landscape of tissues and that mechanical forces can profoundly influence cell signaling and function, research has predominantly focused on individual pathways. The link between fibrosis-induced mechanical forces and cellular metabolism remains insufficiently understood14,15. Lipid metabolism, in particular, plays a pivotal role in cellular homeostasis, energy supply, and membrane remodeling, and its dysregulation has been implicated in fibrosis across multiple organs16,17. However, little is known about how mechanical stress regulates lipid metabolic pathways in fibroblasts, or at which molecular levels such regulation occurs. Most existing studies have focused on transcriptional changes, leaving the roles of translational and post-translational regulation largely unexplored. This gap in knowledge limits our understanding of the fibrosis-mechanical stress-metabolism axis and its potential therapeutic implications.

In this study, we established an in vitro model by culturing corpus cavernosum fibroblasts on substrates of three defined stiffness levels (soft, moderately stiff, and stiff) to mimic the progressive increase in mechanical stress associated with fibrotic remodeling. To comprehensively delineate the effects of mechanical stretch on lipid metabolism, we integrated four complementary omics approaches: transcriptomics, translatomics (Ribo-seq), proteomics, and lipidomics. This multi-omics framework allowed us to examine lipid metabolic regulation at the transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels. By identifying key genes and pathways that govern lipid metabolic reprogramming in response to mechanical stress, our work provides novel insights into how biomechanical cues shape cellular lipid metabolism and function (Scheme 1). Results

Fibrotic remodeling in DMED increases tissue stiffness and alters fibroblast lipid metabolism

To establish the pathological model, we first assessed collagen deposition and tissue stiffness in the corpus cavernosum of DMED model rats compared to normal controls (NC). Masson’s trichrome staining revealed a significant increase in collagen fiber (blue) accumulation in the DMED group, indicating substantial ECM deposition and fibrosis (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in collagen content in DMED tissues (Fig. 1B). To further assess tissue biomechanical properties, nanoindentation assays were performed on frozen cavernosal sections. Primary fibroblasts were successfully isolated from human corpus cavernosum tissues and verified by immunostaining. The cells exhibited high expression of fibroblast marker LUM, while showing no detectable expression of the smooth muscle marker calponin or the endothelial marker CD31, confirming fibroblast identity (Fig. 1D).

To model the effects of mechanical stress, fibroblasts were cultured on substrates with three distinct stiffness levels: soft (SO), moderately stiff (MS), and stiff (ST). Morphological observations showed that fibroblast shape and organization changed markedly with increasing matrix stiffness (Fig. 1E). Lipid metabolic status was then evaluated by multiple staining approaches. Oil red O staining revealed a progressive reduction in lipid droplets in fibroblasts cultured on stiffer substrates (Fig. 1F). Consistently, Nile red and filipin staining demonstrated a decrease in intracellular lipid droplets and cholesterol content with increasing substrate stiffness (Fig. 1G). This pattern is consistent with previous reports that increased mechanical stress can suppress lipid storage18.

Together, these findings indicate that DMED induces fibrotic remodeling of the corpus cavernosum, leading to increased tissue stiffness, and that elevated mechanical stress reshapes fibroblast lipid metabolism, characterized by reduced lipid storage and cholesterol content.

Mechanical stress regulates fibroblast gene expression through multi-layered control

To systematically assess the regulatory layers through which mechanical stress modulates fibroblast function, corpus cavernosum fibroblasts were cultured on matrices of different stiffness and subjected to multi-omics analysis, including transcriptome, translatome, proteome, and lipidome profiling (Fig. 2A). While specific metabolite and gene expression trends were evaluated across all three conditions to capture the continuous nature of the cellular response, our global differential expression analyses and multi-omics integration focused exclusively on the maximal stiffness contrast (ST vs. SO). This strategy was employed to capture the full amplitude of mechanotransduction-driven regulatory remodeling. Using the ST and SO groups as representative comparisons, we first examined the relationships between transcription, translation, and protein expression. Scatter plot analysis revealed a strong correlation between transcriptomic and translatomic changes, with genes falling into distinct categories of regulation (Fig. 2B). Comparison between the transcriptome and proteome (Fig. 2C) and between the translatome and proteome (Fig. 2D) further demonstrated that regulation occurs at multiple layers, with varying degrees of concordance across datasets. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed on genes showing consistent directional change (homodirection) in mRNA expression, translation (ribosome-protected fragments, RPF), and protein abundance. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of homodirectional genes in DNA replication and the cell cycle. This finding is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that increased substrate stiffness mechanically promotes cell cycle entry and drives rapid cell proliferation19,20. The consistent enrichment of these functions across all three molecular layers indicates that substrate stiffness exerts a profound and coordinated effect on cell division, demonstrating the conservation of this regulatory response at transcriptional, translational, and post-translational levels (Fig. 2E). To evaluate the global concordance across different regulatory layers, we performed a pairwise correlation analysis of the expression changes (log₂FC) observed in the transcriptome, translatome, and proteome. Diagonal panels show the distribution of log₂FC values for each dataset. Off-diagonal scatter plots display the relationship between each pair of omics layers, with the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) indicated. The analysis reveals a stronger correlation between transcriptome and translatome (r = 0.52) than between either of them and the proteome (transcriptome-proteome: r = 0.26; translatome-proteome: r = 0.42), highlighting the role of post-transcriptional and post-translational regulation (Fig. 2F).

We observed that the transcriptome and proteome differ significantly, while the translatome and transcriptome are more similar. In order to investigate the molecular basis of this discordance, we analyzed genes exhibiting discordance between translatome and proteome levels, which were significantly enriched in lipid metabolism pathways (Fig. 2G). This suggests that lipid metabolic genes serve as key indicators of the post-translational divergence under mechanical stress.

Together, these findings demonstrate that mechanical stress regulates gene expression in corpus cavernosum fibroblasts through a multilayered mechanism, with substantial contributions from transcriptional, translational, and post-translational processes.

Mechanical stress reprograms fibroblast lipid metabolism in a stiffness-dependent manner

To gain functional insights into how increased substrate stiffness influences cellular processes, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) identified in the ST group compared to the SO group. Pathway enrichment analysis of the DEPs identified in the ST-versus-SO comparison revealed a significant enrichment of pathways related to lipid metabolism, confirming that mechanical force is a potent regulator of lipid homeostasis. Notably, pathways associated with ECM organization and myofibroblast activation were also prominently enriched, underscoring the link between the mechanical microenvironment and the fibrotic phenotype (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Data 1). We next conducted an untargeted lipidomic analysis across all three conditions (SO, MS, and ST) with six biological replicates per group. Principal component analysis (PCA) of the lipidomic profiles demonstrated clear and distinct separations among the three groups, indicating that substrate stiffness is a major driver of global lipidomic remodeling (Fig. 3B). To delineate the specific lipid alterations, we generated bubble plots visualizing the changes in major lipid subclasses. Comparison of the ST versus SO groups showed a pronounced reduction in neutral lipids and fatty acids, including triacylglycerols (TAGs) and free fatty acids (FFAs). In stark contrast, several species of glycosphingolipids were significantly upregulated (Fig. 3C). This pattern of changes was largely recapitulated in the ST versus MS comparison (Fig. 3D) and, to a lesser but evident extent, in the MS versus SO comparison (Fig. 3E). Stiffness-dependent remodeling of fatty acid metabolism revealed by integrated omics analysis

To further dissect the effects of mechanical stress on lipid metabolism, we focused on fatty acid and triglyceride pathways. A schematic overview illustrated the metabolic routes of fatty acids, including de novo synthesis, cellular uptake, activation, and β-oxidation (Fig. 4A). Quantitative lipidomic analysis demonstrated a stiffness-dependent reduction in fatty acid content, with the lowest levels observed in the ST group compared to MS and SO groups (Fig. 4B). We next examined gene expression patterns associated with specific fatty acid-related processes across transcriptome, translatome, and proteome datasets. For instance, de novo fatty acid synthesis genes exhibited divergent expression patterns across different omics layers: the trends observed in Ribo-seq and proteomics were consistent with each other but opposite to those in RNA-seq (Fig. 4C). This suggests that mechanical stress may regulate fatty acid synthesis at the translational level. Fatty acid uptake-related genes displayed a pattern that was more consistent with the lipidomic data, implicating reduced uptake as a major contributor to decreased intracellular fatty acids (Fig. 4D). Fatty acid activation (Fig. 4E) and β-oxidation genes (Fig. 4F) were downregulated at the protein level in the ST group, which theoretically should lead to fatty acid accumulation. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) enrichment analysis of ST versus SO groups across the three omics layers further reinforced these observations. In the transcriptome (Fig. 4G) and translatome (Fig. 4H), fatty acid metabolism pathways showed weak or no significant enrichment. By contrast, proteomic enrichment (Fig. 4I) displayed a clear and consistent enrichment pattern that matched the direction of lipidomic changes. Consequently, the proposed stiffness-dependent reprogramming of fatty acid synthesis, uptake, and β-oxidation is predominantly driven by alterations at the proteomic level, rather than direct transcriptional or translational regulation. Notably, none of the fatty acid-related gene sets displayed identical expression trends across all three omics datasets. This finding indicates that mechanical stress does not rely solely on transcriptional regulation but instead engages distinct mechanisms at different layers of gene expression. Although only matrix stiffness was varied, the regulation between each pair of groups may differ, reflecting the complexity of how mechanical forces influence gene expression. Thus, it is insufficient to evaluate regulatory mechanisms by considering trends across all three omics simultaneously. We did not present the expression levels of all genes involved in the metabolic pathways, as some genes were not detected at the protein level.

Mechanical stress alters free unsaturated fatty acid composition via desaturase activity

To further investigate the regulation of fatty acid subclasses, we analyzed free unsaturated fatty acids with different chain lengths. Relative abundance analysis revealed that C20–C22 unsaturated fatty acids progressively decreased with increasing matrix stiffness, whereas C24–C26 unsaturated fatty acids remained largely unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 1A). To explore the underlying regulatory mechanisms, we examined the expression of chain elongases and desaturases across transcriptome, translatome, and proteome datasets. ELOVL1 and ELOVL5, the major elongases responsible for fatty acid chain extension, showed minimal alterations at the protein level across stiffness conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C). In contrast, desaturases including SCD, FADS1, and FADS2 exhibited consistent downregulation at the protein level with increasing stiffness (Supplementary Fig. 1D–F). These results suggest that the reduced abundance of C20–C22 unsaturated fatty acids may be primarily attributed to decreased desaturase activity rather than impaired elongation.

To probe the underlying mechanisms, we analyzed the expression of glyceride metabolism-related gene sets. While the RNA-seq and Ribo-seq changes for key regulatory enzymes—such as DGAT1, PNPLA2, and MGLL—were modest or inconsistent across stiffness conditions, the strongest evidence for metabolic remodeling emerged from the proteomic and lipidomic shifts. DAG synthesis-related genes decreased with increasing stiffness (Fig. 5D), a trend that would be expected to lower DAG content. However, the actual DAG abundance did not fully match this pattern, possibly because DAG also functions as a second messenger in multiple signaling and metabolic pathways. For TAG metabolism, the expression of the key synthetic enzyme DGAT1 (Fig. 5E) did not account for the observed changes, whereas the lipase PNPLA2, a critical enzyme for TAG hydrolysis, showed increased expression under stiff conditions (Fig. 5F). This suggests that enhanced TAG degradation may drive the observed reduction in TAG levels. Similarly, MGLL, the major enzyme responsible for MAG hydrolysis, was also upregulated with increasing stiffness (Fig. 5G). However, despite this increased lipolytic activity, overall fatty acid levels still declined with stiffness. GSEA analysis of TAG metabolism in ST versus SO conditions revealed weak or inconsistent enrichment in transcriptome (Fig. 5H) and translatome (Fig. 5I) datasets, while proteomic enrichment (Fig. 5J) showed a clear enrichment pattern consistent with lipidomic results. These findings further support the notion that protein-level regulation more closely reflects actual metabolic outcomes.

Stiffness-driven remodeling of sphingolipid metabolism in fibroblasts

We next investigated sphingolipid metabolism under varying matrix stiffness (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Ceramide abundance was consistent with the expression of the de novo synthesis gene set at the protein level (Supplementary Fig. 2B, E), suggesting that ceramide content was mainly regulated via de novo biosynthesis. In contrast, ceramide phospholipid abundance displayed only minor variation among groups (Supplementary Fig. 2C), which corresponded to the relatively stable protein expression of enzymes mediating its interconversion with ceramide (Supplementary Fig. 2H, I). Sphingomyelin content, however, increased under stiff conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2D), which may be explained by the reduced protein expression of enzymes responsible for sphingomyelin degradation (Supplementary Fig. 2J, K).

Gene sets involved in ceramide conversion to other lipids or regeneration from other lipids (Supplementary Fig. 2F, G) did not match the actual ceramide abundance trend, indicating these routes contributed less to ceramide regulation. Notably, the elevation of sphingomyelin under stiff conditions is consistent with previous reports linking sphingomyelin accumulation to myofibroblast activation, suggesting that sphingomyelin may serve as a metabolic hallmark of fibroblast mechanotransduction21.

High matrix stiffness promotes ganglioside accumulation, potentially linked to fibrotic and senescent phenotypes

We next examined glycosphingolipid metabolism under varying matrix stiffness. The pathway schematic is shown in Fig. 6A. Quantification of glycosphingolipid subclasses revealed distinct trends: primary glycosphingolipids, such as glucosylceramide (GlcCer) and lactosylceramide (LacCer), decreased with increasing stiffness (Fig. 6B, C). In contrast, their downstream metabolites, the gangliosides Gb3, GD3, GM3, GM2, and GM1, all exhibited progressive increases under stiffer conditions (Fig. 6D–H). The concomitant reduction in primary glycosphingolipids (GlcCer, LacCer) and accumulation of downstream gangliosides prompted us to investigate its biological significance. To explore the potential biological implications of these findings, we considered the established roles of these lipids. Previous studies have reported that GM1 is highly expressed in fibroblast subtypes associated with fibrosis, where its upregulation actively drives the fibrotic phenotype rather than simply reflecting it. This suggests that mechanical stress may promote fibroblast fibrotic transformation through enhanced GM1 expression22,23. To investigate the effects of substrate stiffness on the chain length and saturation of GM1 and GD3, we performed a deeper analysis of our lipidomics data. Ganglioside accumulation may be attributed to impaired degradation

To further investigate how mechanical stress regulates glycosphingolipid metabolism, we examined the expression of key enzymes involved in glycosphingolipid synthesis and degradation. Line plots of representative genes (Fig. 7A–I) revealed that many synthesis-related genes showed no consistent trend or were undetectable at the protein level. In contrast, degradation-related genes exhibited clearer expression patterns, but specifically within the proteomic layer. Indeed, although quantitative lipidomics clearly demonstrated the increased accumulation of downstream gangliosides (GM1, GD3, and related species), the transcriptome and translatome changes for key degradation enzymes (such as GLB1, NEU1, HEXA/B, GLA, and CTSA) were subtle or inconsistent. Notably, several degradation enzymes, including GLB1, NEU1, HEXA/B, and GLA, were downregulated as stiffness increased but did not further decrease at the highest stiffness, which was inconsistent with the progressive accumulation of their corresponding lipid substrates. We propose two possible explanations: first, post-translational modifications might reduce the functional activity of these proteins; second, the downregulation of cofactors required for their enzymatic activity could contribute. Supporting the latter, we observed decreased expression of CTSA, a known activator of lysosomal glycosidases (Fig. 7H). GSEA further confirmed this interpretation, showing consistent downregulation of glycosphingolipid degradation pathways that was most pronounced in the proteome (Fig. 7J-L). Together, while the RNA-seq and Ribo-seq layers provide supportive context, the biological interpretation of a degradative bottleneck heavily relies on the definitive evidence provided by the proteomic and lipidomic alignments. Enhancing degradation efficiency may represent a potential strategy to mitigate fibroblast fibrotic and senescent phenotypes.

Regulation of phospholipid metabolism by matrix stiffness

To further investigate phospholipid metabolism, we examined their relative abundance across different stiffness conditions. Supplementary Fig. 5A presents an overview of phospholipid metabolic pathways. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5B–K, major membrane-forming phospholipids, such as PC, PE, and PS, exhibited no substantial changes, despite fibroblast morphological alterations under varying stiffness. In contrast, lysophospholipids displayed more pronounced changes, particularly LPI, which increased progressively with stiffness. Previous reports indicate that LPI accumulation is associated with liver fibrosis, often resulting from impaired MBOAT7 activity25,26. Consistently, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 7B, MBOAT7 protein expression initially decreased with increasing stiffness but did not continue to decline further at higher stiffness. This suggests that additional post-translational modifications may reduce its enzymatic activity, thereby contributing to LPI accumulation. Expression patterns of other key enzymes and genes in phospholipid metabolism across transcriptome, Ribo-seq, and proteome layers are shown in Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7. The analysis of distinct LPI species suggests that the impact of substrate stiffness is manifested at the overall class level, without specificity toward particular chain lengths or saturation states (Supplementary Fig. 8).

Discussion

Fibrotic diseases are characterized by ECM accumulation and tissue stiffening, which in turn impose enhanced mechanical stress on resident cells27. This biomechanical alteration profoundly reprograms cellular activities, including metabolism28,29. Using DMED as a model, our study systematically demonstrates the impact of matrix stiffness on fibroblasts, with a particular focus on lipid metabolism.

Our multi-omics strategy—combining transcriptome, translatome, proteome, and lipidome—revealed the complexity of mechanical regulation. Transcriptome data alone were insufficient to capture the full spectrum of regulation, while integration of ribosome profiling and proteomics provided deeper insights into translational and post-translational control. Lipidomics further uncovered marked metabolic rewiring under stiffness, with a consistent reduction of fatty acids and triglycerides, alongside an accumulation of gangliosides and lysophospholipids associated with fibrotic and senescent phenotypes.

Notably, gangliosides such as GM1 and GD3 emerged as stiffness-sensitive lipids with strong pathological relevance. GM1 has been reported to define fibroblast subtypes that drive fibrosis and to promote amyloid-β deposition in Alzheimer’s disease22,30,31. GD3 enables senescent cells to evade immune clearance by natural killer (NK) cells, while anti-GD3 therapy mitigates fibrosis and age-related disorders24. Aberrant accumulation of gangliosides and LPI may serve as metabolic hallmarks of fibroblast fibrotic or senescent states. Together, these findings position lipid metabolism as a key mechanistic link between mechanical stress and pathological remodeling.

Our findings reveal that mechanical stress triggers extensive lipid remodeling in cavernous fibroblasts. This aligns with emerging evidence that lipidomic reprogramming is a common adaptive response in fibroblasts under various stressors. For instance, Volt et al. identified 24 altered lipid species in fibroblasts following near-infrared (NIR) exposure32, while another study demonstrated that the spatial localization of dermal fibroblasts significantly reshapes their lipid composition22. Specifically, this work provides the first panoramic map of stiffness-induced lipid metabolic reprogramming in fibroblasts. While our findings were derived from DMED, the mechanistic principles linking lipid dysregulation to fibrosis may be generalizable across fibrotic diseases. Previous studies have implicated specific lipids in fibrotic processes in other tissues: GM1 is enriched in pro-fibrotic fibroblast subtypes and actively drives fibrotic phenotypes22; GD3 accumulates in senescent cells, a key feature of fibrotic niches24; and LPI accumulation, often due to impaired MBOAT7 activity, is associated with liver fibrosis25,26. Furthermore, in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, sphingolipids, such as SM(34:1), show strong spatial correlation with activated myofibroblasts21. Importantly, while these studies establish lipid remodeling as a hallmark of fibrosis, our work uniquely identifies substrate stiffness as a direct mechanical driver of this metabolic reprogramming in fibroblasts, providing a novel mechanistic link between tissue mechanics and lipid-mediated fibrotic progression. In addition to its mechanistic insights, a notable strength of this study lies in the construction of comprehensive lipid metabolic pathway diagrams, facilitating a clearer understanding of the complex regulatory network. By integrating multi-omics layers, we provide the first lipid-centric explanation for fibroblast reprogramming under mechanical stress.

This study has certain limitations. The proteomic analysis was restricted to relatively abundant proteins, leaving some key metabolic genes undetected. Moreover, our work primarily focused on constructing a multi-omics map of mechanical stress-induced lipid metabolic reprogramming without direct functional validation. Future studies will experimentally manipulate candidate genes or enzymes to confirm their roles in lipid metabolism and fibrosis. Another limitation lies in the limited utilization of transcriptomic and translatomic data in the present work. Our primary evidence for alterations in enzymatic levels is derived from proteomic data. Notably, we frequently observed discordant expression patterns across the molecular layers, where transcriptomic and translatomic changes often showed inconsistent trends with proteomic alterations. This observed disconnect between mRNA/RPF levels and corresponding protein abundance underscores the significant role of post-transcriptional and post-translational regulation in mediating the cellular response to mechanical stress. In future studies, these datasets will be leveraged in greater depth to elucidate the detailed regulatory mechanisms of key genes under mechanical stress.

In conclusion, this study establishes an integrative link among fibrosis, mechanical stress, and lipid metabolism, providing a mechanistic explanation for the accumulation of specific lipids under fibrotic conditions from a biomechanical perspective. The findings indicate that mechanical stress-induced lipid metabolic reprogramming extends beyond transcriptional and translational regulation, relying more heavily on proteomic modulation. These insights offer a new perspective for understanding lipid metabolic abnormalities in fibrotic diseases and highlight potential therapeutic targets for anti-fibrotic strategies aimed at the mechanical microenvironment.

Methods

Animal model construction for DMED

Specific pathogen-free (SPF) male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (10–12 weeks old, 300–350 g) were obtained from the Guangdong Provincial Medical Laboratory Animal Center. Rats were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with free access to food and water. To induce the DMED model, rats received a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 60 mg/kg). Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured 72 h later; rats with FBG > 16.7 mmol/L were considered diabetic. After 10 weeks, erectile function was assessed via apomorphine screening (100 μg/kg, s.c.). Rats with no erectile response were assigned to the DMED group. Rats were euthanized via CO2 inhalation (30–70% displacement rate) followed by cervical dislocation. Corpus cavernosum tissues were then collected for further analysis. All procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (approval no. 00496).

Masson trichrome staining

To evaluate collagen deposition and tissue fibrosis, Masson’s trichrome staining was performed on paraffin-embedded sections (3–8 μm thick) following standard protocols with minor modifications. Briefly, sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a series of graded ethanol concentrations to distilled water. The sections were first stained with Biebrich Scarlet-Acid Fuchsin solution for 5–10 min. After rinsing with a weak acid working solution (distilled water: weak acid solution = 2:1, v/v), the sections were treated with phosphomolybdic acid solution for 1–2 min to differentiate the staining. Subsequently, the sections were stained with Aniline Blue solution for 1–2 min to visualize collagen fibers. Following another rinse with the weak acid working solution, the sections were rapidly dehydrated in 95% and absolute ethanol, cleared in xylene, and finally mounted with neutral resin. Under this staining procedure, collagen fibers were stained blue, while muscle fibers and cytoplasm were stained red. The collagen volume fraction, expressed as the percentage of the blue-stained collagen area relative to the total tissue area, was quantified using ImageJ software.

Nanoindentation assay

The mechanical characteristics of the rat corpus cavernosum was determined using the nanoindentation system (Optics11 Life). The rat penile tissue were performed on 50-μm-thick transverse sections mounted on glass slides. A nanoindentation system (Optics11 Life) equipped with a spherical probe (radius = 27.5 μm) mounted on a cantilever with a calibrated spring constant of 0.52 N/m was employed. To map the mechanical heterogeneity, a grid of individual force-indentation curves was acquired across the tissue surface. The probe was approached at a constant velocity of 2 μm/s to a maximum depth of 3 μm. Load-indentation data were analyzed by applying the Hertzian contact model for a spherical indenter to derive the Young’s modulus (E) of the tissue. The contact point for each curve was determined algorithmically, and the model was fitted to the initial, linear-elastic segment of the approach curve. The indentation depth (δ) was calculated as the z-piezo displacement minus cantilever deflection. Curves showing unstable contact detection, excessive noise, or obvious drift/slippage were excluded. Young’s modulus was obtained by fitting the loading segment to the Hertz model F = (4/3)·(E/(1−ν²))·R(1/2)·δ(3/2), where F is the applied force, R is the probe radius, and ν is Poisson’s ratio.

Isolation and primary culture of human penile cavernous fibroblasts

Penile tissue samples were obtained and processed under sterile conditions. The corpus cavernosum was carefully dissected and immediately placed in ice-cold, sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The accompanying urethral corpus spongiosum was meticulously removed.

The cavernous tissue was then minced into approximately 2 mm³ fragments using sterile scissors. These tissue fragments were transferred to a 50 mL conical tube and digested with 5 mL of collagenase solution (0.2% collagenase type II in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)) for 60 min in a shaking water bath at 37 °C. Following the collagenase digestion, dispase was added to the mixture at a final concentration of 0.04 U/mL, and the digestion continued for an additional 30 min under the same conditions.

The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding 10 mL of DMEM containing 10% FBS. The resulting cell suspension was subsequently filtered through a 70 μm nylon cell strainer into a new 50 mL centrifugal tube to remove undigested tissue fragments. The filtered cell suspension was centrifuged at 1400 rpm for 5 min to pellet the cells.

Hydrogel fabrication

The preparation of polyacrylamide gels with varying stiffness was conducted as follows. Acrylamide and bis-acrylamide were dissolved in distilled water or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to achieve the desired final concentrations, with the specific ratios of these components determined by the target stiffness of the gel. Subsequently, ammonium persulfate (APS) and tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED) were added to the gel solution at volumes equivalent to 1/100 and 1/1000 of the total solution volume, respectively, to initiate polymerization. The solution was then vortexed thoroughly to ensure complete mixing.

The mixture was promptly transferred into a culture dish and allowed to polymerize completely. Following polymerization, any unreacted acrylamide was removed, and the gel was rinsed twice with PBS or distilled water, each rinse lasting for 5 min. To functionalize the gel surface for protein coating, approximately 500 μl of a Sulfosuccinimidyl 6-(4′-azido-2′-nitrophenylamino) hexanoate (sulfo-SANPAH) solution (0.2 mg/ml)—or a volume sufficient to cover the entire gel—was applied to the gel surface. The gel was then exposed to ultraviolet light for 10 min at a distance of approximately 90 cm from the light source.

After UV exposure, the gel was washed at least two to three times with 2 ml of 50 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid (HEPES) buffer to remove any excess sulfo-SANPAH. A solution containing collagen at a concentration of 0.10 mg/ml and either fibronectin or laminin at a concentration of 10 μg/ml, prepared in 50 mM HEPES, was then added to cover the gel. The gel was incubated with this protein solution overnight at 37 °C. Finally, the protein-coated gel was rinsed with 2 ml of distilled water or PBS, consistent with the solvent originally used for diluting the acrylamide monomers33–35. The protocol for fabricating hydrogels with tunable stiffness was adapted from Tse et al. Briefly, varying stiffness levels were achieved by modulating the ratio of acrylamide to bis-acrylamide during hydrogel polymerization. The specific formulations used to obtain the soft (SO), moderately stiff (MS), and stiff (ST) conditions are provided in Supplementary Data 2.

Primary corpus cavernosum fibroblasts were cultured on the surface of Matrigel-coated dishes with pre-adjusted stiffness. After 48 h, the samples were collected for subsequent experiments.

Oil red O staining

Oil red O staining was performed to detect intracellular lipid accumulation using a commercial kit (Solarbio, G1262, China). The oil red O staining procedure was performed as follows: after removal of culture medium and two PBS washes, cells were fixed with ORO fixative for 20–30 min, followed by two distilled water washes and a 20–30 s soak in 60% isopropanol. Subsequently, cells were stained with freshly prepared ORO Stain for 10–20 min, rinsed with 60% isopropanol until the background became clear, and washed repeatedly with water to remove residual dye. Nuclei were then counterstained with Mayer hematoxylin solution for 1–2 min, washed with distilled water, treated with oil red O buffer for 1 min, and finally covered with distilled water for microscopic observation. Lipid droplets were stained red, and nuclei were stained blue.

RNA-seq

Human corpus cavernosum fibroblasts were seeded onto hydrogel substrates of defined stiffness (soft, medium, and stiff) and cultured for 48 h. For each stiffness condition, cells were collected from three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Total RNA was extracted from each replicate, and RNA integrity was verified using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared independently for each biological replicate. mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification were performed according to standard protocols. The quality of the final libraries was assessed prior to sequencing.

To eliminate potential batch effects, all libraries were sequenced simultaneously on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform in a single run to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were subjected to rigorous quality control, including adapter trimming and the removal of low-quality bases/reads, to obtain clean data. For bioinformatics analysis, the clean reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) utilizing the Ensembl release 91 annotation file using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). The featureCounts tool (v1.5.0-p3) was used to generate the raw read count matrix. Only uniquely mapped reads were retained, and multimapping reads were discarded. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.38.3)36, utilizing the raw read counts as the direct input. Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values were calculated solely for the purpose of visualizing gene expression levels. Significantly differentially expressed genes were defined by an adjusted P-value (padj) < 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses for GO terms and KEGG pathways were conducted on these gene sets using the clusterProfiler R package (v4.6.2)37. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA; MSigDB version: msigdb.v2024.1.Hs.symbols) was also performed to identify significant shifts in pre-defined biological pathways. The statistical significance of enrichment was calculated using the default adaptive permutation algorithm implemented in the clusterProfiler package, and pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Ribo-seq

Primary corpus cavernosum fibroblasts were cultured on Matrigel-coated dishes with pre-adjusted stiffness for 48 h. For ribosome profiling, cells from each experimental group (n = 2 independent biological replicates) were treated with 100 μg/ml cycloheximide at 37 °C for 8 min to arrest elongating ribosomes prior to lysis and RNA extraction. Cells were then lysed on ice for 10 min using a lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl₂, 1 mM DTT, 100 μg/ml cycloheximide, and 1% Triton X-100. The lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. The absorbance of the supernatant at 260 nm was measured to standardize the ribosome content across groups. Lysates with equal A260 values were subjected to RNase 1 digestion (15 units per 1 OD of lysate) at 22 °C for 40 min with gentle mixing to generate RPFs. The digested fragments were purified using MicroSpin™ S-400 columns, followed by RNA extraction using the Zymo Research RNA Clean & Concentrator-25 kit. The purified RPFs were size-selected on a 15% polyacrylamide TBE-urea gel, and fragments between 25–34 nucleotides were excised and recovered using the ZR small-RNA™ PAGE Recovery Kit. The recovered RNA was treated with polynucleotide kinase (PNK) and used to construct sequencing libraries with the VAHTS™ Small RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina®. All Ribo-seq libraries were sequenced simultaneously on an Illumina platform in a single run to prevent potential batch effects.

For data processing, adapter sequences were removed from the raw sequencing data using the cutadapt software. Subsequently, based on the characteristic size of ribosome-protected fragments, only reads with lengths between 25 and 34 bp were retained for downstream analysis. These size-selected reads were first aligned to rRNA and tRNA sequences using Bowtie to strictly deplete ribosomal and transfer RNA contaminants. The remaining unaligned, clean reads were then mapped to the human reference genome (GRCh38) and transcriptome (Ensembl Version 91) using HISAT2 and Bowtie, respectively. Importantly, only uniquely mapped reads were retained; multimapping reads were discarded.

To ensure high data quality, the revised riboWaltz package was utilized to systematically evaluate read length distribution statistics, trinucleotide (3-nt) periodicity, and codon usage frequency. Furthermore, riboWaltz was used to determine and assign P-site offsets for each read. Specifically, the P-site offset for each read length was calculated by utilizing the massive accumulation of ribosomes at the start codon as a reference; the distance from the 5′ end of the reads to the start codon was measured, and the most frequent distance was assigned as the exact P-site offset for that specific read length. Open reading frame (ORF) identification was performed using Price software. Finally, read counts were calculated using featureCounts, with mapping rules inclusive of both the coding sequences (CDS) and untranslated regions (UTRs). Of note, translational differential expression was evaluated directly using the log2 fold-change (log2FC) differences calculated by DESeq2 from the Ribo-seq raw count matrix; a separate translation efficiency (TE) metric was not utilized in this study.

Proteomic

Cell lysates were prepared by homogenizing samples in RIPA buffer (Cat# EA0002, SparkJade) containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1% sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% SDS, followed by vigorous vortexing, followed by sonication on ice for 4 min (15% power, 2 s on/3 s off). After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and stored at –80 °C for subsequent analysis. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay.

Aliquots of proteins were mixed with 200 μl of 8 M urea in Nanosep Centrifugal Devices (PALL). The device was centrifuged at 12,000 × g at 20 °C for 20 min. All following centrifugation steps were performed applying the same conditions, allowing maximal concentration. Then, 200 μl of 8 M urea solution with 10 mM DTT were added, and the reduction reaction was kept for 2 h at 37 °C. The solution was removed by centrifugation, and 200 μL of 8 M urea solution with 50 mM iodoacetamide (IAA) was added. The sample was incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. The ultra-fraction tube was washed with 200 μl of 8 M urea three times and 200 μl of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate three times by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 20 min at room temperature. Then, 100 μl of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate containing 0.01 μg/μl trypsin was added to each filter tube. The tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. The filter tubes were washed twice with 100 μl of 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The flow-through fractions were collected and lyophilized.

The timsTOF Pro 2 was operated in DIA-PASEF mode with a scan range of 100-1700 m/z and 10 PASEF ramps. The TIMS settings were 100 ms ramp and accumulation time (100% duty cycle) and an ramp rate of 9.43 Hz; this resulted in 1.17 s of total cycle time. Linear precursor repetitions were set at a 15,000 target intensity with a 5000 intensity threshold. Isolation widths were set at 2 m/z at <700 m/z and 3 m/z at >800 m/z. The collision energy remained at default with a base of 1.61/K0[V s/cm2] set at 59 eV and 0.61/K0[V s/cm2] at 20 eV. Active exclusion was enabled with a 0.4 min release. TIMS ranges were set initially from one range of 0.6–1.6 1/K0[V s/cm2] as seen in most published studies.

The MS/MS data were analyzed for protein identification and quantification using DIA-NN 1.8.1. The local false discovery rate at PSM was 1.0% after searching against the Homo sapiens protein database with a maximum of two missed cleavages. The following settings were selected: oxidation (M), acetylation (Protein N-term) for variable modifications, as well as fixed carbamidomethylation of cysteine.

Lipidomic

Metabolite extraction

Briefly, 25 mg of cells were weighed and placed into a 2 mL reinforced centrifuge tube. Simultaneously, two small magnetic beads and two small steel beads were added, followed by the addition of 800 µL of pre-chilled precipitant (dichloromethane/methanol, 3:1, v/v). Subsequently, 10 µL of a prepared internal standard mixture was added to each sample. This internal standard mixture comprised 14 isotope-labeled or exogenous lipid species (including 1-pentadecanoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine-d7, cholesteryl oleate-d7, N-Oleyl sphingomyelin-d9, etc.). Importantly, this internal standard mixture was utilized exclusively for real-time instrument performance monitoring (e.g., assessing retention time stability and mass spectrometer signal robustness) and was not used for absolute quantification or peak area normalization. The mixture was ground for 5 min using a TissueLyser and subjected to ice-bath sonication for 10 min. The samples were then left to stand overnight at −20 °C.

Following incubation, the samples were centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. A volume of 600 µL of the supernatant was collected and lyophilized in a freeze dryer. The dried samples were reconstituted with 120 µL of lipid resuspension solution (isopropanol:acetonitrile:water = 2:1:1), vortexed for 10 min, and sonicated in an ice bath for another 10 min. After a final centrifugation at 25,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, 20 µL from each sample supernatant was pooled to create a quality control (QC) sample, and the remaining supernatants were transferred for subsequent instrumental analysis.

UPLC-MS analysis

This experiment utilized a Waters 2777C UPLC (Waters, USA) coupled with a Q Exactive HF high-resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for metabolite separation and detection.

Chromatographic conditions: the chromatographic column used was a CSH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 100 mm, Waters, USA). The mobile phase for positive ion mode consisted of 60% acetonitrile in water + 10 mM ammonium formate + 0.1% formic acid (Solvent A) and 90% isopropanol + 10% acetonitrile + 10 mM ammonium formate + 0.1% formic acid (Solvent B). For negative ion mode, the mobile phase consisted of 60% acetonitrile in water + 10 mM ammonium formate (solvent A) and 90% isopropanol + 10% acetonitrile + 10 mM ammonium formate (solvent B). The following gradient was used for elution: 0–2 min, 40–43% B; 2–2.1 min, 43–50% B; 2.1–7 min, 50–54% B; 7–7.1 min, 54–70% B; 7.1–13 min, 70–99% B; 13–13.1 min, 99–40% B; 13.1–15 min, 40% B. The flow rate was 0.4 mL/min, the column temperature was 55 °C, and the injection volume was 5 µL.

Mass spectrometry conditions: AQ Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was used for data-dependent acquisition (DDA) of MS1 and MS2 data. The MS scan m/z range was 200–2000. The MS1 resolution was set to 120,000, with an AGC target of 3e6 and a maximum injection time (IT) of 100 ms. The top 3 most intense precursor ions were selected for fragmentation (MS2 scan). The MS2 resolution was set to 30,000, with an AGC target of 1e5, a maximum injection time of 50 ms, and stepped normalized collision energies (NCE) of 15, 30, and 45 eV. Electrospray ionization (ESI) source parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 40; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10; spray voltage, 3.80 kV for positive mode and 3.20 kV for negative mode; capillary temperature, 320 °C; auxiliary gas heater temperature, 350 °C.

Bioinformatic analysis

Bioinformatic analysis primarily included: data preprocessing, data quality control, and overall analysis38.

According to the LipidSearch 4.1 software algorithms, lipid identifications are classified into four confidence grades (A, B, C, and D). The specific definitions are as follows:

Grade A: both lipid categories and all fatty acid chains can be completely identified.

Grade B: category-specific ions and fatty acid fragment ions can be detected.

Grade C: either class-specific ions or fatty acid fragment ions are detected (but not both).

Grade D: specific lipid structures, such as dehydrated ions, cannot be confidently recognized.

Data preprocessing

The results exported from LipidSearch software were imported into metaX for data preprocessing and subsequent analysis. As previously noted, peak areas were not normalized to the internal standard 2. Instead, to robustly correct for instrumental drift and systemic variations across the analytical run, data normalization and batch effect correction were performed based on the pooled QC samples. Data preprocessing included:

Removal of lipid molecules with missing values in more than 50% of QC samples and more than 80% of experimental samples.

Imputation of missing values using the K-nearest neighbor (KNN) algorithm.

Data normalization using the probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN) method to obtain relative peak areas39.

Batch effect correction using quality control-based robust LOESS signal correction (QC-RLSC)40.

Removal of lipid molecules with a coefficient of variation (CV) greater than 30% across all QC samples.

KNN algorithm: missing values are prevalent in mass spectrometry-based metabolomics datasets. Their presence can introduce significant bias in data mining results and reduce the efficiency of statistical analysis, necessitating imputation. The KNN algorithm first calculates the Euclidean distance between a metabolite containing missing values and all other metabolites. It then uses the weighted average peak intensity of the K variables with the smallest Euclidean distance to impute the missing data. This method provides different estimates for each missing value, making it closer to the real data.

Probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN): PQN is a sample normalization method that improves comparability between samples. It works by analyzing the ion intensity distribution of each sample to obtain a global reference vector. Correction factors between the actual samples and this reference vector are then calculated and applied to the actual samples for correction, enhancing sample comparability and achieving normalization.

QC-RLSC: quality control-based robust LOESS signal correction (QC-RLSC) is an effective data correction method in the field of metabolomics data analysis.

Data quality control

Principal component analysis (PCA) is a data dimensionality reduction method. It performs a linear transformation to reduce multiple variables to a new set of composite variables (principal components), from which the first few components that reflect as much of the original variable information as possible are selected. For PCA calculation, log2 transformation and Pareto scaling were applied. The PCA score plot reflects the true distribution of samples, primarily used to observe the separation trend between groups and identify potential outliers, while also indicating the degree of variation between and within groups from the raw data.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). Continuous data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Depending on the experimental design and data distribution, appropriate statistical tests were applied, including the unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test for comparisons between two groups, or one-way ANOVA for comparisons across multiple groups. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with specific significance levels denoted as P < 0.05, P < 0.01, and P < 0.001. All experiments were repeated in at least three independent replicates.
