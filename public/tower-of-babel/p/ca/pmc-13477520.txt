Prevalence and associated factors of smartphone addiction among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Shreesti Sharma, Rajan Bhusal, Sanjiv Prasad Khatiwada, Prakash Kumar Yadav, Bibek Banskota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmartphone addiction is an emerging public health concern, particularly among caregivers experiencing chronic stress. Caregivers of children with physical disabilities may be especially vulnerable, yet research on this topic in low-resource settings like Nepal is scarce. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of smartphone addiction and identify its sociodemographic, psychological, and social determinants among caregivers of children with physical disabilities.MethodsA quantitative, cross-sectional, descriptive study was conducted at the country’s only specialized pediatric rehabilitation center in Nepal, a national referral hub providing comprehensive care for children with physical disabilities. Consecutive sampling was used to recruit 227 primary caregivers. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years, primary responsibility for the child’s daily care, and ability to complete a structured questionnaire. Exclusion criteria included caregivers with severe cognitive impairments or acute illness preventing participation. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire incorporating the Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV). Descriptive statistics, Chi-square tests, t-tests, and multivariable logistic regression were used to examine associations between smartphone addiction and participant characteristics.ResultsIn this sample of 227 caregivers, 62.1% (n=141) screened above the SAS-SV risk threshold for problematic smartphone use. Significant determinants identified in multivariable logistic regression included female gender (AOR=5.83, 95% CI 1.58–21.45, p=0.008), unemployment (AOR=19.17, 95% CI 1.14–323.85, p=0.041), ethnicity (Brahmin/Chhetri: AOR=0.009, 95% CI 0.000–0.234, p=0.005; Janajati: AOR=0.015, 95% CI 0.001–0.420, p=0.014), chronic illness (AOR=0.009, 95% CI 0.000–0.514, p=0.022), frequent headaches (AOR=0.004, 95% CI 0.000–0.093, p<0.001), and occasional eye strain (AOR=0.081, 95% CI 0.009–0.748, p=0.027).ConclusionIn this sample, a high proportion of caregivers screened at risk for problematic smartphone use, with female gender and unemployment associated with higher adjusted odds. Given the cross-sectional design, these findings are preliminary and hypothesis-generating, and further longitudinal research is needed before screening or intervention strategies can be recommended.

Background

The rapid expansion of smartphone technology has revolutionized communication, information access, and social interaction worldwide. Yet, alongside these benefits, a behavioral pattern variously described as smartphone addiction or problematic smartphone use (PSU) has attracted growing attention. It is important to note that PSU is not currently classified as a formal disorder in either the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) or the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR); rather, it is widely recognized as a problematic behavioral pattern associated with adverse health and psychosocial outcomes. 1 Globally, about 6.3% of the population is estimated to be affected as of 2024, underscoring its significance as a pressing public health concern. 2

In high-income contexts, evidence suggests the scope of the problem is extensive. In the United States, 57% of people report being addicted to their phones, and 44% of adults feel anxious without them. 3 This pattern of use has been associated with compulsive checking behaviors and self-reported psychological distress when disconnected, although such features remain correlational rather than established clinical symptoms. Nearly 40% of all consumers, and 60% of those aged 18–34, acknowledge overuse, with daily phone unlocks averaging 150(1). The COVID-19 pandemic further intensified this pattern: 60% of users worldwide reported excessive usage during and after lockdowns, reflecting smartphones’ dual role as connection tools and drivers of unhealthy digital behaviors. 4

South Asia presents a particularly complex context for examining smartphone addiction within caregiving populations. The region has the world’s highest prevalence of neurodevelopmental delays and disabilities (NDDs), with 95% of children with autism spectrum disorder living there. 5 This creates intensive caregiving responsibilities, often shouldered by family members in the absence of formal support systems. Cultural norms commonly assign caregiving roles to mothers or grandparents, leading to high emotional and practical demands. 6 In this setting, smartphones may serve as coping mechanisms, sources of information, or avenues for social connection—but also expose caregivers to risks of overuse and addiction.

This study is framed within established models of stress and addictive behavior. Drawing on the stress–coping framework, smartphone use among caregivers can be understood as a coping response to chronic caregiving stress, in which devices provide emotional relief, information, and social connection. The behavioral-addiction framework further situates problematic smartphone use alongside other non-substance addictions characterized by compulsive engagement and impaired control, while self-regulation theory suggests that depleted regulatory resources under sustained caregiving demands may heighten vulnerability to overuse. Together, these perspectives provide a conceptual basis for examining the sociodemographic, psychological, and social determinants assessed in this study.

Smartphone penetration across South Asia has expanded rapidly due to affordability and broadening access to digital platforms. However, limited awareness of healthy digital practices increases vulnerability to addictive use. 7 The global smartphone user base is projected to be 7.2 billion by 2024. South Asia represents a substantial share but remains under-researched regarding PSU and its intersection with caregiving and mental health.

Nepal illustrates these challenges vividly. As a lower-middle-income country with an HDI of 0.602—the lowest in South Asia—and a population of 29.2 million, about 2.2% live with some form of disability. 8 Caregiving is primarily family-based due to scarce institutional services and limited health infrastructure. Studies show that caregivers of patients with mental disorders in Nepal frequently experience stress, depression, and anxiety, highlighting their psychological vulnerability. 9

Caregivers of children with autism in Kathmandu Valley report average burden scores of 41.49±12.25, within the moderate-to-severe range. Education level, anxiety, and depression are closely linked to this burden. Mothers account for 54% of caregivers, followed by grandparents (29%) and fathers (10%), reflecting the gendered nature of caregiving. 10 Such strain may increase reliance on smartphones for distraction, support, or escape. Yet, while smartphone penetration in Nepal is rising rapidly, digital literacy and guidelines for balanced use remain limited. 11

Despite this backdrop, a striking research gap persists: limited evidence is available on smartphone addiction among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal. Globally, caregiver burden in disability and smartphone addiction in general populations are well-documented. Still, their intersection—especially in low-resource settings—remains underexplored. 12 This is concerning, as caregivers already face chronic stress, social isolation, and emotional strain, all of which are known risk factors for addictive behaviors. Nepal’s cultural and socio-economic context may create distinct vulnerability profiles for PSU that need targeted exploration. 13

Existing Nepali research on smartphone addiction has largely focused on medical students, showing bidirectional relationships between PSU and psychiatric conditions, but has not extended to caregivers. 14 Similarly, research on caregiver burden has overlooked digital behavior. This study, therefore, provides one of the first examinations of smartphone addiction prevalence and its determinants among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal.

This study will establish baseline data to inform national digital health strategies, integrate smartphone addiction screening into mental health and disability services, and guide community interventions that promote healthy digital habits while addressing caregivers’ needs for information and connection. It will also support training healthcare providers to identify and manage PSU among caregivers. By focusing on digital well-being in this vulnerable population, the research aligns with Nepal’s public health priorities and the UN Sustainable Development Goals 3 (Good Health and Well-being) and 10 (Reduced Inequalities). Ultimately, the study aims to quantify the prevalence and determinants of smartphone addiction among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal, providing evidence for culturally appropriate interventions and informing understanding of digital health disparities in South Asia and similar low-resource settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study utilized a quantitative cross-sectional descriptive design to investigate the prevalence of smartphone addiction and its determinants among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal. The study was designed, conducted, and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies, and a completed STROBE checklist is provided as a supplementary file. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire that included the validated Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version (SAS-SV), alongside items capturing demographic, psychological, and social characteristics of caregivers. The study was conducted at the Hospital and Rehabilitation Centre for Disabled Children (HRDC), Kavre, Nepal, the country’s only specialized pediatric rehabilitation center functioning as a national referral hub. HRDC receives children with diverse physical disabilities from all seven provinces, encompassing varied socio-economic, ethnic, and geographical backgrounds. 15 This centralization allows the inclusion of caregivers who are actively engaged in intensive caregiving activities, including regular hospital visits for medical consultations, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and rehabilitation interventions. Conducting the study at HRDC is methodologically and logistically justified. As a single-center study, the hospital’s national catchment provides a diverse sample of caregivers exposed to a wide range of caregiving responsibilities and associated psychosocial stressors; nonetheless, the sample may not be fully representative of all caregivers in Nepal, and single-site recruitment may introduce selection bias. 16 Moreover, the centralized setting enables high-quality data collection, minimizes variability in exposure assessment, and enhances internal validity by ensuring uniformity in participant selection and data collection procedures. Therefore, HRDC provides a feasible and scientifically robust setting for examining caregiver smartphone addiction in the Nepali context.

Participants and sampling

The study targeted primary caregivers (parents, guardians, or immediate family members) of children aged 1–18 years with formally diagnosed physical disabilities in Nepal. Eligible caregivers provided at least four hours of daily care for at least six months, including assistance with daily activities, medical coordination, or supervision. This group was selected due to their potential vulnerability to excessive smartphone use as a coping mechanism, social connection tool, or resource for managing caregiving responsibilities. A consecutive sampling approach was used to recruit eligible caregivers of children with physical disabilities at the study site during the data collection. Daily rosters of inpatient and outpatient services were reviewed to identify potential participants, who were then assessed for eligibility. Based on Severin et al.'s study in Switzerland, reporting a 16.9% prevalence of smartphone addiction. 17 The sample size was 216 using n = Z2pq/d2 (p=0.169, q=0.831, Z=1.96, d=0.05). With a 5% non-response rate, there were 227 participants in total.

Inclusion criteria

Caregivers were eligible for inclusion if they (i) were primary caregivers — parents, guardians, or immediate family members — of children aged 1–18 years with a formally diagnosed physical disability; (ii) were directly involved in daily caregiving activities for at least four hours per day over a minimum of six months; (iii) were visiting the study site (HRDC, Kavre) for inpatient or outpatient services during the data collection period; (iv) owned and regularly used a smartphone; (v) were able to communicate in Nepali or another locally spoken language; and (vi) were willing to provide written or verbal informed consent.

Exclusion criteria

Caregivers were excluded if they (i) were unable to communicate in Nepali or another locally spoken language; (ii) did not own a smartphone or had not used one in the preceding six months; (iii) were not directly involved in the daily care of the child (e.g., distant relatives or occasional visitors); (iv) were caregivers of children whose physical disability had not been formally diagnosed by a qualified clinician.

Measures

Dependent variable

The primary outcome variable was smartphone addiction among caregivers, assessed using the Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV). 18 This validated 10-item self-report instrument evaluates the prevalence and severity of problematic smartphone use behaviors and addiction symptoms. Each item is scored on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree), with total scores ranging from 10 to 60. Higher scores indicate greater levels of smartphone addiction 18 The SAS-SV measures dimensions such as preoccupation with smartphone use, difficulty controlling usage, neglect of daily activities, and withdrawal symptoms. Based on established thresholds from prior studies, a cutoff score of 31 for males and 33 for females was used to identify smartphone addiction. 19 The scale’s reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.91) and validity make it suitable for assessing smartphone addiction in diverse populations, including caregivers under chronic stress. The English version of the SAS-SV was translated into Nepali using a standard forward–backward translation procedure: two independent bilingual translators produced forward translations, which were reconciled by the research team; a separate bilingual translator then back-translated the reconciled version into English, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus to preserve conceptual rather than literal equivalence. The Nepali version was pre-tested with 15 caregivers (not included in the final sample) to confirm clarity, cultural appropriateness, and comprehension across literacy levels; minor wording adjustments were made based on feedback. Internal consistency of the adapted Nepali SAS-SV in the present sample was excellent (Cronbach’s α = 0.927). Because no prior formal Nepali validation of the SAS-SV exists, findings using this adapted instrument should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Data were collected through structured face-to-face interviews to ensure comprehension and accurate responses, particularly considering varying literacy levels among participants.

Independent variables

The independent variables, collected via a structured survey from caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal, included age (years), monthly income (NPR), caregiving hours per day (hours), sleep hours per day (hours), screen time before bed (minutes), child age (years), gender (female, male), ethnicity (Brahmin/Chhetri, Dalit, Janajati, Madhesi, Muslim, Other), marital status (married, unmarried), education level (Bachelor & above, Secondary [9–12], Upper Basic [6–8], Lower Basic [1–5], No Education), residence (India, Metropolitan City, Sub-Metropolitan City, Municipality, Rural Municipality), family type (Joint, Nuclear), occupation (Agriculture, Business, Daily Wages Worker, Unemployed, Others), relationship to child (Mother, Father, Grandfather, Grandmother, Others), self-reported health (Good, Not Good), chronic illness (No, Yes), current smoking (No, Yes), current alcohol use (No, Yes), experience of headache from phone use (Frequent, Occasional, Infrequent/None), experience of eye strain using smartphone (Frequent, Occasional, Infrequent/None), social media use (Yes, No), and availability and affordability of internet services (Yes, No). These were gathered through face-to-face interviews using a standardized questionnaire.

Data collection

The primary data collection instruments for this study included a structured general questionnaire and an adapted version of the Smartphone Addiction Scale – Short Version (SAS-SV) developed by Kwon et al. (2013). 20 The internal reliability of the tools in this study was excellent, with Cronbach’s α = 0.927. The structured general questionnaire captured the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal. Data were collected from 1 March 2025 to 30 April 2025 by trained public health professionals. Variables included caregiver age, monthly income, daily caregiving hours, sleep duration, screen time before bed, and child-related factors such as age, gender, and ethnicity. Additional information collected encompassed marital status, education level, residence, family type, occupation, relationship to the child, self-reported health, presence of chronic illness, current smoking and alcohol use, experience of headaches or eye strain related to smartphone use, social media usage, and accessibility and affordability of internet services. The SAS-SV is a validated 10-item self-report questionnaire that assesses smartphone addiction severity across six core dimensions. In this study, the items were adapted to reflect the context of caregiving. Data was collected by a trained public health graduate using KoboToolbox to ensure secure and accurate entry. Each participant was assigned a unique identification code to maintain confidentiality and enable anonymous analysis. All data were encrypted and stored securely, with access restricted to authorized research team members, in accordance with rigorous ethical standards and data protection protocols.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 26. Descriptive statistics were computed for all variables, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables summarized using means (SD) and medians. Age and income are reported as median and interquartile range (IQR); the remaining continuous variables are reported as median and range (minimum–maximum). Associations between smartphone addiction risk and categorical variables were assessed using Chi-square tests. In contrast, differences in continuous variables were evaluated using independent samples t-tests (attached in a supplementary file). Variables with a p-value <0.20 in bivariate analyses were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. A multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent determinants of smartphone addiction among caregivers, and adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with high collinearity were removed from the final model to ensure the stability and reliability of estimates. Statistical significance was set at p <0.05. Given the moderate sample size (n = 227) and several categorical predictors with small cell counts, we recognised that some adjusted odds ratios would carry wide confidence intervals and limited precision. To examine model stability, we additionally inspected standard errors, ran the regression with and without variables showing sparse-cell patterns, and verified VIFs were below 5 for all retained predictors. Adjusted odds ratios with very wide 95% CIs are flagged in the Results and interpreted cautiously as indicative associations rather than precise effect estimates.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Baidya & Banskota Institutional Review Committee (B&B IRC) board (IRB No. B&BIRC-25-42). The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and national ethical guidelines. All participants were fully informed about the study objectives, their voluntary participation, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from literate participants, while verbal consent and thumbprints were collected from illiterate participants, with documentation verified by an independent witness. Confidentiality of all responses was strictly maintained throughout the study.

Discussion

The findings from this study reveal a high prevalence of smartphone addiction risk (62.1%) among caregivers of children with physical disabilities, highlighting a significant public health concern. The sociodemographic profile indicates that caregivers, predominantly female (58.6%), married (96.9%), and engaged in agriculture (45.8%), spend substantial time caregiving (mean 10.3 hours/day) and exhibit prolonged screen time before bed (mean 87.6 minutes). These characteristics and low educational attainment (32.6% no education) suggest a population with limited resources and high caregiving demands, potentially exacerbating vulnerability to smartphone overuse as a coping mechanism.

The current study represents one of the first investigations into smartphone addiction among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal, In this sample, 62.1% of participating caregivers screened above the SAS-SV risk threshold; given the moderate sample size, hospital-based recruitment, and absence of a prior Nepali validation study, this proportion should be interpreted as a preliminary, sample-specific estimate rather than a national prevalence figure for Nepal. This figure substantially exceeds prevalence rates reported in other Nepalese populations, such as medical students, where estimates range from 21.7% to 39.7%.7,21–23

In contrast, it aligns closely with findings from broader South Asian contexts, such as a 61.4% prevalence among young adults in Bangladesh., 24 highlighting a regional vulnerability to problematic smartphone use amid rapid digital penetration and socioeconomic stressors. Globally, smartphone addiction affects approximately 6.3% of the population., 25 but rates are markedly higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) and among vulnerable groups, often due to limited digital literacy and reliance on devices for social support and information. 26 In high-income settings like the United States or Europe, prevalence among general adults is lower (e.g., 40–60% admitting overuse but not necessarily addiction),27,28 potentially attributable to better access to mental health resources and awareness campaigns. However, literature on caregivers specifically remains scarce worldwide; existing studies focus more on children’s addiction influenced by parental habits rather than the caregivers’ own risks.29–31 In Nepal, where caregiver burden is pronounced due to inadequate disability support systems and cultural expectations of family-based care, 32 smartphones may serve as a double-edged sword—offering coping mechanisms like online support networks but exacerbating addiction in resource-constrained environments. This high prevalence in Nepalese disability caregivers contrasts with South Asian trends, where addiction is more studied in youth or urban professionals., 33 and underscores a health disparity amplified by Nepal’s low Human Development Index and rural-urban divides.

Multivariable logistic regression identified several significant determinants of smartphone addiction risk, revealing unique patterns in this caregiving population. Female caregivers exhibited substantially higher odds of addiction (adjusted odds ratio [AOR] = 5.83, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.58–21.45, p = 0.008) compared to males. This finding is consistent with global and South Asian literature, where females often show greater vulnerability due to higher engagement in social media and emotional coping via digital platforms.,34–36 as opposed to males’ more utilitarian or gaming-oriented use. 37 In Nepal and South Asia, gendered caregiving roles—predominantly shouldered by mothers (52% in this study)—may intensify this risk, as women balance intensive duties with smartphone-mediated social isolation relief, differing from Western contexts where gender differences are less pronounced in non-caregiving populations. 35

Unemployment was associated with markedly higher odds of being at risk of smartphone addiction (AOR = 19.17, 95% CI 1.14–323.85, p = 0.041); however, the extremely wide confidence interval reflects small cell counts in this subgroup and means that the magnitude of this association should be interpreted with considerable caution rather than as a precise effect size, aligning with evidence from LMICs indicating that unemployed individuals are 2.06 times more likely to develop addiction due to unstructured time and economic stress, leading to escapism through devices.38,39 In Nepal’s agricultural-dominant economy (45.8% of participants), unemployment exacerbates caregiver isolation, contrasting with South Asian urban studies where employment buffers addiction through routine, 33 and global patterns where socioeconomic status moderates risks more variably. 40

Ethnicity appeared associated with lower odds of being at risk, with Brahmin/Chhetri (AOR = 0.009, 95% CI 0.000–0.234, p = 0.005) and Janajati (AOR = 0.015, 95% CI 0.001–0.420, p = 0.014) groups showing lower odds compared to the reference (Other); these very small point estimates and wide confidence intervals indicate model instability driven by sparse cells, so the direction rather than the magnitude of this association is what we interpret here. This may reflect cultural variations in technology adoption; in Nepal’s diverse ethnic landscape, traditional values in these groups could promote communal support over digital reliance. Limited South Asian evidence on ethnic disparities often highlights urban-rural divides rather than ethnicity. 33 Globally, ethnic differences are understudied, but in multicultural settings like the U.S., minority groups sometimes exhibit higher risks due to digital divides., 28 contrasting Nepal’s context, where the majority ethnicities appear resilient.

Presence of chronic illness was associated with lower odds of being at risk in the adjusted model (AOR = 0.009, 95% CI 0.000–0.514, p = 0.022); again, the wide confidence interval limits the precision of this estimate, potentially because health limitations reduce overall smartphone engagement or prioritize offline coping. This contrasts with broader literature linking addiction to worsened mental health outcomes like depression and anxiety.,41,42 but less so with physical chronic conditions. In Nepal and South Asia, where chronic illness prevalence is high amid poor healthcare access, 20 this finding suggests adaptive behaviors among caregivers, differing from high-income countries where addiction often co-occurs with comorbidities. 12

Physical symptoms from smartphone use showed complex associations: frequent headaches (AOR = 0.004, 95% CI 0.000–0.093, p < 0.001) and occasional eye strain (AOR = 0.081, 95% CI 0.009–0.748, p = 0.027) were linked to lower addiction odds. While bivariate analyses indicated higher symptoms among addicted individuals, multivariable adjustment revealed protective effects, possibly reflecting symptom awareness that may prompt reduced use—a pattern consistent with self-regulation, although the cross-sectional design does not allow this to be tested directly,.43,44 In disability caregiving contexts, this may reflect heightened health vigilance, contrasting with youth-focused studies in Nepal, where symptoms exacerbate cycles of use. 22

These findings challenge assumptions from high-income literature, where youth and mental health are primary risks., 27 and emphasize Nepal-specific factors like ethnicity and caregiving burden. 45 In South Asia, similar high addiction rates underscore the need for regionally tailored interventions, such as digital literacy programs integrated into disability support services. 46 Globally, this highlights disparities in LMICs, where informal caregiving amplifies vulnerabilities absent in structured welfare systems. 25

Strengths of the study

This study adds to the limited evidence on smartphone addiction among caregivers of children with physical disabilities in Nepal. Using the validated Smartphone Addiction Scale-Short Version (SAS-SV) with high reliability (Cronbach’s α=0.927) ensures robust measurement of problematic smartphone use. The hospital-based setting at the Hospital and Rehabilitation Centre for Disabled Children provided access to a diverse sample of caregivers, though the findings should be generalized with caution. Comprehensive data collection, including sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral variables, allowed for a nuanced analysis of determinants. The application of multivariable logistic regression strengthened the identification of independent risk factors, adjusting for potential confounders. Ethical rigor, including IRB approval and informed consent, ensured participant safety and data integrity, aligning with international standards.

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, preventing the determination of whether smartphone addiction precedes or results from caregiving stress. The study’s single-center, hospital-based setting may not fully capture caregivers in remote areas with limited access to specialized services, potentially underestimating prevalence. Self-reported data via the SAS-SV may introduce recall or social desirability bias, particularly among caregivers with low literacy. Although statistically adequate, the sample size (n=227) may limit the precision of estimates for rare subgroups. Exclusion of non-smartphone-owning caregivers may overlook alternative digital behaviors. Participants were enrolled through consecutive, non-probability sampling at a single center, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. The absence of randomization and the single-center, non-probability design further limit the external validity of the findings. In addition, the moderate sample size relative to the number of candidate predictors yielded a low number of events per variable, raising the possibility of model overfitting; this is reflected in the wide confidence intervals for several adjusted estimates, which should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Finally, the study did not assess psychological factors like depression or anxiety, which could mediate smartphone addiction, limiting the depth of psychosocial insights. In addition, several adjusted odds ratios in the multivariable model carried very wide confidence intervals, reflecting small cell counts for some predictor categories and limiting the precision of those estimates; these associations should be read as directional rather than precise. The SAS-SV was adapted and translated into Nepali for this study but has not previously undergone formal psychometric validation in a Nepali population, so the prevalence figure reported here is best understood as a sample-specific estimate using an adapted instrument rather than a national prevalence rate.

Implications of the study

The high prevalence of problematic smartphone use (62.1%) observed in this sample points to a need for further research on smartphone use in Nepal’s caregiving population. Larger, multi-site, and longitudinal studies are required to confirm these associations and to clarify the relationship between caregiving burden and smartphone use, particularly among the female and unemployed caregivers who showed higher odds in this sample. Given the exploratory, cross-sectional nature of these findings, they are best regarded as hypothesis-generating and are not sufficient, on their own, to support specific screening, intervention, or policy recommendations at this stage.

Conclusion

In this sample of caregivers of children with physical disabilities attending a tertiary rehabilitation centre in Nepal, 62.1% screened above the SAS-SV risk threshold for problematic smartphone use, with female gender and unemployment associated with higher odds in adjusted analyses, and certain ethnicities and health conditions associated with lower adjusted odds. These findings underscore the intersection of caregiving burden and problematic smartphone use in a low-resource context, where smartphones serve as both coping tools and addiction risks. The results indicate a need for further research to better understand problematic smartphone use among caregivers in this setting. These findings contribute to the broader evidence base on digital behavior in underserved caregiving populations. Because this is a cross-sectional, single-site pilot study with a moderate sample size and an instrument that has not previously been formally validated in Nepali, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary, hypothesis-generating associations rather than causal effects or national prevalence estimates for Nepal. Future research should explore longitudinal trends and psychological mediators to inform comprehensive strategies.
