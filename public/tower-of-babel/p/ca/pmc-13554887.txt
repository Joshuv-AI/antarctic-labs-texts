Orthostatic hypotension (OH) is an underrecognized complication of continuous-flow preload-sensitive left ventricular assist device (LVAD) support driven by reduced pulsatility with baroreflex unloading and venous pooling. In LVAD cohorts, classical orthostatic symptoms are reported in ∼12%, while formal orthostatic testing identifies OH in up to ∼30% of patients.1,2 Physiologic studies demonstrate altered sympathetic control and reduced cardiac baroreceptor sensitivity during continuous-flow LVAD support, revealing a mechanistic link between nonpulsatile flow and orthostatic intolerance.3,4 Clinically, autonomic testing can clarify persistent postimplant symptoms once volume status and device parameters are optimized.4 The 2 key phenotypes that drive orthostatic intolerance and can drive low-flow alarms due to left-sided ventricular underfilling are the “autonomic dysfunction–predominant failure” and “right ventricular failure.” Routine clinical testing using traditional supine and/or resting approaches provides a static volume status estimation and often cannot distinguish the 2 phenotypes, commonly underdiagnosing the “autonomic dysfunction–predominant failure” phenotype. Posture-linked inferior vena cava (IVC) assessment has a diagnostic advantage and differs fundamentally from this conventional supine evaluation because postural changes unmask the 2 described phenotypes. Against this backdrop, we report a pragmatic, bedside echocardiography approach that uses posture-linked IVC size and collapsibility to help distinguish right ventricular (RV) failure (plethoric, poorly collapsible IVC) from autonomic dysfunction–predominant OH (small, highly collapsible IVC) in symptomatic patients with LVADs, illustrated by representative cases validated with right-heart catheterization.1, 2, 3, 4, 5 All data were derived from routine clinical care and presented in a de-identified form; ethical standards were upheld in accordance with institutional policy, and institutional review board approval was not required.

Case 1: Autonomic Dysfunction–Predominant OH/Preload-Reserve Limitation

The patient is a 67-year-old man supported by a HeartMate 3 LVAD since 2015 (11 years of support) for nonischemic cardiomyopathy (diagnosed in 2009). His medical history is notable for ventricular tachycardia/ventricular fibrillation (status post implantable cardioverter-defibrillator shock in 2025), paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, stage 3 chronic kidney disease, iron deficiency anemia, major depressive disorder, and obstructive sleep apnea. His concurrent medication regimen includes atorvastatin 80 mg daily, allopurinol 100 mg daily, mexiletine 150 mg twice daily, metoprolol succinate 50 mg twice daily, spironolactone 25 mg daily, torsemide 20 mg as needed, and warfarin 2 mg daily. He presented with disabling lightheadedness when upright. Orthostatic vitals were positive with ∼15-18 mm Hg mean arterial pressure (MAP) fall as measured by Doppler. Focused upright transthoracic echocardiogram showed a small, highly collapsible IVC (Figure 1) with a marked posture-linked decrease in left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD; ∼5.2 → 3.6 cm), consistent with underfilling rather than congestion. During follow-up hemodynamics (speed 5,800 rpm), right-heart catheterization showed: supine: MAP 102, right atrium (RA) 12 mm Hg, pulmonary artery (PA) 35/12 (20) mm Hg, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) 14 mm Hg, cardiac index (CI) 2.9 L/min/m2; standing: MAP 85, RA 8 mm Hg, PA 32/12 (19) mm Hg, PCWP 12 mm Hg, CI 2.6 L/min/m2. Symptoms improved with preload augmentation and autonomic support (compression, oral fluids, midodrine).1,5

Case 2: Right Ventricular Failure/Cardiac Origin of Orthostatic and Exercise Limitations

The patient is a 48-year-old woman, supported by a HeartMate 3 LVAD since 2015 (10 years of support) for nonischemic cardiomyopathy (diagnosed in 2014). Her medical history is notable for implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement, type 2 diabetes mellitus, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and pulmonary embolism. Her concurrent medication regimen includes midodrine 5 mg 3 times daily, spironolactone 25 mg daily, tirzepatide 0.5 mL weekly, torsemide 20 mg as needed, and warfarin 2 mg daily. She had MAP decline with tilt and upright exercise, accompanied by rising right-sided pressures and a fall in CI. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed a decompressed left ventricle at rest with severely dilated, moderately dysfunctional RV; with positional changes, LVEDD fell (4.1 → 2.9 cm) while the RV remained dilated, and the IVC was plethoric (Figure 1). Hemodynamics (speed 5,400 rpm) progressed from baseline: MAP 90, RA 9 mm Hg, PA 26/7 (17) mm Hg, PCWP 7 mm Hg, CI 2.4 L/min/m2, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) 1.9 WU; to tilt stage 1: MAP 74 mm Hg, RA 18 mm Hg, PCWP 13 mm Hg, CI 1.5 L/min/m2, PVR 2.5 WU; and to tilt stage 2: MAP 61 mm Hg, RA 17 mm Hg, PCWP 15 mm Hg, CI 1.5 L/min/m2, PVR 2.8 WU. Management emphasized RV-directed strategies (careful volume, pulmonary vasodilator consideration) with thoughtful speed/afterload tuning. Procedural Steps

After initial device and volume optimization, the proposed test is as follows. 1) Obtain a baseline supine subcostal long-axis IVC view ∼1-2 cm caudal to the hepatic vein confluence. 2) Record maximal IVC diameter at end-expiration during quiet respiration, and inspiratory collapsibility during spontaneous inspiration without Valsalva. When image quality permits, obtain M-mode measurements to improve reproducibility. Consistent with American Society of Echocardiography guidance, a dilated IVC (>2.1 cm) with reduced inspiratory collapse (<50%) suggests elevated RA pressure, whereas a small IVC with marked collapsibility favors low filling pressures; however, in patients with LVADs, posture-linked changes may be more informative than static measurements alone. 3) Repeat imaging within 3 to 5 minutes of sitting or standing (depending on patient's clinical stability) and simultaneously assess the LVEDD and qualitative RV size/function in paired views. Interpretation hinges on change-with-posture of the IVC diameter rather than any single static cutoff. A small, collapsible IVC with an LVEDD that falls supports autonomic dysfunction–predominant OH, while a plethoric, fixed IVC after positional change, with an LVEDD that similarly falls, indicates RV failure–predominant OH. A rapid IVC/LVEDD posture check can 1) triage autonomic dysfunction vs RV-predominant physiology at the bedside; 2) guide therapy (preload/vasoconstriction vs RV-directed strategies); and 3) identify those warranting autonomic testing or invasive hemodynamics when signals are discordant. In these cases, low upright RA/PCWP with preserved/low PVR (case 1) favored autonomic dysfunction-predominant OH, whereas tilt/exercise-induced rise in filling pressures, high exertional PVR, and depressed CI (Case 2) supported RV-limited forward flow. Potential Pitfalls

It is important to acknowledge the inherent limitations of IVC assessment in the LVAD population. Interpret IVC cautiously in obesity, elevated intraabdominal pressure, or positive-pressure ventilation. In patients with chronically RA pressures or long-standing right heart failure, structural remodeling and loss of venous compliance can result in a chronically dilated, “fixed” IVC. In such cases, the IVC may not exhibit normal collapsibility during orthostatic challenges, even if true filling pressures decrease. This is described as a “ceiling effect” given that once the IVC reaches maximal distension (typically >2.5 cm without respirophasic variation), it provides the same appearance whether the RA pressure is 15, 20, or 30 mm Hg6; therefore, a posture-linked change (or lack thereof) is more informative than a single measurement. Furthermore, variable loading conditions unique to continuous-flow physiology—such as dynamic changes in LVAD pump speed, concomitant tricuspid regurgitation, or extreme volume depletion—can independently confound standard IVC measurements. Consequently, posture-linked IVC changes must not be interpreted in isolation, but rather integrated with a holistic clinical assessment and other echocardiographic parameters. When feasible, pair with pump parameters (flow/pulsatility index) and LVEDD trajectories. Ultimately, these cases are illustrative and hypothesis-generating. Prospective validation of a standardized, posture-based echo protocol in LVAD troubleshooting is warranted.

Conclusions

Across both cases, posture-linked changes in IVC collapsibility and LVEDD, corroborated by invasive hemodynamics, reliably tracked the underlying physiology. In case 1, upright IVC collapse with LVEDD reduction and falling filling pressures identified a volume-dependent, autonomically mediated mechanism amenable to preload augmentation. In Case 2, a plethoric IVC with tilt-induced rises in right-sided pressures and a marked CI reduction identified RV-limited forward flow under gravitational stress, directing management toward RV-targeted strategies. In both cases, static IVC measurements alone were insufficient; it was the directional change with posture that distinguished the phenotypes. This bedside framework offers a rapid, physiology-based triage heuristic, potentially avoiding or better targeting invasive testing, and warrants prospective validation as a routine component of LVAD troubleshooting.
