Comprehensive in silico analysis of eggNOG-annotated orthologous genes infers functional dynamics and energy metabolism in the microbiome of Abutilon fruticosum
Authors: Abeer M Almutrafy, Abeer S Aloufi, Abeer Al‑Andal, Mohammed Y Refai, Manal Tashkandi, Alaa A Alnahari, Salwa S Bagabas, Fahd MF AlDowsari, Haneen W Abuauf, Fatimah M Alshehrei, Sahar A Alshareef, Aala A Abulfaraj, Reem Nabil Hassan, Rewaa S Jalal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbutilon fruticosum is an ecologically and pharmacologically important wild Malvaceae species whose rhizospheric microbiome remains poorly resolved at the level of orthologous-group (OG) genes. Shotgun metagenomic sequencing and eggNOG/COG-based annotation were used to compare rhizosphere and bulk-soil microbiomes, quantify OG repertoires, and infer in silico functional modules.ResultsPrincipal coordinate and Bray–Curtis analyses of COG categories revealed clear functional segregation between rhizosphere and bulk communities, with the rhizosphere enriched in high-abundance OGs linked to energy metabolism, nutrient transport, stress response, and secondary metabolism. Computational ranking identified a cohort of highly recurrent OGs, predominantly associated with Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria but also with Streptophyta, that dominate the predicted functional landscape and are markedly more abundant in silico in rhizospheric soil. Using eggNOG/COG assignments, ten interacting putative functional modules were delineated in silico, encompassing NADH–quinone oxidoreductase–centered bioenergetics, ABC-type nitrogen and sulfur acquisition, fatty-acid and propionate catabolism, sulfur scavenging and detoxification, cell-envelope and biofilm formation, multidrug efflux, DNA maintenance, environmental sensing and transcriptional regulation, specialized competition/protection, and mobile genetic elements. Conceptual, hypothesis-generating frameworks integrating selected modules posit that rhizosphere dominance could arise from the coordinated coupling of ATP/proton motive force (PMF) generation with high-affinity nutrient uptake, sulfur and carbonyl detoxification, iron-sequestering and antioxidant secondary metabolism, and stress-responsive multidrug efflux, based on our analyses.ConclusionsThese predictions suggest that specific OG cohorts act as keystone energetic, metabolic, and defense hubs in the A. fruticosum rhizosphere and provide testable hypotheses for future experimental work linking module-level functions to root colonization, stress tolerance, and plant performance. Introduction

Abutilon fruticosum is a xerophilous perennial Malvaceae herb native to arid and semi-arid regions and valued for its ecological resilience and ethnomedicinal uses, including production of diverse secondary metabolites with reported antimicrobial activity [1–8]. These traits imply a chemically distinctive rhizosphere, shaped by drought adaptation and bioactive root and leaf exudates, that may impose strong selection on associated soil microbial communities and favor specialized functional traits [4, 7, 9–11].

Recent studies indicate that the Abutilon fruticosum rhizosphere harbors denser and more diverse microbial communities than surrounding bulk soil, with a consistent shift toward Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, Chloroflexi, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes near the roots, while Cyanobacteria are comparatively more prominent in bulk soil [9, 12–15]. These rhizosphere–bulk contrasts have been attributed to root-driven gradients in exudates and microscale soil conditions, which are thought to structure both community composition and functional potential at the soil–root interface [9, 13–15]. Metagenomic analyses of A. fruticosum soils further suggest an enrichment of genes encoding Carbohydrate-Active Enzymes (CAZymes), particularly glycoside hydrolases, alongside diverse KEGG-annotated functions, pointing to an enhanced genomic capacity for carbohydrate transformation and energy metabolism in the rhizosphere compared with bulk soil [9, 13–15]. On the basis of these predictions and prior work, rhizosphere bacteria have been proposed to carry pathways linked to phytohormone biosynthesis, nutrient mobilization, and stress mitigation, although these putative contributions to plant growth and resilience remain to be verified experimentally [12]. In parallel, phageome surveys in A. fruticosum and other wild plants show that soil bacteriophages can carry bacterial CAZyme and antibiotic resistance genes—including soxR, oleC, parY mutant, arr-1, iri, and AAC [3]-Ic, identified by similarity to the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD)—highlighting a genomic potential for phage-mediated dispersal of such traits, even though actual transfer events and ecological impacts have not yet been demonstrated [9, 16–18].

Although A. fruticosum and other wild plant species have recently been substantially investigated for their rhizosphere-mediated plant–microbe interactions [9, 12–15, 18–20], there appears to remain a clear gap in the systematic application of the Clusters of Orthologous Genes (COG) framework to dissect the specific enzymatic components encoded by orthologous gene sets within the soil microbiome of this ecologically resilient species. The COG resource is widely recognized as a robust, evolution-based system for organizing orthologous gene sequences across a broad taxonomic spectrum encompassing both prokaryotic and eukaryotic organisms, thereby providing a structured context for comparative functional inference. Leveraging this framework enables a more integrative and multidimensional exploration of the molecular, biochemical, and functional features underpinning plant–microbiome associations, potentially clarifying key mechanisms through which rhizospheric microbial consortia promote plant vigor, stress resilience, and adaptive plasticity [19, 21]. Building on earlier integrative pipelines that employed KEGG, CAZy, and CARD, the present investigation is conceived to incorporate COG-based functional annotation in order to generate a more comprehensive depiction of the A. fruticosum rhizosphere microbiome, thereby helping to address existing gaps and to refine theoretical and applied understanding of microbially mediated processes important for sustainable agroecosystems and broader ecosystem stewardship [19, 21].

This investigation addresses the knowledge gap in the functional repertoire of the Abutilon fruticosum rhizospheric microbiome by using a COG- and eggNOG-based orthology framework to compare rhizosphere and bulk-soil communities, delineate their taxonomic structure, and pinpoint orthologous-group (OG) cohorts and functional categories enriched at the root–soil interface, with a focus on roles in bioenergetics, ATP/proton motive force (PMF) turnover, nutrient acquisition, stress responses, and interkingdom plant–microbe communication. Orthology-driven inferences are integrated with KEGG-, CAZy-, and CARD-derived annotations to generate an overview of metabolic plasticity and resistome features, while associating highly represented ortholog sets with specific microbial lineages to propose candidate keystone taxa that may shape rhizosphere ecosystem functioning. Together, these in silico and literature-based insights support a model in which a modular network of energetic, metabolic, regulatory, and defense functions underlies the rhizosphere’s contribution to plant performance and ecological sustainability around A. fruticosum.

Materials and methods

Metagenomic sampling, DNA extraction, and sequencing

In this study, soil samples for metagenomic analysis were systematically collected in biological triplicates from the rhizosphere zone surrounding native Abutilon fruticosum plants in the Mecca district, Saudi Arabia (21°54′33.6″N 39°16′48.4″E) [5], together with control bulk soil samples obtained at a defined distance of one meter from each plant. Cryogenic stabilization with liquid nitrogen was applied immediately after collection, and all samples were subsequently stored at − 20 °C to help preserve nucleic acid integrity. Genomic DNA was isolated using a cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol [22], followed by enzymatic removal of residual RNA with RNase A at 37 °C. DNA integrity was evaluated on 1% (w/v) agarose gels, and concentrations were determined and normalized using a Qubit® dsDNA Assay Kit (Life Technologies, Catalog No. Q32851) prior to downstream processing. Standardized DNA aliquots from the six samples were submitted to Novogene Co., Ltd. for next-generation sequencing and in silico bioinformatic analysis. Genomic DNA was mechanically sheared with a Covaris sonicator to generate ~ 350 bp fragments, which then underwent end repair, A-tailing, and adaptor ligation compatible with amplification using dual-indexed primers (i7/i5, E7600); libraries were prepared with the Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Quality control steps included stringent filtering to remove reads with low base quality scores (Q ≤ 38), reads containing more than 10 ambiguous bases (N), and reads exhibiting adapter overlap greater than 15 bp. The resulting high-quality reads were deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA, https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena) under study accession PRJEB62870, with rhizosphere samples R1–R3 corresponding to SAMEA113585679, SAMEA113585680, and SAMEA113585681, and bulk soil samples S1–S3 to SAMEA113585682, SAMEA113585683, and SAMEA113585684, respectively. De novo assembly was performed using MEGAHIT with a k-mer size of 55, after removal of putative chimeric sequences as described in previous methodological studies [23–25]. A secondary assembly of unaligned reads produced NOVO_MIX scaffolds, which, together with predicted open reading frames (ORFs), were then aligned using Soap 2.21 [23, 26].

Gene prediction, functional annotation, and taxonomic assignment

Gene prediction was performed using MetaGeneMark to identify potential coding regions within assembled metagenomic contigs derived from soil microbiome samples. Following initial gene calling, redundancy was reduced by applying CD-HIT clustering algorithms, which dereplicate similar sequences and generate clusters of non-redundant genes. This process yielded comprehensive non-redundant gene catalogues (nrGC), constructed through an iterative, greedy pairwise comparison to ensure the uniqueness and integrity of each representative gene sequence [26–29]. Gene annotation utilized MEGAN, with quantification of functional abundance carried out against the eggNOG database (v4.0) [30–33]. Clustering analyses and Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) were performed [34–36] as part of the quality validation of the dataset. Amino acid sequences from annotated genes were mapped to the eggNOG database using Diamond and eggNOG-mapper (v2.0.1) [33, 37], with subsequent categorization in Gene Ontology, KEGG, and COG databases for enzyme characterization and orthologous gene clustering [38–40]. Identified OGs were screened for antibiotic resistance by high-stringency Blastp searches against CARD (e-value ≤ 1e-5) [41], and antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) were further classified into AMR families for resistome profiling [42]. For taxonomic resolution, HMMER-based identification of single-copy marker genes from the Genome Taxonomy Database provided robust taxonomic assignment of metagenomic sequences, ensuring reliable microbial community characterization [43].

Statistical analysis of orthologous group abundances

Functional orthologous groups (OGs) were quantified from the bulk (S) and rhizospheric (R) metagenomes as normalized gene query counts per COG/ENOG identifier. For each OG, we calculated the median normalized abundance and interquartile range across replicate bulk and rhizosphere samples separately. Differential representation of OGs between S and R was assessed using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test applied to normalized abundances, and the resulting p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate (FDR) procedure [44–48]. OGs were considered significantly enriched in the rhizosphere if they met two criteria: (i) a prevalence threshold of at least 1000 total gene queries across all samples, and (ii) an FDR-adjusted p value < 0.05. These high-prevalence, FDR-significant OGs were used as the final cohort of rhizosphere-enriched functions for downstream interpretation.

Results

Overview of assembly and alignment statistics

The data summarized in Table S1 furnish a detailed overview of orthologous gene queries following their alignment to homologous subject sequences in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) repository. The assembly pipeline for OGs and gene fragments yielded on the order of 3.5 × 10⁵ discrete open reading frames (ORFs) and 7.0 × 10⁵ scaftigs (NOVO_MIX), underscoring the depth and complexity of the metagenomic dataset. These queries map to subject sequences spanning 27–4255 nucleotides, with pairwise identity values distributed between 50% and 100%. Observed mismatches range from 0 up to roughly 1370 nucleotides, whereas gap lengths in both query and subject alignments are strikingly limited, varying only between 0 and 59 nucleotides. Taken together, the large number of assembled sequences, the broad yet well-resolved length distribution, the high sequence identity thresholds, and the minimal gap incidence attest to the high fidelity of the assembly and alignment procedures and provide a rigorous quantitative foundation for all downstream annotation, comparative, and functional analyses.

The robustness of the eggNOG-based annotations was further supported by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), applied to resolve multivariate relationships among samples from the two soil compartments surrounding Abutilon fruticosum (Fig. 1). At the level of standard functional categories, bulk-soil microbiomes consistently segregated along the positive axis of the first principal coordinate (PC1), whereas rhizospheric communities clustered along the negative PC1 axis (Fig. 1a). In contrast, when the analysis was performed at the individual orthologous-group (OG) level, the ordination pattern was inverted (Fig. 1b). This reproducible bidirectional separation across hierarchical functional resolutions, albeit derived solely from computational profiling, is consistent with divergence in community structure and functional potential between bulk and rhizosphere soils. Figure S1 corroborates the principal coordinate patterns by independently resolving rhizosphere and bulk samples into distinct Bray–Curtis clusters on the basis of COG functional categories A–Z. In this dendrogram, all rhizospheric replicates (R1–R3) consistently group together and away from bulk-soil replicates (S1–S3), reinforcing the functional enrichment trends highlighted in Figure S1 and supporting the view that the observed compartment-specific COG profiles represent reproducible differences in overall community functional structure.

Multivariate validation of functional annotations

Assembled gene queries derived from individual and composite microbiome libraries were aligned to homologous sequences in the NCBI repository and subsequently assigned to Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COGs) and eggNOG orthologous groups (ENOGs), as summarized in Table S2. Each query–subject alignment was linked to a specific COG identifier, denoting a protein cluster inferred to be orthologous across multiple genomes and mapped to a level-1 functional category, and, where applicable, to an ENOG identifier reflecting broader eggNOG orthology relationships. This dual annotation framework established the evolutionary and functional landscape of gene repertoires in rhizospheric and bulk soils and underpinned all subsequent comparative assessments of OG composition between the two compartments (Table S3). The computational robustness of these eggNOG-based assignments is reinforced by the patterns shown in Figure S2, where functional COG categories C, E, G, L, P, and S exhibit pronounced enrichment in A. fruticosum soils. Consistent with this pattern, profiles of absolute and relative abundances for functional orthologous-gene categories (Figures S3, S4; Table S4) reveal a pronounced separation between rhizospheric and bulk-soil microbiomes, indicating substantial divergence in predicted functional potential, except for COG categories W, Y, and Z, which display an inverse trend relative to the other functional classes.

Rhizosphere-enriched high-abundance orthologs

Figure S5, together with the supporting datasets in Tables S5 and S6, demonstrates that in silico ranking of orthologous-group (OG) gene queries across the composite A. fruticosum rhizosphere and bulk-soil metagenomes delineates a discrete cohort of ≥ 1000 highly recurrent orthologs that dominate the predicted functional gene repertoire of these microbial consortia. Within this high-abundance tier, a substantial fraction of OGs exhibits markedly elevated representation in rhizospheric communities [33], visualized as green-shaded bars, thereby defining a suite of functions that are computationally inferred to be more abundant at the root–soil interface than in adjacent bulk soil.

As a second layer of selection, Table S7 provides a false-discovery-rate (FDR)–adjusted comparison of OG abundances between bulk (S) and rhizospheric (R) microbiomes, reporting median normalized abundance, interquartile ranges, Wilcoxon rank-sum p values, and corresponding FDR-adjusted significance scores for each COG/ENOG entry. Within this framework, the 33 OGs that are highlighted as recurrent across both soil types (≥ 1000 gene queries) are specifically flagged in red to denote those functions that remain significantly enriched in the rhizosphere after multiple-testing correction (Wilcoxon, FDR < 0.05, Table S7), ensuring that the final OG cohort consists of genes that are not only numerically dominant but also statistically overrepresented at the root–soil interface relative to surrounding bulk soil, and thus may represent candidate functions that are preferentially enriched in the rhizosphere.

The results in Table S8 present an exhaustive inventory of these selected OG genes that are markedly more abundant in silico in the A. fruticosum rhizosphere relative to adjacent bulk soil as defined by the median/IQR and Wilcoxon–FDR criteria (see Table S7), all belonging to the highest abundance tier with more than 1000 query hits each. The listed OGs span diverse COG categories, including energy metabolism (e.g., NuoN; category C), high-affinity amino acid and peptide ABC transporters (LivG, LivF, PotA, AppF; category E), multidrug and metabolite efflux systems (ENOG410XNN3, NorM; categories G and V), sulfur scavenging and detoxification (SsuD, PspE; categories HR and P), fatty-acid and propionate catabolism (MaoC, Sbm1, MmdA; category I), transcriptional regulation and stress-responsive control (ArsR, SoxR, RpoC, CitB; category K/KT), DNA repair and cell division (UvrD, FtsI; categories L and M), cell-wall and exopolysaccharide glycosyltransferases (WcaA, BcsA; categories M and N), carotenoid-linked oxidative protection (phytoene dehydrogenase; category Q), glyoxalase-mediated detoxification (GloB; category R), mobile genetic elements and retrotransposon-associated proteins (multiple S and X category OGs), signal-transduction histidine kinases and serine/threonine kinases (Sps1, ComP and related; category T), type III secretion–associated effectors (YopO, YopM; categories T and U), cytochrome P450–dependent functions (CypX; category VQ), and additional retroelement-like or uncharacterized transposase-related OGs (e.g., InsO; category X). Collectively, these highly recurrent, rhizosphere-enriched orthologs delineate a functionally complex repertoire centered on energy generation, nutrient uptake, redox and xenobiotic stress mitigation, regulatory plasticity, cell-envelope remodeling, and mobile-element dynamics that are preferentially enriched at the root–soil interface in our in silico data. Any specific contributions to energy generation, nutrient uptake, stress responses, mobile-element dynamics, or rhizosphere colonization should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating and remain to be tested experimentally.

Integrated orthologous-group functional frameworks

The selected orthologous-group (OG) genes were then organized into ten interacting functional modules, several of which were further integrated into three higher-order frameworks that are schematically depicted in (Figs. 5, 6, and 7). In Fig. 5, these in silico–selected OG genes are assembled into an energetically centered framework in which four modules—NADH–quinone oxidoreductase–driven respiratory bioenergetics (Module 1), ABC-type nitrogen nutrient acquisition (Module 2), fatty-acid and B12-dependent propionate catabolism (Module 3), and sulfur scavenging plus detoxification of organosulfur and cyanide-like compounds (Module 4)—are conceptually depicted as a putative network that could channel root-derived substrates into PMF generation, ATP production, and coordinated carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur fluxes at the soil–root interface. Within this schematic, Module 1 is hypothesized to function as the central NADH–ubiquinone oxidoreductase complex establishing the PMF that drives ATP synthase, Module 2 comprises the ATP-powered transporters LivG, LivF, PotA, and AppF for high-affinity uptake of branched-chain amino acids, polyamines, and oligopeptides, Module 3 integrates MaoC, Sbm1, and MmdA to route fatty acids and propionate into acetyl-CoA and succinyl-CoA for the TCA cycle, and Module 4 links SsuD-mediated use of alkanesulfonates with PspE-mediated sulfur detoxification, collectively modeling a self-reinforcing circuit in which energy conservation, nutrient acquisition, and sulfur management are reciprocally integrated in silico to sustain rhizosphere colonization.

Figure 6 depicts a framework in which three of the ten OG–based modules—Module 9 (specialized competition and protection), Module 2 (nutrient acquisition and biosynthesis), and Module 8 (environmental sensing and transcriptional regulation)—are integrated into a single, interdependent regulatory and metabolic circuit relevant to rhizosphere adaptation around A. fruticosum roots. In this scheme, Module 2 is proposed to supply the biosynthetic substrates (amino acids, iron, precursors for isoprenoids and pigments) required for the production of Module 9 effectors such as CypX-linked pulcherriminic acid, carotenoid-like antioxidants (COG1233), and GloB-mediated glyoxalase detoxification, while Module 8 regulators (e.g., SoxR, ArsR and associated two-component systems) are inferred to sense redox and metal stress and transcriptionally tune the expression of both nutrient-uptake and specialized-defense genes, thereby creating a feedback-controlled system in which competitive exclusion, oxidative and carbonyl stress buffering, and nutrient foraging are tightly co-regulated in silico to support persistent rhizosphere colonization.

Figure 7 illustrates an in silico model in which three OG–based modules—Module 1 (energy conservation), Module 6 (multidrug efflux), and Module 8 (stress sensing and transcriptional regulation) are conceptually coupled and may help safeguard A. fruticosum–associated rhizospheric bacteria against chemical stressors. In this framework, Complex I–driven PMF generation in Module 1 provides the obligatory energetic and ion-gradient foundation that powers MFS- and MATE-type efflux pumps in Module 6, while Module 8 regulators (e.g., SoxR and ArsR) are proposed to sense accumulating xenobiotics and redox-active compounds and transcriptionally upregulate efflux genes, thereby establishing a reciprocal loop in which energy transduction enables active detoxification and, conversely, efflux-mediated toxin clearance preserves respiratory function and membrane gradients that are essential for sustained rhizosphere colonization in this scenario.

Discussion

In silico analysis of eggNOG-annotated orthologous genes portrays the Abutilon fruticosum rhizosphere microbiome as a set of hierarchically interacting functional modules that, at the level of genomic potential, sketch possible routes for energy generation and partitioning, nutrient acquisition and transformation, and resistance to chemical and abiotic stressors under plant-mediated selection. Because these inferences rely exclusively on computational annotation and integration of orthologous groups, the present data cannot establish whether the identified genes are expressed in situ or directly support ecosystem processes under natural conditions.

Integrated functional COG modules of A. fruticosum rhizosphere microbiome

The orthologous group genes tentatively identified in the rhizosphere of Abutilon fruticosum can be grouped into ten interacting functional modules that, in a conceptual sense, suggest how this microbiome might generate energy, acquire and transform nutrients, assemble and protect root-associated biofilms, preserve genome integrity, synthesize specialized metabolites, and adapt to plant- and soil-driven selection pressures. These interpretations are derived solely from in silico analyses and therefore should be viewed as working hypotheses that will need targeted experimental validation before firm functional conclusions can be drawn.

Module 1: Bioenergetics & respiratory metabolism

This module is conceptualized as the energetic core of the system, organized around COG1007/NuoN, a membrane subunit of proton-translocating Complex I (NDH-1). NuoN, together with its antiporter-like partners NuoL and NuoM, has been experimentally shown in model bacteria to couple electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone with the movement of protons across the cytoplasmic membrane, generating the PMF that drives ATP synthesis and multiple secondary transport processes. In a rhizosphere context, this PMF would be expected to support motility, chemotaxis, biofilm development, high-affinity uptake of root-derived substrates, and multidrug efflux (for example via MATE-type pumps such as NorM), so disruption of NDH-1/Complex I subunits is predicted to reduce fitness and competitive potential of plant-associated soil bacteria during root colonization [49–55].

Module 2: Nitrogen & nutrient acquisition

This module appears specialized for scavenging nitrogenous compounds from root exudates and includes four ABC transporter ATPases that may function together as a nitrogen acquisition system adapted to the rhizosphere [56]. COG0411/LivG and COG0410/LivF likely form a heterodimeric ATPase pair in a high-affinity LIV transporter that imports leucine, isoleucine, and valine for protein synthesis and energy metabolism [57, 58]. COG3842/PotA, a spermidine/putrescine transport ATP-binding protein, probably has the potential for polyamine uptake that helps stabilize nucleic acids and membranes and enhances tolerance to stress. COG4608/AppF may energize an oligopeptide transporter that recovers short peptides from soil organic matter, improving nitrogen yield per ATP spent [59]. As all four ATPases depend on ATP from respiratory Module 1, their activity is likely coupled to cellular energy status, with high respiration enabling active nutrient foraging. Together, these predicted transporters could enhance nitrogen uptake and stress resilience, potentially providing rhizosphere microbes with a selective advantage in nutrient-rich yet competitive exudate environments [60–67].

Module 3: Carbon & lipid metabolism

This module appears to support the catabolism of complex carbon sources, potentially enabling the use of plant-derived lipids and organic acids by channeling their breakdown products into central metabolism [68]. COG2030/MaoC, a predicted MaoC-family dehydratase, may function as an enoyl-CoA hydratase in fatty acid β-oxidation, facilitating acyl-chain shortening and acetyl-CoA release [68]. COG1884/Sbm1, corresponding to the small subunit of methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, and COG4799/MmdA, a putative acetyl-/propionyl-CoA carboxylase component, are inferred to act in propionate metabolism, converting propionyl-CoA into methylmalonyl-CoA and subsequently into succinyl-CoA, a TCA cycle intermediate [69]. The central enzyme, methylmalonyl-CoA mutase, requires vitamin B₁₂ (adenosylcobalamin) to sustain flux from propionate and odd-chain fatty acids into the TCA cycle [70–73]. Under B₁₂-limiting conditions, methylmalonyl-CoA may accumulate, restricting this conversion and reducing the generation of NADH/FADH₂ for respiratory Module 1. Through the predicted integration of β-oxidation and B₁₂-dependent propionate metabolism, this module may enhance metabolic flexibility, allowing rhizosphere microbes to exploit plant-derived lipids and exudates less accessible to competitors [68, 74–76].

Module 4: Sulfur scavenging & detoxification

This module suggests microbial adaptation to both nutrient scarcity and plant-derived toxins in the soil environment [77, 78]. COG2141/SsuD, an alkanesulfonate monooxygenase, may enable the utilization of aliphatic sulfonates as alternative sulfur sources under sulfate limitation, supporting amino acid and cofactor biosynthesis when inorganic sulfate is scarce [79, 80]. COG0607/PspE, a rhodanese-like sulfurtransferase, is predicted to detoxify plant-derived cyanide by converting it to the less toxic thiocyanate, thereby mitigating the effects of plant chemical defenses [81, 82]. Acting together, these enzymes may help stabilize microbial activity under variable sulfur conditions and intermittent cyanide exposure, improving resilience in chemically dynamic rhizosphere soils.

Module 5: Cell envelope biogenesis & biofilm formation

This module is proposed to facilitate the physical anchoring and spatial organization of microbial communities on the root surface by linking cell division with biofilm matrix production [83–85]. COG0768/FtsI, a peptidoglycan D, D-transpeptidase also referred to as penicillin-binding protein 2 (PBP2) or 3 (PBP3), is predicted to function as a septal transpeptidase catalyzing peptidoglycan cross-linking during cell division, supporting proper cell wall formation and population expansion along the rhizoplane [86]. COG1215/BcsA, a cellulose synthase, and COG0463/WcaA, a glycosyltransferase involved in exopolysaccharide synthesis, are inferred to promote extracellular matrix formation that embeds cells within a cohesive biofilm scaffold [85, 87–92]. This hydrated matrix is thought to reinforce to root surfaces, reduce desiccation and abiotic stress, and maintain a localized microenvironment favorable for nutrient exchange and signaling.

Module 6: Multidrug efflux & defense

This module is proposed to provide an inducible defense layer that may help rhizosphere microbes tolerate xenobiotics, antibiotics, metals, and plant-derived antimicrobials in a chemically aggressive root environment [93, 94]. COG0534 (NorM, a Na⁺-dependent MATE-family efflux pump) and ENOG410XNN3 (an MFS-type transporter) are predicted to mediate active export of diverse toxic small molecules, including plant-derived antimicrobials, root-exudate-associated xenobiotics, and antibiotics from competing microbes, thereby potentially supporting rhizosphere survival [95]. Multidrug MATE and MFS transporters are widely recognized as major components of bacterial multidrug resistance systems that extrude chemically diverse cationic drugs and natural products, consistent with the proposed broad-spectrum detoxification role of these COGs in the root environment [94, 96]. The activity of these efflux systems is expected to depend on transmembrane ion gradients generated by the energy-conserving machinery in Module 1, which provides the driving force for Na⁺- and proton-coupled transport, and their expression is envisaged to be coordinated with stress-responsive circuits outlined in Module 8, enabling rapid induction upon xenobiotic exposure and supporting long-term rhizosphere colonization [85].

Module 7: DNA maintenance & genome integrity

This module is proposed to safeguard the microbial genome and its expression capacity under chronic environmental stress. COG0210 encodes UvrD, a superfamily 1 DNA helicase/translocase involved in nucleotide excision repair, mismatch repair, and replication restart, thereby helping to remove DNA lesions and secondary structures generated by oxidative and other genotoxic stresses and supporting genome stability and persistence [97]. COG0086 corresponds to RpoC, the β′ subunit of DNA-directed RNA polymerase, which forms part of the catalytic core for template-dependent RNA synthesis and provides key active-center residues for RNA chain elongation and DNA/RNA binding, making it essential for faithful transcription of genes underlying other functional modules [98, 99]. Together, these components may help maintain genome integrity and transcriptional competence, limiting deleterious mutation accumulation and supporting stable community functioning in a mutagenic soil habitat.

Module 8: Environmental sensing & transcriptional regulation

This module is proposed to act as a regulatory core that translates fluctuating rhizosphere signals into coordinated transcriptional and physiological responses, by analogy with characterized networks in soil and rhizosphere bacteria [100–102]. COG0640/ArsR, a small helix–turn–helix DNA-binding repressor of arsenic/metal resistance operons, is considered as a metal-sensing regulator that adjusts gene expression to help maintain metal homeostasis and mitigate toxicity. COG0789/SoxR, a redox-sensing transcriptional regulator with a [2Fe–2 S] cluster, is inferred to respond to redox-active compounds and oxidative stress, triggering defense programs against reactive oxygen species and redox-cycling metabolites near roots. In addition, multiple signal-transduction circuits, including two-component systems (COG2197/CitB, a NarL/FixJ-family response regulator; COG4585/ComP, a sensor histidine kinase; ENOG410XNMH, a NarL-like sensor kinase group) and COG0515/SPS1, eukaryotic-like Ser/Thr kinases, are predicted to convert external cues into phosphorylation cascades and specific transcriptional outputs [103–105].

Module 9: Secondary metabolism & specialized functions

This module is proposed to act as a specialized bioactivity hub that shapes both stress tolerance and competitive behavior in the rhizosphere. COG2124/CypX, a cytochrome P450 implicated in secondary metabolite biosynthesis, is considered as contributing to pulcherriminic acid production, leading to the iron-chelating pigment pulcherrimin that can trap extracellular iron and promote interference competition while supporting iron acquisition [106–110]. COG1233, annotated as a phytoene dehydrogenase–related protein, is inferred by analogy to characterized phytoene desaturases/dehydrogenases to participate in carotenoid-like pigment biosynthesis that helps quench reactive oxygen species and protect cells from oxidative damage, including root-derived oxidants and antagonistic microbial activities in the rhizosphere [111]. COG0491/GloB, a family of hydroxyacylglutathione hydrolase/glyoxalase II, is predicted to function in the glyoxalase pathway, regenerating glutathione and contributing to methylglyoxal and reactive-carbonyl detoxification, thereby preventing accumulation of cytotoxic metabolic by-products and supporting redox homeostasis and stress tolerance [112, 113]. Collectively, these activities could enhance competitive fitness by improving access to limiting micronutrients, buffering cells against oxidative and chemical injury, and modulating the local chemical landscape in ways that favor persistence of the producing population.

Module 10: Mobile genetic elements & adaptation

This module is proposed to capture the plasticity and evolutionary potential of the rhizosphere-associated genome under strong host- and environment-driven selection pressures [114–116]. COG2801, encoding InsO-family transposases of IS3-like insertion sequences, and ENOG410Y65V, corresponding to retrotransposon-related proteins, can be considered as mediating DNA rearrangements and horizontal gene transfer that promote shuffling, mobilization, and acquisition of adaptive loci across community members [116–118]. COG4886 encodes YopM, a leucine-rich repeat (LRR)–containing type III secretion system effector that is delivered into eukaryotic host cells and modulates host signaling, suggesting potential host-modulation capabilities when such effectors occur in rhizosphere genomes [119–122]. Acting together, these elements may foster rapid genomic diversification and incorporation of novel functional traits, enabling the microbiome to track plant-driven selection and fluctuating soil conditions on ecological and evolutionary timescales.

Conceptual integration of rhizosphere functional modules

Conceptual framework integrating functional modules underpinning rhizosphere energy and nutrient fluxes

Multiple functional modules are hypothesized to engage in extensive cross-talk, enabling plant-associated bacteria to convert root exudates into usable energy and biomass in an integrated framework (Fig. 5). Modules 1–4 are conceptually depicted as a single, coupled system in which Module 1 functions as the energetic hub of the A. fruticosum rhizosphere consortium, with the COG1007/NuoN Complex I acting as NADH dehydrogenase that oxidizes NADH, transfers electrons to ubiquinone, and exports protons to generate a proton motive force (PMF) sustaining the electron transport chain and F-type ATP synthase. ATP and redox cofactors (NADH, FADH₂, NAD⁺, FAD) produced by Module 1 are proposed to energize the four ABC-type transport ATPases of Module 2 (COG0411/LivG, COG0410/LivF, COG3842/PotA, COG4608/AppF), driving high-affinity import of branched-chain amino acids, polyamines, and oligopeptides from root exudates [123–126].

Within this scheme, substrates captured by Module 2 can be deaminated to keto acids that feed central nodes such as pyruvate, acetyl-CoA, and particularly the propionyl-CoA → succinyl-CoA → TCA branch, converging with Module 3, which integrates fatty-acid β-oxidation and propionate catabolism via COG2030/MaoC, COG1884/Sbm1, and COG4799/MmdA to deliver lipids and short-chain acids into the shared TCA-linked carbon pool [123–126]. Enhanced amino-acid uptake is therefore predicted to boost flux through Module-3 pathways, modulating energy yield, membrane fatty-acid composition, and precursor supply for biofilm matrix and secondary metabolites, whereas shifts in β-oxidation or propionate flux would reciprocally alter demand for amino-acid-derived carbon and nitrogen, tuning transporter activity and expression in Module 2 [118–121]. In parallel, Module 4 (COG2141/SsuD, COG0607/PspE) is proposed to exploit ATP from oxidative phosphorylation to utilize and detoxify organosulfur compounds, providing sulfur when sulfur-containing amino acids imported via Module 2 are limiting, thereby sustaining sulfur amino-acid pools and maintaining sulfur homeostasis [77, 78, 127–129].

In aggregate, these in silico interactions outline a flexible, energetically integrated network that could, in principle, allow Modules 1–4 to co-adjust carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur flows to match root-derived substrate availability, while remaining explicitly hypothetical and hypothesis-generating pending experimental confirmation of the strength and directionality of the proposed couplings.

The convergence of specialized metabolism, biosynthetic capacity, nutrient fluxes, and environmental stress sensing

Figure 6 encapsulates an in silico model in which rhizosphere colonization competence is hypothesized to be influenced by tight trimodular coupling among Module 9 (Specialized Competition and Protection; COG2124/CypX, COG1233, COG0491/GloB), Module 2 (Nutrient Acquisition and Biosynthesis), and Module 8 (Environmental Stress Sensing and Transcriptional Regulation), treated as a single integrated biosystem rather than three autonomous pathways. In the upper schematic, Module 9 is depicted as a tripartite specialized-function hub: CypX-driven pulcherriminic acid biosynthesis could sequester Fe³⁺ into pulcherrimin, creating iron-depleted exclusion zones for competitors; COG1233-linked carotenoid formation furnishes antioxidant shields via conjugated π-electron networks that quench ROS; and GloB-mediated glyoxalase activity channels methylglyoxal into S-D-lactoylglutathione and then D-lactate, thereby regenerating glutathione and converting detoxification into a source of pyruvate and alanine precursors for central carbon and amino-acid metabolism. The lower panel depicts the predicted reciprocal interdependence: Module 2 supplies ferric iron, amino acids, and isoprenoid precursors necessary for the synthesis and operation of Module-9 enzymes, while Module 8, via SoxR-mediated redox sensing and ArsR-type metal sensing, scales Module-9 transcription proportionally to oxidative and metal stress.

In turn, Module 9 is hypothesized to shield Module-2 transport and biosynthetic machinery from oxidative and carbonyl damage, and to generate negative-feedback signals that dampen Module-8 activation once chemical threats are neutralized. This construct therefore is consistent with the idea that enhanced rhizosphere performance may emerge from reciprocal potentiation: Module 2 enables specialized metabolite production, Module 9 safeguards and augments nutrient-acquisition efficiency, and Module 8 synchronizes both with environmental conditions. The implied prediction—that selection may favor lineages carrying the full complement of nutrient transporters (LivG, LivF, PotA, AppF), stress sensors (SoxR, ArsR), and specialized enzymes (CypX, COG1233, GloB) enriched in Abutilon fruticosum microbiomes—remains provisional and will require rigorous experimental corroboration through targeted mutagenesis, graded-stress transcriptomics, enzyme kinetics, metal and nutrient flux assays, and competitive colonization experiments.

Reciprocal energetic coupling and stress-responsive detoxification

Diagrammatically, Fig. 7 advances an in silico model in which chemical-stress tolerance is contingent on tight energetic and regulatory coupling among Modules 1, 6, and 8. Module 1 (Energy Conservation) is depicted as the indispensable energetic backbone: proton pumping by Complex I establishes the PMF and, via Na⁺/H⁺ antiporters, the Na⁺ gradient that directly energizes the MFS exporter (ENOG410XNN3) and indirectly powers the MATE transporter (COG0534/NorM) in Module 6. In the absence of sustained ATP generation and PMF maintenance, these multidrug efflux systems are predicted to be functionally inert, irrespective of their abundance or transcriptional state.

Module 8 (Stress Sensing and Transcriptional Regulation) is conceptually depicted as the supervisory layer that tunes this machinery, with SoxR and ArsR sensing redox-active and metal/arsenical toxins and transcriptionally upregulating efflux genes (COG0534, ENOG410XNN3) so that export capacity scales with xenobiotic load, avoiding both wasteful overexpression and dangerous under-expression. Module 6 (Multidrug Efflux) then performs the executive detoxification task: MATE and MFS pumps recognize chemically diverse toxicants—phenolics, alkaloids, isothiocyanates, antibiotics—and actively extrude them to the extracellular milieu, that could function as part of a detoxification barrier to preserves Module-1 energy transduction, Module-2 biosynthetic processes, and Module-8 sensing circuitry. The schematic further emphasizes reciprocal feedback: toxin molecules are shown approaching from the soil but being intercepted by active efflux before they inactivate respiratory complexes or ATP synthase, so that energy generation enables detoxification, and detoxification, in turn, preserves energy-generation capacity. The “Transmembrane Gradients” inset underscores that successful rhizosphere colonization under intense chemical stress requires continuous maintenance of electrochemical gradients that are simultaneously the product of Module-1 activity and the driving force for Module-6 export. Cells lacking any one module are predicted, in this conceptual model, to be strongly disadvantaged—those missing Module 6 accumulate toxins despite functional respiration and sensing; those lacking Module 1 cannot energize efflux; those without Module 8 cannot upregulate pumps in high-toxin environments—whereas only organisms possessing all three modules in concert are hypothesized to be better positioned to achieve the robust, energy-dependent detoxification that may be required under rhizosphere conditions dominated by plant antimicrobials, microbial antibiotics, and soil xenobiotics.

In summary, the hierarchical integration of Modules of the three complementary frameworks is consistent with the idea that rhizosphere success may depend on coordinated coupling of energy transduction, nutrient acquisition, specialized metabolism, stress sensing, and active chemical defense—not from isolated metabolic competencies. Collectively, these three frameworks offer a systems-level, hypothesis-generating scaffold for thinking about rhizosphere colonization competence, where natural selection operates as a stringent filter enriching organisms exhibiting complete multimodular integration and explaining the conserved genomic signatures observed in successful colonists. Experimental validation through targeted gene disruption, quantitative transcriptomics, metabolic flux analysis, and competitive colonization assays is essential to mechanistically establish these predictions and enable rational engineering of enhanced rhizosphere competence.

In silico modular architecture of the A. fruticosum rhizosphere microbiome

In the context of taxa harboring orthologous genes predicted to be enriched in the rhizospheric soil of Abutilon fruticosum, Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria appear as predominant bacterial phyla associated with the ten functional modules, with affiliated genera disproportionately contributing high-copy orthologs linked to energy metabolism, nutrient uptake, and stress responses [21, 130–142]. Actinobacteria typified by Streptomyces additionally harbor OGs linked to fatty-acid β-oxidation and vitamin B₁₂-dependent propionate catabolism (Module 3), which could funnel plant-derived lipids and short-chain carboxylates into tricarboxylic-acid-cycle intermediates that supply reducing equivalents to Complex I and precursors for cell-envelope and secondary-metabolite biosynthesis [143–146].

Modules 4–6 and 9 highlight OGs associated with sulfur scavenging and detoxification, multidrug efflux, and specialized protective metabolites. Proteobacterial genera such as Microvirga and Sphingomonas are enriched in OGs related to organosulfur transformation (Module 4), consistent with the capacity to use or detoxify sulfonate-bound sulfur and cyanogenic defense metabolites in chemically complex, xeric rhizospheres [147–150]. Module 5 includes Actinobacterial peptidoglycan-biosynthetic enzymes such as FtsI-type D, D-transpeptidases and Proteobacterial cellulose and exopolysaccharide synthases (e.g. BcsA, WcaA), which together may support biofilm matrix formation and root-surface attachment [151–154]. Within Module 6, Proteobacterial multidrug MATE and MFS exporters (e.g. NorM-type systems), alongside Actinobacterial efflux-related OGs, likely contribute to a PMF-driven capacity for extrusion of phenolic allelochemicals, antimicrobial compounds, and other xenobiotics, while Module 9 OGs associated with CypX-linked pulcherriminic acid, carotenoid-like antioxidants, and glyoxalase-mediated detoxification are concentrated in Streptomyces and related Actinobacteria and are compatible with iron chelation, oxidative protection, and carbonyl-stress mitigation in the rhizosphere [155–159]. Modules 7 and 8, finally, are enriched in Actinobacterial DNA-repair helicases and RNA-polymerase core subunits together with Proteobacterial metal- and redox-sensing regulators of the ArsR/SmtB and SoxR families, suggesting that these taxa disproportionately carry OGs implicated in maintaining genome integrity and coordinating transcriptional responses to redox and metal stress [160–164].

Streptophyta-derived orthologs, although less abundant than their bacterial counterparts, plausibly contribute complementary functions by encoding plant-origin transporters, signaling proteins, and stress-response factors that influence rhizosphere chemistry and thereby the performance of bacterial modules [12, 165–167]. Root-expressed genes that affect exudation profiles, redox status, and metal availability can shape the availability of carbon substrates, micronutrients, and redox-active metabolites relevant to Modules 1–4 and 9, while Streptophyta orthologs linked to cell-wall remodeling, defense signaling, and hormone pathways may interact indirectly with microbial sensing and regulatory circuits (Modules 8 and 10) to favor consortia best aligned with A. fruticosum physiology and stress-adaptation strategies [147, 165–172].

In the present study, functions were first annotated at the level of orthologous groups (COGs/ENOGs) and then associated with taxa by mapping gene-bearing contigs to higher-level bacterial and eukaryotic clades using single-copy marker genes from the Genome Taxonomy Database (GTDB) (https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org/) [173, 174]. As such, the enrichment of particular functions in Actinobacteria or Proteobacteria reflects a two-step inference—orthology-based functional annotation followed by marker-gene–based taxonomic profiling—rather than direct assignment of individual functions to genome-resolved bins or metagenome-assembled genomes, a limitation that has been emphasized for complex soil microbiomes where many lineages remain without cultivated representatives or complete reference genomes [175, 176]. Our phylum-level patterns (for example, the dominance of Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria among high-abundance OGs) should therefore be considered as broad lineage–function associations rather than precise genotype–phenotype relationships and will require genome-resolved metagenomics and complementary approaches to verify at finer taxonomic resolution [175, 176].

A second limitation concerns the connection between these inferred functional–taxonomic patterns and measured environmental gradients. Although our sampling design captured contrasting microhabitats (rhizosphere versus bulk soil) in arid A. fruticosum stands and builds on previous work showing that Actinobacteria and Proteobacteria frequently dominate carbon and nutrient cycling and stress responses in dryland and post-disturbance soils, we did not perform a formal multivariate analysis linking OG or module abundances to specific soil physicochemical parameters or plant functional traits in the present dataset [137, 175, 177, 178]. The ecological interpretations we offer—such as the potential roles of Actinobacteria- and Proteobacteria-enriched modules in coping with low moisture, nutrient limitation, and plant-derived antimicrobials—should therefore be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Future work that combines genome-resolved metagenomics with detailed soil chemistry, plant trait measurements, and temporal sampling along environmental gradients will be needed to more directly connect particular taxa–function assemblages to soil properties, plant physiology, and broader ecosystem processes.

Taken together, this in silico framework suggests that Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Streptophyta may function as a tightly coupled triad in which bacterial orthologous cohorts execute the core energetic and defensive modules, while plant-encoded orthologs dynamically sculpt the rhizosphere milieu that selects for, sustains, and fine-tunes these multimodular consortia.

Limitations, and future directions

This study indicates that the rhizospheric microbiome of Abutilon fruticosum has a distinct OG-resolved functional repertoire, with dominant COG/eggNOG assemblages more strongly represented in energy metabolism, nutrient acquisition, stress adaptation, and secondary biosynthetic capacity than in neighboring bulk soil. By organizing these orthologs into ten interacting functional modules nested within three overarching frameworks, we propose that the coordinated action of Complex I–driven ATP/proton-motive force generation, high-affinity nitrogen and sulfur uptake systems, redox- and xenobiotic-detoxification machineries, biofilm and cell-surface adherence factors, multidrug efflux systems, and mobile genetic elements may contribute to rhizosphere colonization and plant-associated performance and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. However, these conclusions are based entirely on in silico functional annotation and integration, so we cannot yet determine whether the highlighted genes and modules are expressed at substantial levels in situ or directly support biogeochemical processes and plant fitness under natural conditions. Moving forward, integrative multi-omics approaches—combining genome-resolved metagenomics with metatranscriptomics, metaproteomics, metabolomics, and targeted physiological assays—will be essential to validate these predictions, bridge the gap between genomic potential and realized function, refine the proposed modular networks, and ultimately enable the rational deployment of A. fruticosum–associated microbiomes to enhance plant performance and promote sustainable soil-ecosystem management.
