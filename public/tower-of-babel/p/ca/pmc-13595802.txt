The impact of protective eyewear and device position on operator radiation dose during handheld portable X‑ray device use
Authors: Sinem Coşkun Albayrak, Alican Kuran, Murat Doğuş Günel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe use of handheld dental X-ray devices has been increasing, raising concerns about operator radiation exposure. This study evaluated the effects of handheld dental X-ray device positioning and protective eyewear on the absorbed dose received by the operator’s eye lens and pituitary gland.MethodsCalibrated optically stimulated luminescent dosimeters were placed at the eye lens and sphenoid regions of an anthropomorphic head phantom. Measurements were obtained under different device positioning scenarios, with and without protective eyewear. Four operator positions were evaluated, and 30 repeated irradiations were performed under each condition, both with and without protective eyewear, resulting in a total of 720 individual measurements.ResultsBoth operator positioning and the use of protective eyewear significantly influenced absorbed dose levels. Protective eyewear reduced ocular and sphenoidal exposure across all evaluated positions, with the greatest reduction observed when combined with optimal device angulation. Across all positions, the use of protective eyewear decreased absorbed dose by approximately 48–70% (p < 0.001 for all comparisons), with the largest reductions observed under the shielding condition.ConclusionsAppropriate operator positioning and the consistent use of protective eyewear are essential for minimizing radiation exposure to radiosensitive structures during handheld dental X-ray procedures. Under the conditions evaluated in this phantom study, the combination of a 45° device angulation and protective eyewear resulted in the lowest absorbed dose values, suggesting that this approach may help reduce operator radiation exposure.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Biological effects, Absorbed dose, Radiation protection

Introduction

Handheld intraoral dental X-ray devices, operated by holding the unit during image acquisition, have been used in dental radiology for over a decade [1]. Due to their portability, they provide significant advantages over conventional wall-mounted dental X-ray units, particularly for patients with limited mobility, those unable to leave their homes, residents of nursing homes, or patients treated under general anaesthesia. In addition, these devices are also utilised in forensic dental imaging at archaeological excavation sites, crime scenes, disaster areas, and in veterinary applications [2]. The guideline prepared by the European Academy of DentoMaxilloFacial Radiology (EADMFR) introduces strict limitations on the use of handheld X-ray devices [3]. According to this guidance, their use should be restricted to operating theatres or emergency departments where no fixed equipment is available, nursing homes accommodating immobile patients, detention centres, or remote settings without dental facilities such as rural areas, isolated sites, or military operations. However, owing to their increasing accessibility, innovative features, and affordability, in recent years their use has expanded beyond their original purpose into routine clinical practice. This growing use has raised concerns, as reduced distance from the radiation source and difficulties in maintaining optimal protection increase the operator’s exposure risk [2, 4]. For this reason, several countries and professional associations have issued recommendations regarding the correct and safe use of these devices [3, 5, 6]. For example, current regulations in the United Kingdom stipulate that handheld X-ray devices should not replace conventional units, should be used strictly in accordance with the user manual, and must be equipped with a rectangular collimator and a backscatter shield to protect the operator from scattered radiation [5, 7]. However, no specific recommendation has been made regarding the use of protective eyewear.

Although dentists and radiology operators working in the field of dentistry are exposed to low levels of X-radiation during intraoral and extraoral radiographic procedures, operator protection measures are essential to minimise occupational exposure to ionising radiation [8]. It is important that dentists and all oral healthcare professionals are aware of existing recommendations and understand their roles in protecting patients, operators, and the public [9]. Therefore, appropriate operator positioning and the regular use of protective equipment are necessary to minimise radiation reaching sensitive tissues. Personal protective equipment, such as lead aprons and thyroid collars, constitutes an essential part of routine practice for radiation protection [10]. Leaded protective eyewear typically provides attenuation equivalent to a tenth-value layer, resulting in a substantial and expected reduction in eye-lens dose. Despite this strong scientific evidence, the rate of protective eyewear usage remains remarkably low, with compliance reported at only around 2.5% [11, 12]. Radiation-induced posterior subcapsular cataracts have been documented among professionals with prolonged X-ray exposure [12]. Therefore, considering the persistently low usage rates, it is evident that improving compliance with leaded protective eyewear among healthcare professionals working with radiation is an urgent necessity. However, while studies exist regarding radiation doses to operators and patients from handheld X-ray devices, and various usage guidelines have been provided, the literature on dose effects to specific organs such as the eyes and the sphenoid region is very limited. In addition, guidelines and publications issued by major organisations do not provide information on the protection of the eyes or sphenoid region. Although recommendations for protective equipment include thyroid shields, lead aprons, and even lead gloves, these regions are overlooked. The concept of a “controlled area” is also challenged by the handheld nature of these devices; although they include integrated lead shielding, the distance between the device and the body varies depending on how the operator holds it [3]. Therefore, it can be hypothesised that the design of handheld devices increases the risk of direct radiation exposure to the eyes and, consequently, to the sphenoid region.

Although the potential risks of operator radiation exposure during dental radiographic procedures using handheld X-ray devices have been investigated in several studies [2, 4, 13–15], research employing highly realistic anthropomorphic phantoms that closely replicate human soft tissues and bone density remains limited. In particular, previous studies evaluating radiation exposure to the eye have generally relied on mannequin models, with dosimeters positioned on their surfaces rather than within tissue-equivalent structures [2, 13]. Consequently, these experimental setups may not accurately reflect radiation absorption and attenuation by surrounding anatomical tissues, potentially limiting the clinical relevance of the reported dose estimates. Previous reports have therefore emphasized the need for future investigations to utilize tissue-equivalent phantoms to obtain more reliable and clinically relevant data [13]. Addressing this gap, the present study was designed to evaluate the effect of protective eyewear and device positioning on the radiation dose to the eye lens and the sphenoid region during the use of handheld dental X-ray devices, employing the Alderson (Rando) phantom model. The Alderson (Rando) phantom, with its layered, sliceable structure, allows the placement of dosimeters in various anatomical regions such as the eyes, thyroid, brain, and lungs. With its bone density and soft tissue equivalence, it accurately simulates realistic human morphology and is therefore preferred for assessing multiple organ doses or calculating effective dose [16–19].

This study aimed to quantitatively assess scatter radiation dose to the operator’s eye lens and sphenoid region under different handheld X-ray device positioning scenarios and to evaluate the protective effect of leaded protective eyewear using an anthropomorphic phantom.

Methods

Study design

This experimental study evaluated the absorbed radiation dose received by the operator at the eye lens and sphenoid regions during the use of a handheld X-ray device and assessed the protective effect of protective eyewear across different operator positions. All measurements reflect the scattered radiation originating from the handheld X-ray device itself, independent of patient-related attenuation or scatter, as no patient or anthropomorphic phantom was used as a scatter simulator. Ethical approval was not required because the study did not involve human participants or human biological materials.

Phantom and dosimeter placement

A tissue-equivalent Alderson phantom model, consisting of layered slices simulating human soft tissues and bone density, was used to enable placement of dosimeters at radiosensitive intracranial locations. Three optically stimulated luminescence (OSL) dosimeters (Physmart, Ankara, Türkiye) were positioned at the right corneal level, left corneal level, and the sphenoid bone region (Fig. 1). Each dosimeter is disc-shaped with a diameter of 4.5 mm and a thickness of 0.89 mm.

X-ray equipment and protective eyewear

The handheld X-ray device (Diox-602, DigiMed, Seoul, Korea) operated at 60 kVp and 2 mA, with total filtration of 1.6 mm Al, a focal spot size of 0.8 mm, a circular diaphragm of 6 mm diameter, and a 10 cm cone length. The device weighs 1.8 kg and is designed for two-handed operation. The wall-mounted reference unit was the NewTom RxDc (NewTom CEFLA, Imola, Italy), operated at 8 mA, 60–70 kVp, and 0.10 s.

The protective eyewear evaluated in this study was the Panorama front-and-side protective eyewear (Aktif X-Ray, Istanbul, Turkey; 0.75 mm Pb equivalent). Both the handheld X-ray device and the protective eyewear were chosen to reflect equipment commonly used in routine dental practice, and their technical specifications are representative of commercially available systems.

Exposure protocol

The exposure protocol was adapted from Makdissi et al. [2]. The handheld device was operated at 60 kVp and 2 mA using the anterior periapical setting, with a fixed exposure time of 0.16 s applied uniformly across all irradiations. This exposure time approximates routine clinical intraoral radiographic parameters and ensures methodological standardization across experimental conditions. For each position, 30 repeated irradiations were performed both with and without protective eyewear, yielding 720 individual measurements in total. The handheld device was triggered using a remote control from behind a lead shield, ensuring that no personnel were exposed to radiation.

OSL dosimetry and calibration

The OSL dosimeters were calibrated by the manufacturer in accordance with international dosimetry standards for diagnostic radiology, using reference X-ray beam qualities representative of dental radiographic energies. The detectors exhibit a linear dose response with a reported measurement uncertainty of approximately ± 5% under standard conditions, and minimal energy dependence within the diagnostic X-ray spectrum. The detectors are responsive to photon energies in the range of 16 keV to 6.7 MeV. The manufacturer-specified personal monitoring range of 0.05 mSv to 10 Sv corresponds to the routine occupational reportable dose threshold and does not represent the minimum detectable dose of the detector under controlled experimental conditions. OSL dosimeters can reliably detect doses as low as 5–10 µGy in diagnostic radiology applications. In the present study, 30 repeated irradiations were performed for each position. A new set of dosimeters was used for each experimental position, and readings were obtained before and after irradiation to minimize measurement error and cumulative signal bias. A control dosimeter kept in the irradiation room was used to determine background radiation, which was subtracted from all measurements prior to dose calculation. This repetition protocol was adapted from the methodology adopted by Altındağ et al. [13] and all exposures were conducted under standardised conditions, and the recorded data were subsequently analysed.

Statistical analyses

A priori power analysis was performed using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), based on a 95% confidence level, 80% power, and a medium effect size. The minimum required sample size was calculated as 18 exposures per comparison group; 30 repeated exposures per condition were performed to improve measurement reliability and reduce variability.

For each experimental condition, 30 irradiations were performed while maintaining a fixed and standardized phantom geometry. Each exposure was recorded as a separate measurement, and these repeated irradiations were treated as independent observations obtained under identical experimental conditions. This approach was adopted to characterize measurement variability and to improve the precision of dose estimates. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. As the measurements did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were used. The Mann–Whitney U test was applied for comparisons between two independent groups (with vs. without protective eyewear), whereas the Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons among multiple groups (different operator positions), followed by pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction where appropriate. Each measurement contributed a single value to the relevant comparison group. The use of repeated measurements under controlled phantom conditions and their analysis using inferential statistical methods is consistent with previous phantom-based dosimetric studies investigating handheld dental X-ray devices [13]. Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

For the right lens, left lens, and sphenoid regions, the lowest radiation doses were observed in the shielding position with protective eyewear (right lens: 6.92 ± 1.42 µGy, left lens: 6.84 ± 1.46 µGy, sphenoid: 3.70 ± 1.94 µGy). When radiation doses across all positions were compared, sphenoid region doses were significantly lower than those of the right and left lenses (p < 0.001), whereas no significant difference was observed between the right and left lenses (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Regarding the lens regions, radiation exposure followed a progressive pattern: shielding position with protective eyewear (lowest), Position 3 with glasses, Position 2 with glasses, Position 1 with glasses, Position 3 without glasses, Position 2 without glasses, and Position 1 without glasses (highest). A similar trend was observed for the sphenoid region, with the lowest dose recorded in the shielding position with protective eyewear, gradually increasing through Positions 3, 2, and 1 with glasses, and reaching the highest levels in Positions 3, 2, and 1 without glasses. These findings indicate that both operator–patient distance and the use of protective eyewear are decisive factors in reducing ocular and sphenoidal radiation exposure, with the combination of shielding and protective eyewear providing the most effective protection.

In Position 1, the use of protective eyewear reduced radiation exposure by 58.9% in the right lens, 57.6% in the left lens, and 66.6% in the sphenoid region. In Position 2, the respective reductions were 59.7%, 60.2%, and 58.1%. In Position 3, reductions were 47.9% for the right lens, 48.2% for the left lens, and 64.4% for the sphenoid. The shielding position demonstrated the greatest percentage reductions, reaching 68.0% in the right lens, 68.7% in the left lens, and 70.2% in the sphenoid region. These results confirm that protective eyewear consistently and substantially reduce radiation exposure across all positions, with the most pronounced protective effect observed in the shielding condition. The observed variation in percentage dose reduction across different positions, despite the use of the same protective eyewear, can be attributed to differences in source-to-detector distance and scatter radiation geometry. Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that, across all handheld device positions, the use of leaded protective eyewear significantly reduced the absorbed dose to both the eyes and the sphenoid region, with a high level of statistical significance (p < 0.001). Even when the wall-mounted X-ray device was operated with the operator positioned outside the room behind a lead barrier, leaded protective eyewear further contributed to a substantial reduction in absorbed dose at these sensitive sites. The study also revealed statistically significant differences in operator dose across the evaluated positions. Absorbed dose was highest when the device was held close to the body with arms flexed (Position 1). Although extending the arms straight at a distance of 40 cm, as recommended by manufacturers (Position 2), markedly reduced exposure compared with Position 1, the most pronounced reduction in the handheld configurations was observed when the device was operated at a 45° inclination (Position 3).

In radiology, the ALARA (As Low As Reasonably Achievable) principle has progressively evolved into ALADA (As Low As Diagnostically Acceptable) and ALADAIP (As Low As Diagnostically Acceptable being Indication-oriented and Patient-specific) approaches. These concepts aim not only to minimize patient radiation exposure but also to reduce occupational exposure for operators performing imaging procedures. The International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP) defines three fundamental principles of radiation protection: justification, optimisation, and the application of dose limits [21]. These principles govern protection both at the level of radiation sources and exposed individuals. In this context, the use of handheld dental X-ray devices should also adhere to these principles. Their application only when clinically justified reflects the principle of justification, while efforts to minimize exposure during use correspond to optimisation. Optimisation further requires adequate operator training, appropriate handling of the device, and effective use of protective equipment for both patient and operator safety. Previous studies on handheld dental X-ray devices have demonstrated that various protective measures significantly reduce scattered radiation exposure to the operator, thereby supporting their use within the ALARA framework. These protective measures include maintaining the device at the maximum possible distance from the operator’s body with fully extended arms, the use of backscatter shielding at the end of the tube, longer collimation tubes, lead aprons, lead gloves, and rectangular collimators [2, 22–32]. These precautions are particularly critical in situations where the operator is in close proximity to both the radiation source and the patient. Consistent with the literature, the findings of the present study indicate that positioning the device farther from the body and at an angled orientation reduces the radiation dose to the eye lens and sphenoid region. These results further emphasize the importance of operator training and proper technical knowledge in ensuring the safe use of handheld dental X-ray devices.

The wall-mounted reference condition (Position 4) yielded eye-lens and sphenoid doses that were lower than or comparable to those obtained in the handheld positions. This pattern reflects several factors intrinsic to the fixed-unit setup rather than a direct experimental equivalence. First, the phantom was positioned behind a lead-lined door at approximately 1.5 m from the source, providing both geometric reduction of scatter and additional structural attenuation does not present in Positions 1–3. Second, the distribution and directionality of secondary radiation differ markedly between handheld and wall-mounted systems; in the latter, a substantial portion of scatter is absorbed by structural shielding and room materials before reaching the operator location. These combined effects likely explain why Position 4 produced lower absorbed doses despite the higher tube current of the fixed device. The greater proportional reduction achieved with leaded protective eyewear in this position may also reflect the smaller amount of scatter reaching the eye region, making the relative impact of attenuation more apparent. Therefore, Position 4 should be regarded as a reference condition for conventional radiographic imaging rather than as a true experimental control, as differences in geometry, exposure parameters, and shielding limit direct comparability with the handheld configurations.

It is important to acknowledge that the absorbed dose values reported in the present study are higher than those reported in some previous investigations using handheld dental X-ray devices, particularly the work of Makdissi et al. [2] and Altındağ et al. [13]. Several methodological factors likely contribute to this difference. First, the NOMAD Pro 2 device used by Makdissi et al. is equipped with a lead-embedded acrylic backscatter shield (16 cm diameter, 0.5 mm Pb equivalent), whereas the Diox-602 device used in the present study does not include such a backscatter shield. Rottke et al. [14] reported that the backscatter shield of the NOMAD Pro 2 attenuates scatter radiation to levels below detection thresholds at the operator position, indicating that the absence of this shield in our device may substantially increase the scatter dose reaching the operator. From an ALARA perspective, the higher radiation dose values observed in the present study compared with previous reports further emphasize the importance of device-specific integrated shielding features. These findings suggest that the incorporation of protective components, such as backscatter shields, into handheld X-ray devices may play a substantial role in reducing operator exposure. They also highlight the need for operators to be adequately informed and trained regarding the presence, function, and appropriate use of such shielding systems in routine clinical practice. Second, dosimeter placement differed between studies: previous investigations typically used surface placement on a mannequin or measurement directly at the operator’s eye level on a non-anatomical mannequin, whereas the present study placed dosimeters at anatomically defined intracranial sites within an Alderson phantom. Third, OSL dosimeters offer greater sensitivity for low-dose scatter radiation than the TLD systems used in earlier work [33]. Notably, in Makdissi et al. [11], several values for ocular exposure fell below the TLD detection threshold (recorded as 0 mGy after background subtraction), suggesting that direct numerical comparison may be limited by the dynamic range of the dosimetric methods used. Fourth, inter-device variability is well documented in the handheld X-ray literature; Altındağ et al. [13] also reported substantially different operator doses among the NOMAD Pro 2, Rextar X, and Diox-602 devices, with differences exceeding an order of magnitude depending on the anatomical region and exposure protocol. Furthermore, when our findings are compared with those of Altındağ et al. [13], the ocular dose values obtained in Position 3 of the present study were broadly comparable in magnitude to the µGy-level lens doses they reported using the same Diox-602 device. However, Altındağ et al. [13] did not specify the exact operator position from which their irradiations were performed, and it therefore remains uncertain to which of our experimental positions their measurements most closely correspond. As a result, a fully direct comparison cannot be established, and the methodological differences outlined above must also be considered when interpreting the observed dose values. Therefore, the values reported here should be interpreted within the context of the specific device, dosimetric method, and experimental geometry, and direct numerical comparison with previous studies should be made with caution.

Several methodological considerations should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study. First, the study was not designed to estimate absolute in vivo occupational exposure. Rather, its purpose was to examine, under standardized experimental conditions, the extent to which leaded protective eyewear and operator positioning influence radiation exposure to radiosensitive structures in the head and neck. The experimental setup was therefore intended as a comparative model rather than a simulation of routine clinical dosimetry, and the findings should be interpreted within this context. Within these experimental conditions, the use of leaded eyewear reduced absorbed dose by approximately 48–70% across all operator positions. The lowest dose values were consistently observed when both a structural shield and leaded protective eyewear were used, whereas the highest exposures occurred in Position 1 without protective eyewear. The right and left corneal regions received higher doses than the sphenoid region, most likely because of their more direct exposure to scattered radiation. No significant differences were found between the right and left corneas, suggesting that both eyes are similarly vulnerable irrespective of operator stance.

An important limitation of the present experimental design is the absence of a patient or tissue-equivalent phantom within the primary beam. In clinical practice, the patient’s head and neck represent the principal source of secondary scatter generated by interactions between X-rays and biological tissues. Because no patient-equivalent scattering medium was included in the current setup, the measured values predominantly reflect radiation scattered from the device and the surrounding environment. Consequently, the absorbed doses reported here should not be regarded as direct estimates of in vivo operator dose.

A further consideration relates to the dosimetric quantity employed. Occupational exposure to the eye lens is conventionally expressed as the personal dose equivalent, Hp(3). In the present study, however, OSL dosimeters were calibrated in terms of absorbed dose (µGy). Accordingly, these measurements should not be converted to Hp(3) values or extrapolated to annual occupational dose limits. Instead, they should be viewed as a relative measure for comparing the effectiveness of different operator positions and protective strategies under identical experimental conditions. Because all measurements were acquired using the same dosimetric approach, the comparative validity of the results remains unaffected by the choice of dosimetric quantity. Taken together, these findings support the recommendation of the EADMFR guideline [3] that handheld X-ray devices should be used only when clinically justified. When their use is unavoidable, operators should maximize their distance from the patient and maintain an appropriate device angulation, approximately 45° in the present experimental conditions, to reduce radiation exposure. The present findings also draw attention to the potential benefit of leaded protective eyewear, a precaution not specifically addressed in the current guideline [3], and suggest that its routine use during handheld radiography merits further consideration. Further investigations incorporating an anthropomorphic patient phantom positioned within the primary beam, together with Hp(3)-calibrated dosimetry, are needed to quantify the contribution of patient-generated scatter and to determine whether the relationships observed in this experimental study remain valid under conditions that more closely resemble routine clinical practice.

Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. First, although the operator phantom closely approximates human anatomy, the experiments were performed under controlled phantom conditions and may not fully replicate clinical practice; variations in patient anatomy, soft tissue composition, and operator movement could all influence the distribution of scattered radiation. Second, only a single type of leaded protective eyewear and a single handheld device were evaluated, which limits the generalizability of the results across different equipment. Third, although the OSL dosimeters were calibrated in absorbed dose, the operational quantity recommended for evaluating regulatory eye-lens dose limits is Hp(3); future studies should therefore incorporate Hp(3)-based dosimetry for direct comparison with occupational dose thresholds. Fourth, while OSL dosimeters provide reliable comparative dose assessment under controlled experimental conditions, complementary measurements using small-volume ionization chambers could provide independent absolute dose verification in future investigations. Finally, the experiments were restricted to predefined positions, and hand exposure and the potential role of lead gloves were not assessed, representing additional limitations. Despite these constraints, the controlled experimental design provided a reliable basis for comparative analysis and yielded valuable insights into strategies for reducing operator radiation exposure during handheld dental X-ray procedures.

Conclusion

In this phantom-based simulation, absorbed doses to the eye lens and sphenoid region were evaluated under different operator positions with and without protective eyewear. Within the tested configurations, the lowest dose values were observed in Position 3, corresponding to a downward device inclination of approximately 45° combined with leaded eyewear. Across all experimental conditions, the use of protective eyewear was associated with a consistent reduction in absorbed dose at both anatomical sites. These findings are limited to the specific geometry and exposure settings used in this study and should be interpreted within the boundaries of this simulation model.
