Durable complete metabolic responses to androgen receptor-targeted therapy in metastatic primary cutaneous apocrine carcinoma: a case report and literature review
Authors: Jindřich Kopecký, Alžebeta Kořená, Peter Priester, Jiří Doležal, Petra Kašparová, Aneta Kyllarová
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary cutaneous apocrine carcinoma (PCAC) is an exceptionally rare adnexal malignancy with limited evidence to guide systemic treatment. Androgen receptor (AR) expression is common, but durable responses to AR-targeted therapy have been reported only in isolated cases. This case highlights a uniquely reproducible and profound complete metabolic response to AR blockade in metastatic PCAC, offering valuable insight into its potential hormonal dependence.Case presentationA 57-year-old man with AR-strong PCAC of the axilla developed metastatic relapse involving mediastinal and axillary lymph nodes, lung lymphangitic spread, and a lytic vertebral lesion. Introduction

Primary cutaneous apocrine carcinoma (PCAC) is a rare malignant adnexal neoplasm derived from apocrine sweat glands. It typically arises in regions with high apocrine gland density, particularly the axilla, anogenital area, or scalp, but it may occur at virtually any cutaneous site. Due to its extreme rarity (estimated annual incidence 0.18–0.23 per 1,000,000 among Caucasians), its biological behaviour, staging, and evidence-based systemic management remain poorly defined [1].

Histologically, PCAC presents as an adenocarcinoma with glandular and ductal differentiation, often mimicking metastatic breast carcinoma, which necessitates thorough immunohistochemical and molecular workup for accurate diagnosis. Immunophenotypically, most PCACs express cytokeratin 7, epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), and gross cystic disease fluid protein-15 (GCDFP-15) [2]. Estrogen and progesterone receptors are typically negative, while androgen receptor (AR) expression is positive in up to 75–100% of tumours [3], a finding that has attracted increasing attention for potential therapeutic targeting.

The current therapeutic paradigm is primarily surgical. Wide local excision with negative margins, accompanied by lymph node dissection when indicated, remains the mainstay of management. Adjuvant radiotherapy may be employed for close or positive margins or for regional nodal involvement [2, 4, 5]. However, no standardised systemic regimen has been established for unresectable or metastatic disease.

Chemotherapeutic approaches—taxanes, anthracyclines, or platinum compounds—have produced only transient or partial responses, and HER2-targeted agents apply only to a minority of HER2-amplified tumours [2, 5, 6].

Recent evidence suggests that PCAC shares molecular features with apocrine-differentiated carcinomas of other organs, notably salivary duct carcinoma and the apocrine subtype of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), both of which are characterised by high AR expression and potential sensitivity to antiandrogen therapy. The case by Collette et al. described a patient with metastatic PCAC achieving a durable response to antiandrogen therapy, first with bicalutamide and later with enzalutamide [6]. This report remains one of the few documented instances of systemic response in metastatic PCAC.

We present a case of a 57-year-old man with metastatic AR-strong PCAC of the axilla who achieved repeated complete metabolic remissions confirmed by PET-CT under androgen receptor blockade with enzalutamide, including a second remission after treatment reinduction following toxicity-related interruption. This case further underscores the androgen dependence of this rare malignancy and the therapeutic potential of AR inhibition.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old man presented in November 2021 with a painless left axillary mass. A core biopsy revealed a poorly differentiated apocrine carcinoma (ER 0%, PR 0%, Ki-67 20%) showing strong, diffuse nuclear expression of androgen receptors (Figs. 1 and 2). The tumour was PD-L1-negative and mismatch-repair proficient, with no driver alterations identified by next-generation sequencing. A major diagnostic challenge at the time of primary diagnosis was distinguishing primary cutaneous apocrine carcinoma from metastatic apocrine breast carcinoma, necessitating an extended IHC panel and clinical and radiologic correlation. In our case, the patient had no personal history of breast cancer, and a comprehensive clinical evaluation, including physical examination and imaging studies, revealed no evidence of a breast primary tumour.

In January 2022, the patient underwent a wide excision and axillary lymphadenectomy with flap reconstruction. Histopathology confirmed a grade 2 apocrine carcinoma (tubules 3, atypia 3, mitoses 1 = 7) with a micropapillary component, extensive angioinvasion, and nine of twelve lymph nodes involved. Margins were microscopically positive. A re-excision in March 2022 showed residual carcinoma within 0.2 mm of the deep and lateral margins. Adjuvant radiotherapy to the chest wall, supraclavicular fossa, and axilla (60 Gy in 30 fractions) was completed by June 2022.

In October 2022, PET-CT revealed new right (contralateral) mediastinal, axillary and hilar lymphadenopathy. A right axillary node biopsy and subsequent excision confirmed metastasis consistent with the previously diagnosed apocrine carcinoma (ER 0%, PR 0%, HER2 2+ non-amplified, Ki-67 35%, strong AR positivity). Tumour-infiltrating immune cells expressed PD-L1 in ≥1% of the tumour area (IC ≥ 1%). Additional findings included multiple foci of increased FDG uptake in the upper lobe of the right lung, consistent with carcinomatous lymphangitis, and a small lytic lesion involving the body and pedicle of the fifth thoracic vertebra (Th5), compatible with osseous metastasis (Fig. 3).

Given the lack of standard systemic options and strong AR expression, enzalutamide 160 mg daily (Xtandi) was initiated in January 2023, together with androgen blockade with goserelin 10.8 mg every 3 months (Reseligo), similar to the prostate cancer treatment protocol, which initially administers bicalutamide to prevent a "tumour flare". The patient achieved a complete metabolic and radiologic response (CR) confirmed by PET-CT in April 2023, which persisted on serial imaging through January 2024 (Fig. 4). Adverse events included mild fatigue and hypertension, managed conservatively. Because of cumulative fatigue and vasomotor symptoms (even with dose reduction), therapy was temporarily suspended in January 2024.

Whole-body and fused PET-CT images demonstrating (A) multiple hypermetabolic right-sided mediastinal, axillary, and hilar lymph nodes (arrows) in October 2022; B complete response after systemic therapy in April 2023

A follow-up PET-CT in January 2025 showed mild progression of mediastinal nodes (non-FDG-avid) and a new cutaneous lesion over the left shoulder. Excision in February 2025 confirmed recurrent apocrine carcinoma with extensive lymphangioinvasion and positive deep margins. Given the residual disease and surgical limitations, reinduction therapy with enzalutamide at full dose 160 mg daily plus goserelin (10.8 mg every 3 months) was initiated in April 2025. By June 2025, PET-CT again demonstrated a complete metabolic and radiologic response, which persisted in October 2025 (Fig. 5). Laboratory evaluations—including complete blood count, renal and hepatic profiles, and inflammatory markers—remained within reference ranges throughout treatment. The patient’s comorbidities included haemophilia A (FVIII activity 9%), hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia. Despite mild haemophilia, all invasive procedures were completed uneventfully under hematologic supervision. At his most recent follow-up in November 2025, the patient remained asymptomatic (ECOG 1), with controlled blood pressure and no biochemical and radiological evidence of disease activity. Freely given, informed consent to participate in this case study was obtained from the patient in accordance with institutional and international ethical standards.

Discussion

PCAC represents less than 0.005% of cutaneous malignancies [1, 2]. It typically presents in the fifth to seventh decade, with a slight male predominance. The axilla is the most frequent site due to its dense apocrine glands, though the anogenital area, scalp, and trunk have also been described [4, 7, 8]. In one of the most extensive retrospective series with PCAC, the median overall survival was 55 months for localised disease, 33 months for positive lymph node disease, and 14.5 months in the metastatic setting [9].

Histologically, PCAC shows glandular and ductal differentiation with decapitation secretion and eosinophilic cytoplasm. A key diagnostic challenge is distinguishing primary PCAC from cutaneous metastases of breast carcinoma, as they share morphologic and immunophenotypic features. Immunohistochemistry is thus essential: PCACs usually express CK7, EMA, and GCDFP-15, are negative for ER and PR, and strongly express androgen receptor (AR) [4, 10, 11]. HER2 positivity has been reported inconsistently, usually in a small subset [12].

Surgical excision with histologically negative margins remains the cornerstone of treatment for PCAC. Given its locally aggressive nature and tendency toward lymphovascular invasion, wide local excision with margins of at least 1–2 cm is generally recommended. Regional lymph node dissection is indicated in cases of clinically or radiologically involved nodes or when sentinel node biopsy confirms microscopic metastasis. Nodal involvement is reported in 50–70% of cases and is the most significant prognostic factor for recurrence and overall survival [9, 13].

Adjuvant radiotherapy is commonly used for close or positive margins, lymphovascular or perineural invasion, and nodal metastases. Although prospective data are lacking, retrospective series suggest improved locoregional control. Recommended indications include large (>5 cm), poorly differentiated tumours, multiple nodal metastases (≥4 LNs), or extranodal extension [4]. Adjuvant doses typically exceed 60 Gy, comparable to those used for head-and-neck squamous carcinoma. However, as in our case, despite adjuvant treatment, the recurrence rate is high.

By contrast, adjuvant chemotherapy offers no proven survival benefit. Cytotoxic regimens based on anthracyclines or taxanes have been extrapolated from breast carcinoma, but responses are transient and toxicity significant [6].

For advanced or metastatic PCAC, evidence guiding systemic therapy remains extremely limited, mainly consisting of single case reports and small retrospective series. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy—most commonly taxane- or platinum-based regimens—has been employed empirically, often by analogy to triple-negative or apocrine breast carcinoma. Objective responses, however, are infrequent and typically transient, underlining the need for biologically guided treatment strategies [5, 6]. Therefore, molecular profiling is indispensable in advanced disease. Beyond standard immunohistochemistry (AR, ER, PR, HER2, EGFR), next-generation sequencing can identify actionable alterations, most often within the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [14]. These findings support experimental use of PI3K or mTOR inhibitors (alpelisib, everolimus) when relevant mutations are present. Although most PCACs are microsatellite-stable and have low TMB [15], detection of MMR deficiency, BRCA1/2 mutations, or high PD-L1 expression may open therapeutic options for immune checkpoint inhibitors or PARP inhibitors [15, 16]. For HER2-amplified tumours, targeted agents such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, or lapatinib have shown clinical benefit [17, 18].

Expression of hormonal receptors is well documented in apocrine-eccrine carcinomas [14]. Since hormonal modulation plays an essential role in the prevention and treatment of many hormone-positive carcinomas, and hormonal receptor expression can have treatment implications, it is crucial to know the hormonal status, including androgen receptor (AR) expression. In contrast to rare cases of targetable receptors or pathways, AR expression is highly prevalent in apocrine carcinomas, including PCACs [3, 14]. The consistent expression of androgen receptor and lack of ER/PR and HER2 amplification define PCAC as a “molecular apocrine” carcinoma, suggesting a biologic dependency on AR signalling and providing a strong rationale for antiandrogen therapy in advanced disease [6].

The first documented durable systemic response was reported by Collette et al., who described a patient with metastatic AR-positive PCAC achieving long-term remission on enzalutamide [6]. Our present case contributes further to this limited body of evidence by documenting two separate PET-CT–confirmed complete responses under enzalutamide therapy—one at initial presentation and one after reinduction of treatment. Such durable, reproducible metabolic remissions strongly suggest that PCAC is truly dependent on AR signalling. The observed therapeutic sensitivity aligns with molecular findings indicating that PCAC shares the “molecular apocrine” signature, characterised by AR activation and the absence of ER/PR signalling, similar to the apocrine subtype of TNBC [19]. Enzalutamide’s mechanism—preventing AR nuclear translocation and DNA binding—therefore represents a rational intervention. Collectively, these observations identify AR blockade (e.g., enzalutamide, bicalutamide, abiraterone) as the most biologically rational and clinically effective systemic option in AR-positive PCAC. Our case aligns with this concept, demonstrating repeated complete metabolic remission following enzalutamide, even after treatment interruption and re-induction, with overall survival over 24 months since metastatic disease.

Contrary to the case reported by Collette et al. [6], we combined enzalutamide with goserelin (an androgen-deprivation therapy). The reason for this combination was based on extrapolation results from prostate cancer showing better survival rates and slower disease progression when an androgen-deprivation treatment is also used [20]. Goserelin suppresses testosterone production, while enzalutamide blocks androgen receptors. Together, they provide a more complete blockage of the androgen signalling pathway. However, combining the two may increase the risk or severity of specific side effects such as hot flashes, tiredness, and joint pain.

Our patient’s clinical course highlights several aspects that are both diagnostically and therapeutically exceptional within the context of PCAC. First, he achieved a complete and reproducible metabolic response confirmed by serial PET-CT imaging, demonstrating not only radiologic remission but also full metabolic quiescence under AR blockade with enzalutamide and goserelin. Durable responses of this depth have rarely been documented in the literature. Second, following a toxicity-related interruption of therapy, disease control was successfully re-established upon treatment reinduction, indicating preserved tumour dependence on AR signalling and renewed sensitivity to pharmacologic blockade even after a period of drug withdrawal. This observation supports the feasibility of a “stop-and-go” approach, potentially allowing treatment holidays in selected patients while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. Third, the patient tolerated long-term AR blockade remarkably well, despite significant comorbidities including haemophilia A, hypertension, and obesity. The patient reported that the treatment allowed him to return to an active life with minimal symptoms. He expressed relief after each PET-CT confirmation of remission and noted that fatigue during therapy interruption was manageable. The favourable safety profile and the absence of serious adverse events demonstrate that enzalutamide-based therapy can be delivered safely even in complex patients, provided that close monitoring is ensured.

At the same time, this case underscores that prolonged hormonal manipulation, while effective, may gradually impact quality of life, with fatigue, vasomotor symptoms, and metabolic effects being common cumulative toxicities. Clinicians should thus balance disease control against quality-of-life preservation and individual tolerance thresholds.

Strengths of this report include comprehensive molecular profiling and repeated PET-CT documentation. Limitations include the single-patient nature of the observation and the absence of molecular biomarkers explaining AR dependence. Importantly, our experience highlights the potential reversibility of AR-driven oncogenic activity and the critical need to confirm persistent hormonal dependence through repeat histopathological and immunohistochemical reassessment—especially at relapse or progression. In such scenarios, rebiopsy can help distinguish between continued AR dependence and potential phenotypic evolution toward AR-independent clones, which could necessitate alternative systemic strategies.

Finally, this case exemplifies how even in an ultra-rare malignancy with no established standard of care, targeted therapy based on molecular and receptor profiling can yield durable, complete, and repeatable remissions. However, it also emphasises that treatment efficacy appears conditional upon continued drug exposure—with relapse expected upon withdrawal—further supporting the concept that AR signalling remains a central oncogenic driver in at least a subset of apocrine carcinomas.

Conclusion

Primary cutaneous apocrine carcinoma is an exceptionally rare adnexal malignancy with unpredictable biological behaviour and no standardised systemic treatment. This case provides real-world evidence that AR blockade with enzalutamide in combination with goserelin can induce profound and durable metabolic remissions in metastatic AR-positive PCAC, including successful re-challenge following treatment interruption. The reproducibility of complete responses after drug withdrawal and reinduction strongly supports the concept of persistent AR pathway dependence. It validates AR signalling as a central therapeutic target in this tumour entity.

From a broader clinical perspective, complete surgical excision with clear margins and selective use of adjuvant radiotherapy remain the cornerstone for localised disease. However, in advanced or inoperable cases, treatment must be guided by the tumour’s molecular and immunohistochemical profile. This approach enables rational treatment selection, access to basket clinical trials, or off-label use of targeted agents, which may provide meaningful benefit.

Finally, adopting a “stop-and-go” approach, supported by close clinical and imaging surveillance and, where feasible, confirmatory rebiopsy to assess ongoing AR expression, may help balance disease control and quality of life. Given the rarity of PCAC, international case registries and inclusion in molecularly driven basket trials are essential to better define therapeutic strategies.
