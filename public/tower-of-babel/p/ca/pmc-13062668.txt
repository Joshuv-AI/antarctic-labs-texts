Application of Enhanced Recovery After Surgery in high-risk patients undergoing colon cancer surgery: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Reem Mohammed H. Alshiakh, In Kyeong Kim, Jung Hoon Bae, Yoon Suk Lee, In Kyu Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeEnhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols have demonstrated significant benefits, but their safety and effectiveness in high-risk patients remain unclear. This study evaluated clinical postoperative outcomes in elderly patients and those with comorbidities undergoing colon cancer surgery.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 1,035 patients who underwent elective colon cancer surgery from 2017 to 2022. A standardized 25-element ERAS protocol was implemented across all groups. Patients were categorized into a control group (aged <70 years without comorbidities, n = 534) and high-risk groups including elderly patients (≥70 years, n = 312) and those with cardiac (n = 60), pulmonary (n = 81), renal (n = 24), or hepatic disease (n = 24).ResultsERAS compliance rates were significantly lower in all high-risk groups compared to controls (79.39% ± 10.9%), with renal disease patients showing the most pronounced reduction (69.69% ± 17.0%). Post-hoc analysis revealed that only renal disease patients demonstrated significantly higher complication rates (45.8% vs. 18.9%, P < 0.01). Despite lower compliance, multivariate analysis revealed ERAS compliance ≥70% as the strongest independent predictor of both length of stay (odds ratio [OR], 0.139; P < 0.001) and complications (OR, 0.298; P < 0.001), substantially outweighing patient risk factors. After adjusting for treatment factors, patient comorbidity groups showed no independent association with clinical outcomes.ConclusionERAS protocols can be successfully implemented in high-risk patients with appropriate attention to compliance optimization. Treatment factors, particularly ERAS adherence, consistently outweigh patient risk factors in determining outcomes, and well-tailored ERAS implementation should be considered with high-risk patients for perioperative care.

INTRODUCTION

Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols have revolutionized perioperative care in colorectal surgery, demonstrating consistent benefits in reducing complications, shortening hospital stays, and improving patient satisfaction [1]. However, the implementation and effectiveness of ERAS protocols in high-risk patient populations, including elderly patients and those with significant comorbidities, remains a topic of considerable clinical interest.

Traditional perioperative care has often approached high-risk patients with caution, sometimes excluding them from enhanced recovery protocols due to concerns about safety and tolerability. Patients with cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal diseases are considered high-risk for colorectal cancer surgery, with established associations between comorbidities and increased postoperative complications [2]. Similarly, advanced age has been recognized as an independent risk factor for perioperative morbidity and mortality [3].

Recent evidence, however, suggests that ERAS protocols may be particularly beneficial for high-risk populations who have the most to gain from optimized perioperative care. A systematic review of 16 studies concluded that ERAS application in elderly patients led to reduced postoperative complications and shortened length of hospital stay (LOS) [4]. They have demonstrated the feasibility and safety of ERAS implementation in elderly and high-risk patients.

Despite this growing evidence, significant gaps remain in our understanding of ERAS implementation in high-risk populations. Only limited studies have systematically evaluated adherence patterns and their relationship to outcomes across different risk groups. A multi-center study from Italy including 706 patients undergoing elective colorectal cancer surgery noted significant variation in ERAS implementation among centers, highlighting the need for studies in centers with standardized protocols and systematic compliance assessment [5].

Compliance with the ERAS protocol plays a crucial role in the successful implementation and outcomes optimization [16]. Our institution has a standardized ERAS pathway for both highvs.and low-risk patient groups, with systematic compliance assessment throughout the perioperative process [78].

This study aims to assess the implementation and clinical effectiveness of a standardized ERAS protocol in high-risk patients undergoing colon cancer surgery. Specifically, we evaluate ERAS compliance patterns across different risk groups and examine the relationship between compliance, patient characteristics, and postoperative outcomes.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a retrospective cohort study with retrospective analysis of electronic medical records. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Catholic University of Korea, Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, Seoul, Korea (No. KC23RISI0717). This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and written informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature.

We reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent elective colon cancer surgery at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital from January 2017 to December 2022. All patients were managed using our ERAS protocol. Inclusion criteria were patients aged 18 years or older undergoing elective colon cancer surgery with curative resection. Patients requiring intensive monitoring after surgery in the intensive care unit were excluded from analysis.

Definitions of the elderly and comorbidities

Elderly patients were defined as those aged 70 years or older, a threshold commonly used in colon cancer surgery to distinguish patients with potentially increased perioperative risk. This age cutoff was selected based on evidence demonstrating increased surgical risk and potential differences in recovery after surgery [89].

Patients were categorized into distinct groups: the control group comprised patients under 70 years old without comorbidities, representing the low-risk population. High-risk patients were defined as those with comorbidities, including cardiac diseases such as angina, arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and other known cardiac conditions; pulmonary diseases encompassed asthma, pulmonary tuberculosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and interstitial lung diseases; renal diseases, including chronic kidney disease (CKD) above stage 3, end-stage renal disease, or previous history of nephrectomy; hepatic diseases such as hepatitis and cirrhosis.

The elderly group included all patients aged 70 years or older, regardless of comorbidity status. In subsequent subgroup analyses, we redefined and selected patients who were both aged 70 years or older and had comorbidities for inclusion in these analyses.

Implementation of our ERAS protocol

Our institution has implemented and modified ERAS protocols based on ERAS Society guidelines since 2017, with annual monitoring and feedback for continuous improvement [10]. Our multidisciplinary ERAS team consists of physicians, ward nurses, nutritionists, pharmacists, and specialized ERAS nurses who provide comprehensive support throughout the perioperative care.

The standardized protocol included 25 evidence-based elements across 3 phases. Preoperative items included education and counseling, nutritional care, oral carbohydrate loading, deep vein thrombosis (DVT) prophylaxis, prophylactic antibiotics, and medication for pain control. Intraoperative items were hypothermia prophylaxis, fluid restriction, nausea and vomiting prophylaxis, and intraoperative pain control. Postoperative items were stimulation of gut motility, chewing gum, nausea and vomiting prophylaxis, adequate pain control, early removal of surgical drain, early removal of urinary catheter, early removal of intravenous line, early mobilization, and early restarting of diet.

The multimodal pain management in our institution was as follows: preoperative medications for pain control included oral NSAIDs (celecoxib), acetaminophen, and gabapentin, administered 2 hours before surgery. Contraindications were gabapentin for the elderly patients older than 76 years, acetaminophen for elevated AST/ALT (more than 51 IU/L), and celecoxib for serum creatinine above 1.6 mg/dL. Intraoperative pain control was achieved through an ultrasound-guided transversus abdominis block with ropivacaine. Postoperative pain management involved administration of patient-controlled analgesia through the intravenous route and oral NSAIDs (celecoxib), acetaminophen, and gabapentin [11].

Definitions of outcomes

The compliance rate was calculated as the percentage of successfully implemented elements relative to total applicable ERAS elements for each patient. High compliance was defined as ≥70% based on established literature and institutional experience.

Postoperative complications were graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification (CDC). Major complications were defined as CDC grade III or higher, requiring surgical, endoscopic, or radiological intervention. The comprehensive complication index (CCI) was calculated to capture overall morbidity burden, with scores ranging from 0 to 100.

LOS was calculated from the day of surgery to discharge, with prolonged stay defined as ≥5 days based on our institutional median values.

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary endpoint was the evaluation of postoperative outcomes, such as LOS and postoperative complications in each high-risk group.

The secondary endpoints were the correlation between postoperative outcomes and compliance, and we analyzed the differences between each group. We also examined whether there were differences in compliance between each group for each ERAS item.

Statistical analysis

Demographic, surgical, and postoperative data were retrospectively collected from electronic records. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 for primary analyses. Given the multiple group comparisons (5 high-risk vs. control), Bonferroni correction was applied with α = 0.01 (0.05/5) to control for Type I error inflation.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using analysis of variance or Kruskal-Wallis test as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher exact test.

For post-hoc pairwise comparisons following significant omnibus tests, individual group comparisons against the control group were performed using appropriate statistical tests (t-test for continuous variables, the chi-square or Fisher exact test for categorical variables) with Bonferroni-adjusted significance levels.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of prolonged LOS and complications. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate models. The final models included patient characteristics, surgical complexity indicators (operative time, >171 minutes; estimated blood loss [EBL], > 50 mL), and treatment factors (ERAS compliance, surgical approach).

Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for continuous variables and presented with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Forest plots were generated to visualize effect sizes with statistical power indication based on sample sizes. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 25.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

An analysis was conducted on 1,035 patients who underwent colon surgery, comprising 534 individuals in the control group and 501 high-risk patients. The high-risk patients were further categorized into 5 subgroups: elderly patients (n = 312), those with heart disease (n = 60), pulmonary disease (n = 81), renal disease (n = 24), and liver disease (n = 24).

After Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (α = 0.01), significant differences were observed between groups and controls in baseline characteristics (Table 1). All high-risk groups were significantly older than controls (P < 0.001), with the elderly group having the highest mean age (76.73 ± 5.4 years). American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA PS) classification differed significantly across groups (P < 0.001), with higher proportions of patients with ASA PS class III–IV in the cardiac (31.7%), pulmonary (16.0%), and renal (25.0%) disease groups compared with controls (3.4%).

Serum albumin levels were significantly lower in all high-risk groups compared to controls (4.37 ± 1.56 g/dL, P < 0.001), indicating compromised nutritional status. Tumor location distribution also differed significantly between groups (P = 0.002), though operative time and EBL showed no significant differences after correction.

ERAS compliance and postoperative outcomes

ERAS compliance was significantly lower in all high-risk groups compared to controls (Table 2). Mean compliance rates were 79.39% ± 10.9% in controls vs. 73.99% ± 11.7% in elderly, 73.84% ± 13.1% in cardiac, 75.57% ± 13.2% in pulmonary, 69.69% ± 17.0% in renal, and 72.85% ± 14.5% in liver groups (P < 0.001). The proportion of patients achieving high compliance (≥70%) was consistently lower across all high-risk groups: 82.0% in controls vs. 65.4% in elderly, 68.3% in cardiac, 70.4% in pulmonary, 50.0% in renal, and 66.7% in liver groups (P < 0.001).

Total complication rates within 30 days were significantly higher overall when comparing all groups (P = 0.009). Post-hoc analysis revealed that the renal disease group showed significantly higher complication rates (45.8%) compared to controls (18.9%; P < 0.01), while elderly (18.6%), cardiac (16.7%), and liver (33.3%) groups showed numerical increases that did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction. Major complications (CDC ≥III) did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (P = 0.184). The CCI remained significantly different across groups (P < 0.001), with higher severity scores in pulmonary (7.59 ± 18.2, P < 0.01), renal (9.97 ± 13.2, P < 0.01), and liver (7.78 ± 12.0, P < 0.01) groups compared to controls (3.48 ± 8.1).

LOS showed numerical trends toward prolongation in high-risk groups, though statistical significance was lost after multiple comparison correction (P = 0.046). Mean LOS was 4.90 ± 3.2 days in controls compared to prolonged stays in high-risk groups, with liver groups showing the longest stay (6.33 ± 4.9 days), followed by renal groups (5.88 ± 3.5 days).

Subgroup analysis: elderly patients with comorbidities

Subgroup analysis of elderly patients with specific comorbidities revealed consistent patterns despite small sample sizes (Tables 3, 4). After Bonferroni correction, significant differences persisted in age distribution (P < 0.001), ASA PS classes (P < 0.001), and serum albumin levels (P < 0.001) when comparing elderly subgroups to controls.

ERAS compliance remained significantly lower across all elderly subgroups (79.93% ± 10.9%) (P < 0.001). Specifically, compliance rates were 73.98% ± 11.7% in patients aged ≥70 years without comorbidities, 71.65% ± 14.1% in those with cardiac disease, 72.51% ± 13.6% in those with pulmonary disease, 68.39% ± 14.5% in those with renal disease, and 70.91% ± 16.2% in those with hepatic disease. Notably, the very small subgroups (≥70 years with renal disease, n = 13; ≥70 years with hepatic disease, n = 10) showed extremely wide CIs, and these findings should be interpreted as descriptive data only due to insufficient statistical power.

Effect size analysis

Forest plot analysis (Fig. 1) revealed consistently large negative effect sizes for ERAS compliance across all elderly subgroups compared to controls. Cohen’s d ranged from –1.19 to –1.61, indicating clinically meaningful differences beyond statistical significance. Groups with adequate sample sizes (n≥30) demonstrated narrow, precise CIs, while extremely small subgroups (n = 10–13) appropriately displayed wide intervals reflecting their limited statistical power. The consistency of large effect sizes across all groups suggests real clinical differences in ERAS implementation challenges, independent of statistical power limitations.

Multivariate analysis: treatment factors vs. patient risk factors

Comprehensive multivariate analysis including surgery-related factors revealed that treatment factors consistently outweighed patient risk factors in predicting LOS. After adjusting for all confounding variables, the final model identified independent predictors. Protective factors were high ERAS compliance (OR, 0.139; P < 0.001), minimally invasive surgery (OR, 0.327; P = 0.002), and female sex (OR, 0.705; P = 0.046). R isk factors were EBL ≥50 mL (OR, 2.144; P < 0.001) and operative t ime ≥171 m inutes (OR, 1.696; P = 0.003). Importantly, none of the patient comorbidity groups showed an independent association with prolonged LOS after adjusting for treatment factors (all P > 0.05), suggesting that clinical outcomes are primarily determined by modifiable treatment approaches rather than patient risk stratification (Table 5).

In complication predictors, multivariate analysis revealed remarkably consistent patterns with LOS analysis. High ERAS compliance (OR, 0.298; P < 0.001), female sex (OR, 0.399; P < 0.001), and minimally invasive surgery (OR, 0.411; P = 0.008) were protective factors, and EBL ≥50 mL (OR, 1.734; P = 0.005) was a risk factor.

Again, patient comorbidity groups demonstrated no independent association with complications after comprehensive confounding adjustment (all P > 0.05), reinforcing the primacy of treatment optimization over patient selection (Table 6).

Specific ERAS components

In preoperative ERAS elements compliance across all high-risk groups, there was no significant difference in preoperative counseling, DVT prophylaxis, and preoperative antibiotic use. Preoperative nutritional care and carbohydrate loading were significantly lower in the elderly group (98.5% vs. 9 4.7%, P = 0.004; 88.2% vs. 82.7%, P = 0.034), but compliance remained above 70%, respectively. The preoperative pain control was significantly lower for all high-risk patients compared to the control group (P < 0.001 for all).

In the intraoperative elements, intraoperative nausea, vomiting prophylaxis (98.0 % vs. 9 5.1%, P = 0.007), and intraoperative pain control (98.0% vs. 92.8%, P = 0.016) were compared to the control group.

In the postoperative elements, the use of postoperative pain control, removal of surgical drains, urinary catheters, and early removal of intravenous lines showed no significant differences among all high-risk patients. In patients with renal disease, the compliance rate for postoperative fluid restriction was significantly lower (76.9% vs. 50.0%, P = 0.006). Elderly patients showed significantly lower compliance in early ambulation, and there was no significant difference in dietary progress between the groups (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 1).

In the analysis of elderly patients with comorbidities, the number of factors contributing to low compliance was highest in elderly patients, followed by patients with renal disease (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This study provides compelling evidence for a paradigm shift from risk-based patient selection toward treatment optimization in perioperative care. Our comprehensive analysis demonstrates that ERAS compliance consistently emerges as the strongest predictor of clinical outcomes, substantially outweighing traditional patient risk factors.

The most striking finding of our analysis is the consistent predominance of ERAS compliance across both lengths of stay and complication outcomes. With odd ratios of 0.139 for prolonged LOS and 0.298 for complications, high ERAS compliance (≥70%) demonstrated protective effects of 86% and 70% respectively, even after comprehensive adjustment for surgical factors including operative time and EBL. These effect sizes substantially exceed those reported for many established perioperative interventions and suggest that protocol adherence represents a fundamental determinant of surgical outcomes.

This finding challenges the traditional focus on patient risk stratification and supports a treatment-centric approach to perioperative optimization. The consistency of these results across 2 distinct outcome measures, combined with large effect sizes that persist after extensive confounding control, provides robust evidence for the independent and predominant importance of protocol implementation.

Our multivariate analysis reveals a crucial insight into the relationship between patient characteristics and clinical outcomes. While high-risk patient groups initially demonstrated poorer outcomes in univariate analysis, these associations disappeared after adjusting for treatment factors, particularly ERAS compliance. This pattern suggests a mediation model where patient risk factors influence outcomes primarily through their effect on protocol implementation rather than through direct physiological mechanisms. This mediation framework has profound implications for clinical practice. Rather than viewing certain patient populations as inherently high-risk and potentially unsuitable for aggressive perioperative protocols, our findings suggest that the focus should shift toward understanding and addressing the barriers to protocol implementation in these populations. The observation that high-risk patients can achieve outcomes comparable to controls when ERAS compliance is optimized supports this treatment-optimization approach.

Our detailed analysis of individual ERAS components reveals specific areas where high-risk patients face implementation challenges. The most dramatic differences occurred in preoperative pain control protocols, with compliance rates dropping from 94.0% in controls to as low as 20.0% in the renal disease group. Similarly, postoperative mobilization showed consistently lower compliance across high-risk groups.

The significantly poorer compliance and outcomes observed in renal disease patients result from multiple interconnected factors. CKD fundamentally alters drug metabolism, creating contraindications to standard ERAS elements. Pharmacological limitation directly impacts preoperative pain medication compliance and may contribute to suboptimal pain control throughout the perioperative period [1213]. Fluid management represents another critical mechanism, as the standard ERAS principle of goal-directed fluid therapy becomes complex in patients with compromised renal function who are more susceptible to both fluid overload and dehydration [14]. Additionally, electrolyte imbalances common in CKD patients can affect cardiac function, wound healing, and overall recovery trajectory, contributing to extended hospital stays and higher complication rates [15].

From our multivariate analysis, these disease-specific challenges can be overcome through optimized overall protocol implementation. The observation that patient comorbidity groups lose their predictive value after adjusting for ERAS compliance suggests that comprehensive protocol adherence can offset disease-specific limitations. Therefore, the high compliance rates achieved in most high-risk groups, coupled with the comparable postoperative outcomes to the control group, suggest that the benefits of ERAS can be extended to a wider range of patients than previously thought. This finding has the potential to shift the paradigm of perioperative care for high-risk patients, moving away from traditional approaches towards evidence-based, standardized care pathways.

This study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the single-center observational design from a specialized institution limits the generalizability of our findings to diverse healthcare settings, particularly community hospitals or resource-limited environments. Second, certain subgroups, especially elderly patients with renal (n = 13) and liver (n = 10) comorbidities, are severely underpowered for definitive statistical inference and should be interpreted as exploratory findings requiring validation in larger cohorts. Third, our compliance measurement approach did not systematically distinguish between medical contraindications and system-level implementation failures, which may affect the interpretation of absolute compliance rates, though relative comparisons between groups remain valid. Fourth, while our multivariate models included surgical factors (operative time, EBL), unmeasured confounders such as anesthesia duration, surgeon experience, and detailed comorbidity severity indices may still influence our results. Fifth, the observational design precludes definitive causal inferences despite the strong associations observed, and the direction of causality between ERAS compliance and outcomes cannot be definitively established. Finally, our study focused on short-term outcomes (30-day complications, LOS) and did not evaluate long-term functional recovery or quality of life measures, which may be equally important in assessing the true impact of ERAS protocols in high-risk populations. Despite these limitations, the consistency and magnitude of our findings, combined with rigorous statistical methodology, including Bonferroni correction and effect size analysis, provide valuable preliminary evidence for the treatment-optimization paradigm that warrants validation in multi-center randomized controlled trials.

In conclusion, ERAS protocols can be safely and effectively implemented in elderly and high-risk patients undergoing colon cancer surgery, with most groups maintaining high compliance and achieving comparable postoperative outcomes. It also highlights the importance of tailoring ERAS protocols to specific patient needs, particularly in those with specific comorbidities. As we continue to refine and adapt ERAS strategies, we move closer to a truly patient-centered approach in perioperative care, one that optimizes outcomes for all patients, regardless of their risk profile.
