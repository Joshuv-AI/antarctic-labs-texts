Social devaluation and mental health among refugees in the Middle East
Authors: Frank Neuner, Rezhna Mohammed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Refugees who have experienced war and persecution often suffer from psychological disorders resulting from adverse events and living conditions. Current theories of traumatic stress focus on processes resulting from threats to physical integrity, with little consideration of social stressors.Objectives: In this study, we proposed that perceptions of social devaluation, which include specific emotions, appraisals, physiological responses, and behavioural impulses associated with devaluation, partially mediate the relationship between stress and psychological symptoms in refugees.Methods: We measured social devaluation in a survey of 511 Syrian refugees living in the Kurdistan region of Iraq, and related it to social and physical stressors and symptoms of depression and post-traumatic stress.Results: Using structural equation modelling, we confirmed the proposed mediating role of social devaluation in the relationship between social and traumatic stressors and symptoms of posttraumatic stress and depression.Conclusions: This study suggests that populations experiencing high levels of adversity may develop psychopathology not only due to psychological trauma caused by physical threats, but also due to processes of social traumatisation involving perceptions of devaluation.KEYWORDS: Refugees, social stressors, physical stressors, social devaluation, PTSD, depression

It is a persistent problem of humanity that people are forced to flee their homes from violence due to wars and political persecution. Each year several million people need to flee (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, 2023). While some displaced people find refuge in their home countries, the majority of refugees flee to regions adjacent to their home countries. Depending on the policies of the receiving countries, refugees are most often forced to live with restricted fundamental rights (e.g. limited freedom of movement and access to labour market and to health care). Refugees are commonly segregated from the host community and forced to stay in refugee accommodations, camps, or settlements, with limited opportunities for integration or participation (Hilbig & Riaz, 2022; Hynie, 2018). Many refugees experience this condition for years or even decades. They may only return home when it is safe, flee to other countries, or receive a permit to stay with proper rights.

It has been repeatedly documented that mental disorders are more common among refugees than in populations that are unaffected by mass violence in the context of war, terrorism or political persecution. In particular, high rates of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and depression have been repeatedly documented in various refugee populations across the globe (Bogic et al., 2015; Morina et al., 2018). These high rates of psychological disorders have been commonly attributed to the frequency and severity of traumatic experiences due to war and persecution (Steel et al., 2009). In fact, a clear dose–response relationship between trauma exposure and PTSD has been found in both, war-affected populations who stayed in their home countries, and refugees who fled to other countries (Neuner et al., 2004).

Among refugees, the amount of exposure to war and non-war related traumatic events seems to be the main predictor of both, PTSD and depression (Steel et al., 2002). However, on top of traumatic experiences before and during the war as well as due to the flight, refugees’ mental health is also impaired by stress experienced in exile. Aversive events and conditions in exile have generally been referred to as post-migration living difficulties (Silove et al., 1997), and include factors such as duration of stay, residency status, lack of social network or family support, financial problems, lack of employment and others stressors. Several studies have shown that such post-migration stressors contribute to mental disorders including PTSD and depression over and above the effect of pre-migration and peri-migration trauma exposure (Chen et al., 2017; Gleeson et al., 2020; Li et al., 2016).

In order to protect against and treat mental disorders, it is important to understand the mechanisms that translate stressful life experiences into mental disorders. There are several psychological and neurocognitive theories that offer explanations for the psychological damage caused by traumatic events, such as the so-called dual or multiple representation models (Brewin et al., 1996; Dalgleish, 2004) or the cognitive model (Ehlers & Clark, 2000). These theories share the assumption that the core of PTSD is a distorted memory of the traumatic event in terms of an excessive associative memory, fragmented autobiographic memory, and a maladaptive or shattered cognitive concept of the self (Brewin, 2011). These trauma-focused theories have been developed to explain PTSD resulting from events that meet the criteria for traumatic events, i.e. involving a threat to a person's life or limb in the form of a violation of physical or sexual integrity. As such, they are valuable in explaining the mechanisms of the commonly found relationship between traumatic events and PTSD. However, current trauma theories recognise that it is not soley the experience of threats to health and survival that causes PTSD, but also the social meaning attached to the event, emphasising the contribution of ‘secondary emotions’ (Brewin et al., 1996) such as guilt and shame that result from dysfunctional appraisals of the events (Ehlers & Clark, 2000).

Despite this consideration of social emotions, contemporary trauma-focused theories, due to their emphasis on posttraumatic stress disorder, are inadequate in explaining the traumatising effects of events that do not meet the trauma criterion as defined in diagnostic manuals. In challenging circumstances, occurrences involving social exclusion and degradation may exceed the nonspecific stress that typically accompany traumatic events. In such cases, the social emotions elicited by these stressors may emerge as the ‘primary’ emotions. For refugees, for example, the stressors experienced after migration are predominantly associated with social threats, such as exclusion, separation, and discrimination, rather than life threats (Hynie, 2018). Several recent socio-ecological models of refugee mental health highlight the fact that it is not only past war trauma, but also ongoing stressors that contribute to mental disorders among refugees (Miller & Rasmussen, 2017; Tay & Silove, 2017). While these frameworks conceptualise the types of environmental events and conditions that are associated with impaired mental health among refugees, they do not provide an explicit explanation of how these events cause or maintain PTSD and depression in the individual person.

To account for the contribution of social stressors, we have extended current trauma memory theories to include processes of social traumatisation outlined in the Physical and Social Trauma (PAST) theory (Neuner, 2023). Despite the growing recognition of social emotions in trauma responses, most contemporary models remain anchored in threats to physical integrity. The construct of social devaluation broadens this lens by conceptualising a multicomponent defensive response – comprising shame, perceptions of rejection or inferiority, autonomic blunting, and inhibited action tendencies – that arises from repeated social threat. This state captures the affective imprint of experiences such as discrimination, abandonment, or humiliation. It extends dual-representation (Brewin et al., 1996) and cognitive (Ehlers & Clark, 2000) trauma models by specifying how social-threat memories – not only fear-based memories – are encoded and linked to depressive and post-traumatic symptom clusters. The concept draws on social rank theory (Gilbert & Allan, 1998) and positions threats to status and belonging as central to trauma-related psychopathology.

A more detailed theoretical and empirical elaboration of social devaluation is provided in a different study, in which the construct of social devaluation was operationalised and validated across multiple populations using the Social Devaluation Questionnaire (SODEQ; Neuner et al., 2025). In short, the development of the SODEQ is based on the concept that emotions are evolved affective programmes that help individuals respond to environmental threats. This follows the common understanding that emotions represent a specific combination of thoughts, physiological responses and behavioural impulses. While basic emotions such as fear and anger are universally recognised, more complex emotional states, such as social devaluation, lack clear verbal labels. The SODEQ aims to measure these nuanced states by combining theoretically associated labels that are verbally accessible (e.g. shame) with descriptions of thoughts, bodily reactions and behaviours.

Based on this framework, we aimed to test the hypothesis that the affective state of social devaluation mediates the relationship between stress exposure and psychopathology among refugees. We had the opportunity to assess these indicators in a survey of refugees who fled the war in Syria to the Kurdistan Region of Iraq and have been living in refugee camps for over a decade. The majority of these refugees fled the civil war and terrorism in Syria and have been living in isolated camps since late 2012. In this context, these refugees have faced both physical threats during the war in Syria and ongoing social threats due to their flight and life as refugees. While some facilities such as basic health care and schooling are provided in these camps, the rights of refugees have been restricted due to limited employment opportunities and the need to obtain permission to leave the camps. Refugees have also faced loss of valued social roles, loss of important life projects, social isolation, separation from family, and lack of environmental mastery. For example, many, if not most, of the refugees were homeowners, business owners, or at least had jobs and were able to provide for themselves and their loved ones before the war broke out, only to find themselves in tents, dependent on aid agencies for their next meal, and with very few rights. In addition, the social networks of the vast majority of refugees were damaged, as they often had to leave relatives behind or watch others flee to other countries, mostly in Europe. In this context, we assessed threats to physical and social integrity through simple event checklists, counting the number of event types they had been exposed to, and relating these stressors to perceptions of social devaluation as well as mental health outcomes.

Methods

Participants

This study uses data from a survey of N = 511 adult refugees living in four camps (Darashakran, Basirma, Kawargosk, and Qushtapa) in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq. The main aim of the survey was to gain knowledge relevant for the evaluation of psychosocial activities in the camp, which also determined sample size and selection of participants. The camps host refugees who had fled from the civil war and the occupation by ISIS terrorists. The refugee camps are isolated by a fence from the neighbouring communities and consist of small self-built houses as well as basic infrastructure including shops, schools and primary health care centres. The refugees are not allowed to leave the camp without permit from the authorities. Participants were randomly selected from within the camp. Enumerators made door-to-door visits to randomly selected households, working from lists of camp households. One adult from each selected household was randomly chosen to participate in the interview. None of the selected individuals refused to take part in the interview. Mean age of the selected participants was M = 40.0 years (SD = 12.5), 59% of the sample specified their sex as female, 40% as male and two individuals did not report their sex. The majority of participants were married (80%), the households reported a median income of 200,000 Iraqi dinar, which is equivalent to about 150 US$.

Measures

Depression and posttraumatic stress

In this study, psychopathology was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) and the Refugee Health Screener 13 (RHS-13). The PHQ-9 is a highly cited and widely used instrument that assesses each of the nine DSM-IV criteria for major depression on a scale ranging from ‘0’ (not at all) to ‘3’ (almost every day). We used the validated Arabic version of the PHQ-9 (AlHadi et al., 2017). To assess the mental health of refugees, the RHS-13 (Hollifield et al., 2013) was administered. The RHS-13 is a 13-item short form of the well-validated RHS-15 screening instrument. It includes items on somatisation, anxiety, depression, and PTSD that can be answered on a 5-point scale (0 = ‘not at all’ to 4 = ‘extremely’). The instrument has been validated in different contexts (Kaltenbach et al., 2017; Schmidt et al., 2023) and is available in different translations, including Arabic, which was used for this study.

This study aimed to determine the contribution of perceived social devaluation to depression and posttraumatic stress disorder. For several reasons, we decided to select single items rather than the full questionnaires to represent psychopathology. First, as a transdiagnostic screening instrument, the RHS-13 consists of items representing posttraumatic stress but also somatic complaints and anhedonia rather than measuring more specific psychopathological entities. Secondly, beyond the conceptual relatedness, in particular in the area of negative self-appraisal, single items of the SODEQ, the PHQ-9 and the RHS-13 are semantically overlapping, which may contribute to spurious associations. Thirdly, for the purpose of identifying specific relationships, we aimed at increasing the contrast between depression and posttraumatic-stress by focusing on characteristic items and excluding overlapping features of both psychological disorders, such as anhedonia. As a consequence, we focused on negative mood (PHQ item 1) and loss of interest (PHQ item 2) to represent depression, and reexperiencing (RHS item 10) and physical reactions to reminders of traumatic events (RHS item 11) to represent posttraumatic stress. To test the validity of this selection of items, we performed a factor analysis of all psychopathology items of RHS and PHQ. Bartlett’s test of sphericity ensured that the correlation matrix was not random (χ2(190) = 6604.02, p < .001) and the KMO statistic reached an overall MSA of .95. The minimum of Velcier’s Minimum Average Potential indicated a three-factor solution as the best fit. All PHQ items loaded on one factor, obviously representing a depression factor. Consistent with previous factor analyses of the PHQ, the first two items showed the highest factor loadings (.80 and .71 after oblimin rotation). Another factor, consisting of four RHS items, consisted of symptoms of posttraumatic stress, with RHS items 10 and 11 showing highest factor loadings (.86 and .88, after rotation). The remaining RHS items represented somatic and unspecific items and loaded predominantly on a third factor.

Social devaluation

The Social Devaluation Questionnaire (SODEQ; see supplementary document) is a newly developed 14-item instrument designed to assess the subjective experience of social devaluation related to a stressful event or condition. The items are designed to capture related emotions (e.g. shame, humiliation, degradation), appraisals (‘I had the impression that … ’), behavioural impulses (‘I felt the urge to … ’), and physiological responses (‘I was frozen’). To reflect the concept of blocked defensive responses, behavioural items included urges to fight, flee, or withdraw, while physiological items assessed paralysis and numbness. The questionnaire was pre-tested with therapists and clinical researchers in both healthy and clinical populations, including trauma patients and refugees, and tested in different contexts (Neuner et al., 2025). The final version of the SODEQ consisted of 14 items, each formulated as a self-report. The response format is a five-point scale ranging from 0 to 4, with identical anchors 0 = ‘not at all,’ 1 = ‘a little,’ 2 = ‘moderately,’ 3 = ‘quite a bit,’ and 4 = ‘extremely’. Because the SODEQ assesses the affective response to a specific event, condition, or period of time, the SODEQ requires that a reference event or condition be predefined in the instructions. The SODEQ was originally developed in German and translated into English and Arabic by clinical experts familiar with the concept of social devaluation, including a standard procedure of instrument translation based on translation and blinded back-translation. For the purposes of this study, the Arabic translation of the SODEQ was used. The reference condition was ‘being a refugee’. In a validation study (Neuner et al. 2025), the SODEQ had reached a Cronbach’s α in different samples ranging between .87 and .94, in the sample of this survey α was .94.

Physical and social stressors

The assessment of physical and social stressors was based on two different instruments. Adverse and traumatic life events were assessed with the War Adversity Exposure Checklist (WAEC), that had been developed to assess traumatic and stressful events in the context of the war in the Middle East (Ibrahim et al., 2018). To minimise common-method variance between stressors and social devaluation, the former were recorded as binary exposure indicators (‘yes’/‘no’), whereas the latter were rated on a five-point Likert scale reflecting subjective experience. Furthermore, item wording was purposefully non-overlapping (events vs. internal states). The WAEC is a self-report checklist that consists of 26 items assessing the number of adverse experiences related to family and organised violence, and general lifetime traumatic experiences such as sexual violence, accidents, or natural disasters. From this instrument, we chose those event types (witnessed or experienced) that fulfil the criteria of traumatic events according to the DSM-5 or present predominantly a threat to the physical integrity (health or survival) of a person to create a scale of Physical Stressors. The event types represented by this scale are presented in Figure 1. The score of this variable resulted from the number of items affirmed by the respondent. A second scale was developed for Social Stressors (see supplementary document). This scale consisted of stressors that are not related to physical threats or sexual violence, but to a threat to social motives of belonging (i.e. loss) or status (i.e. discrimination). This scale was constructed from single items from the WAEC, and complemented by social stressors that had been commonly reported by the refugees in the camp in pilot interviews that were carried out with refugees for the preparation of this survey (Figure 2).

Procedure

The data used in this study were collected as part of an evaluation of a psychosocial support programme provided by the NGO Un Ponte Per (UPP). One of the authors (RM) of this study trained the local psychologists of the MHPSS team to conduct this evaluation. The 5-day training was conducted in Arabic. The psychologists were introduced to the research tools and conducted simulated interviews through role-playing during the training. Three of the local psychologists had psychology degrees from Syria, and the fourth had an engineering degree. All team members had been extensively trained by UPP for nearly a decade and worked under the supervision of a clinical supervisor and a clinical consultant. Interviews were conducted either in the participant's home or at one of the MHPSS centres. At the beginning of the face-to-face interviews, a detailed informed consent form was read to the participants, informing them of the risks and benefits of the study and their right to withdraw. Participants were also given the opportunity to ask questions. The interview was conducted only with the verbal consent of the participant. All interviews were conducted in the Syrian dialect of Arabic. All participants who were approached agreed to be interviewed. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Bielefeld.

Data analysis

To test the mediating role of social devaluation in the relationship between stressful events and psychopathology, we calculated a structural equation model (SEM) using the lavaan package within the R (R Core Team, 2023) environment. Given that a visual inspection of the histograms indicated that some single variables were not normally distributed, we used the robust maximum likelihood estimator (MLR) in all structural equation models to obtain standard errors and fit indices that are robust to deviations from multivariate normality. In this model, traumatic stressors and social stressors were entered as separate, but potentially interdependent observed predictor variables. These predictor variables were regressed onto social devaluation (as observed variable). In turn, social devaluation was regressed onto the outcome variables depression and posttraumatic stress, that were entered as latent variables, each represented by two items from the questionnaires (see instrument section for the selection of items). To determine the direct effect of stress exposure on the outcomes, traumatic and social stressors were also regressed onto the outcome variables. The residuals of the latent variables were permitted to correlate.

Results

Structural equation model

The SEM (Figure 3) showed the following fit to the data: χ²(7) = 23.41, p = .01, RMSEA = .062, CFI = .99, TLI = .97, SRMR = .020. All indices met or exceeded conventional cut-off criteria (Hu & Bentler, 1999), with the exception of the chi-square test. However, given the known sensitivity of the χ² statistic to large sample sizes (Kline, 2023), and the strong theoretical foundation of the model, we considered the overall fit acceptable. All total and indirect effects were statistically significant (p < .01). To test the robustness of the model we calculated another SEM that controlled for the potentially confounding influence of age and sex on psychopathology by including both variables in the estimations of the direct effects on depression and reexperiencing. In this model, female sex was significantly associated with depression (.42) and reexperiencing (.29) and age was significantly associated with depression (.14). However, the general pattern of the model remained unchanged with the exception of the direct effect of social stress on depression that reached significance under control of age and sex with a coefficient of .11. All other differences between values of controlled and uncontrolled coefficients were below .02 which did not influence statistical significance of any other association.

Discussion

Our survey of Syrian refugees residing in refugee camps in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq confirmed that the accumulation of various types of potentially traumatic events and social stressors contributes to mental health impairments. Additionally, we have demonstrated that the impact of traumatic events experienced during or before the war, as well as events and conditions encountered in exile, is partially mediated by a sense of devaluation related to refugee status.

The results of this study are in line with a consistent body of findings indicating that stressful events that had occurred over the life-span have a cumulative impact on symptoms of posttraumatic stress and depression among war-affected populations and refugees (Li et al., 2016; Neuner et al., 2004; Steel et al., 2009). The current definition of potentially traumatic events is limited to experiences that involve a threat to physical integrity. Therefore, we divided stressors into two categories: physical stressors and social stressors, based on the type of threat involved. Our findings are consistent with theoretical assumptions, as we found that both types of stressors independently contribute to symptoms of depression and PTSD. Previous research with war-affected populations and refugees has been inconsistent in classifying stressors. Previous studies have separated war-related traumatic events into ‘displacement stressors,’ ‘daily stressors,’ or ‘post-migration life difficulties’ (Gleeson et al., 2020; Miller & Rasmussen, 2010; Nickerson et al., 2011; Silove et al., 1997). These classifications differentiate event types based on timing rather than threat type. However, it is likely that these concepts are related to the distinction between physical and social stressors. Pre-migration events are sometimes equated with traumatic war events, and post-migration stressors are considered stressors that go beyond a trauma perspective (Miller & Rasmussen, 2010), although this distinction may be oversimplified (Neuner, 2010). However, regardless of the specific conceptualisation, stressors that are compared to pre-migration war events usually do not meet the criteria for trauma because they do not primarily pose a threat to life. Instead, they often threaten social integrity. It is a consistent finding that these stressors negatively impact mental health in addition to the effects of war experiences (Chen et al., 2017; Hou et al., 2020).

This study indicates that a proposed affective state of social devaluation mediates the relationship between stress and mental health. The research distinguishes between stressful events and the cognitive and affective reactions to these events, overcoming a common shortcoming of stress research in war-affected populations. Many studies assessing post-migration difficulties conflate objective stressors with subjective stress reactions when assessing events and conditions. Common instruments that measure daily stressors are not limited to assessing exposure to an event. Instead, they ask respondents to rate the extent to which they feel stressed by a potential stressor (e.g. Miller et al., 2008). The widely used Post-Migration Living Difficulty Checklist (PMLD) assesses stress by asking respondents to rate how problematic an event or condition has been for them (Silove et al., 1997). However, combining a situational factor with an appraisal of the situation, such as labelling it as ‘stressful’ or ‘problematic’, may make it difficult to determine the causal contribution of events to mental health impairment. Research on stress has generally found that appraisals of challenges are influenced by various biases, including mood bias and psychopathology. As a result, ratings may inflate the association between stress and mental disorders (Neuner, 2010). To avoid confusion, we have simplified all stress scales, including social stressors, to a yes/no format. We ask whether the respondent has experienced a particular event or condition. Our results confirm that social and physical events contribute to symptoms of depression and PTSD, regardless of potential biases.

Furthermore, separating the appraisals from the event itself allowed us to isolate the contribution of a specific type of affective response. With an emphasis on social types of stressors, we postulated that the social devaluation affect, consisting of appraisals of rejection and degradation as well as related emotions of shame and inferiority mediates the relationship between stress exposure and psychological disorders. Previous studies have consistently shown that a broad range of immediate emotional responses to stressors, including fear, helplessness, disgust and anger (Brunet et al., 2001; Gorman et al., 2015) and mental states such as dissociation (Marmar et al., 2004) predict the intensity of the psychological sequalae of the event. Among refugees, Hoffman et al. (2018) showed that perceived moral injury mediates between trauma exposure and posttraumatic stress. We complemented these findings by documenting the mediating role of social devaluation. Consistent with our hypotheses, social devaluation was most highly predicted by social types of stressors, and fully mediated the relationship between social stress and depression. This finding confirms the theoretical assumption that social stressors threaten motives of belonging and status, and cause a consistent defensive emotional, cognitive and physiological pattern which underlies symptoms of depression but also symptoms of PTSD (Neuner, 2023).

In addition, we found that social devaluation contributes to the link between physical stressors, particularly potentially traumatic events, and psychopathology. This finding demonstrates that the distinction between physical and social stressors is not always clear-cut. In reality, many potentially traumatic events involve power imbalances, convey social messages about injustice and humiliation, and elicit social emotions such as shame (Lee et al., 2001). In our study, the relationship between physical stressors and depression was fully mediated by perceived social devaluation. While we could observe a similar mediation of the relationship between traumatic and stressful events and posttraumatic-stress symptoms, the effect was smaller and there remained a consistent direct effect between traumatic effects and posttraumatic stress. It is likely that affects related to physical stress, including fear, helplessness or horror (Gorman et al., 2015), and related processes of appraisal (Ehlers & Clark, 2000) or associative learning (Foa & Kozak, 1986) play a key role as a mechanism. While the role of social devaluation for depression is in line with the social rank theory of depression (Gilbert & Allan, 1998), the PAST theory (Neuner, 2023) predicts that social devaluation would contribute to psychological disorders across diagnoses, particularly also to posttraumatic stress symptoms.

Although our findings are consistent with the predictions from theories, some important limitations need to be considered in the interpretation of our findings. As a cross-sectional study, it is impossible to draw definite conclusions about the temporal sequence or causality of events. In particular, the perception of social devaluation could also be a consequence rather than predictor of depression. Although the SEM supports our theoretical ordering of variables, its cross-sectional nature prevents causal inference. Alternative temporal sequences, including bidirectional or symptom-driven processes such as stress generation, remain viable and even plausible in a refugee context (Neuner, 2010). In addition, as both the predictors, mediators and outcomes were self-report measures, some shared method variance cannot be ruled out. Future longitudinal and experimental studies are required to test the temporal relationships between the variables and the causal contribution of social devaluation. Such research could include refugees living in different types of accommodation, which would allow objective conditions and hardships to be measured rather than self-reported social stressors. At the same time, we are unable to determine the specificity of our findings. Since we studied only a single potential mediator and did not assess potentially contrasting or confounding affects and appraisals, such as helplessness, fear, anger and loneliness, the rating of social devaluation might, to some extent, cover the shared variance of the perceived severity of the event.

Although there are limitations, we have identified a mechanism that may link stress to psychological disorders among refugees. The practical implication of our findings is clear: host countries must be mindful of the negative impact of exclusionary policies that transmit messages of rejection and degradation in order to prevent psychological disorders and impairment among refugees. It is alarming that many high-income countries are attempting to reduce the number of asylum seekers and refugees by limiting so-called ‘pull factors’ of migration, such as access to healthcare, the labour market, and proper housing. These measures may communicate that refugees are not welcome and are seen as a burden on host societies. Countries should be aware that using dehumanising language to restrict migration can cause emotional pain and long-term harm to the mental health of refugees, limiting their ability to adjust and integrate, consequently harming the overall society by hindering an entire population (immigrants and refugees) from unlocking their full potential.

The concept of social devaluation is still limited, despite being based on a theoretical foundation and linked to a substantial body of research on social stress. Future studies should aim to determine the specific impact of social devaluation in contrast to processes related to well-established concepts such as learned helplessness (Maier & Seligman, 1976). It is evident that various appraisals, affects, and distinct neurocognitive processes mediate the relationship between stress and negative outcomes. Future studies should examine the influence of multiple potential mediators. To confirm the postulated mechanism, a longitudinal design is necessary. It is also important to study the contribution of social devaluation in other samples, particularly individuals and populations affected by injustice, discrimination and exclusion.
