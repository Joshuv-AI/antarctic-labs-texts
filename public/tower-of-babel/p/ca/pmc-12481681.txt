An image-domain deep-learning denoising technique for accelerated parallel brain MRI: prospective clinical evaluation
Authors: Laura Onac, Lorand Dobai, Andrei Mouraviev, Mihai A Badila, Denise Yap, Samantha Kennedy, Annie Nguyen, Emi Gal, Daniel K Sodickson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParallel imaging can accelerate MRI acquisitions, but excessive accelerations can introduce amplified noise and aliasing artifacts.PurposeTo evaluate a vendor-agnostic AI-based approach to remove image degradation artifacts in highly accelerated MRI scans, improving image quality and reducing scan time.Materials and methodsTraining was performed by retrospectively degrading standard accelerated images. Evaluation was performed with both retrospective and prospectively-collected highly accelerated images. Retrospective data were taken from ∼2000 MRI studies obtained between August 2016 and October 2022, and prospective data were collected in >200 subjects between June and November 2022, using scanners from multiple vendors and locations. Scan time data were collected from prospective studies and used to compute time savings per sequence and per protocol for each vendor. Images were evaluated qualitatively by 5 board-certified radiologists and quantitatively by assessing noise, contrast, and spatial resolution. Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare model outputs to model inputs and low-acceleration images.ResultsImages from 101 adults from 5 sites and 6 scanner models from different vendors were enrolled. 89% of imaged subjects had noteworthy imaging features or pathology. Model outputs were rated superior to model inputs (P < .001) and most were either non-inferior (Pinf > .05) or superior (Psup < .05) to baseline images in qualitative metrics of image quality and feature visibility. Quantitative evaluation of signal-to-noise ratio and contrast-to-noise ratio improved for model outputs compared to inputs (P < .001) or baseline images (P < .005). Apparent resolution measured using the full width at half maximum or minimum was either enhanced (Psup < .05) or preserved (non-superior Psup > .05 and non-inferior Pinf > .05). The scan time was reduced by an average of 29% (19%-41% per sequence).ConclusionThis vendor-agnostic AI-based method achieved robust scan time savings without loss of image quality, potentially allowing for reduced cost and improved patient experience.Keywords: magnetic resonance imaging, rapid imaging, parallel MRI, image enhancement, image denoising, artificial intelligence, machine learning, U-Net

Summary

A deep learning-based method for enhancing accelerated head MRI following parallel image reconstruction improves image quality and enables scan time reduction of approximately 29% while preserving visibility of key pathology.

Introduction

MRI is a powerful tool for medical evaluation and scientific research, providing rich information about the body’s internal structure. However, the prolonged data acquisition time associated with MRI examinations presents both an economic and a practical challenge, spurring an ongoing need for rapid imaging methods. As such, the history of MRI has been marked by progressive speed enhancements using any means available, from rapid imaging sequences1–3 to parallel imaging (PI) methods4–6 to compressed sensing7 to, more recently, deep-learning-based reconstruction of undersampled image data.8–10 Deep learning reconstructions, in particular, have been shown to perform best when guided by the physics of image acquisition.8,10 However, physics-guided approaches generally require access to proprietary raw data and detailed knowledge of system hardware and software.

At the same time, extensive progress has been made on various computer vision tasks by employing deep learning methods that operate entirely in the image domain. Recent years have seen notable advances in denoising networks,11–15 in networks designed to reduce image artifacts,16,17 as well as in super-resolution networks.18–20 Recently, the use of a deep learning method operating in the image domain was reported to recover resolution in MR images obtained with reduced in-plane spatial resolution.21 Results showed that, although recovery of spatial sharpness was not complete, clinicians remained comfortable with the quality and information content of super-resolved accelerated images.

This study proposes a two-stage approach, leveraging deep learning as a second stage of accelerated image reconstruction. This approach obviates the need to access raw k-space data, which is required for most other acceleration methods, and it can operate without detailed knowledge of many scan acquisition parameters. It is, therefore, straightforward to implement across multiple vendor platforms. This method maintains some protections associated with state-of-the-art physics-guided approaches, since the first stage commercial parallel image reconstruction already incorporates acquisition-specific knowledge, removing the majority of undersampling artifacts and leaving the second stage with the less demanding task of correcting residual errors. The removal of these residual errors, meanwhile, constitutes a less challenging inverse problem than the super-resolution problem, enabling robust preservation of target resolution while maintaining the benefits of vendor-agnostic image-domain operation.

The goal of this study was to accelerate brain imaging protocols using this vendor-agnostic deep learning-based approach while maintaining standard-of-care image quality and information content. Three sequence types were targeted: sagittal T1-weighted (SAG T1), axial T2-weighted (AX T2), and axial fluid attenuated inversion recovery (AX FLAIR).

Materials and methods

Data

We used retrospective data for training, and both retrospective and prospective data for evaluation. Retrospective data were acquired over 6 years, between August 2016 and October 2022. Prospective data were acquired over 6 months between June and November 2022 as part of a clinical trial registered on ClinicalTrials.gov with identifier NCT05385718. Both datasets included fully anonymized SAG T1, AX T2, and AX FLAIR 3-Tesla MR brain images in U.S.-based adult participants aged ≥18 that had consented to the use of de-identified information for research purposes, in accordance with HIPAA requirements. A contracted institutional review board (WCG IRB, Study Number 1332590) approved the study.

The majority of the retrospective dataset, identified hereafter as “internal retrospective” data, was drawn from 1923 examinations in 1780 clients of Ezra AI, acquired at 10 imaging sites using various scanner models produced by Siemens (Lumina, Skyra, Verio), GE (Discovery, Pioneer, Premier) and Philips (Ingenia Elition X). Images were acquired with standard-of-care protocols using parameters within the ranges shown in Table S1, with the PI acceleration factor set to 1 or 2.

An “external retrospective” data collection of 141 studies in 131 subjects obtained from Segmed Inc. using multiple Siemens scanner models (Aera, Biograph mMR, Espree, Skyra, Vida, Verio) at unknown imaging sites was included to increase the representation of common pathologies. The range of acquisition parameters is presented in Table S2, with the unrecorded PI acceleration factor likely between 1 and 2.

The prospective dataset comprised 218 examinations of Ezra AI clinical trial participants and was acquired at 15 imaging sites, 10 for validation and 5 for testing, using multiple scanner models produced by Siemens (Lumina, Skyra, Vida) and GE (Architect, Discovery), with acquisition parameters specified in Table S3. Two sets of scans were performed for each participant: a standard-of-care lower-acceleration scan with acceleration factor 2 and a reduced quality higher-acceleration scan with acceleration factor 3 (for GE) or 4 (for Siemens).

Model

In this 2-stage approach, images are acquired with higher PI acceleration factors than typically used in clinical imaging protocols and a neural network is deployed to correct the spatially inhomogeneous noise patterns and residual aliasing artifacts produced by built-in commercial PI algorithms. Traditional denoising networks do not perform well in the presence of such losses in image quality. Therefore, we trained a tailored network using a range of representative image quality degradations retrospectively applied to a diverse training dataset. The image restoration model is a U-Net network22 whose architecture and training details are presented in Section S1. The model was cleared by the Food and Drug Administration, under 510(k) number K230264.

To simulate noise and artifacts in images for model training, a degradation algorithm was designed that adds patterned noise resembling known noise amplification patterns (Figure S1) and introduces mild to moderate aliasing (Figure S2). The noise added to the images follows a Rician distribution23 and it is simulated by taking the magnitude of scaled and patterned Gaussian noise added to the real and imaginary components of complex images. Residual artifacts are simulated by undersampling in patterns corresponding to typical high PI accelerations. The degradation model and data preprocessing pipeline are described in Sections S2 and S3.1, respectively.

Cohort for image quality analysis

Section S3.2 describes the allocation of data into canonical subsets, as presented in Table S4. The assembly process of the full test set and its subsets is shown in Figure 1, using the inclusion and exclusion criteria from Table S5. The selection of 101 test studies was designed to ensure a data distribution consistent with the general U.S. population (chi-square test, P > .05 per subgroup), but also the presence of representative features and pathologies in 90 subjects. The distribution of pathology in the training and testing subsets is presented in Table S6, while Table S7 provides illustrative examples for each pathologic category.

Study flow diagram showing the formation process of the 4 test cohorts: the Full Test Dataset, the Prospective Test Data, the Prospective Test Subset with Baseline Scans, and the Prospective Test Subset with Aligned Baseline Scans.

The full test set consisted of 101 subjects (300 scans), including 81 subjects (241 scans) from the prospective data subset and 20 subjects (59 scans) from the external retrospective data subset. These data were used for the comparison of scans before and after AI-enhancement. The retrospectively collected scans were only used for the qualitative analysis.

The prospective subset contained paired high/low-acceleration scans for only 78 subjects (234 scan pairs) due to data collection issues. These data were used for the qualitative comparison between high-acceleration AI-enhanced scans and low-acceleration scans. For the quantitative evaluation, paired scans were visually inspected and filtered out if misaligned, to ensure the regions of interest (ROIs) were situated on the same anatomical regions, resulting in a data subset of 56 subjects (107 scan pairs).

Qualitative image analysis

A Likert study was conducted with 5 board-certified neuroradiologists (with 13, 12, 11, 4, and 2 years of post–fellowship experience), who performed an independent and blinded evaluation of the accelerated and enhanced images in a randomized order. Three of the radiologists evaluated the baseline images following the same methodology. They assessed overall image quality, perceived noise level, perceived aliasing artifact level, visibility of key features/pathologies, and visibility of 2 classes of anatomical structures: (1) <6 mm, (2) 6-15 mm. The rating scale is presented in Table S8.

Quantitative image analysis

Quantitative evaluation was performed to assess levels of noise, contrast, and spatial resolution. Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) were measured using the mean and standard deviation in 2 ROIs manually delineated by an MRI specialist (Figure S3). Effective spatial resolution was measured as the full width at half maximum or minimum (FWHM) of the intensity profile associated with a manually drawn line crossing clinically significant anatomical boundaries: sulci for SAG T1 and AX T2, or longitudinal fissure for AX FLAIR (Figure S4). Section S4 details these metrics.

Estimate of scan time reduction

Scanning time data were collected during the acquisition of a prospective test study per vendor with both low-acceleration and higher-acceleration sequences. The time was displayed by the scanners as part of the examination information provided to the MRI technician.

Statistical analysis

Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to evaluate the differences in all qualitative and quantitative image quality metrics, comparing model inputs (accelerated images) and outputs (enhanced images), but also enhanced images and low-acceleration (baseline) images, with statistical significance defined at α = .05. Inter-rater agreement scores were computed using the quadratic weighted Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Model availability

The code and model used for this article are proprietary and are not at present publicly available.

For the comparison between model inputs and model outputs, qualitative analyses were performed on the full test set (300 scan pairs), while quantitative analyses were performed on the prospectively collected subset (241 scan pairs).

An additional comparison of model outputs to baseline images was performed in a smaller subset of the test set for which high/low acceleration image pairs were available to confirm that the improvements yielded by our model approached baseline image quality. All the available 234 scan pairs were used for qualitative analyses, while only 107 aligned pairs could be used for quantitative analyses. SNR and CNR were measured for the pairs of AI-enhanced and baseline scans, including 20 SAG T1, 43 AX T2, and 44 AX FLAIR sequences. We did not perform FWHM analysis in the baseline comparison because of the much more stringent requirement on alignment between accelerated and baseline scans. We found that even very small misalignment resulted in major changes in the FWHM measurements thereby obscuring our ability to interpret the apparent resolution for baseline series.

Based on the statistical tests performed, the model outputs were non-inferior (Pinf > .05) or even superior (Psup < .05) to baseline images in the majority of cases. The model outputs were inferior to baseline images in only two instances: Artifact Reduction for AX T2, and Image Quality for AX FLAIR. In the first instance, the Artifact Reduction score for model outputs was characteristic for mild aliasing artifacts and pathology visibility was not diminished. The inter-rater agreement scores showed none to slight agreement between the radiologists (Figure S7), highlighting the variability in radiologist impressions and the importance of multiple readers.

The model output showed consistently significant improvements in SNR and CNR overall and for each contrast type (P < .001). FWHM measurements also showed significant improvement overall for enhanced images (P < .01), and particularly for SAG T1 (Psup < .01) and AX T2 (Psup < .05), but not for AX FLAIR (non-superior Psup > .05 and non-inferior Pinf > .05). It should be noted that degraded resolution is not a principal consequence of PI reconstruction, but is instead a common side-effect of denoising algorithms, so that either preserved or improved resolution as compared with input images may be viewed as a successful outcome.

When comparing the model outputs against the baseline scans, the enhanced images were superior to baseline scans based on SNR and CNR scores, overall and for each sequence type (P < .005), as shown in Table S12.

Scan time reduction

Time data were collected and used to compute the time savings achieved by increasing the acceleration factor per sequence and per protocol for each vendor, as listed in Table 4. Using GE scanners, the scan time for the accelerated sequences is expected to be reduced by around 24% for SAG T1, 20% for AX T2, and 19% for AX FLAIR. The duration reduction for Siemens scanners is around 41% for SAG T1, 37% for AX T2, and 33% for AX FLAIR. On average, the scan time reduction per sequence was close to 29%. The overall reduction in scan time for the high-acceleration protocol was approximately 21% for GE and 38% for Siemens compared to the low-acceleration baseline protocol.

Discussion

In this study, we presented an AI-based method for reduction of noise and aliasing artifacts following parallel image reconstruction with higher-than-standard acceleration factors to reduce scan time. Since this approach operates entirely in the image domain without relying on raw k-space data or coil sensitivity information, it can be used as a second stage post-processing step following commercially available reconstruction algorithms.

Since our model was trained on retrospectively degraded data to simulate highly accelerated images, it was vital to test the model’s performance on prospectively acquired images with high acceleration factors. These scans were collected at different imaging sites than those used for gathering training data to ensure that our model would generalize across various clinical settings. To ensure the safety of our method, the visibility of any noteworthy image features was recorded in our qualitative prospective evaluations. Since this study was performed on a screening population, comparatively few significant pathologies were found in the prospectively collected data with high acceleration. For this reason, the qualitative assessments were supplemented with external retrospective data containing known pathologies (especially tumors).

Qualitative assessments using a five-point Likert scale showed statistically significant improvement when comparing model outputs to model inputs, not only in overall image quality, but also in measures associated with our primary tasks of noise reduction and artifact reduction. Visibility of anatomical structures, both normal and pathological, also showed a significant improvement after model enhancement. Model outputs were similar to baseline images across most sequences and criteria. The low to fair inter-rater agreement underscored the need for having multiple radiologists perform the review, thereby washing out individual preferences in assessment of quality.

SNR and CNR measures showed consistent improvement when comparing model outputs to model inputs across all sequences, demonstrating our model’s ability to reduce noise. The AI-enhanced images were also superior to baseline scans in terms of SNR and CNR scores. We recognize that ROI-based approaches to SNR and CNR measurement are not the gold standard for images reconstructed using PI algorithms. Such gold standard approaches, however, require access to raw data, coil sensitivities, and/or information about the details of commercial reconstruction algorithms used to generate input images. Since our goal was to enable increased acceleration in the absence of any of this information, and since we were less interested in absolute performance than in the comparison between input and enhanced images, we selected ROI-based measurements as approximate but generally fair comparison metrics, as a supplement to the clinically-focused Likert evaluations.

Another potential danger of denoising algorithms is their tendency to introduce blurring as a means to improve SNR. To confirm that our model was not reducing image sharpness, we measured the FWHM of intensity profiles crossing clinically significant boundaries. Our model showed small, but significant improvements in sharpness for SAG T1 and AX T2 sequences, and equivalent sharpness for the AX FLAIR sequence.

Our approach benefited from a large and diverse dataset for training and evaluation. Scans encompassed a relatively wide range of imaging parameters, sites, and technologies, as well as a deliberately enriched set of imaging findings. The degradation model used to introduce noise and artifacts into images retrospectively was also a key component of our method. For example, varying the level and character of added noise was found to yield substantial improvements in denoising performance. Adding noise in characteristic spatial patterns generally reflective of parallel-imaging-related noise amplification prevented the model from overfitting on uniform noise patterns and resulted in improved denoising performance while preserving anatomic fidelity.

The principal limitations of this study relate to the sequence types, protocols and field strength used. We tested our method on three 3-Tesla brain sequences. Note also that achievable acceleration factors in this study were limited to 3 or 4 due to the constraints of commercial scanner software, which generally place restrictions on acceleration in their user interfaces based on the performance limits of traditional parallel image reconstruction algorithms.

In summary, this method allows for faster scanning while mitigating associated losses in diagnostic image quality and preserving diagnostic information about pathology, potentially allowing for reduced cost and improved patient experience. Generalization and tailoring to other anatomical areas, orientations, contrasts, and field strengths (such as 1.5-Tesla) will be explored in future work.
