The Prognostic Interplay of Heart Failure and Chronic Kidney Disease in Atrial Fibrillation: Focus on Cardiorenal Outcomes
Authors: Ulrike Baumer, Lea Pedarnig, Andreas Hammer, Eva Steinacher, Niema Kazem, Lorenz Koller, Ulrike Pailer, René Rettl, Gere Sunder-Plassmann, Alice Schmidt, Christian Hengstenberg, Patrick Sulzgruber, Alexander Niessner, Felix Hofer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) create a mutually reinforcing cycle, escalating disease development, and increasing morbidity and mortality rates. Both are common comorbidities promoting AF and contributing to heightened symptom burden and poorer outcomes in AF. Here our aim was to investigate the relationship of HF and CKD with cardiorenal outcomes in patients with atrial fibrillation (AF).MethodsPatients with AF, treated at a tertiary centre between January 2005 and July 2019, were included. The primary endpoint was a composite of cardiovascular (CV) death and hospitalization for HF (HHF). Secondary outcomes were the individual components of the primary endpoint, all-cause death, renal death, and dialysis.ResultsWe included a total of 7,412 patients (median age 70 years, 39.7% female) with AF and followed them over a median of 4.5 years. There was a significant stepwise increase in 5-year event rates for the composite of CV death/HHF (no CKD and no HF: 23%, HF: 61%, CKD: 63%, CKD and HF: 82%; P log-rank <0.001). Both CKD (adjusted hazard ratio [HR]: 1.87, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.55–2.25) and HF (adjusted HR: 2.57, 95% CI: 2.22–2.98) were significantly associated with CV death/HHF after multivariable adjustment. A similar association was observed for the individual components of the primary endpoint and renal death/dialysis.ConclusionsBoth CKD and HF significantly increase the risk of CV death and HHF, as well as renal death/dialysis in patients with AF. Risk assessment should expand beyond stroke and bleeding to cardiorenal complications including HHF, CV, and renal death, as well as kidney failure.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is considered one of the most important cardiac arrhythmias of our time with an assumed prevalence in the European population of up to 3.6% by the year 2060 [1]. Sharing many risk factors such as diabetes mellitus and arterial hypertension, AF is the most common arrhythmia in patients with heart failure (HF). Higher rates in both morbidity and mortality in patients with concomitant disease are observed, as they reinforce each other’s disease development and progression [2]. Approximately 50% of patients with HF are diagnosed with impaired kidney function, thereby emphasizing the significance of chronic kidney disease (CKD) as another pillar of current cardiovascular (CV) research [3]. CKD and HF are both highly prevalent comorbidities that promote AF and are associated with aggravated symptom burden and worse outcomes in patients with AF [4]. Finally, CKD and AF exhibit a bidirectional relationship, as each condition independently increases the risk for the other. This interplay perpetuates a vicious circle, characterized by accelerated disease progression, while also contributing to a higher risk of thromboembolism and all-cause mortality in patients suffering from both AF and CKD [5].

Commonly used risk scores such as the CHA2DS2-VASc score usually see stroke as the most dreaded complication in AF. Now, in the presence of effective anticoagulation strategies, the focus has shifted from solely concentrating on the thromboembolic risk to addressing further CV events, such as HF [6]. Taking CKD as an important risk factor for both the development of AF and HF, as well as its increased risk of complications such as stroke and mortality into consideration, a particularly vulnerable group of patients is formed. In addition, both HF and AF exert a significant impact on the development and progression of CKD, directing attention not only to CV outcomes but also to renal outcomes [7, 8]. We therefore intend to close a gap in risk stratification by enhancing the understanding of this interconnected trinity and subsequently enable optimizing the management of patients with concurrent conditions.

Methods

Study Population

We conducted a search of electronic health records at the Vienna General Hospital (Medical University of Vienna) to identify patients with AF who were admitted between January 2005 and July 2019 and required inpatient care. We collected various available data, including patients’ demographics, medical history, echocardiograms, and laboratory measurements, for all eligible patients. The presence of AF was determined by referencing the International Classification of Diseases codes reported on the patients’ discharge letter, HF was assessed using the echocardiographic data as well as the International Classification of Diseases codes. The diagnosis CKD was established based on International Classification of Diseases codes as well as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≤60 mL/min/1.73 m2.

The study protocol was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK 2267/2019), meeting the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent from participants was not required in accordance with local/national guidelines.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was defined as the composite endpoint of hospitalization for HF (HHF) or CV death. Secondary endpoints were the individual components of the primary endpoint and all-cause death, as well as renal death and dialysis. The cause of death was obtained via the national death registry, while HHF and dialysis was determined using the Vienna Healthcare Group hospitalizations database (“Wiener Gesundheitsverbund”) including all hospitalizations in Viennese hospitals. All outcomes were determined using the International Classification of Diseases codes reported on the death certificate or the admission diagnosis in the Vienna Healthcare Group hospitalizations database, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are displayed as median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables are displayed as counts and proportions. For comparison between subgroups, the χ2 test was applied to categorical variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for continuous variables. The median observation time is reported using reversed Kaplan-Meier estimator. To compare the Kaplan-Meier event rates for the combined endpoint as well as individual components, the log-rank test was used. We assessed the association between AF, HF, and CKD using Cox regression models adjusted for age (as continuous variable), sex, diabetes mellitus, HF, CKD, body mass index (as continuous variable [kg/m2]), coronary heart disease, prior myocardial infarction, arterial hypertension, and C-reactive protein levels (as continuous variable [mg/dL]). Results are presented as hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI).

For all calculations, a two-sided p value of <0.05 was considered as significant. SPSS (version 29.0.0.0) and R (version 4.0.4; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) were used for statistical analysis.

Results

Study Population

In total, we examined 7,412 patients over a median follow-up time of 4.5 (IQR 1.8–7.9) years. The median age was 70 (IQR 61–78) years, with a total of 2,945 (39.7%) individuals being female. The median body mass index was 26.9 kg/m2 (IQR 24.2–30.4). Regarding comorbidities, 1,356 (18.3%) had diabetes and 3,582 (48.3%) presented with hypertension. A total of 1,668 patients (22.5%) were diagnosed with HF, 806 (10.9%) with CKD, and 372 (5.0%) were suffering from both conditions. In 4,543 patients, an echocardiography was performed, there were 777 (17.1%) with a left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 40% or less. Moderate to severe mitral valve insufficiency was diagnosed in 1,094 (14.8) patients. The median eGFR was 65 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR 50–81 mL/min/1.73 m2), and the median NT-proBNP levels were 1,167.0 pg/mL (IQR 337 to 2,965.0 pg/mL).

The median CHA2DS2-VASc score was 3 (IQR 1–4). Among the patients with a documented type of AF, 1,214 individuals (72.4%) exhibited paroxysmal AF, while 462 patients (27.5%) presented with permanent/persistent AF. During the observation period, catheter ablation was carried out in 1,660 patients (22.4% of the total), with 124 cases (1.7%) in individuals with HF and 47 cases (10.9%) in those with CKD. In total, 57 (0.8%) patients received a sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitor, with 24 (1.4%) in the HF cohort compared to 6 (0.7%) in the CKD cohort. Relationship of CKD and HF with CV Outcomes

Figure 1 displays the 5-year Kaplan-Meier event rates for HHF/CV death. The combined endpoint of HHF or CV mortality was reached by 81.6% of patients with both HF and CKD, 63.2% of patients with only CKD, 60.6% with only HF, and 23.4% of patients with neither comorbidity. Similar event rates could be observed with the individual components of the primary endpoint and all-cause mortality (Fig. 1). Both conditions, CKD, and HF, remained independently associated with HHF/CV death and its components after multivariable adjustment. The highest risk increase for the combined endpoint could be observed in patients with both HF and CKD with an adjusted HR of 2.94 (95% CI: 2.44–3.54), followed by patients with only HF (adjusted HR 2.37, 95% CI: 2.11–2.66) and only CKD (adjusted HR 1.70, 95% CI: 1.42–2.04, p value of <0.001, respectively). A similar pattern was observed for HHF, CV death, and all-cause mortality (Fig. 2). A sensitivity analysis with further adjustment for NT-proBNP yielded similar results for HHF/CV death (HF and CKD: adjusted HR of 2.70, 95% CI: 2.16–3.37; only HF: adjusted HR of 2.12, 95% CI: 1.83–2.45; only CKD: adjusted HR of 1.72, 95% CI: 1.38–2.14; p < 0.001, respectively).

Relationship of CKD and HF with Renal Outcomes

The composite endpoint of renal death or dialysis was documented in 277 (3.7%) patients. As seen in Figure 3, the lowest 5-year Kaplan-Meier event rates were seen in patients with no CKD and no HF (1.5%) followed by patients with only HF (4.0%). Patients with only CKD showed an event rate of 11.8%, and the highest event rate was seen in patients with both CKD and HF with 17.7% after 5 years (p < 0.001). Both conditions, CKD and HF, remained independently associated with the combined endpoint of renal death/dialysis after multivariable adjustment (only HF: adjusted HR of 2.80, 95% CI: 1.84–4.25; only CKD: adjusted HR of 5.31, 95% CI: 3.22–8.76). The highest risk increase was observed in patients with both CKD and HF with an adjusted HR of 7.47 (95% CI: 4.70–11.87). Similar results could be observed for the individual components of the combined endpoint (Fig. 4, online suppl. Fig. 1).

Cardiorenal Outcomes Stratified by Stages of eGFR

There was a noteworthy stepwise increase in Kaplan-Meier event rates for HHF/CV death after 5 years, corresponding to the worsening eGFR stage. Specifically, 19.7% of patients in stage I experienced these events, contrasting with 80.1% in stage IV and V (online suppl. Fig. 2). As displayed in online supplementary Figure S2, similar trends of event rates could be observed for HHF and CV death as separate endpoints as well as all-cause mortality (p < 0.001, respectively). A similar pattern could be observed for renal death/dialysis (online suppl. Fig. 3). After multivariable adjustment, the eGFR stage III–V remained independently associated with the risk of HHF/CV death (stage III: adjusted HR of 1.84, 95% CI: 1.5–2.3; stage IV and V: adjusted HR of 3.22, 95% CI: 2.40–4.33; p < 0.001, respectively). Conversely, individuals with an eGFR of 60–90 mL/min/1.73 m2 (eGFR stage II) did not demonstrate an increased risk (adjusted HR of 1.00, 95% CI: 0.81–1.24, p = 0.988, reference: eGFR stage I). In terms of renal outcomes, we could also observe a gradual escalation in risk corresponding to higher eGFR stages (online suppl. Table 2).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of 7,412 individuals, we showed that both HF and CKD are independently associated with the composite endpoints of HHF or CV mortality and renal death or dialysis in an unselected AF patient population. Patients suffering from both HF and CKD showed the highest risk for adverse events, yet both HF and CKD were independently associated with higher rates of HHF, CV mortality, renal death, and dialysis. Furthermore, our analysis demonstrated a stepwise increase in the primary and secondary endpoints with worsening renal function, underscoring the significant impact of renal function on HHF and mortality in patients with AF. Given the high prevalence of both, HF and CKD in patients with AF, a particularly vulnerable patient population is formed.

HF and AF – A Vicious Circle

AF and HF have a bidirectional relationship, inducing and exacerbating each other due to shared pathophysiological mechanisms and a complex interplay of neurohumoral, structural, and haemodynamic factors. Patients with HF often present themselves with an atrial pressure overload, as well as atrial enlargement, both of which facilitate the pathogenesis of AF. Combined with altered myocardial conduction, dysfunctional gene expression, remodelling, and neurohumoral as well as structural changes, an arrhythmogenic substrate is created, leading to both the development as well as the perpetuation of sustained AF. In turn, the irregularity in contraction given in AF can lead to a dysfunctional systolic and diastolic atrial function, and the increased heart rate can also impair the ventricular function, resulting in a decreased blood pressure, stroke volume, and cardiac output. Both conditions are closely related to systemic inflammation, leading to both structural and electrical remodelling [9]. Mitral valve insufficiency represents another important link within this triad. The regurgitant flow increases atrial pressure, atrial enlargement, and electrophysiological remodelling, thereby facilitating both AF and HF [10, 11]. Conversely, the structural changes in AF and HF including mitral annular dilation can aggravate mitral regurgitation, thus reinforcing a cycle of mutual disease progression [10, 12]. Commonly used risk scores in patients with AF, such as the CHA2DS2-VASc score, the ABC-stroke score, or the HAS-BLED score, primarily focus on either stroke risk or bleeding events [13–15]. However, Haley et al. [6] conducted a large prospective cohort study of 15,361 patients who presented to the emergency department with AF or atrial flutter. Among the outcome data, only 4% of patients suffered from a stroke within the first year, and 2% experienced major bleeding. In contrast, 13% of patients were hospitalized for HF. Remarkably, HF accounted for one third of all deaths in this population, compared to only 8% attributed to stroke, highlighting the importance of HF as an important risk factor in AF. With effective anticoagulation strategies being available, a focus on HF prevention and therapy harbours great potential to improve both outcome and quality of life in patients with AF. When focusing on HF in AF, the analysis by Hofer et al. [16] showed an increase in HHF/CV death for both diabetes and HF in patients with AF. In the meantime, the CASTLE-AF trial was able to show a beneficial outcome in patients with HF with reduced EF undergoing catheter ablation, for the composite of all-cause mortality or HHF, compared to medical therapy. This highlights the potential for an interventional approach in this vulnerable patient population, yet our data were able to show that there are significantly less catheter ablations performed in patients with HF as well as CKD [17].

Impaired Renal Function as Risk Parameter in AF and HF

Considering the prevalent occurrence of impaired renal function in patients with HF and AF, it is crucial to integrate its impact on the development and progression of both conditions into the risk evaluation of patients with concurrent diseases. Data from the GARFIELD-AF registry, which analysed patients with newly diagnosed AF across 35 countries, demonstrated that worsening renal function was associated with higher rates of stroke or systemic embolism, major bleeding, all-cause and CV mortality, as well as new congestive HF [18]. Focusing on HF, studies showed that with decreasing renal function, the risk of developing HF is rising; meanwhile, patients of all stages of HF demonstrate a significantly higher mortality rate in presence of impaired renal function (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) [3, 19].

Our data support the significant interaction between HF and CKD in patients with AF, indicating a nearly twofold risk for the composite endpoint of HHF or CV death in patients with concomitant disease, compared to those with AF alone. This emphasizes the importance of HHF or CV death as clinically significant complications. In line with these findings, our analysis showed that there was a stepwise increase of risk for the combined endpoint HHF/CV mortality with worsening renal function in a patient population with AF, showing a more than threefold increase in risk for the combined endpoint in eGFR stages IV and V compared to those in stage I. These findings underscore the pivotal role of renal function in risk assessment for patients with AF.

While impaired renal function elevates the risk of AF, the presence of AF itself is also significantly associated with the development of CKD [7]. In patients with CKD, concurrent HF is linked to higher rates of CKD progression and death, indicating the importance of HF prevention and medication in patients with CKD [8]. Our analysis emphasizes the heightened risk of renal death or dialysis in patients with AF who concurrently experience HF and CKD. Our findings reveal the most substantial increase in risk for both the combined endpoint of renal death or dialysis and the individual components among patients with both HF and CKD.

AF, HF, and CKD – Converging Treatment

Since HF and CKD significantly influence each other, there are notable similarities in their efficient treatment. Both conditions are preventable and treatable, and optimal management can mitigate the risks of CV and renal complications in this susceptible patient group. Since the early 2020s, a major shift has occurred in HF therapy.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have demonstrated benefit in reducing the risk of CV death and HF events in patients with both CKD and HF, regardless of the presence or absence of AF or diabetes. Our data support the importance of optimized HF therapy in patients with CKD, yet as shown by Patel et al. [20] worsening renal function in CKD leads to lower rates of optimized medical therapy in patients with HF with reduced EF, even at eGFR levels that still would allow medical therapy. As seen in a sub-analysis of the DECLARE-TIMI 58 trial, the SGLT2i dapagliflozin was also able to reduce the incidence of AF in patients with diabetes mellitus [21].

In addition, SGLT2i therapy demonstrates a favourable impact on renal function. Both dapagliflozin and empagliflozin were able to show a slower decline in renal function among patients with CV disease utilizing SGLT2i [22–25]. The EMPA-KIDNEY trial and the DAPA-CKD trial showed a risk reduction for renal-related outcomes in patients with CKD receiving SGLT2i, thus emphasizing the renal benefit of SGLT2i, regardless of the occurrence of diabetes or HF [26, 27]. These results led to profound changes in the therapy of both HF and CKD, including SGLT2i in both the ESC (European Society of Cardiology) as well as the KDIGO (Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes) guidelines [28, 29]. As our results display data until 2019, they do not reflect the current therapeutic landscape. Nevertheless, considering the significant increase in the risk of renal death or dialysis in AF patients with both HF and CKD, our analysis underscores the critical importance of optimized medical therapy for HF and CKD in individuals with AF.

Conclusions

AF patients suffering from CKD and HF are at high risk of HHF and CV death as well as renal death and dialysis. However, commonly used risk scores for AF mainly focus on the prediction of stroke or bleeding as the primary complication. With our data, we emphasize the importance of both CV as well as renal complications in patients with AF.

Limitations

Some limitations need to be considered. First, there was no standardized follow-up procedure. Additionally, clinical endpoints were not validated; therefore, imprecise diagnoses as well as underreporting are possible. Certain baseline data, such as echocardiographic data, medications, or laboratory measurements, were not available for all patients, introducing a possible selection bias. The data were obtained from a single tertiary academic site, and as such, drawing comprehensive conclusions about the general population may be constrained. Finally, even though comprehensive adjustments were made, residual confounding is still possible.
