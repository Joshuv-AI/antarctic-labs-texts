Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in patients with anthracycline related cardiac dysfunction
Authors: Isabel G Scalia, Ramzi Ibrahim, Mahmoud Abdelnabi, Hoang Nhat Pham, Juan M Farina, Milagros Pereyra Pietri, Kwan S Lee, Balaji K Tamarappoo, Reza Arsanjani, Chadi Ayoub
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD) is recognized complication of antineoplastic therapies, and is particularly associated with anthracyclines. Treatment revolves around heart failure management, with limited evidence for other medications. Specifically, the aim of this study is to evaluate the role of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) in patients with CTRCD.MethodsAll adult cancer patients with a diagnosis of anthracycline induced CTRCD between 2012 and 2022 were retrieved from the TriNetX Research Network. Baseline characteristics, oncology data, and laboratory parameters were also abstracted. Patients were grouped based on administration of GLP-1RAs, with the development of a propensity matched non-GLP-1RA treatment cohort. Clinical outcomes were compared between the cohorts including all-cause mortality, all cause hospitalizations, acute heart failure events, acute renal failure, new atrial fibrillation, and cardiac arrest.ResultsOverall, 2282 cancer patients with CTRCD were identified. Following propensity matching, a control cohort of 201 patients who did not receive GLP-1RAs were compared to 201 patients receiving GLP-1RAs. Over a mean follow-up of 295.4 ± 109.6 days, patients receiving GLP-1RAs treatment had significantly lower risk of all-cause hospitalization (HR 0.617, 95% CI 0.474–0.803, p < 0.001), acute heart failure events (HR 0.612, 95% CI 0.428–0.875, p = 0.007), and acute renal failure (HR 0.577, 95% CI 0.408–0.815, p = 0.002). There were no significant differences in the risk of all-cause mortality, atrial fibrillation, or cardiac arrest.ConclusionIn cancer patients with anthracycline induced CTRCD, those treated with GLP-1RAs had significantly lower risk of adverse clinical outcomes.Graphical abstract Keywords: Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, Anthracycline chemotherapy, Cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction

Background

Guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) has been shown to improve clinical outcomes in patients with cardiomyopathy and heart failure. In addition, glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), such as semaglutide, tirzepatide, and liraglutide, have an increasing body of evidence evaluating their role in these patients [1–3]. Though traditionally an anti-hyperglycemic diabetic medication, and more recently indicated for weight loss, GLP-1RAs have since been shown to improve cardiovascular outcomes in patients with and without diabetes [4, 5]. These effects have been attributed to systemic factors such as improved glycemic control and lipid profile, and reduced inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [4, 6, 7]. Furthermore, direct action on GLP-1 receptors in cardiac tissue may account for observed improvements in blood pressure control, reduced atherosclerotic plaque formation, and decreased atrial and ventricular remodeling due to increased filling pressures [6]. Despite this, there has been conflicting evidence regarding the specific prognostic value of GLP-1RAs in patients with heart failure, and as such it is not yet incorporated into GDMT [1].

Certain chemotherapy agents, such as anthracyclines, have been associated with an increased risk of cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD), which most commonly manifests as left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) and heart failure [8, 9]. Regardless of symptomatology, CTRCD secondary to anthracycline therapy has been associated with increased morbidity and mortality [10, 11]. As such, in addition to thorough pre-chemotherapy cardiac evaluation and aggressive risk factor optimization, multiple therapies are now recommended for the treatment of CTRCD [12, 13]. Specifically, guidelines recommend initiation of heart failure therapy including angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB)/angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEi)/angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI), beta-blocker, mineralocorticoid receptor agonist, and sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitor in patients with either symptomatic heart failure or moderate-severe asymptomatic CTRCD [14]. However, there have been no studies to date evaluating the clinical impact of GLP-1RAs in patients with CTRCD.

Methods

Aim

We sought to investigate the impact of GLP-1RAs on cardiovascular outcomes in a large-cohort retrospective study of patients with known anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity.

Data obtainment

All data utilized in this study was independently obtained by authors from the TriNetX Research Network, a multi-national collaborative database with de-identified electronic health records of over 135 million patients, predominantly in the United States. The study reviewed and analyzed only publicly available data and involved no interaction or intervention on patients, and was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cohort studies.

Population

From this database, adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) with a history of cancer with anthracycline based-chemotherapy (including epirubicin, valrubicin, mitoxantrone, doxorubicin, daunorubicin, and idarubicin) use between 01/01/2012 and 12/31/2022 were identified. Patients with a new diagnosis of cardiomyopathy (defined as clinical diagnosis via the International Classification of Diseases-10 [ICD-10 I42 or I50] or left ventricular ejection fraction < 40% in the absence of ischemic heart disease) following anthracycline use were included. Those with prior history of reduced left ventricular ejection fraction or cardiomyopathy before the initiation of anthracycline use were excluded from analysis. Subsequently, patients actively receiving GLP-1RA therapy (including semaglutide, dulaglutide, tirzepatide, or liraglutide), commenced either prior to or at the time of diagnosis of cardiomyopathy, were identified. Patients with a concurrent history of acute myocardial infarction, coronary artery bypass grafting, or percutaneous coronary intervention were excluded from analysis with the assumption that cardiomyopathy was ischemic rather than related to anthracycline therapy.

Baseline characteristics and comorbidities were collected for all patients, and a propensity matched control cohort was established including patients with anthracycline induced cardiomyopathy, not on GLP-1RAs. All patients in both cohorts were on GDMT for CTRCD, including ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, and mineralocorticoid, as per current Cardio-Oncology guidelines [14]. Parameters included for propensity matching included age, sex, race and ethnicity, comorbidities (i.e., atrial fibrillation, hypertension, cerebral infarction, diabetes mellitus, obesity, dyslipidemia, chronic kidney disease, sleep apnea, tobacco use, and alcohol use), medication use (i.e., loop diuretics, anti-lipemic agents, calcium channel blockers, platelet aggregation inhibitors, anticoagulation, antiarrhythmics, digoxin, metformin, and insulin), and laboratory data at time of diagnosis of cardiomyopathy (i.e., left ventricular ejection fraction, body mass index, N-Terminal pro-B type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), hemoglobin A1C, and creatinine).

Clinical outcomes

Clinical outcomes were collected from TriNetX Research database via ICD-10 codes. All patients that have newly diagnosed cardiomyopathy post-anthracycline use were included, and cohorts were stratified by GLP1-RA use. Day zero was considered as the time of diagnosis with CTRCD. Events included all-cause mortality, acute heart failure (including either new diagnosis or episode of acute on chronic heart failure), cardiac arrest, acute renal failure diagnosis or new requirement for renal replacement therapy (RRT), and new onset atrial fibrillation or flutter. Outcomes were considered with a time-to-event approach, censored by event, death, or last known follow-up within one year following anthracycline administration.

Statistical analysis

Clinical parameters were considered as either categorical variables (presented as n, %) or continuous variables, presented as either mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] based on normality of distribution. Two groups were established based on administration of GLP-1RA, with propensity matching via the greedy nearest-neighbor algorithm and pooled standardized mean difference caliper of < 0.1 as considered being appropriately balanced. Comparison between GLP-1RA cohort and propensity matched control group utilized chi-square testing for categorical variables and independent samples t-test or non-parametric testing for continuous variables. Survival analyses were performed for all clinical outcomes using Kaplan-Meier survival estimates and log-rank testing as well as Cox Regression to determine hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Statistical significance was considered as a two-sided p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R software (v 4.3.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and the TriNetX platform.

Results

A total of 2282 patients were identified to have anthracycline induced cardiomyopathy, of whom 211 patients were in the GLP1-RA-treated cohort and 2,071 in the non-GLP1-RA-treated cohort before propensity score matching (PSM) (Fig. 1). Compared to the non-GLP1-RA-treated cohort, more patients in the GLP1-RA-treated group were of Black race, and had higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation/flutter, ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, dyslipidemia, elevated body mass index and NT-proBNP levels (Table 1). After PSM, 201 patients remained in each cohort, achieving clinically appropriate matching (Table 2). The mean follow-up duration was 295.4 days (standard deviation [SD] 109.6) for the GLP1-RA-treated cohort and 305.9 days (SD 102.0) for the non-GLP1-RA-treated cohort.

Among patients with anthracycline-induced cardiomyopathy, those in the GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 23, 11.4%) had no significant difference in crude rates of all-cause mortality compared to the non–GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 36, 17.9%) (aOR 0.592, 95% CI 0.337–1.041) (Table 3). All-cause mortality-free survival probabilities were also comparable between the two cohorts (HR 0.653, 95% CI 0.387–1.101, p = 0.107). In contrast, all-cause hospitalization crude rates were lower in the GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 95, 47.3%) compared to the non-treated cohort (n = 133, 66.2%) (aOR 0.458, 95% CI 0.306–0.685). Additionally, the GLP1-RA cohort demonstrated improved all-cause hospitalization-free survival compared to the non-treated cohort (HR 0.617, 95% CI 0.474–0.803, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

The incidence of acute renal failure or the need for renal replacement therapy was lower in the GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 52, 25.9%) compared to the non-GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 84, 41.8%) (aOR 0.486, 95% CI 0.319–0.741). Similarly, acute renal failure or renal replacement therapy-free survival probabilities by the end of the study period favored GLP1-RA use (HR 0.577, 95% CI 0.408–0.815, p = 0.002).

Crude acute heart failure events were lower in the GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 50, 24.9%) compared to the non-GLP1-RA-treated cohort (n = 75, 37.3%) (aOR 0.556, 95% CI 0.362–0.854). Similarly, acute heart failure event-free survival probability favored the GLP1-RA-treated group (HR 0.612, 95% CI 0.428–0.875, p = 0.007). Discussion

This study is the first to date to evaluate the impact of GLP-1RA in patients with anthracycline mediated CTRCD, demonstrating prognostic benefit in survival free from heart failure events, acute renal failure or requirement of renal replacement therapies, and reduction in all-cause hospitalizations. Although follow-up time was limited, there was no observed association with all-cause mortality, cardiac arrests, or new atrial fibrillation/flutter in patients administered GLP-1RAs. With increasing overall cancer survivorship and pre-existing cardiovascular comorbidities, CTRCD presents a significant morbidity and mortality burden in cancer patients. As such, early identification, risk factor stratification, aggressive preventative therapies should be considered for patients prior to commencement of high-risk chemotherapeutic agents such as anthracycline therapy. Furthermore, current Cardio-Oncology guidelines suggest the use of maximally tolerated heart failure therapy dosing including ACEi/ARB/ARNI, beta blockers, mineralocorticoids, and more recently sodium-glucose transport protein-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors in patients with chemotherapy induced LVSD [14]. However, the utility of GLP-1RAs has not previously been shown.

In this cohort, the reduced incidence of acute heart failure exacerbations may have been attributed to cardioprotective effects of GLP-1RAs, which are thought in part to be due to reduced oxidative stress and inflammation resulting in decreased cardiomyocyte apoptosis and adverse ventricular remodeling [7, 15]. This has been shown in patients following acute myocardial infarction, whereby administration of GLP-1RAs resulted in improved left ventricular systolic function [16, 17]. Furthermore, there has been increasing literature to support the role of GLP-1RAs in patients with heart failure, however this is yet to be evaluated specially in the oncology population.

GLP1-RAs have shown significant benefit in heart failure across the spectrum of ejection fraction. For example, the recently published prospective SUMMIT trial of 364 patients with obesity and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) demonstrated improved clinical outcomes following tirzepatide [3]. Patients randomized to GLP-1RA therapy had decreased risk of composite heart failure events/cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.62, 95%CI 0.41–0.95, p = 0.026) through a median follow-up of 104 weeks. The earlier STEP-HFpEF study reported a reduction in composite of heart failure event, death, 6-minute walk test distance, and quality of life in obese patients with HFpEF taking semaglutide compared to placebo [18]. A similar result was seen in the subgroup analysis of the SELECT Trial, which evaluated 4286 patients with HFpEF and 1347 patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), out of a total cohort of 17,604 obese patients without diabetes, randomized to either semaglutide or placebo [5, 19]. Over a mean follow up of 39.8 months, patients with HFpEF receiving semaglutide had significantly reduced incidence of cardiovascular complications (HR 0.69, 95%CI 0.51–0.91 for MACE and HR 0.75, 95%CI 0.52–1.07 for composite endpoint of death of heart failure hospital presentation). Patients with HFrEF randomized to semaglutide therapy also had significantly improved clinical outcomes compared to placebo (HR 0.65, 95%CI 0.49–0.87 for major adverse cardiac events and HR 0.79, 95%CI 0.58–1.08 for the composite endpoint).

The prospective Functional Impact of GLP-1 for Heart Failure Treatment (FIGHT) trial in 2016 evaluated 300 patients admitted with HFrEF, reported no significant improvement in the composite endpoint of time to death, repeat heart failure admission, or change in NT-proBNP from baseline [20]. There was also no significant improvement in LVEF, 6-minute walk distance, or subjective quality of life scoring at 180 days post admission. Furthermore, the LIVE study demonstrated no improvement in left ventricular systolic function in 241 patients with chronic HFrEF taking liraglutide compared to placebo, including patients both with and without diabetes [21]. Significantly, this study reported a higher rate of adverse events in patients taking GLP-1RAs. These studies however, both evaluated only liraglutide, with the more recent studies previously mentioned demonstrating positive results with other GLP-1RAs, suggesting an intra-class variability for the reduction in cardiovascular adverse outcomes [22, 23], and suggest novelty in our findings.

Kidney dysfunction is another known complication of many chemotherapeutic agents, with increased risk of both acute kidney injury and chronic kidney failure [24]. Clinically, this may also interact with heart failure and may subsequently be associated with increased morbidity and mortality in cancer patients. Specifically, acute kidney injury with severe proteinuria, secondary to mechanisms such as podocyte damage, proximal renal tubular dysfunction, and tubulointerstitial inflammation, has been observed in patients on anthracycline therapy [24, 25]. In this cohort, the overall incidence of renal failure was higher than expected, which may have been accounted for by other pre-existing comorbidities. Despite this, patients receiving GLP-1RAs had significantly lower incidence of acute kidney injury or new renal failure compared to patients not receiving these medications. This is in line with previous literature, which has shown robust evidence for the reno-protective effects of GLP-1RAs in patients with diabetes, particularly in concurrent use with SGLT2 inhibitors [26–28]. This was highlighted recently in the large prospective FLOW Trial involving 3533 patients, which reported significantly fewer major adverse kidney events in patients with diabetes and chronic kidney disease receiving semaglutide therapy compared to those randomized to placebo therapy (HR 0.76; 95%CI 0.66–0.88, p = 0.0003) [26].

Overall, our study demonstrated prognostic significance associated with the use of GLP-1RAs in cancer patients with CTRCD. This, combined with increasing evidence for the safety and efficacy of GLP-1RAs in patients with heart failure, encourages future prospective studies to validate these findings. Clinically, this further research is of paramount importance in patients where CTRCD is associated with increased morbidity and mortality and may result in cessation of life-saving chemotherapeutics.

Limitations

The retrospective nature of this study lends to inherent limitations, including risk of data collection bias and unknown confounders. Specifically, with data extraction from the TriNetX database as well as utilization of ICD coding for diagnosis of baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes, some data may be missed or misclassified due to external coding or inaccurate reporting. In particular, data including type and stage as well as concurrent oncologic therapies were not able to be obtained, and therefore the impact of these factors on clinical outcomes could not be determined. In addition, the dosages and duration of GLP-1RA therapy were not available and reflect a common real-world limitation when evaluating drug therapies that are prescribed and administered in the outpatient setting. Furthermore, cause of death was not able to be extracted from this database. Despite this, our propensity matching protocol allowed for a well-fitted control cohort for clinical comparison, without significant loss of patients from the GLP-1RA patient group. Another limitation of the current study is the duration of follow-up, with clinical outcomes assessed only through one year follow-up, and longer follow-up in future studies may better permit assessment for effect on all-cause mortality.

One strength of the current study is the evaluation of patients without a diagnosis of diabetes mellitus. Previously, the majority of studies evaluating the impact of GLP-1RAs on cardiovascular outcomes involve only patients with diabetes [4, 29]. For example, in the Cardio-Oncology field, a recent study by Avula et al. demonstrated the benefit of SGLT-2 inhibitors in patients with CTRCD, however all patients in this study were diabetic [30]. Although our overall cohort still had a high incidence of diabetes, roughly 15% of patients were non-diabetic with no significant difference between our treatment groups. Future subgroup analysis of these patients may allow for more clarity on the impact of GLP-1RAs in patients with CTRCD, without diabetes.

Conclusions

In cancer patients with anthracycline induced cardiomyopathy, treatment with GLP-1RAs in addition to GDMT for heart failure may be associated with improved clinical outcomes at one year, including reduction in all cause hospitalizations and acute heart failure events, and reduction in risk of acute renal failure or need for renal replacement therapy. In this high-risk cohort, GLP-1RAs hold potential promise for improved clinical outcomes.
