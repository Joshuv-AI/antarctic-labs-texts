Comparative chloroplast genomics of Erigeron (Asteroideae, Asteraceae)
Authors: Abdullah, Aysha Rahmatulla, Chengyu Chen, Rushan Yan, Irum Naz, Parviz Heidari, Ibrar Ahmed, Atinga Vallada, Xiaoxuan Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The genus Erigeron L. (Astereae, Asteroideae, Asteraceae), commonly known as fleabanes, comprises approximately 457 species with a near-cosmopolitan distribution. However, taxonomic discrepancies persist within the genus. In this study, the complete chloroplast (cp.) genomes of 16 Erigeron species were sequenced and/or de novo assembled and compared with those of nine previously reported species. The complete cp. genomes ranged from 152,108 to 153,430 base pairs (bp) in length, with a large single-copy region of 84,250–85,191 bp, inverted repeat regions (each IRa and IRb) of 24,688–25,054 bp, and a small single-copy region of 18,093–18,530 bp. Each genome contained 112 unique genes, including 79 protein-coding genes, 29 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes. Comparative analyses revealed conserved gene order, GC content, codon usage patterns, simple sequence repeats, and substitution trends across Erigeron species. A consistently low transition-to-transversion ratio (mostly <1) was observed. Analysis of selective pressure revealed gene-wide positive selection acting on ycf2, while some codons of accD, ndhF, rbcL, and ycf1 showed signatures of positive selection. Furthermore, ten highly polymorphic loci were identified, including psaC–ndhD, ndhD–ccsA, trnT–trnL, psbI–trnS, and ndhI–ndhG. Phylogenetic analysis of 25 Erigeron species revealed paraphyly within sect. Erigeron and sect. Trimorpha. The former Conyza species were embedded within the Erigeron species. The species Erigeron breviscapus (Vaniot) Hand.-Mazz., which is endemic to China, showed close relationships with species of sect. Trimorpha. This study provides insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the cp. genome and the phylogeny of the genus, with the identified polymorphic loci offering robust and cost-effective markers for resolving phylogenetic relationships within Erigeron.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07484-9.

Introduction

The genus Erigeron L. (Conyzinae, Astereae, Asteroideae, Asteraceae), commonly known as fleabanes, comprises approximately 457 species of annual, biennial, and perennial herbs distributed across temperate and mountainous regions worldwide, exhibiting a near-cosmopolitan range that reflects their ecological success [1, 2]. North America, considered the evolutionary cradle of Erigeron, is home to over 300 species, with others extending into South America, Eurasia, and East Asia, highlighting their remarkable adaptability to diverse habitats [3–5]. Erigeron species exhibit notable ecological versatility from arctic-alpine environments to disturbed weedy landscapes. Nevertheless, despite this broad distribution, their evolutionary relationships remain complex and poorly resolved, primarily due to polyphyly, frequent hybridization, and persistent taxonomic challenges, particularly among Old World species [1, 6 and citations therein]. Taxonomically, the genus Erigeron has been classified into 35 infrageneric groups, excluding former Conyza species, supporting its origin in North America [1, 6]. Despite these advances, phylogenetic inconsistencies persist. The subtribe Conyzinae Horan. was taxonomically revised and divided into eight distinct genera: Aphanostephus DC., Apopyros G.L. Nesom, Conyza Less., Darwiniothamnus Harling, Erigeron L., Hysterionica Willd., Leptostelma D. Don, and Neja D. Don [1]. However, Plants of the World Online (POWO) now treats Conyza and Darwiniothamnus as synonyms of Erigeron [2]. The reclassification of former Conyza species (e.g., Erigeron bonariensis L., Erigeron canadensis L.) into Erigeron has been inconsistently accepted, with some researchers continuing to recognize these species under Conyza. For example, E. bonariensis has been placed in Erigeron sect. Conyza (Less.) and E. canadensis in Erigeron sect. Caenotus Nutt. [1 and citations therein]. This taxonomic ambiguity is further complicated by the presence of an estimated 80–100 Old World species, including many East Asian endemics such as Erigeron breviscapus (Vaniot) Hand.-Mazz., which remain unassigned to any section due to the historical focus on New World taxa in earlier systematic studies [1, 5, 6].

The chloroplast (cp.) is an organelle enclosed in a double membrane and vital for the process of photosynthesis. It contains its own DNA that typically exhibits a conserved quadripartite structure comprising a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeat (IR) regions [7–9]. This genome structure generally ranges in size up to 160 Kbp and contains up to 120 genes, including protein-coding genes (CDS), transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs); all of these components contribute to the facilitation of photosynthetic function and enable semi-autonomous replication from the nuclear genome [10]. Numerous mutations observed in the cp. genome, including insertions-deletions (indels), substitutions, and inversions, have also been associated with repeats in cp. genomes [11]. Due to its uniparental inheritance, lack of meiotic recombination, and moderate polymorphic variation, the cp. genome serves as a valuable tool in evolutionary research, including phylogenetics, population genetics, DNA barcoding, and conservation studies [8, 12, 13].

Previously, the cp. genomes of nine Erigeron species were reported. A recent study compared cp. genome features of five Erigeron species and reconstructed the phylogeny among nine Erigeron species based on complete cp. genome data [14]. However, all five species were exclusively from the Juan Fernández Islands, Chile. Other studies have primarily focused on individual species or included only one or two species for comparative analysis [5, 15, 16]. To expand the genomic resources and gain broader insight into the evolutionary dynamics of the Erigeron cp. genome, we sequenced E. bonariensis and de novo assembled its cp. genome. We also de novo assembled cp. genomes for 15 additional species for which raw sequencing data were available but cp. genomes had not yet been reported. We compared these 16 species with the nine previously reported species. This combined dataset represents a broad spectrum of taxonomic and geographic diversity, including species from the New World (e.g., E. bonariensis, Erigeron philadelphicus L., E. canadensis, Erigeron peregrinus Greene), the Old World (e.g., Erigeron alpinus L., Erigeron elongatus Ledeb., E. breviscapus), and several island endemics from the Juan Fernández Archipelago (e.g., Erigeron fernandezianus (Colla) Solbrig, Erigeron ingae Skottsb., Erigeron turricola Skottsb.). These species span a wide range of ecological zones—subarctic, alpine, temperate, and tropical—across both hemispheres, enabling a detailed and comprehensive comparative genomic analysis. Detailed information on species names, distributions, and data sources is provided in Table 1. The objectives of this study are to: (a) examine the characteristics of cp. genomes through a comparative genomics approach, (b) reconstruct a preliminary phylogeny of the genus using cp. genome data, and (c) identify polymorphic loci suitable for phylogenetic inference within Erigeron.

Materials and methods

Plant collection, DNA extraction, and sequencing

Erigeron bonariensis was collected from The Islamia University of Bahawalpur, Punjab, Pakistan (Latitude: 29.398299, Longitude: 71.690882). Dr. Sayed Afzal Shah, a plant taxonomist at the National University of Medical Sciences, Rawalpindi, identified the plant, and a voucher specimen was deposited in the herbarium of the Department of Botany at The Islamia University of Bahawalpur under accession number IUB20250526/1745. Fresh, healthy leaves showing no visible signs of disease or damage were selected, dried using silica gel, and stored for DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted using the manufacturer’s protocol with the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and then sent to Novogene (Tianjin, China) for sequencing. Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate short-read data.

Chloroplast genomes assembly and annotation

The cp. genome of E. bonariensis was de novo assembled from raw sequencing data generated in this study, while raw sequencing data for 15 additional Erigeron species were retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) and de novo assembled as part of this study (Table 1). For these 15 species, we compiled information on collection locations, altitudes, habitat characteristics, voucher specimen details, and BioSample accessions from the corresponding records in the NCBI database (Table S1). All cp. genomes were de novo assembled using GetOrganelle v1.7.5+ under default parameters [17] and annotated using GeSeq v2.03 (accessed online: https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) [18] and PGA v1 [19]. For validation, gene annotations were cross-checked against published cp. genomes of other Asteraceae species—Blumea balsamifera (L.) DC. (BK013127), Ambrosia trifida L. (MG029118), Duhaldea nervosa (Wall. ex DC.) Anderb. (OQ863197), and Erigeron annuus (L.) Desf. (MZ361990)—using Geneious R8.1 [20]. The predicted tRNAs were further verified with tRNAscan-SE v2.0.7 [21] and ARAGORN v1.2.38 [22]. Pseudogenes at IR/SC junctions were identified as truncated gene copies resulting from partial duplication due to boundary shifts, rather than from internal stop codons or frameshift mutations. Specifically, if a gene was predominantly located in a single-copy region (e.g., SSC or LSC) and only partially duplicated into the adjacent IR, the shorter, non-functional copy in one IR was annotated as a pseudogene (e.g., ycf1Ψ, rps19Ψ, rpl2Ψ). These annotations were based on alignment comparisons and structural outputs from GeSeq v2.03, following established criteria in cp. genome studies [23, 24]. All such pseudogenes were excluded from the total counts of functional genes. Finally, assembly quality was assessed by mapping raw reads back to each respective genome in Geneious (using default parameters) for coverage depth analysis and minor corrections. All the assembled cp. genomes were submitted to GenBank of NCBI, where accession numbers BK070963-BK070977 were assigned to them. These genomes were also made available as Supplementary Data 1 for quick access.

Comparative analysis and evaluation of polymorphic loci

In total, 25 Erigeron species were analyzed, including 16 de novo assembled species and nine species (excluding one duplicate of E. bonariensis) retrieved from the NCBI database: E. annuus (MZ361990), E. bonariensis (MF276802, reported from Australia), E. breviscapus (NC_043882), E. canadensis (NC_046789), Erigeron multiradiatus (Lindl. ex DC.) Benth. & Hook.f. (NC_056169), E. ingae (OR036878), E. turricola (OR036881), Erigeron luteoviridis Skottsb. (OR036879), E. fernandezianus (OR036882; OR036877), and Erigeron rupicola Phil. Genomic features of all species were examined using Geneious R8.1 [20], and circular cp. genome map were generated using Chloroplot [25]. The lengths, numbers, and positions of introns in the genomes were determined using custom Python scripts (Scripts 1 and 2). We also manually visualized each intron-containing gene and illustrated the intron structures in Erigeron alpiniformis Cronquist as a representative using CPGview v1 [26], as the genes were conserved across all species. Codon usage and amino acid frequencies were analyzed using custom Python scripts (Scripts 3 and 4).

Gene order rearrangements across all genomes were assessed using Mauve alignment [27], and contraction/expansion of IRs was visualized using IRscope v1 and CPJSdraw v1 [28, 29]. To evaluate whether IR length variation is associated with phylogenetic relationships among the sampled Erigeron species, we calculated the IR length for each species and constructed a phylogenetic tree-like diagram using an R script (Script 5). IR lengths were mapped onto the phylogenetic tree using a color gradient to visualize patterns across clades. We also computed pairwise absolute differences in IR length and patristic distances from the tree. Finally, a Mantel test with 9,999 permutations was performed to assess the correlation between IR length differences and phylogenetic distances.

Transition and transversion substitution rates were estimated for complete cp. genomes as well as for the LSC, IR, and SSC regions, following pairwise alignments between each Erigeron genome and E. peregrinus using MAFFT v7 [30] and further analyzed using a custom Python script (Script 6).

We first estimated pairwise nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates, along with their ratios (Ka/Ks), for all cp. CDS using Ka/Ks Calculator [31], designating E. peregrinus as the reference genome. To further characterize selective pressure, we employed HyPhy v2.5.7 [32], applying the branch-site unrestricted statistical test for episodic diversification (BUSTED) [33] to detect gene-wide signatures of episodic positive selection across phylogenetic branches, and the Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (MEME) [34] to identify site-specific episodic diversifying selection affecting a subset of lineages. Codon alignments for each gene were performed using MUSCLE v5 [35]. The maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree reconstructed in this study was incorporated into the analysis (details provided about phylogeny reconstruction in the relevant section).

Simple sequence repeat (SSR) comparisons were performed across all species using MISA [36], with a minimum threshold of ten repeats for mononucleotides, five for dinucleotides, four for trinucleotides, and three for tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotides. Genetic divergence among Erigeron species was assessed across different datasets by calculating nucleotide diversity (π) for intergenic spacers (IGS), introns, and coding regions using CPStool [37].

Phylogenetic tree reconstruction

To reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships within Erigeron, we included the complete cp. genomes of 25 Erigeron species along with one outgroup species, Solidago decurrens Lour., following recent phylogenetic studies [23, 38]. Solidago decurrens was selected because it has been consistently shown to share a close evolutionary relationship with Erigeron within the tribe Astereae [23, 38]. Compared to other potential outgroups, such as Conyza—which has been partially merged into Erigeron and remains taxonomically unstable—S. decurrens offers a clearer phylogenetic distinction and avoids potential classification ambiguities. Additionally, the cp. genomes of Conyza are currently scarce. The IRa region was removed from all genomes prior to alignment to prevent overrepresentation of duplicated regions. After alignment with MAFFT v7 [39] under default parameters, the resulting dataset comprised 133,682 base pairs, 1,777 parsimony-informative sites, and 2,601 distinct site patterns.

Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using IQ-TREE v2.3.4 [40]. The best-fitting nucleotide substitution model, TVM + F + R4, was identified using ModelFinder [41]. Under this model, a maximum likelihood tree was constructed, and nodal support was evaluated with 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates and 10,000 SH-aLRT (Shimodaira-Hasegawa approximate likelihood ratio test) replicates [42].

Results

Genome sequencing and de novo assembly of genomes

The Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform generated approximately 7.94 gigabytes (Gb) of data for E. bonariensis, consisting of 19.1 million 150-bp paired-end reads. The cp. genome of E. bonariensis was assembled with a high average coverage depth of 270×, with 0.28 million reads corresponding to the cp. genome. For 15 additional Erigeron species, publicly available whole-genome sequencing data, ranging from 1.58 to 10 Gb (containing 3.06 to 75.47 million paired-end reads), enabled successful cp. genome assembly with average coverage depths of 38× to 8356×, corresponding to 0.06 to 8.52 million reads for the cp. genomes (Table 1).

Comparative analysis of chloroplast genome features

Previous studies reported cp. genomes for two E. bonariensis samples from Australia (accessions: NC_035884 and KX792499). The KX792499 accession exhibited assembly anomalies, including undetected IR regions by GeSeq during annotation and inconsistencies between IRa and IRb upon manual inspection. Due to these assembly issues, this accession was excluded from further analyses. A comparative analysis between the remaining Australian sample and the Pakistani sample uncovered nine indels—one of which was a large 193-bp deletion in the accD-psaI intergenic spacer—while the others were small and associated with simple sequence repeats, along with six nucleotide substitutions. None of these genetic variations were located within coding regions, and the LSC, IRs, and SSC junctions were identical between accessions. A single-base-pair variation in the Australian sample likely introduced an assembly error, leading to inconsistent IR annotation as indicated by GeSeq. Upon addressing this discrepancy, the junction boundaries were found to be identical (GenBank file 1; Figure S1).

The genome sizes and gene contents of the cp. genomes, including those of ten additional Erigeron species downloaded from NCBI (as described in the methodology), were analyzed. Among the studied species, the complete cp. genomes ranged from 152,108 bp (Erigeron acris L.) to 153,430 bp (Erigeron muirii A.Gray), the LSC region ranged from 84,250 bp (E. peregrinus) to 85,191 bp (Erigeron hyperboreus Greene), the SSC region spanned from 18,093 bp (E. humilis) to 18,530 bp (Erigeron muirii), and the IR regions ranged from 24,688 bp (E. humilis) to 25,054 bp (E. peregrinus). These findings highlight a general consistency in the sizes of the major structural regions and complete cp. genomes across Erigeron species (Table 2).

Among 15 cp. genomes, thirteen were successfully assembled from the data reported in Alsos et al. [43], and genome lengths were compared with those reported in their study (Table S2). Minor differences in genome lengths were observed in five species: E. acris (+ 10 bp), Erigeron compositus Pursh (+ 1 bp), Erigeron denalii A. Nelson (−1 bp), E. humilis (−116 bp), and Erigeron silenifolius (Turcz. ex DC.) Botsch. (+ 1 bp). Four species not previously assembled—Erigeron uniflorus L., E. muirii, Erigeron borealis Simmons, and E. alpiniformis—were successfully assembled in the present study. These differences and newly completed assemblies likely reflect the use of GetOrganelle, a robust and highly efficient assembly pipeline, rather than variations in data origin, as the previous study employed a custom assembly script. Notably, the previous study focused primarily on demonstrating the utility of herbarium specimens and other preserved samples for cp. genome assembly and barcoding marker extraction, rather than on comprehensive comparative genomics.

The gene and intron content remained highly conserved across the 25 Erigeron species. Each cp. genome contained 112 unique genes, including 79 CDS, 29 tRNA genes, and 4 rRNA genes (Fig. 1). The IR regions duplicated 16 genes: 5 CDS (2 with introns), 7 tRNA genes (2 with introns), and 4 rRNA genes. Analysis identified 18 intron-containing genes—12 protein-coding and 6 tRNA genes—with conserved positions across species (Table S3). Collinear block analysis (progressive Mauve alignment) confirmed consistent arrangement of these genes in the cp. genome and did not indicate any long inversions (Fig. 2).

The cp. genomes exhibited remarkably consistent GC content of up to 37%. However, regional differences were observed, with IRs having significantly higher GC content (up to 43%) compared to LSC (up to 37%) and SSC (up to 31%) regions (Table 2).

Inverted repeats contraction and expansion

Analysis of the 25 Erigeron species revealed minor variations in IR boundary positions across the four junctions: LSC/IRb (JLB), IRb/SSC (JSB), SSC/IRa (JSA), and IRa/LSC (JLA) (Fig. 4). Gene organization at these junctions was remarkably consistent. The JSB junction invariably contained a functional ycf1 gene, while its 565–586 bp truncated pseudo-copy (Ψ) consistently appeared at JSA. The ndhF gene was entirely confined to the SSC region. At JLB, the rpl2 gene was consistently positioned 116–144 bp away from the junction in the LSC, while rps19 spanned the boundary with 62–89 bp in IRb and 190–212 bp in the LSC, except in Erigeron turricola Skottsb. In E. turricola, rpl2 spanned JLB with 1345 bp in IRb and 151 bp in the LSC, whereas rps19 was located 205 bp away from the junction in the LSC. Similarly, at JLA, trnH was consistently located in the LSC, adjacent to a 62–89 bp pseudogene of rps19 (rps19Ψ) in IRa in all other species, while E. turricola harbored a 1345 bp pseudogene of rpl2 instead of rps19Ψ. These conserved architectural features suggest strong evolutionary constraints on IR boundary maintenance in Erigeron cp. genomes.

Mapping the IR length onto the phylogenetic tree revealed that variation in IR length is not randomly distributed but tends to group within specific clades (Figure S2). A Mantel test showed a strong and statistically significant positive correlation between IR length differences and phylogenetic distances (Mantel statistic r = 0.767, p = 0.0001), indicating that more distantly related species exhibit greater differences in IR length. These results suggest that IR length variation is at least partly reflective of evolutionary divergence among species. To further illustrate this relationship, we generated a phylogenetic tree-like diagram with a color gradient representing IR length variation, highlighting its correlation with phylogeny (Figure S2).

Codon usage, amino acid frequency, repeats, and transition and transversion substitutions

All species within the genus Erigeron demonstrated high similarity in codon usage patterns, amino acid frequencies, and SSRs. Analysis of relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) showed that most amino acids were encoded by codons ending in A or T at the 3′ position (Table S5). Codons with A/T at the 3′ end generally had RSCU values greater than 1. Among all amino acids, leucine was the most frequently encoded, followed by isoleucine, whereas cysteine was the least frequently encoded (Table S6).

Simple sequence repeats analysis revealed that the total number of repeats ranged from 75 to 97 across species. Mononucleotide SSRs were the most abundant, followed by dinucleotide and trinucleotide SSRs. Among mononucleotide SSRs, A/T repeats dominated; AT/TA motifs were most common among dinucleotide SSRs, while AAT/TTA motifs were most frequent among trinucleotide SSRs. Complete details are provided in Table S7.

Substitution types were analyzed across the cp. genome and within the three major regions (LSC, SSC, and IR). A higher frequency of transversion substitutions was observed compared to transition substitutions. The transition-to-transversion (Ts/Tv) ratio ranged from 0.73 to 0.90 in the complete cp. genome, 0.65 to 0.87 in the LSC, 0.85 to 1.14 in the SSC, and 0.58 to 1.47 in the IR. Notably, only three species in the SSC and four species in the IR exhibited Ts/Tv ratios greater than 1. Most substitutions occurred in the LSC region, followed by the SSC and then by IR. Among transversions, A/C, A/T, C/G, and G/T substitutions were more frequent than transition types A/G and C/T. Complete substitution data are presented in Table S8.

Synonymous and non-synonymous substitutions and evaluation of positive selection

The evolutionary rate of CDS revealed strong purifying selection acting on these genes, with an average Ka/Ks value of 0.068. No genes were under positive selection based on the Ka/Ks data calculated (Figure S3). As expected, the strongest purifying selection pressures were observed for genes involved in photosynthesis.

Some genes showed Ka/Ks values greater than 0.5. These genes were further tested using BUSTED and MEME. BUSTED analysis of 16 cp. CDS revealed limited evidence of episodic diversifying selection across Erigeron species, with only ycf2 showing statistically significant gene-wide positive selection (p = 0.0380) (Table S9). While ycf2 exhibited a complex evolutionary pattern—with 6.04% of codon sites under strong diversifying selection (dN/dS = 12.539)—the majority of sites (93.96%) were subject to strong purifying selection (Table S9).

We also utilized MEME to detect specific codons under positive selection (Table S9). MEME detected episodic diversifying selection at specific codon sites in five genes: accD, ndhF, rbcL, ycf1, and ycf2. This contrast underscores how ycf1 and ycf2—while largely conserved overall—harbor transient adaptive signatures at discrete residues. Collectively, these findings indicate that adaptive evolution in Erigeron cp. genomes predominantly occurs through highly localized, site-specific mechanisms superimposed on pervasive purifying selection.

Intergenic spacer regions, intronic regions, and protein-coding sequences were analyzed to identify highly variable regions across all sampled species. The average nucleotide diversity (π) across the cp. genome was 0.0038 when all the species were included in the analysis. Two other regions—trnT–trnL and ndhI–ndhG—were highly polymorphic in three datasets but were absent only in the Chile clade. Additionally, trnR–trnG appeared among the top ten polymorphic regions in three datasets, but not in Clade II.

Four regions—psbI–trnS, clpP–psbB, ccsA–trnL, and ycf1–rps15—were exclusive to the Whole dataset, indicating their utility in capturing deeper evolutionary splits not recovered within more narrowly defined clades. A similar pattern was observed in CDS. Only ycf1 and ccsA appeared in the top ten polymorphic CDS across all datasets, though their diversity remained lower than that of intergenic regions (Table 4, Table S11). Each dataset revealed a different set of highly variable CDS, emphasizing the importance of clade-specific analyses to uncover variations that may remain undetected in broader, combined datasets.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis of 25 Erigeron species—24 assigned to eight sections (Erigeron [9 species], Terranea [5 species], Trimorpha [4 species], Fruticosus [1 species], Tridactylia [1 species], Phalacroloma [1 species], Quercifolium [1 species], Caenotus [1 species], and Conyza [1 species]) and one unassigned (E. breviscapus)—revealed paraphyly within multiple sections (Fig. 6; Figure S4). The sole representative of sect. Fruticosus, E. peregrinus, formed the basal lineage in the phylogeny. Notably, the monotypic sect. Quercifolium (E. philadelphicus) and sect. Phalacroloma (E. annuus) shared a common node, which was subsequently rooted by sect. Caenotus (E. canadensis). The five species of sect. Terranea formed a monophyletic clade and showed a close relationship with sect. Conyza (E. bonariensis). Both E. canadensis and E. bonariensis are also often classified by some taxonomists under a separate genus, Conyza.

Discussion

This study presents the first comprehensive comparative analysis of cp. genomes across 25 Erigeron species, including nine publicly available genomes. The conserved genome architecture—spanning size (152,108–153,430 bp), gene order, and coding content—aligns with patterns observed across Asteraceae [23, 38, 44] and broader angiosperm lineages [24, 45], reinforcing the cp. genome’s role as a slow-evolving genomic compartment in plants. This structural conservation extends to intron content, which remains highly stable in length and distribution across species—supporting the view that cp. introns are subject to strong purifying selection due to their roles in splicing, RNA stability, and regulation of gene expression [46, 47]. Such evolutionary stasis in Erigeron likely stems from core mechanisms in plant organelle genomics as proposed previously [48]: (1) plastid genes organized into operons that resist structural rearrangement; (2) uniparental inheritance limiting recombination; (3) efficient DNA repair systems curbing mutation accumulation; and (4) rare plastid fusion events preserving lineage-specific integrity. These factors collectively maintain cp. genome stability, contrasting with the dynamic structural evolution characteristic of plant nuclear genomes.

The conserved GC content profile—particularly elevated GC enrichment in inverted repeats (IRs, ~43%) compared to single-copy regions (~37%)—mirrors trends observed in diverse plant clades [23, 38, 44, 49, 50]. This pattern correlates with IR-localized rRNA genes, which exhibit GC content up to 55%, reflecting strong selective pressures to preserve ribosomal function—a critical constraint in cp. genome evolution. Such conservation underscores the dual role of cp. genomes as metabolic powerhouses and genomic fossils, retaining structural signatures shaped during early land plant adaptation.

The pseudogenization of trnT-GGU in Erigeron aligns with recent reports in core Asteraceae, where compensatory “super wobbling” mechanisms in translation may offset tRNA loss [51]. While cp. genome stability dominates Erigeron evolution, these subtle variations highlight lineage-specific adjustments within an otherwise constrained genomic system. High similarity in genes located at the JLB, JSB, JSA, and JLA junctions highlights the genomic stability of cp. genomes across Erigeron species. IR boundary fluctuations—a phenomenon associated with gene duplications, deletions, and the origination of pseudogenes such as rps19 and ycf1 [24, 29, 52, 53]—showed minimal variation within this genus. These conserved patterns align with the close evolutionary relationships among Erigeron taxa, as previously reported for other genera of Asteraceae [23, 44, 54]. For IR length, we detected a strong phylogenetic signal in its variation. The significant positive correlation between IR length differences and phylogenetic distance (r = 0.767, p = 0.0001) indicates that while IR length is generally conserved, it accumulates divergence over time. Furthermore, comparative studies, including those on Berberis [12], demonstrate that IR contraction and expansion dynamics can diverge significantly even within closely related lineages, underscoring how phylogenetic history and lineage-specific evolutionary pressures influence cp. genome structural evolution.

Initial pairwise Ka/Ks analyses across all CDS in Erigeron revealed strong purifying selection, with Ka/Ks ratios consistently below 1. However, a few genes showed slightly elevated Ka/Ks ratios and were further analyzed for episodic selection using BUSTED. The BUSTED results revealed that only ycf2 showed significant evidence of episodic diversifying selection (p = 0.0380), aligning with studies that highlight its complex and often adaptive evolutionary dynamics in plants [55, 56]. In contrast, ycf1—though highly variable and widely used in plant DNA barcoding and phylogenetics [57]—did not exhibit significant gene-wide selection, despite a moderate global dN/dS and site-level signals. MEME analyses identified episodic selection at specific codons in accD, ndhF, rbcL, ycf1, and ycf2, underscoring the importance of localized adaptive changes that gene-wide tests may not capture [34]. Previous reports have similarly shown that some codons, rather than entire genes, display signs of positive selection, as reported for atpB, ndhD, ndhF, rpl22, rpoA, ycf1, and ycf2 [52, 58]. Together, these findings suggest that cp. evolution in Erigeron is primarily shaped by purifying selection, with occasional bursts of positive selection acting on individual codons. Hence, they support the utilization of integrated approaches—Ka/Ks, BUSTED, and MEME—to capture the full spectrum of evolutionary pressures acting on organellar genomes.

Not all regions of the cp. genome are equally informative for phylogenetic inference or DNA barcoding, as their levels of nucleotide polymorphism vary considerably [24, 45, 49, 57]. In the present study, a comparative analysis of cp. genome regions revealed ten highly polymorphic loci, all located in intergenic spacer regions: psaC–ndhD, ndhD–ccsA, trnT–trnL, psbI–trnS, ndhI–ndhG, clpP–psbB, trnD–trnY, ccsA–trnL, ycf1–rps15, and ndhC–trnV. These loci exhibited nucleotide diversity (π) values ranging from 0.0314 to 0.0142. Interestingly, these regions differ from those reported in a recent study of 11 cp. genomes from nine Erigeron species, which identified clpP–psbB, rps4–trnT, trnS–trnC, ndhF–rpl32, and ycf1 as the most polymorphic [14]. Only clpP–psbB was shared between the two datasets. In the current analysis, ycf1 showed moderate polymorphism (π = 0.0075), which was lower than that of the identified intergenic spacers but ranked second among protein-coding regions.

This discrepancy likely stems from two main factors. First, the present study included 25 phylogenetically diverse Erigeron species, whereas the earlier study focused on only nine species, most of which were closely related and geographically restricted to Chile. This broader taxonomic sampling in our dataset is a critical factor, as analyses across different clades consistently revealed distinct sets of polymorphic loci, indicating that nucleotide polymorphism is often clade-specific. For example, when we restricted our analysis to the five species reported from Juan Fernández Islands, Chile, in the previous study and reanalyzed them using CPStools, we identified ten polymorphic loci: ycf2–trnL, psaC–ndhD, trnG–trnfM, trnR–trnG, rps11–rpl36, atpA–trnR, accD–psaI, petB–petD, rpl33–rps18, and atpH–atpF. These loci differed substantially from those reported in the earlier study, yet two of them overlapped with the top ten polymorphic loci identified in our whole-clade analysis. This suggests that the differences were not solely due to methodology but were also strongly influenced by the taxonomic composition of the datasets.

Second, methodological differences likely contributed to the contrasting results. The earlier study employed a sliding window approach in DnaSP, which calculates nucleotide diversity over small, overlapping segments and can exaggerate variation in short hypervariable regions. In contrast, the current study used CPStools, which independently extracts and analyzes full-length coding, intergenic, and intronic regions, offering a more region-specific and integrated assessment of nucleotide diversity. For instance, while the previous study identified ycf1 as one of the most polymorphic loci, the CPStools-based analysis in this study revealed that ycf1 exhibited relatively low polymorphism (π = 0.00182). This difference highlights the influence of analytical methodology on the identification and ranking of polymorphic regions.

Together, these differences in sampling scope and analytical methods underscore the importance of both comprehensive taxon representation and consistent methodology when identifying polymorphic loci for phylogenetic and population-level studies.

Twenty-four species were categorized into eight sections based on previous taxonomic frameworks, providing a reference point for phylogenetic interpretation. Previous molecular studies have reported paraphyletic relationships within the genus Erigeron [1, 6]. Currently, the POWO database recognizes a broadly circumscribed Erigeron, comprising approximately 467 species, including taxa formerly treated as a separate genus (Conyza) [1–4, 6]. In our reconstructed phylogenetic tree, species historically classified as Conyza (E. bonariensis and E. canadensis) were embedded within the Erigeron clade, providing preliminary phylogenomic support—based on complete cp. genomes—for POWO’s expanded taxonomic treatment and supporting their inclusion within Erigeron rather than as a distinct genus. However, given the limited sampling of Conyza-type species and reliance solely on cp. data, we regard this evidence as preliminary. Further studies incorporating broader taxon sampling, morphological traits, ecological niche data, and nuclear genomic resources are needed to rigorously test the monophyly of Erigeron and the placement of former Conyza species. Therefore, our findings should be considered a first step toward clarifying these taxonomic relationships rather than conclusive evidence for formal revision.

Phylogenetic inference based on whole cp. genome data revealed that certain traditional infrageneric sections within Erigeron are not monophyletic. Specifically, sections Erigeron and Trimorpha exhibited paraphyletic patterns. In sect. Erigeron, paraphyly was primarily driven by the placement of E. muirii and E. denalii, which grouped outside the section with other species. Similarly, sect. Trimorpha displayed a non-monophyletic topology, as E. alpinus and E. elongatus formed a clade with E. alpiniformis, rather than grouping with other members of the section. These results are in agreement with previous studies [1, 3, 6].

The unassigned species E. breviscapus, endemic to China, shares phylogenetic affinities with Eurasian species of the sect. Trimorpha, although further resolution is required. Additional data are needed to determine whether this species should be incorporated into an existing section or recognized within a newly circumscribed group. However, it has been suggested that many Old World species may be part of the sect. Trimorpha [1].

Although our analysis included a limited number of species, the observed paraphyly underscores the incongruence between traditional sectional delimitations and phylogenetic relationships inferred from cp. genome data. Because cp. genomes exhibit uniparental (typically maternal) inheritance and do not account for hybridization or biparental gene flow, their phylogenetic signal may be limited in complex or reticulate lineages such as Erigeron, as previously reported for tribes of the subfamily Asteroideae [23, 59]. To overcome these limitations and establish a more robust and stable taxonomic framework, future studies would benefit from broader taxon sampling and the incorporation of nuclear genomic resources. Specifically, integrative approaches that combine target enrichment methods (e.g., Angiosperms353) or transcriptomic datasets with detailed morphological reassessment have proven effective in resolving taxonomic complexity within Asteraceae at both the family and tribal levels [59–61].

Conclusion and future perspectives

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the cp. genomes across 25 Erigeron species, revealing conserved genomic features and offering significant insights into the genus’s evolutionary dynamics and providing evidence of low evolutionary rates in its cp. genome. Furthermore, the identified polymorphic loci present valuable markers for future phylogenetic studies, enabling a more in-depth exploration of Erigeron’s evolutionary history. The paraphyletic relationships observed within the Erigeron and Trimorpha sections suggest the need to re-evaluate current sectional boundaries.

Our results underscore the importance of re-assessing genus and sectional boundaries within Erigeron using comprehensive genomic datasets and modern phylogenetic methods. Future studies should aim to generate multilocus nuclear datasets and apply coalescent-based species tree approaches to refine evolutionary relationships and inform taxonomic reclassification. This integrative strategy, grounded in both morphological and genomic evidence, will improve the accuracy of infrageneric taxonomy in Erigeron.
