Best Practices in Physiologically Based Pharmacokinetic (PBPK) Modeling
Authors: Marylore Chenel, Andre Dallmann, Ibrahim Ince, Michaela Meyer, Jan‐Frederik Schlender, Donato Teutonico, Erik Sjögren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPhysiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling is a mechanistic cornerstone of model‐informed drug development (MIDD), providing evidence for regulatory decisions. PBPK models integrate physiological and pharmacological data and have been applied across a plethora of contexts of use, including assessing drug–drug interactions (DDIs), optimizing dosing for special populations (e.g., pediatrics and those with organ impairment), and supporting clinical study waivers. However, despite the established history of PBPK modeling, a significant gap persists between the quality of submitted PBPK analyses and regulatory expectations. Recent reviews by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and other regulatory bodies indicate that many PBPK models are not deemed adequately qualified for their intended application. This discrepancy highlights the need for consolidated guidance on model development, evaluation, and application, particularly to harmonize terminology and expectations with the ICH M15 guideline. To address this challenge, this paper provides comprehensive best‐practice guidance for the development, verification, validation, and applicability assessment of PBPK models. We delineate strategies for defining explicit technical criteria to ensure models are appropriately qualified and demonstrably fit‐for‐purpose for a specific context of use. This guidance aims to enhance the quality, consistency, and regulatory acceptance of PBPK analyses, thereby strengthening the reliability of MIDD evidence supporting drug development and approval.

Background

Physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling, a cornerstone of model‐informed drug development (MIDD) [1], provides a mechanistic basis for understanding drug behavior by integrating physiological, pharmacological, and formulation‐specific data. This framework enables the prediction of pharmacokinetics across diverse populations, species, and administration routes, thereby informing critical decisions throughout the drug development pathway [2, 3]. PBPK modeling is integral to development strategy [3] and regulatory submissions [4, 5, 6] supporting pivotal decision‐making in areas such as drug–drug interaction (DDI) risk assessment [7, 8], extrapolation to special populations (e.g., pediatrics [9, 10, 11, 12], renal and hepatic impairment [13]), and dose selection [14, 15]. Regulatory agencies increasingly recognize PBPK modeling as a valuable tool for supporting clinical study waivers [16, 17], guiding bioequivalence assessments [18, 19, 20], and informing dosage recommendations [21] and clinical study designs [22]. In translational science, PBPK modeling bridges preclinical and clinical data, facilitating the integration of in vitro and in vivo findings to drive or support inflection point decision making and first in human dose predictions in the early stages of clinical drug development [23, 24, 25]. Consequently, PBPK applications are continuously expanding in use throughout drug discovery and development [5, 6, 26] and are routinely incorporated into regulatory submissions [4, 5, 27, 28, 29].

However, a recent European Medicines Agency (EMA) review of PBPK models in new medicinal product approvals indicates that regulatory expectations are frequently unmet, with many submitted models not deemed adequately qualified for their intended application [30]. The need for further guidance, best practice, and standardization was in addition highlighted during a workshop hosted by EMA in 2025 focusing on reporting and qualification of mechanistic models for regulatory assessment [31]. This observation is further supported by discussions regarding submissions to other agencies, such as the FDA [5, 6], which also highlight general challenges and knowledge gaps impeding routine utilization of PBPK models for specific regulatory decisions [32]. This underscores the imperative for best‐practice documents to guide PBPK applications, with the objective of enhancing model quality, regulatory acceptance, and harmonizing terminology with the ICH M15 guideline on MIDD [33].

Despite numerous published examples and successful regulatory submissions showcasing their application, there is a notable lack of consolidated guidance on the development, evaluation, and application of PBPK models. While some high‐level frameworks have been published [34, 35] and remain largely valid, they often remain too theoretical to provide clear, actionable guidance for application. Furthermore, existing literature, such as the paper by Johnson et al. [36], focuses specifically on pediatric populations. In contrast, our initiative aims to provide a comprehensive ‘good practice’ paper that standardizes the development, applications, and documentation of PBPK modeling across a general context, ensuring utility for a broader range of applications. It details guidance for PBPK model development, verification, validation, and applicability assessment. Furthermore, it delineates strategies for defining explicit technical criteria ensuring models are appropriately qualified and demonstrably fit‐for‐purpose, fostering consistent and reliable MIDD evidence, supported by key regulatory guidelines [16, 37], established credibility assessment frameworks [38] and aligned with the ICH M15 draft guideline [33]. Process Description and Points to Consider in Planning, Developing, Evaluating, Applying and Reporting a PBPK Model

Planning: Understand Modeling Aim and Objectives

Robust PBPK model development requires explicitly defining the Question of Interest (QoI) and Context of Use (CoU) [16, 33, 37, 38, 39, 40]. As established in the ICH M15 guideline, the CoU dictates the necessary model complexity, data requirements, and validation rigor [38, 40]. A prospective model risk assessment, derived from the intended model influence [33] and the consequence of a wrong decision (CoWD), determines the target level of model evaluation. To promote overall transparency and facilitate regulatory interactions and multi‐disciplinary alignment, developers should prospectively document these elements, preferably using the ICH M15 assessment table (Table S1). While modeling objectives may iteratively evolve following a ‘learn and confirm’ paradigm as new data emerges, any modifications to the CoU must be transparently documented and justified.

The application of PBPK modeling to replace a clinical DDI study for vericiguat provides a compelling case study for the ICH M15 “assessment table.” By utilizing a PBPK model for vericiguat and its primary metabolite (M‐1), its DDI liability towards the UGT1A1 inhibitor atazanavir was evaluated [41]. Although this modeling effort predated the establishment of the ICH M15 guidance and its associated framework, this retrospective analysis highlights how the vericiguat case study, which successfully replaced a clinical DDI study with PBPK‐based predictions as accepted by regulators [41], can now be aligned with the “assessment table” (Table S1).

The initial definition of the modeling aim and objectives is not necessarily static, adhering to a “learn and confirm” cycle [42]. As new data become available, the understanding of the drug's behavior may evolve. This, in turn, may necessitate refinement of the initial modeling objectives, model refinement to include emerging information, or the identification of new QoIs that can be addressed with an appropriately qualified model within a revised or expanded CoU. Any post hoc modifications to the objectives or CoU should be transparently documented and justified, particularly concerning their impact on the planned model influence.

Data Compilation: Gathering System Parameter, Drug and Formulation‐Specific Information and Experimental PK Data

Collection, curation, and critical evaluation of system parameters, drug‐specific information and relevant experimental data are foundational to developing a credible PBPK model. Ideally, a comprehensive MIDD strategy identifies the necessary data, either existing or in need of generation, for model development. This cyclical process involves designing studies, potentially with existing PBPK models, analyzing the generated data to confirm hypotheses, and finally integrating these new data for model refinement [42]. The quality, precision, and completeness of these input data directly and significantly impact the predictive performance and reliability of the PBPK model [16, 37, 43]. The focus should be on the quality and information that the data brings to the model development not the quantity of data included. Regulatory authorities expect clear documentation regarding the origin, justification, and potential uncertainties associated with all data used in model development and evaluation [16, 37]. To clarify which data informs each part of the model, a schematic representation (Figure 2, modified from Kanacher et al. [44]) should be provided in addition to a data summary table. In this illustrative example, the authors predicted the impact of the CYP3A4 inducer, rifampicin, on the PK of Bevurogant/BI 730357 in fed conditions to inform future study design. System Parameter Information

These define the physiological framework of the model. Modern PBPK software platforms include extensive, built‐in databases of anatomical and physiological parameters for humans and various preclinical species, curated from peer‐reviewed literature. These databases should be version‐controlled and transparently sourced by the software or platform provider [45]. To simulate population pharmacokinetics and predict variability, Inter‐Individual Variability (IIV) in physiological parameters is incorporated. This is often achieved by defining statistical distributions and covariance structures for key parameters within the virtual population generation algorithms of PBPK software [46]. This allows for the creation of virtual populations aimed at reflecting real‐world heterogeneity [47]. However, the modeler retains the responsibility to critically assess and verify that default physiological parameters are appropriate for the specific population, disease state, and QoI under consideration.

Drug Information

A thorough inventory of the investigational drug's physicochemical and in vitro ADME characteristics is essential. Key parameters include, but are not limited to, molecular weight, lipophilicity (logP or logD at physiological pH or logMA), pKa values, solubility, plasma protein binding, and intestinal permeability. Biopharmaceutics data, such as particle size distribution and dissolution profiles for orally administered drugs, are also critical. Document data generation methods (in silico, in vitro, in vivo), acknowledging inherent assumptions, variability, and limitations.

In silico predictions: Often used for initial estimates (e.g., logP [48] and permeability [49]), their uncertainty should be acknowledged, and they should ideally be confirmed by experimental data where feasible. Quantitative Structure–Activity Relationship (QSAR) and Quantitative Structure‐Properties Relationship (QSPR) models are widely applied computational tools for generating in silico predictions by establishing relationships between chemical structure features and biological/physicochemical endpoints [49]. Detailed discussion on these methodologies are however outside the scope of this report.

Interpreting these parameters requires careful consideration of the specific in vitro assay used, as experimental setups like preincubation conditions and substrate concentrations can significantly influence the derived kinetic constants. Crucially, understanding the mechanism of inhibition (e.g., competitive, non‐competitive, uncompetitive, mixed, or mechanism‐based inactivation) is essential for accurate DDI prediction. Thorough documentation of these mechanistic details, along with the methodologies, ensures the predictive power of PBPK models for complex DDI scenarios.

In vivo data: Information from preclinical, and when available clinical, mass balance studies is critical for understanding the overall routes and extent of elimination (metabolism and excretion) and identifying relevant metabolites [51]. A key aspect is distinguishing between methodologies using radiolabelled compound, enabling the monitoring, identification, and quantification of the parent drug and all drug‐related substances (including metabolites) by tracking total radioactivity, versus methods without radiolabel with the inherently risk of missing uncharacterized or novel metabolites that may contribute significantly to the overall drug‐related material. Another critical aspect of interpreting mass balance data for PBPK modeling is accounting for conversion and reconversion phenomena. This refers to the interconversion of pharmacologically active and inactive drug enantiomers or a drug and its metabolites, or even between different metabolites, within the body (e.g., methylprednisolone and methylprednisone interconversion [52]). Radiolabelled studies, by tracking total radioactivity, help to capture these complex dynamics by providing the sum of all drug‐related species. However, chromatographic and structural elucidation, often aided by enzyme incubation experiments (e.g., with glucuronidases or sulfatases), is necessary to identify and quantify individual metabolites and infer their interconversion pathways [53]. This detailed understanding of metabolite fate, including potential enteric excretion and reabsorption, is paramount for building accurate PBPK models that faithfully represent the in vivo disposition of the parent drug and its key metabolites. These insights guide the appropriate partitioning of clearance between different organs and the incorporation of relevant transport and metabolic pathways within the PBPK framework. Furthermore, integration of clinical DDI data may provide for retrospective refinement of f m by specific enzymatic pathways. While in vitro recombinant systems and liver microsomes offer initial estimates of enzymatic contribution, these estimates may require scaling as they may not fully account for in vivo complexities such as compensatory metabolism or transporter‐enzyme interplay. By leveraging clinical data from co‐administration studies with potent, selective inhibitors (e.g., ketoconazole for CYP3A4 or fluconazole for CYP2C9), the in vivo f m can be determined with higher precision. This iterative optimization ensures that the PBPK models remain predictive across diverse patient populations and polypharmacy scenarios.

Formulation‐Specific Information

This information is essential for biopharmaceutics application (using PBBM), where it is crucial to understand the relationship among the drug, formulation excipients, and the physiological system. Key parameters include the aqueous drug solubility at varying pH, solubility in presence of bile salts and polymers (relevant for, e.g., Amorphous Solid Dispersion (ASD) formulations), and the drug or product particle size distribution. In vitro dissolution data, ideally obtained from aqueous buffers and biorelevant media, are vital for understanding dissolution kinetics, while potential precipitation should be assessed using, for example, a two‐stage assay. Additionally, critical material attributes (CMAs), like the amorphous content, should be quantified, especially when manufacturing flexibility is important. Further details can be found in the recently published PBBM report template [54].

For non‐oral routes (e.g., dermal, intramuscular, subcutaneous, inhalation), specific formulation quality attributes (e.g., viscosity, droplet size, specific excipients, particle size distribution in aerosols) substantially influence both local and systemic exposure and should be similarly characterized.

Experimental Pharmacokinetic (PK) Data

Observed PK data from both preclinical and clinical studies are fundamental for both the development and evaluation of PBPK models. Complete concentration versus time profiles, including detailed absorption phase data and sufficient sampling during the elimination phase to accurately characterize clearance and volume of distribution, are of highest utility. It is imperative to thoroughly understand and document the context in which these PK data were generated:

Population Characteristics

Comprehensive details of the study population are essential. This includes species, strain, and genetic background (for animals); for human studies, age, sex, body weight/height (BMI), ethnicity, relevant genetic polymorphisms (e.g., for metabolizing enzymes like CYPs or transporters like OATPs), health status (healthy volunteers or specific patient populations), disease state and severity (including quantitative measures of organ function, for example, liver or kidney function tests if impaired), and any co‐administered medications that could be confounders. For preclinical studies, it is important to note whether studies were conducted in conscious, freely moving animals versus anesthetized animals, as anesthetics and surgical procedures can significantly alter physiological parameters (e.g., organ blood flows, cardiac output, body temperature) and thereby influence drug PK [16, 37].

Drug Administration: Route, Dosing, Formulation and Conditions

Precise details of drug administration are required: the route of administration (e.g., intravenous (IV) (bolus or infusion), oral, transdermal, subcutaneous, inhalation, ocular), the exact dose administered (e.g., mg/kg or total dose), the dosing regimen (single dose, multiple doses with the dose interval (tau), time to reach steady state) and infusion duration for IV infusions. The exact formulation (e.g., solution, suspension, capsule, solid tablet, microarray patch, nasal spray) used in the PK study with reference to its composition if relevant (batch number can be useful for formulation prototype in early stages). For oral studies, the conditions of administration (e.g., fasted or fed state, timing relative to meals, nature and caloric/fat content of meals if fed) must be documented due to potential food effects on bioavailability [16, 37].

Reference Compounds

In circumstances where the PBPK model for the investigational drug incorporates complex mechanisms or is being extrapolated to new scenarios, the use of PK data from well‐characterized reference compounds can be instrumental. This approach is particularly relevant when specific model extensions are made (e.g., incorporating detailed transporter kinetics, specific enzyme isoforms, or unique distribution characteristics).

These data are primarily used to evaluate the consistency and robustness of the PBPK model's description of the shared mechanisms. Successful simulation of the reference compound(s) PK, using the same mechanistic model structure and system parameters (where appropriate), lends credibility to the model's ability to characterize these specific pathways before applying it to predict the behavior of the investigational drug (or test and reference formulations in the case of virtual bioequivalence [vBE] assessment). This is a critical step in building confidence in the model, particularly for its prospective applications. If reference compounds are used:

Selection criteria: These compounds should be selected based on their sharing of essential physicochemical properties and, most importantly, the same relevant physiological and biochemical ADME pathways that are being modeled for the investigational drug.

Selection criteria: These compounds should be selected based on their sharing of essential physicochemical properties and, most importantly, the same relevant physiological and biochemical ADME pathways that are being modeled for the investigational drug.

Data requirements: Comprehensive and high‐quality PK data for these reference compounds, under various relevant conditions (e.g., different doses, routes of administration, in the presence of inhibitors/inducers if DDI mechanisms are being evaluated), should be gathered with the same level of detail regarding population, dosing, and assay as for the primary drug.

Model Development

Once the modeling objectives (QoI, CoU, MI) are defined and the necessary drug information and experimental data have been gathered and critically evaluated, the process of establishing the PBPK model structure commences. The development of a PBPK model is an iterative, science‐driven undertaking that integrates known physiological information with drug‐specific properties to simulate its PK behavior. Careful consideration of model structure, assumptions, parameterization, and data sources is fundamental to constructing a model that is fit for its CoU and capable of reliably addressing the QoI. This typically involves a cycle of model building, parameterization, simulation, comparison with experimental data, and refinement.

Navigating the Landscape of PBPK Software

The landscape of PBPK modeling is characterized by both well‐established commercial software and powerful open‐source platforms. Commercially available systems such as Simcyp [55, 56], with its extensive curated population and compound libraries, and GastroPlus [57], which provides detailed mechanistic models for oral absorption, have a long history of application in regulatory submissions. In 2017, the PBPK/QSP software platform consisting of PK‐Sim and MoBi, which was previously available only commercially, was released as open‐source software. This platform became part of the Open Systems Pharmacology (OSP) Suite [45, 58], combining complete software transparency with the methodological flexibility that characterized the earlier versions of these tools.

Regulatory authorities, including the FDA and the EMA, maintain a platform‐agnostic stance given that the fundamental requirement of adequate verification is fullfilled [59]. Verification serves as the foundational quality control process to ensure that the technical implementation is both accurate and reliable. It focuses on auditing model code, confirming that mathematical equations correctly represent model assumptions, and validating the precision of all calculations. Beyond the code itself, verification encompasses the Quality Assurance of the software environment, requiring rigorous testing, installation protocols, and version tracking. To meet regulatory standards, all verification activities must be thoroughly documented and maintained for potential inspection, ensuring full transparency in how data was processed and analyzed. This ensures that the environment of the analysis is scientifically robust and reproducible, to allow for establishment of a valid PBPK model for regulatory decisions.

Approaches for Model Development

The first step is to outline in what way processes should be described and informed. This is influenced by both the current level of information and the QoI. In case of mechanism‐based processes, specific parameter values of each process can then either be informed purely via scaling of in vitro measurements (bottom‐up approach) or optimized towards reference data (middle‐out approach). In cases where processes are to be generically described, most often due to limited mechanistic knowledge, the processes can be informed solely by observed PK data (top‐down approach). In complex scenarios, it is not uncommon that different approaches will be adopted for different processes depending on different levels of information. Similarly, the selected approach may need to be revised during development to achieve adequate results. Such cases should be clearly indicated and properly discussed.

Establishing Systemic Distribution and Elimination (IV Model)

The foundational step in PBPK model establishment is typically the development of a model describing the drug's systemic distribution and elimination processes (metabolism and excretion), utilizing IV pharmacokinetic data. IV administration bypasses the complexities of drug absorption, allowing the model development process to focus initially on characterizing how the drug moves between blood and tissues and how it is removed from the body. This core systemic model serves as the scaffold upon which absorption models (for extravascular routes) are later built.

Systemic Distribution

The first stage involves selecting an appropriate mathematical description for drug distribution into various tissues and initialising model parameters based on the gathered drug information. Model structure defines the mathematical representation of the organism and the drug's disposition pathways. Key structural decisions encompass the level of detail in representing tissues and the assumptions governing drug transport across membranes.

Choosing between whole body versus minimal PBPK models: A primary structural decision is the level of granularity. Whole‐body PBPK models endeavor to represent all physiologically relevant organs and tissues as discrete compartments, interconnected by systemic blood flow. This approach affords a high degree of mechanistic detail, enabling prediction of drug concentrations in specific tissues and a granular understanding of disposition processes. Such models are often preferred when the QoI involves tissue‐specific effects or complex distribution. Conversely, Minimal PBPK (mPBPK) [60, 61] or Lumped PBPK models [62, 63] offer a simplified representation by grouping multiple tissues with similar characteristics or combining less critical tissues into a “rest‐of‐body” compartment. This approach can be advantageous when available data for detailed parameterization are limited or for reducing model complexity. The selection of model structure must be justified based on the QoI, data availability, and the required level of mechanistic detail for the CoU. For instance, if the CoU is to evaluate DDI potential at the hepatic transporter/metabolizing enzyme level, a mPBPK model may still be acceptable as long as the mechanistic factors involved in the DDI process are adequately described.

Test different distribution models: PBPK platforms often offer several algorithms to calculate tissue‐to‐plasma partition coefficients (K p values), which determine the extent of distribution into different organs. Common approaches range from empirical correlations based on lipophilicity and plasma protein binding to more mechanistic methods incorporating tissue composition (water, lipid fractions, proteins) and the drug's physicochemical properties, including its ionization state (pKa) [64]. For example, methods described by Rodgers & Rowland, Poulin & Theil, or Schmitt and implemented in various software differ in their assumptions about binding within tissues and the influence of ionization [64]. It is important to recognize that these standard distribution algorithms function as prediction models, generating a priori estimates of tissue partitioning based primarily on drug physicochemical properties and average tissue compositions. The choice of distribution model should be considered carefully, justified based on the drug's properties, and the impact of different plausible models on drug distribution predictions should be evaluated, particularly for tissues relevant to the drug's efficacy, toxicity, or elimination. Some methods explicitly use pKa values to account for differential partitioning of ionized and un‐ionized species and potential ion‐trapping, which can be crucial for accurately predicting distribution, especially for ionizable compounds. Regardless of which distribution method is used, the model‐predicted distribution should be in agreement with the observed distribution.

Expected prediction performance: Standard distribution algorithms function as prediction models. Given inherent biological variability and the simplifications involved in these predictive algorithms, it is generally not expected that a priori calculations will accurately predict tissue distribution (e.g., within a narrow margin like 20% error) across all tissues for every compound [35, 64]. Significant deviations between predicted and actual tissue distribution can occur. Modelers are encouraged to consult the original publications describing these distribution methods to understand their derivation, underlying assumptions, and reported predictive performance or limitations [64]. Comparative evaluations, such as the PhRMA CPCDC initiative assessing Vss prediction methods [65] and studies using standardized tissue compositions to compare K p methods directly [66], have consistently shown that no single a priori K p prediction method is universally superior across all chemical space (e.g., acids, bases, neutrals, zwitterions) [65, 66]. The predictive performance of a given method can vary depending on the specific properties of the drug relative to the assumptions underlying the algorithm [66]. Therefore, a recommended practice during initial model development is to evaluate the sensitivity of the PBPK model predictions (e.g., simulated PK profiles, key parameters like Vss) to the choice of K p prediction method [66]. This may involve running initial simulations using K p sets derived from multiple plausible methods to understand the range of potential outcomes based solely on this structural choice.

Systemic Elimination

In similarity with distribution, parameters governing metabolic and/or excretory clearance are frequently adjusted to match the in vivo observed systemic clearance and PK profile shape. It is preferable to input and adjust parameters directly related to the mechanism, such as intrinsic clearance (CLint) for hepatic metabolism or renal secretory clearance (CLsec), rather than adjusting total plasma clearance (CLp). These parameters represent the intrinsic capacity of the system independently of other factors, such as blood flow or protein binding, providing a more mechanistically sound basis for extrapolations. In the bottom‐up approach, CLint derived from in vitro systems (scaled using IVIVE—in vitro‐in vivo extrapolation) provides a mechanistic basis. This involves utilizing scaling factors (e.g., microsomal protein per gram of liver (MPPGL), hepatocellularity, organ weights, blood flows) to extrapolate in vitro rates to the whole organ or systemic level [68, 69, 70, 71]. The specific IVIVE methods, scaling factors, and underlying assumptions (e.g., well‐stirred model for hepatic clearance) must be clearly described and justified. The uncertainties associated with IVIVE should be explored (e.g., via sensitivity analysis). Furthermore, data from human radiolabelled mass balance studies are highly valuable. These studies provide crucial information on the quantitative contribution of different elimination pathways (e.g., metabolism vs. renal excretion vs. biliary excretion) to the overall clearance of the drug and its metabolites. However, it is crucial that these mass balance data are critically interpreted within the context of all known or suspected disposition mechanisms for the drug. Failing to consider potential complexities, such as extensive enteric excretion and reabsorption significantly altering the apparent contribution of fecal excretion, formation of pharmacologically active or long‐lived metabolites, or complex transporter interplay affecting secretion, can lead to a distorted view of the drug's overall disposition and subsequent misinformed parameterisation of clearance pathways in the PBPK model. Incorporating mass balance information allows for a more mechanistically accurate parameterisation of the respective clearance processes within the PBPK model structure, moving beyond simply fitting total systemic clearance. If multiple elimination pathways exist and mass balance data is limited or complex to interpret, information from specific in vitro phenotyping studies should be used to inform the relative contribution of each pathway, allowing for more targeted parameter adjustments (e.g., refining the CLint for a specific enzyme).

Parameter Refinement Using IV Data

If initial ‘bottom‐up’ parameterisation does not adequately capture the observed IV PK profiles, refinement of key drug‐dependent parameters is often necessary. PBPK models integrate two primary categories of parameters: those describing the biological system (organism‐dependent) and those describing the drug (drug‐dependent). This middle‐out adjustment should be performed sensibly, focusing on parameters known to have significant uncertainty or influence on the PK profile, and always with physiological/biochemical plausibility in mind. Understanding which secondary parameters are derived from the drug‐dependant parameters are essential (e.g., the drug lipophilicity is used to calculate multiple distribution‐ and permeability‐related parameters) as well as paying attention to the correlation of parameters in case multiple parameters are estimated simultaneously. It is often recommended to first perform manual adjustments of a few key selected parameters (identified potentially via sensitivity analysis) to understand their impact on model behavior and ensure physiological plausibility before employing automated parameter estimation algorithms. In addition to enzymatic and transporter kinetics, model refinement frequently requires the adjustments of other parameters to better capture the compound's disposition:

Lipophilicity (logPo:w or logMA): This parameter significantly influences calculated tissue partition coefficients (K p values) and potentially permeability. While experimentally measured or reliably predicted values should be the starting point, some adjustment might be needed to better match observed distribution volumes or terminal phase slopes. Although existing software's offer different adjustment procedures (e.g., K p scalar or direct adjustment of the lipophilicity parameter), these procedures are basically equivalent. Adjustments should generally be kept minimal; for instance, changing an initial logPo:w value by more than approximately 1 log unit often requires strong justification, especially for lipophilic compounds (logP > 1). Larger adjustments might be justifiable for very hydrophilic compounds or if the initial estimate was highly uncertain. Finally, logD at physiological pH may be a more relevant descriptor for ionizable compounds as it reflects lipophilicity in biological environments.

Illustrative example: refinement of f m using clinical DDI data.

A PBPK model was developed for an SAR compound, initiating with preclinical species (rat, dog, monkey) to establish clearance, distribution, and permeability parameters. IV administration data informed the estimation of species‐specific intrinsic clearance, while oral solution data served to validate intestinal permeability derived from Caco‐2 cell studies. These parameters were subsequently scaled to humans by maintaining key drug properties (lipophilicity, distribution model, intestinal permeability, solubility) fixed while implementing human physiological parameters. Human clearance and Weibull dissolution parameters were estimated from Phase 1 clinical data (naïve‐pooled approach; Monte‐Carlo estimation). Metabolism was initially assigned based on in vitro data as 90% CYP3A4 and 10% non‐CYP processes (i.e., f m CYP3A4 = 0.9). However, this initial model build resulted in an overprediction of the observed DDI with itraconazole. Model refinement was thus necessitated. After confirming that the model adequately described the independent pharmacokinetics of the victim (SAR compound) and perpetrators (itraconazole, hydroxy‐itraconazole) in a simulated population (n = 100), the f m CYP3A4 was manually adjusted from 0.90 to 0.57 (with 0.43 assigned to non‐CYP processes). This adjustment was performed to align model predictions with the observed clinical DDI data (e.g., AUC and C max ratios), while the total intrinsic clearance value remained unchanged. The resulting refined model, incorporating an f m value informed by clinical DDI data.

Systemic Distribution and Elimination Model Evaluation

The adequacy of the developed IV model is assessed by comparing its simulations against the available experimental IV PK data.

Data comparison: All available relevant IV data (e.g., multiple dose levels, different infusion rates, data from different populations) should be used for model development and evaluation. Visual predictive checks (comparing simulated concentration‐time profiles with observed data, using prediction intervals) and quantitative metrics (e.g., comparing simulated vs. observed AUC, C max, CL, Vss) should be employed.

Use of IV microdose data: In this context, IV data obtained from microdose (Phase 0) studies can also be highly valuable, particularly in early development when therapeutic dose IV data may be unavailable or difficult to obtain [72]. Such data can provide initial insights into human systemic clearance, volume of distribution, and help assess pharmacokinetic linearity at very low exposures, thereby informing PBPK model development at an early stage [72, 73]. However, a critical consideration when using microdose data for PBPK model development is the assumption of pharmacokinetic linearity [73, 74]. Extrapolation from microdose to therapeutic dose levels may not be reliable if non‐linear processes (e.g., saturable metabolism, transporter effects, target‐mediated drug disposition) become significant at higher concentrations [74]. Therefore, while potentially informative, models developed or evaluated using solely microdose IV data require careful assessment regarding their applicability to the therapeutic dose range. If microdose data are used, the PBPK model may need confirmation or refinement when therapeutic dose data become available.

A sensitivity analysis performed prior to parameter estimation is highly recommended to identify which parameters have the most significant influence on the model outputs of interest (e.g., plasma concentrations, AUC) and are therefore most suitable and necessary to include in the estimation process [33, 75]. Systematic evaluation is essential for understanding the impact of parametric uncertainty, identifying the most influential parameters that may require further refinement or data, and ultimately building confidence in the model's assumptions and its predictive capabilities [75]. It is crucial to recognize that identifying parameters associated with non‐linear processes (e.g., Michaelis–Menten constants K m and V max for saturable clearance or transport) requires data that demonstrate this non‐linearity, typically PK data across a range of doses where saturation becomes evident. Fitting such models to data from only a single dose level often leads to non‐identifiable parameters. Bayesian methods offer a framework to integrate prior information about parameters (from in vitro data or literature, along with their uncertainty, expressed as prior distributions) with observed in vivo PK data (via a likelihood function) to derive updated parameter estimates (posterior distributions) [76]. This approach is particularly useful for sparse data situations and for robustly quantifying parameter uncertainty. Model averaging can also be employed to account for structural uncertainty [64, 65]. This involves developing multiple plausible models, each reflecting a different reasonable assumption (e.g., different K p prediction methods or clearance scaling assumptions). By averaging their predictions, weighted by each model's probability of being correct (often based on goodness‐of‐fit), the resulting averaged prediction provides a more robust estimate that reflects the range of possibilities for these uncertain parameters [33, 75].

Value of IV data and alternatives: IV data are highly valuable because they allow distribution and elimination processes to be characterized largely independently of absorption complexities. Establishing a robust IV model provides a strong foundation for subsequent development of models for extravascular routes. Developing a PBPK model using only per oral (PO) data is possible but introduces significant challenges and uncertainties. Without IV data, estimates of systemic clearance (CL) and volume of distribution (V) are confounded by the unknown bioavailability (F) and absorption rate (k a). The model must then rely more heavily on IVIVE predictions for clearance and PBPK methods for distribution, potentially increasing uncertainty. An alternative option is to fix the absorption (permeability) to measured or predicted values and still estimate clearance. This uncertainty is particularly pronounced for drugs with low solubility and/or low permeability (BCS Class II, IV), or those subject to significant gut‐wall metabolism or transporter effects, where absorption processes heavily influence the observed PK. In situations where human IV data are unavailable, PK data in humans from an oral solution or immediate‐release (IR) formulation may be a better alternative to animal data when the drug shows complete gut absorption and/or has > 85% absolute bioavailability. In some instance, if animal IV and PO data exist, scaling an animal PBPK model (validated with both IV and PO data) to humans might be a viable strategy. If IV data are completely lacking (both human and relevant animal) and the drug does not show complete gut absorption nor bioavailability > 85%, the suitability and credibility of using a PBPK approach must be carefully evaluated in light of the specific QoI and CoU. The increased uncertainty associated with key systemic parameters (CL, V, F) must be acknowledged and assessed (e.g., through extensive sensitivity analysis) to determine if the model can still provide sufficiently reliable information for its intended purpose [35].

Initial parameter adjustments and need for optimization: Before adding more complex mechanisms (like active transport), it is essential to evaluate the model's performance using the chosen distribution and permeability approaches and the initial ‘bottom‐up’ parameters according to the middle‐out approach. As highlighted by comparative initiatives [65], while a priori tissue‐composition based K p predictions provide necessary initial estimates for PBPK models, subsequent optimization or refinement of tissue distribution parameters is often necessary and expected once relevant in vivo reference data (e.g., observed plasma PK profiles suggesting specific apparent volumes of distribution or distribution kinetics, or ideally, measured tissue concentration data) become available [65]. Adjustments to uncertain drug‐dependent parameters within scientifically plausible ranges should be explored at this stage to assess the basic model's capability to describe the observed IV PK data, serving as a bridge between initial prediction and data‐informed refinement using in vivo evidence.

Adjustments to uncertain drug‐dependent parameters within scientifically plausible ranges should be explored at this stage to assess the basic model's capability to describe the observed IV PK data, serving as a bridge between initial prediction and data‐informed refinement using in vivo evidence.

Extravascular Model: Characterizing Absorption and First‐Pass Effects

Once a robust PBPK model describing the systemic distribution and elimination characteristics of the drug has been established and verified using IV data (the “IV model”), the model can be extended to describe PK profiles following PO or other extravascular routes of administration. A cardinal principle in this sequential approach is that parameters primarily influencing systemic distribution and elimination (e.g., tissue partition coefficients, systemic clearances), which were defined and validated with IV data, should generally remain fixed or be subject to minimal, highly justified changes during the development of the PO model. The primary focus of the PO model development is to accurately characterize the processes governing drug absorption from the gastrointestinal (GI) tract and any first‐pass extraction in the gut wall and liver.

Adjust Parameters Influencing Absorption

In analogy with IV model development, if initial ‘bottom‐up’ parameterisation does not adequately capture the observed PO PK profiles, refinement of key PO drug‐dependent parameters is often necessary. When fitting the model to PO data, adjustments will typically then focus on parameters directly related to the rate and extent of drug absorption:

Intestinal permeability (P int ): Initial estimates of P int are often derived from in vitro measurements, for example, cell monolayer or artificial membrane assays, or in silico predictions based on physicochemical properties. These a priori permeability values need to be appropriately scaled given the parameterization of the absorption model at hand. An adjustment by a factor of up to approximately 3‐fold is often considered reasonable to describe the observed fraction absorbed and absorption rate for BCS Class I drugs. For drugs with predicted permeability and/or solubility limitations, larger adjustment factors might be justifiable, but should be supported by an understanding of the drug's properties and potential limitations of the initial prediction method. In addition, the potential for colonic absorption needs to be assessed.

Modify Model Structure for Oral Absorption

If adjustments to the absorption parameters are insufficient to describe the observed PO PK profiles, or if known mechanisms influencing oral bioavailability are not yet included, structural modifications to the GI tract model may be necessary. This often involves incorporating:

Intestinal active transport processes: If the drug is a known substrate for intestinal influx (e.g., PEPT1, OATPs) or efflux transporters (e.g., P‐glycoprotein (P‐gp), BCRP), these processes should be incorporated into the intestinal segments of the PBPK model [47]. This requires parameterisation of transporter kinetics (e.g., K m, V max), which should ideally be informed by in vitro data; however, developing reliable, quantitative, biopredictive assays for most transporters remains challenging. The directionality (uptake from lumen or efflux into lumen) and location (apical or basolateral membrane of enterocytes) of these transporters are critical.

Comparing Simulations With Experimental PK Data for Oral Administration

The principles for evaluating the PO model against experimental data are similar to those for the IV model, involving visual predictive checks and quantitative comparison of PK parameters (AUC, C max, T max).

Specific strategy for different formulations: A recommended best practice, if PK data are available for both an IR formulation and one or more modified‐release (MR) or controlled‐release (CR) formulations, is to adopt a stepwise approach: ○First, establish and validate the absorption model using data from the IR formulation. During this step, parameters like intestinal permeability, solubilities, and potentially gut‐wall metabolism/transport parameters are adjusted. This step should preferably also include data from an oral solution or suspension to delineate any influence from the IR formulation.○Once the IR model adequately describes the data, this core absorption model (with its now‐defined parameters) is used as the basis for modeling the MR/CR formulations. For the MR/CR data, only the formulation‐specific drug release parameters (e.g., dissolution profiles, release rate constants) should be adjusted. Parameters related to intrinsic permeability, solubility (unless formulation significantly alters it in a way not captured by release), and systemic disposition should remain fixed from the previous steps. This approach helps to deconvolve drug‐specific absorption properties from formulation‐specific release properties. Still, if absorption extends into colonic segments for MR/CR formulations, regional intestinal permeability may require reassessment and cannot always be directly informed from the IR model.

Note on Sequential Versus Simultaneous IV/PO Model Development

The stepwise approach described (establishing the IV model first, then fixing systemic parameters and developing the PO absorption model) is generally recommended. This ensures that information from IV data, which directly informs systemic distribution and clearance processes without the confounding factor of absorption, is appropriately leveraged. This separation helps to improve parameter identifiability for systemic versus absorption‐related parameters. However, there are situations where this strictly consecutive model building might not be optimal, and a simultaneous fitting approach using both IV and PO data might be preferred or necessary [35]:

Shared mechanistic pathways: If the PBPK model incorporates processes that are critical for both systemic disposition after IV administration and oral absorption/first‐pass effects (e.g., significant hepatic uptake transport influencing both systemic clearance and hepatic first‐pass extraction; saturable systemic metabolism that also contributes to first‐pass metabolism), then simultaneously fitting IV and PO data can provide more robust and identifiable estimates for the parameters governing these shared pathways.

Shared mechanistic pathways: If the PBPK model incorporates processes that are critical for both systemic disposition after IV administration and oral absorption/first‐pass effects (e.g., significant hepatic uptake transport influencing both systemic clearance and hepatic first‐pass extraction; saturable systemic metabolism that also contributes to first‐pass metabolism), then simultaneously fitting IV and PO data can provide more robust and identifiable estimates for the parameters governing these shared pathways.

Data richness and quality: If the available IV data are sparse (e.g., limited sampling times, short duration profiles, high variability) while the PO data are more extensive and informative, a simultaneous fit might better leverage the richer dataset to inform shared parameters, provided the model structure is appropriate. Appropriate weighting of the different datasets (IV vs. PO, different dose levels) is crucial in such simultaneous estimations.

Even when a simultaneous approach is chosen, the fundamental principle of trying to distinguish systemic disposition from absorption processes as much as possible should guide the modeling strategy and parameterisation. Sensitivity analyses and careful evaluation of parameter correlations are particularly important in simultaneous fitting to ensure robust parameter estimation [75].

Other Extravascular Routes

While oral administration is the most common route, PBPK models are increasingly developed and applied to understand and predict drug disposition following various other extravascular routes, including pulmonary (inhalation) [82], dermal [83], subcutaneous [84], intramuscular, ocular [85], and nasal delivery [86]. Detailed modeling strategies and data requirements are highly specific to each administration route. For instance, PBPK modeling of pulmonary delivery necessitates careful consideration of particle deposition models, dissolution in lung lining fluid, mucociliary clearance mechanisms, and absorption across the alveolar‐capillary barrier [87]. Similarly, models for subcutaneous administration must account for factors such as local blood flow and lymphatic drainage at the injection site, drug binding to interstitial components, potential local degradation, and the influence of injection volume and formulation properties on absorption kinetics [88]. PBPK models for dermal absorption often include multiple skin layers (stratum corneum, viable epidermis, dermis) with distinct diffusional and partitioning properties [89]. Developing credible PBPK models for these routes requires a deep understanding of the route‐specific physiology, formulation science, and drug properties, along with relevant experimental data to inform and validate the model structure and parameters.

Note on Parameter Estimation

If parameters (e.g., refined CLint, transporter kinetics, binding parameters) need to be determined by fitting the model to the data, formal parameter estimation (optimization) techniques are used. This process aims to find parameter values that minimize the difference between model predictions and observed data according to a defined objective function. Any parameter estimation or optimization strategy should be prospectively defined, with clear justification for the parameters selected for optimization, the data used, the objective function, and the method employed. The resulting parameter estimates should be critically evaluated for their mechanistic plausibility. When estimating multiple parameters simultaneously or when data are sparse, assessing parameter identifiability is crucial. Approaches to address non‐identifiability include stepwise parameter estimation, incorporating informative prior distributions (Bayesian approaches), or conducting local and global sensitivity analyses to ensure that the data contain sufficient information to uniquely determine the parameters.

Note on Sensitivity Analysis and Parameter Uncertainty Quantification

Local sensitivity analysis, conducted using a base case and perturbing parameters one‐at‐a‐time, is used to identify the primary drivers influencing the model outcomes and highlighting any non‐linear “tipping points” where the output is most volatile. Such analyses are readily reported via Tornado plots for ranking variable impacts and Spider plots for visualizing linearity and rates of change. Global sensitivity analysis explores the entire parameter space simultaneously to quantify how interactions, correlations, and collective variations drive model uncertainty, often visualized through Sobol indices or scatterplots. Report these findings by highlighting total‐effect indices to account for multi‐variable synergies, providing a more comprehensive risk assessment than isolated, one‐at‐a‐time perturbations [75, 90, 91, 92, 93].

While global sensitivity analysis and Monte‐Carlo simulation‐based uncertainty propagation methods are applied to quantify the relative contribution of each process to the total uncertainty of the model outcome, Maximum Likelihood Estimation and advanced Bayesian methods have been proposed for quantification of population parameters, variability, and uncertainties. A Bayesian Hierarchical Meta‐Analysis approach has also been suggested to assess bias and imprecision on a platform level by analyzing collated data from multiple reference studies. Still, it is currently premature to establish definitive best practices for uncertainty quantification, and it is important to remember that the output from conventional PBPK models does not intend to fully replicate clinical trial outcomes as residual uncertainty is not an integrated part of the fundamental methodology.

Model Evaluation

The primary objective of model evaluation is to establish sufficient credibility that the model is fit‐for‐purpose within its defined CoU [16, 33, 37]. The required evaluation rigor must be proportional to the model risk [33, 94]. Evaluation is not a single event but an iterative process, and should be integrated throughout model development and refinement, where findings inform further model improvements until the necessary level of credibility for the CoU is achieved [16, 33, 40]. Aligned with these principles, PBPK model evaluation encompasses: (1) Verification: confirming the mathematical and computational integrity of the model implementation; (2) Validation and applicability assessment: demonstrating the model's accuracy in representing the biological system through qualitative and quantitative comparisons against experimental data and ensuring the validated model scope, data, and underlying assumptions are adequate to address the specific QoI.

Model Verification

Model verification focuses on ensuring the integrity and correctness of the PBPK model implementation [33]. This involves confirming:

Correctness of implementation: The PBPK model equations representing physiological processes, drug‐specific properties, and any structural assumptions are correctly translated into the modeling software code. This may involve code review, comparison with standard model implementations, or unit testing of individual model components.

It should be noted that in applications using predefined PBPK platforms, software‐level verification of the mathematical implementations and parameterization is often conducted by the provider of the platform. Ideally, these verification processes should be transparent and accessible for peer review; otherwise, full accountability rests with the provider. Nevertheless, the onus is always on the modeler to confirm that the model architecture is appropriate for the QoI and that it is applied correctly within the study.

Model Validation

Model validation aims to establish that the PBPK model provides a credible and accurate representation of the relevant aspects of the biological system for the defined CoU [33]. This is primarily achieved by comparing model predictions with experimental data:

Goodness‐of‐fit assessment: This involves both qualitative and quantitative comparisons: ○ Graphical evaluation: Visual inspection of overlay plots comparing simulated concentration‐time profiles (for plasma, and tissues/excreta if available and relevant) against observed experimental data, including measures of central tendency and variability (e.g., mean profiles, individual data points, prediction intervals) [16, 37, 95]. Plots of observed versus predicted values for concentrations and key PK parameters (e.g., C max, AUC, t 1/2), and various types of residual plots (e.g., weighted residuals vs. time or prediction) should be used to identify systematic deviations, biases, or trends [95].○ Quantitative metrics: Calculation of statistical metrics to objectively assess the agreement between predictions and observations. These should include measures of overall agreement, bias, and precision. Common metrics include prediction error (PE), average fold error (AFE), absolute average fold error (AAFE), and root mean squared error (RMSE) for PK parameters and/or concentrations. For instance, AFE and AAFE are preferred for assessing systematic bias and average error, while RMSE is useful for overall predictive performance. Acceptance criteria, set with the CoU and model risk in mind, should be prospectively predefined in the analysis plan (e.g., predictions for key exposure parameters like AUC and C max should be within a predefined fold range of observed values). The 0.8–1.25‐fold criterion is typically appropriate for virtual bioequivalence (vBE) assessments [96], whereas a more flexible 2‐fold criterion may be justified for early‐stage development.While the aforementioned metrics provide a foundational assessment of model performance, several alternative expressions and refinements can offer more nuanced insights or facilitate easier interpretation across different datasets. Many of these measures represent mathematical transformations of the primary metrics; for instance, while AFE provides a measure of bias, the Mean Logarithmic Error (MLE) and Geometric Mean Fold Error (GMFE) offer standardized ways to express this central tendency on a logarithmic scale, preventing large values from disproportionately influencing the bias assessment. Similarly, refinements of precision such as the Relative Root Mean Squared Error (rRMSE) normalize the RMSE to the observed mean, allowing for a more direct comparison of model performance across different dose levels or physiological compartments. Furthermore, metrics such as the Concordance Correlation Coefficient (CCC) and Adjusted R‐squared extend the analysis of ‘overall agreement’ by accounting for repeated measures or penalizing for model complexity, ensuring that the assessment of predictive power remains robust against overfitting.

Applicability Assessment

Following verification and validation, an applicability (or “fit‐for‐purpose”) assessment should be conducted to determine if the evaluated model is indeed suitable and adequate to address the specific QoI within the defined CoU [33]. This involves considering:

Alignment of model scope with QoI and CoU: Does the model include all key physiological and drug‐specific mechanisms relevant to the question being addressed? Are the model assumptions appropriate and justified for the specific application and target population?

Adequacy of validation for intended use: Is the validation evidence (e.g., range of doses, populations, conditions under which the model showed good performance) relevant and sufficient for the intended prospective application of the model? Model Application

Once a PBPK model has been adequately developed, verified, validated, and applicability assessed for its predefined CoU, it can be applied to address the specific QoI. The application phase involves designing and executing simulation studies that leverage the mechanistic framework of the PBPK model to generate evidence supporting drug development decisions or regulatory submissions. This process should be prospectively outlined in the analysis plan and its outcomes thoroughly documented in the analysis report [33].

Designing Simulation Scenarios Aligned With the Question of Interest

The design of simulation studies should be directly guided by the QoI and the specifics of the CoU.

Defining virtual populations: Simulations are typically conducted in virtual populations that accurately represent the target patient population(s) for which predictions are sought. This includes defining subject characteristics such as age, sex, ethnicity, body weight distribution, and relevant genetic polymorphisms (e.g., for metabolizing enzymes or transporters if pertinent to the QoI). PBPK models are powerful tools for predicting PK in populations where clinical studies may be challenging to conduct. Incorporating and Quantifying Uncertainty and Variability

A key strength of PBPK modeling is its ability to explore the impact of uncertainty and variability on pharmacokinetic outcomes.

Parameter uncertainty: Uncertainty associated with key model input parameters (both drug‐specific, like in vitro clearances or permeability, and system‐specific, if customized) should be propagated through the model to understand its impact on the predictions relevant to the QoI. By assigning distributions to uncertain parameters, a distribution of predicted outcomes can be generated, providing prediction intervals (e.g., 5th‐95th percentile) around the simulated mean or median PK profiles or parameters [97, 109].

By assigning distributions to uncertain parameters, a distribution of predicted outcomes can be generated, providing prediction intervals (e.g., 5th‐95th percentile) around the simulated mean or median PK profiles or parameters [97, 109].

Inter‐individual variability (IIV): Accounting for inter‐individual variability is crucial for the characterization of the expected range of PK responses and the identification of potential sources of high variability. Indeed, PBPK models can mechanistically account for sources of IIV by incorporating variability in physiological parameters (e.g., organ volumes, blood flows, enzyme/transporter expression levels) within the virtual population [46, 47]. To better define these parameters, new frameworks are being developed, such as coupling whole‐body PBPK models with SAEM algorithms to estimate population parameters and inter‐individual variability directly from individual concentration data [110]. Simulating a sufficiently large virtual population (e.g., 500–1000 subjects) is critical for applications like predicting the range of exposures or to identify sub populations at risk (under or over exposed). When assessing the probability of success of virtual bioequivalences with parallel design, simulating a minimum of 100 times the number of planned subjects in the trial is good practice.

IOV can either be applied to post hoc pharmacokinetic estimates, as it has been done in the context of DDI simulations [111], and obviously in the context of vBE [112] or IOV can be estimated from multidose studies, and included on relevant physiological parameters known to vary from one occasion to the other (e.g., gastric emptying). This is critical for virtual bioequivalence assessments aimed at for example probability of success and study design optimization (e.g., sample size).

Documentation for Regulatory Interaction

Comprehensive, transparent, and well‐organized documentation is a critical prerequisite for the regulatory acceptance and efficient review of any MIDD submission involving PBPK models [16, 33, 37]. For regulatory interactions the fundamental goal is to provide a clear and complete understanding of the modeling approach, underlying data and assumptions, the evaluation performed, and the rationale for concluding that the model is fit for its intended CoU in addressing the specific QoI [33].

Conclusion

This paper presents a best‐practice PBPK focused framework, detailing a structured process for robust model development, evaluation, and reporting aligned with regulatory expectations.

This process commences with prospectively defining the QoI, CoU, and associated risks (Model Influence, Consequence of a Wrong Decision) per ICH M15. This definition dictates the necessary rigor for all subsequent activities. In addition, it is highly advisable to make use of the “assessment table” provided in the ICH M15 guideline in order not only to aid in regulatory interaction but also in discussions proceeding regulatory interaction. The central tenet is the ‘fit‐for‐purpose’ principle: a model's credibility must be demonstrably sufficient for its CoU, proportional to the influence and risk of the decision it supports.

Adhering to these practices—including systematic development, formal evaluation, and comprehensive documentation—will enhance the quality and regulatory acceptance of PBPK submissions, solidifying their role in quantitative decision‐making.
