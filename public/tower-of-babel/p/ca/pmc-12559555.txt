Factors associated with taxane-associated acute pain syndrome in pancreatic cancer patients receiving gemcitabine and nab-paclitaxel
Authors: Yoshitaka Saito, Yoh Takekuma, Yoshito Komatsu, Mitsuru Sugawara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTaxane-associated acute pain syndrome (T-APS) is a common adverse event in patients treated with gemcitabine (GEM) + nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-PTX) for pancreatic cancer. As several T-APS risk factors, such as age, cancer type, and taxane dose or formulation, differ between pancreatic cancer and other malignancies in previous studies, we aimed to identify factors associated with T-APS in a real-world setting of GEM + nab-PTX treatment for pancreatic cancer.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 394 patients with pancreatic cancer receiving GEM + nab-PTX. The primary endpoint was the identification of factors associated with all-grade T-APS. Symptom incidence was also compared across specific patient groups.ResultsT-APS occurred in 26.1% of patients after the first GEM + nab-PTX administration (grade 1: 21.6%; grade 2: 4.6%). Among patients with T-APS, 39.8% received medication, with more than two-thirds reporting symptom relief. Multivariable logistic regression analyses identified body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 as a risk factor and baseline use of mild analgesics (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [NSAIDs] and/or acetaminophen) as a preventive factor (adjusted odds ratio, 2.98; 95% confidence interval, 1.68–5.31; P = 0.0002 for BMI, 0.48; 0.26–0.89; P = 0.02 for mild analgesics). A subgroup analysis comparing baseline NSAIDs and acetaminophen in patients receiving mild analgesic monotherapy showed no significant difference (P = 0.49).ConclusionOur study identified BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 as a risk factor and baseline use of mild analgesics as a preventive factor for T-APS development in a real-world GEM + nab-PTX regimen for pancreatic cancer.Keywords: Taxane-associated acute pain syndrome (T-APS), Gemcitabine (GEM), Nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-PTX), Body mass index (BMI), Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), Acetaminophen

Introduction

Taxane-associated acute pain syndrome (T-APS) is one of the most frequently reported adverse events caused by taxanes such as paclitaxel, nanoparticle albumin-bound paclitaxel (nab-PTX), docetaxel, and cabazitaxel [1]. T-APS presents as a regional pain in the upper and/or lower extremities and lower back following taxane administration, though it can sometimes become generalized. It usually occurs 1 to 3 days after administration and lasts for approximately 1 week [2]. As T-APS is often unevaluated or underestimated in clinical studies assessing antitumor efficacy [3], its reported incidence varies widely; studies specifically focusing on T-APS report rates of 30.0–95.2% [4]. These symptoms significantly impair patients’ quality of life (QOL) and activities of daily living (ADL), often leading to treatment dose reductions.

Gemcitabine (GEM) + nab-PTX is one of the most effective treatment regimens for advanced pancreatic cancer [5–7]. Although the prescribing information for nab-PTX reports a T-APS incidence of approximately 10% [8], limited data exist on its incidence in clinical practice. Based on our experience, we believe the real-world incidence is higher than reported.

Previous reports have identified several risk factors for T-APS, including higher taxane dosage, paclitaxel use, younger age (< 55 years), metastatic disease, breast cancer, and co-administration of pegfilgrastim [1, 3, 4, 9, 10]. In contrast, corticosteroids [2, 10, 11], Shakuyaku-Kanzo-To [12], gabapentin [13], minocycline [14], and etoricoxib, a selective cyclooxygenase (COX)−2 inhibitor [15], have been suggested to prevent or attenuate T-APS. Because key factors like age, cancer type, and taxane dosage or formulation administered differ between pancreatic cancer and other cancers (most prior studies focused on breast cancer) [1–3, 9, 10], evaluating T-APS characteristics in this regimen is important for effective symptom management. Consequently, this study aimed to identify the risk factors for T-APS development in real-world GEM + nab-PTX treatment for pancreatic cancer.

Methods

Patients

Patients with pancreatic cancer who received GEM + nab-PTX between January 2015 and March 2025 at Hokkaido University Hospital were retrospectively assessed. All enrolled patients met the following baseline inclusion criteria: age ≥ 20 years; Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status (ECOG-PS) of 0 to 2; absolute neutrophil count ≥ 1.5 × 103 cells/µL; platelet count ≥ 7.0 × 104 cells/µL; adequate liver and renal function for treatment initiation; and sufficient medical record information. Patients were excluded if they had uncontrolled pain at baseline; were receiving regular corticosteroids, Shakuyaku-Kanzo-To, gabapentin, or minocycline at baseline; transferred to or from another hospital during the first treatment cycle; discontinued treatment within the first cycle; or had a prior history of taxane administration. The required sample size was calculated as at least 300, based on the assumption that the incidence of all-grade T-APS, the primary symptom consistent with our previous study [10], would be 25–30%, considering prior reports and our clinical experience [3, 4, 8], with approximately 10 covariates included in the multivariable analysis.

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Board for Life Science and Medical Research of Hokkaido University Hospital (approval number: 024–0298) and was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE statement. Because the present study was retrospective in nature, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Ethical Review Board for Life Science and Medical Research of Hokkaido University Hospital.

Treatment methods

GEM (1000 mg/m2) and nab-PTX (125 mg/m2) were intravenously administered on days 1, 8, and 15, every 28 days [5–7]. Palonosetron (0.75 mg) and dexamethasone (9.9 mg) were typically administered intravenously as premedication to manage chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting [16]. Analgesic drugs such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), acetaminophen, and opioids, were prescribed to manage symptoms.

Assessment of T-APS

All required information was extracted from patient medical records. T-APS was defined as the incidence of arthralgia or myalgia within a week following GEM + nab-PTX administration, based on previous reports [1, 10]. Its incidence and severity were assessed in the first week, considering the frequent changes in analgesic regimens in clinical practice for pancreatic cancer treatment. Symptoms were evaluated in accordance with the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0, through medical interviews and daily treatment diaries, by physicians or pharmacists in clinical practice. The worst grade of symptoms was recorded. The primary endpoint was identifying factors associated with the incidence of all-grade T-APS, while the secondary endpoints involved comparing symptoms between specific patient groups and sensitive analysis for the primary results.

Statistical analysis

Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent factors for the incidence of all-grade T-APS, using baseline covariates such as age, sex, PS, body mass index (BMI), hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin levels < 3.5 g/dL), liver dysfunction (grade 2 or higher aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, or total bilirubin elevation), prior chemotherapy history, dose reduction of nab-PTX from treatment initiation, and baseline administration of mild analgesics (NSAIDs and/or acetaminophen) or opioids. Previous studies [1, 3, 4, 10, 17] and our clinical experience guided these covariates. Metastatic setting was not included owing to multicollinearity with hypoalbuminemia, analgesic administration, and PS. Fisher’s exact test was used to compare T-APS incidence between patient populations. A receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) curve analysis was used to estimate the optimal cutoff values of nab-PTX dosage. All statistical analyses were conducted using JMP (version 17.0, SAS Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan). P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 394 patients were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). The baseline patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The median age of the patients was 69 years (range, 29–85 years), and approximately 70% had unresectable tumors. Patients with a history of chemotherapy accounted for 21.6% of all patients. The median BMI was 21.2 (14.2–35.0) kg/m2, with 16.2% of patients having BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. Baseline use of mild analgesics was reported in 37.6% of patients, with most using monotherapy (21.6% NSAIDs, 11.9% acetaminophen), and 4.1% using combination therapy. Approximately 20% of patients were regularly prescribed opioids. Treatment dose reduction was performed in 12.4% of patients, with a same rate of reduction in nab-PTX.

Incidence and severity of T-APS, and symptomatic medication

The incidence and severity of T-APS following the first GEM + nab-PTX administration are shown in Table 2. The incidence of all-grade T-APS was 26.1%, with 21.6% grade 1 and 4.6% grade 2 (no cases of grade ≥ 3). The median onset of symptoms was day 3 (range; 1–5), and the median duration was 3 days (range, 1–8). Medication was administered to 39.8% of patients with T-APS. The common treatments were oral acetaminophen (400–600 mg), oral or topical NSAIDs (almost all loxoprofen), and oral opioids, with more than two-thirds of patients reporting symptom relief.

Risk factor analysis

Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses are presented in Table 3. BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 was identified as a significant risk factor for the incidence of all-grade T-APS (adjusted odds ratio, 2.98; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.68–5.31; P = 0.0002). Baseline administration of mild analgesics was found to be a preventive factor (adjusted odds ratio, 0.48; 95% CI, 0.26–0.89; P = 0.02).

Impact of actual nab-PTX dosage on T-APS

As overweight can be associated with higher nab-PTX dosage, we additionally assessed the impact of actual administration dosage on the results. Using an ROC curve analysis, we assessed a cut-off of nab-PTX, which resulted in 190 mg/body (area under the curve 0.62; sensitivity 0.62; specificity 0.57), and included in the multivariable analysis instead of BMI. This analysis indicated that a nab-PTX dosage ≥ 190 mg is also a risk factor for T-APS (adjusted odds ratio, 2.38; 95% CI, 1.29–4.40; P = 0.006).

Discussion

As T-APS is one of the problematic adverse events associated with GEM + nab-PTX treatment and can occur with each administration, its appropriate management is critical for maintaining patients’ QOL and ADL. Identifying high-risk populations is essential for early detection and timely intervention. Moreover, the characteristics of T-APS may vary depending on several factors, such as cancer type or setting and the dose or type of taxanes used, suggesting the need to evaluate T-APS within each specific treatment regimen [1–4]. Consequently, we aimed to assess factors associated with T-APS in real-world GEM + nab-PTX treatment of pancreatic cancer.

Our results identified a BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 as a risk factor and baseline administration of mild analgesics as a preventive factor for T-APS. Previous reports have suggested other risk factors such as higher taxane dosage, paclitaxel use, age < 55 years, metastatic setting, breast cancer, and co-administration of pegfilgrastim, primarily in patients with breast cancer [1, 3, 4, 9, 10]. Differences in taxane type and dosage, along with distinct patient characteristics in pancreatic cancer, may explain the discrepancies between these findings and prior studies.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to identify overweight as a risk factor for T-APS. We hypothesized two rationales: (1) high-dose administration owing to larger body surface area and (2) chronic joint stress. ROC curve analysis indicated that a nab-PTX dosage ≥ 190 mg is also a risk factor for T-APS, when the dosage, instead of BMI, was included in the multivariable analysis. As high-dose administration is a key factor for symptom development [4], higher nab-PTX dosage owing to high BMI might have affected the results. Moreover, considering the older patient population in our study (median age: 69 years), chronic joint stress associated with higher body weight may have also contributed to the higher T-APS incidence observed in this group.

In contrast, baseline administration of mild analgesics such as NSAIDs or acetaminophen appeared to reduce the incidence of T-APS. Despite different mechanisms of action between these two agents [18], no significant difference was observed in T-APS incidence between patients on NSAID or acetaminophen monotherapy at baseline. This suggests that either agent may help reduce T-APS. Interestingly, none of the patients receiving both agents (n = 16) developed T-APS, indicating a potential additive or synergistic effect. Conversely, baseline opioid use was not associated with T-APS prevention, with an incidence of 26.7% (4/15) in patients on opioid monotherapy. The rationale for this difference may lie in the anti-inflammatory effects of NSAIDs and acetaminophen. NSAIDs act directly by inhibiting COX, thereby reducing inflammation [18]. Acetaminophen weakly and indirectly blocks COX [19, 20], and its metabolite, N-acylphenolamine, has been shown to produce analgesic effects in inflammatory pain models in rats [19]. Both acetaminophen and N-acylphenolamine induce analgesia during thermal stimulation in an inflammatory pain rat model. Additionally, N-acylphenolamine inhibits excitatory synaptic transmission via transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily V member 1 (TRPV1) receptors located on C-fiber terminals in the spinal dorsal horn. TRPV1-positive neurons increase during inflammation, particularly in the dorsal root ganglion and unmyelinated axons of the digital nerves [21]. Furthermore, their analgesic effects have been found to be stronger in an inflammatory pain model than in rats without inflammation [19]. Besides, inflammation of the dorsal root ganglion may be responsible for the development of T-APS [22]. These findings suggest that NSAIDs and/or acetaminophen may reduce T-APS, maybe derived from inflammation, more effectively than opioids.

Effective management of T-APS is key to delivering less onerous chemotherapy. Previous reports have shown that corticosteroids, Shakuyaku-Kanzo-To, gabapentin, minocycline, and etoricoxib have prophylactic efficacy against T-APS [2, 4, 10–15]. These prophylaxes may serve as potential pain management strategies, particularly in patients with risk factors; however, their use must be carefully considered, particularly in weekly treatment regimens or long-term treatment, owing to potential adverse events. Furthermore, as T-APS is considered to involve nerve inflammation, injury, and nociceptor sensitization [1, 2, 4, 23, 24], medications targeting these mechanisms may help prevent or attenuate symptoms. In contrast, analgesics, including NSAIDs, acetaminophen, and opioids, are typically administered empirically to treat the symptoms, with limited evidence for their efficacy in this setting [4]. Consequently, T-APS management should ideally involve both prophylactic and therapeutic approaches, informed by a comprehensive understanding of its pathophysiology. Further studies are warranted to explore the effectiveness of combination prophylaxis and symptomatic treatment strategies.

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective and included a relatively small patient population from a single institution. Second, considering the retrospective nature of the study, we adopted a clinical practice-based objective evaluation by physicians or pharmacists alone. Subjective assessment may offer additional comprehension. Third, we evaluated the effect of baseline analgesic use on T-APS, rather than analgesic intervention at treatment initiation. Further studies on prophylactic NSAIDs or acetaminophen use may provide new treatment options. Fourth, we did not assess the relationship between T-APS and values involving treatment efficacy such as progression-free survival or time to treatment failure. While treatment suspension owing to severe neutropenia requires careful consideration [25, 26], its assessment will provide new insights. Finally, we evaluated T-APS following the first administration of GEM + nab-PTX, considering the frequent changes in analgesic regimens during pancreatic cancer treatment. However, as several studies suggest T-APS may become chronic [23, 24], assessment over longer treatment periods is warranted. Consequently, future studies are needed to validate these preliminary findings.

In conclusion, our study identified BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2 as a risk factor and baseline administration of mild analgesics as a preventive factor for T-APS development in real-world GEM + nab-PTX treatment for pancreatic cancer. Further evaluation of the underlying mechanisms and management methods may contribute to the development of less onerous treatments.
