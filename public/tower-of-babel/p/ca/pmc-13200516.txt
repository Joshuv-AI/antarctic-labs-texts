ORAL PRESENTATIONS: Improvement in Safety Measures to Reduce ED Returns

Ghadah F. Almugren, Mufareh Al Katheri, Ali M. Al Khathaami, Abdul-Hadi Al Gahtani, Abdulmohsen Al Saawi, Shiela Javllana, Mashael Basakran, Sara Al Yehya

Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia

Introduction: Emergency department (ED) return cases as critical or deceased poses a significant concern for any healthcare organization and needs to be evaluated and addressed. Methods: The implementation of initiatives began in January 2022 to monitor the ED return cases. Interventions were developed using the PDSA (Plan Do Study Act) cycles in January 2022. Cycle 1 captured ED returns within 72 hours that were flagged by daily systemic trigger notifications received through the email by the dashboard and the cases were analyzed by our quality improvement specialists team daily in January 2022. In any sentinel or severe event, an escalation via SMS to the leadership ensured immediate attention and action. Cycle 2 included Patient Safety Leadership meetings within 24 hours of the return visit. This was initiated in April 2022 and discussed with all stakeholders for the immediate implementation of recommendations and action plans to improve the system and address individual concerns. Cycle 3 included a comprehensive consultation checklist integrated into the HIS in July 2023 to ensure that all necessary steps and consultations were completed during discharge. This included specific questions regarding the consultant’s awareness about the treatment plan provided to the patient. Results: As shown in the figure, a statistical process control chart was used to present the data through the dashboard from January 2021 until January 2023 and continued till December 2023. The results revealed significant improvements. Overall, the number of return patients in the ED reduced by 46.60% from 0.13% in 2021 to 0.08% in 2022–2023. Conclusion: The project was highly effective for patients. It reduced the morbidity and mortality for these patients over time; however, it also increased immature admissions. Continuous monitoring and application of different measures to analyze ED return cases are recommended.

Risk Mitigation Using FMEA for Concentrated Potassium Chloride in Feticidal Processes

Ghadah Almardawi, Laila Abu Esba, Hedaya Alblewi, Nadia Alghilan, Sara Albilal, Suwarnnah Sinnappan, Arvin Singh, Roba Alamer

King Abdulaziz Medical City, Riyadh. Saudi Arabia

Introduction: Fetal intracardiac injection of concentrated potassium chloride (KCl) requires the direct administration of undiluted KCl into the fetal heart to induce fetal demise and is performed exclusively by Maternal–Fetal Medicine (MFM) consultants. Institutional policy dictates that concentrated electrolytes must remain under the control of pharmacy, with limited exceptions when immediate access is necessary to avoid compromising patient care. Given the high-alert nature of concentrated KCl and in accordance with Joint Commission International (JCI) requirements for risk assessment of high alert concentrated electrolytes, a comprehensive evaluation of this new process was essential. To address any risks, the Medication Safety Officer, in collaboration with a multidisciplinary team, initiated a Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) to proactively identify and mitigate failures throughout the medication-use process. Methods: A FMEA approach was adapted, and a multidisciplinary team was established, including representatives from the Medication Safety, Quality, Pharmacy, Nursing, Medical staff, and Health Informatics. Approximately twelve structured one-hour meetings were conducted to complete the following activities. The team reviewed and categorized the proposed workflow, identified potential failure modes along with their causes and effects, and scored each risk using severity, occurrence, and detection criteria to determine the Risk Priority Number (RPN). Recommended actions were then reassessed, RPNs recalculated, and pre-and post-implementation results compared. An action plan defining responsibilities and timelines was developed, and high-risk steps were revised with corresponding workflow updates. An audit tool established to audit the implementation. Results: A total of 78 potential failures were identified. The highest RPN values were 300 and 230 in process steps four and eleven, respectively. Following the review, multiple corrective actions were implemented, reducing those RPNs to 37 and 18. Overall, the average RPN decreased from 164.4 to 25.9. The interventions included several Health Informatics improvements, such as creating a restricted order set for concentrated KCl, implementing a documentation form within the HIS that requires completion of all necessary clinical fields, initiate a hospital policy, revising workflow and introducing a monitoring system to track the medication’s presence and use, and conducting targeted awareness. Continuous monitoring was performed to evaluate both implementation and effectiveness of these measures. Conclusion: FMEA is a flexible tool that enables teams to anticipate potential failures, along with their causes and effects. By addressing these risks proactively, it helps reduce the likelihood of errors before the process is implemented.

Enhancing Occupational Safety Through Systematic PPD Screening and Accurate Latent TB Reporting

Ahmed Newera1, Fahad Alomari2, Abdullah AlGhamdi2, Mahmoud Osama2, Fahad Alzulfi2, Marjorie Manalus2, Bassam Alzaidi2, Jazel Lauretta2

1Alhada Armed Forces Hospitals, Taif, Saudi Arabia; 2Prince Sultan Military Hospital, Taif, Saudi Arabia

Background: Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health challenge, particularly in regions with high population density and mobility. In Saudi Arabia's western province, Taif is uniquely vulnerable due to its proximity to the holy cities of Makkah and Madinah, which host millions of pilgrims annually. This influx increases the risk of TB transmission, placing healthcare workers at Prince Sultan Hospital at heightened risk of latent TB infection. Prince Sultan Hospital identified deficiencies in its TB screening practices among healthcare workers. Issues included low screening rates, inadequate staff training in administering the Purified Protein Derivative (PPD) test, improper induration measurement, inconsistent follow-up readings within the required 48-72 hours, and poor documentation. All these issues hindered effective monitoring and increased the risk of TB transmission within the hospital. Methods: Project CLEAR was initiated to improve compliance with PPD testing and the accuracy of documentation. A multifaceted intervention was implemented, focusing on staff education, workflow optimization, and documentation process enhancement. The project employed the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle for continuous quality improvement and tracked key performance indicators (KPIs) over 22 months from December 2022 to September 2024. The interventions implemented in this project were multifaceted, aiming to improve the overall PPD testing and reporting process. A key focus was staff education and training, with comprehensive sessions emphasizing the importance of TB screening. The training also covered proper administration of the PPD test, accurate measurement of induration, and the critical need for timely follow-up readings. In addition, workflow optimization was achieved by standardizing protocols and implementing a manual reminder system to ensure follow-ups occurred within the essential 48-72 hour window. A user-friendly database registry was established to enhance documentation, enabling accurate recording of PPD test results, follow-ups, and previous test histories. Finally, performance was regularly monitored by reviewing key performance indicators (KPIs), with feedback provided to staff, creating a culture of accountability and continuous improvement. Results: The results of the interventions demonstrated significant improvements in PPD testing compliance and follow-up. Compliance rates for PPD testing increased from 35% to 90% across all hospital staff, with staff in high-risk areas achieving 100% compliance, up from 41.05%. Timely follow-up readings within the 48-72 hour window also saw substantial improvement, rising from 16% to 100% compliance. Additionally, the number of positive PPD results increased from 20 to 85, and 100% of these cases received chest X-rays and appropriate follow-up care. These outcomes reflect the success of the implemented measures in enhancing TB screening and care within the hospital. Conclusions: Implementing Project CLEAR significantly enhanced compliance with PPD testing, timely follow-up, and accurate documentation, thereby improving the identification and management of latent TB cases among healthcare workers. These interventions reduced the risk of TB transmission within the hospital and highlighted the importance of rigorous TB screening protocols in high-risk settings. The project's framework offers a scalable model that can be adapted to similar healthcare environments to improve TB control measures.

A Three-Priority Implementation Framework for the Development of Standardized ICU Medication Safety Protocols

Sandra Adams Sallar1, Bishop Essien2, Alice Sallar Adams3, Ernest Ofosu-Appiah2

1University of Florida, Gainesville, FL; 2University of Ghana Medical Center, Accra, Ghana; 3University of Ghana School of Public Health, Accra, Ghana

Introduction: Medication errors in critical care settings cause significant patient harm, yet standardized protocols with practical implementation tools remain limited. Existing safety interventions often focus on specific resources, technologies, or professional roles in specific settings, limiting their applicability in ICUs with different workflows and staffing models. This project aimed to develop standardized ICU medication safety protocols with adaptable implementation tools for diverse ICU practice settings. Methods: We conducted a retrospective analysis of the US FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) data from January 1, 2020, through September 30, 2025, identifying 244,630 ICU-related adverse event reports. Of these, 13.7% (n=33,443) involved ICU medications including vasopressors, sedatives, analgesics, and paralytics as primary suspect drugs. We characterized error types, frequencies, and outcomes by drug class using descriptive statistics and chi-square tests. Based on identified error patterns, we prioritized error types with the greatest potential for prevention. We developed three evidence-based safety protocols, with designs drawing on clinical experience across ICU practice models in tertiary settings. As this was a quality improvement protocol development project using de-identified, publicly available data, ethical approval was not required. Results: As shown in the figure, analgesics were associated with the highest reported mortality among ICU medications, ranging from 35-64%, with a mean mortality of 35.4% (n=7,410) and serious outcomes reported in 48.0% (n=10,028) of cases. Error analysis showed that 58.4% (n=28,054) involved overdose, 24.6% (n=11,812) were dose omissions, and 4% (n=1,913) involved underdosing, indicating 87% (n=41,779) of medication errors were dose-related and potentially modifiable. We developed three standardized protocols: (1) a High-Risk Medication Protocol using weight-based standardized dosing tools, organ function-guided dose adjustments, and standardized reversal pathways integrated with sedation/analgesia scoring scales; (2) a Dose Verification Protocol to address dose calculation and administration errors through structured double-check processes which are adaptable to electronic or manual systems; and (3) a Medication Reconciliation Protocol targeting omission, duplication, and transition errors through standardized review tools at admission, care transitions, and discharge. Each protocol includes comprehensive implementation guidance for adapting to local workflows, personnel roles, and documentation systems. Conclusion: Our three-priority framework, using standardized protocols and adaptable tools, can address most ICU medication errors identified in national pharmacovigilance data. By focusing on high-impact preventable errors, this approach supports practical improvements in medication safety across diverse ICU settings and is ready for local implementation and future multicenter testing.

Reducing Preventable Inpatient Venous Thromboembolism After a Fatal Pulmonary Embolism: Impact of Electronic Prescribing Prompts and Pharmacist Support

Jomol Joseph, Htin Aung

University Hospital of Wales, Cardiff & Vale University Health Board, Cardiff, UK

Introduction: Venous thromboembolism (VTE) remains a significant and preventable cause of hospital-associated morbidity and mortality despite established national guidance from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). All patients admitted to hospital should undergo formal VTE risk assessment and receive appropriate prophylaxis where indicated; however, compliance in clinical practice remains variable. Following a fatal pulmonary embolism identified through a morbidity and mortality review, gaps in VTE risk assessment, documentation, and prescribing were identified in our acute medical unit. This project aimed to improve compliance with VTE risk assessment and appropriate prophylaxis through implementation of an electronic prescribing prompt supported by multidisciplinary input. Methods: This quality improvement project was conducted in an acute medical ward at University Hospital of Wales, a UK tertiary hospital. Baseline data were collected to assess VTE risk assessment documentation and prophylaxis prescribing practices. An electronic prescribing and medicines administration prompt was introduced requiring clinicians to complete a mandatory VTE risk assessment. The prompt asked whether the patient required VTE prophylaxis (yes/no); if ‘no’, clinicians were required to document a reason, including existing anticoagulation (e.g. direct oral anticoagulant or warfarin) or other contraindications. This intervention was supported by targeted staff education, regular feedback, and reinforcement during clinical handovers. A re-audit was conducted following implementation. A further improvement cycle compared outcomes in a ward with dedicated pharmacist involvement. Data were analyzed as percentages. This work was undertaken as a service evaluation; formal ethical approval was not required. Results: At baseline, 69% of patients had no documented VTE prophylaxis decision, representing a significant patient safety concern. Following implementation, non-documentation reduced to 38%, with a corresponding increase in appropriate thromboprophylaxis prescribing and improved documentation of contraindications. In a ward with pharmacist involvement, non-documentation reduced further to 24%, alongside higher rates of appropriate prophylaxis use and more consistent documentation practices. Conclusion: Implementation of an electronic prescribing prompt significantly improved VTE documentation and prescribing, addressing a key preventable patient safety risk. Pharmacist support further enhanced compliance, highlighting a scalable multidisciplinary approach to improving patient safety.

Enhancing Diagnostic Performance and Workflow Efficiency in Cervical Cytology Using Artificial Intelligence–Assisted Screening in a High-Volume Laboratory

Siti Zulaikha Binti Md Daud, Sayyidi Hamzi Bin Abdul Raub, Hareef Bin Muhammad

Premier Integrated Labs Sdn Bhd, Kuala Lampur, Malaysia

Introduction: High-volume cervical cytology services face increasing demands while maintaining diagnostic accuracy, timely reporting, and patient safety. Conventional manual screening may result in variability in case detection and inefficient utilization of manpower. Artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted cytology screening offers a promising approach to enhance diagnostic performance and optimize workflow in modern laboratory practice. This study aims to evaluate the impact of AI-assisted cytology screening on diagnostic performance, rescreening strategy, turnaround time, and manpower utilization. Methods: A retrospective quality evaluation was conducted in a specialized cytology laboratory processing approximately 80,000–100,000 cases annually. AI-assisted digital cytology screening was fully implemented in 2024 to support abnormal cell detection and case prioritization in liquid-based cervical cytology. Laboratory quality data were reviewed before and after AI implementation, including total workload, abnormal detection patterns, and rescreening rates. Diagnostic performance was assessed using sensitivity and specificity analysis based on correlation with final confirmed diagnosis. The impact on workflow was evaluated through rescreening coverage, turnaround time performance, and manpower utilization. Cytoscreeners verified AI-flagged regions as part of standard quality assurance practice. Results: AI-assisted screening demonstrated improved sensitivity for detection of epithelial abnormalities while maintaining acceptable specificity. Rescreening coverage increased following AI implementation, enhancing detection confidence and strengthening internal quality assurance. Workflow efficiency improved with reduced time spent on negative cases and enhanced prioritization of suspicious slides. These improvements contributed to more effective manpower utilization, allowing cytoscreeners to focus on complex and high-risk cases. Turnaround time performance was maintained or improved despite increasing workload. Conclusion: AI-assisted cytology screening improves diagnostic performance, enhances patient safety through increased rescreening, and optimizes laboratory workflow. This approach demonstrates how integration of digital technology with cytoscreener expertise can strengthen healthcare quality and support reliable cervical cancer screening services in high-volume settings.

Improving Quality and Patient Safety Capability for High Reliability Transformation in Ambulatory Care

Muhammad Hasan Abid1, Najwan Babatain2, Saud Alenazi3, Ibrahim Alzamil3, Seham Alanazi2, Khaled Alnafee3

1Armed Forces Hospitals Taif Region, Taif, Saudi Arabia; 2Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia; 3King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Center, Riyadh, Saudi Arabia

Introduction: Quality and patient safety (QPS) capabilities are increasingly essential competencies for the modern healthcare workforce. Despite significant investment in QPS capability building within hospital and corporate healthcare sectors, evidence on its structured implementation and measurable impact within ambulatory care settings remains scant. Ambulatory care's fragmented nature presents unique challenges to building and sustaining improvement capability at scale. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of a structured, tiered QPS capability-building program on staff competency, quality improvement (QI) project performance, and high reliability organization (HRO) maturity in an ambulatory care setting. Methods: A quasi-experimental pre-post study was conducted across an ambulatory care setting over 13 months. A multiprong intervention was applied across three integrated domains: (1) tiered capacity building through structured educational programs aligned with international QPS frameworks, delivered across beginner, intermediate, and advanced levels, (2) governance strengthening through new policy development, standardized QI methodology adoption, and individualized staff mentoring, and (3) staff engagement through structured improvement events and recognition initiatives. Outcomes were evaluated using the Kirkpatrick Model across four levels: staff reactions (Level 1), pre-post QPS competency gains (Level 2), behavioral change measured through QI project participation trends and adherence to standardized QI methodology (Level 3), and organizational outcomes including HRO maturity progression and QI project performance (Level 4). This QI initiative was deemed exempt from organizational ethics committee approval. Results: A total of 122 staff were enrolled across various tiered QPS capability-building programs. As shown in the figure, at Kirkpatrick Level 1, staff reactions to the QPS capability-building programs were highly favorable, with 90% of participants rating the program as ‘effective’ or ‘highly effective’. At Level 2, pre-post assessments demonstrated a significant improvement in QPS competency scores, rising from 47% to 93% following completion of the tiered educational program. At Level 3, behavioral change was evident through a substantial increase in QI project participation, rising from 14 to 56 (300% increase) and adherence to standardized QI methodology across governance indicators rose from 49% to 94%. At Level 4, organizational outcomes reflected meaningful HRO maturity progression, with maturity scores advancing from 45% to 65% and the proportion of QI projects achieving and sustaining their stated aims improving from 49% to 93%. Conclusion: A structured, multiprong approach to QPS capability building is effective in improving staff competency, advancing QI project performance, and driving measurable HRO maturity progression in ambulatory care settings. These findings support broader adoption of tiered QPS capability frameworks across ambulatory care systems.

Utilizing Human Factor Framework (SEIPS 2.0) to Eliminate Systemic Defects: A Socio-Technical Approach to Prevent Sharps Injuries among Healthcare Workers

Shaikh Muhammad Saif ud Din, Hesham Felemban, Muhammad Halwani

Alhada Armed Forces Hospital, Taif, Saudi Arabia

Introduction: Achieving “Zero Harm” in preventing sharps injuries (SIs) among healthcare workers requires a shift from traditional educational methods to a Systems Engineering approach. This study uses the SEIPS 2.0 (Systems Engineering Initiative for Patient Safety) framework to analyze 170 sharps-injury incidents over 53 months in a tertiary care hospital. The focus is on identifying misalignments within the work system comprising the Person, Tools, Tasks, Organization, and Environment, rather than solely addressing individual errors. Methods: A retrospective analysis of SI data was conducted at a tertiary care hospital. Data collected from the hospital's sharp-injury reporting form were standardized to enable statistical modeling. Chi-Square Tests of Independence and Binary Logistic Regression were used to map injury etiology to SEIPS 2.0 dimensions, identifying how work system interactions drive unsafe processes and outcomes. Results: The analysis revealed a stagnant five-year trend in the sharp injury rate (p = 0.27), suggesting that current generalized protocols have reached a performance plateau. A highly significant association was found between healthcare worker categories and injury mechanism (p < 0.001), identifying two distinct socio-technical failure modes: Clinical Process Failures: Clinical staff (Physicians: OR=5.13; Nurses: OR=2.57) are primarily injured as “original users” during active clinical Tasks (e.g., suturing, injections). These injuries stem from high-load Organizational conditions, Deviations from SOPs, and unpredictable Environmental factors, such as sudden patient movement. Downstream System Failures: Housekeeping staff are injured almost exclusively as “non-users.” These represent latent conditions in which Sharps are left in improper Environments (e.g., linen, trash bags) due to upstream Organizational breaches in disposal accountability. Conclusion: According to the SEIPS 2.0 framework, achieving a lasting reduction in sharps injuries (SIs) requires more than just focusing on human behavior or individual tools. A sustainable solution necessitates a comprehensive socio-technical redesign that considers the interactions among healthcare professionals, the tools they use, their physical and digital environments, and the organizational culture. Safety-engineered devices (SEDs) can optimize the internal work system by reducing the cognitive burden on healthcare workers. Redesigning bedside layouts can enhance the physical space between clinicians, sharps, and patients, while implementing departmental sharps-disposal audits may promote accountability.

Opening Doors: A QI Initiative to Increase Arrivals in a Regional Family Medicine Department

Austin White1, Shelley Mitra2, Samina Ayub2,3, Allison Crain2,3, Erum Azhar2,4, Abdul Waheed1,3

1Creighton University East Valley Arizona Family Medicine Residency Program, Gilbert, AZ, USA; 2Creighton University School of Medicine, Phoenix, AZ, USA; 3Dignity Health Medical Group, Phoenix, AZ, USA; 4Creighton University East Valley Arizona, AZ, USA

Introduction: Patient volume is a key determinant of financial sustainability and operational efficiency in primary care. In family medicine, visit volume is influenced by panel management, appointment availability, scheduling practices, and referral workflows. Misalignment between patient demand and clinician capacity can reduce access to care and limit opportunities for preventive services and chronic disease management. Our academic family medicine department had fewer arrived visits per clinical full-time equivalent (FTE) and more limited appointment access compared with national benchmarks. This study evaluated the impact of a multidisciplinary, process improvement–based intervention designed to increase arrived visits per clinical FTE. Methods: We conducted a quasi-experimental pre/post study in an academic family medicine residency clinic. An interdisciplinary team consisting of physician leaders, operational dyads, a population health expert, a data analyst, and a nursing expert collaborated with frontline clinicians and operations staff. Using process mapping, Fishbone (Ishikawa) analysis, and stakeholder analysis, the team developed and implemented a multifaceted bundled intervention over one year. Interventions targeted key drivers of access and visit volume, including stakeholder engagement, redesigned access workflows, earlier schedule opening, point-of-care follow-up scheduling, marketing initiatives, referral coordination with the Clinically Integrated Network (CIN), empanelment optimization, and electronic health record (EHR) scheduling template improvements. Changes in arrived visits per clinical FTE were evaluated using statistical process control with Shewhart charts and phase analysis, supplemented by one-way ANOVA using JMP Pro 17. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board. The project used operational and de-identified clinical data collected as part of routine quality improvement activities. Therefore, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived in accordance with institutional and ethical guidelines. Results: As shown in the figure, statistical process control analysis demonstrated a sustained increase in arrived visits per clinical FTE per month across study phases. Baseline performance averaged 157 visits per clinical FTE per month beginning in November 2023. During the intervention rollout in 2024, the average increased to 162 visits per clinical FTE per month. Following implementation, the average rose to 196 visits per clinical FTE per month by December 2025, despite additional clinical FTEs still ramping up. One-way ANOVA confirmed statistically significant differences between phases (p < 0.0001). Conclusion: A multidisciplinary bundled intervention using structured process improvement tools and leadership engagement can improve appointment access and arrived visit volumes in an academic family medicine setting, supporting sustainable primary care capacity.

Impact of Early Detection of Malnutrition by Implementing a Comprehensive Malnutrition Assessment Protocol for Dialysis Patients Using Advanced Cost-effective Diagnostics Tools

Mehad Alrewaithi, Arwa Alsubaie, Shadan Hanjour, Nihal Sadagah, Salem Alqurashi, Eman Qattan, Aisha Qattan

King Fahad Armed Forces Hospital, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia

Introduction: Protein-energy wasting (PEW) is a common yet under-recognized complication among patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) undergoing hemodialysis (HD) and peritoneal dialysis (PD), and is associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and reduced quality of life. Despite its high prevalence, early detection remains suboptimal due to limited awareness and lack of standardized screening practices. In our dialysis unit, delayed recognition of malnutrition was contributing to increased reliance on advanced nutritional support, including intradialytic parenteral nutrition (IDPN). This quality improvement project aimed to implement a structured nutritional screening process to enable early identification of at-risk patients and reduce the need for IDPN. Methods: A quality improvement initiative was conducted using the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) framework. Baseline assessment identified gaps in routine nutritional screening. Interventions included implementing the Dialysis Malnutrition Score (DMS) as a standardized screening tool, combined with comprehensive risk assessment using anthropometric measurements, biochemical markers, and dietary intake analysis. Education and training were provided to healthcare providers, patients, and caregivers. The intervention was initially piloted in a small group of patients within the dialysis unit and subsequently expanded to a larger population within the same setting. Ethical approval was obtained, and informed consent was secured. Results: A total of 245 patients (200 HD, 45 PD) were screened. Among HD patients, 95% had mild malnutrition, 2% moderate malnutrition, and 3% normal nutritional status. Among PD patients, 93% had mild malnutrition, 5% moderate malnutrition, and 2% normal status. Biochemical findings demonstrated reduced albumin, total protein, and phosphate levels across several patient groups. Dietary intake analysis further revealed inadequate energy and protein intake below recommended requirements, indicating a high risk of progression to severe malnutrition. Overall, 89 patients were identified as being at risk of severe malnutrition. Prior to implementation, 7 patients required IDPN, accounting for 349 sessions at a total cost of 15,643 SAR. Without early detection, projected IDPN costs for at-risk patients could reach 961,200 SAR. Conclusion: Implementing a structured nutritional screening process in dialysis patients improves early detection of malnutrition and facilitates timely intervention. This approach has the potential to prevent progression to severe malnutrition, reduce reliance on IDPN, and lower healthcare costs while improving overall patient outcomes.

Increasing the Medicare Annual Wellness Visit Through a Bundled Intervention: A Regional Quality Improvement Initiative

Samina Ayub1,2, Nora Novikova2, Kamal Sumar3, Jessica Povar1, Patricia Johnson3, Sivasakthi Manickavasagam3, Erum Azhar1,2, Abdul Waheed1,2

1Dignity Health Medical Group, Phoenix, AZ, USA; 2Creighton University School of Medicine, Phoenix, AZ, USA; 3CommonSpirit Health, Phoenix, AZ, USA

Introduction: The Medicare Annual Wellness Visit (MAWV) is a preventive service for adults aged 65 years and older, designed to facilitate comprehensive risk assessment, routine screening, and long-term health planning. Strong engagement with primary care has previously been associated with lower mortality, fewer hospitalizations, and better adherence to preventive services. Despite its value as a key metric in value-based care, MAWV completion rates in our geographically diverse medical group remained low, limiting the broader impact of preventive care. To address this, we initiated a quality improvement effort to increase MAWV completion and strengthen population health outcomes. Methods: An interdisciplinary team of physicians, population health specialists, data analysts, and nurses collaborated with frontline clinicians and operations staff to identify barriers to MAWV completion. Root cause analysis incorporated process mapping, Fishbone diagrams, and stakeholder input. Over the course of one year, a multifaceted bundled intervention was implemented to standardize clinic workflows, improve team coordination, and expand patient outreach through direct calls, in-clinic scheduling, patient portal messaging, and online self-scheduling, supported by pre-visit planning and alignment with revenue cycle processes. MAWV completion rates were tracked across seven clinical sites using a quasi-experimental pre–post design. Trends over time were evaluated using statistical process control charts and funnel plot analysis. Statistical analysis was performed utilizing JMP Pro 17. This study was deemed exempt by the Institutional Review Board. Results: MAWV completion steadily improved throughout the study period. Statistical process control chart analysis demonstrated a significant upward shift, with rates increasing from 36% at baseline (Phase 0) to 42% during the intervention rollout (Phase 1) and reaching 57% in the post-intervention period (Phase 2). A one-way ANOVA confirmed statistical significance (p < 0.0001). Funnel plot analysis of MAWV completion rates across clinics identified one positive and one negative deviation with rates outside the expected control limits, indicating a high-performing and a low-performing region. Conclusion: A team-based intervention significantly improved MAWV completion in a diverse primary care network. Since MAWVs are conducted annually, maintaining these gains requires ongoing effort, with future work aimed at understanding site differences and developing strategies to ensure consistent clinic performance.

Transforming Depression Care: A Multifaceted Bundled Intervention to Improve Screening and Follow-Up in a Regional Healthcare Setting

Samina Ayub1,2, Daniel Hoag1,2, Megan Schultz2, Patricia Johnson3, Sivasakhti Manickavasagam3, Nicole Peters1, Erum Azhar1,2, Abdul Waheed1,2

1Dignity Health Medical Group, Phoenix, AZ, USA; 2Creighton University School of Medicine, Phoenix, AZ, USA; 3CommonSpirit Health, Phoenix, AZ, USA

Introduction: Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common behavioral health condition linked to poor quality of life and socioeconomic burdens. Over 50% of those needing care do not receive it, leading to severe consequences like hospitalization and increased morbidity/mortality from untreated milder symptoms. Thus, depression screening and management rates are key metrics for value-based care. Our medical group launched a quality improvement project to boost depression screening and follow-up management in its ambulatory clinics across a wide geographical area. Methods: An interdisciplinary team, including physician leaders, operations dyads, a Population Health expert, a data analyst, and a nursing expert, collaborated with front-line staff to develop a multi-faceted intervention to improve depression screening and management. They used process mapping, Fishbone exercise, and stakeholder analysis. A quasi-experimental study with a pre- and post-design assessed the intervention's impact on depression screening completion rates for eligible patients over one year, utilizing Shewhart charts and JMP Pro 17. Results: The bundled intervention, engaging all stakeholders, included clear expectations, a post-intervention process map, implementation of a 3-step process during primary care visits, pre-visit planning, EHR solutions, and appropriate claims submission. One-way ANOVA confirmed this difference was statistically significant (p-value <0.0001). Conclusion: Depression screening and Follow Up management rates are dynamic, requiring consistent yearly repetition. Future improvements need a targeted approach, analyzing clinics/regions above and below average. A funnel chart identified two positive and two negative deviant groups. We will use a qualitative approach with focus groups for these contrasting groups in the next improvement phase.

Kamishibai Visual Management as a Strategy for Improving Surgical Flow and Hospital Discharge in Brazilian Hospitals

Marco Saavedra Bravo1, Breno Douglas Dantas Oliviera2

1Fundação Dom Cabral, Nova Lima, Brazil; 2Hospital Sírio-Libanês, São Paulo, Brazil

Introduction: Visual management is a core pillar of Lean Healthcare, enabling continuous monitoring of process compliance and rapid response to frontline deviations. Kamishibai boards (K-boards) are structured visual audit tools used to standardize and control critical care processes. In Brazil, national quality improvement programs have scaled K-board adoption across public and philanthropic hospitals. A nationwide collaborative (Saúde em Nossas Mãos, PROADI-SUS) demonstrated feasibility across 189 ICUs, achieving 93.7% adoption and sustaining bundle compliance above 85%. The Lean nas Emergências program further expanded implementation to surgical centers, inpatient units, and discharge workflows. Despite broad adoption, evidence on K-board use in surgical flow and discharge management remains limited. This study analyzes K-board implementation in these settings, focusing on operational efficiency, care coordination, and patient safety. Methods: A structured review of K-board implementations in Brazilian hospitals participating in national quality improvement initiatives was conducted. Data were drawn from institutional guides, operational reports, and public sources. Implementations included surgical centers and inpatient units across three hospitals in different regions. K-boards were analyzed as the central visual management tool within Lean practices, considering audit routines, board structure, and multidisciplinary involvement. Ethical approval was not required due to the use of secondary, non-identifiable data. Results: K-board implementation was consistently associated with measurable improvements in operational performance and care coordination across settings, summarized in the table. In surgical centers, productivity increased by up to 12% and room turnover time decreased by up to 50%, alongside reduced inter-case variability and improved scheduling efficiency, indicating more stable and predictable surgical flow. In inpatient units, K-boards supported structured discharge planning and bed turnover, reducing the time between medical and administrative discharge by up to 27% and enhancing multidisciplinary coordination. In neonatal care, the tool contributed to the standardization of high-risk medication workflows, reinforcing process reliability in critical settings. Across all contexts, K-boards enabled real-time identification of process deviations and strengthened frontline accountability without requiring escalation, suggesting a shift toward more proactive and decentralized management. These results were consistently supported by key enablers, including leadership engagement, brief daily audits, and integration with Lean governance structures, reinforcing K-boards as a scalable mechanism for operational control and continuous improvement. Conclusion: K-boards represent a scalable and effective strategy for improving patient flow and operational performance in hospital systems. These findings reinforce the role of audit processes and visual management in driving patient flow and sustaining quality improvements projects in hospital systems.

Reducing 48-Hr Emergency Department Revisits Through a Structured Quality Improvement Approach

Samia Othman, Abdelrahman Essam, Maha Yahia, Mohammed Shawky, Bilal Abdou, Hagar Fathi, Ahmed Safwat

Conclusion: This study will identify areas of strengths and needs for clinical improvement to enhance patient safety and care quality. This is the first initiative of clinical audit at our tertiary hospital, and we hope that it would stimulate more opportunities in clinical audit and quality projects.

Addressing Response Bias: A Multi-Domain Teach-Back Framework for Patient Safety Education

Anila Kazmi, Hooria Akbar, Rabia Zehra, Mohammad Maaz Ali Naqvi

SIUT, Karachi, Sindh, Pakistan

Introduction: In public healthcare settings serving both urban and rural populations, patient feedback frequently suffers from “acquiescence bias,” where patients report all services as “good” despite existing quality gaps. This often stems from a lack of baseline knowledge regarding safety standards. The aim of this project is to implement a “patient education and knowledge assessment form” to move beyond passive agreement. This initiative aligns with the six quality domains: safety, by educating patients on risks to reduce harm; effectiveness, by supporting treatment understanding to improve adherence; patient-centeredness, by providing information in an understandable, friendly manner; timeliness, by delivering the right information at admission and discharge; efficiency, by using standardized education to reduce repetition and errors; and equity, by ensuring all patients receive consistent, language-appropriate information. Methods: This initiative is designed as a hospital-wide quality improvement protocol led by the Patient Safety and Quality Assurance Department. The core of the project is a newly developed assessment form intended to guide Quality Assurance staffs/coordinators through standardized verbal briefings. Administrative ethical clearance has been sought from the hospital oversight committee, and verbal informed consent will be secured from all participating patients. The protocol utilizes the “Teach-Back” methodology, where officers explain hand hygiene and cleanliness standards in simplified language and then ask patients to demonstrate or explain the concepts back to verify true comprehension. Results: The project has moved through the design phase, with the assessment form finalized and the implementation strategy approved by the hospital board. Preliminary observations by Quality Assurance officers during the development phase confirmed that patients frequently offer generic positive feedback due to a lack of specific safety knowledge. The proposed framework is designed to bypass literacy barriers by replacing written materials with direct staff-led education. The primary expected outcome is the transition of the patient from a passive observer to an informed participant, ensuring that future feedback is based on informed understanding rather than a lack of baseline expectations. Conclusion: This framework demonstrates that structured verbal interaction is essential for obtaining meaningful patient feedback in resource-limited settings. By integrating all six domains of quality, the project provides a systematic method to empower patients with the knowledge necessary to contribute to a safer, more equitable clinical environment.
