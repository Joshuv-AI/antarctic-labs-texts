Understanding youth preferences for a mentorship program for celiac disease: A qualitative study
Authors: Ashley L Dunn, Parnia Abrishamchian, Ally Koh, Garret Forshee, Jennifer Iscol, Anava Wren, Hilary Jericho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCeliac disease (CeD) is an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten ingestion that causes intestinal inflammation and multiple symptoms. Management requires strict adherence to a gluten‐free diet (GFD), yet youth with CeD on a GFD report lower quality of life (QoL) and higher rates of depression and anxiety. Although mentorship programs for youth with chronic illnesses can enhance QoL, increase self‐efficacy, and improve mental health outcomes, research on CeD‐specific programs remains sparse. This qualitative study explored the needs and preferences of youth with CeD to inform the development of a tailored mentorship program.MethodsYouth ages 13–25 with CeD participated in three online focus groups in fall 2023. Interviews were recorded, transcribed, and analyzed thematically.ResultsSixteen participants (66.67% female, 60% White), mean age 16.0 years and mean diagnosis age 8.47 years, completed focus group interviews. Three themes emerged: (1) shared lived experiences, (2) accessibility and flexibility in program structure, and (3) fostering connection and community. Participants favored peer relationships matched on age, gender, and shared interests over traditional hierarchical mentorship models. They preferred a hybrid format combining in‐person and virtual one‐on‐one interactions focused on living with CeD, with flexible monthly meetings over 4–6 months.ConclusionsFindings highlight the importance of peer mentorship rooted in shared experiences and adaptable structures. Prioritizing peer connections, flexible program design, and community building may foster meaningful engagement among youth with CeD. These results guide the development of programs that support social connection, self‐management, and psychosocial well‐being in youth with CeD.Keywords: adolescent health, peer mentorship, qualitative research

INTRODUCTION

Celiac disease (CeD) is an autoimmune condition triggered by gluten, a protein found in wheat, rye, and barley, in genetically susceptible individuals. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 CeD causes small intestinal inflammation and diverse gastrointestinal and extra‐intestinal symptoms. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Studies indicate it affects approximately 1% of the population. 4 , 5 , 6

Managing CeD requires lifelong, strict adherence to a gluten‐free diet (GFD), posing significant psychosocial challenges for youth. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Adolescents and young adults (AYAs) diagnosed with CeD struggle with adherence. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 The daily vigilance required to avoid gluten, combined with social isolation and stigma, can negatively impact quality of life (QoL) during adolescence. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 11 Elevated rates of depression and anxiety have also been reported among youth with CeD compared to healthy peers. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 12

Social support through mentorship programs improves QoL in youth with chronic illnesses. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 These programs use two models: (1) traditional mentorship, which pairs a young adult with an adolescent, and (2) peer mentorship, which pairs youth of similar ages. 13 , 17 , 18 , 19 Traditional mentorship involves an experienced individual providing structured guidance and knowledge to help mentees develop skills or strategies to manage their condition, while peer mentorship emphasizes camaraderie and mutual support in navigating common challenges. 17 , 18 , 19

Despite research highlighting mentorship benefits for youth with chronic illnesses, models addressing the unique needs of youth with CeD remain underexplored. 17 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 This formative study aimed to explore AYA perspectives on the design of an ideal mentorship program for CeD, with the goal of identifying relational, structural, and developmental features to inform future intervention development.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study was approved by Stanford University's Institutional Review Board (IRB) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from participants aged ≥18 years, and assent with parent/guardian consent was obtained for minors.

Participants

Youth ages 13–25 with CeD were recruited through a collaboration between Stanford University and the Celiac Community Foundation of Northern California. Three virtual focus groups were held via Zoom (September to October 2023): two comprised participants <18 years and one comprised participants 18–25 years. All participants received a $15 gift card.

Procedures

Focus groups were designed to inform the development of a mentorship program for AYAs with CeD. A standardized interview guide (developed by authors A. L. D. and A. W.) covered program goals, characteristics, and mentorship attributes (Table 1). Discussions explored participants' understanding and expectations of mentorship, preferences for program design, and desired qualities of relationships. Topics included program structure and features, communication frequency and methods, preferred activities, and perceived benefits and concerns. Participants were encouraged to provide detailed, open responses.

To promote a respectful and inclusive environment, facilitators reviewed guidelines, including respect for differing opinions, equitable airtime, and the option to step out. Participants were asked to keep cameras on to foster group cohesion. Sessions were audio‐ and video‐recorded, with audio used for verbatim transcription. Data were stored in a secure, health insurance portability and accountability Act (HIPAA) ‐compliant location, and participants were identified by unique study identifiers and pseudonyms. Pseudonyms were used to honor participants' individuality and preserve narrative richness, aligning with qualitative methodology that centers their lived experiences.

Data analysis

Transcripts were analyzed thematically to identify and interpret patterns. 24 , 25 The process included iterative transcript review to build a comprehensive understanding, followed by inductive coding of relevant text segments. 24 , 25 Codes were reviewed, grouped into potential themes, and refined to ensure alignment with the data. 24 , 25

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study findings, study team members independently coded transcripts, then met to compare codes and resolve discrepancies through consensus. 25 , 26 , 27 Findings were supported with thick descriptions and participant quotes to enhance transparency and credibility of the results. 25 , 26 , 27 These descriptions conveyed data depth and complexity, while quotations provided a vivid, authentic representation of participants' experiences.

RESULTS

Sixteen participants (mean age: 16.0 years; mean diagnosis age: 8.47 years), completed one of three focus groups. Two‐thirds identified as female, and 60% as White (Table 2). Thematic analysis identified three elements of an effective peer mentorship program for youth with CeD: (1) shared lived experiences in mentorship, (2) fostering connection and community, and (3) accessibility and flexibility in program structure. These themes highlight relational, informational, and structural features participants viewed as essential.

Theme 1: Shared lived experiences in mentorship relationships

Participants described matching as the foundation of an effective mentorship program, emphasizing that shared lived experience with CeD was more important than demographic similarities. While age or gender could help ease initial conversations, meaningful matches were grounded in shared experience.

Chris, 17, explained: “I think the most important thing is somebody [you] can relate to whether it is…where you live or if you got diagnosed at the same age.”

Eli, 15, added: “It's good to be talking to somebody that has the same experiences and got diagnosed recently because then you can share experiences and learn with each other along the way.”

Lived experience was also tied to credibility and trust, with mentors valued for their knowledge and strategies for managing CeD.

Jordan, 18, emphasized: “the mentor has to be more experienced. I don't think age really matters,” reframing the mentor role as one defined by knowledge and problem‐solving ability rather than seniority.

Jessica, 22, echoed: “as long as they're knowledgeable about the information they're trying to give, that's the only thing that matters.”

Mentors with similar experiences were further seen as better positioned to offer reassurance, validation, and empathy.

Lila, 16, shared that this familiarity “takes away the awkwardness” and allows the conversation to focus on how each person is coping.

Amelia, 13, reflected that knowing her mentor “had been through the same situations” made it easier to be honest about struggles without fear of judgment.

Participants also identified practical considerations for mentorship matching, including aligning diagnosis timing to create an immediate sense of shared understanding.

Gayle, 14, explained that it helped to connect with “someone that was diagnosed at around the same time.”

Yousef, 17, agreed: “matching on when you were diagnosed just makes it easier because you've been through the same first challenges.”

Beyond the diagnosis stage, age and shared interests were viewed as factors that made interactions feel more natural.

Ciara, 17, emphasized: “If you're closer in age, it might be easier to connect and talk with them.”

Lila, 16, noted: “Being paired with other teens ‘cause you could probably relate on more experiences and have the same struggles with them.”

Finally, mentorship was described as a mutual, bidirectional relationship where both peers could learn and benefit.

Avishka, 15, described a good mentorship relationship with someone who has “the ability to listen to what you have to say… so that you can share your own experiences with them.”

Chris, 17, reinforced: “I feel like it would be a symbiotic relationship, even with a newer diagnosed person and a more experienced person because I still learn new things all the time.”

This findings indicate shared lived experience with CeD was foundational to effective peer mentorship. Relatability, reciprocity, and depth of experience shaped relationship quality, with mentorship framed as a collaborative process where both individuals contribute and benefit. Centering these dynamics builds the trust, support, and engagement needed for sustained impact among AYAs with CeD.

Theme 2: Accessibility and flexibility in program structure

Participants emphasized the importance of accessibility and adaptability, recommending multiple modes of connection (e.g., in‐person, virtual, text‐based) to accommodate diverse needs and preferences of youth with CeD.

Eli, 15, reflected: “To me at least it's important that you can actually talk to them either text or in person.”

Jordan, 18, added: “Sometimes I feel more comfortable just talking on the phone or over Zoom… it depends on the day and how I'm feeling.”

Given the unpredictability of CeD symptoms and the demands of school and extracurricular activities, flexible scheduling was seen as essential.

Sasha, 15, noted: “Sometimes you might have a flare or just a bad week, so it's important to have a mentor who understands if you need to reschedule.”

Claire, 14, emphasized: “It's really what makes sense to the mentee and what fits their schedule, because personally with school, extracurriculars, it's hard to fit stuff in.”

Lila, 16, echoed: “Life can get busy with school, sports, and everything else—it's nice when the program doesn't make you feel bad if you need to move a meeting.”

Participants also recommended keeping the program length manageable but long enough to build trust and connection.

Kate, 15, recommended a timeframe of “4–6 months, because you can keep talking afterwards.”

Transitional periods, particularly the shift to college, were identified as times when mentorship could have the greatest impact, helping youth navigate new logistical and social challenges.

Jessica, 22, reflected: “When I was 17–18 I really wanted help with the transition to college. Like how to find food in the dining hall or talk to people about cross‐contact.”

These findings highlight that accessibility in mentorship is not solely about removing logistical obstacles; it requires designing a program that adapts to the realities of living with CeD. By offering multiple modes of connection, accommodating schedules and health needs, and providing support during key life transitions, mentorship programs can empower participants to engage on their own terms and foster authentic, sustainable connections.

Theme 3: Fostering connection and community

Participants emphasized that a mentorship program's value extended beyond peer mentorship relationships to fostering belonging within the broader CeD community. They envisioned the program as a supportive space where shared experiences could normalize challenges, reduce isolation, and create genuine connection.

Avery, 19, shared: “That feeling of sanctuary and safe space and talking about my struggles…I enjoy connecting and talking to people and sharing experiences…and no one in my family has celiac…so I actively seek that companionship…simply what I want from the program is a buddy.”

Lila, 16, echoed: “I would say friendship and then having someone who gets you and just getting advice in another kind of perspective from someone who's going through the same things as you.”

Social activities were seen as especially valuable for strengthening connections. Shared meals and gatherings transformed potentially stressful situations into moments of solidarity.

Chris, 17, explained: “You always feel more connected to people when you are sharing a meal, or food.”

Sasha, 15, added: “I feel like it will be more helpful if people could meet in person.”

Ultimately, these findings underline the need for a mentorship program that fosters connection and community. Providing spaces for collective engagement can enhance emotional well‐being, foster belonging, and strengthen identity within the CeD community.

DISCUSSION

Peer mentorship programs have been shown to enhance psychosocial well‐being and QoL among AYAs with chronic conditions by reducing feelings of isolation, fostering emotional resilience, and supporting the development of self‐management skills. 13 , 18 However, research specific to CeD remains limited. 13 , 18 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 This qualitative study identified the needs and preferences of AYAs with CeD to inform a tailored peer mentorship program. Three themes emerged: (1) shared lived experiences in mentorship relationships, (2) accessibility and flexibility in program structure, and (3) fostering connection and community. These findings directly informed the design and implementation of a tailored peer mentorship program (results forthcoming in a future publication).

A noteworthy finding was participants' preference for peer mentorship over traditional hierarchical mentorship models. Whereas prior literature acknowledges bidirectional mentorship, participants emphasized that relatability and trust were best achieved when the relationship felt equal rather than supervisory. 17 , 33 , 34 , 35 Peers of similar age and with shared lived experiences were viewed as uniquely positioned to provide both connection and practical guidance, reinforcing evidence that peer‐based models enhance trust, relatability, and engagement. 13 , 33 , 34 , 35 Interestingly, while financial burden is often cited as a barrier to GFD adherence, participants did not identify it as central to mentorship. 8 , 9 , 10 , 36 , 37 , 38 Instead, they prioritized practical strategies, emotional reassurance, and social connection. This suggests CeD mentorship programs may need to center lived experience and peer relatability more than financial considerations, though the latter may merit exploration in resource‐limited settings.

While most participants lived with CeD for several years, those recently diagnosed (within 2 years) emphasized being paired with someone close in age while also valuing guidance from experienced peers. Additionally, young adults highlighted challenges of independent living, including navigating dining halls and advocating for accommodations. These subthemes suggest that both stages of diagnosis and life stage may influence mentorship needs and warrant further exploration.

Another important finding was the need for a peer mentorship program that balances structured, regular meetings with the flexibility to adapt to schedules and lifestyles. Both virtual and in‐person options were valued, alongside manageable program duration. Flexibility was central to ensuring broad, diverse participation and sustained engagement, which aligns with implementation research that underscores the importance of adaptable program design. 17 , 18 , 19

Beyond individual dyads, participants envisioned opportunities for fostering community connection. Shared meals, group gatherings, and in‐person events were described as ways to transform dining into solidarity and normalize the daily challenges of CeD. This aligns with literature on community‐oriented interventions for chronic conditions, which show benefits such as reduced isolation, enhanced well‐being, and stronger social networks. 13 , 16 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42

Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of peer mentorship relationships grounded in shared lived experiences. Relatability was valued during transitional life stages and in navigating social contexts. 33 , 34 , 35 , 43 , 44 , 45 Peer mentorship models may address these needs more effectively than traditional hierarchical mentorship dynamics by creating safe, inclusive spaces for shared experiences, resilience‐building, and lasting relationships. 17 , 20 , 39 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 , 50 , 51

These findings directly informed the development and implementation of GlutenFree Buddies, a peer mentorship program for youth with CeD. The program was designed to enhance psychosocial well‐being and QoL by fostering connections among peers of similar ages, interests, and locations, targeting youth aged 13–25 in the San Francisco Bay Area. As the program is evaluated and scaled, periodic monitoring guided by implementation science frameworks will be essential to ensure it remains acceptable, feasible, and responsive to youth needs, supporting adaptation and long‐term sustainability.

Limitations and future directions

While this study offers important insights into celiac mentorship interventions, the study had a few key limitations. First, the sample was predominantly white and female, limiting generalizability and excluding underrepresented racial, ethnic, and gender groups. Second, focus groups were conducted in English via Zoom, potentially excluding individuals with limited English proficiency or internet access and narrowing linguistic and socioeconomic perspectives. Third, recruitment challenges, including inadvertent flyer dissemination beyond the intended community, led to participation by individuals outside the United States and without confirmed CeD diagnoses. Although IRB approval was obtained to review internet protocol (IP) addresses and exclude ineligible participants, residual selection bias remains possible. Finally, with only 16 participants across three focus groups, data saturation may not have been achieved, limiting how broadly the findings can be applied. Despite these limitations, findings provide valuable youth‐driven guidance for peer mentorship programs tailored to AYAs with CeD.

Future research should expand on the sub‐themes identified in this study. Stage of diagnosis and life stage may shape mentorship needs, highlighting the importance of exploring tailored matching approaches for those newly diagnosed and for young adults living independently. Participants also recommended extending mentorship beyond individual dyads to include community‐based opportunities such as shared meals and group gatherings to foster connection and normalize daily challenges. Evaluating hybrid models that integrate peer‐to‐peer mentorship with community engagement could clarify strategies to sustain connection and support. Recruiting more diverse participant samples and partnering with community organizations using culturally sensitive methods will be essential to ensure findings are inclusive and broadly applicable.

CONCLUSION

Findings underscore the importance of peer mentorship for AYAs with CeD, suggesting programs may be more effective when matches are based on lived experience, age, and connection. Flexible structures that blend one‐on‐one, group, and virtual components can enhance inclusivity and engagement, while strengthening recruitment and eligibility processes will be essential to improve rigor and broaden participation.
