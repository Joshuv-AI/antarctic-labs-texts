The Diagnostic Promise of Serum S100B Protein in Pregnancy‐Related Hypertension: A Narrative Review
Authors: Moldir Dossimbetova, Nurgul Ablakimova, Lazzat Balymbetova, Andrei Gaiday, Kazbek Yermagambetov, Saule Bermagambetova, Mukhit Sarsembayev, Gulnara Gubasheva, Andrii Dinets, Akylbek Tussupkaliyev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) are a major cause of illness and death for mothers and babies worldwide. Their number is rising due to older maternal age, obesity, and other health problems. Early diagnosis is important. Serum protein S100B, a calcium‐binding protein studied in brain diseases, is now being considered as a possible marker for predicting HDP.MethodsThis narrative review looked at studies on S100B and HDP. A search was done in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science up to April 2025, with extra searches in Google Scholar. Sixty‐nine studies were included. We reviewed data on S100B structure, function, and its role in blood vessel injury, oxidative stress, and inflammation.ResultsStudies show that S100B levels are higher in women with preeclampsia than in healthy pregnancies. Increased S100B is linked with blood vessel problems, oxidative stress, and damage to the blood–brain barrier. High levels are also related to more severe disease and neurological symptoms. Other studies found that S100B may help predict complications like preterm birth, growth restriction, and poor newborn outcomes. Experimental studies suggest that S100B acts through the RAGE pathway to worsen inflammation and vascular dysfunction.ConclusionsS100B protein has strong potential as a simple, non‐invasive biomarker for early detection and risk assessment in HDP. However, larger studies and standardized testing methods are needed before it can be used in clinics. Combining S100B with known markers such as PlGF and sFlt‐1 may improve accuracy in predicting pregnancy complications.

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) remain a significant global health concern, contributing to maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality, and they represent the second leading cause of maternal death worldwide after hemorrhage [1]. These disorders encompass chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia, and preeclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension [2].

Despite advances in prenatal care, the incidence of HDP continues to rise due to factors such as increasing maternal age, obesity, and underlying cardiometabolic conditions [3, 4]. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and other international guidelines define hypertension in pregnancy as blood pressure (BP) ≥ 140/90 mmHg. Yet, there is a debate on the optimal threshold for antihypertensive treatment to balance maternal and fetal well‐being [5].

Recent studies have explored biomarkers for early prediction of hypertensive complications in pregnancy [6, 7], with a growing interest in serum S100B protein [8]. S100B is a calcium‐binding protein primarily expressed in astrocytes and Schwann cells, as demonstrated in studies of neurological disorders [9, 10], and more recently investigated in pregnancy‐related conditions such as preeclampsia [8].

However, emerging evidence suggests its potential role in vascular dysfunction and endothelial injury, both of which are key pathophysiological mechanisms in HDP [11]. Elevated levels of serum S100B have been linked to oxidative stress, inflammation, and blood‐brain barrier (BBB) disruption. These processes may contribute to the development of PE and other hypertensive disorders [12, 13].

This narrative review aims to examine the predictive value of serum S100B protein in HDP. We summarize current evidence to evaluate its clinical relevance, underlying mechanisms, and implications for early diagnosis and risk stratification. To our knowledge, this is the first review to synthesize mechanistic, clinical, and translational data on S100B in HDP, integrating findings from both human and animal studies. A clearer understanding of S100B may guide the development of prognostic markers and targeted therapies to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Methodology

This study is based on a narrative literature review aimed at exploring the potential role of serum protein S100B as a biomarker for predicting HDP. A structured literature search strategy was employed to identify relevant publications.

We searched three primary databases such as Web of Science (WoS), PubMed, and Scopus to retrieve peer‐reviewed English‐language articles published in English up to April 2025. The search was conducted using a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings, including: “S100B”, “serum protein S100B”, “pregnancy”, “hypertension”, “preeclampsia”, “eclampsia”, and “hypertensive disorders in pregnancy”. Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to refine the search results. In addition to database searches, we performed a supplementary manual search using Google Scholar to identify additional relevant studies not indexed in WoS, PubMed, or Scopus. In total, we identified 100 records. After removing 28 duplicates, 72 studies remained for screening. Among these, three were excluded due to closed access or insufficient information. Finally, 69 studies were included in this review (Figure 1). As this work is a narrative review, no original statistical analyses were conducted; however, clinical data summarized from included studies were reported in accordance with recommended standards for reporting in clinical research [14].

S100B Protein: Structure and Function

The S100B protein was first identified in the 1960s during studies aimed at comparing proteins found in nervous tissue with those present in other tissues [15]. S100B belongs to the family of calcium‐binding proteins, having various cellular functions [16]. Its name originates from its distinctive solubility in a fully saturated ammonium sulfate solution. Over time, research has expanded our understanding of this protein, revealing its inclusion in the S100 family, which consists of more than 20 calcium‐binding proteins. These proteins are distributed across multiple tissues, where they regulate a diverse range of cellular functions [17].

S100B is a small acidic homodimer with a molecular weight between 9 and 14 kDa, which was initially believed to be exclusive to the brain [18]. For nearly two decades, it was regarded as a brain‐specific protein [19]. However, subsequent studies confirmed its presence in various non‐neural cell types, challenging this notion. The high degree of conservation in its amino acid sequence and conformation across vertebrate species suggests that S100B plays a crucial biological role, though its precise functions remain not fully defined [20].

In the nervous system, S100B is most abundant in astrocytes but is also expressed in other glial cells, including oligodendrocytes, Schwann cells, ependymal cells, retinal Müller cells, and enteric glial cells. Additionally, certain neuronal subpopulations in the brainstem and peripheral ganglia have been found to contain S100B.

Beyond the nervous system, this protein has also been detected in various non‐neural cell types, such as melanocytes, Langerhans cells, chondrocytes, dendritic cells, specific lymphocyte subsets, adrenal medulla satellite cells, skeletal muscle satellite cells, and renal tubular cells. It is also present in ocular structures, including corneal endothelial cells and the epithelial cells of the lens, iris, and ciliary body. Notably, Leydig cells and adipocytes have also been identified as additional reservoirs of S100B, with concentrations comparable to those found in astrocytes [18].

While extensive research has explored the localization and function of S100B in neural tissue, its role in non‐neural tissues remains underexplored. This knowledge gap may hinder a comprehensive understanding of its physiological and pathological significance. The widespread distribution of S100B across multiple tissues does not provide definitive clues about its function. Similar to other calcium‐binding proteins in the S100 family, S100B participates in intracellular processes such as calcium homeostasis, enzyme activity regulation, cytoskeletal dynamics, cell survival, differentiation, and proliferation. However, its intracellular functions are highly context‐dependent, making it difficult to assign a singular, well‐defined role [21].

Emerging evidence suggests a more defined role for S100B when secreted into the extracellular space, particularly by astrocytes in the nervous system. In this environment, S100B primarily interacts with the transmembrane Receptor for Advanced Glycation End Products (RAGE), a multi‐ligand receptor that triggers intracellular signaling cascades. These interactions exhibit a dual nature: at low nanomolar concentrations, S100B promotes physiological regulation, whereas at higher micromolar concentrations it contributes to pathological processes. As a pattern recognition receptor, RAGE detects molecules linked to pathological states and activates transcription factors such as NF‐κB, ultimately leading to the upregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines [22].

Ongoing research is investigating the post‐translational modifications of S100B following its extracellular interactions, with the goal of developing targeted therapeutic interventions. Elevated S100B levels have been detected in various biological fluids—including cerebrospinal fluid, blood, amniotic fluid, saliva, urine, and feces—under pathological conditions affecting the nervous system [18]. These include neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and multiple sclerosis. Elevated levels are also observed in acute brain injuries, epilepsy, inflammatory bowel disease, perinatal neural disorders, gliomas, and psychiatric conditions [23, 24, 25]. Furthermore, S100B has been implicated in extra‐neural diseases, including obesity, diabetes, and melanoma [25, 26, 27]. While its broad involvement in multiple disorders reduces its diagnostic specificity, S100B remains a valuable biomarker for tracking disease progression and therapeutic responses.

Pathophysiological Link Between S100B and Hypertension

The role of S100B in the pathogenesis of hypertension during pregnancy, particularly in conditions like PE, is an area of growing interest. Although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, several pathophysiological pathways have been proposed to explain how elevated S100B levels contribute to HDP (Figure 2).

Illustration of the S100B roles in hypertension during pregnancy.

One of the key mechanisms by which S100B contributes to hypertension is through its interaction with the RAGE, a transmembrane receptor widely expressed on endothelial cells. Binding of S100B to RAGE initiates a cascade of intracellular signaling pathways, including the activation of nuclear factor kappa B (NF‐κB), mitogen‐activated protein kinases (MAPKs), and Janus kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK/STAT) [28, 29, 30]. These pathways promote endothelial dysfunction through multiple interconnected processes. Activation of RAGE by S100B leads to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [31]. For example, superoxide anions (O₂⁻) react with NO to form peroxynitrite (ONOO⁻), a highly reactive molecule that reduces NO bioavailability [32]. NO is essential for vasodilation, and its depletion results in increased vascular tone and elevated blood pressure [33]. NF‐κB activation leads to the upregulation of adhesion molecules, such as intercellular adhesion molecule‐1 (ICAM‐1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1 (VCAM‐1), promoting leukocyte infiltration into the vascular endothelium [34]. This inflammatory response further disrupts endothelial integrity, leading to vascular stiffness and increased systemic vascular resistance [35]. Chronic exposure to high S100B levels triggers mitochondrial dysfunction in endothelial cells, leading to apoptosis [36, 37]. Loss of endothelial cells impairs the regulation of vascular tone and exacerbates hypertension. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the characteristic endothelial dysfunction seen in HDP, resulting in increased systemic vascular resistance, reduced uteroplacental perfusion, and fetal hypoxia [38].

S100B is widely recognized as a biomarker of BBB integrity, and its elevated levels in PE patients suggest an underlying BBB disruption [39]. The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a specialized endothelial barrier that protects the brain and maintains central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis [39]. Pro‐inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐1β—whose production is stimulated by S100B—lead to the downregulation of tight junction proteins (e.g. claudins and occludins) [40, 41]. This weakens the BBB, allowing the passage of neurotoxic molecules and inflammatory mediators into the CNS [41]. Disruption of BBB integrity results in extravasation of plasma proteins and water into the brain parenchyma, increasing intracranial pressure and contributing to cerebral edema [42]. This is a critical factor in the development of eclampsia, characterized by seizures and altered mental status. Astrocytes and microglia in the CNS respond to increased S100B by releasing additional pro‐inflammatory cytokines. This exacerbates neuroinflammation [43]. This self‐perpetuating cycle of inflammation has been suggested to worsen neurological outcomes in PE patients. The association between high maternal S100B levels and neurological complications underscores the importance of investigating BBB integrity as a potential target for therapeutic interventions in HDP.

S100B functions as a damage‐associated molecular pattern (DAMP) protein, amplifying systemic inflammation through multiple mechanisms [35]. Beyond binding to RAGE, S100B also activates Toll‐like receptor 4 (TLR4), another key mediator of innate immune responses [44]. Activation of TLR4 leads to the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and the recruitment of immune cells, further exacerbating vascular inflammation [45]. Elevated S100B expression correlates with increased concentrations of TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐8, which contribute to a state of chronic low‐grade inflammation. These cytokines impair vascular relaxation, promote endothelial permeability, and enhance leukocyte adhesion, all of which contribute to sustained hypertension. S100B has been implicated in activating the complement cascade, further amplifying inflammation and endothelial injury [46]. This process is particularly relevant in PE, where complement dysregulation has been observed. Chronic inflammation driven by S100B and its downstream signaling pathways plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of HDP [47].

Animal Models of Hypertensive Disorders During Pregnancy and Potential Role of S100B Protein

Direct studies on S100B in animal models of pregnancy‐induced hypertension are limited, but existing research indicates that S100B and related proteins play significant roles in the inflammatory processes associated with HDP. Developing animal models to study HDP presents several challenges. One significant issue is the difference in organ development timing between species. For example, kidney development in humans is completed before birth, whereas in rodents it continues postnatally, affecting the translatability of findings related to blood pressure regulation. Additionally, variations in gestation periods and litter sizes complicate the extrapolation of results to human pregnancies; rodents have shorter gestation periods and larger litters compared to humans, which can influence the study of developmental programming of hypertension. Furthermore, many animal models do not fully replicate the complex pathophysiology of human HDP, such as PE, limiting their applicability in understanding and treating these conditions [48, 49].

Studies have demonstrated that S100B expression is elevated in the myocardium of human subjects and experimental rat models following myocardial infarction. In transgenic mice with high levels of S100B expression in cardiac and aortic smooth muscle cells, there was an inhibition of cardiac hypertrophy and arterial smooth muscle proliferation, respectively. However, an increase in apoptosis was observed following α [1]‐adrenergic stimulation or myocardial infarction. In contrast, S100B knockout mice exhibited augmented hypertrophy, decreased apoptosis, and preserved cardiac function post‐myocardial infarction [50]. These findings support the role of S100B as an intrinsic negative regulator of hypertrophy and an apoptotic agent in cardiovascular pathophysiology [51]. Additionally, research has shown that S100B protein is increased in the maternal circulation in response to fetal brain injury. Studies in fetal sheep demonstrated that intravenous administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) led to periventricular white matter injury and significantly elevated S100B levels in both maternal and fetal bloodstreams. This highlights the potential role of S100B as a biomarker for detecting fetal distress and neuroinflammation in complicated pregnancies [52, 53]. Although this study does not directly address hypertension, it highlights the role of S100B as a systemic marker of fetal stress, which may be relevant in hypertensive pregnancy conditions where placental and fetal stress responses are heightened.

In the context of pregnancy, elevated levels of S100B have been associated with preterm labor and PE. A study demonstrated that intra‐amniotic injection of S100B in pregnant mice activated the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to fetal inflammatory responses and preterm birth [54]. NLRP3‐deficient mice exhibited reduced inflammatory responses and delayed preterm birth, indicating a critical role for S100B‐induced NLRP3 activation in pregnancy‐related complications [55]. Furthermore, research has shown that S100A9, a protein related to S100B, is elevated in the plasma and placentas of PE patients. S100A9 administration in pregnant mice resulted in increased maternal blood pressure and placental inflammation, effects that were significantly reduced in NLRP3‐knockout mice [54]. This suggests that S100A9 acts as a danger signal, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome and contributing to HDP.

A more directly relevant study assessed brain damage using the neuroinflammation marker S100B in a PE rat model. Non‐pregnant and pregnant rats were infused with saline or low‐dose endotoxin on day 14 of pregnancy. S100B expression in the brain (immunohistochemistry) and S100B plasma concentrations (ELISA) were analyzed. The results showed no significant differences in S100B expression in brain tissue among the groups. Pregnant endotoxin‐treated animals did not exhibit increased plasma S100B levels compared to control pregnant rats. By contrast, non‐pregnant endotoxin‐treated rats showed significantly higher plasma S100B levels [56]. These findings suggest that pregnancy may exert a protective effect against S100B‐related neuroinflammation in hypertensive conditions.

Clinical Studies and Evidence

Recent studies have further elucidated the association between serum S100B levels and HDP, particularly PE. Research indicates that women with PE exhibit significantly higher serum S100B concentrations compared to normotensive pregnant women. A study published in 2014 reported that plasma S100B levels were elevated in preeclamptic women and were associated with central nervous system symptoms such as visual disturbances [57]. Similarly, a 2015 study found increased maternal serum S100B levels in severe PE cases, suggesting a potential role of S100B as a marker of disease severity [58].

Emerging evidence suggests that S100B may serve as an early indicator for adverse pregnancy outcomes associated with hypertensive disorders. A 2023 case‐control study demonstrated that serum S100B levels, when combined with glucose metabolism indices, could effectively predict adverse pregnancy outcomes in patients with severe PE [59]. This highlights the potential of S100B as a non‐invasive biomarker for early detection of complications.

In addition to its role in PE, S100B has been implicated in other pregnancy‐related complications. A recent study investigated the concentration of S100B in maternal and cord blood (CB) plasma, as well as in placenta supernatant, in cases of spontaneous preterm birth (sPTB) and PE/HELLP syndrome. The study found that S100B concentration was significantly elevated in maternal and CB plasma of sPTB and PE/HELLP patients. It also showed that S100B positively correlated with interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) levels. Moreover, increased S100B expression was confirmed in CB of small‐for‐gestational‐age (SGA) infants, suggesting a broader role of S100B in inflammatory events associated with pregnancy complications.

Another study focused on intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), a leading cause of perinatal mortality and morbidity. Researchers assessed longitudinal S100B levels in maternal blood across multiple gestational periods to evaluate its predictive value for IUGR. Their findings indicated that lower maternal S100B levels at early gestational stages were predictive of IUGR. The highest sensitivity and specificity were observed in the first trimester. This supports the notion that S100B assessment could enable early, non‐invasive diagnosis and monitoring of fetal growth restrictions.

Traditional biomarkers like soluble fms‐like tyrosine kinase‐1 (sFlt‐1) and placental growth factor (PlGF) are commonly used for PE screening [60, 61]. However, incorporating S100B measurements may enhance predictive accuracy. For example, the 2015 study conducted by Artunc‐Ulkumen et al. found that maternal serum S100B levels were significantly higher in severe PE cases, suggesting that S100B could complement existing biomarkers in assessing disease severity [58]. Furthermore, a nested case‐control study evaluated the concentrations of neuronal proteins, including neurofilament light chain and tau, in women developing PE. The study demonstrated that S100B, along with these neuronal biomarkers, could predict neurological impairment before the clinical diagnosis of PE. Plasma concentrations of neurofilament light chain were significantly elevated in women who later developed PE, particularly in late gestation, further strengthening the link between S100B and neurological involvement in pregnancy complications [62]. Notably, elevated S100B levels may persist postpartum. Bergman et al. found that women who had preeclampsia continued to exhibit higher median levels of S100B 1 year after delivery compared to those with normal pregnancies [63]. This suggests potential long‐term neurological involvement associated with PE. A recent clinical study also confirmed that maternal serum S100B levels are significantly increased in women with PE, supporting its value as a biomarker [64]

As summarized in Table 1, multiple clinical studies have investigated the role of S100B in HDP, demonstrating its potential association with disease severity and outcomes. These findings underscore the emerging role of S100B as a clinically valuable biomarker in the early detection and management of HDP. Further large‐scale, prospective studies are warranted to validate these associations and to establish standardized protocols for integrating S100B measurement into clinical practice.

Future Research Directions

While current studies have established a potential link between serum S100B protein and HDP, several knowledge gaps remain. Prospective cohort studies are needed to evaluate the temporal relationship between serum S100B levels and the onset of HDP, with serial measurements across different trimesters to determine predictive accuracy. Further exploration of the molecular pathways linking S100B to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation is essential. Specifically, studies should assess the role of b More comprehensive studies utilizing transgenic or knockout animal models of preeclampsia and gestational hypertension are needed to establish causality. These models should examine whether pharmacological modulation of S100B expression or RAGE antagonists can mitigate hypertensive complications. Standardised reference values for serum S100B levels in pregnant women should be established. Large‐scale clinical trials are required to assess whether S100B testing improves risk stratification when combined with existing biomarkers such as PlGF and sFlt‐1. Given its involvement in inflammatory pathways, targeted therapies aimed at modulating S100B activity, such as small‐molecule inhibitors or monoclonal antibodies, may offer novel treatment strategies. Future studies should investigate whether anti‐RAGE therapeutics or S100B inhibitors can prevent or mitigate HDP progression. Since S100B has been suggested to be involved in fetal neurodevelopment, research should explore its potential effects on neonatal brain health, particularly in pregnancies complicated by PE.

In addition, future diagnostic applications should consider specific technologies for S100B measurement. Currently, enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is the most widely used method. Advanced platforms such as surface (SPRi) and multiplex assays may enhance sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility in clinical settings. ELISA is relatively low‐cost and widely available, making it feasible even in low‐resource clinical settings. In contrast, SPRi and multiplex assays are more expensive and less accessible. Therefore, developing simple and affordable point‐of‐care tests will be essential for broader clinical use, especially in resource‐limited regions. It should also be considered that S100B is not disease‐specific, as elevated levels are frequently observed in other inflammatory and neurological conditions. Future research should therefore evaluate its use in combination with additional biomarkers to improve diagnostic specificity for HDP.

Nevertheless, it is important to note several limitations of the current evidence. Existing studies are heterogeneous in terms of design, study populations, and outcome measures, making direct comparisons challenging. Moreover, there is no standardized method for measuring S100B levels, as assays vary across laboratories and use different biological samples (e.g., serum, plasma, amniotic fluid). These methodological differences contribute to variability in reported results and reduce the reproducibility of findings. Addressing these limitations through standardized protocols and large multicenter trials will be essential to establish the reliability of S100B as a biomarker in HDP.

Conclusions

HDP remains a major challenge in obstetric care, necessitating early predictive biomarkers for timely intervention. Emerging evidence suggests that serum S100B protein may serve as a valuable biomarker due to its association with endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation, as hallmarks of HDP. Although elevated S100B levels have been observed in PE and gestational hypertension, further research is required to validate its clinical utility. Future investigations should prioritize longitudinal studies, mechanistic research, and therapeutic trials to delineate the role of S100B in HDP pathogenesis. Establishing standardized diagnostic thresholds and integrating S100B measurements into routine prenatal screening protocols could significantly enhance risk assessment. To our knowledge, this is the first narrative review to combine mechanistic, clinical, and translational evidence on S100B in HDP. It draws on findings from both human and animal studies and offers a broader perspective than previous reviews. Ultimately, a deeper understanding of S100B's involvement in HDP may pave the way for novel diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, improving maternal and neonatal health outcomes.
