Physicians’ perspectives on shared decision-making in antibiotic prescriptions for acute respiratory tract infections at a primary healthcare centre in ethiopia: a qualitative study
Authors: Gashaw Enbiyale Kasse, Suzanne M. Cosh, Judy Humphries, Shahidul Islam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Shared decision-making (SDM) is a collaborative process in which healthcare providers and patients work together to make informed healthcare choices, significantly reducing unnecessary antimicrobial prescriptions by aligning treatment decisions with both clinical evidence and patient values. There is limited qualitative research exploring physicians’ perspectives on its implementation in the study area. This study aims to explore physicians’ perspectives and experience of SDM in the management of Acute Respiratory Tract Infections (ARTIs) at public primary healthcare centres. Purposive sampling was applied to recruit physicians from selected primary healthcare centres in the Amhara region of Ethiopia. The sample size was determined using the concept of data saturation. The data were collected through semi-structured interviews and analysed using thematic analysis with the assistance of NVivo software (version 14). Twenty physicians were interviewed, and four main themes were identified: (1) awareness and understanding of SDM; (2) perceived value of SDM; (3) barriers to implementing SDM; (4) facilitators to enhance SDM implementation. The study found that, despite its recognised benefits, SDM is not yet a routine practice in primary healthcare centres in the study area due to identified barriers. Addressing these challenges and implementing the solutions suggested by participants will be essential to realise the full benefits of SDM and strengthen antimicrobial resistance mitigation efforts.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical global health threat, projected to cause 39 million deaths between 2025 and 2050, undermining modern medicine and making routine treatments and disease control efforts less safe and reliable1. Acute respiratory tract infections (ARTIs) are among the most common reasons for patient visits to primary healthcare centres worldwide, often resulting in high antimicrobial prescription rates despite strong evidence that antibiotics offer little benefit for most uncomplicated cases2,3. This widespread and often inappropriate use of antibiotics significantly contributes to the global threat of AMR, particularly in low- and middle-income countries like Ethiopia, where healthcare resources and diagnostic capabilities are often limited4,5.

Studies have shown that good patient-physician communication during consultation can reduce inappropriate antimicrobial prescribing6,7. Even if patients have limited medical knowledge, they possess the greatest insight into their values and preferences. Shared decision-making (SDM) leads to high patient satisfaction and improved treatment adherence. SDM is a collaborative process in which healthcare providers and patients work together to make informed healthcare choices by integrating clinical evidence with patients’ values, preferences, and circumstances8. This participatory model transcends traditional paternalistic approaches and recognises the importance of dialogue and mutual understanding in medical decision-making. Central to the ethical foundations of SDM is the principle of patient autonomy, which affords patients the right to be informed and actively involved in decisions about their health interventions9. By providing patients with clear, balanced information about treatment options and the potential benefits and harms of antibiotics, physicians can guide them toward evidence-based decisions, reducing unnecessary antibiotic use. Evidence suggests that interventions promoting SDM in primary healthcare can significantly reduce unnecessary antimicrobial prescription by aligning treatment decisions with both clinical evidence and patient values10. However, the adoption and effectiveness of SDM are shaped by local context, including healthcare professionals’ perceptions, cultural norms, and the realities of the healthcare system11.

Evidence suggests that antimicrobial use in Ethiopia is often characterised by rationality challenges, including inappropriate and overprescribing tendencies, particularly in primary health care settings6,7. A systematic review conducted in Ethiopia reported that the pooled prevalence of AMR is approximately 59.7%8. In these settings, prescribers face various factors, including patient expectations, limited laboratory diagnostics capabilities and insufficient healthcare worker training that influence antimicrobial prescribing practices9,10. These challenges underscore the importance of interventions that improve clinical decision-making, including SDM approaches that encourage evidence-based discussions between patients and clinicians. In this context, discussing the likely cause of illness, benefits and harms of antimicrobials, non-antimicrobial alternatives, and patients’ ability and willingness to follow recommended regimens, using the SDM approach is potentially important. However, the practice of SDM in Ethiopia is still limited and not widely institutionalised within routine healthcare delivery12. Several contextual factors influence this situation. For instance, limited consultation time due to high patient loads in primary healthcare facilities reduces the opportunity for detailed discussions between clinicians and patients about treatment options. In busy outpatient departments, clinicians may prioritise rapid diagnosis and treatment, which limits the implementation of SDM approaches13,14. In addition, in many Ethiopian communities, patients traditionally view healthcare professionals as authorised figures and may feel reluctant to be involved in treatment decisions, reflecting entrenched paternalistic norms in doctor-patient relationships15.

Despite the theoretical and empirical benefits of SDM, there is limited qualitative research focused on physicians’ perspectives on its implementation in LMICs, including Ethiopia. Many studies in Ethiopia have highlighted the barriers and facilitators of SDM in the context of cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and mental health, but limited attention has been given to how physicians perceive and engage in SDM, specifically in the treatment of ARTIs16,17. Furthermore, previous studies indicate antimicrobial prescribing in Ethiopia often relies on custom and habit rather than evidence-based guidelines, with regulatory challenges; however, none have comprehensively investigated how these issues affect physicians’ ability and attitudes towards engaging patients collaboratively through SDM in antimicrobial decisions11,18. Understanding physicians’ perspectives is critical for identifying context-specific challenges and opportunities that influence antimicrobial prescribing behaviours. Such perspectives can reveal key factors, including knowledge gaps, misconceptions, cultural norms, and systemic pressures, that shape prescribing practices. Additionally, gaining insights into physicians’ readiness and capacity to integrate SDM approaches can inform targeted interventions aimed at optimising antimicrobial use and combating AMR. Therefore, this study aims to explore physicians’ perspectives and experiences related to SDM in the management of ARTIs at public primary healthcare centres in Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed semi-structured interviews to explore physicians’ perspectives on SDM in antimicrobial prescribing for ARTIs in a primary healthcare centre in the Amhara region of Ethiopia. This study is guided by a realist epistemological position, which assumes that reality exists but is understood through participants’ experiences and contextual interpretations. Accordingly, a qualitative descriptive approach was employed as it allows for straightforward description of participants’ perceptions and experiences, while remaining closely tied to their actual words. The presentation of the study followed the 21-item Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist19. It offers structured guidance on essential elements of qualitative reporting, including research design, data collection and analysis, and reflexivity, to ensure methodological transparency and rigour.

Setting and participant recruitment

The study was conducted from February 2024 to July 2024 in purposively selected public primary healthcare centres across three cities in the Amhara region, Ethiopia: Bahir Dar, Gondar, and Debre Tabor. These cities are characterised by high population density and many primary healthcare centres. According to the Ethiopian Ministry of Health (2021), public health centres are designed to serve 25,000–40,000 people, providing outpatient, maternal and child health and pharmacy services. In practice, however, particularly in urban areas, the patient load is often much higher, with some centres serving up to 100,000 people. Each centre is staffed by a multidisciplinary team including a GP, health officers, nurses, midwives, laboratory and pharmacy personnel and health extension workers. Bahir Dar, the regional capital, has three hospitals and ten public primary healthcare centres. Similarly, Gondar, which has eight health centres and two hospitals, Debre Tabor has four health centres and one hospital. From these three cities, a total of seven health centres were proportionally selected: three from Bahir Dar, two from Gondar, and two from Debre Tabor.

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit physicians with relevant experience in antimicrobial prescribing. Eligible participants were licensed physicians working in the selected healthcare centres who were actively involved in treating patients and diagnosing. Physicians who are not involved in antimicrobial prescribing, were on leave during the data collection period or declined to participate were excluded from the study. The exclusive inclusion of physicians is intentional, as this study is part of a larger project specifically focusing on physicians’ perspectives on SDM in antimicrobial prescribing. While other healthcare cadres, such as health officers, also prescribe antimicrobials, they were beyond the scope of this study. Future research could extend this investigation to other prescriber groups to enhance generalizability and explore potential differences in SDM practices. A Total of 28 physicians were approached; 27 agreed to participate, while one declined due to a lack of time for the interview. The sample size was determined using the concept of data saturation20, which emphasises the quality, specificity and relevance of the information provided by participants rather than simply counting the interviews by considering the study’s narrow aim, participants’ expertise, and richness of data. Sufficient information was determined iteratively during data collection. The first author (GEK) continuously assessed whether interviews were generating new insights or concepts. Interviews were conducted until no additional information relevant to the research questions was generated. No additional or new information emerged after interviewing 20 participants.

Data collection procedure

Data collection was conducted by the first author (GEK), who holds an MSc degree in public health and biostatistics and has experience in both qualitative and quantitative research. He is a staff member at the University of Gondar, Ethiopia and was supported by experienced professors from the University of New England, Australia. Lists of licensed and active physicians at the selected healthcare centre were obtained from the outpatient department managers. Physicians were initially approached in person and invited to participate. Those who volunteered to participate were provided with a verbal explanation of the study’s purpose and procedures, along with a written information sheet. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their involvement in the study. Most interviews took place in a private office within the clinic, while some were arranged on weekends in a quiet location outside the clinic, for participants who were busy on working days, depending on their availability and preferences. The interviews took 20–40 minutes. Participants were informed of the confidentiality and anonymity measures in place to protect their data. To maintain confidentiality, no identifying information such as participants’ names, addresses or institutional details was collected. All interviews were audio recorded using a smartphone, and the recordings were securely transferred to a password-protected computer accessible only to the research team. Participants were informed that their response would be recorded and assured that they could withdraw from the study at any stage without consequence, including after the interview. To ensure accuracy, all recordings were transcribed verbatim, and any potentially identifying information was removed from transcripts to ensure that confidentiality was maintained throughout the research process.

Interviews

Data were collected through semi-structured, face-to-face interviews at the selected healthcare centres, using an interview guide. The questions were designed to elicit targeted information on specific aspects of SDM, resulting in high-quality, content-dense data within a relatively short time frame. The researchers prepared a list of semi-structured interview questions aligned with the study objectives. The interview guide was developed based on relevant literature21 and then reviewed by members of the research team to ensure clarity and relevance to the study objectives. The guide was further refined through a pilot interview (see supplementary file 1). The focus was on participants’ broad experience, knowledge, attitudes, and perceptions regarding patient involvement in SDM during the management of ARTIs. For example, have you ever heard of the term shared decision-making, and if so, could you explain what it is? Do you believe shared decision-making is important for antibiotic prescription for acute respiratory tract infection? If so, how? And can you tell me what challenges hinder the practice of shared decision-making? The interview guide included open-ended questions designed to encourage free and in-depth responses, with follow-up prompts such as “What do you mean?” and “Can you explain more?” or Can you give me an example?” used as needed. The interviews were conducted in Amharic, the participants’ primary working language. The interview guide was first developed in English and then translated into Amharic. The first author performed the translation. To minimise potential social desirability bias, participants were informed that the study aimed to understand their experiences rather than evaluate their performance.

Data analysis and trustworthiness

All transcribed data were translated from Amharic to English before coding and were back-checked with participants to confirm that their views had been accurately represented. The original and back-translated versions were compared for discrepancies in meaning. The transcribed data were analysed using an inductive reflexive thematic analysis in line with Braun and Clarke (2013). Data were initially analysed using NVivo (version 14)22. The software was used to organise and provide preliminary coding of the data, which were then used to identify emerging patterns across interviews in order to develop themes. The first author (GEK) carried out the initial thematic analysis. The analysis followed six iterative steps: (1) immersing in the interview transcripts to gain a thorough understanding of physicians narratives, (2) generating initial codes inductively to capture key ideas and meaning directly from the data regarding experiences and perceptions of SDM, (3) grouping similar codes into potential themes and subthemes that reflect physician perspectives of SDM, (4) reviewing and refining these themes to ensure they accurately reflected the data, (5) defining and naming the final themes and selecting representative quotations that best illustrated each theme, and (6) interpreting the findings by linking them to the research questions and existing literature on SDM. The other three authors (SC, JH, SI) independently reviewed the codes and themes developed by the first author, with the themes then revised through discussion in order to ensure that the analysis best reflects the data. The final themes and illustrative quotes were reviewed and agreed upon by all authors.

To ensure the quality and trustworthiness of the study, we applied the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba23: credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability. To uphold these criteria, we employed peer debriefing with team members. Peer debriefing sessions were conducted regularly with co-authors to discuss coding decisions, theme development, and interpretation. A detailed audit trail was maintained, including notes on coding decisions, theme revision, and supporting quotations.

Ethical approval

The study procedure was approved by the University of New England Human Research Ethics Committee (HE23-165). Additionally, support letters were provided by the University of Gondar and the director of the selected cities’ health administrators. All patients provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Result

Participants characteristics

Of the 20 participants interviewed, 8 were female, and 12 were male. The mean age of the participants was 37.2 years (range: 30–57 years), and their mean working experience was 11.8 years (range: 2–20 years). For instance, participant p05 had 20 years of experience, while participant p06 had two years of experience.

Themes and subthemes

Based on the analysis of interview data, four main themes were identified that reflect physicians’ perspectives and experiences of SDM in the management of ARTIs (Supplementary file 2). These themes include: (Theme 1) awareness and understanding of SDM, (Theme 2) perceived value and benefits of SDM, (Theme 3) Strategies to enhance SDM implementation and (Theme 4) barriers to SDM implementation. Each theme comprised several sub-themes. Theme 1 included two sub-themes, Theme 2 and 4 each comprised six sub-themes, and Theme 3 comprised three sub-themes and 10 lower-order sub-themes. The theme and sub-themes are presented in detail below, with illustrative quotations from participants to demonstrate how the findings are grounded in the interview data. The corresponding codebook and structure of themes and subthemes are provided as a supplementary file for further reference (Supplementary file 3).

Theme 1: Awareness and understanding of SDM

This theme reflects how physicians conceptualise SDM, their level of awareness of the concept, and how they define it. Participants had considerable variation in their awareness and understanding of SDM. Their conceptualisation of SDM was categorised into two subthemes, as described below.

Basic understanding/conceptual awareness of SDM

In discussing their perspectives and experiences of SDM, many participants demonstrated an understanding of the concept and could articulate its definition. They described SDM as a collaborative process involving both doctor and patient in treatment decisions. They emphasised that the approach requires mutual respect, discussion and sharing of information between healthcare professionals and patients. However, despite this awareness, many participants acknowledged that their understanding of SDM remains largely theoretical and is not consistently woven into their clinical practice. In other words, although participants recognised that SDM is intended to empower patients and improve the quality of care, the transition of this awareness into routine decision-making was limited.“Umm…, Yes, I have heard about SDM. It means shared decision-making means the doctor presents various treatment options to the patient, explains the benefit of each option, and allows the patient to make a choice.” (P06)“…. It seems to have heard this (SDM) before… It means the doctor and the patient reach a decision together.” (P12)

“Umm…, Yes, I have heard about SDM. It means shared decision-making means the doctor presents various treatment options to the patient, explains the benefit of each option, and allows the patient to make a choice.” (P06)

“…. It seems to have heard this (SDM) before… It means the doctor and the patient reach a decision together.” (P12)

Lack of awareness or unfamiliarity

In contrast, several participants indicated that they were unfamiliar with the concept of SDM. Some openly acknowledged that they had never heard of SDM and were unsure about its meaning or relevance. Their response indicated that while they occasionally invited patient input or listened to patient preferences, decision-making remained largely clinician-driven. For instance, one participant acknowledged that although they listen to patients’ opinions, they do not consistently take them into account when prescribing treatment.“I have never heard [of SDM]… what I usually do is, after hearing the patient’s opinion, I prescribe medication. However, when prescribing, I don’t always take the patient’s opinion into account. I don’t know what SDM is…” (P05).

“I have never heard [of SDM]… what I usually do is, after hearing the patient’s opinion, I prescribe medication. However, when prescribing, I don’t always take the patient’s opinion into account. I don’t know what SDM is…” (P05).

Furthermore, participants reported that their limited awareness of SDM was primarily attributed to the lack of formal training on its principles and practice.“…….. No, I have not received any training” (P03).

“…….. No, I have not received any training” (P03).

Theme 2: perceived value and benefits of SDM

Although participants varied in their awareness and understanding of SDM, those who were familiar with the concept, as well as those who initially had no awareness but were briefed on the concept by the interviewer, described several potential benefits. Participants emphasised that SDM is not only a process of involving patients but also a tool that can improve the quality of healthcare delivery. They described a range of perceived benefits for both patients and healthcare providers. The value of SDM was reflected in six interrelated themes.

Enhanced patient satisfaction and trust

Participants highlighted that SDM positively influenced the patient-physician relationship by fostering satisfaction and building trust. They noted that when patients felt actively involved in decisions about their care, they perceived physicians as more transparent, respectful, and supportive. This sense of inclusion encouraged openness, strengthened rapport, and reassured patients that their perspectives were valued. Participants also noted that patients who felt listened to and respected expressed satisfaction with the overall consultation process.“………….. we have a conversation with patients, it will encourage them to express their opinions, increase their satisfaction, and trust” (P11).

“………….. we have a conversation with patients, it will encourage them to express their opinions, increase their satisfaction, and trust” (P11).

Improved treatment adherence

Several participants observed that SDM encourages patients to follow treatment advice consistently. “When patients engage in a thorough discussion with me, I feel more confident about the treatment and they are more likely to follow it correctly” (P11).

Physicians observed that by involving patients in treatment planning and openly discussing options, patients were able to clarify doubts, express concerns, and gain insights into the rationale behind the recommendations. Participants explained that, when patients participate meaningfully in decision-making, they transition from passive recipients of care to active partners, leading to stronger motivation and consistency in following prescribed regimens.“If I prescribe a medicine without considering their opinion, they may not take it properly. But when they understand the reason, even if I prescribe medicine, they take it properly” (P05).

“If I prescribe a medicine without considering their opinion, they may not take it properly. But when they understand the reason, even if I prescribe medicine, they take it properly” (P05).

Reduced unnecessary antimicrobial use

Most participants highlighted that incorporating SDM approaches during consultation would reduce unnecessary antimicrobial prescribing, which they viewed as directly contributing to the mitigation of AMR. They explained that when physicians engaged patients in clear discussion about treatment options, including the risks, benefits, and possible alternatives, patients were more likely to understand why antibiotics might not be needed and felt empowered to accept non-antibiotic management strategies. This led patients to feel comfortable with decisions not to use antimicrobials and to be less likely to seek unnecessary medicines independently.“Let me tell you something a patient told me: ….. one day, the patient came to me and asked me to prescribe antibiotics. After diagnosis, I advised him that he could recover without antibiotic treatment by taking care at home. I saw him again on another day and asked, ‘How are you feeling?’ and he replied, ‘I got better just by your advice, without taking any medicine.’ This shows that if the doctor and the patient discuss, we can reduce unnecessary medications” (P14).

“Let me tell you something a patient told me: ….. one day, the patient came to me and asked me to prescribe antibiotics. After diagnosis, I advised him that he could recover without antibiotic treatment by taking care at home. I saw him again on another day and asked, ‘How are you feeling?’ and he replied, ‘I got better just by your advice, without taking any medicine.’ This shows that if the doctor and the patient discuss, we can reduce unnecessary medications” (P14).

Strengthened therapeutic relationship

The participants reported that implementing SDM has the potential to strengthen the therapeutic relationship between healthcare providers and patients by fostering a more positive connection between healthcare providers and patients. They believed that such a relationship helps to create a safe, supportive, and non-judgmental environment in which patients feel understood and valued, thereby encouraging their active participation in the treatment decision-making. Additionally, participants described that through SDM, patients feel respected and recognised as partners rather than passive recipients, which contributes to a positive and lasting therapeutic alliance.“It builds a strong relationship, patients feel respected and that their opinions matter…….” (P18)

“It builds a strong relationship, patients feel respected and that their opinions matter…….” (P18)

Patient empowerment and autonomy

This subtheme captures how SDM enhances patients’ sense of ownership and respect over their healthcare decisions. Participants viewed SDM as a transformative process that shifts power dynamics in clinical encounters, moving from the traditional physicians-dominated mode toward one that values patient voice, dignity and choice. They noted that when patients are involved in discussing treatment options and their opinions are genuinely considered, they feel more respected, valued, and motivated. This shifts the traditional dynamics by balancing clinician expertise with patients’ personal values and preferences, fostering a collaborative environment where patients gain confidence to make informed, autonomous choices about their health.“Umm … this approach [SDM] helps patients to make autonomous decisions and physicians to respect their decisions” (P04).“..Yes, it is very helpful. It is not good to base decisions only on our own opinion. Therefore, I believe that if the doctor and the patient can discuss and the decision is made based on the patient’s opinion, the patient feels respected and empowered. This helps to motivate patients” (P18).

“Umm … this approach [SDM] helps patients to make autonomous decisions and physicians to respect their decisions” (P04).

“..Yes, it is very helpful. It is not good to base decisions only on our own opinion. Therefore, I believe that if the doctor and the patient can discuss and the decision is made based on the patient’s opinion, the patient feels respected and empowered. This helps to motivate patients” (P18).

Improved information sharing and mutual understanding

This subtheme emphasises how open dialogue and the exchange of information establish the basis for informed decision-making. Participants described SDM as an interactive process that promotes reciprocal knowledge sharing, where physicians explain medical details and patients contribute their context, experiences, and preferences. Through these interactions, physicians develop a more complete understanding of patients’ conditions, past experiences, and expectations, which might otherwise be overlooked. This mutual understanding was seen as improving the appropriateness of prescribed treatments and boosting the confidence of both parties in the chosen decision. Furthermore, information sharing was regarded as a key enabler of trust and ongoing relational continuity in care, with physicians feeling that being open to patients’ own sources of knowledge (including information from the internet or peers) not only enhances engagement but also helps align clinical advice with patients’ beliefs.“…. Yes. Because every patient has their own knowledge, and they can get information in different ways: for example, they may have searched on Google before coming. So, discussing with the patient is very important to understand what they think about antimicrobials” (P06).

“…. Yes. Because every patient has their own knowledge, and they can get information in different ways: for example, they may have searched on Google before coming. So, discussing with the patient is very important to understand what they think about antimicrobials” (P06).

Theme 3: Barriers to implementing SDM

In the interviews, participants identified various challenges that hinder the implementation of SDM in clinical practice. These challenges were categorised under the main theme of barriers to implementing SDM, which comprised three sub-themes: patient-related barriers, health professional-related barriers, and system-related barriers. Each of these themes contains lower-order sub-themes that are discussed in the following sections.

Patient-related barriers

This theme reflects factors related to patients’ knowledge, behaviours and attitudes that limit their active involvement in SDM. The factors discussed by participants that related to patients were grouped into three subthemes: low health literacy and awareness, medication-seeking behaviour and expectations, and the influence of prior experiences and self-medications.

Low health literacy and awareness

As described under the theme of perceived value and benefit of SDM, participants acknowledged the advantage of involving patients in treatment decisions. However, they reported that the effective implementation of SDM often depends on patients having adequate health literacy and awareness. Participants highlighted that many patients lack sufficient knowledge, understanding, or confidence to engage in SDM, which limits their ability to participate meaningfully in the consultation process. This lack of understanding was perceived to hinder patients from articulating their preferences, asking questions or critically assessing treatment options, particularly regarding antibiotic use for ARTIs.“… Patients have different levels of understanding. Some come after researching their condition using various technologies, while others come with no knowledge at all about participation in the decision-making process” (P08).

“… Patients have different levels of understanding. Some come after researching their condition using various technologies, while others come with no knowledge at all about participation in the decision-making process” (P08).

Medication-seeking behaviours and pressure

Unlike low health literacy, which reflects patients’ limited knowledge of SDM, this theme reflects the behavioural tendencies and entrenched expectations of patients who equate antibiotics with appropriate or high-quality care. Participants frequently reported encountering strong patient expectations for antibiotics, even when such treatment was not clinically indicated. They perceived that these expectations often created pressure on physicians during consultations. Some participants described that refusing to prescribe antibiotics could result in patient dissatisfaction.“…. Well, as it is known, most patients come seeking medication. If we do not prescribe medication, they are not satisfied. However, as it is known, this illness is mostly caused by a virus, and medication does not help in curing the illness” (P07).

“…. Well, as it is known, most patients come seeking medication. If we do not prescribe medication, they are not satisfied. However, as it is known, this illness is mostly caused by a virus, and medication does not help in curing the illness” (P07).

Beyond immediate expectations, participants also explained that patients’ previous experiences and self-medication habits influenced their treatment preferences. Many relied on antibiotics they had used before or obtained without prescriptions, often coming to clinics primarily to confirm a diagnosis rather than to discuss treatment options. Some would later purchase antibiotics from private pharmacies even when physicians advised against them. Participants perceived that this reduces the opportunity for mutual exchange of information, and balanced discussion of risks and benefits; consequently, the interaction becomes more of a confirmation of the patient’s expectations rather than a collaborative decision-making process.“Yes, surprisingly, some even buy medication from private pharmacies and use it, and when we refuse to prescribe the same medication and prescribe something else, they say, ‘I already took this, it didn’t help me, so give me something else.’ Some even insist, for example, ‘Don’t prescribe me the yellow one’” (P04).

“Yes, surprisingly, some even buy medication from private pharmacies and use it, and when we refuse to prescribe the same medication and prescribe something else, they say, ‘I already took this, it didn’t help me, so give me something else.’ Some even insist, for example, ‘Don’t prescribe me the yellow one’” (P04).

Some participants also highlighted that the patients’ expectations regarding medication varied between urban and rural areas. Patients from urban areas were more assertive and informed, sometimes arriving with a specific request for medications, whereas those from rural areas were more likely to defer to the physician’s recommendation.“Their preferences vary depending on where they come from. For instance, urban patients often come specifically asking for certain medications. This creates difficulty in providing proper care” (P08).

“Their preferences vary depending on where they come from. For instance, urban patients often come specifically asking for certain medications. This creates difficulty in providing proper care” (P08).

Furthermore, some participants also highlighted that misconceptions, such as believing that having health insurance entitles them to receive antibiotics during every visit, further hindered constructive discussion about treatment options.

V“Another issue is health insurance. Many patients believe that having insurance entitles them to antibiotics every time. For instance, one patient didn’t even visit due to illness, but to use their insurance to get medicine for someone else. This is very challenging because they lack sufficient knowledge, so they expect antibiotics,” (P04).

Fear and hesitancy to share opinions

Several participants explained that, during consultations, many patients appear reluctant or hesitant to express their views or preferences regarding treatment. Patients often appear to feel intimidated by their physicians, with cultural perceptions of physicians as authority figures, combined with fear of judgment or offending the provider, perceived to be contributing to this hesitancy. The perception of the physicians was that this discourages patients from openly expressing their preferences or sharing their experiences, which in turn limits their active participation in decision-making.“……There are many challenges. First, patients often don’t realise they are supposed to participate in the decision-making …… many patients are afraid of their doctors, so they hesitate to express their opinions” (P19).

“……There are many challenges. First, patients often don’t realise they are supposed to participate in the decision-making …… many patients are afraid of their doctors, so they hesitate to express their opinions” (P19).

Religious influence on patient participation

Some participants who had some awareness of or a positive attitude toward SDM noted that religious practices and expectations influence patients’ engagement in the SDM process. For example, one participant highlighted that when attempting to involve patients in treatment discussions, some patients strictly adhered to their religious practices or observances, such as fasting periods, which sometimes conflicted with medical recommendations. He added that some patients requested medications to be taken at specific intervals, such as every 12 hours, while others completely rejected our medical recommendations. In this context, SDM was not only shaped by individual patient preference but also by broader religious and cultural frameworks that influence health-seeking behaviours.“Another problem is religious—for example, during fasting periods, if we advise them to do something, they say, ‘Today is fasting’. Patients might avoid taking the prescribed medication or might make decisions based on their religious beliefs and might not accept our recommendations.” (P15).

“Another problem is religious—for example, during fasting periods, if we advise them to do something, they say, ‘Today is fasting’. Patients might avoid taking the prescribed medication or might make decisions based on their religious beliefs and might not accept our recommendations.” (P15).

Health professionals’ related barriers

This sub-theme highlights the challenges originating from physicians themselves, which hinder the effective implementation of SDM. The factors were grouped into three lower-order sub-themes: lack of understanding of SDM, persistence of hierarchical attitudes and time pressure constraints.

Lack of understanding of SDM

As discussed earlier in relation to participants’ awareness of SDM, several participants lacked awareness, which limits the implementation of SDM. Many participants highlighted that they had not received any training on SDM practices and were largely unfamiliar with the underlying concepts. They perceived that this lack of understanding often led physicians to rely on traditional approaches to clinical decision-making. They believed that understanding the concept of SDM was a critical step for practising it in healthcare.“No, I don’t know. I haven’t received any formal training on this topic” (P12).

“No, I don’t know. I haven’t received any formal training on this topic” (P12).

Persistence of hierarchical attitudes

Some participants emphasised that physicians should retain greater decision-making power, which often led to decisions being made without meaningful patient input. These physicians expressed a preference for an authoritative approach, based on the belief that as medical experts, they are best positioned to determine the most appropriate course of treatment. This perspective reflects a more traditional, hierarchical model of care in which the physician assumes primary responsibility for clinical decisions, while patients are expected to comply with recommendations.“.. to be honest, involving the patient’s decision is considered to interfere with the treatment. Therefore, if only the professional decides, it is better because the doctor has a good, that is to say, well-informed understanding”(P17).

“.. to be honest, involving the patient’s decision is considered to interfere with the treatment. Therefore, if only the professional decides, it is better because the doctor has a good, that is to say, well-informed understanding”(P17).

Time pressure constraints

Nearly all participants reported that time pressure was the primary barrier to practising SDM. Throughout the interviews, participants expressed the view that implementing SDM during consultations would require more time, which could limit their ability to see all patients within the available timeframe. This often led to rushed consultations where doctors felt forced to prioritise quick prescribing over in-depth discussion, thereby reducing patient participation and shared understanding.“….. in my understanding, since shared decision-making requires detailed explanation, it can be quite time-consuming to practice” (P09).

“….. in my understanding, since shared decision-making requires detailed explanation, it can be quite time-consuming to practice” (P09).

System-related barriers

Participants highlighted various system-related factors that hinder the effective practice of SDM. They perceived that these systemic constraints not only limit the practical implementation of SDM but may also contribute to overprescribing and undermine the quality of the therapeutic relationship. These factors formed three lower-order sub-themes: poor financial incentives, shortage of physician staff, and inadequate regulation of private pharmacies.

Poor compensation and incentives for healthcare providers

Most participants consistently emphasised that physicians’ motivation to practice SDM is strongly shaped by their working conditions, especially pay and recognition. Many perceived that inadequate compensation, coupled with heavy workloads, reduces physicians’ willingness and ability to engage patients in comprehensive discussion. This lack of adequate pay for their work was reported to contribute to feelings of disappointment and dissatisfaction, which in turn reduced their commitment to practising SDM. Some participants perceived that SDM was an additional burden rather than an integral component of patient-centred care.

“Doctors are often unhappy due to low salaries, which affects the quality of care and willingness to engage in lengthy SDM conversations” (P11).“Because the payment for health professionals is not adequate, many don’t deliver care wholeheartedly” (P08).

“Because the payment for health professionals is not adequate, many don’t deliver care wholeheartedly” (P08).

Shortage of physician staff

Participants consistently identified the shortage of physicians as a key structural barrier to SDM. They emphasised that when a single clinician is required to shoulder the responsibilities of many, their main concern becomes attending to all patients rather than engaging in meaningful dialogue with patients. They perceived that this not only limits opportunities for patients to express their concerns and preferences fully but also reduces clinicians’ capacity to explain treatment options in detail.“Because there are not enough professionals, the available doctors try to see all patients quickly, often ending up just prescribing antimicrobials and sending the patient away” (P06).“The imbalance between the number of patients and doctors makes it difficult to reach everyone…..” (P12).

“Because there are not enough professionals, the available doctors try to see all patients quickly, often ending up just prescribing antimicrobials and sending the patient away” (P06).

“The imbalance between the number of patients and doctors makes it difficult to reach everyone…..” (P12).

Inadequate regulation of private pharmacies

Participants also highlighted that the widespread practice of selling antimicrobials without prescriptions in private pharmacies poses a major barrier to SDM. When patients can easily obtain antimicrobials directly from these pharmacies, often without consulting a healthcare provider, they may not perceive the need to engage in discussions about appropriate treatment options. Participants perceived that this practice diminishes patients’ appreciation for the value of consultation and hinders physicians’ efforts to involve them in SDM.“Private pharmacies sell antibiotics without a prescription, so patients don’t see the value of discussion.” (P15)

“Private pharmacies sell antibiotics without a prescription, so patients don’t see the value of discussion.” (P15)

Theme 4: Strategies to enhance SDM implementation

Participants emphasised the importance of employing deliberate strategies to enhance patient participation during consultations. They noted that encouraging active involvement not only fosters better communications and trust but also supports more informed and collaborative decision-making. These strategies encompass patient-focused, provider-focused and system-level interventions, which are organised into six subthemes described below.

Building trust and rapport

Most participants emphasised the importance of establishing trust to enable patient participation. They believed that building trust and rapport is crucial for effective healthcare because it establishes the foundation for open collaboration, honest communication and patient engagement. Trust was described as a prerequisite for patients to feel comfortable sharing personal health information, expressing their concerns, and voicing their concerns without fear of judgment or reprimand. Participants noted that when trust is established, patients are more likely to actively participate in consultations, ask questions, and consider different treatment options, which facilitates SDM.“First, approaching them as family—like a sister, a brother, or a father—is necessary. Making them feel comfortable is important. It’s important to create trust and make them feel comfortable” (P05).

“First, approaching them as family—like a sister, a brother, or a father—is necessary. Making them feel comfortable is important. It’s important to create trust and make them feel comfortable” (P05).

Recognising the value of patient experience and input in treatment

Participants highlighted that recognising patients’ lived experiences, prior knowledge, and perspectives is essential for fostering meaningful dialogue during consultations. They noted that when patients are offered options and provided with opportunities to make informed decisions, they feel respected and more engaged in the treatment process. Participants suggested that acknowledging the patient’s experiential knowledge not only enhances understanding of their condition but also supports tailored and acceptable treatment plans.“…Because the patient has their own experience and knowledge, this really helps during treatment. If this is not the case, and I only offer treatment based on my own opinion, the patient will not be satisfied and may not follow our advice” (P05).

“…Because the patient has their own experience and knowledge, this really helps during treatment. If this is not the case, and I only offer treatment based on my own opinion, the patient will not be satisfied and may not follow our advice” (P05).

Similarly, some participants noted that patients come with information obtained through different sources, such as online searches, which can inform the consultation if acknowledged constructively. When acknowledged constructively, this pre-existing information can serve as a starting point for dialogue: it signals patient interest, reveals their concerns and assumptions, and offers clinicians an opportunity to correct misunderstandings and to build shared evidence for decision-making.“Every patient has their own knowledge, and they can get information in different ways—for example, they may have searched on Google before coming. So, discussing with the patient is very important” (P06).

“Every patient has their own knowledge, and they can get information in different ways—for example, they may have searched on Google before coming. So, discussing with the patient is very important” (P06).

Respecting privacy and confidentiality

Participants emphasised that ensuring privacy and maintaining confidentiality during consultations is fundamental to fostering open communication with patients. They reported that when patients perceive the consultation environment as private, respectful, and non-judgmental, they are more likely to share information honestly, discuss concerns, and actively participate in decision-making. Privacy and confidentiality were described as particularly important when discussing personal health issues, medications, or conditions that may carry social stigma, as patients may otherwise withhold information that is crucial to making informed decisions.“Respecting their privacy, showing a good attitude, especially starting with a calm and friendly approach. Then, approaching them as a friend and making them feel free to express their opinions without hesitation.” (P07)

“Respecting their privacy, showing a good attitude, especially starting with a calm and friendly approach. Then, approaching them as a friend and making them feel free to express their opinions without hesitation.” (P07)

Patient education and awareness

Most participants emphasised the importance of raising community awareness about SDM through various outreach efforts. They argued that many patients are unaware of their rights to participate in treatment decisions and often enter consultation with the expectation of receiving a prescription, particularly for antimicrobials. Suggested approaches included mass media campaigns, community-based education programs, and the active involvement of health extension workers. By improving patient knowledge, participants believed it would be possible to empower individuals to take a more active role in discussions, reduce misunderstandings, and align expectations with appropriate clinical care.“Patients don’t understand their role and often come expecting a prescription; educating them through health extension workers and media can change this” (P09)“It is important to increase awareness among patients about their rights to be involved in decisions; community education using media, TV, and social platforms would help.” (P02, P19)

“Patients don’t understand their role and often come expecting a prescription; educating them through health extension workers and media can change this” (P09)

“It is important to increase awareness among patients about their rights to be involved in decisions; community education using media, TV, and social platforms would help.” (P02, P19)

Training healthcare professionals about SDM

Participants also highlighted the need to build the capacity of healthcare providers through formal training in SDM principles and practices. Many participants acknowledged that they had not received any formal instruction in this area, which limited their ability to confidently and consistently involve patients in decision-making. They suggested that targeted training programs and continuous professional development strengthen their ability to balance clinical expertise with patient values and preferences.“Most health professionals have not received formal training on SDM; a short, effective training session would improve understanding and practice” (P02).“… What I think could help is providing training to professionals, especially on how to care for patients properly” (P13).

“Most health professionals have not received formal training on SDM; a short, effective training session would improve understanding and practice” (P02).

“… What I think could help is providing training to professionals, especially on how to care for patients properly” (P13).

Regulation and monitoring of private pharmacies

Several participants underscored the importance of stronger regulation and monitoring of private pharmacies, particularly in relation to the unregulated dispensing of antimicrobials. They noted that when patients can easily access antimicrobials without consultation, it undermines the relevance and credibility of SDM within clinical settings. Stricter enforcement of pharmacy laws, oversight mechanisms, and penalties for unauthorised sales were proposed as essential steps to ensure that antimicrobials are prescribed and used appropriately.“Private pharmacies sell medications without prescriptions, and when patient numbers increase, we are under pressure, so this indicates proper monitoring or regulation of private pharmacies that sell antimicrobials without prescriptions.” (P04).“Strengthening pharmacy oversight and controlling unauthorised antimicrobial sales would support SDM goals” (P18).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore physicians’ perspectives and experiences regarding SDM in treating ARTIs within public primary healthcare. It identified four major themes with related subthemes. The results showed that SDM has not yet become routine practice in primary healthcare centres. The findings highlighted both opportunities and challenges in adopting SDM, offering insights into its perceived value, barriers to implementation, and ways to encourage its practice. Although participants recognised SDM as a potentially valuable approach to improving care, their understanding of the concept was often limited; some had only a basic or theoretical awareness, while many others were unfamiliar with the term, lacked knowledge of its principles, or had not received formal training in its application.

Even though the majority of participants had limited experience with SDM, they described its benefits, including enhanced patient satisfaction, stronger therapeutic relationships, improved adherence, and reduced unnecessary antimicrobial prescriptions. A systematic review of the effect of SDM on patient satisfaction, treatment adherence, and health status reported that SDM interventions significantly improve patient satisfaction and treatment adherence compared with usual care24. These findings suggest that involving patients in their healthcare decisions fosters both motivation and the understanding necessary for following the physician’s recommendations. Engaging patients in transparent discussions of risks and alternatives of antimicrobial treatments can reduce inappropriate prescribing while maintaining trust, a dynamic particularly crucial in contexts where antimicrobials are often overused and easily accessible without a prescription16,25. Additionally, SDM was perceived as enhancing information sharing and mutual understanding, providing clinicians with valuable insights into patients’ perspectives, experiences, and knowledge. This helps clinicians to move beyond surface-level symptoms and history, gaining insights into what matters most to the patient, such as past experiences with similar treatments and any concerns or misconceptions they may hold26. This reciprocity fosters mutual respect, deeper trust, and supports tailored, patient-centred care, which has been shown to improve satisfaction, treatment adherence, and outcomes.

Despite recognising its benefits, participants identified several barriers to practising SDM, which were grouped into patient-related, provider-related, and systemic factors. Physicians highlighted that low health literacy among patients significantly limits their ability to engage meaningfully in healthcare decisions. Evidence shows that patients often struggle to understand medical information or evaluate the risks and benefits of treatment options, limiting their ability to make informed decisions27. In Ethiopia, low educational attainment and limited awareness of AMR further compound these challenges, as patients commonly expect to be prescribed antimicrobials even though such antimicrobials are not clinically indicated16,18. Moreover, feelings of intimidation and hierarchical patient-provider dynamics also discouraged individuals from voicing preferences or concerns, restricting opportunities for the open dialogue fundamental to SDM18,27. Furthermore, this study indicated that patient behaviours, shaped by prior experiences and the accessibility of private pharmacies, further complicated SDM practices. Frequently, patients expected physicians to prescribe antimicrobials rather than engage in discussions about alternatives, which could lead to dissatisfaction when consultations did not result in a prescription28. Such behaviours not only diminish patient engagement but also place pressure on clinicians to prescribe inappropriately, thereby undermining SDM and contributing to AMR. beyond individual knowledge gaps, broader socio-cultural factors including reliance on traditional beliefs, limited insurance coverage incentives and perception that insurance entitles patients to prescriptions, further shape patients’ expectations and constrain SDM in low-and middle-income settings like Ethiopia29,30. This insights suggest that addressing patient-related barriers to SDM requires multifaceted strategies: not only improving health literacy but also modifying systematic incentives, empowering patients to participate confidently, and fostering a clinical culture that shapes SDM despite entrenched hierarchical norms.

The limited routine practice of SDM observed in this study should also be understood within the health system context of Ethiopia. Primary healthcare facilities often experience high patient volumes and heavy workload, which restricts the time available for detailed discussions with patients. The national analysis “Patient Volume and Quality of Primary Care in Ethiopia” showed that many facilities see very high outpatient volumes, and that one facility exceeded about 90 outpatients per day13. Medical training in many LMIC settings has traditionally emphasised a paternalistic model of care31. Limited formal training on SDM and communication skills further constraints it implementation in routine practice. In this study, many participants reported that they had never received training on its application. This lack of training contributes to limited conceptual understanding and reliance on traditional, physician-centred decision-making approaches. The results indicated that participants often confused the concept of SDM with general patient counselling or health education. However, SDM is a collaborative process in which clinicians and patients jointly discuss the likely course of illness, the benefits and harms of treatment options, considering patient preferences and values, and make decisions rather than simply providing information or advice. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies where insufficient training of SDM health professionals has been identified as a key obstacle to its practice17,18. Additionally, the presence of hierarchical and authoritative attitudes among some physicians reflects a deeply rooted cultural and professional norm in which clinical expertise is perceived as the sole basis for decision-making. Consequently, physicians often retain ultimate authority over treatment decisions. Previous studies conducted in low- and middle-income countries have similarly reported physician dominance in the consultation process, often justified by concerns over patient safety, time constraints, or perceptions of patients’ limited knowledge32,33. While such attitudes may stem from a desire to ensure evidence-based care, they inadvertently marginalise patient voices and reduce opportunities for SDM. Overcoming this barrier requires cultural shifts within the profession, supported by institutional policies that actively promote patient involvement as a marker of quality care. Targeted SDM training, along with encouraging providers to value patient input, is essential for shifting practice towards SDM.

This study identified that low motivation and job dissatisfaction among physicians are important barriers, primarily resulting from inadequate financial compensation for their extra work time, heavy workloads, and a perceived lack of recognition. Participants indicated that they handle patient volumes exceeding the standards for a single physician. Such heavy workloads limit the time available for meaningful patient interactions, making it challenging to engage in the detailed, two-way communication essential for SDM. Under such conditions, health professionals tend to prioritise task completion over patient engagement, which inevitably undermines the practice of SDM32,34. Additionally, participants reported receiving inadequate compensation for the additional time devoted to patient care. The imbalance between patient numbers and physician availability results in insufficient time to engage patients fully in decision-making, causing some physicians to perceive SDM as an added burden rather than an essential part of care delivery34. This leads to rushed consultations where doctors often must prioritise quick prescribing over in-depth dialogue, thus limiting patient participation and shared understanding. Such hurried prescribing practices can contribute to inappropriate prescription of antimicrobials, which in turn promotes the growth of AMR. Addressing these issues requires workload reduction through adequate staffing.

Limitation and future research implications

This study has some limitations. First, it was limited to physicians working in public primary healthcare centres and did not include those in secondary, tertiary, academic teaching hospitals or private healthcare centres. Future research should explore both physicians’ and patients’ perspectives on SDM across diverse healthcare settings in Ethiopia. Second, the study focused solely on the perspectives of physicians and their experience; objective observations of physicians interacting with patients were not conducted, and the extent of any reporting bias is unclear. Further research should incorporate direct observations of physicians’ interactions to provide a deeper understanding of how SDM is practised in clinical settings. In addition, social desirability bias may have influenced participants’ responses, as physicians may have presented their practice more favourably during interviews. Although efforts were made to minimise this by ensuring confidentiality and conducting interviews in private settings, this possibility cannot be entirely excluded. Third, the sample in this study is not representative of all Ethiopian physicians, as participants were recruited only from three cities in the Amhara region. Finally, the study did not capture patients’ perspectives on SDM, which may significantly influence its implementation. Addressing these gaps in future studies would provide a more comprehensive picture of SDM. Additionally, intervention studies evaluating SDM training programs, patient education initiatives, and antimicrobial stewardship strategies are needed.

Conclusion

Evidence in this study highlights physicians’ experience with potential benefits and the challenges of implementing SDM in the management of ARTIs treatment. While participants acknowledged its potential benefits, including enhanced patient engagement, improved treatment adherence, and strengthened trust, its practice is hindered by patient, provider, and system-level barriers. Alongside these challenges, participants also identified potential solutions, most notably the need for targeted provider training, patient education, stronger regulation of private pharmacies, and workload reforms to balance the patient-physician ratios. It is worth considering these to realise the full benefits of SDM and strengthen the fight against AMR.
