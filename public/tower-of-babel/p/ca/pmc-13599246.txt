Tobacco marketing and youth perceptions: connections between industry tactics and beliefs about harm and addiction among those under 21
Authors: Suzanne Frisbee, Meghan B. Moran, Maryam Ibrahim, Lauren Czaplicki, Ryan D. Kennedy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTobacco marketing shapes consumer perceptions, particularly among young people. Despite regulatory restrictions, subtle tactics may continue to mislead youth and young adults. This study examines how specific features in cigarette and e-cigarette ads affect perceived harm and addictiveness among 18-20-year-olds.MethodsWe recruited 1,708 U.S. participants aged 18–20 across two survey waves (2022–2023). Participants viewed randomized cigarette and e-cigarette ads and rated perceived harm and addictiveness. Ads (n = 221) were coded for youth-relevant features. Mixed-effects logistic regression assessed associations between ad features and perceptions, adjusting for demographics, tobacco use, brand familiarity, and wave.ResultsFor e-cigarette ads, flavors and gift promotions were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions, while sweepstakes, social media engagement, and socialization themes were linked to a higher likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions. In cigarette ads, use cues and pleasure claims were linked to a lower likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions, whereas gift promotions were linked to a higher likelihood of misperceptions. Regarding addiction, price promotions and use cues in e-cigarette ads were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate addiction perceptions, while sweepstakes, social media engagement, and socialization themes were linked to a higher likelihood of inaccurate addiction perceptions. For cigarette ads, only use cues predicted a lower likelihood of inaccurate addiction perceptions.ConclusionAdvertising features can influence young adults’ misperceptions of nicotine and tobacco product risks. Specifically, sweepstakes, social media engagement, and youth-oriented socialization themes may obscure harm and addictiveness, especially for e-cigarettes. Findings support comprehensive regulation to address misleading marketing.

Introduction

Tobacco marketing has long been a driving force behind the widespread initiation of smoking [1], with companies investing billions annually to promote their products through a variety of advertising and promotional strategies [2, 3]. These efforts, spanning television, radio, print, digital media, branding, and incentives, are designed to attract consumers, foster brand loyalty, and shape public perceptions of tobacco products. In particular, tobacco companies have increasingly employed “modified risk” marketing strategies, promoting products with descriptors like “additive-free,” “natural,” or “light” [4–6], that imply reduced harm or addictiveness. Under the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) regulatory framework, only products authorized through the Modified Risk Tobacco Product (MRTP) pathway may legally use FDA-approved modified-risk or reduced-exposure messaging in marketing materials [7, 8]. For example, certain smokeless tobacco products and heated tobacco products have received FDA authorization for limited reduced-exposure claims [9]. However, tobacco companies have historically used explicit and implied reduced-risk messaging for products that are not FDA-authorized MRTPs, particularly cigarettes marketed with descriptors, packaging, branding, or product design features that suggest reduced harm. These unauthorized or implied claims may mislead consumers into perceiving certain cigarette products as inherently safer or less harmful than other cigarettes [7, 8]. Unregulated or implied reduced-risk claims, especially when conveyed through product design, packaging, or terminology, can shape consumer perceptions in ways that minimize perceived harm [10, 11].

Evidence from national and international studies suggests that even without explicit health claims, marketing features such as flavors, color schemes, filter types, and brand imagery can decrease perceived risk [10, 11] and increase appeal [12]. Misconceptions persist around nicotine’s role in causing cancer and the idea that some products promote weight control, (10) or deliver less harmful smoke [12, 13]. Additionally, descriptors such as “smooth,” “mild,” or “low tar,” [12, 13] and sensory cues like taste or harshness reinforce these misperceptions and foster product loyalty [11, 12]. These dynamics are particularly problematic when consumers interpret certain products as safer alternatives to quitting altogether, potentially delaying cessation attempts [10, 12].

Despite regulatory advances, misleading marketing remains widespread. The 2009 Family Smoking Prevention and Tobacco Control Act (FSPTCA) granted the FDA authority to regulate tobacco product marketing, including restrictions on flavors (excluding menthol), misleading descriptors (e.g., “light,” “mild”), and advertising tactics likely to appeal to youth [14]. However, the tobacco industry continues to evolve its strategies, often using subtle and modern approaches—such as packaging design, digital promotion, and social media advertising—to influence public perception while avoiding regulatory scrutiny.

One population particularly vulnerable to these marketing messages is young adults, especially those just below the legal purchase age. While youth-focused advertising has been historically prevalent, young people remain a key demographic for tobacco prevention efforts, given that initiation and patterns of use are often established before age 21 [15, 16]. Traditional cigarette advertising has long targeted youth through themes of independence, rebellion, and social appeal [15], with campaigns such as those for Joe Camel establishing lasting brand recognition among youth [16]. Even after the Master Settlement Agreement restricted certain types of cigarette advertising [17], studies suggest that indirect appeals to youth, such as lifestyle imagery (e.g., advertisements portraying social success, popularity, attractiveness, nightlife, or aspirational identities associated with tobacco use), and placement in youth-oriented media (e.g., social media platforms, influencer content, streaming media, retail environments, online advertising, sponsorships, and youth-oriented entertainment media) have persisted [18–20]. For example, the rise of e-cigarettes, exemplified by Juul’s rapid expansion from 2015 to 2018, demonstrates how newer products and platforms can reach this group effectively [21–23]. Marketing strategies often highlight product design features, such as flavored options and sleek device aesthetics [24], through appealing branding and social media promotion [25], making these products especially attractive to youth despite claims of adult-focused cessation support [21–23].

This study addresses critical gaps in understanding how specific features of cigarette and e-cigarette marketing influence young adults’ perceptions of product harm and addictiveness. By focusing on individuals aged 18 to 20, who are legally restricted from purchasing nicotine and tobacco products, this research provides timely insights into how marketing strategies contribute to risk misperceptions during a developmental period characterized by heightened vulnerability to tobacco initiation and progression to regular use [26]. Findings will inform regulatory approaches designed to protect this vulnerable population and strengthen tobacco control policies.

Methods

Sample

We aimed to recruit 2,000 participants aged 18–20 residing in the U.S. across two waves of data collection: 1,000 in Wave 1 (August–September 2022) and 1,000 in Wave 2 (July–September 2023). Participants were recruited using convenience sampling via Qualtrics Panels. Within Qualtrics, eligible participants were first identified through a short online screener, and those meeting eligibility criteria were then directed into the full study survey. We used quotas to recruit an approximately equal number of participants who currently use e-cigarettes (some days or every day) and who do not currently use e-cigarettes (not at all or only rarely). After applying procedures for data quality control (including embedded attention checks, consistency checks between screener and survey responses, and manual review of open-ended responses), 1,708 participants remained in the final analytic sample.

Procedure

We employed a two-part procedure combining a survey in which participants viewed and responded to questions about cigarette and e-cigarette ads with a content analysis of those ads. Participants received monetary compensation through the Qualtrics Panels system in accordance with standard panel incentive structures. Study procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board at the authors’ university (IRB#[redacted]).

Survey: Participants first completed an online screener survey using the Qualtrics platform to determine their eligibility to participate. After providing informed consent, eligible participants completed the online survey. Each participant viewed 3 randomly selected cigarette ads (from a pool of 109) and 3 randomly selected e-cigarette ads (from a pool of 112). Ads were presented one at a time, with the advertisement and corresponding survey questions displayed on the same page. Participants could view each ad for as long as they wished before responding to questions assessing their perceptions of each ad. No minimum or maximum viewing times were imposed in order to approximate naturalistic advertisement exposure, consistent with methods used in our prior published advertising studies [18–20]. The survey also collected sociodemographic information and data on participants’ tobacco use experiences. The full survey took approximately 8–10 min to complete.

Content analysis: Ads used in the survey were obtained from market research firms (Numerator and Kantar, which have since merged and are now known as MediaRadar [27], the Rutgers Institute for Nicotine and Tobacco Studies, “Trinkets and Trash” repository [28], and from the official social media accounts of major tobacco brands. We identified the top 10 cigarette and e-cigarette brands by market share as of January 2021 (Wave 1) and July 2023 (Wave 2), excluding those with insufficient representation in our ad inventory. A pool of 221 ads were selected: 108 cigarette ads (21 Camel, 21 Marlboro, 18 Natural American Spirit, 15 Newport, 21 Winston, 12 L&M), and 113 e-cigarette ads (19 Bidi, 16 JUUL, 23 blu, 23 Logic, 23 Vuse, 9 Leap). Ads were excluded if they were audio-only (e.g., radio), video-based, not consumer-facing, or tied to specific events (e.g., holidays). A total of 78 cigarette ads and 84 e-cigarette ads were shown in Wave 1, and 78 cigarette and 81 e-cigarette ads were shown in Wave 2.

Ads were double-coded for a variety of features potentially linked to tobacco product appeal and perceptions of harm or addiction [20, 22, 23, 25, 29, 30]. Ads could be coded for multiple features; however, theme coding was limited to a maximum of two predominant themes per ad, consistent with prior tobacco advertising content analyses [24, 31]. Two trained coders, achieving intercoder reliability above 0.80, independently coded all ads; discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Measures

The two primary outcomes were perceptions of harm and addictiveness for both cigarette and e-cigarette products. To assess relative harm and addictiveness perceptions, participants were asked how harmful/addictive they thought cigarettes/e-cigarettes are compared to other electronic nicotine products, with the following response options: “A lot less”, “A little less”, “Same”, “A little more”, “A lot more”, and “Don’t know”. Responses were dichotomized as “Accurate” (“A lot more harmful,” “A little more harmful,” “About the same”) vs. “Inaccurate” (“A little less harmful,” “A lot less harmful,” “Don’t know”).

Additional Variables: Participants reported demographic information including age, gender identity, race, ethnicity, and sexual identity. We also measured brand familiarity for each product type and tobacco use experience (never tried and not susceptible, never tried and susceptible, or ever tried, based on standard susceptibility measures [32], for cigarettes and e-cigarettes.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in Stata SE, Version 18. Content coding for ads was merged with participant survey responses to create a single dataset that included both participant-level (e.g., demographics, tobacco use, brand familiarity) and ad-level (e.g., content features) variables. Ad features appearing in fewer than five ads across both products (e.g. cigarettes and e-cigarettes) were excluded from analysis to ensure adequate representation.

Descriptive statistics summarized participant characteristics. Chi-square tests confirmed random assignment of ads across participant sociodemographic and tobacco-use characteristics and examined prevalence of harm and addiction perceptions by ad feature.

To assess associations between ad features and outcome variables, we conducted mixed-effects logistic regression models. These models accounted for fixed effects (e.g., ad features, participant covariates) and random effects (participant ID and ad ID). Separate models were conducted for each product (cigarettes and e-cigarettes) and each outcome (harm and addictiveness).

Model 1 included each ad feature entered individually as a fixed effect, controlling for product brand and Wave (1 or 2) as fixed effects, and specifying random intercepts for participant and ad.

Model 2 included all ad features that were significant at p < .10 in Model 1. This model also adjusted for participant-level covariates, including age, gender identity, race, ethnicity, sexual identity, smoking status, and brand familiarity, while again controlling for product brand and Wave (1 or 2) as fixed effects, and specifying random intercepts for participant and ad.

This modeling approach allowed us to estimate the independent effects of ad features on participants’ perceptions of harm and addictiveness, while accounting for between-subject and between-ad variability and potential differences between the two waves of data collection.

Results

The study sample included 1,708 young adults aged 18 to 20, with a relatively even distribution across age groups (36.5% age 18, 31.1% age 19, and 32.5% age 20). The majority identified as female (54.9%), followed by male (38.2%), with smaller proportions identifying as transgender (2.5%) or nonbinary/another gender identity (4.4%). By race and ethnicity, participants self-identified as Non-Hispanic White (66.9%), Hispanic/Latino/a (26.9%), Non-Hispanic Black (20.7%), and Non-Hispanic Asian (7.8%), with smaller representation for other racial and ethnic groups. Regarding sexual orientation, 67.6% identified as straight, 22.0% as bisexual, 5.3% as lesbian or gay, and 5.2% as another orientation. In terms of tobacco use, 61.5% had never tried cigarettes and were not susceptible, 38.5% had never tried but were susceptible to cigarette use, and 37.3% had ever tried cigarettes. For e-cigarettes, 51.3% had never tried and were not susceptible, 48.7% had never tried but were susceptible to e-cigarette use, and 56.7% had ever tried the products. Awareness of cigarette brands was high, with Marlboro (76.0%), Camel (66.7%), and Newport (61.5%) being the most recognized. Among e-cigarette brands, Juul was the most widely known (86.2%), followed by Puff (64.5%), and Vuse (37.2%). Participant characteristics are reported in Table 2. No significant differences were found in participant characteristics based on ad exposure.

Harm perceptions

Mixed effects models (Table 3) showed that, for e-cigarette advertisements, ads featuring flavors were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions (i.e., more accurate perceptions) (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.5–0.9, p = .006), as were those with gift promotions (OR = 0.1, 95% CI: 0.01–0.5, p = .007). In contrast, ads containing sweepstakes promotions were associated with a higher likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions (i.e., participants were more likely to underestimate harms) (OR = 3.1, 95% CI: 1.5–6.4, p = .003), as were those including social media engagement (OR = 1.9, 95% CI: 1.3–2.7, p = .001) and youth-targeted socialization themes (OR = 3.0, 95% CI: 1.4–6.2, p = .004).

For cigarette advertisements, use cues (OR = 0.7, 95% CI: 0.5–0.9, p = .007) and pleasure claims (OR = 0.5, 95% CI: 0.2–0.9, p = .035) were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions, while gift promotions were linked to a higher likelihood of such misperceptions (OR = 2.8, 95% CI: 1.6–4.9, p < .001).

Addiction perceptions

For e-cigarette advertisements, price promotions (OR = 0.5, 95% CI: 0.3–0.8, p = .005) and use cues (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.5–0.9, p = .009) were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate addiction perceptions (i.e., more accurate perceptions). Conversely, ads featuring sweepstakes promotions (OR = 4.6, 95% CI: 2.0–10.4, p < .001), social media engagement (OR = 1.7, 95% CI: 1.1–2.6, p = .023), and youth-targeted socialization themes (OR = 6.2, 95% CI: 2.6–14.3, p < .001) were associated with a higher likelihood of inaccurate addiction perceptions (i.e., participants were more likely to underestimate addictiveness).

In cigarette advertisements, only use cues significantly predicted a lower likelihood of inaccurate addiction perceptions (OR = 0.6, 95% CI: 0.4–0.8, p = .002). No other features were significantly associated with addiction misperceptions in this context.

Discussion

This study investigated the association between specific advertising features and young adults’ perceptions of product harms and addictiveness. The findings offer important insights into which components of tobacco marketing may contribute to misperceptions among individuals aged 18 to 20—a population under the legal age for tobacco purchase in the United States. The results reveal that advertisement has the potential to play a significant role in shaping public understanding of product risks, with certain features associated with misperceptions of harm and addictiveness. Notably, the features associated with misperceptions differed between cigarette and e-cigarette ads, highlighting the need for comprehensive regulations that account for variation in marketing strategies across product types. These findings highlight the importance of tailoring policy responses to address the distinct marketing strategies used across product types.

For e-cigarettes, several advertising features were significantly associated with young adults’ misperceptions of harm. Presence of flavors and gift promotions were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions. Flavors are often considered a marketing tactic because they enhance product appeal and are used to differentiate products in ways that can normalize use and increase attractiveness, particularly among youth and young adults [33, 34]. Although prior literature has often suggested that flavored tobacco products may downplay perceived risk by increasing palatability and reducing perceived harshness, our findings suggest a more complex pattern in which flavor exposure may also prompt greater scrutiny of product harms. This may reflect increased public awareness and regulatory attention to flavored tobacco products, which could heighten risk salience among consumers [35–37]. Similarly, gift promotions may highlight the transactional or incentive-based nature of the product, possibly prompting skepticism or closer evaluation of the product’s safety. In contrast, sweepstakes promotions, social media engagement, and socialization themes were linked to significant underestimation of harm. These features may cultivate a more positive brand image or foster perceptions of e-cigarettes as lifestyle enhancers, thereby distracting from or minimizing the perceived health risks. This aligns with prior research suggesting that promotional framing and social appeal can reduce risk salience in tobacco marketing [38–41].

For addiction perceptions in e-cigarette advertisements, price promotions and use cues were associated with more accurate perceptions. Price promotions, while often seen as increasing accessibility and use [42, 43], may paradoxically increase risk awareness by drawing attention to the commercial nature of these products. Use cues may directly illustrate patterns of consumption, thus reinforcing the potential for dependency. Again, sweepstakes, social media engagement, and youth-targeted themes significantly contributed to underestimation of addictiveness. Particularly striking is the strong association between youth-oriented content, and addiction misperception, which raises concern about the vulnerability of younger audiences to advertising strategies that normalize or glamorize nicotine use.

For cigarette advertisements, use cues were associated with a lower likelihood of inaccurate harm and addictiveness perceptions. This may reflect a greater public awareness of the health risks of smoking, which strengthens the connection between smoking imagery and recognition of its dangers. Interestingly, pleasure claims were also associated with more accurate harm perceptions—potentially indicating a public recognition that immediate sensory benefits do not negate long-term risks. However, gift promotions in cigarette ads were associated with an increased likelihood of inaccurate harm perceptions, suggesting that incentive-based messaging could obscure product dangers even for well-known harmful products like cigarettes.

Overall, these findings have important regulatory implications. Across both product types, specific features—namely sweepstakes promotions, youth-targeted socialization themes, and social media engagement —were consistently associated with significant underestimation of both harm and addictiveness. This suggests that such elements not only enhance product appeal but also obscure risks, especially among young adults who are still forming beliefs about nicotine products. These patterns were especially pronounced in e-cigarette advertising, where youth-oriented content and lifestyle framing may contribute to an unduly favorable image of the product. Restricting these types of promotional strategies, particularly in media accessible to underage audiences, may be an important step toward preventing misperceptions and reducing uptake. Importantly, the observed differences between e-cigarette and cigarette advertising highlight the diverse ways marketing tactics interact with existing product perceptions—a pattern also noted in prior work on youth appeal in tobacco marketing [18]. Rather than focusing on individual brand tactics or product-specific restrictions alone, these findings support stronger, regulations that target marketing features shown to influence youth risk perceptions across product types, such as promotional incentives, social engagement strategies, and lifestyle-oriented messaging. Such an approach would fit under the FDA purview to limit youth appealing marketing and address the mechanisms through which marketing shapes perceptions, regardless of the specific product being advertised [44, 45]. As marketing strategies continue to evolve, regulatory frameworks should remain adaptable and comprehensive, ensuring they capture the full range of tactics used across nicotine and tobacco products.

Limitations

This study is subject to several limitations. First, it relies on self-reported perceptions of harm and addictiveness, which may not directly translate into actual use behavior or long-term risk assessment. Second, while the sample includes young adults aged 18 to 20, it may not represent the broader population in this age group. Individuals with lower media literacy, different socioeconomic backgrounds, or cultural contexts may interpret advertising features differently, limiting the generalizability of the findings. The online nature of the study and recruitment methods may have further introduced sample bias. Third, although the data were collected across two cross-sectional survey waves, participants’ perceptions of harm and addictiveness were assessed immediately after viewing specific advertisements. While this design does not allow for long-term causal inference, it does provide temporal alignment between ad exposure and immediate perceptions. Lastly, although the study lacks experimental manipulation, the within-persons design helps mitigate concerns about pre-existing beliefs by allowing for comparisons across different ad exposures within the same individuals.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that tobacco advertising plays a critical role in shaping young adults’ perceptions of harm and addictiveness, with distinct patterns emerging across product types. While some features, such as flavors or price promotions, may paradoxically heighten awareness of risk, others like sweepstakes, social media engagement, and youth-targeted themes consistently promote misperceptions, particularly for e-cigarettes. These effects are especially concerning among individuals under the legal purchase age, who remain vulnerable to advertising that normalizes or glamorizes nicotine use. Importantly, the features influencing perceptions differ between cigarettes and e-cigarettes, underscoring the need for comprehensive and adaptive regulation. Rather than relying on narrow or blanket policies, regulatory strategies should address the full range of marketing tactics and their context-specific impacts on perception. Content-neutral approaches that target the function and impact of messaging, regardless of product format, will be essential as the tobacco and nicotine landscape continues to evolve.
