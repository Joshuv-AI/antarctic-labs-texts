Identification of two distinct molecular endotypes in cough-variant asthma based on sputum transcriptomics and clinical pathophysiological features
Authors: Wenzhi Zhan, Wei Luo, Feng Wu, Yunhui Zhang, Lin Lin, Chen Qiu, Yadong Yuan, Fang Yi, Ziyu Jiang, Jiaxing Xie, Ruchong Chen, Yuanrong Dai, Jiangtao Lin, Dejun Sun, Chunxing Guo, Mei Jiang, Hu Li, Rongchang Chen, Nanshan Zhong, Kefang Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
RationaleAsthmatic cough may not respond well to corticosteroids. The underlying mechanisms and heterogeneity of cough variant asthma (CVA) remain poorly understood. The objectives of the present study were to explore airway immunological mechanisms and identify molecular endotypes in CVA by analysing sputum transcriptomics, clinical and pathophysiological characteristics.MethodsRNA sequencing and cytokine measurement were performed on sputum samples from newly diagnosed patients with CVA (n=72), classic asthma (CA) (n=28) and healthy controls (HC) (n=28). Patients with CVA were treated and followed-up for 6 months.ResultsThe majority of differentially expressed genes in CVA versus HC overlapped with those identified in CA versus HC. However, the type 2 immunity co-expression network in CVA was lower than that in CA. Based on sputum transcriptomics profiles, two endotypes of CVA were identified: mixed-inflammatory CVA (n=40) and pauci-inflammatory CVA (n=32). Mixed-inflammatory CVA showed higher inflammation-related gene set signatures compared to both pauci-inflammatory CVA and HC. Mixed-inflammatory CVA also showed elevated levels of eosinophils, neutrophils, type 2, type 1 and type 3 cytokines in sputum compared to HC. Conversely, pauci-inflammatory CVA had slightly elevated sputum eosinophils, but no significant gene signatures and cytokine differences compared to HC. During 6 months’ follow-up, pauci-inflammatory CVA showed a trend of less complete resolution in cough (56.2% versus 80.0%, p=0.0553) compared to mixed-inflammatory CVA. Kaplan–Meier analysis found significantly higher cough persistence in pauci-inflammatory versus mixed-inflammatory CVA.ConclusionsCVA exhibits overlapping but distinct airway transcriptomics profiles compared to CA. Two distinct molecular endotypes are identified in CVA, presenting different clinical and pathophysiological features.

Background

Asthma is a heterogeneous airways disease, characterised by variable airflow obstruction and symptoms such as wheeze, dyspnoea and/or cough. Cough could be a troublesome symptom in asthma, which might be refractory to treatment with corticosteroids, resulting in significant physical, psychological and social morbidity [1–4]. Cough variant asthma (CVA), presenting cough as the sole symptom with bronchial hyperresponsiveness (BHR), is one of common causes of chronic cough [5–7].

In general, patients with CVA show similar or slightly milder BHR and airway eosinophilic inflammation compared to those with classic asthma (CA) [8–10]. Both CVA and CA showed sputum cell-subtype profiles, which might be related to anti-asthmatic treatment response, and cough might be refractory to corticosteroids in some patients [4, 11]. Hidden in the different features may be a diverse range of molecular endotypes, where lack of knowledge of the underlying mechanisms hampers determining the optimal treatment regime and the discovery of new therapy targets. Transcriptional profiling of airway immune cells in CA has revealed different mechanisms related to severity, exacerbation, progression and/or treatment response [12–14], and different endotypes have also been identified in terms of type 2, non-type 2 or interleukin (IL)-17 driven immunological endotypes, which is helpful for developing new therapeutic targets and promoting individualised treatment [15–17]. However, inflammatory mechanisms and heterogeneity in asthmatic cough, including CVA, remains poorly understood.

Induced sputum with different components at the cellular, gene expression and protein level has showed to be valuable for the revelation of mechanism in asthma [18]. The present study aimed to investigate the underlying airway inflammatory mechanisms and identify molecular endotypes of CVA through sputum gene signatures and cytokine protein levels in a subset of individuals from the Atypical Asthma in China cohort [8, 19]. Our results indicated that sputum transcriptomics offers potential to unravel novel molecular endotypes with distinctive clinical and pathophysiological features.

Method

Study design and subjects

Newly physician-diagnosed adult patients with CVA or CA were recruited as described in our previous report [8, 19]. Healthy control (HC) subjects with no lifetime history of chronic airway disease were recruited from the community. Patients with CVA were managed with anti-asthmatic treatment including inhaled corticosteroids plus bronchodilators with or without add-on leukotriene receptor antagonists. Patients with CVA were reviewed monthly and followed-up for 6 months. Clinical measurement, questionnaire evaluation, laboratory assessment and induced sputum collection were performed at baseline and follow-up visits using standardised and uniform protocols [8, 19]. Sputum cell for RNA sequencing and sputum supernatants for cytokine measurement were collected at the baseline visit. In this study, complete resolution of cough was defined as the absence of cough or the presence of only very mild cough lasting for ≥1 month. Improvement in cough with anti-asthmatic therapy was defined as a reduction in cough visual analogue scale score by ≥30% compared to pre-treatment baseline scores, accompanied by self-reported improvement in cough symptoms. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Guangzhou, China; identifier number 201675). All participants provided written informed consent.

CVA was diagnosed based on 1) chronic cough as the sole or predominant symptom lasting ≥8 weeks; 2) presence of variable expiratory airflow limitation, demonstrated by positive bronchial challenge test (fall in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) from baseline of ≥20% with 12.8 μmol of methacholine) or positive bronchodilator reversibility test (increase in FEV1 ≥12% and 200 mL from baseline); and 3) cough improved with anti-asthmatic therapy during the follow-up [5]. CA was diagnosed according to typical symptoms such as wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness and/or cough, and presence of variable expiratory airflow limitation [20]. This study ruled out the patients who had other chronic respiratory disease, experienced acute respiratory tract infection or receiving inhaled/oral corticosteroid within the past 4 weeks.

Inflammatory mediator measurement

Sputum supernatants were assayed for IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, IL-13, IL-17A, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-18, IL-21, IL-23, interferon (IFN)-γ, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-β, and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) using the Luminex xMAP Technology by using commercially available Milliplex Human Cytokine Panels (Merck Millipore) following to the manufacturer's guidelines. Values that fell below the lower limit of detection or exceeded the upper limit of detection were reassigned to half or double the respective limit for statistical analysis. Cytokines with >30% undetected values were excluded. GM-CSF and IL-21 were discarded due to high undetected rates.

RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted using standard protocols [19]. Samples with an RNA integrity number >6 were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Genes with a mean count of three or more were used for downstream analyses. The DESeq2 pipeline was used to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (|fold change|>2 and adjusted p<0.05) between conditions [21]. Adjusted p-values (padjust) were obtained using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for multiple comparison. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was used for identifying modules of genes with similar expression patterns [22]. Using the WGCNA R package, automated network construction was performed to create a signed network using a minimum module size of 50 members, a deep split value of 3 and an appropriate power parameter (β=22). The module eigengenes were calculated using the first principal component of all genes within each network. Spearman's correlation was used to assess associations between module eigengenes and clinical traits (e.g. blood eosinophils, fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FENO), Asthma Control Test scores, FEV1 % predicted). The key module demonstrating significant associations with clinical traits was selected for endotype classification, with the threshold defined as the mean+2sd of the module eigengene expression observed in healthy control subjects. This cut-off was established to statistically differentiate pathological expression patterns from physiological variation in the healthy population. Enrichment analyses for DEGs and WGCNA modules were conducted using clusterProfiler (v4.4.4) with Gene Ontology Biological Process (GOBP) terms via the Fisher test [23]. Gene set variation analysis was used to identify differences in the expression of inflammation-related signatures of interest between heterogeneous groups by calculating a sample-wise enrichment score ranging from −1 to 1 for each gene set for each subject, in which positive scores indicated relative elevations in signature expression and negative scores indicated relative decreases in signature expression [24]. Inflammation-related gene set signatures were curated from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as frequency (%), or median with interquartile range (25–75%), unless otherwise stated. Statistical comparisons were conducted using the independent sample t-test for normally distributed data, Kruskal–Wallis test for skewed data and Chi-squared or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Repeated-measures data were analysed using a mixed-effects model with Geisser–Greenhouse correction, followed by post hoc tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to visualise time-to-event outcomes and tested using log-rank statistics. Statistical analysis was performed using R software version 4.2.1 (https://CRAN.R-project.org) and GraphPad Prism version 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software).

Results

128 induced sputum samples were processed for RNA sequencing from 72 patients with CVA, 28 patients with CA and 28 HC. Some of the clinical characteristics of CVA and HC subjects have been reported previously [19]. No significant differences were observed between CVA and CA in terms of age, gender, disease duration, blood eosinophils, FENO, sputum eosinophils or sputum neutrophils (supplementary table E1).

CVA gene expression profiles were similar to CA

Comparing CVA to HC identified 2909 upregulated DEGs (fold change >2 and padjust <0.05) and 49 downregulated DEGs (fold change <−2 and padjust <0.05) (figure 1a). These DEGs were involved in cytokine-mediated signalling pathways, regulation of inflammatory response and regulation of T-cell activation (figure 1b). Compared to HC, 3801 DEGs were upregulated and 124 DEGs were downregulated in CA (figure 1c). Most DEGs of CVA compared with HC were among the DEGs of CA, including CLC, CPA3, IL4, IL5 and IL13 (figure 1a, c). 2558 common upregulated and 30 downregulated genes overlapped across CVA and CA (figure 1d). Compared to CA, CVA identified only 29 upregulated DEGs which were related to defence response to virus (CXCL10, ACOD1, AICDA, PRF1, IL27, IFIT1, HERC5, CXCL9) and chemokine-mediated signalling pathways (CCL8, CXCL11), and five downregulated DEGs which were associated with positive regulation of T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity (CD1A, CD1B, CD1E, CD1C) (figure 1e, f).

Type 2 immunity co-expression gene network was lower in CVA compared with CA

WGCNA analysis was conducted to gain deeper insights into the associations between clinical features and co-expression gene modules, which comprise groups of genes exhibiting similar expression profiles. 13 co-expression gene modules were identified that were randomly assigned colour labels, and 4495 genes that did not belong to any of these modules were excluded from further analysis. The purple module was positively correlated with type 2 inflammation markers (blood eosinophils ρ=0.55, p<0.001; FENO ρ=0.43, p<0.001; and sputum eosinophils ρ=0.64, p<0.001), and was negatively correlated with Asthma Control Test scores (ρ=−0.28, p=0.001) and FEV1 % predicted (ρ=−0.32, p<0.001) (figure 2a). Furthermore, the expression level of the purple module network in CVA was significantly higher compared to HC, yet lower than that in CA (figure 2b). The purple module network comprised 185 genes (supplementary table E2), including those associated with type 2 inflammation, such as IL4, IL5, IL13, CLCA1, CLC and CPA3 [25, 26]. GOBP term enrichment of genes in the purple module included mast-cell-mediated immunity, positive regulation of immune effector process and eosinophil chemotaxis (supplementary table E3). Except for the purple module, no significant differences in expression levels were observed for the other module networks between CVA and CA (figure 2b).

Sputum transcriptomics identify two distinct inflammatory endotypes of CVA

CVA patients were categorised into two endotypes: endotype 1 (n=40, 55.6%) and endotype 2 (n=32, 44.4%), using a threshold of more than two standard deviations above the mean (mean+2sd) of the purple module network expression level in the HC group. Figure 3 depicts the mean enrichment score for inflammation-related gene set signatures, revealing a significant increase in the enrichment score for inflammatory signatures in endotype 1 compared to endotype 2 or HC, including the chemokine signalling pathway, B-cell receptor signalling pathway, T-cell receptor signalling pathway, type 1 and 2 T-helper (Th)1 and Th2 cell differentiation, IL-17 signalling pathway and natural killer cell mediated cytotoxicity. Based on the aforementioned gene set variation analysis, endotype 1 could be characterised as mixed-inflammatory CVA, whereas endotype 2 should be classified as pauci-inflammatory CVA, given the low inflammation-related signatures observed in these patients.

Consistent with transcriptomics profiles, pauci-inflammatory CVA presented slightly increased level of sputum eosinophils, but, no differences in blood eosinophils, sputum neutrophils, type 2 cytokine (IL4, IL-5, IL-9 and IL-13), type 1 cytokine (IFN-γ, TNF-β, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, IL-18) or type 3 immunity (IL-17A, IL-23) in sputum compared to HC (figure 4). In contrast, mixed-inflammatory CVA showed significantly higher level of eosinophil and neutrophil in blood and sputum, as well as increased level of sputum cytokines IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, IL-13, IL-23, IFN-γ and TNF-β.

Pauci-inflammatory CVA was less response to anti-asthmatic therapy compared to mixed-inflammatory CVA

Compared to pauci-inflammatory CVA, mixed-inflammatory CVA demonstrated more severe cough (higher cough visual analogue scale scores) and worse asthma control (lower Asthma Control Test scores) at baseline, despite comparable demographics, comorbidities and lung function (table 1). Both endotypes received similar anti-asthmatic regimens (figure 5), yet 6-month follow-up revealed a trend towards a higher proportion of complete resolution of cough in mixed-inflammatory CVA (80.0% (32 out of 40) versus 56.2% (18 out of 32), p=0.0553), with Kaplan–Meier analysis confirming significantly greater cough persistence in pauci-inflammatory patients (figure 6). Sputum eosinophil levels decreased significantly at 2 and 6 months post-treatment in mixed-inflammatory CVA (figure 7a), although they remained elevated relative to pauci-inflammatory cases throughout follow-up. Sputum neutrophil counts showed no endotype-specific changes post-treatment (figure 7b). Both FEV1 (% predicted) and MMEF (% predicted) improved with therapy across groups, but spirometry values remained similar between endotypes at all time points (figures 7c,d).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate underlying molecular mechanisms and endotypes of CVA through sputum transcriptomics. Our findings revealed that upregulated gene signatures in CVA were similar to those in CA, which associated with regulation of inflammatory response, cytokine-mediated signalling pathways and regulation of T-cell activation. Compared to CA, genes related to defence responses to viruses were upregulated in CVA, while expression of type 2 immunity co-expression network were downregulated. Furthermore, two CVA endotypes driven by different molecular signatures pattern were identified, which presented with distinct clinical pathophysiological features. CVA with a pauci-inflammatory endotype showed less response to anti-asthmatic therapy compared to mixed inflammatory CVA.

Airway inflammation is fundamental to pathophysiology and mechanisms in asthma, which triggers processes such as mucus production, airway remodelling and bronchial hyperresponsiveness [27]. Like in CA, increased eosinophils in sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and bronchial mucosa and airway remodelling changes have been demonstrated in CVA [8, 10, 11, 28]. In alignment with these discoveries, our sputum transcriptomics analysis revealed that the majority of DEGs observed in CVA compared to HC were also present among the DEGs of CA, particularly those associated with eosinophilic airway inflammation, further emphasising the shared inflammatory mechanisms underpinning these respiratory conditions. In many studies, airway eosinophilic inflammation in CVA were slightly milder compared with CA, as indicating by lower levels of eosinophils in sputum and blood and/or FENO [8, 11]. Despite the absence of significant differences in those markers of eosinophilic inflammation between CVA and CA in current study, the co-expression gene network associated with type 2 immunity remained lower in CVA compared to CA. This observation underscores the complexity of the underlying nerve-immune mechanisms in CVA, suggesting that other mechanisms such as cough hypersensitivity may contribute to asthmatic cough except to eosinophilic inflammation. CVA and cough-predominant asthma exhibit heightened cough sensitivity compared to CA [29]. Harford et al. [30] found that respiratory syncytial virus infection enhances transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV)1 channel-mediated calcium transport in the bronchial epithelium of asthmatic individuals. Activation of TRPV1 results in cough. Additionally, respiratory viral infections may induce airway vagal neuroplasticity, which is frequently associated with cough hypersensitivity and chronic cough [31, 32]. In this study, all participants had been free of acute respiratory tract infections for ≥4 weeks. Intriguingly, we still observed an upregulation of genes related to viral defence responses in CVA compared to CA. We reported previously that CVA was common in patients with subacute cough initially trigged by viral infection [33]. Notably, emerging evidence suggests that coronavirus disease 2019 may be associated with an increased incidence of new-onset asthma [34]. Viral infection might result in longer effect on immune regulation and cough hypersensitivity, further investigation is required to elucidate the role of viruses in CVA, particularly in the context of asthmatic chronic cough.

Sputum transcriptomics has been successfully used to identify asthma with different inflammatory endotype [15, 26, 35]. In this study, transcriptomics profiles identified a large subtype of CVA which not only present higher level of eosinophils in blood and sputum, but also showed increased neutrophils in blood and sputum, enhanced type 1 and type 3 cytokine in airways. We defined loosely this endotype subjects as mixed inflammatory CVA. Based on varying cut-off points for differential cells in induced sputum across different studies, airway inflammation in asthma, including CVA, can be broadly categorised into four inflammatory subtypes: eosinophilic, neutrophilic, mixed granulocytic and pauci-granulocytic phenotypes [3, 4]. The response to anti-asthmatic treatment in CVA was not directly correlated with the inflammatory phenotype [3]. Matsuoka et al. [4] observed that mixed granulocytic phenotype required higher inhaled corticosteroid doses compared to other inflammatory phenotypes over a 24-month follow-up period. Our data reveal a promising rate of complete cough resolution among CVA with mixed inflammatory endotype, indicating phenotype might be not equal to endotype in treatment response. Both eosinophilic and neutrophilic airway inflammation in this mixed inflammatory endotype showed improvement following anti-asthmatic treatment, while sputum eosinophils remained higher compared to those observed in pauci-inflammatory CVA, even after 6 months of therapy. Notably, high sputum eosinophils were found to be important predictors of the subsequent CA development from CVA [36]. Longer follow-up is required to determine the prognosis of CVA with mixed inflammation.

A lack of eosinophilic airway inflammation and/or high sputum neutrophils is usually related to poor responsiveness to corticosteroid treatment [3, 4, 37]. Both eosinophilic and neutrophilic airway inflammation in pauci-inflammatory CVA was at persistent low level either at baseline or at follow-up visit. Furthermore, CVA exhibiting these two endotypes displayed no notable differences in factors that may potentially impact the therapeutic response, including age, disease duration, comorbidities or lung function. With 6 months of anti-asthmatic treatment, cough and spirometry were improved; however, the proportion of complete resolution of cough was only 56.2% in CVA with pauci-inflammatory endotype. Transcriptomic analysis of sputum cells primarily reflects the pathophysiological characteristics of airway inflammation. Our findings revealed not only the absence of significant inflammatory-related gene signatures but also comparable levels of type 1, type 2 and type 3 cytokines in sputum between pauci-inflammatory CVA and HC. These observations suggest that noninflammatory mechanisms may play a predominant role in pauci-inflammatory CVA, potentially involving cholinergic dysfunction, airway remodelling, vagal hypersensitivity or neurogenic inflammation. We previously found that two-thirds of patients with CVA respond to only bronchodilator treatment, declaring the solid relationship bronchoconstriction and asthmatic cough [38]. The clinical manifestation of asthmatic cough appears to be closely associated with cough hypersensitivity, a key pathophysiological feature influenced by multiple factors including neuroplasticity, vagal hypersensitivity and neurogenic inflammation [1, 29, 39]. Importantly, cough hypersensitivity may be resistant to conventional anti-inflammatory therapies [40]. To further elucidate these underlying mechanisms, a comprehensive investigative approach is essential, incorporating advanced bronchoscopic evaluation of airway remodelling and neuroplasticity alongside precise quantitative measurements of cough sensitivity and airway neurogenic inflammation. Such multimodal investigations may elucidate the underlying pathophysiological and molecular mechanisms of pauci-inflammatory CVA, potentially revealing novel therapeutic targets for this complex condition.

There were limitations to the study. Firstly, airway inflammation subtype may change with anti-inflammatory treatments. In the current study, sputum samples for RNA sequencing were collected at the baseline visit. Further study is required to assess how these endotypes of CVA change with therapy. Secondly, asthma with pauci-inflammation could result from suppression of airway inflammation by corticosteroids [35]. It may be arguable whether pauci-inflammatory CVA is a distinct entity. In this study, all patients with CVA were newly diagnosed in outpatient clinics, and patients had not received anti-inflammatory medications within the previous 4 weeks. Therefore, “pauci-inflammatory” CVA in our data should be a naïve status of distinct disease entity without interference effect of treatment. Thirdly, the sample size in this study, particularly for CA, was relatively small. Due to this limitation, endotype analysis of CA was not conducted. Further research is required to determine whether there are similar pauci-inflammatory endotypes in CA. Lastly, given the observational nonintervention study, the treatment medications prescribed for each patient were not identical and might change according to the responsible physician's assessment. These variations in treatment between the two CVA endotypes may affect the study results. Thus, controlled trials are required to determine whether pauci-inflammatory CVA demonstrates a lesser response to anti-asthmatic therapy.

In conclusion, this study provides insights into the airway immunological mechanisms of CVA through sputum transcriptomics, revealing overlapping but distinct inflammatory profiles compared to classic asthma. By identifying two distinct endotypes of CVA (mixed-inflammatory and pauci-inflammatory), we demonstrate that these endotypes exhibit differential inflammatory characteristics and clinical outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of endotype classification in asthmatic cough for better understanding cough heterogeneity and guiding personalised treatment strategies.
