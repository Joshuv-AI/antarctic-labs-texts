Meprin β is specifically inhibited by its cognate synthetic propeptide
Authors: Fred Armbrust, Hannah Bruhn, Kira Bickenbach, Nicolas Steen, Sapir Shcoory, Nikolaos Afratis, Nele Korn, Hagen Körschgen, Catherine Moali, Claus U. Pietrzik, Irit Sagi, Christoph Becker-Pauly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Meprin β is a zinc-dependent metalloprotease of the astacin family highly expressed in kidney and intestine. Dysregulation of meprin β expression and activity was observed in several pathological conditions such as Alzheimer’s disease, cancer, fibrosis, and inflammatory bowel disease. Therefore, inhibition of meprin β could be a promising therapeutic option to treat pathological conditions influenced by this protease. However, available small-molecule compounds such as the broad-spectrum metalloproteinases inhibitors marimastat or actinonin lack specificity. Hence, more selective meprin β inhibitors needs to be developed. Here, we provide evidence that the synthetically generated cognate meprin β propeptide serves as highly specific inhibitor for the protease (IC50 = 1.35 µM), while other related metalloproteases such as meprin α and a disintegrin and metalloproteinases (ADAMs) are not affected. Employing a targeted approach revealed that removal of the last four C-terminal amino acids of the propeptide significantly improved its inhibitory capacity (IC50 = 0.68 µM). In cell-based experiments, treatment with the propeptide resulted in diminished meprin β activity and decreased cleavage of its substrates, including the interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) and the G protein-coupled receptor latrophilin-3 (LPHN3). Suitability was further confirmed in ex vivo cultivated organotypic brain slices, where the administration of the propeptide also resulted in decreased meprin β activity and diminished latrophilin-3 cleavage. Thus, not only the synthetic wildtype propeptide but especially a C-terminally truncated propeptide of meprin β represent highly specific inhibitors with potential for therapeutic application.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00018-026-06361-4 .

Background

Meprin β, a zinc-dependent metalloprotease of the astacin family, is highly expressed in the kidney and intestine [1]. While its physiological role in the kidney has not been fully addressed yet, meprin β has been shown to play a crucial role for the host-microbiome homeostasis in the intestine by cleaving mucin-2, a key component of the mucus layer, and the bacterial agglutination factor galectin-3 [2, 3]. Thus, dysregulation of meprin β in the gut is associated with bacterial overgrowth and chronic intestinal inflammation [4]. Moreover, meprin β is tissue-specifically upregulated in different pathological conditions such as fibrosis [5] or Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) [6, 7], where it cleaves procollagens and the amyloid precursor protein (APP), respectively. Increased meprin β activity additionally contributes to inflammatory processes by releasing the soluble interleukin-6 receptor (IL-6R) through ectodomain shedding and thereby inducing pro-inflammatory IL-6 trans-signaling [8]. Given its involvement in these pathological conditions, targeting meprin β could be a promising therapeutic approach. However, developing applicable and selective inhibitors has proven challenging. On the one hand, hydroxamate-based metalloproteinase inhibitors such as batimastat or actinonin can be used to inhibit meprin β activity [9]. However, their use as therapeutic meprin β inhibitors would presumably come along with detrimental side effects resulting from their lack of specificity [10, 11]. On the other hand, mammalian plasma fetuin-B was identified as potent endogenous meprin β inhibitor. However, its therapeutic potential is limited by its relatively large size of 40 kDa and the inhibition of other proteases of the astacin family [12]. A more promising approach involved the development of a transition-state analog inhibitor (named F2) featuring an acidic amino-acid moiety surrounding a non-cleavable phosphinic group, which demonstrated good affinity for inhibiting meprin β [13]. However, the synthesis of this compound is challenging and both time- and cost-intensive.

The aim of this study was to investigate whether the synthetically produced cognate propeptide of meprin β has sufficient specificity and bioavailability and is therefore suitable as a potential therapeutic drug. A similar approach has been successfully conducted with the metalloproteases ADAM10 and ADAM17. It has been shown that the addition of their recombinantly expressed prodomains lead to the inhibition of protease activity [14, 15]. Interestingly, in a tumor mouse model, the intravenous injection of the recombinant prodomain of ADAM17 markedly reduced tumor burden in the lung, and partially in liver and kidney [16].

Microscale thermophoresis

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) was used as a fluorescent-based method to evaluate protein-ligand interactions [20]. MST works as follows: A fluorescent-labeled protein is incubated with different concentrations of a potential ligand and fluorescence intensity, measured in arbitrary units, is detected over time while a temperature gradient is applied, impacting the migration of the fluorescent-labeled protein, which depends on ligand binding. The ratio Fhot/Fcold yields the normalized fluorescence, Fnorm, which is plotted against the logarithmic concentrations of the ligand. Pro- and mature meprin β were labeled with NT-647 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The labeled meprin β (30 nM) was incubated with different concentrations of the synthetically produced propeptides ranging from 6 pM to 50 µM. Subsequently, mixtures were transferred to capillaries and measured using the Monolith NT.115 device (NanoTemper Technologies GmbH). The dissociation constant (KD) was calculated using the NanoTemper software.

Cell culture, transfection and lysis

HEK293T cells and HEK293T cells deficient for ADAM10 and ADAM17 (HEK293T_A10/17−/−) [21] were maintained at 37 °C in an atmosphere of 5% CO2 in Dulbecco´s modified Eagles´s medium (DMEM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transfection with plasmid DNA, pre-mixed with polyethyleneimine (PEI) (1:3) in serum-free medium was performed at 80–90% cell confluence. Plasmid DNA containing cDNA for human soluble ADAM10 ectodomain, mouse ADAM10, human BMP1, N-terminal HA-tagged mouse latrophilin (latrophilin-3), human meprin α, human meprin β wt, human meprin β R238Q, human IL-6R, human APP695 and pcDNA3.1 as empty vector control in different combinations were added together with transfection reagent to the cell culture medium. After 24 h, the cell medium was changed to serum-free DMEM. For cell surface activity measurements, after another 24 h cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and counted using a Neubauer counting chamber. If indicated, the propeptide or its C-terminally truncated variant (propeptideΔC) was added at the specified concentrations concurrently with serum removal. After an initial incubation period of 3 h, the same concentration of the propeptide variant was added again for an additional 3 h. The cells were harvested, washed with PBS and then incubated in lysis buffer (1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in PBS (pH 7,4) with cOmplete™ protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche), 1% (v/v) Triton X-100 in PBS (pH 7,4)) for 30 min at 4 °C. Lysates were centrifuged at 15,000 x g at 4 °C, cell debris was discarded. The protein concentration in the lysates was determined using the BCA assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instructions.

SDS-PAGE and western blot analysis

Cell lysates or supernatants were incubated for 10 min at 95 °C with sample buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 2% (w/v) sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS), 0.1% bromophenol blue (Merck), 10% (v/v) glycerol, 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)). The protein separation was performed by SDS-PAGE (120 V, 90 min) using the Mini-PROTEAN® system (Bio-Rad). Western blot was accomplished with a tank-blot system (Bio-Rad) onto PVDF membranes (0.8 A, 2 h, 4 °C). Afterwards, the membranes were blocked with 5% milk (w/v) in TBS for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies against glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (14C10, Cell Signaling, dilution 1: 5,000), HA-tag (C29F4, Cell Signaling), meprin β (polyclonal antibody, generated against a peptide from the meprin/A5 protein/receptor protein tyrosine phosphatase µ (MAM) domain (Pineda)), latrophilin-3 (B-6, Santa Cruz), IL-6R (4–11, kindly provided by Stefan Rose-John (Kiel, Germany)), APP/sAPPα (6E10, Thermo Fisher), sAPPβ + Asp (Pineda, [22]) unless not stated differently in the respective figure legend, were incubated using a dilution of 1:1,000 with the membrane over night at 4 °C. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch) were diluted in TBS-T (TBS with 0.1% (v/v) Tween® 20) and incubated with the membranes for 1 h at room temperature. The chemiluminescence signal was detected in the Intelligent Dark Box (Fujifilm) or Amersham ImageQuant™ 800 (Cytiva) using the Super Signal® West Pico/Femto Kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Western blot signals were quantified with ImageJ.

Preincubation and stability testing of propeptide inhibitor

To analyse stability properties of propeptideΔC pre-incubations at a concentration of 20 µM at different temperatures for 20 min, freeze-thrawn for indicated times or preincubated using PBS with different pH values for 20 min (buffer only as a control, measurement of meprin β activity after adjustment to pH 7.4). To test proteolytic stability propeptideΔC (20 µM) was incubated with trypsin (1–5 µg/ml) in PBS, chymotrypsin (EDQM) (1–5 µg/ml) in 100 mM Tris, 10 mM CaCl2 (pH 7.8) or thrombin (Sigma) (1–5 µg/ml) in PBS for 20 min at 37 °C or in case of chymotrypsin at 30 °C. To inhibit trypsin/chymotrypsin afterwards PMSF (phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) (final 1 mM) was added. To test stability in mouse serum, mouse serum from C57BL/6 mice were incubated in a 1:1 ratio at 37 °C with 200 µM propeptideΔC for 20 min. Corresponding incubation conditions without propeptideΔC were used as controls for subsequent activity assays.

Experimental animals and Generation, cultivation and lysis of organotypic brain slice cultures (OBSCs)

Mice were maintained under constant environmental conditions (12-hour light-dark cycles, temperature) and in an individually ventilated cage system with food and drinking water ad libitum in accordance with the ethical standards and current guidelines of the European Union. The animals were kept solely for organ removal. This is not an animal experiment in the sense of the German Animal Welfare Act, as no animal was stressed or manipulated. Therefore, no further approval from the responsible authority (Ministry of Agriculture, Rural Areas, Europe and Consumer Protection including the Ethics Committee according to Sect. 15 of the Animal Welfare Act) was required. Meprin β knock-in mice overexpressing meprin β with C-terminal HA tag in astrocytes were generated [22]. Rosa26Mep1b−HA mice, which were heterozygous for GFAP-dependent Cre, are termed as GFAPCre+/−;Rosa26Mep1b−HA mice (or briefly Cre+/−). The respective Cre-negative control animals are referred to as GFAPCre−/−;Rosa26Mep1b−HA (or briefly Cre−/−). Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and brains were isolated. OBSC generation, cultivation and lysis was conducted as described in [22]. Half of the brain slices of each genotype were randomly selected and treated with 5 µM propeptide∆C together with the serum-free medium 24 h prior harvesting, while the rest were left untreated. Additional 5 µM propeptide were added every 6 h.

Statistical analysis and illustrations

All statistical analyses were performed with the GraphPad Prism 10 software. Conducted tests are stated in the respective figure descriptions (*: p < 0.05; **: p < 0.01; ***: p < 0.001). Protein structures were generated using Chimera 1.17.3. The figures were created with Microsoft PowerPoint and BioRender.com.

Results

Meprin β is inhibited by exogenous addition of its propeptide

To analyze whether the propeptide of meprin β serves as a specific inhibitor, we synthesized the propeptide based on the amino acid sequence annotated in uniprot (Fig. 1A). From AlphaFold modeling, the complex of active meprin β together with the synthetic cognate propeptide clearly resembles the structure of pro-meprin β, where the propeptide is covalently linked to the enzyme with only little differences in the very C-terminal part of the propeptide. MST measurements were conducted to analyze the interaction between recombinant meprin β and the synthetically produced propeptide and the sigmoidal curve obtained validated an interaction with a KD of 3.95 µM (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Figure S1A). As negative control, the propeptide was additionally applied to pro-meprin β, which showed no interaction as indicated by the absence of a sigmoidal curve (Supplementary Figure S1B).

To evaluate the inhibitory potential of the propeptide, we added various concentrations to recombinant meprin β in a fluorescent-based peptide cleavage assay (Fig. 1C). In the micromolar range, we observed a concentration-dependent decrease in activity, up to 90% inhibition with 10 µM propeptide. We also analyzed the known meprin β inhibitors actinonin and fetuin-B in the same experimental setup and estimated IC50 values of about 7.5 µM for actinonin and 200 nM for fetuin-B (Fig. 1D and E), which is in the range of previous observations [9, 12, 31]. To investigate the inhibitory capacity of the propeptide towards proteolytically shed meprin β, we analyzed soluble meprin β from conditioned HEK cell supernatants (Fig. 1F). Therefore, we transfected HEK ADAM10/17−/− cells with meprin β and ADAM10. Of note, ADAM10 and ADAM17 are sheddases of meprin β releasing the ectodomain of meprin β to the supernatant, which was also the case upon retransfection of ADAM10. Of note, in the conditioned medium meprin β activity was completely abolished by addition of the propeptide in a concentration-dependent manner. To assess inhibition of meprin β upon application of the propeptide, actinonin and fetuin-B to the conditioned medium, we determined the necessary concentrations for each individual inhibitor leading to a similar reduction of meprin β activity of approximately 75% (Fig. 1G).

Regarding pathological conditions, the inhibition of increased meprin β activity, especially in the membrane-bound state, is important. Therefore, we quantified meprin β cell surface activity using transfected HEK cells (Fig. 1H). We observed that membrane-bound meprin β was concentration-dependently inhibited by its propeptide, down to 10% remaining activity at a concentration of 10 µM propeptide. To further investigate the inhibition of membrane bound meprin β by the propeptide, actinonin, and fetuin-B, we applied these inhibitors at concentrations previously established in Fig. 1G (Fig. 1I). Notably, the propeptide resulted in meprin β activity inhibition of 80%, while actinonin and fetuin-B revealed only inhibition of about 40%. In terms of inhibiting the membrane-bound form of meprin β, these results indicate that the inhibitory potency of the propeptide exceeds that of actinonin and fetuin-B. Additionally, we examined the effects of meprin β inhibition using HEK ADAM10/17−/− cells and observed similar results (Supplementary Figure S2A, B).

The inhibition of meprin β by its synthetic propeptide is highly specific

To assess the specificity of the meprin β propeptide as an inhibitor, we additionally tested other related proteases. Therefore, we examined meprin β’s closest relative meprin α, BMP1 - another member of the astacin protease family group, and ADAM10, which share a similar substrate pool with meprin β. Peptide cleavage assays revealed that the propeptide of meprin β is not capable of inhibiting recombinant meprin α (Fig. 2A). This finding was further confirmed with HEK cell-derived meprin α, which, however, could be inhibited by actinonin and fetuin-B (Fig. 2B). Additionally, recombinant BMP1 (Fig. 2C) and ADAM10 (Fig. 2D) were not inhibited by the propeptide, demonstrating the remarkable specificity of the meprin β propeptide as an inhibitor.

The inhibitory capacity of the propeptide is increased by truncation of the C-terminus

In a targeted approach, we aimed to investigate how truncation of the termini influences the inhibitory capacity of the propeptide. Given the significance of D36 in its interaction with R238 and the crucial role of D49 in binding to the zinc ion within the active site cleft [13], we preserved these residues and their neighboring amino acids (Fig. 3A). Notably, we observed that truncating the C-terminus resulted in significant increase of the inhibitory capacity (Fig. 3B). Surprisingly, when the N-terminus was removed, no inhibitory capacity of this truncated propeptide could be observed anymore. As previously demonstrated in an experimentally solved crystal structure of pro-meprin β (PDB: 4GWM) [32] (Fig. 3C), the N-terminal part of the propeptide is in close proximity to both the catalytic and tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor (TRAF) domain of meprin β, harboring critical interaction sites for propeptide binding (Fig. 3C-G). In contrast, the C-terminal loop of the propeptide protrudes away from the catalytic domain of meprin β. As a result, no crucial interaction sites are present in this region, and its deletion seems to confer a sterical advantage. Calculation of local interaction score in the contact interface (cLIS, normalized between 0 (very low confidence of interaction) and 1 (very high confidence of interaction) as described in [33]) further supports these findings, as cLIS was decreased for propeptide ∆N and propeptide ∆N/C compared to propeptide und propeptide∆C (Fig. 3H). Consequently, this led to a decrease in the IC50 from 1.35 µM for the wild-type propeptide to 0.68 µM for propeptide∆C (Fig. 3I).

To assess the specificity of propeptideΔC, we analyzed its potential inhibitory effects on a panel of other metalloproteases, including angiotensin-converting enzyme 1 (ACE1), ACE2, ADAM17, MMP1, MMP2, MMP14, and ovastacin (Supplementary Figure S3). No effect of propeptideΔC on the proteolytic activity of these metalloproteases was observed, with the exception of ovastacin, whose activity was significantly reduced at propeptideΔC concentrations of ≥ 1 µM.

The synthetic propeptide is a weak inhibitor of the cancer associated meprin β R238Q variant

We tested the inhibitory capacity of the propeptide towards the cancer-associated meprin β R238Q variant described in [13]. This variant was found in various cancer tissues and harbors a mutation in the S1’ site of meprin β. R238, located at the bottom of this specificity pocket, predominantly governs the preference for acidic amino acids and interacts with D36 of the propeptide (Fig. 4A). With the substitution R238Q in meprin β, D36 of the propeptide lacks a partner for forming an ionic interaction, potentially leading to reduced inhibition upon exogenous addition of the propeptide. Indeed, in a peptide cleavage assay we observed that application of 1 µM of the propeptide resulted in inhibition of meprin β wt by 99%, while the variant meprin β R238Q is not inhibited at all (Fig. 4B). Only when the propeptide concentration was increased to 5 µM we observed inhibition of the meprin β R238Q variant of 70%. This outcome underscores how specificity is enabled by intermolecular interactions between meprin β and its propeptide, in particular involving the S1’ position (Fig. 4C). To generate a propeptide inhibitor with improved efficacy against the cancer-associated meprin β R238Q variant, the aspartate at position 36 (D36) in the propeptide was replaced by a smaller alanine residue. Indeed, this leads to a more efficient inhibition of meprin β R238Q, while at the same time the inhibition of meprin β wt by propeptid∆C D36A was strongly reduced compared to propeptide∆C. This is reflected in the calculated Ki values obtained for the various combinations of meprin β and propeptide variants (Fig. 4G–J). Meprin β wt is efficiently inhibited by propeptideΔC, with a Ki of 0.019 µM, whereas the Ki value for inhibition of meprin β R238Q by propeptideΔC is markedly increased to 0.352 µM. The opposite effect is observed for the optimized propeptideΔC D36A variant: the Ki for propeptideΔC D36A for the meprin β R238Q with 0.092 µM is lower compared to 10.56 µM for meprin β wt, indicating substantially increased inhibitory potency against the cancer-associated variant.

Substrate cleavage by meprin β is inhibited by the synthetic propeptide in cellulo

To assess whether the propeptide variants also inhibit the cleavage of meprin β substrates in a cellular context, we transfected HEK cells with meprin β and the well-established meprin β substrates IL-6R [8], latrophilin-3 [35] as well as the amyloid precursor protein (APP)6. Transfecting HEK cells with meprin β and IL-6R, resulted in a 55 kDa cleavage fragment that accumulates in the supernatant (Fig. 5A, B). In the presence of the propeptide, the levels of the released IL-6R cleavage fragment are reduced in a concentration-dependent manner. This effect is even more pronounced when propeptide∆C was added.

Upon transfection of HEK cells with meprin β and latrophilin-3, the full-length form of latrophilin-3 in the lysate is decreased, and simultaneously, a 90 kDa cleavage fragment in the lysate and a 55 kDa fragment in the supernatant accumulates (Fig. 5C, D). In the presence of the propeptide, these proteolytic effects on latrophilin-3 were diminished in a concentration-dependent manner.

Meprin β acts as an alternative β-secretase, generating pre-dominantly N-terminally truncated Aβ2−x peptides (Fig. 5E) 6, which are highly abundant in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients’ brains [36]. Co-transfection of meprin β and APP, compared to transfection with APP only, leads to higher amounts of sAPPβ (~ 100 kDa, detected with a neo-epitope specific antibody [22]) and a contrary effect on sAPPα fragments (~ 100 kDa), produced by α-secretases such as a ADAM10 or ADAM17 [37] (Fig. 5F). In the presence of the propeptide, and even more efficiently by the propeptide∆C, the generation of sAPPβ by meprin β was reduced in a concentration-dependent manner. Due to the competition of α-secretases and β-secretases for the processing of APP, successful inhibition of meprin β by the propeptide is also detectable by elevated sAPPα generation (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, an Aβx−40-ELISA showed a significant inhibitory effect of the propeptide inhibitors on generation of neurotoxic Aβ peptides (Fig. 5G).

In brain lysates of the GFAPCre+/−;Rosa26Mep1b−HA mice overexpressing meprin β, the band corresponding to full-length latrophilin-3 at 130 kDa was decreased, whereas a cleavage fragment of 100 kDa was increased compared to control brain lysates. Treatment with propeptide∆C resulted in a reduction of the cleavage fragment (Fig. 6B). By quantifying the ratio between cleaved and full-length latrophilin-3, we observed a significant inhibition of meprin β-mediated latrophilin-3 cleavage by propeptide∆C (Fig. 6C). To evaluate the potential in vivo applicability of propeptideΔC as a therapeutic inhibitor, we assessed its stability under various pH and temperature conditions as well as its susceptibility to proteolytic degradation (Supplementary Figure S4). PropeptideΔC remained stable over a broad pH range (pH 1–10) and at temperatures up to 95 °C, and it also tolerated multiple freeze–thaw cycles without loss of inhibitory capacity. In contrast, pre-incubation with trypsin, and to a lesser extent with chymotrypsin or thrombin, reduced the inhibitory capacity of propeptideΔC, likely due to proteolytic processing of the inhibitor. Importantly, pre-incubation with murine serum did not affect the inhibitory capacity of propeptideΔC. Taken together, these results identified propeptideΔC as a highly stable and selective inhibitor with promising characteristics for future in vivo studies.

Discussion

Meprin β is an astacin metalloprotease that is upregulated in several pathological conditions [1]. For instance, elevated levels of meprin β have been detected in the brains of Alzheimer’s disease patients, where it may contribute to the generation of neurotoxic Aβ peptides [6, 7]. In peripheral tissues such as skin and lung, meprin β participates in extracellular matrix remodeling and has been implicated in fibrotic processes, partly through cleavage of collagen and other matrix components [5, 8, 38]. In addition, meprin β modulates inflammatory responses through proteolytic processing of cytokines and cytokine receptors. It has been shown to activate pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their bioactive forms, thereby promoting inflammation [39]. Furthermore, meprin β can cleave the membrane-bound IL-6R, generating soluble IL-6R and enabling IL-6 trans-signaling [8]. Notably, meprins can also directly cleave and inactivate IL-6 itself, indicating a complex and context-dependent role in regulating IL-6–mediated signaling [8].To achieve a treatment with minimal side effects for pathologies associated with meprin β, the use of therapeutic inhibitors is a promising option. However, actinonin [9] and fetuin-B [12], the currently available inhibitors for meprin β, do not meet the requirements in terms of specificity and, for fetuin-B, in terms of size and accessibility the of membrane-bound protease. In this regard, peptide-based inhibitors could represent a more promising approach. A published phosphinic peptide-based F2 inhibitor for meprin β [13] has been developed, but synthesis of this compound remains challenging and time-/cost-intensive. Furthermore, such phosphinic-based peptides are more likely to cause an immune response when applied in vivo. Thus, the use of endogenously occurring peptides may represent a preferable approach.

Therefore, in this study, we characterized the synthetically produced cognate meprin β propeptide as a competitive inhibitor for the enzyme. We demonstrated that the propeptide effectively inhibits both membrane-bound and soluble forms of meprin β with an inhibition constant in the micromolar range. Other meprin β-related proteases, such as meprin α and BMP-1 from the astacin family [40], as well as ADAM10, ADAM17, MMP1, MMP2 and MMP14 from the metzincin superfamily, are not inhibited by the propeptide, demonstrating the specificity of the compound for meprin β. Importantly, also activity of ACE1 and ACE2, which colocalize with meprin β in the kidney [41], is not affected by the cognate meprin β propeptide. However, we observed that the activity of the closely-related astacin protease ovastacin, is reduced by the addition of 1 µM or higher concentrations of the meprin β propeptide inhibitor. Ovastacin, like meprins exhibits a strong preference for aspartate in P1’ position [42]. From our experiments, it remains unclear whether the observed effect results from the meprin β propeptide acting as an inhibitor of ovastacin or as a competing substrate. However, given that ovastacin expression is restricted to oocytes, the observed effect is unlikely to have a significant impact on the potential therapeutic use of the inhibitor under pathophysiological conditions [40, 42, 43]. The observed specificity is a significant improvement over commonly used meprin β inhibitors. Typically, broad-spectrum metalloproteinase inhibitors like actinonin, galardin and batimastat are employed, whose mode of function is primarily based on zinc chelation through a hydroxamate group [9, 44–46]. Furthermore, TAPI-1, and TAPI-2, also inhibit meprin β activity [9]. However, several members of the metzincin family are targeted by these inhibitors, particularly matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which are affected to a much greater extent than meprin β. This makes these broad-spectrum inhibitors unsuitable for therapeutic applications that specifically target meprin β. More selective hydroxamate-based inhibitors have been developed, published lately in [47–49], showing inhibition in the in the lower nanomolar range, however, still facing problems typical for hydroxamates like metabolic instability, for example in plasma, and toxic metabolic byproducts [50].

Another prominent and endogenous inhibitor of meprin β is the plasma protein fetuin-B. Fetuin-B has been shown to inhibit meprin β in the nanomolar range [12]. However, the specificity is still an issue as it also inhibits closely related proteases like meprin α or ovastacin. Another drawback of fetuin-B is the molecular weight of 40 kDa, which would lead to high production costs of the recombinant protein or batch to batch variances. Furthermore, we observed that while soluble meprin β is strongly inhibited by fetuin-B, the potency is significantly reduced towards membrane-bound meprin β. As previously discussed, inhibition by fetuin-B probably requires a molecular rearrangement of the meprin β dimer from a “membrane-distal” to a “membrane-proximal” conformation [19]. Therefore, the propeptide has advantages over fetuin-B in terms of size, specificity, and accessibility. By deleting amino acids from the N- and C-termini of the synthetic meprin β propeptide, we observed that the N-terminus, which interacts with the TRAF and catalytic domain of meprin β, is crucial for appropriate binding of the propeptide. However, removing the C-terminal loop improves the inhibitory capacity and leads to increased inhibition of substrate cleavage, as demonstrated with the well-established substrates IL-6R, latrophilin-3 and APP. IL-6R serves as the receptor for the key regulatory cytokine IL-6. Shedding of IL-6R by meprin β enables a process known as IL-6 trans-signaling, where shed IL-6R binds to IL-6 and subsequently activates signaling via gp130 on cells that do not express IL-6R [8, 51]. Excessive trans-signaling may lead to detrimental pro-inflammatory signaling [52]. Besides latrophilin-3, expressed by astrocytes, plays a crucial role in synapse formation by interacting with neuronal proteins during synapse development. Impaired latrophilin-3 function has been associated with hyperactivity in mice and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in humans. Finally, upregulation of meprin β in AD patients’ brains [7] and its function as an alternative β-secretase by generating truncated Aβ peptides [6], suggest meprin β as a potential target in AD. As shown in cellulo for these substrates propeptide inhibitors could presents promising options for therapeutic intervention. To investigate the inhibitory effect of the propeptide in a more physiological context, we utilized ex vivo cultured brain slices overexpressing meprin β in astrocytes. By applying the propeptide to these cultures, we demonstrated inhibition of endogenously expressed latrophilin-3 and its cleavage by meprin β in this model. This supports the feasibility of using the propeptide in potential in vivo applications, a strategy which has already been successfully implemented for ADAM17. In a tumor mouse model, where ADAM17 is a key driver of metastasis, the prodomain of ADAM17 was intravenously injected and led to decreased metastasis [16]. As shown for the cancer-associated variant meprin β R238Q, the synthetic cognate propeptide inhibitor can easily be optimized to inhibit cancer associated protease variants by the simple exchange of certain amino acids, which may be of interest regarding a personalized anti-cancer-therapy. This might also be a suitable strategy to decrease the interaction of the meprin β propeptide with ovastacin. Overall, peptide-based inhibitors show potential for high target specificity and low off-target activity and can be easily synthesized and modified. However, some limitations of peptide drugs require further discussion, e.g. poor membrane permeability and limited in vivo stability. Despite these disadvantages, peptides have both high affinity and specificity for their biological targets, thereby reducing adverse effects and toxicity. Furthermore, peptides generally exhibit lower immunogenicity, are able to modulate protein–protein interactions and can be produced cost-effectively. Peptide-based inhibitors comprise several options for peptide modifications, that can enhance inhibitory properties and enhance stability [52, 53]. Regarding the use of the meprin β propeptide inhibitor for in vivo application it might be necessary to increase the stability and bioavailability. As demonstrated in this study, the propeptide exhibits limited proteolytic stability toward serine proteases such as trypsin, chymotrypsin, and thrombin; however, its stability may be enhanced by rationally designed amino acid substitutions. Notably, in our in vitro testing the propeptide inhibitor is stable against preincubation with mouse serum, which is important for the options of intravenous injections. This observed stability may result from limited proteolytic activity in the circulation due to endogenous protease inhibitors and supports a potential suitability of our inhibitor for a targeted in vivo applications, which however requires more detailed investigations.

In summary, we characterized and optimized the synthetic propeptide of meprin β as a competitive inhibitor with promising molecular properties in terms of size, specificity, and bioavailability. The translation to in vivo applications in preclinical mouse experiments and clinical trials will be part of future studies.
