Autonomous Bayesian Optimization‐Based Control System for Droplet Generation
Authors: Seongsu Cho, Haengyeong Kim, Seonghun Shin, Minki Lee, Jinkee Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDroplet generation has been utilized in various applications, including drug delivery, the fabrication of functional particles, and material synthesis. Achieving the goals of these applications requires droplet generation of a desired size. Microfluidic droplet generation offers precise control of droplet dimensions. However, the flow rates of the droplet and medium phases depend on the channel configuration and properties of the working fluids, and their optimization is a labor‐intensive and time‐consuming process. To overcome these limitations, an autonomous Bayesian optimization (BO)‐based control system for droplet generation (ABCD) is developed. In ABCD, BO is employed to inform decision‐making and refine the experimental conditions. Additionally, computer vision techniques, including image processing and convolutional neural networks, are utilized to analyze the experimental results and provide a dataset for use in decision‐making. The ABCD identified the optimal flow rates to achieve desired droplet sizes and generation frequencies via precise and efficient searches, regardless of the droplet generation target, working fluids, channel geometry, and droplet morphology, within 15 iterations on average. It is anticipated that this system will contribute to the acceleration of research utilizing droplet‐based microfluidic systems while also extending microfluidic process automation in various industrial applications.

Introduction

Droplet‐based microfluidic systems offer high throughput, low reagent consumption, and precise control over chemical and biological reactions,[ 1 , 2 ] making them highly suitable for applications such as microparticle fabrication,[ 3 ] drug delivery,[ 4 ] and diagnostic devices.[ 5 , 6 ] However, the effectiveness of these applications strongly depends on the precise control of the droplet size, as requirements vary significantly. For instance, drug delivery systems often utilize droplets in the tens of micrometers range,[ 7 ] whereas functional particles for water treatment typically require droplets in the hundreds of micrometers range.[ 8 , 9 ] Consequently, a droplet generation control algorithm must accommodate a range of droplet sizes, from tens to hundreds of micrometers. Droplet generation is affected by several parameters, including viscosity, interfacial tension, channel geometry, and flow rates of each phase.[ 10 , 11 , 12 ] Users of droplet‐based microfluidic systems typically control flow rates to optimize droplet size. Previous studies have investigated the relationship between experimental conditions and droplet generation results using dimensionless numbers, such as the capillary and Weber numbers.[ 13 , 14 ] However, although these approaches provide valuable insights into droplet formation dynamics, their reliance on fixed channel geometries and fluid properties limits their practicality for systems with variable experimental conditions.[ 15 ] When the microfluidic channel structure or working fluid properties change, these empirical relationships often break down, necessitating extensive trial‐and‐error. If the user of a droplet‐based microfluidic system lacks experience in droplet generation, then finding the optimal flow rates consumes considerable time and human resources and thereby complicates the use of droplet‐based microfluidic systems.

To address these challenges, machine learning (ML) has recently emerged as a powerful tool for automating experimental optimization—a concept referred to as lab automation.[ 16 , 17 ] ML techniques not only significantly reduce labor, time, and cost of experimental research[ 18 ] but also contribute to optimizing conditions in complex microfluidic experiments[ 19 , 20 ] and automating microfluidic experiments.[ 21 ] Among various ML techniques, neural networks and Bayesian optimization (BO) are widely used in microfluidic experiments. Neural network—a computational model that excels in pattern recognition, data classification, and prediction tasks—are particularly useful for image analysis[ 22 , 23 ] and experimental outcome prediction.[ 20 , 24 ] In droplet microfluidics, neural networks, including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have been utilized to analyze[ 21 , 25 , 26 ] and predict droplet generation results.[ 27 , 28 ] For example, Bariki et al. developed an artificial neural network model to predict droplet size in double‐emulsion generation within a double T‐junction channel configuration.[ 29 ] Wang et al. introduced a dual‐directional deep learning model for predicting droplet generation results in co‐flow channel configurations.[ 30 ] Despite these advancements, existing ML‐based approaches face several critical limitations. First, neural network models require large datasets, often comprising hundreds or thousands of experimental results, for reliable predictions.[ 31 , 32 ] Second, these models have poor generalizability because they are highly specific to a given channel geometry and working fluid, indicating that any modification to the microfluidic system necessitates the complete retraining of the model. This significantly increases the experimental costs and time required.[ 33 ] Third, although neural networks can provide rapid predictions, they lack the ability to dynamically explore experimental conditions and adaptively refine results in real time.[ 34 ] These limitations hinder the practical implementation of neural network‐based approaches for the dynamic control of microfluidic droplet generators.

BO is an alternative ML technique that offers a promising solution to these challenges. BO is a global optimization algorithm that employs a probabilistic surrogate model, typically a Gaussian process (GP), to predict function behavior and iteratively refine experimental conditions.[ 35 ] Unlike neural networks, BO requires significantly fewer experiments to identify the optimal parameters, making it highly suitable for optimizing costly and time‐consuming experimental setups.[ 36 , 37 ] Furthermore, BO offers a more computationally efficient approach for finding optimal experimental conditions compared to methods such as reinforcement learning or a Gaussian mixture model. Several researchers have successfully applied BO to experimental optimization.[ 38 , 39 , 40 ] For instance, Gongora et al. demonstrated that BO could efficiently optimize mechanical design parameters,[ 41 ] whereas Baek et al. utilized BO for the optimal operation of direct ammonia solid oxide fuel cells.[ 42 ] Recently, Siemenn et al. applied BO in combination with computer vision to optimize droplet generation at various length scales.[ 43 ] However, despite its advantages, BO has not yet been extensively utilized to optimize droplet generation under diverse experimental conditions, including varying working fluids and channel configurations. Furthermore, the fixed number of BO iterations meant the process continued even after optimal experimental conditions were identified.

In this study, an autonomous BO‐based control system for droplet generation (ABCD) is presented. ABCD automatically identifies the optimal flow rates for each phase to achieve user‐defined target droplet sizes, eliminating the need for large training datasets and extensive trial‐and‐error optimization, thereby minimizing labor and material consumption. To demonstrate its versatility, we apply ABCD to various target results, working fluids, and channel geometries without requiring prior training data. Furthermore, we confirm that ABCD can optimize droplet generation for different droplet morphologies that require more complex flow rate adjustments. In all cases, our approach successfully identifies the optimal flow conditions across diverse experimental settings, thus paving the way for more efficient and adaptive control of droplet‐based microfluidic systems.

Results and Discussion

Targets and Experimental Conditions

ABCD aims to achieve autonomous control of droplet generation with target size and frequency. In this system, the targets of droplet size (D t) and generation frequency (f t) were decided first, as shown in Figure 1a‐i, and the target size of the core (D t,core) was added for double‐emulsion droplet generation. We set the search range of the flow rate, the thresholds for errors of size (esize≡|D−Dt|Dt) to 0.05, and the thresholds for errors of generation frequency (efreq≡|f−ft|ft) to 0.1, where D and f represent the measured droplet size and generation frequency, respectively. These error thresholds were selected based on prior AI‐driven studies reporting ≈10% error in droplet generation frequency, with stricter accuracy (5%) applied for droplet size owing to its frequent emphasis in the literature.[ 29 , 30 , 32 ] The flow rates of the droplet phase (Q d) and medium phase (Q m) were used as control parameters for single‐emulsion droplet generation. For double‐emulsion droplet generation, control of three flow rates was required, and the flow rates of the core phase (Q c), shell phase (Q s), and medium phase (Q m) were used as control parameters. We reduced these terms to two parameters via mass conservation. Instead of using Q c and Q s separately, the sum of the flow rates for the core and shell droplet phases (Q cs = Qc + Q s) was employed as a control parameter, and Q c and Q s were calculated from the target volumetric ratio of the core and shell droplets.

Experiments and Analysis

Once the targets and search conditions were assigned, ABCD autonomously controlled the droplet generator to determine the optimal flow rates through an iterative loop of experimentation guided by BO. The ABCD comprises two parts: a control section with a computer, as shown in Figure 1a‐ii, and an experimental section with a microfluidic system, including syringe pumps and a CCD camera, as shown in Figure 1a‐iii. After determining the flow rates that generated droplets that satisfied the targets, the system exported the optimal flow rates and droplet generation results.

In the control section, a computer analyzes the experimental results to update the dataset and conducts the decision‐making process using the BO to determine the next experimental conditions to gradually reduce errors. Here, BO suggested the following experimental conditions using the surrogate model and acquisition function. The surrogate model provides a statistical approximation of the objective function, which aims to predict and fit, whereas the acquisition function is a mathematical criterion used to determine subsequent experimental conditions within the search range. This study constructed a surrogate model using the GP with a Matern kernel, which is well‐suited for BO based on actual experimental results.[ 44 ] An expected improvement function was employed as an acquisition function. Figure 1b shows the BO‐based decision‐making flowchart. We constructed the initial dataset from eight experiments generated with Latin Hypercube Sampling (LHS). LHS is widely used to design starting points for BO because its stratified,[ 30 , 45 ] equal‐probability sampling reduces sensitivity to the initial experimental conditions, even though the randomization introduces inter‐run variability. As LHS was employed to set the experimental conditions for the initial experimental dataset, the results of the initial experimental dataset were different for each trial. Based on these datasets, the surrogate model predicted the posterior distribution of the objective function. The objective function is the evaluation score (S), defined as: (1) S=max0,esize−0.05+max0,efreq−0.1

The acquisition function selected the next experimental condition based on the posterior distribution of the surrogate model. Once the experiment was conducted, the system evaluated the results by evaluating the score of S and subsequently updating the dataset for the surrogate model. The system iteratively refines the experiments until S = 0. The entire procedure was implemented in Python code based on the scikit‐learn platform.

In each iteration, the experimental results were autonomously analyzed using image processing and CNNs. The droplets in the images were monitored using the findCircles function in Python, and the droplet diameter was calculated using the cropped images. The droplet generation frequency (f) was Q d/(πD 3/6). Occasionally, the droplet generation results showed a non‐uniform droplet size during the change in flow rates. Moreover, an ill‐conditioned dataset or image can result in system malfunction. To prevent this, a data quality check was conducted before the update of the dataset, as shown in Figure 1b. In the data quality check process, re‐experiments were conducted when the coefficient of variation was over 0.1, owing to non‐uniform droplet generation or the presence of bubbles or dust in the microfluidic device. Furthermore, the generation of double‐emulsion droplets occasionally fails to achieve the desired morphology. To filter these failures, we built a CNN via transfer learning based on InceptionV3, which has a high accuracy in classifying images. The CNN classified the cropped images according to whether the droplets exhibited the morphology of a double‐emulsion droplet. Therefore, the system utilized only the results confirmed for the double‐emulsion droplet generation. (See “Methods” for details.)

The computational process, including control of equipment and calculation for BO and CNN, was performed on a PC with an AMD Ryzen 2400G CPU and 8GB of RAM without an additional GPU. Each iteration took 194.48± 0.19 and 202.81± 4.67 s for single‐emulsion and double‐emulsion droplet generation, respectively. A detailed analysis of time consumption by process in each iteration is provided in Table S1 (Supporting Information).

Conclusion

In this study, we developed an autonomous control and optimization system using BO and confirmed its versatility. The system autonomously conducts the experiments, analyzes the experimental results using image processing and CNN, and refines the experimental conditions at each iteration using a BO‐based control algorithm. The system successfully identified the optimal flow rates for various droplet‐generation targets. For instance, when D t = 450 µm and f t = 7 Hz using DI water and silicone oil in a co‐flow type channel configuration (Case 1 in Table 1), the system identified the optimal flow rates within an average of 16.8 iterations across five trials, achieving droplet generation with errors of 2.92% in size and 5.71% in frequency using only eight initial datasets. Overall, the system successfully identified the optimal flow rates within an average of 14.98 iterations across all cases, thus demonstrating its robustness in achieving consistent results. Optimal flow rates were reliably identified in all five independent trials.

This high performance in identifying optimal experimental conditions was achieved with a zero‐labor system. In this system, experimenters simply set targets and search ranges; the remaining processes are conducted autonomously. This system can provide homogeneous results in droplet generation regardless of knowledge or experience of experimenter and minimize labor by automating the entire optimization process, such as experimentation, analysis, and decision‐making. This approach can optimize experimental conditions for droplet generation without requiring complex measurements of fluid mechanical properties for working fluids, thereby preventing variance in droplet generation results depending on the experience of the experimenter.

The system maintained high performance, even when the experimental conditions varied, including changes in the working fluids, channel configurations, and droplet morphologies. Without additional training, the optimal flow rates within the predefined error thresholds were consistently identified. Furthermore, the use of BO significantly improved search efficiency. Approximately 30% of the results showed S < 0.1 in the case of using LHS with BO, compared with only 10% without BO, thus demonstrating the advantage of leveraging the cumulative experimental results over iterations.

These findings highlight the potential of BO for the autonomous optimization of droplet generation in microfluidic devices, thus enabling the precise and efficient identification of optimal flow rates. This was achieved without numerous datasets for training or physical knowledge, making droplet microfluidic devices more accessible for less experienced experimenters. Beyond this proof of principle, the BO methodology can be applicable to more complex droplet‐based microfluidic platforms—such as droplet‐based microreactors regulated by temperature, reactant composition, or interfacial additives—and to droplets with advanced morphologies, including onion‐ and dumbbell‐like architectures. By accelerating the exploration of vast experimental design spaces, the proposed approach promises to streamline both fundamental research and industrial processes that hinge on finely tailored droplets—ranging from drug‐delivery formulations and droplet polymerase chain reaction to cosmetic manufacturing—which rely on droplets with diverse morphologies and sizes, thereby underscoring its versatility and far‐reaching impact.

Experimental Section

Working Fluids

For single‐emulsion droplet generation, DI water (Sigma–Aldrich) and a 50% aqueous solution of polyethylene glycol diacrylate (PEGDA average Mn 575, Sigma–Aldrich) with 1% of photoinitiator (Irgacure 2959, Sigma‐Aldrich) were utilized as the droplet phase. Mineral oil (mineral oil, heavy, Daejung Chemicals & Materials Co., Ltd.) and silicone oil (KF‐96, Shin‐Etsu Chemical Co.), having a viscosity of 500 cSt were utilized as the medium phase. For double‐emulsion droplet generation, a 33% aqueous solution of polyethylene glycol (PEG) (PEG average Mn 20 000, Sigma–Aldrich) and a 33% aqueous solution of dextran (Dextran from Leuconostoc spp. Mr 450 000–650 000) were utilized as the shell and core droplet phases, respectively. Silicone oil, having a viscosity of 500 cSt was used as the medium phase.

Fabrication and Operation of Microfluidic Devices

Microfluidic devices for droplet generation were fabricated via 3D printing. First, the microfluidic devices were designed using a commercial 3D modelling software (Solidworks, Dassault Systems, MA) and saved in STL format files. These files were printed using a multi‐jet printing‐type 3D printer (Projet MJP 2500 Plus, 3D Systems) with a transparent ultraviolet‐curable polymer resin (VisiJet M2R‐CL) and wax (VisiJet M2 SUP) for the material of the device body and essential sacrificial supporting parts, respectively.[ 12 ] After printing, the printed microfluidic devices were placed in a forced‐convection oven (OV‐15F, Daeyang ETS Co., LTD.) at 80 °C to remove the wax until the wax fully melted. The microfluidic devices were then placed in a hot oil bath to remove residual wax and rinsed in an ultrasonication water bath. Syringe pumps (PHD Ultra, Havard Apparatus) were used to supply the working fluids in the microfluidic devices, and the syringe pumps communicated with the computer via pyVISA, following the control algorithm.[ 46 ] The droplet generation results were observed using an optical microscope (U‐LH10003, Olympus Co.) and recorded using a CCD camera (Manta G‐282C, Allied Vision), which was also communicated to a computer via pyVISA.

CNN and its Training

A total of 8471 RGB images of double‐emulsions were collected and resized to 224 × 224 pixels. The dataset was randomly divided into training and validation sets at a ratio of 8:2. To enhance model generalization, data augmentation was applied during training, including random rotations (0–90°), width and height shifts (0–0.3), shear transformations (0–0.3), adjustments in contrast (0.8–1.2) and brightness (0.8–1.2), horizontal and vertical flips, and varied fill modes (nearest, reflect, wrap, and constant). Transfer learning was conducted using the InceptionV3 CNN, pre‐trained on ImageNet, with a newly added classification head utilizing a softmax activation function for binary classification (double‐emulsion versus not double‐emulsion). The model was compiled using the Adam optimizer (default learning rate of 0.001) and categorical cross‐entropy loss. After training, the proposed CNN achieved training and validation accuracies of 96.95% and 93.75%, respectively.
